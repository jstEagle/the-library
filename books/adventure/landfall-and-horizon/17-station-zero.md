## 17. Station Zero

The intake station didn't look like a fortress. That was the first thing that unsettled me. It looked like a water treatment plant, which is to say it looked like nothing at all—low concrete buildings the color of the fog, a chain-link fence, a gravel lot with two trucks and a flagpole flying no flag. Somewhere beneath our feet, tunnels ran into the mountain like roots, drinking the Serran down through grates and gates and delivering it, purified and deniable, to cities that had never heard of Vessia.

"They built it boring on purpose," Tomas whispered. We lay in the scrub above the compound, watching a guard make his rounds at the pace of a man who had never once been surprised by his job.

"Everything about this place is on purpose," I said. "Boring is armor."

Anselm was gone. I still couldn't say his name without my chest doing something structural. We'd left him at the tree line with what remained of the boat gear, told him to wait three days, knowing he wouldn't, knowing he'd come looking if we weren't back, because that was who he'd turned out to be under all the greed. Grief disguised as greed, right up until it wasn't anymore.

"Guard changes at the top of the hour," Tomas said. He'd been counting for two days off the ridge, patient as an archivist, which is to say patient only when it suited him. "There's a gap of maybe ninety seconds between the east rounds and the west. The fence sensor on the north side is dead—I watched them test it Monday and shrug."

"You've been watching them test fences."

"I've been watching everything. It's the one skill they gave me before they exiled me." He glanced at me. "You don't have to do this part. I can go in alone."

"Tomas." I let the name sit there until he understood. "My mother's report is in there. If it exists, I'm the one who reads it. You're the one who carries copies. That's the division of labor."

He didn't argue. One of the many things I'd come to respect about him: he argued constantly, but never about the things that mattered.

* * *

The gap in the rounds lasted eighty-seven seconds. I know because Tomas counted aloud under his breath while we crossed the gravel, flat on our bellies like the world's least dignified lizards, and I wanted to strangle him for talking and kiss him for being right, and settled for neither.

The dead sensor section of fence lifted away from its post like a curtain. Inside, the compound smelled of diesel and wet stone. The main building's door wasn't even locked—a staff door, propped with a brick, because nobody breaks into a place this boring.

The interior was fluorescent-lit corridors, linoleum, the hum of machinery below us like a sleeping animal. And then, past the operations room, a corridor that changed. Thicker walls. A steel door with a keypad. A sign that read ARCHIVE — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY, in four languages, none of them Vessian.

"They kept records here," Tomas breathed. "Not in the capital archives. Here. Under the mountain."

"Where no subpoena could ever reach them." I tried the keypad. Locked, obviously. "Ideas?"

Tomas produced a laminated card from inside his jacket with the sheepishness of a man showing a forged passport. His old credentials—the ones he was supposed to have surrendered when they exiled him. The ones he'd told me he burned.

"You said you destroyed these."

"I said I chose not to use them. There's a difference. There's always a difference; that's basically the job description of an archivist." He swiped it. The lock thought about it for a long, insulting moment—and clicked.

Inside, the archive was cold and dry and orderly in a way that made my skin crawl. Rows of gray cabinets. Boxes labeled by year, by project, by euphemism. VERRIK HYDROLOGICAL SURVEY. RESETTLEMENT COMPLIANCE. REGIONAL MEMORY MANAGEMENT.

That last one stopped us both.

"Regional memory management," Tomas read aloud, in the voice people use for epitaphs. "They had a department for forgetting."

I pulled the box. Inside were files, and inside the files were the things I'd been chasing up a river for weeks, laid out neat as a picnic. Engineering assessments of the Serran headwaters, dated twenty-two years back. Hydrological models showing exactly how much rain would be needed to trigger a catastrophic flood in the middle valley. A schedule of sluice gate tests. And a memo, initialed by three men whose names meant nothing to me and everything to someone, recommending that the "controlled release scenario" be timed to coincide with the testimony of a key witness scheduled to appear before the border commission.

A key witness. Scheduled to testify that Vessia existed, that it had been flooded deliberately, that the maps were lies.

The flood hadn't been weather. It hadn't even been the dam alone. It had been a *demonstration*, staged on schedule, to destroy the credibility of the one person about to say too much.

And that witness—

I knew before I opened the next folder. Some part of me had known since the willow root, since the photograph. But knowing a thing in your bones and reading it in a stranger's memo are different animals. The folder was thin. My mother's name on the tab, spelled correctly, which somehow hurt worse than a misspelling would have. WITNESS DISCREDITATION — EXECUTED PER PLAN. Attached: clippings of the disaster coverage. The collapsed bridge. The dead clients. My name, twenty years old and already ruined, listed among the survivors whose guide error had caused it all.

"She didn't cause the flood," I said. My voice came out flat, which is how I sound when I'm closest to screaming. "She was going to testify. So they drowned her testimony in mud and bodies and blamed—" I stopped. Started again. "They used *me*. They put me on that mountain so that when it came apart, the story would have a face. A disgraced guide's daughter. Nobody would ever look upstream from me."

Tomas said nothing, which was correct. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a wound.

Then he made a small sound, and I turned, and he was holding a sealed folio. Heavy paper, wax-stamped, the seal cracked long ago and re-taped by careful hands. FINAL REPORT — E. REYES. APPENDIX SUPPRESSED BY ORDER.

"My mother's report," I said.

"It was never destroyed." Tomas's hands were shaking. "It was *sealed*. They sealed it and filed it here, in the building where they manage memory. Freya—they kept it. People like keeping things. Even the things that damn them. Especially the things that damn them; power hoards its own evidence like a dragon."

I took the folio. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the chest had.

Her handwriting filled the first pages—tight, slanted, the handwriting of someone writing fast because she might not get another chance. She described the dam's true purpose. The reservoir's true extent. The drowned capital, drowned not as an engineering necessity but as an erasure, ordered from above, executed with survey maps drawn wrong on purpose. She named names. She attached schedules. She wrote, in the last paragraph, a line I will carry until I die:

*I have a daughter. When she is old enough to ask why we live so far from rivers, I hope someone has the courage to tell her the truth before the consortium tells her a story.*

We lived far from rivers because she was hiding me. All those years of moving, of mountains, of her flinching whenever I talked about becoming a guide—she wasn't running from the consortium. She was running from the day I'd follow the water home.

"Freya." Tomas's voice, very quiet. "We need copies. Now. Before—"

The lights went red.

* * *

The alarm wasn't loud. That was the worst part. A soft, insistent tone, polite as a hotel wake-up call, and every light in the corridor shifting to red, and somewhere below us the great machinery changing pitch.

"Motion sensors in the archive," Tomas hissed. "Of course. Of course they'd have motion sensors in the—"

"How long?"

"Minutes. Maybe less. They'll seal the surface doors first, then sweep." He was already stuffing documents into a waterproof satchel, hands moving fast now, the panic converted directly into efficiency. "Freya. Surface is cut off. Once they seal those doors we're in a box."

I looked at the folio in my hands. My mother's handwriting. Twenty years of silence ending in a red-lit basement.

"There'll be another way out," I said. "A facility like this doesn't have one door. Not into a mountain full of tunnels."

"The intake tunnels." He said it reluctantly, the way you hand someone bad news you've been carrying a while. "They run from the reservoir side, under the reserve, all the way to the source chamber. The maintenance schematics showed access galleries. If we go deep enough—"

"We come out where the river is born."

"Or we drown in the dark halfway there. Those galleries flood. That's rather their entire function."

Above us, boots on stairs. More than two sets. Moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly how small the building was.

I sealed my mother's report into the waterproof roll with the core evidence, strapped it tight against my spine, and felt it there like a second heartbeat. Everything I'd run from, riding between my shoulder blades.

"Tomas. Which way is down?"

He pointed at a steel hatch in the floor marked GALLERY ACCESS — LEVELS 1 THROUGH 9, and gave me a look that said, plainly, *this is insane*, and then hauled the wheel anyway, because he was Tomas, and stubbornness was the whole of his character and the best of it.

The hatch opened onto a ladder dropping into darkness and the smell of cold stone and moving water. Below us, nine levels of tunnel threaded through the mountain toward the source. Above us, the boots reached the archive door.

I went down first. I'm the guide. That's the rule, and rules are the last thing I own.

Behind me, Tomas pulled the hatch shut, and the red light died, and the dark took us—the two of us and my mother's voice, descending together into the mountain that had swallowed a country, toward the place where the river keeps its secrets and, if the old stories were right, gives some of them back.

Somewhere above, a voice on a radio said words I couldn't make out, and then, clearly enough:

*"Seal Level One. They're in the galleries."*

The hunt had moved underground. So had we.
