## 18. Where the River Is Born

The sound reached us before the light did—a deep, tidal breathing, like something enormous asleep under the mountain. Then Tomas's headlamp swept across the cavern and I forgot, for one full second, that we were running for our lives.

The spring chamber was a cathedral no god had ever been invited to. The river rose out of the rock itself in a hundred silver threads, seeping from walls the size of cliff faces, gathering in black pools that spilled over ledges of pale stone and fell away into darkness below. Stalactites hung in forests from a ceiling lost somewhere above our lights. And everywhere, everywhere, water—moving slow and patient, the way it had moved for ten thousand years before anyone thought to dam it, and the way it would move ten thousand years after every last one of us was gone.

"The source," Tomas whispered. His voice did something strange on the second word, cracked down the middle. Twenty years an archivist of things that officially didn't exist, and here was the original document: the river, signed by no one.

"Don't get religious yet," I said. "We're not safe until we're out."

I was wrong about that, but I didn't know it yet, and there's a mercy in ignorance I've learned to take where I find it.

We picked our way along the ledge above the main pool, Anselm in front with the waterproof satchel strapped to his chest like a bandolier. He'd barely spoken since Station Zero. When the alarms started screaming behind us he'd gone very quiet in the particular way of men who have already decided what they're willing to lose, and I'd seen that quiet before—in mirror glass, mostly—and hadn't liked what it meant coming from him.

The far side of the chamber had a service walkway, rusted railings bolted into the rock, part of the intake infrastructure her mother's generation had built and the consortium had spent two decades pretending wasn't there. If we could reach it, it ran to a maintenance stair and up, out through the karst, into country where three people with a satchel could actually disappear.

We were halfway across when the lights came on.

Not our lights. Floodlamps, banked along the upper gallery, snapping on in sequence with a mechanical certainty that told me everything about how much money had been spent keeping this place secret. They lit the cavern like a stage set, and standing at the center of the upper gallery, hands folded behind his back like a man admiring architecture, was Director Voss.

No relation. I'd checked years ago, back when checking such things still felt like paranoia instead of homework. But the name had always felt like a bad joke the world was playing on me.

"Freya Reyes." His voice carried down the rock without effort; the acoustics were perfect, because of course they were. "Or should I say Mara Reyes's daughter. You have your mother's jaw. And apparently her talent for being exactly where she shouldn't be."

Tomas went rigid beside me. Anselm didn't move at all, which frightened me more.

"You know," I called up, "most people introduce themselves before they start monologuing."

"I'm not monologuing. I'm negotiating." He descended the gallery stairs at an unhurried pace, two guards flanking him with rifles held low—not aimed, deliberately not aimed, the courtesy of a man who expected to win without them. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, so attend. Your life. Clean papers in any country you can name. A guide's license, unstamped, unblacklisted, valid from here to the coast. Your friend the archivist gets reinstated with honors—the consortium is generous to reformed idealists. And the boatman walks away with enough money to buy a fleet."

"And all you want is silence."

"All I have ever wanted," Voss said, "is order. Rivers flood. Countries fail. Documents burn. What remains is what we build on top. Your mother understood that better than anyone—that's why she was hired. It's also why she broke, in the end. Idealists always break. I'd hoped you'd inherited her engineering mind instead of her conscience."

"My mother," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and cold, "didn't break. She wrote a report. You sealed it. There's a difference, and the difference is twenty years of drowned houses."

Something flickered across his face—not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt. Recalculation. "So you've read it."

"Every word."

"Then you understand your position is untenable. One report, in one satchel, carried by three fugitives in a hole in the ground I own. The tribunal will never see it. The villages will forget their rooftop miracle by winter. I am offering you the only exit from this mountain that doesn't involve a body bag, and you're quoting poetry at me."

He was right. That was the worst part. Standing on that wet ledge with the alarms finally silent and the floodlamps painting us like specimens, I ran the arithmetic—the same arithmetic I'd been running since Kessel's Landing, since Verrik Gate, since the day I pulled a chest out of a willow root and made myself a target—and every line of it came up dead. Three against however many. One exit route he knew better than we did. A satchel that could be taken off a corpse as easily as off a living woman.

But my mother had run the same arithmetic once, standing in front of a dam she'd designed, holding a report nobody wanted. And she'd written it anyway.

"No deal," I said.

Voss sighed, genuinely regretful, which somehow made it worse. "I was afraid of that. Your mother said something similar, near the end. Also on a ledge, as it happens." He lifted two fingers.

The guards raised their rifles.

And Anselm moved.

* * *

I want to tell it slowly, the way memory insists on telling it, but the truth is it happened fast, the way all the important things happen—fast and loud and without any respect for the people watching.

Anselm tore the satchel strap over his head and shoved it into Tomas's arms, and then he was running—not toward cover, not away from the rifles, but along the ledge toward the intake gate, the great steel bulkhead where the reservoir's pressure leaned against the mountain. The old gate. Her mother's gate. The one thing in this whole cathedral of stone that a man who'd spent thirty years running rivers might know how to kill.

"ANSelm!" My voice bounced off the walls and came back at me sounding like someone else's.

The first guard fired and missed, the shot cracking around the chamber like the sky breaking. The second hesitated—shooting at an unarmed old man in a flooded cathedral apparently required a moment's administrative review—and that moment was all Anselm needed.

He reached the gatehouse wheel. I saw him plant both hands on it, saw the muscles in his back stand out like cable, saw him look back at us just once.

He didn't wave. He didn't shout anything noble. He just nodded, small and businesslike, the nod of a boatman confirming the channel is clear, and then he threw his whole weight against the release dogs.

The gate groaned. The pressure behind it—twenty years of a drowned country leaning on one steel door—found the gap he'd opened and pushed.

"No!" Voss's composure cracked clean down the middle, and for the first time I saw the man underneath, and the man underneath was afraid. "Seal it! SEAL IT!"

The guards turned from us. Toward Anselm. And I stopped thinking.

I've done reckless things in my life. I once free-soloed a face in weather no sane guide would stand under, and I once told a border official the truth about my name, and both times I'd at least pretended to calculate the odds. This time I didn't pretend anything. I went down the ledge at a dead sprint, boots skidding on wet stone, and hit the first guard from behind at the railing, and we both went over the edge together into the black pool below.

The water took me like a fist. Cold—glacier-fed, source-cold, cold enough to stop a heart that wasn't angry enough to keep beating. I came up gasping in the churn, found the railing stanchion, hauled myself half out. Above me the second guard was wrestling with something I couldn't see, and Voss was shouting orders nobody could follow, and Anselm—

Anselm had the gate open.

Not wide. A hand's width. But the reservoir doesn't negotiate, and neither does gravity. Water screamed through the gap in a white rope thick as a body, and the old machinery, unloved and unmaintained for two decades, began to tear itself apart around the opening with a shriek of metal that sounded almost human.

Anselm stood at the wheel with the flood rising past his knees, and he looked at me across the chamber, and he smiled.

It was the first time I'd ever seen him smile. Thirty years I'd known him—no, six days, but some men you know in six days—and in all that time his face had been a locked room with grief living in it. Now the door was open, and what was inside wasn't grief anymore. It was purpose. He'd lost a family to this water when it rose. He'd spent his life running cargo on top of their graves. And now he got to choose how the water behaved, one last time, and he chose it the way a boatman chooses: for the current, against the wall, all hands committed.

"For the village," he said. I don't know if anyone else heard it. The chamber was too loud. But I heard it, and I will hear it until I die.

Then he wrapped both arms around the wheel and let the gate finish tearing loose, and the reservoir came through.

* * *

There is no describing the sound. I've guided rapids that sounded like artillery, and this was beyond that—it was the mountain itself clearing its throat, twenty years of impounded river remembering what it was for. The bulkhead failed section by section, each failure a thunderclap, and the flood came into the station galleries in a brown rolling wall that ate the floodlamps one by one, ate the walkways, ate the upper gallery where Voss had stood making his careful offers.

I saw him go under. I want to be honest about that: I saw it, and some part of me that I'm not proud of watched without moving. Then the water took him too, and I lost sight of whether he surfaced, and I had larger problems, namely staying alive.

Tomas had the satchel strapped tight and was clinging to a stanchion halfway up the service stairs, screaming my name. I climbed. The water climbed faster. We went up the maintenance stair two steps at a time while the chamber below filled like a bathtub, the greatest spring in the range drowning its own birthplace, and somewhere in the roar I heard—or imagined I heard—one long note of metal settling, the intake gate collapsing shut behind the flood, sealing the tunnels, sealing the station, sealing everything we'd found down there under a hundred feet of new river.

Anselm's last work. He'd closed the door behind us so no one could follow, and so no one could ever quietly reopen it.

At the top of the stair, in a ventilation gallery lit by our dying headlamps, Tomas and I collapsed against the rock and shook. The satchel was between us, soaked outside, dry within. Through the stone under our palms we could feel the mountain trembling with the water's passage, the river finding its old paths, the paths her mother had mapped and the consortium had buried.

"He knew," Tomas said. His teeth were chattering. "When he signed on at Kessel's Landing. He knew how it ended."

"He suspected." I pressed my forehead against the cold rock. "He just didn't know he'd be the one holding the door."

We sat there while the tremors faded, two drowned rats guarding a bag of paper, and I thought about Anselm's price—the double pay, the no questions, the greed he wore like a coat over the hole in him. It had never been greed. It had been a man saving up for one good act, the way other men save for a boat.

Grief hit me then, delayed like all my worst weather, and fury came with it riding the same wind, and I let them fuse, because separately they would have broken me and together they were fuel.

"Get up," I said.

Tomas looked at me.

"We're not done. The evidence goes to the tribunal, the villages, every exile network that still remembers the word Vessia. All of it. Everything he bought." I stood, legs shaking, and slung the satchel across my chest the way Anselm had worn it. "Come on. The flood's going to show the whole river what's under it—and we're going to be downstream to meet it."

Far below us, muffled through a hundred meters of stone, the river roared toward the light.
