## 19. The Long Way Down

The water hit us like a verdict.

I'd braced for the surge—Anselm had given us the only warning he could, a shout and a shove and that last look I'll carry until they put me under—but knowing a wall of water is coming and surviving one are different professions entirely. The intake gate came down behind us with a sound I felt in my teeth, in my sternum, in the fillings I'd lost on the Kessel run three years ago, and then the tunnel simply *changed its mind* about what it was. It stopped being a passage. It became a throat.

"Tomas!" My voice didn't even make it past my own ears. The roar ate it.

He was ten feet ahead of me on the maintenance catwalk, or had been. Then the catwalk wasn't there anymore—it was a suggestion, a memory of engineering—and Tomas was in the black water with his arms around the document case, hugging it to his chest like a child, like a lifeboat, like both.

The waterproofing was my one good decision of the night. Back at Maret's, wrapping the core evidence in oilcloth and waxed canvas, sealing it with pitch while Anselm watched and said nothing in that way he had of saying entire paragraphs nothing—I'd done it because I was a guide, and guides prepare for the river doing the worst thing it can think of. I hadn't known then that the worst thing would be my own making. That Anselm would pull the collapse lever and give the station back to the water, and the water would come looking for whoever was standing in its house.

Freya Reyes, cause of disasters. The blacklist had always said so. Tonight, for the first time, I almost believed it.

The surge took me off the ledge before I finished the thought.

Cold is a strange mercy. It shut down everything except the animal parts—the part that knows to point your feet downstream, the part that knows not to fight a hydraulic, the part that knows to breathe when breathing is offered and never when it isn't. I rode the tunnel the way I'd taught a hundred clients to ride rapids: loose everywhere except where it counted. The water slammed me into a wall and I gave it my shoulder instead of my skull. It dragged me under and I counted—one, two, three, four—until the count became a prayer became an argument with myself: *not yet, not here, not after all of this, you do not get to drown forty meters from daylight, Freya, that would be embarrassing.*

Embarrassment has saved my life more times than skill has. I surfaced gasping.

Ahead: light. Not the gray seep of the intake tunnels but actual daylight, ragged and white, tearing open ahead of us where the spillway mouth gaped like a wound. The old Vessian engineers had built their dam to fail gracefully—that's what Maret's father's generation called it, graceful failure, as if drowning a capital could ever be graceful—but they'd also built the spillway wide, because rivers remember what engineers forget, and the Serran was going to need somewhere to be.

Tomas was ahead of me, still clutching the case, being carried toward that light at a speed no swimmer chooses. I kicked after him. The water did most of the work. The water does all the work; you just try to have opinions about where.

We shot out of the mountain together.

* * *

What people don't tell you about near-drowning is how quiet it gets afterward.

The spillway dumped us into the flooded gorge below Station Zero—a churning, boiling mess of released reservoir, brown and furious—but the fury spent itself fast against the gorge walls, and by the time I clawed onto a shelf of wet rock no wider than a coffin, the roar had dropped to a growl and my own heartbeat was the loudest thing left in the world.

I got an arm out, hooked Tomas's collar as he went by, and hauled him onto the shelf beside me. He came up coughing in that awful rhythmic way, folding double, and for a moment we just knelt there on our little island, two drowned rats holding each other up, breathing like bellows.

"Case," he rasped.

"You're welcome," I said. "Yes. Case is here."

He fumbled it open with fingers that wouldn't obey, and we looked inside together. Oilcloth. Waxed canvas. Dry. Her mother's final report, the survey maps, the sealed orders, the photographs—all of it dry, all of it intact, all of it *real*, sitting in a rectangle of lamplight-proof darkness between two people who had no right to still be alive.

Tomas started laughing. It was half sob, and it scared me more than the tunnel had.

"Don't," I said. "Don't do that yet. We're not done."

"We're never done," he wheezed. "That's the whole—*hah*—that's the whole point of archives, Freya. Nothing is ever done. It's just waiting."

"Then it can wait five minutes." I lay back on the rock. The sky above the gorge was going pale at the edges—not dawn yet, but the rumor of it, the sky clearing its throat. Somewhere below us, the river was still rising, still sorting itself out, still deciding what shape to be now that the consortium's twenty-year lie had been punctured at the source.

Anselm was dead. I made myself say it inside my own head, plainly, without decoration, because Anselm deserved plainness. He had run cargo for a drowned country, lost his family to the water that replaced it, and died putting the water back where it belonged. Grief disguised as greed, I'd thought once. There was no disguise left. Just grief, and greed's ghost, and a debt I intended to pay in the only currency that mattered: the truth, delivered.

"Freya." Tomas touched my arm. "Look downstream."

I sat up.

The gorge opened below us into the long valley of the middle Serran, and the valley was *wrong*. Or rather, it was right for the first time in twenty years, and twenty years of wrong had trained everyone—including me—to read rightness as catastrophe.

The reservoir was dropping. Fast. With the intake gates collapsed and the spillway torn open, the water that had stood over the old capital for two decades was running downhill as fast as gravity and geography allowed, and it was taking the surface of the lake down with it like a sheet being pulled off furniture in a house whose owner finally came home.

And underneath—emerging, dripping, impossible—

Rooftops.

* * *

They came up out of the water the way things do in dreams, slowly and all at once. First the high ones: the tower of the old customs house, its copper dome gone green-black, streaming water. Then the ridge of a market hall roof, then chimney stacks in rows, then the long slate spine of what could only be a cathedral, its bell tower leaning drunkenly but standing, standing, standing, shedding two decades of lake in silver sheets.

The city of Vessia. The capital that was never on any map. Surfacing.

"It's real," Tomas whispered. He'd gone very still, the case hugged against him again. "It's actually—Freya, it's *there*. People said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "My grandfather used to say there was a city under the lake. Everyone's grandfather says something like that. You stop listening after a while. You decide they're old men telling stories."

"Yeah," I said. My voice came out strange. "Well. Grandfathers keep better records than governments."

The water kept falling. We watched it happen from our shelf of rock, hour after hour, too wrung out to move and unwilling to try, as the lake surrendered meter after meter and the drowned capital rose into the morning like a bruise coming up through skin. Streets appeared. A bridge, broken mid-span. Gardens gone to silt and skeleton trees. And everywhere, on every rooftop, the marks—carved stones, dated lintels, the seal of Vessia worn but legible on the customs house dome, unmistakable, undeniable, *visible*.

Visible from the valley road. Visible from the terraced fields above the waterline. Visible from every village along fifty kilometers of river where people had been told for twenty years that the lake covered nothing, that there had never been anything there, that asking otherwise was the kind of question that got you deported.

By full morning, we could see them. Figures on the far shore. Small at first—two, then five, then dozens—standing at the water's edge, staring. Someone was ringing the cathedral bell. Badly, arrhythmically, the way you ring a bell you haven't touched since childhood, and the sound rolled up the gorge and broke over us and I discovered I was crying, which was inconvenient, and I let it happen anyway, because some things you don't get to be professional about.

"They're seeing it," Tomas said. His voice cracked clean through. "Every village. They're all seeing it. There's no unseeing it now."

"No," I agreed. "There isn't."

That was the arithmetic I'd been unable to do in the dark of the tunnels, the sum Anselm had solved with his life: a secret in a chest can be buried, burned, bought, or blacklisted. But a secret standing in open air, ringing its own bell, reflected in the eyes of a few thousand witnesses along a river—you can't drown that twice. The consortium had erased Vessia by controlling the water. We'd just handed the water back.

Survival, it turns out, is testimony. All you have to do is stay visible.

* * *

Getting downstream was its own ugly business. The flood had wrecked the valley road in stretches, and the shoreline was chaos—new channels, stranded boats, fences standing in six inches of water that used to be dry pasture. We walked most of the day along the high bank, following the crowd-current inland toward Kessel's Landing, because if the truth needed delivering anywhere first, it was there: the town with the burned library records, the librarian who'd warned me, the four chests and the collectors who wanted them.

Tomas carried the case. I offered twice; he refused twice, with a look that told me not to offer a third time. Fair enough. I'd carried it this far; he could carry it home.

We came down to the river landing at dusk, and that's where the story stopped belonging to us.

There were people everywhere. Not crowds exactly—Kessel's Landing wasn't big enough for crowds—but knots of them, clustered around the ferry office, around the well, around a cart where someone had nailed up a hand-lettered sign. I couldn't read it from the bank. I didn't need to. I knew what it said. Something about the lake. Something about the city. Something about *come and see*.

A boy ran past us toward the water, barefoot, shouting over his shoulder to friends: "—the customs house! You can see the seal! My da says it's been there the whole time, under everything, the whole *time*—"

Tomas caught my sleeve. "Freya. The chest. If anyone asks where—"

"Then we don't get asked." I pulled my hood up out of habit, then pushed it back down, deliberately, feeling the evening air on my face. Old habit. Old fear. I was done dressing for it. "We walk in like we own the road. Because tonight, we kind of do."

The librarian saw us first. She was standing in her doorway with a lamp, watching the river, and when she turned and recognized me—of course she recognized me; I'd asked enough questions to burn a hole in her reading room—her face did something complicated: relief, terror, vindication, all fighting for space.

"You," she said. "You're alive. And the lake—"

"The lake is emptying," I said. "Ma'am, I need your back room, your ink, and every scrap of paper you can spare. What's surfacing downriver needs to match what's written down, or they'll find a way to call it a trick of the light."

She looked at me a long moment. Then she stepped aside and held the door.

Inside, Tomas spread the documents across her table under lamplight, and I stood at the window with the case finally out of my hands for the first time in weeks, watching the river run brown and low and honest past the town, carrying the last of the reservoir out to sea.

Downstream, faint on the wind, the cathedral bell was still ringing.

And on the road into Kessel's Landing, kicking up dust in the last light, came three riders moving fast—too fast, too purposeful, dressed too well for a town celebrating a miracle.

Clean boots. I'd know that silhouette anywhere.

"Tomas," I said quietly, not turning from the window. "How attached are you to finishing this chapter?"

He looked up from the papers. Followed my gaze. Went pale, then set his jaw in that stubborn line I'd first seen in a library doorway what felt like a lifetime ago.

"I'm not done," he said.

"Good," I said. "Neither am I."
