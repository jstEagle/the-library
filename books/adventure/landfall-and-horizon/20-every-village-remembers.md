## 20. Every Village Remembers

I have spent my professional life among documents that no one wanted to read. I can tell you the exact humidity at which vellum curls, the precise temperature that kills mold without bleaching ink, and the particular despair of a man who has catalogued four thousand sealed files and been told, politely, by his own directorate, that none of them exist. What I cannot tell you—could not have told you, three weeks ago—is what it feels like to watch a lie die in public.

It feels like a festival. That's the part nobody warns you about. The truth, when it finally surfaces after twenty years underwater, doesn't arrive solemnly. It arrives like a traveling circus.

* * *

It started with the water.

The surge Freya and I rode out of the spillway—that churning, screaming, half-drowned hour I will be reliving in my sleep for the rest of my natural life—did more than spit us out downstream. It dropped the reservoir. The consortium's intake gates were wrecked, their control systems flooded, and for the first time since the flooding of Vessia, the water level fell below the rooftlines and stayed there.

Freya understood it before I did. She was lying on the gravel bank coughing up half the Serran River when she stopped, mid-cough, and stared upstream.

"Tomas," she said. "Look."

I looked. I saw mist, and morning light, and what I took for a line of dark rocks breaking the river's surface.

"Those aren't rocks," she said. "That's a street."

She was right. For miles upriver, where the drowned capital of Vessia had slept beneath forty meters of cold green water, the rooftops stood exposed in the dawn like the backs of surfacing whales. Slate and tile and copper, weeded and pale, dripping. A bell tower leaning at an angle no architect intended. Chimneys. Guttering. A weathervane, still turning, on a house whose address I later learned was Kolemen Street 14, and whose family had been given six days' notice in the spring of the year they erased the country from the maps.

Six days. I know because the notice itself was in the chest. Form 11-C, Resettlement Order, stamped by an office that officially never existed, signed by a man who went on to a decorated career in the consortium's hydrology division. I had read it by lantern light in a boat while Anselm grumbled about the weight of paper versus the weight of conscience. I had held it in my hands and felt nothing but scholarly satisfaction—a source confirmed, a citation secured.

Then I saw a woman standing knee-deep in the shallows at Kessel's Landing, holding her shoes, staring at a rooftop as if it were the face of a dead relative. Because it was.

Scholarly satisfaction is a poor substitute for that.

* * *

Word travels faster than water. By the second day, every town on the lower Serran knew. By the third, they were coming to see.

We had gone to ground at a landing called Bravik's Ferry, a settlement of perhaps two hundred souls whose principal industry was suspicion of strangers. It was Maret who sent word to us there—or rather, it was Maret's network, which turned out to be considerably larger and better organized than any of us had guessed. The old woman who tended memorial cairns along the shore had been doing something else all these years, it emerged. She had been keeping a list. Names, addresses, loyalties. Every family that remembered Vessia, every household that still set out bread on the old feast days, every child taught the anthem in whispers. Twenty years of quiet tending, and when the rooftops rose, Maret lit a fire on the tallest cairn, and within a day, people came from thirty kilometers around to stand beside it.

We arrived on the fourth day, because Freya said we had to see it, and because I said we had to document it, and for once our reasons pointed the same direction.

What I saw at the cairn field I will be describing to tribunals for years, so let me get the details right now, while they're fresh.

There were hundreds of them. Farmers, ferrymen, grandmothers, children riding shoulders. They had brought offerings—the way you bring flowers to a grave, except these were not flowers. Bread wrapped in cloth. River-smoothed stones painted with names. Small brass objects that I recognized, with a jolt, from the chest: seal-stamps, household marks, the little emblems of a country that had supposedly consented to its own dissolution. People pressed them into the gaps between the cairn stones until the monuments glittered.

An old man was teaching children a song. He sang badly. They sang worse. Nobody corrected anybody.

And everywhere, threaded through the crowd, people were looking at Freya. Not at me—I am, thank the various gods, a forgettable face attached to a memorable amount of paperwork—but at her. Word had come downriver ahead of us, distorted in transit the way words always are: the guide who opened the chest, the daughter of the engineer, the one the consortium wanted silenced. Some of them touched her sleeve as she passed. One woman simply took her hand, held it a moment, and walked on without a word, and I watched Freya Reyes—a woman who has spent a decade perfecting the art of being untouchable—stand there and let herself be touched.

"Your mother tried to stop this," Maret said to her, nodding at the water, at the roofs. "Now look what her daughter did."

"I didn't do anything," Freya said. "The river did it."

"The river," Maret said, "was waiting for someone to open the gate." And she looked at Freya the way you look at a lock you've finally found the key for, and Freya, who fears very few things, visibly decided not to pursue the metaphor.

* * *

Here is what I did, in those same days, and I record it plainly because I suspect someone will ask me to account for it eventually, possibly under oath:

I made copies.

Not elegant copies. I am an archivist; under proper conditions I could produce facsimiles worthy of the national collection. But we had no press, no plates, no proper light, and approximately four days before the consortium finished regrouping. So I sat in the back room of Bravik's Ferry's only inn—which doubled as its tax office, its chapel, and, on Saturdays, its boxing venue—and I copied documents by hand onto whatever paper the innkeeper would sell me. Core evidence only, per Freya's dry-season arithmetic: the Form 11-C resettlement orders. The survey maps with the deliberate falsifications marked against the corrections. Her mother's final report, the sealed one, the one that named names and dates and tonnage of explosive used in the "geological disaster" that destroyed the last witness to Vessia. And the photograph—always the photograph—of Elena Reyes standing before a dam that officially never existed, looking at the camera with an expression I have come to know intimately, because her daughter makes it too.

Eleven copies. That was all I managed. Eleven handwritten dossiers, each one a week of work compressed into four desperate days, each one wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas against the river.

And then I sent them out. This is the part I did well, and I will admit to a certain pride in it, because exile has its compensations and one of mine is that I know the exile networks the way the consortium knows its ledgers. There is a former junior registrar in Ostrava who owes me a favor and hates the consortium more than he fears it. There is a printing collective in the borderlands that runs pamphlets under three different business licenses. There is a woman—I will not describe her further, because she asked me once, very seriously, never to describe her—who moves documents across borders inside crates of preserved fish, and who has never lost a shipment or answered a question.

By the fifth day, eleven dossiers were moving in eleven directions along eleven routes, and somewhere in each of them was a sentence I had added myself, in my best archival hand: *Verify independently. Publish widely. Do not negotiate.*

"Do not negotiate," Freya read over my shoulder. "You've met me, yes? Negotiation is the entire reason either of us is alive."

"That instruction is for the recipients," I said. "You negotiated your way into my life. You don't get to negotiate your way out of the consequences."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was the day the agents reached Bravik's Ferry, though we didn't know it yet.

* * *

I want to be fair to the consortium's local operatives, in the way that fairness is possible toward men whose boots are always too clean for the terrain. They were not stupid. They had lost the reservoir station, lost the gate, lost their monopoly on the physical evidence, and lost, in the space of seventy-two hours, the thing that matters most to any apparatus of silence: the assumption of privacy. You cannot quietly drown a city when ten thousand people are standing on the banks photographing its chimneys.

So they adapted. They always adapt. On the sixth day, notices appeared in the river towns, printed on good stock with official letterhead: *Unstable structures. Hazardous waters. Public access to the reservoir zone prohibited pending engineering assessment.* Signed by a Waterways Safety Directorate that I happen to know, from four years of cataloguing its correspondence, does not concern itself with public safety and did not exist in its current form until the flooding of Vessia required a bureaucratic fig leaf.

Nobody obeyed. That was the beautiful, terrible thing. A safety notice that might have cleared a beach in the capital was laughed at in Bravik's Ferry, where the ferryman's grandfather had drowned—not in the reservoir, in the old river, before the dam—and where the word *assessment* had never once preceded anything good.

But the notices were not really for the townspeople. They were cover. Where the notices went, the men in clean boots followed, and on the evening of the sixth day, one of Maret's runners—a girl of maybe fourteen with the unshakeable calm of someone raised on a river—came to the inn's back room and said, "Three strangers at the landing asking after the archivist. Described you wrong, but described the lady right."

Freya was already moving. I want that recorded too: she didn't hesitate, didn't weigh options, didn't do the grim arithmetic she does so well. She started packing the remaining originals into the waterproof case with the efficiency of a woman who has fled places before and gotten frighteningly good at it.

"No," I said.

She looked at me.

"We don't run," I said. "Not from here. Not this time." My voice did something embarrassing halfway through the sentence, and I pushed through it. "Freya. There are four hundred people between us and that landing, and every one of them knows why we're here. If we slip out the back, we teach them the lesson the consortium wants taught—that this ends the way everything ends, with the guilty walking away and the witnesses scattering. If we stay—"

"If we stay, we get taken," she said. "Or shot. Or 'drowned in a boating accident,' which is their preferred genre."

"Yes," I said. "All of which happens in front of witnesses. Four hundred of them. Possibly five hundred by morning, because the cairns—" I stopped, because I'd heard it from the runner on her way in. "The cairns are already becoming shrines. Did you know? People are coming from the other side of the reserve. Pilgrims. There's a man selling roasted chestnuts at the Kessel cairn, Freya. Chestnuts. The truth has a concession stand."

Something shifted in her face. Not softening exactly—Freya's face doesn't soften so much as reorganize. But I watched her run the calculation she's best at, margins and loads and water levels, except this time the variable wasn't the river. It was the crowd.

"They'll use the pilgrims as leverage," she said quietly. "Take us quietly, or make noise and hurt people."

"Or," I said, "we go public first. Tonight. Before they can stage anything. We stand up at the cairn field with the evidence and we read it aloud, and we let every one of those four hundred people become a witness who can't be disappeared, because you can't disappear a congregation."

"That's insane."

"It's also," I said, "chapter twenty-one of whatever this is, and I've read enough sealed reports to know how the alternative version ends."

* * *

We told Maret. Maret listened with her hands folded, the way she listens to everything, as if the world were a slightly disappointing document she had already annotated. Then she said, "The cairn field holds more people after dark than during the day. Fishermen, mostly. And the ones who keep vigil." A pause. "My father kept vigil for nine years before he died. Someone should tell him it worked."

So it was arranged, and I write this next part slowly because I still don't entirely believe it happened.

At moonrise, Freya Reyes stood on the great cairn at the center of the field—the one Maret's father built first, the one with the brass seal-stones packed into its base—and she read her mother's final report aloud to several hundred people by lamplight. Not summarized. Read. Every name, every date, every tonnage figure, in a clear guide's voice pitched to carry over wind, the same voice that shouts rapids warnings, and I stood beside her holding the lamp and turning pages with hands that shook only a little, which for me constitutes heroism.

When she read the section describing the staged mountain disaster—the engineered collapse, the manufactured evidence, the deliberate destruction of her own mother's credibility—I heard the crowd make a sound I had never heard a crowd make. Not gasping. Something lower. A sound like a foundation settling.

When she finished, there was silence long enough that I could hear the river.

Then an old fisherman began, quietly, to sing the anthem. The bad one, the whispered one, the one taught under breath for twenty years. And four hundred people sang it at full volume for the first time in their lives, standing over a drowned city's risen rooftops, and I—Tomas Ilic, junior archivist, exiled for filing things in the wrong order—stood in the middle of it holding a lamp and crying in a manner I will deny under oath.

That was when the runner found us again. The girl with the calm. She touched Freya's elbow and said, not loudly, over the singing:

"Boats on the water. Three of them. No lights. Coming up from the landing."

Freya closed the report, kissed it—actually kissed it, which I will also deny witnessing—and handed it to Maret.

"Copy it," she said. "Tonight. All of it. More hands than mine now."

"And you?" Maret asked.

Freya looked at the water, where three sets of oars were making the particular muffled rhythm of men trying not to be heard, and then at the crowd, and then at me, and I watched her stop running—not physically, not yet, but somewhere underneath, in the place where the decision gets made.

"Now," she said, "they're the ones arriving uninvited."

The first boat grounded on the shingle while the anthem was still going. Men in clean boots stepped out into the mud, and four hundred people turned to look at them, and I confess that some small archival corner of my mind was already composing the incident report, dated, witnessed, and filed.

Under the circumstances, it seemed only appropriate that everyone should get to read it.
