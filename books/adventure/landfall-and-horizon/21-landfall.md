## 21. Landfall

The morning the director came for us, the river was doing the one thing it had always done better than any of us: telling the truth.

The rooftops of Vessia still broke the surface downstream—miles of them, terracotta and slate, glistening in the early light like the backs of sleeping animals. Word had outrun us by three days. Word, it turns out, travels faster than any boat Anselm ever ran, and it doesn't need a channel to do it. By the time we reached Kessel's Landing, half the river towns had already sent people ahead to see it for themselves, and the other half had sent people to ask us if it was true.

We'd gone public on the stone quay outside the old customs house, Tomas and I, with the waterproofed satchel of core evidence sitting on a fish-crate between us like a third witness. Maret had sent word from upriver: her cairns were becoming shrines overnight, strangers leaving stones and bread and small brass trinkets on the memorials her father built, and she wanted us to know that whatever happened at the landing, the country had already come back to life without anyone's permission.

I should have felt triumphant. Mostly I felt like a woman standing in front of a firing squad, trying to remember which pocket she'd put her courage in.

"They're coming," Tomas said. He'd been at the rail of the customs house porch for an hour, watching the river road. "Two vehicles. Not patrol boats this time. They want an audience."

"Then we'll give them one," I said, and heard my mother in my own voice, and decided I could live with that.

* * *

The crowd gathered the way crowds do—slowly, then all at once. Fishermen who'd ferried us. The librarian from chapter three of my life in Kessel's Landing, the one who'd warned me that questions got people deported, standing now with her arms crossed like a woman who'd been waiting twenty years for someone to ask the questions out loud. Boatmen. Market women with baskets they'd forgotten to put down. A handful of foreign observers who'd arrived on the morning packet—two from the Border Commission, one thin man with a diplomatic pouch and the particular patience of someone paid to watch things happen.

And then the vehicles. Clean, dark, unhurried. The director got out of the first one the way he did everything: like the world had been arranged in advance for his convenience.

He was smaller than I remembered from the intake station. That's the thing about men like him—underground, with alarms screaming and water rising, they fill whatever space they're in. Out here, in daylight, with a hundred people watching, he was just a well-dressed man of about sixty with the tan of someone who summers in places that don't have winters.

"Freya Reyes," he said. Not my false name. He'd never once used my false name, even when it would have been smarter to. I think he liked saying it. I think he liked the idea that he could.

"Director," I said. "You're a long way from your reservoir."

"I'm a long way from a great many things." He looked at the crowd the way you'd look at weather—something to be accounted for, not negotiated with. "I've come to make you an offer. One last time. And I'd ask these good people to remember, afterward, that I offered."

"There's a saying on this river," I said. "The water keeps what it takes. It also gives back. You should know something about that, since you're standing in front of a drowned country that just surfaced."

His jaw did something small and controlled. "Ms. Reyes. You are a blacklisted guide facing charges in two jurisdictions, standing beside an archivist who burned his own credentials, having survived an incident at a protected facility that killed at least one member of my staff. I can give you clean papers. Both countries. Your name restored, your record sealed, a commission if you want one. All I require is the satchel, and a statement that the documents are forgeries."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I arrest you. Here, now, under consortium authority, and you go to a tribunal that my organization has influenced for twenty years." He smiled, and it was almost a kind smile, which was the worst thing about it. "You've read the files, Freya. You know how the tribunals work. You know what happened to the last witness who stood where you're standing."

"I do," I said. "She was my mother."

The crowd made a sound. Not a gasp—something lower, a collective exhale, like the river deciding on a direction.

"And here's what I learned from her file," I went on, because my mouth had started and there was no stopping it now. "She built your dam. She surveyed your reservoir. And when she understood what was about to be drowned—not just a capital, but a country, every map, every record, every grave—she tried to stop it. So you staged a disaster in the mountains to discredit her. You killed people to bury a report. And when her daughter turned out to be inconvenient too, you made sure I'd spend ten years running, blacklisted in two countries, working rivers under other women's names." I looked at the crowd. "That's the offer on the table. Clean papers, in exchange for helping bury her twice."

"Sentiment," the director said quietly. "Is a poor foundation for a life."

"You'd know," I said. "You've been building on it for twenty years."

* * *

Here's the thing nobody tells you about courage: it doesn't feel like the stories. It doesn't feel like fire in the blood. It feels like arithmetic.

I did the arithmetic while he was talking. If I took the deal, I'd be free, and I'd be free the way I'd been free for ten years—running, renamed, hollowed out, one town at a time, one month at a time. I'd have clean papers and no self to put them in. And everyone who'd sheltered me—Maret, Anselm, the boatmen, the librarian with her crossed arms—would go on living under the same quiet threat that had hung over this whole river for two decades.

If I refused, I'd be arrested, probably today. I'd face a tribunal the consortium had influenced for twenty years. I might lose. I might disappear, the way my mother had disappeared, the way Vessia had disappeared.

But there was a difference, and the difference was the crowd. My mother had stood alone with her report. I was standing in front of a hundred people who had seen the rooftops with their own eyes, and two foreign observers with a diplomatic pouch, and an archivist who had already chosen his side by burning his own bridge behind him. Evidence in a satchel can be stolen. Evidence in a hundred memories has to be drowned all over again—and the water had already shown what it thought of that.

"Before you arrest me," I said, "I'd like to surrender the evidence. Publicly. To the Border Commission, not to you."

The director's composure flickered—just a hairline crack, but I'd spent ten years reading water, and I knew a seam in the rock when I saw one.

"That's not—"

"You said you came to make an offer in front of witnesses. Fine. Here's mine, in front of the same witnesses." I lifted the satchel off the fish-crate and held it where everyone could see it. "I am Freya Reyes. I am surrendering these documents voluntarily to the Border Commission observers present, and I am submitting myself to whatever tribunal will take the case. I am not running. I have been running for ten years, and I am done. Whatever happens to me happens in the open, on the record, with these people watching. That's the deal. It's not negotiable, and unlike yours, it doesn't require anyone to lie."

The thin man with the diplomatic pouch stepped forward before the director could answer, which told me everything about how the morning was going to go. "The Border Commission accepts the surrender of materials," he said, in the flat voice of a man who had clearly rehearsed this on the packet boat. "The commission also notes, for the record, that it has received independent documentary submissions from exile networks over the past seventy-two hours, and that a formal inquiry into the Vessian erasure has been opened."

Seventy-two hours. Tomas. He'd been broadcasting through the exile networks since the night we surfaced, and I'd thought he was just being Tomas—thorough, obsessive, unable to leave a record alone. He'd been building the floor I was standing on right now.

The director looked at the pouch. Then at the satchel. Then at the crowd, which had grown while we talked, because crowds do that when they smell a turning tide, and there were now more people on the quay than there were stones in it.

"You understand," he said to me, very quietly, "that this changes nothing at the top."

"Probably not," I agreed. "But it changes everything at the bottom. And the bottom is where rivers start."

* * *

He arrested me anyway. Of course he did. A man like that doesn't leave a quay without the thing he came for, even when the thing he came for is now a symbol with a diplomatic escort.

They put flex-cuffs on me on the customs house steps, in front of everyone, and I made sure to hold my head the way Maret held hers when she talked about her father—not defiant, just certain. Tomas tried to step in and one of the agents put a hand on his chest, and he said, "I'm her archivist," with such absurd dignity that several people in the crowd laughed, and the laugh did more damage to the director than anything I'd said.

"You'll be held pending tribunal," the director said, fastening the last of the paperwork with his own hand, because he was that kind of man. "The tribunal will be fair."

"I've read your tribunals," I said.

"Yes," he said. "Well." And for just a moment—half a second, maybe less—something moved behind his eyes that wasn't confidence. Something that looked, in the morning light, like a man hearing water where he'd been assured there was only stone.

They took me to a consortium holding office two streets up from the quay, which turned out to be my first piece of good luck, because consortium holding offices are staffed by consortium clerks, and consortium clerks had spent the last three days watching the rooftops of a drowned country surface outside their windows.

The first defection happened before they'd finished booking me. A young woman at the records desk—someone who'd processed a hundred blacklists, maybe including mine—walked out of the building at four in the afternoon with a document case under her coat and didn't come back. The second happened at six. By eight, according to the increasingly frantic radio chatter the remaining staff weren't bothering to hide from me, three regional officers had resigned by letter and one had simply left his keys on the desk with a note that read, in full, *The river keeps what it takes.*

Tomas got word to me through a guard who'd gone to school in Vessia's exile communities and had opinions. The Border Commission had the evidence. The inquiry had subpoena powers. The director's own regional staff were delivering documents to the Commission faster than the Commission could log them—twenty years of sealed orders, payment ledgers, the staged disaster's operational files, all of it walking out the door in the arms of the people who'd filed it.

"They're not defecting because of you," Tomas's note said, in his cramped, urgent hand. "They're defecting because the country surfaced and they had to look at it. You didn't free them. You just made it impossible to keep pretending. That's better. That lasts."

I sat on a cot in a clean, well-lit cell—the consortium did everything well, that was always the horror of it—and I read that line about ten times, and then I lay back and did something I hadn't done in ten years.

I slept without planning an exit.

* * *

They came for me on the third day. Not the director—the Commission, with papers, with the thin man and one of the observers from the quay, with a formal transfer of custody that the remaining consortium staff signed with the enthusiasm of people throwing ballast overboard.

"The tribunal convenes in six weeks," the thin man told me as we crossed the quay, past the fish-crate where the satchel had sat, past the spot where I'd said my mother's name out loud for the first time in a decade. "You'll be released on your own recognizance. You're the complainant now, Ms. Reyes, not merely the accused. Your blacklist in Vessia's successor jurisdictions has been suspended pending review."

"And the other country?"

"Will take longer." He almost smiled. "Bureaucracies forgive at the speed of paper. But the paper is moving."

Tomas was waiting at the quay rail, and beside him, to my complete lack of surprise, was a boat—Anselm's old boat, patched, refloated, its paint still scorched from the checkpoint run. A couple of the Kessel's Landing boatmen stood with him, arms crossed, wearing the particular expression of men who had decided something and were prepared to be difficult about it.

"They'll want you on the river," Tomas said. "For the tribunal. To testify at the source. To show them the station, the intake tunnels, the spring chamber." He hesitated. "It's a long way upstream."

I looked at the water. It was running high for the season again—of course it was; it had things to say—and somewhere up past the gorges and the reserve and the drowned villages, the rooftops caught the light all the way to the horizon.

"Tomas," I said, "I've been running down this river for ten years. The least I can do is take one trip up it."

I stepped aboard. My hands, I noticed, were steady.

Behind us, on the quay, the librarian had opened the customs house doors to the public for the first time in twenty years, and people were going in—not to hide from questions, but to ask them.
