## 22. Horizon

The tribunal was scheduled to rule in eleven days, and I had decided to spend eight of them on the river.

"You understand this is insane," said Dr. Pell, adjusting her spectacles against the spray as the bow of my boat kissed the first riffle of the morning. "You're the central witness. If you drown, the whole proceeding becomes a memorial service."

"If I drown," I said, "you can tell them I died doing the one thing I was ever actually good at."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be true, which is a different commodity entirely."

She made a note in her waterproof journal. I liked Dr. Pell. She was one of the foreign observers the tribunal had attached to my case, a hydrologist by trade, and she had the particular courage of a woman who had spent forty years measuring things that could kill her. She'd also paid me, in advance, to guide her up the Serran, which made her my client, which made her my responsibility, which meant that for eight days I got to be exactly what I had always claimed to be on the forged papers: a river guide named Reyes, no first name required.

The name was real now, anyway. That was the strange part. After twenty-two chapters of other women's names stitched over mine—Mara Voss, Ilse Brandt, a ferry hand called Kess—I had walked into the tribunal chamber in the spring and said *Freya Reyes* out loud, under oath, and the world had failed to end. One clerk had looked up. Nobody had arrested me. I had sat back down and discovered that my own name fit the same way a well-worn boot does: you forget it's there until you take it off.

One country had struck me from the blacklist in June. The other—the one where the mountain disaster happened, the one where the consortium's lawyers were still filing paper by the kilogram—would take longer. The lawyers had made that very clear, in the way lawyers do, which is to say they'd made it clear that it would take exactly as long as it was profitable for it to take.

Fine. I had learned to read water. I could read that too.

* * *

We made camp that first night on the gravel bar where it had all started, or close enough to it. Rivers move their bars the way towns move their reputations—you come back looking for the place you remember and find it a hundred meters downstream, wearing a different shape.

I didn't tell Pell what had happened here. She knew anyway; everyone knew; the chest had been in three museums and one very tedious documentary by now. But knowing a story and standing on the ground where it happened are different animals, and she stood at the waterline with her hands in her pockets for a long time, looking at the willows, before she said anything.

"It was wedged in a root, they said."

"Right there." I pointed. The root was still there. Roots are more permanent than most countries. "I almost didn't open it. I told myself I'd throw it back in the morning. Let the river keep what it takes."

"What changed your mind?"

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because I'd had months to figure it out and still hadn't quite. "I don't know that anything did. I think I opened it the way you check a wound. You know it's going to hurt. You look anyway, because not looking is worse."

She nodded and wrote something down. I let her. Some things should be in the record.

* * *

The second day we passed Kessel's Landing, and I did not stop, but I slowed, because a guide slows for her client to see the sights, and Kessel's Landing had become a sight.

The librarian's library had a new wing. I could see it from the water—a low stone annex with a copper roof going green, and above the door, in letters that had been painted by hand and repainted at least twice, the word *VESSIA* in the old Vessian script, the one with the doubled consonants that made foreigners sound like they were clearing their throats. The librarian herself—I never did learn her name, which is a small shame I intend to correct—stood on the steps talking to a cluster of students. She saw my boat. She raised one hand, not a wave exactly, more like a salute between soldiers of the same losing-then-winning war. I raised mine back.

"Friends of yours?" Pell asked.

"Someone who told me to stop asking questions," I said. "The only person in that town with the decency to be honest about what asking would cost."

"And you asked anyway."

"I have been told I'm stubborn. By people who were being generous."

The town had changed in the way river towns change, which is slowly and then all at once. There were pilgrims now. That was the word everyone had settled on, though half of them were tourists and a quarter were scholars and the rest were something else—people who came to stand on the banks and look upstream toward the place where, for three days every dry season, the water dropped and the drowned capital stood up out of the reservoir like a man rising from a long illness, blinking, unchanged, unmistakably real.

Every village remembered. That had been Tomas's line, in the first dispatch he ever filed, and it had been picked up and printed and carved into a plaque in at least two towns, and it was true, and it was also the thing the consortium had never understood. You can erase a country from the maps. You can burn the archives and redact the indexes and buy the historians and stage the disasters. What you cannot do is make ten thousand people forget the shape of a church steeple breaking the surface of the water where their grandmothers used to hang laundry.

They had tried, though. I gave them that. They had tried for twenty years, with budgets and lawyers and, when those failed, with men in clean boots. And the whole apparatus had come apart not because of my evidence, not really, but because of a chest that a flood gave back, and an old woman who kept stones, and a junior archivist who wouldn't leave.

I've thought a great deal about that since. About how the truth doesn't need to be strong. It just needs to outlast the people paid to sit on it. Money has a fiscal year. Grief doesn't.

* * *

We hired the last leg at the same disreputable dock where I had once hired Anselm.

He wasn't there, of course. Anselm was under the intake gate, under the mountain, in the dark where the river is born, and there is a cairn for him now on the shore below Verrik Gate, one of Maret's, built the Vessian way with the largest stone last and the name spoken into the gap beneath it. I had been there in the spring, when they let us out of custody long enough to attend. I had spoken his name into the gap. *Anselm.* Just that. He'd have hated anything longer.

His boat was still there—no. Not his boat. A boat, at the dock where his had been, run by a broad young woman with his same way of looking at you like you were cargo that might bite. I asked her, casually, what she charged for the upper gorge.

"Depends what you're carrying," she said.

"Documents," I said. "And some grief."

"Grief rides free," she said, and I had to turn and look at the water for a moment before I could negotiate like a professional.

She was Anselm's niece. Of course she was. Half the boatmen on the Serran were Anselm's something-or-other now; he had trained a generation of them in the years after the reservoir filled, running cargo and questions and eventually, without ever saying so, memory. The consortium had thought he was a smuggler. He was. But smuggling, it turns out, is just logistics with a conscience, and the thing he'd been smuggling all those years was the fact of Vessia itself, one crate at a time, upriver and down, waiting for someone to open the chest.

I didn't tell the niece all of that. I paid double, the way he would have wanted—no. He'd have wanted triple and then given half back, complaining the whole time. I paid double and she took it and asked no questions, and as we pushed off she said, "You're the one from the trial," and I said, "I'm the one from the trial," and she said, "He talked about you. Once. He said you rowed like you were angry at the water." And I laughed, which surprised us both, because it was true, and because grief, after enough months, will occasionally let you do that.

* * *

The dry season had done its work. The reservoir had dropped nine meters, and the Serran ran narrow and fast between banks it hadn't touched in twenty years, banks that were remembering how to be banks.

And then, around the last bend below the old capital, the water shallowed and cleared and the rooftops came up to meet us.

I have seen this now four times, and it has not once failed to stop my breath, so I will not pretend it failed to stop it this time. The city of Vessar lay below us in the green water, whole. Streets. A plaza with a fountain still standing, dry as a bone, its basin furred with silt. The bell tower of the cathedral—the one whose steeple had been the only thing visible for two decades, the one Maret's father had pointed to across the water and said *that is where we are from*—now stood its full height above the surface, and the bell was still in it. Someone had climbed up, at some point in the spring, and rung it. I don't know who. I hope I never find out, because I like the version where it was everyone.

"God," Pell said quietly. She had her instruments out and was not looking at them.

"That was my reaction too," I said. "First time. I stood in a church steeple overnight with two men and a chest full of paper and I couldn't sleep, and it wasn't fear. It was—" I hunted for the word. "Homesickness. For a home I never had. My mother built the thing that drowned this place, and then she spent the rest of her life trying to unbuild it, and I inherited the trying without ever getting the having."

Pell was quiet for a while. Then she said, "The tribunal will want to hear that. The part about the trying."

"The tribunal," I said, "can read the final report like everyone else. My mother wrote forty pages. She was better at this than I am. I'm just the one who carried it out of a hole in a mountain."

"You carried it out of a hole in a mountain *while it was flooding.*"

"Anselm made the hole. I just declined to drown in it. There's a difference, and the difference is a dead man, and I try to remember that on the days the newspapers get it wrong."

The papers got it wrong a lot. That's fine. Papers have a fiscal year too.

* * *

We made the plaza by the third morning, poling through streets that had been canals for twenty years and were now, briefly, streets again, with weed growing between the cobbles and a pike the length of my arm cruising what had once been someone's kitchen.

I had come ashore here in the spring, in custody, in cuffs, escorted by consortium men who were very careful to walk only where the consortium's lawyers had determined the water line legally began. Now I came ashore in my own boat, in my own boots, with a client who had paid me, and the difference between those two landings was the entire distance I had traveled, and I stood on the cobbles of the plaza of Vessar and let it land on me.

The fountain. I went to the fountain first, because Maret had told me about it—her father used to throw coins in it, she said, before the dam, and her mother had told her *never, the fountain is for wishes and your father's wishes are all the same wish*—and there, in the silt of the basin, half-buried, was a coin. Old Vessian minting. Someone had put it there recently; the silt around it was disturbed.

People were already making the pilgrimage down into the exposed streets. I could see them, far off, small figures moving through the drowned city with lanterns, laying offerings on doorsteps the way someone had once left them in the steeple for me to find. The consortium had sealed this place for twenty years and it had lasted them one dry season. You cannot guard a memory with a fence. You'd need a fence with a memory, and no one has ever built one.

"Reyes." Pell had come up beside me. "The ruling. Eleven days. What happens if it goes against you?"

"Then I appeal, and the appeal goes on for years, and I guide boats the whole time, under my own name, in a country that has already struck the blacklist." I crouched and touched the coin in the fountain basin. Cold. River-cold. "The other country will come around. Countries are like rivers. They all go downhill eventually, and they all arrive."

"And if it goes for you?"

I stood up. That was the question, wasn't it. Everyone assumed I wanted vindication the way a drowning woman wants air, and I did, I do, but there was something past it that I had only recently been able to look at directly.

"Then I'm just a guide," I said. "A guide with a clean name and a mother who was an engineer and a father I never met and a country I never had, standing on stones that belong to somebody else's grandchildren." I looked out over the plaza, at the lanterns moving in the far streets, at the bell tower with its bell. "And I think—I think that's enough. I think that might be the whole prize. Not the name on the paper. This. Being somewhere on purpose."

* * *

We camped that last night on the high bank above the city, where Maret's cairns stood in their long patient row along the shore, each one a family, each stone a name spoken into the gap. Maret herself had died in the winter—quietly, in her sleep, in a house that was finally, legally, hers again—and the cairns were tended now by a rotating roster of volunteers who had organized themselves with the efficiency of people who had waited twenty years for a job. There was a sign-up sheet. There was a sign-up sheet for grief. The Vessians would have found this hilarious. I find it correct.

I walked the row at dusk, the way I always do when I'm up here, and I stopped at Anselm's cairn, and then at the one Maret had built for her father, and then at the newest one, at the end, which I had not seen before.

It was small. Four stones. Unfinished.

The volunteer keeping the ledger that evening was a young man with ink-stained fingers, and when I asked him whose it was, he checked his book and said, "Reserved. It says—" he squinted at the entry, and then looked up at me, and his face did something complicated. "It says *Reyes.* It's been reserved since spring. Nobody would tell us who for. The old woman's instructions."

I stood there for a long time.

A place in the row. Among the families. Among the remembered. Not for the dead—for the living, for whoever came after, for a name that had been forged and burned and blacklisted and finally, at the end of a river, spoken into a gap beneath a stone.

"Tell them it's claimed," I said. My voice did not entirely cooperate. "Tell them the name is Freya Reyes. Tell them she's coming back to finish it herself, with her own stones, when the ruling comes through. Tell them she knows the largest one goes last."

He wrote it down. He wrote it down the way Tomas writes, carefully, like it matters, because it does.

* * *

We ran downstream the next morning with the light behind us, and the city sank away beneath the stern, going green and dim, keeping itself for next dry season, for the season after that, for all the seasons the consortium will not be around to count.

Eleven days. Then the ruling. Then whatever comes after rulings, which is life, which is the part nobody writes chapters about, and which I am beginning to suspect is the only part that was ever the point.

I put my boots on the thwart and let the river carry us, and for the first time in as long as I can remember, I was not running from anything, or toward anything, or under a name that wasn't mine. I was just going downstream, on a river I knew, in the last week of the woman I had been.

The horizon ahead was the same one it had always been. It just finally had me in it.
