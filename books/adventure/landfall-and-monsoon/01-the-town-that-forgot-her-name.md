## 1. The Town That Forgot Her Name

The river came first, always. Before the church bell, before the fish carts rattled down the cobbled hill, before the smell of woodsmoke and wet stone rose from the lower town—there was the sound of the water, low and constant as breathing, working its way under every door in Puerto Vado.

Casper Sandoval had learned to sleep inside that sound the way a man learns to sleep inside his own skin: not by loving it, but by having no other choice.

She woke before dawn on the third of October to rain ticking against the tin roof of her room above the chandlery, and for a moment—the dangerous, unguarded moment between sleeping and waking—she was somewhere else entirely. She was in a bunk car on the mountain division, listening to sleet hit steel, and old Ferro was snoring two bunks down, and tomorrow they'd be running levels over the Kestrel Narrows with the frost smoking off the rails.

Then she opened her eyes and saw the water stain spreading across her ceiling like a map of nowhere, and remembered who she was supposed to be.

Marisol Vega. Seamstress's assistant. Nobody.

She lay still and did what she did every morning: took inventory. Boots under the bed, left toe toward the door. Canvas bag packed, one change of clothes, the false papers sewn into the lining. Window painted shut—her own doing, three months ago, so no one could open it without noise—but the roof outside it was flat and dry-rotted and would take her weight if it came to that. Downstairs, Señora Ibarra slept like the dead and kept a key to the back gate hanging on a nail Casper had already memorized.

Not that anyone was coming. Not that anyone had ever come.

That was the arithmetic she ran every morning, and every morning it came out the same: *no one is looking for you*, followed immediately, in a smaller voice, by *but you are still here, which means someday someone will be.*

---

Puerto Vado forgot names the way a river forgets stones. It was a town built on transit—everything passed through and nothing stayed except the mud. Boatmen, loggers, smugglers, widows, men running from wives and wives running from husbands. If you paid your rent and didn't ask questions at the cantina, the town extended you a kind of citizenship made entirely of indifference. It was, Casper thought some mornings while scrubbing fish blood off the dock planks, the closest thing to grace she'd ever been offered.

Her days had a shape she'd built deliberately, the way she'd once built bridge approaches: load-bearing routine, nothing decorative.

Mornings she worked the docks for Teo Vasquez, who ran cargo upriver when the water allowed and drank downstairs at the Fonda del Río when it didn't. She hauled crates, patched tarps, caulked seams. The work was honest and stupid and hard enough to empty her head by noon.

Afternoons she mended sails and awnings for the Ibarra sisters, who paid badly but never asked why a woman with hands like hers—scarred across both palms, calloused in patterns no needlework explains—had come to their shop knowing nothing about thread and everything about how to keep a machine running. She'd fixed their ancient pedal loom the second week, cleaning thirty years of lint out of the works, and after that nobody questioned her.

Evenings she ate alone, read whatever the lending shelf at the chandlery offered—mostly ruined novels and old almanacs—and went to bed early, because dreams were cheaper than lamplight.

And once a week, without fail, she walked the length of the river road to where the old rail spur came down to a rotted loading platform, and she stood there looking at the tracks until the light went blue, and then she walked home.

The spur was dead. It had been dead eleven years, since the slide took out the upper line and the company wrote the whole branch off. Weeds grew up through the ties. A rusted handcar sat at the platform like the skeleton of an animal that had lain down and died where it stood. Casper looked at it the way other people visited graves.

She told herself it was habit. She was lying, and she knew she was lying, and she let herself lie, because the alternative was admitting that part of her was still standing on Viaduct 7, at four minutes past six on a morning in the wet season, hearing the piers sing.

Bridges sang before they failed. Most people never got close enough to learn that. A pier under strain has a voice—a high thin note, almost sweet, like a wineglass rimmed by a wet finger. Eleven men heard it that morning and didn't know what it meant. Casper had known exactly what it meant, and she had been half a mile away with a surveyor's chain in her hands, and the distance between *knowing* and *being believed* turned out to be exactly long enough for a bridge to fall down.

She hadn't gone back since. She'd gone sideways instead, out of the mountains, down the rivers, through three towns and two names, until the name stuck.

*Marisol.*

It wasn't even a good alias. Too soft. But the woman who'd sold it to her in Corrientes had only had the one set of papers, and Casper had been in no position to bargain, standing in a back room with forty pesos and a face that was starting to appear on notices in the railway towns.

*MARISOL VEGA*, the papers said, and below it, in the clerk's indifferent hand: *seamstress, age 34, no dependents.*

No dependents. No history. No debts.

Well. Two out of three.

---

The trouble started, as trouble usually did in Puerto Vado, at the Fonda del Río.

Casper noticed him on a Thursday evening, which was the night the boatmen settled their accounts and the cantina side of the fonda filled up with wet wool and argument. She was in the corner with a bowl of fish stew, minding her own business the way she minded it professionally—with her back to the wall and her eyes moving.

He sat at the bar like he owned it temporarily. Big man, but big the way freight is big—heavy through the shoulders, deliberate, carrying his weight like something he'd been trained to distribute. City clothes gone wrong in the river damp: good boots ruined by salt water, a coat cut for an office now spotted with mildew. He drank beer and didn't taste it. He watched the room the way she did, and that was the thing that put a cold wire down her spine—not that he was watching, but *how*. He counted exits. He catalogued hands. He was, she realized with a slow sinking feeling, doing exactly what she was doing, because he'd been taught to by the same world.

Collectors. Enforcers. Men whose trade was finding people who had arranged their lives around not being found.

He wasn't there for her. He couldn't be. Nobody knew. The logic ran clean and reassuring, and she made herself believe it, and she finished her stew, and she left by the front door instead of the back purely because using the back door would have been an admission, and she did not run. Running was a language, and everyone in Puerto Vado spoke it fluently.

But she lay awake that night listening to the river, and somewhere around the second hour of dark she admitted the truth she'd been stepping around all evening: the man had asked Teo a question when she'd been hauling the last crate aboard that afternoon. She'd seen his lips move, seen Teo shake his head, seen the man pass something small and flat across the rail of the boat. And Teo had taken it.

Teo, who never took anything from anybody, who bragged about the time he threw a customs officer into the current for suggesting a bribe—Teo had taken it, and had said something afterward, and had pointed.

Not at her. At the chandlery. Which was close enough.

Casper stared at the ceiling and did the arithmetic again, the real arithmetic this time. Three years in Puerto Vado. Three years of being careful, and careful wasn't the same as invisible; careful just meant you were invisible *on schedule*. Somewhere upstream or downstream or inland, a thread had come loose, and threads came loose in only one direction: toward her.

She got up, lit no lamp, and repacked the canvas bag. One change of clothes. The papers. The small tin box with eleven things in it that she had never once looked at and never once thrown away. Then she sat on the edge of the bed with her boots on and waited for morning, because a person who runs at midnight leaves a shape in the dark, and a person who leaves at dawn with the market crowd leaves nothing at all.

---

Morning brought rain, and with the rain, the man.

She saw him from the window at half past seven, standing across the street under the eave of the net-drying shed, not even pretending to be anything other than what he was. He'd found her lodging overnight, which meant he hadn't been guessing—he'd been given an address. Given, or bought. Either way, the loose thread had become a rope, and the rope had a hook in it.

He crossed the street when the fish cart passed, timing himself against the noise, and knocked on the chandlery door below with the flat professional rap of a man who knocks on doors for a living and has stopped expecting welcome.

Casper was down the stairs before the second knock, not because she wanted to meet him but because she wanted the meeting on her ground, in daylight, with Señora Ibarra's customers beginning to drift along the street behind him. Witnesses were armor. Isolation was how men like this worked best.

She opened the door a hand's width.

"Señora Vega." He had a voice like gravel poured slowly. Up close he was older than he'd seemed at the fonda—fifty, maybe, with a face weathered down to function. He held up a leather folio, open, showing a page of figures. "I'll be brief, because I know how you people value brevity."

"You people."

"Debtors." He smiled without warmth. "You have an outstanding obligation. Four hundred and sixty pesos, principal and accrued interest, held against the name Sandoval." He let the name sit in the air between them like a dropped knife. "Transferred through three holding houses since the original judgment. The last one sold it. Debts get sold, señora. Everything gets sold. That's rather the point of them."

Casper kept her face still. Inside, something was falling—a long interior collapse, floor after floor, the way a building goes when the piers go. *Nobody knew.* Except somebody knew, or had known, and had written it down, and paper traveled faster than she ever had.

"I don't know any Sandoval," she said. "Wrong door."

"Of course." He consulted the folio with theatrical patience. "Marisol Vega, seamstress, arrived Puerto Vado three years ago this month on the *Paloma*, out of Corrientes. Paid cash for passage, which is unusual for a seamstress. Hands—" his eyes flicked down, clinical, "—scarred in a pattern consistent with cable work and machinery, not needlework. And three years ago, a woman answering your description boarded a coastal steamer two days after the railway board published its findings on the Viaduct 7 collapse." He closed the folio gently, like a man closing a coffin lid. "I'm not the police, señora. The police want you for questions. I only want money. You'll find I'm the more reasonable of your visitors."

Rain ticked on the awning. Behind her, upstairs, the water stain spread across the ceiling of a life she had built plank by plank, and she could hear it groaning.

"I don't have four hundred and sixty pesos," she said. "I mend sails."

"No. You don't." He reached into his coat and produced an envelope, thick, sealed, expensive paper—the kind of paper that didn't come to Puerto Vado often. He held it out. "Which is why my employer has purchased your debt. All of it. She's prepared to forgive it entirely, along with a considerable bonus, in exchange for a conversation. One conversation. About bridges."

The word went through her like a spike through a tie.

"She?" Casper said, though her mouth had gone dry around it.

"A financier. From the capital." He tucked the envelope into the gap of the door she hadn't widened, wedging it there against her knuckles, forcing her either to take it or let it fall in the mud. "She's lost contact with an expedition upriver. Her people. She needs someone who knows the closed mountain lines—the ones that were abandoned after the slide. The official maps say those routes don't exist anymore." His eyes came up and held hers, and for the first time she saw something almost like sympathy in them, or its well-made imitation. "But maps are drawn by men who weren't there. You were there. She knows that now. That's what the debt bought her—not your money, señora. Your name. Names are worth more."

He stepped back into the rain, done, a transaction concluded.

"Three days," he said. "There's a boat leaving Saturday whether you're on it or not. My employer prefers willing passengers, but she's a practical woman. Practical women make do."

He touched his hat brim—an old courtesy, oddly gentle—and walked away down the street through the fish-smell and the rain, and the town parted around him and closed behind him and forgot him instantly, because that was what Puerto Vado did.

Casper stood in the doorway a long time. The envelope soaked through in her hand. Through the gray curtain of rain she could see the river road, and beyond it, faint in the mist, the dead spur running down to its rotted platform, the rusted handcar lying in the weeds like something waiting to be resurrected or buried.

Eleven years she had kept the mountains on the far side of a locked door. Now someone had bought the key at auction, and the buyer knew about bridges, and the buyer knew about the closed lines, and the buyer was coming Saturday.

Casper Sandoval—who had not existed for three years, who had been very carefully no one—went back inside, sat down at the mending table among the Ibarra sisters' sails, and began, with hands that did not quite shake, to pack.
