## 2. Silence on Channel Nine

Saturday came up gray, the river running the color of pewter under a sky that couldn't decide whether to rain or merely threaten it. Casper had been awake since four, lying in her narrow room above the Ibarra sisters' sail loft, listening to the water slap the pilings and counting the things she would take if she had to run tonight: the canvas roll of tools, the tin box of survey notes she had never found the courage to burn, forty-one pesos hidden in a boot, and nothing else. Eleven years in these mountains and river valleys, and everything she owned still fit into a run.

She had not run yet. That was the strange part, the part she kept turning over like a stone to see what lived underneath. The sensible thing—the thing the woman she'd been before Viaduct 7 would never have hesitated over—was to be on the morning barge downriver, gone before whoever bought the auction lot arrived. Instead she had spent the week mending nets for Doña Ibarra, patching the hull of a leaky skiff, walking the same three streets of Puerto Vado until the town's rhythms wore grooves in her again. As if familiarity were armor. As if the mountains could be held off by routine.

The launch came upriver at half past nine.

She heard it before she saw it—a deep-throated engine working against the current where most boats surrendered to it—and stepped out onto the loft's sagging balcony to watch it round the bend below the church. It was a proper vessel, thirty feet at least, painted a hard industrial white with a blue sheerline, twin outriggers, a radio mast bristling from the wheelhouse like a fishing rod. Not a rich man's toy. A working boat dressed for money, the way a foreman dresses for a banker.

It tied off at the public dock, and one person disembarked.

Casper had expected men. She had expected muscle, lawyers' letters, the blunt machinery of debt. What came up the dock stairs instead was a woman of perhaps fifty-five, silver-haired, cropped short as a soldier's, wearing field clothes that had clearly been chosen by someone who understood what field clothes cost and bought them anyway. She walked with a surveyor's economy—no wasted motion, eyes doing constant arithmetic on the rooftops, the moored boats, the mud-slicked stairs. She carried a leather case in one hand and nothing else.

Behind her, at a respectful distance, came the debt collector, Ferro, looking smaller than he had on Tuesday, the way dogs look smaller beside their owners.

Casper did not go down to meet them. She made them climb.

The loft smelled of tar and hemp and the sisters' lunch—fried plantain and salt fish—and the two of them stood among the hanging sails while Doña Ibarra's youngest, Luz, watched from the cutting table with the frank stare of a child who has never learned that curiosity is impolite. The woman's eyes found Casper immediately, the way a compass needle finds north, and Casper understood that this was not a social call and never had been.

"You're the engineer," the woman said. Not a question. Her voice was low and even, a voice built for radio rooms and board meetings, a voice that had never once needed to shout.

"I mend sails," Casper said.

"You mend sails." The woman let that sit a moment, then set the leather case on the mending table, on top of a half-stitched jib, with the confidence of someone who had never been told not to. "My name is Ilsa Marchetti. You may have heard of Marchetti Consolidated. We hold the mineral concessions above the Río Claro valley, and we are financing an extension of the mountain line through the Cordillera pass."

"I don't follow the railroads."

"No," Marchetti said. "I imagine you make a point of it."

Ferro shifted by the door, uncomfortable, a man who had sold something he was only beginning to understand the weight of. Casper looked at him and he looked away, out the window, at the gray river.

"Three days ago," Marchetti went on, "my survey expedition missed its scheduled check-in on channel nine. Scheduled check-ins every evening at six. They've missed three." She opened the case. Inside, nested in green felt, lay a radio handset, a folded map, and a photograph. "The last transmission we received was garbled—weather interference, we assumed. Then nothing. The pass stations report heavy cloud building over the range, but nothing that should silence a properly equipped party. My people carry two radios each. Both would have to fail."

"Boats sink," Casper said. "Radios drown."

"My people don't drown. Their leader is Tomás Reyes-Ortega, twenty-two years in the field. He does not miss check-ins." Marchetti turned the photograph toward Casper without pushing it across the table—an offering held just out of reach. "This is the route they were surveying."

Casper did not want to look. She looked.

The map was the old closed-line corridor, drawn fresh, and seeing it hit her somewhere below the ribs, the way a smell can hit you before you know what it is. The junction at Kilometer 41. The grade climbing through the burned-over timber. The tunnel mouths numbered in white paint that hadn't been white in eleven years. And beyond them, faintly inked, the switchbacks above the treeline where she had once stood with a theodolite and a crew of twelve and believed, honestly believed, that she was building something that would outlive her.

They were surveying her line. The line they had closed and written off and let the forest take back, the line she had walked out of with a verdict in her ears and eleven names she could not say aloud for three years afterward.

"The closed corridor," she said. Her voice came out flat. Good. Flat was safe.

"The corridor is cheaper than any new cut," Marchetti said. "The grade exists. The tunnels exist, mostly. If the old alignment proves sound, we save two years and forty million." She said the number the way other people said hello. "Reyes-Ortega's team was assessing trestle foundations and tunnel integrity. Standard work. Until the radios went quiet."

"And you want me to find them."

"I want you to lead a party that finds them." Marchetti's eyes were pale gray, nearly colorless, and they did not blink enough. "I'm given to understand there is exactly one person alive who knows that corridor from the inside—who surveyed it, who knows which trestles were rotten in the year they closed and can calculate what eleven more years of rot have done. Ferro tells me that person has been living in Puerto Vado under another name, mending sails."

"Ferro talks too much."

"He talks when he's paid to. Don't blame him; he's a tool, and tools do what they're shaped for." Marchetti said it without contempt, which somehow made it worse. "Here is my offer. Your debt—every peso of it, and I know the number, because I bought it along with everything else Ferro's agency holds—is cleared today. Beyond that, double the amount again, in cash, on your return. Enough to disappear properly this time. A new country, if you want it. Enough that no one ever finds you unless you wish to be found."

Outside, the rain finally committed itself, coming down soft and steady on the tin roof, filling the silences between sentences. Luz had stopped watching and gone back to her cutting, though Casper would have bet the loft the girl was listening to every word.

"And if I refuse?"

Marchetti smiled. It was a small smile, precise, the kind that never reached anything below the cheekbones. "Then nothing happens to you. I'm not in the business of forcing engineers. I'll hire someone from the coastal office, someone who read about the corridor in a textbook, and he will take a boatload of well-meaning men up a dead river into rotten trestles and collapsed tunnels, and some of them will die learning what you already know." She closed the case, unhurried. "And your debt remains. And Ferro remains. And Saturday comes around again next month, and the month after. But nothing happens *to* you, engineer. I deal in incentives, not threats. Threats are for people who can't afford better."

* * *

Afterward Casper stood alone in the loft while the rain worked the roof and the river rose a hand's width against the pilings. The sisters had gone to the market, wisely or by instinct; Luz had been sent on an errand that would take an hour. Casper was grateful for the emptiness. Emptiness didn't ask questions.

She laid the photograph on the mending table and looked at it properly this time, letting herself see what she'd refused to see in front of Marchetti.

Kilometer 41. She knew the junction the way you know the back of your own hand—better, because hands change and the junction hadn't. There was a spring there that ran red with iron in the wet season. There was a bend where the river undercut the bank every year, eating a meter at a time, and in her day they'd argued about riprap until the budget died and the argument was shelved and the river kept eating. There was Trestle 6, whose third pier sat on gravel when the drawings swore it sat on bedrock—she had flagged it, she had *flagged it*, and the flag had come back stamped OVERBUILT, PROCEED, in an assistant director's blue pencil, and eight months later—

Stop. She was good at stopping. Eleven years of practice.

But the dread wouldn't stop, and it wasn't the dread of being found, either. Being found was almost a relief; the waiting was worse than any reckoning could be. No. The dread was simpler and older than that. It was the corridor itself. Somewhere up that grade, past the tunnel mouths, past the switchbacks, was the place where her life had split into before and after, and Marchetti was asking her to walk back into the *before* and pretend the after hadn't happened.

And underneath the dread, quieter, more dangerous, was the other thing. The thing she'd been carrying since Tuesday, since she'd started packing: the knowledge that there were people up there now, real people with radios going silent, missing check-ins, and that she was possibly the only person alive who could reach them before the early snows sealed the pass. Six weeks early, everyone said, this year. The mountain didn't care about schedules. The mountain never had.

If she refused and those people died—not of malice, just of weather and rot and ignorance, the ordinary killers—then their deaths would join the eleven. Not her fault, exactly. Not her doing. But hers to have prevented, and unprevented. She knew how that arithmetic worked. She'd been doing it for eleven years, and it never came out clean.

She thought about the money. Double her debt, cash, freedom. She thought about what freedom had actually felt like these three years—the flinch at every stranger's accent, the false name worn smooth as a river stone, the way she'd stopped signing anything, even the sisters' receipt book, so that no handwriting of hers existed anywhere official. That wasn't freedom. That was a smaller cell with better light.

She thought about Tomás Reyes-Ortega, whom she'd never met, missing his check-ins. Twenty-two years in the field. Men like that didn't miss check-ins. Something had stopped him, and whatever it was, it was sitting up there in her corridor, in her mountains, among her ghosts.

The rain thickened. Down at the dock, Marchetti's white launch rode at its lines, and Casper could see lamplight in the wheelhouse, the radio room lit, someone keeping watch on channel nine out of habit or hope. Listening for voices that weren't coming.

She picked up the photograph, rolled it tight, and put it in her pocket, which surprised her—her own hands deciding things before she did, the way they sometimes had on the worst day of her life, moving without permission.

Not an answer. Just evidence. She'd decide tomorrow.

Except that tomorrow, she suspected, had already been decided for her—because when she reached into the pocket of her other jacket for tobacco, her fingers found the slip of paper Ferro had left behind on Tuesday, the auction notice she'd skimmed and discarded. She smoothed it out under the lamp and read it all the way through this time, past the lot numbers, past the boilerplate, to the buyer's line.

The debt hadn't been bought by Marchetti Consolidated at all. It had been bought by a private holding company, registered in the capital, incorporated eleven months ago—one month after the extension project was announced, one month before the expedition went upriver.

Someone had prepared for this. Someone had bought the key to her door before the door was even locked, and had waited, patient as the river, for the day the radios went quiet.

Casper stood a long time in the lamplight, the rain loud on the roof, the photograph rolled in her pocket like a bone.

Then she took down the canvas tool roll, and began, this time, to pack for the mountains.
