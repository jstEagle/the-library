## 3. What the Bridge Took

The collector came back on a Tuesday, which Casper would remember later as the last ordinary day of her life in Puerto Vado.

She was mending a net at Ondine's dockside shack when his shadow fell across the doorway—long, patient, smelling of diesel and cheap cologne. He had a name she refused to use: Ferrán. He had a ledger of his own, a slim black book he kept in an inside pocket like a priest keeping scripture, and every time he opened it Casper felt the pages breathing against her skin.

"Señora Vasquez," he said, using the name she'd bought for forty dollars and a forged baptismal record from a notary in Santa Rosa. "You're a difficult woman to find."

"You found me fine," she said, and kept mending. The net was full of holes. So was everything else.

He sat down uninvited on an upturned crate, crossing his ankles, letting the silence do its work. Down at the water a heron stood in the shallows with the stillness of something that had learned patience from the river itself. Casper envied it. The river took what it wanted and apologized to no one.

"I have good news," Ferrán said. "And I have news."

"Those are the same thing."

"Not today." He smiled. He had teeth like piano keys, all present, none of them honest. "The news is that a woman arrived on yesterday's steamer. Northern woman. Money like weather—you don't see where it comes from, you just feel it coming. Ilsa Marchetti. You know the name?"

Casper's hands did not stop moving. She had spent four years teaching them not to stop moving when a name landed like a hook in her chest. Marchetti. Marchetti Rail & Mineral. The extension. The pass. The closed line she had helped survey before any of it went wrong, back when the maps were clean and so was she.

"No," she said.

"Mm." Ferrán opened the black book anyway, just a crack, like a man showing a knife's edge without drawing it. "She's asking after engineers. Railroad people. Someone who knows the mountain corridor—the old grade, the tunnels, the switchbacks above the treeline. There aren't many such people, señora. There are perhaps three in this whole country who could take a party up that line before the snows. One is dead. One is in prison." He looked up. His eyes were the color of river ice, pretty and thin. "And one is mending nets under a dead woman's name."

The netting needle bit her thumb. She let it. Pain was an old acquaintance; it knew how to keep quiet.

"I don't know anything about railroads," Casper said. "I'm a fisherman's widow."

"A fisherman's widow who reads bridge load tables for pleasure." He tapped the crate beside him. "I've watched you do it. At the cantina, with the shipping manifests. You corrected the tonnage on the *Marisol* out loud, twice, because the numbers offended you. Widows of fishermen don't do arithmetic like that."

Outside, the light was going amber over the water. Somewhere upriver, invisible beyond the first bend, the mountains stood with their shoulders already whitening. Every year they took a little more of the world into themselves and gave nothing back until spring, if they gave it back at all.

"What does she want?" Casper asked, and hated herself for asking, because asking was the first step across a bridge you couldn't walk back over.

"She wants her people back. An expedition went up the corridor three weeks ago. Survey team, eleven souls, radios. They've missed three check-ins." Ferrán closed the book. "She's offering money. Real money. Enough to clear what you owe me and everyone whose names are written behind yours in my little book, and enough left over to buy a boat, a house, a life with your own name stitched back onto it."

"And the bad news?"

Ferrán stood, brushed off his trousers, and delivered it the way men like him delivered everything: gently, so you understood he could be gentle, which meant he could also be the other thing.

"The bad news is that I've already told her who you are, señora Sandoval. I sold her the name this morning. And the story that goes with it." He paused at the door, silhouetted against the brightening water. "Viaduct Seven. Eleven men. The engineer who walked away while the families buried theirs. She found it very interesting." A beat. "She'll come to you tomorrow. I'd think about what you're willing to sell, and what you're not. In my experience those are the only two categories there are."

Then he was gone, and the river went on doing what rivers do, which is carry things away whether or not you're ready to let them go.

* * *

That night Casper did not sleep. She sat in her room above the chandlery with the lamp turned low and let the past come up the stairs the way it always did when she was too tired to bar the door.

Viaduct 7.

It had a number instead of a name because the railroad named its structures the way it named its workers: by function, by position, by how much weight they could bear before they failed. It spanned the Quebrada Hondo—a gorge so deep the river at its bottom was less a sound than a rumor—and it was to be the crown of the new extension, the crossing that would shave six hours off the freight run to the mines. Two hundred and ten meters of steel and concrete hung between walls of gray stone. Casper had drawn it herself, or most of it. She had been thirty-one years old and the youngest chief engineer on the division, and she had believed, with the whole burning faith of her blood, that a thing built correctly would stand.

The piers were the problem. Piers Three and Four were founded in scree—broken rock, loose as gravel in a giant's fist—where the survey notes said bedrock. Casper had gone down into the gorge herself in the third month of construction, roped against the drop, and hammered test cores until her shoulders screamed. The cores came back wrong. Not catastrophically wrong; that was the devil of it. Wrong in the way a lie is wrong when it's mostly true. The bedrock was there, but fractured, seamed with clay, drinking water every wet season like a drunk drinks gin. On paper the footings might hold. On paper.

She wrote the memo in October. *Recommend relocation of Pier 3 and Pier 4 to confirmed strata, or full redesign of foundation system. Current siting presents unacceptable risk under saturated conditions.* Unacceptable risk. She had chosen the phrase carefully, believing words were load-bearing, believing the company would no sooner ignore an engineer's warning than a pilot would ignore a storm front.

The reply came back from the division office in November, signed by a man whose name she would not say aloud for years afterward: cost projections, schedule commitments, contractual penalties to the mining consortium. *Proceed as designed. Reinforcement measures noted and approved.* Reinforcement measures: two additional tie beams, a drainage curtain, a coat of engineering optimism poured over foundations that wanted to move. Her memo went into a file. Her objection went into a drawer. And the work went on, because the work always goes on; that is the first lesson of large things, and the last.

She should have walked off the job then. People told her so later, kindly and unkindly, and both kinds were right. But walking off meant another engineer would sign her name to the continuation, and she told herself she could protect the structure better from inside than out. She stayed. She inspected. She argued in meetings where the air conditioning hummed and men with cufflinks explained arithmetic to her as though she were a clever child. She documented everything, in triplicate, in a green folder she kept locked in her desk, because some part of her—the part that survives disasters by seeing them early—already knew what the folder was for.

The viaduct stood through two winters. Trains crossed it eleven hundred times, maybe twelve hundred. She rode it herself once, white-knuckled in the cab of a freight drag, listening to the steel sing, and when they reached the far side she laughed out loud and nearly wept, because it held, it held, and she let herself believe that being mostly right about rock was the same as being right.

Then came the monsoon. Not the gentle kind—the kind the old rivermen called a *colera*, a cholera of rain, nine days of water falling out of a sky that had forgotten how to stop. The drainage curtain worked exactly as designed. The problem was that the design assumed the water would leave. Instead it went down, into the seams, into the clay, swelling the fracture zones beneath Pier 4 like bread rising in an oven no one was watching.

At 4:17 in the morning on the ninth day, with a maintenance train crossing—eleven men aboard, fitters and trackwalkers and a boy of nineteen named Aurelio who had been saving to marry a girl in the valley—Pier 4 settled fourteen centimeters in under a second. The span twisted. The deck folded like a knee giving way. And two hundred meters of viaduct, eleven men, three locomotive tons of iron, and every certainty Casper Sandoval had ever built her life upon went down into the Quebrada Hondo together.

They never recovered all of them. The gorge kept what it caught.

The inquiry took five months. She testified for three days. She produced the green folder—every memo, every core sample, every signature—and watched the company's lawyers dismantle it the way water dismantles rock: patiently, along the seams. Her warnings had been *noted*. Her recommendations had been *considered*. The decision to proceed had been made at a level above her authority, yes—but had she, the inquiry asked, done everything within her authority? Had she refused to certify? Had she walked away? Had she, in short, been willing to lose everything, the way they now implied she must have known she ought to?

No. She had stayed, and inspected, and hoped. Hope, the final report observed, was not an engineering control.

Her certification was revoked. Her name went onto the failure like a maker's mark. The men whose signatures actually moved the project retired with pensions and tributes; one of them sent flowers to the hearing, orchids, as if condolence were a currency that could be exchanged. And Casper Sandoval, thirty-three years old, stood outside the inquiry hall with the green folder under her arm and understood that the verdict was not a mistake. It was a design. She had been built to bear the load, the way the piers had been built, and she had failed the same way they had—not because she was false, but because she had been set in ground that was never sound.

So she ran. That was the part she could never forgive, more than the collapse, more than the verdict. Eleven funerals, and she had attended none of them. She had taken the ferry south with a duffel bag and a false name bought from a notary, and she had let the river towns swallow her, and for four years she had mended nets and read tonnage tables and flinched at the word *engineer*, telling herself that disappearing was penance. It wasn't. It was just running with better posture.

Aurelio. She said his name to the dark room, the way she did on nights like this, all of them: Aurelio Vásquez, Tomás Iriarte, Benicio Roldán, Mateo Chumbe, the brothers Ezequiel and Rafael Duarte, Old Sabino who had laid track on three railroads and outlived two of them, Iván Petrov who whistled opera while he worked, Hernán Loayza, Cipriano Mena, and the boy—always last, always longest—Aurelio, who had been on that train because Casper had signed the schedule that put the maintenance run at dawn.

Eleven names. She carried them the way other people carried rosaries.

* * *

Ilsa Marchetti came at ten the next morning, which told Casper she was not a woman who waited for afternoon.

She filled the doorway of the chandlery stair the way weather fills a valley—not large, precisely, but total. Gray traveling suit, silver hair cut short as a verdict, eyes that performed warmth the way a stage performs firelight. Behind her, at a respectful distance, stood a man Casper took at once for security, broad and forgettable as furniture.

"Señora Vasquez," Marchetti said, and then, without a flicker, "or shall we skip to the part where we're honest with each other? Señora Sandoval."

Casper set down the coil of rope she'd been pretending to inspect. "Ferrán talks fast."

"Ferrán talks expensively. There's a difference." Marchetti stepped inside, declined the offered chair with a small motion, and stood looking around the room—at the charts on the wall, the tide tables, the dog-eared shipping manifest—with the expression of a woman appraising property. "Eleven people went up that corridor three weeks ago. My people. My nephew among them. They've missed three scheduled transmissions. The last one was garbled—four seconds of static and half a word." She turned. "The authorities say wait for the snows to melt. The snows are coming *early* this year, señora. Waiting means waiting until June. I don't intend to wait until June."

"So hire guides. There are boatmen who know the river."

"Boatmen know the river. Nobody alive knows the *line*." Marchetti's voice dropped half a register, and the performance thinned enough for Casper to see the machinery underneath: fear, real fear, wearing a tailored coat. "You surveyed that corridor. You walked every kilometer of the grade before it closed. Tunnels, trestles, washouts, the switchbacks above the treeline—it's all in your head, and heads can't be subpoenaed or bribed or lost. I need someone who can take a party up that route in eight days. Before the pass shuts."

"And you came to me because I'm the best." Casper leaned against the table, arms folded. "Or because I'm the cheapest. Disgraced engineers work for whatever they're offered. Isn't that what Ferrán told you?"

"He told me you owed money in four towns." Marchetti didn't blink. "He told me about Viaduct Seven. Eleven men. And that you've spent four years hiding in river towns under other women's names." She let that sit, then added, softly, with terrible precision: "He told me you'd want this debt settled. All of it. Today."

"There it is," Casper said quietly.

"There it is," Marchetti agreed, without apology. "I'm not a sentimental woman, señora. I buy what I need. Right now I need you, and what you need is to stop being hunted by a man with a ledger. I'm prepared to pay his price and yours. Double, if it moves you. In exchange you guide my party up the corridor and bring my people back." Something moved behind her eyes then, quick as a fish under ice—there and gone, but Casper had spent four years learning to read water, and she saw it. "Bring them back," Marchetti repeated. "That's all."

*All.* The word landed strangely, the way a single wrong note lands in a chord you've heard your whole life. Casper couldn't have said why. Later she would understand that it was the emphasis—the *all*, as if the party were cargo, as if rescue were retrieval, as if what mattered was not eleven living people but eleven accounted-for items. But standing in the chandlery room with the river light on the wall, she filed the note away unread.

"And if I refuse?" Casper asked.

Marchetti smiled, and it was almost kind, which was the cruelest thing about it. "Then I imagine Ferrán sells the rest of your story. To newspapers, this time. *Disgraced engineer found alive in fishing village*—they do love that shape of headline. The families of Viaduct Seven would finally learn where their scapegoat has been sleeping." She straightened her gloves. "I'm not threatening you, señora. I'm describing a market. You may choose not to participate. Markets don't require consent."

Casper looked past her, through the window, upriver. The mountains had their morning cloud on like a scarf, and somewhere beyond the visible bend, up past the logging camps and the junction and the drowned country of the closed line, eleven more people were missing check-ins, and the snows were coming early, and the only person who knew the way was a woman who had sworn on eleven graves never to build or climb or claim that country again.

Grief had followed her for four years like a debt collector of its own. Now here were men who had learned to hold its paper.

"When do we leave?" she said.

"Dawn," said Ilsa Marchetti. "There's a launch at the north dock. My nephew Dario will meet you—he's enthusiastic." A pause, the ghost of a smile. "Try to be patient with him. Family is the one asset I've never managed to audit."

She left. The security man left. The room settled around Casper like water closing over a dropped stone.

She stood a long time at the window, watching the river run north toward the mountains, carrying everything it carried—silt, leaves, the brown wash of the high country, and now, riding its current in a launch with an enthusiastic stranger, whatever it was that Ilsa Marchetti actually wanted brought back.

Eleven names in her chest. Eleven missing on the mountain. And dawn coming, the way dawn always came, indifferent and absolute.

Casper packed her father's compass, her old survey notebook wrapped in oilcloth, and the green folder she had promised herself she'd burned years ago and never had.

Some bridges, she thought, follow you across the water.
