## 4. A Crew of Strangers

Marchetti's money arrived before Marchetti did, which told Casper everything about the woman she was dealing with. A bank draft, folded into an envelope with no return address, slid under the door of her room above the chandlery sometime in the small hours—she heard the whisper of paper against the floorboards and lay awake in the dark listening to the river, and in the morning the envelope was there, and she understood that she had been found not once but thoroughly, the way a surveyor finds a benchmark: fixed, recorded, permanent.

She burned the draft in the chandlery stove and watched the ink curl and go green, and felt nothing like relief. The collector had sold her name. Marchetti had bought it. The transaction was complete, and the only thing left to negotiate was what Casper would do with the days she had left as nobody.

She lasted four days. On the fifth, Marchetti came to the chandlery herself.

Casper was re-caulking a punt when the woman appeared at the top of the slipway, dressed in river-gray wool that had never seen a river, gloves folded over one forearm like a surgeon between operations. Behind her the morning mist was burning off the water in long rags, and the fishing fleet was coming in with the night's catch silvering in their holds, and none of it—not the boats, not the gulls screaming over the fish scales, not the boys hauling ice—none of it existed for Ilsa Marchetti except as an obstacle between her and the person she had come to collect.

"You burned my draft," she said. No greeting. She came down the slipway in low-heeled boots that found the wet planks without slipping, which Casper noted despite herself. The woman was careful. Careful people were dangerous in a specific way.

"Paper burns," Casper said. She kept working. The caulking iron had a rhythm to it and she needed the rhythm. "So do bridges, sometimes."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know why you're here." Casper straightened, wiped her hands on her thighs. Up close Marchetti was older than her voice suggested, sixty maybe, with the kind of face that had been handsome and had decided, somewhere along the way, that handsome was a luxury it could no longer afford. Her eyes were brown and very still, like water over a deep hole. "You want me to take people up a river that killed a better crew than yours, over a pass that's already thinking about winter, to find out what happened to an expedition that should never have been there in the first place. And you want me to do it because you own my name."

"I don't own your name, Ms. Sandoval. I own what happens to it if you say no." Marchetti let that sit. Down at the water a boy laughed, high and careless, and the sound seemed to offend her. "Eleven men died at Viaduct Seven. The inquiry blamed you. The industry blacklisted you. You've spent six years being no one in towns that don't ask questions. I can give you all of it back—the license, the record, the name—or I can send your name to every rail office from here to the coast, and you can spend the rest of your life one town ahead of the telegraph. Those are the choices. I didn't make them. The world did."

Casper looked at her for a long moment. The caulking iron was warm in her hand. Six years of careful, small, invisible living, and this woman had walked down a slipway and picked it up like a coin off the street.

"When do we leave?" Casper said.

* * *

Marchetti had chartered the *Marta Luz*, a river launch that had once hauled mail and now hauled whatever paid, and she had assembled her rescue party the way a man assembles a hand of cards he hasn't looked at yet—fast, and hoping for the best.

Casper met them at the dock at four in the morning, in the blue-black hour when the lamps along the quay were the only honest light. She came early on purpose. She wanted to see them before they saw her, wanted to read them the way she'd once read a stretch of track from the cab window—looking for the soft spots, the washouts, the places where the ground under a thing had quietly gone bad.

Teo was first, because Teo was already there, had probably always been there, a broad, gray-bearded man mending a net on the *Marta Luz*'s deck with the economy of someone who had done the same ten thousand times. He looked up when Casper's boots hit the gangplank, looked at her for exactly as long as it took to decide something, and went back to his net.

"You the guide," he said. Not a question.

"Casper Reyes."

"Teo." He didn't offer a hand. His were occupied and, she suspected, always would be; a man like that kept his hands busy so he wouldn't have to shake anyone's. "Marchetti says the river's high for the season."

"Marchetti's never seen the river."

That earned her a grunt that might have been approval. He tied off a knot, tested it with a savage tug, and said, "Boat's sound. Engine's sound. I'm sound. Everything past the third bend is your problem."

"Fair division," Casper said, and went to load the gear.

Ruth Okafor arrived at half past four, on foot, carrying two canvas medical bags that weighed what a man weighs, and Casper knew her at once for what she was—the kind of quiet competence that doesn't need to announce itself because it has never once been doubted. She was small, forty-ish, dark-skinned, with reading glasses pushed up into close-cropped hair and hands that moved with a nurse's certainty even when they were only setting a bag down.

"You're the engineer," she said. It was also not a question, and Casper felt the old flinch, the reflex of a woman whose name had been a headline.

"I'm the guide."

"Mm." Ruth looked at her the way she probably looked at a wound—assessing, unhurried, seeing more than she said. "I've read up on the closed line. The trestles, the tunnels. The report said the grade was unsafe for traffic."

"The report said a lot of things."

"Yes," Ruth said, and let it go, and began stowing her bags with a precision that made Casper feel better about the whole enterprise than she had any right to feel.

Dario Marchetti arrived last, at five, which told Casper everything she needed to know about him before he opened his mouth. He was twenty-four or twenty-five, tall, handsome in a way that hadn't yet been tested by weather, wearing brand-new boots and a brand-new canvas jacket and a brand-new enthusiasm that he wore even more conspicuously than either. He came down the gangplank two steps at a time with a rucksack that was packed wrong—heavy things high, light things low, a rucksack packed by a man who had read about mountains—and stuck out his hand to Casper with a smile that belonged on a poster.

"Dario Marchetti. You must be the famous—"

"Don't," Casper said, and did not take the hand.

His smile flickered, held, recovered. "Right. Discretion. Aunt Ilsa mentioned." He looked around at the boat, the river, the gray light coming up behind the eastern hills, and his face did what young faces do when they finally touch the thing they've been imagining—it went bright and slightly stupid with joy. "God, it's real. Three weeks I've been asking her to let me come. She kept saying the pass was too rough, and then suddenly it's too rough for a professional crew but fine for me, which—" he laughed, catching himself, "—which I'm choosing to interpret as a compliment."

"Interpret it as arithmetic," Casper said. "You're extra weight."

"Casper." Ruth, from the deck, mild as milk.

"I'm being honest. It saves time later."

But Dario only grinned wider, and hauled his badly packed rucksack aboard, and Casper watched him do it and felt the first cold thread of the thing she would spend the next two weeks learning to name: this boy was not here for his aunt's expedition. He was here because he had begged, and because his aunt had, for reasons of her own, decided to let him—and Casper had been around railroads and the men who built them long enough to know that nobody got taken up a closed line by accident.

* * *

They left at dawn, as the light came down the valley in a single long blade, turning the river from pewter to hammered gold.

Casper stood at the bow with the chart she'd drawn from memory—the real charts were locked in a company office she would never be allowed into again—and watched the town shrink behind them. Puerto Vado's roofs went from red to rust to a smudge of rust, then to nothing, and the river took over, and the walls of the valley rose on either side, older than any name anyone had given them.

Behind her, the boat sorted itself into its nature. Teo ran the engine and read the water with a stillness that was itself a kind of speech—a tilt of the head at a riffle, a grunt at a submerged snag, his hands on the wheel making small corrections like a man talking in his sleep. Ruth sat amidships with a medical text she was not reading, watching the banks, cataloguing. Dario was everywhere—forward to look at the gorge, aft to ask Teo about the engine, midships to ask Ruth about her bags, and finally at the bow, beside Casper, breathless with the altitude of his own excitement.

"Have you done this run before?" he asked. "The whole line, I mean. Over the pass."

"Once," Casper said. "The year they closed it."

"What was it like?"

She was quiet long enough that he shifted his weight, and she watched the water instead of him. What was it like. It was like the last morning before the world ended—eleven men alive at breakfast, the viaduct's piers standing in the gorge below, her own signature on the inspection report that said the third pier had a shear crack and needed shoring before the spring floods, and the district office's reply, which had said, in the language of men who had never stood under a bridge in their lives: *proceed as scheduled.*

"Like a place that hadn't decided what it was yet," she said finally. "Now it has."

Dario laughed, delighted, as though she'd said something charming. "You talk like the books my aunt hates. She says engineers romanticize ruin because they can't romanticize anything else."

"Your aunt's right about most things. It's the things she's wrong about that worry me."

He didn't have an answer for that, and for a while he was quiet, and Casper almost—almost—revised her estimate of him. Then he said, "You know what I keep thinking? Those survey people up there. They're probably fine. Probably holed up somewhere laughing at us, eating tinned peaches." He grinned out at the water. "Aunt Ilsa pays for three missed check-ins like it's a war. She's dramatic like that."

"Is she," Casper said.

"She loves that expedition. Funded it herself. She says the extension will open the whole interior—rail to the high valleys, towns where there's nothing now. It's the biggest thing anyone's built out here in fifty years, and everyone said it couldn't be done, and she's doing it." His voice had gone earnest, the earnestness of a young man repeating a creed he'd been raised on. "You have to admire her."

Casper watched the river slide by, the water going from gold to green as the valley walls closed in, and thought about a woman who funded expeditions and missed check-ins and knew, before anyone else, exactly where the survey party had last reported from. Thought about a name sold by a debt collector and bought by a financier, and a guide whose price was her own past.

"Admiration's cheap," she said. "I'll save mine for the ones who come back down."

Dario looked at her sideways, and for one moment the poster-smile was gone and something quicker and less certain moved behind his face—something that had, perhaps, also wondered why his aunt had finally said yes. Then it was gone, and he was twenty-five again, and the gorge swallowed the light, and the *Marta Luz* drove on upriver into the country Casper had sworn she would never see twice.

By noon the town was a rumor. By evening it was a story they had all agreed to stop telling.

They made camp on a gravel bar below the first abandoned logging camp, and Casper stood the first watch, and as she sat by the fire with her chart on her knees she heard, very faintly, from the direction of Dario's bedroll, a small mechanical click. Like a latch. Like a case closing.

She did not look up. She marked their position on the chart, in pencil, in a hand no one would ever mistake for hers, and fed the fire, and watched the dark come down the river toward them like a train on a line with no end of track.
