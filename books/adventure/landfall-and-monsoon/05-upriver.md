## 5. Upriver

The river changed its mind a hundred times between Puerto Vado and the gorge, and Teo respected it for that.

He had run this water since he was nine years old, standing on his father's launch with a pole too long for him, learning the grammar of current and stone. A river never lied, his father used to say. It might hide things—snags under brown water, rocks wearing a skin of foam—but it did not lie. Men lied. Rivers only concealed.

By the second morning the Concepción had narrowed from a broad brown shoulder of water to something leaner and quicker, the banks drawing in like a sentence tightening toward its point. The jungle leaned closer too, ceiba roots gripping the cutbanks, ferns hanging out over the current so their tips trailed in the water like fingers testing temperature. The air was thick enough to chew. Sweat ran down Teo's spine in a steady line, and the engine's heat rose through the deck boards into his bare feet until he steered half-barefoot, half-dancing, shifting his weight off the hot planks and back again.

Behind him in the launch's shallow hold sat four people who were not cargo but who he could not quite call passengers either. The woman Casper stood at the bow rail most hours, saying nothing, watching the water ahead with an expression he recognized because he wore it himself sometimes: the look of someone reading a page she'd read before and hadn't liked the ending. The medic, Ruth Okafor, kept to the middle benches with Emilio—the laborer they'd pulled from the burned camp two days back, still gray-faced, still sleeping more than waking—and her hands were always moving, checking a bandage, measuring out water, doing small useful things with a steadiness Teo approved of. Steady hands told you more about a person than anything they said.

And then there was Dario Marchetti.

Dario sat near the stern, which was Teo's first note against him. Passengers who trusted the boat sat forward where they could see what was coming. Passengers with their own business sat aft, near the gear, near the corners, where a man could turn his shoulders away from the rest of them and do something small with his hands.

Teo had been watching those hands for two days.

* * *

They passed the first dead camp just after noon, when the sun stood straight overhead and the river ran silver where it wasn't green.

Teo saw it before anyone spoke of it—a geometry in the trees that didn't belong. Straight lines. Then the stumps, dozens of them, pale as bone where the bark had weathered off, arranged in rows that followed no logger's sense of efficiency but every foreman's sense of order. Beyond the stumps: the camp itself, or what remained. A long low building with its roof caved in, moss eating the walls in green tongues. A rusted donkey engine sitting on skids, its cable drum furred with orange corrosion. The loading ramp down to the water, rotted through in three places.

"Logging camp," Ruth said, unnecessarily.

"Stripped years ago," Teo said. He throttled down out of habit, though there was nothing in the channel to dodge. "See the donkey engine? Nobody leaves iron behind unless the company already wrote it off. They pulled out everything worth pulling and let the forest have the rest." He squinted at the hillside above the clearing, where the cut ended in a hard line, old growth standing dark and unbroken beyond it. "Fifteen years, maybe twenty. Before your time," he added to Dario, and was mildly pleased to see the young man flinch as if the words had edges.

Casper had come aft without him noticing. She stood at the rail studying the camp with that same flat attention, and then her eyes moved upriver, following the old tram grade where it climbed away from the water and vanished into the trees.

"There'll be more," she said. It wasn't a question.

"There'll be more," Teo agreed. "This whole stretch was cut over once. Company towns every ten kilometers, all the way to the divide. When the timber played out they pulled the plug and let the river take the roads." He eased the throttle back up. "Nobody comes up here now except men running from something."

He said it lightly, to see what it caught.

Casper almost smiled. Almost. "Then we should feel at home," she said, and went back to the bow.

Dario said nothing at all. But Teo caught him, just for a second, looking at the ruined camp the way a man looks at a bill he'd hoped was someone else's.

* * *

The second camp came at dusk, and the third the next morning, and by then Teo had stopped thinking of the journey as a rescue.

It happened gradually, the way a current shifts—you couldn't name the moment the water changed, you could only feel it pulling differently against the hull. At Puerto Vado, when Marchetti had laid out her money and her worry, the story had been simple: her survey expedition, eleven souls, silent for three radio check-ins, somewhere past the pass. Find them. Bring them home. Teo had taken the job for the pay and because a man who lived alone on a boat could not afford to be the kind of man who refused when people were missing. That was the arithmetic he'd done, and it had seemed clean.

But arithmetic done ashore came apart on the water.

It came apart first in small ways. The way Dario volunteered for every watch, including the ones nobody wanted—the gray hour before dawn, the dead-still noon—so that he was often awake and apart while the others slept. The way he asked questions that sounded innocent and weren't. How fast does the launch run light? How far past the junction is the closed-line trailhead? How many days of fuel do we have spare? Each question reasonable. Each one a plank in a bridge leading somewhere Teo couldn't see yet.

It came apart more in one large way, on the second night.

They had tied up below a bend where the bank rose steep and rooty, and Teo had gone ashore to check the mooring lines in the dark, because he trusted no knot he hadn't personally cursed over. Coming back along the bank he heard voices—or a voice, low and clipped, coming from the direction of the launch. He stopped. Stood among the ceiba roots with the river talking around him and listened.

Dario was aft, in the shadow of the fuel drums. His back was to the bank. And in his cupped hands, faintly lit by the binnacle lamp's spill, Teo saw a small dark shape with a stub of antenna.

A radio. Not the expedition's set, which rode in its wooden case amidships where everyone could see it. A second one. Small, personal, held close to the mouth.

Teo couldn't make out the words. The river ate them. But he knew the rhythm of a transmission when he heard one—short bursts, pauses, short bursts again, a man feeding information in sips. Coordinates, probably. Or condition reports. *Still on the water. Making good time. No suspicions.*

Or maybe: *They found the camps. What do I tell her?*

Teo stood in the dark a long moment, mosquitoes finding the sweat at his collar, and felt something settle in his chest that was colder than the river air. He thought about turning back, walking aboard, demanding to see what was in the boy's hands. He thought about how a boat was a small country and a captain was its law, and how a mutiny on the second night of five would leave them all stranded.

He thought about the burned camp they'd left behind that morning, and the man they'd carried out of it.

Instead he stepped on a branch. Deliberately. Let it crack loud as a rifle shot in the still air, and walked back aboard whistling, loud and tuneless, giving whoever needed the warning time to close the little radio away. By the time he reached the stern Dario was sitting with his elbows on his knees, looking at the water, hands empty, face arranged into pleasant blankness.

"Good night for it," Teo said cheerfully. "River's rising. Half a meter by morning, if the storm holds off."

"Storm?" Dario said.

"Always a storm." Teo clapped him on the shoulder, feeling the young man go stiff under his palm, and turned in for his bunk smiling at nothing.

* * *

The next day the river taught him other things, and he was grateful for the distraction, because the thing in his chest was heavy and he needed work.

The channel narrowed past all reason. Walls of stone rose on either side, black and dripping, hung with vines that poured down like green water frozen mid-pour. The current quickened until the launch was running at nearly the river's own speed, the banks sliding past, and Teo worked the wheel in small constant corrections, reading the surface the way a literate man reads a page. A smooth tongue of water meant depth. A line of bubbles meant a ledge underneath. Where the current bent around a point and the foam curled backward against the flow, there was rock just under the skin of the world, waiting.

"Watch the V," he said aloud, because Casper had drifted aft again and because talking helped him think. "See how the ripples make a arrow pointing upstream? Water parts around a rock and tells on it. Always steer for the downstream point of the V, never the upstream. The upstream end will put you on the stone."

"You've run this stretch before," Casper said. Not a question either. She had a way of making statements that wanted confirming.

"Twice. Once hauling machinery up for a mining outfit that went bust before they ever dug anything. Once running medicine during the fever year, when the road washed out." He fed the wheel a quarter-turn as the channel kinked left. "Both times I swore I was done with it. River doesn't hold grudges, but it keeps records."

"And the closed line?" Casper asked. "The grade above the gorge. You'd know it?"

"I know where it crosses the river twice. Old trestles. Whether they'd hold a cat, I couldn't say." He glanced at her. Her face was turned toward the canyon wall, where—if you knew to look—you could pick out the shelf of the old right-of-way cut into the rock forty feet above the water, timbers rotted to lace, a tunnel mouth appearing and disappearing behind hanging vegetation like a word repeated in another language. She was looking at it the way a man looks at a scar on his own arm. Like it was hers.

"You were railroad," Teo said. It wasn't a question either. Two could play.

For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. The launch thudded through a riffle, spray coming over the bow, and Ruth shifted to shield Emilio from it without being asked.

"I was some things," Casper said finally. "Most of them are over."

Teo nodded slowly and let it lie. He liked her, he decided. She lied the way a river concealed things—not lying, exactly, just declining to show you the bottom. There was honesty in the shape of the evasion. Whereas the boy—

Whereas the boy lied the way men lie. Smoothly, and to your face, and with his hands empty.

* * *

Midday brought them to the junction, though they didn't know it yet, and Teo called a halt at a wide gravel bar where the water spread thin and friendly and a man could stand waist-deep and wash a week of river off himself.

While Ruth saw to Emilio—getting broth into him, checking the dressing on the gash along his ribs—Teo waded out to cool the engine's rawhide-wrapped bearing, which had been complaining since dawn. Kneeling in the shallows with his sleeves rolled, he heard footsteps on the gravel behind him and expected Casper. It was Dario.

"Anything I can do?"

"You can hand me that grease tin." Teo worked in silence a moment, packing the bearing, then said, conversationally, to the engine rather than the boy: "Your aunt. She pays well?"

Dario's hands paused on the tin. "She pays what the work is worth."

"Mm. And what's this work worth? Finding eleven people alive—that's worth a lot, I'd think. Worth double, maybe." Teo sat back on his heels and looked up at the young man with the sun full in his own eyes, so his expression was unreadable and Dario's was not. "Worth triple, if it turns out they're not alive, and all that's left to bring down is papers."

Something moved behind Dario's eyes—quick, swallowed, gone. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Teo agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't." He took the grease tin, stood, waded back to the launch. "Fuel's next. We'll make the head of the gorge by dark if the weather holds."

He felt the boy's stare on his back the whole way, and he let him look. Let him wonder how much the boatman had seen, and whether the crack of that branch two nights ago had been accident or message. Fear was a tool like any other. Teo preferred honest tools. But he'd learned, in fifty years on the water, that sometimes the only tool within reach was the one that made a man careful.

* * *

They cast off again in the late afternoon, and the gorge closed over them like a book.

Teo steered and watched and counted. Counted the dead camps—one more on the north bank, its tram grade climbing toward the divide, its donkey engine a hump of rust in the failing light. Counted the rapids ahead, three of them, each one a negotiation. Counted the hours of fuel, the tins of food, the days of margin before the early snows came down off the range and shut the high country like a door.

And counted, without meaning to, the small betrayals. The way Dario checked his pockets after every stop. The way he positioned himself downwind of conversations, close enough to hear, far enough to seem uninvolved. The way his questions had stopped entirely now—which was worse, Teo thought. A curious man was just curious. A man who had stopped asking questions had gotten the answers he needed, and was passing them along.

The rescue was still real. Eleven people were still missing up there in the cold, and whatever else was true, that was true. But somewhere in the last two days the journey had changed its nature, the way the river changed at the gorge mouth—from open water to confined water, from a thing you floated on to a thing that carried you whether you willed it or not. They were not only searching anymore. They were being watched. Every mile they made, every camp they found, every conclusion Casper drew from the rot and the rust—it was all going down that hidden antenna in sips, to a woman at the bottom of the river who paid triple for bad news.

Teo thought about the burned camp again. Supplies intact, radios smashed. Someone had wanted the expedition deaf, but not dead—at least not quickly. Someone had wanted them to starve slowly, up high, where the snow would tidy everything away. And someone below had known enough to know it was working.

His father's voice came back to him across thirty years: *A river never lies, m'ijo. It only hides. So when you can't trust the men in the boat, trust the water. The water will show you where the rocks are.*

Ahead, around the next bend, the water showed him something.

The current flattened. Spread. Went strangely quiet, the way it does above a drop or a barrier, and the evening light lay on it like oil. Teo eased the throttle down and stood higher on the wheel box, and Casper came forward to stand beside him without a word, both of them staring at the same thing.

There, where the gorge pinched to its narrowest, a fresh survey marker stood on the rock shelf above the waterline—a steel pin, bright, unpitted, wrapped at the base with new orange flagging that snapped in the breeze like a small flame. Newer than anything official. Newer than anything abandoned.

Beside him, very quietly, Casper said: "Kill the engine."

Teo reached down and killed the engine, and the silence that rushed in was enormous, and somewhere upstream in that silence—faint, rhythmic, unmistakable to a man who had spent two nights listening for it—came the sound of an axe, ringing off wood.
