## 6. The First Marker

The axe stopped.

That was the first thing Casper noticed—not the sound itself, which had been thin and metallic and carried strangely over water, but the silence after it. A working man does not stop mid-swing unless something has told him to stop. An engine dying in a gorge is not loud, exactly, but it is unmistakable to anyone who has lived by machinery, and whoever stood upstream with an axe in his hands had heard the launch coasting into stillness and understood what it meant.

Casper held up one hand without looking back. She could feel them behind her on the benches: Teo with his hands resting on the tiller as if the boat might yet be argued with; Ruth Okafor sitting very straight, her medical bag between her boots like something she'd decided to defend; Dario leaning forward the way young men lean toward anything that looks like adventure, all elbows and appetite. And Emilio, folded into the bow shadow, wrists still raw where the rope had bitten, watching the rock walls slide past with the expression of a man reading a language written in stone.

Nobody spoke. The river spoke for them, low and continuous over the shallows, and beneath the river's voice there was nothing else now. No axe. No engine. Only water, and wind finding its way down from the pass, smelling faintly of snow though the morning was warm enough that sweat ran under Casper's collar.

She scanned the left bank. The shelf where the marker stood was thirty feet above the waterline, a tongue of gray granite thrust out of the gorge wall, and the steel pin caught the light like a needle catching thread. She had been staring at it since Teo first called it out, and every second she stared, the wrongness of it deepened. She knew survey markers the way priests know scripture. She had set hundreds of them herself—brass caps stamped with the year, the line, the section, sunk in concrete or driven into bedrock and left to the weather's slow arbitration. A marker aged honestly. It pitted. It greened. Lichen took the north face first, always, because lichen is patient and honest about where the sun doesn't go.

This pin was bright. This pin had no stamp she could read from the water, but even at this distance she could see the flagging tape was factory-crisp, the orange of it so new it looked lit from inside. Somebody had stood on this shelf within the year—within the season, if she trusted the tape—and driven steel into rock along a corridor that had been officially dead for eleven years.

The closed line. Her line. She had helped close it. She had walked it end to end in the last month of its operation, cataloguing what would be left to rot: the trestles that would soften, the tunnels that would shed their portals, the culverts that would silt and spill their roads into the creeks. She had signed the report. *Corridor non-viable. Recommend abandonment.* She had signed it with a steady hand, and she remembered thinking, even then, that the word *abandonment* was a strange word for what they were doing—strange, because you abandon things you don't want, and half of her had wanted this line the way you want a thing you're not allowed to have.

Now someone had come back to it with fresh steel and fresh flagging, and had not told anyone. Not the railway board. Not the land office. Not any registry Casper had ever seen, and she had spent three years in Puerto Vado learning to read other people's paperwork because she couldn't bear to read her own.

"There," Ruth said softly, and pointed upstream.

Casper followed her finger. On the far bank, half-hidden behind a fallen spruce, stood a rough timber frame—a tripod of peeled poles lashed with wire, supporting a block and tackle over a cut in the earth. A test pit. Beside it, a wheelbarrow, and a mound of tailings dark against the moss, and a canvas lean-to whose guy lines were taut, whose fire ring held ash that was not old ash. The axe had come from somewhere near there. Whoever swung it had gone quiet, and quiet men in gorges were either hiding or watching.

"We should talk to him," Dario said. His voice had the bright, unweighted quality of a man who had never been shot at, never been hunted, never stood in a place where the geometry of the rocks decided who lived. "One worker, one pit. He can tell us what he's digging for."

"He can tell us nothing," Casper said, "if he runs, and he'll run the moment he sees four strangers and a boat. Or he won't run. He'll radio ahead." She turned and looked at Dario directly, and held the look until the brightness in his face developed its first hairline crack. "And then everyone he radios to will know exactly where we are, and how many we are, and that we found the pit."

Dario opened his mouth. Closed it. He had been doing a great deal of that since the junction, Casper had noticed—since the moment the launch rounded the bend and the old grade appeared above the waterline, cut into the gorge wall like a scar that hadn't finished deciding whether it was a scar or a road. Since that moment, Dario Marchetti had been looking at the closed line the way a man looks at a locked door in his own house that he'd never noticed before.

* * *

They put ashore two hundred meters downstream, in a gravel eddy Teo chose with the particular contempt of a man who considered rivers personal acquaintances with bad manners. He backed the launch in stern-first, killed the current's grip with the prop, and held them there while they stepped onto stones that shifted and knocked like teeth.

Casper went up the bank first. It was not a decision anyone made aloud; it was simply understood, the way it is understood in a country house when the floor creaks at night that the person who knows the house goes first. She climbed through alder scrub with the river loud below her and the grade visible above, a green shelf running level along the contour, and she felt the old knowledge wake in her body before it woke in her mind—the spacing of the ties, the cant of the curves, the way the cut slopes had been laid back at one-and-a-half to one because the man who surveyed them, a younger and stupider version of herself, had insisted on it against the contractor's grumbling.

Eleven years of rot had softened everything. Alder grew from the ballast. A culvert had let go somewhere above and spilled a fan of gravel across the grade, and the rails themselves had been pulled in the first year—she remembered the contract, the scrap price, the shame of it—but the roadbed remained, and roadbed, unlike pride, keeps.

She reached the marker.

Up close it was worse than it had been from the water. The pin was a modern stainless survey spike, the kind sold by supply houses in the capital, the kind that cost real money and required a reason to order. There was no brass cap. There was no stamp. There was only the spike, driven true and deep into granite—which takes a hammer of some weight and a man who knows how to swing it—and around its base, wound twice and knotted, the orange flagging. And on the flagging, in black marker, in a hand too controlled to be hurried:

**KM 214.7 — R**

No date. No company name. No initials beyond that single letter, and Casper stood very still in the alder shade and looked at that letter for a long time.

*R.*

Railway crews marked kilometers. Surveyors marked stations. But neither railway crews nor government surveyors used bare letters without a registry code, and no registry code existed for this corridor anymore, because registries do not carry codes for lines that are dead. Which meant the letter belonged to something that had never been registered at all. Something private. Something that had measured this gorge in secret, kilometer by kilometer, and labeled its measurements with a single initial like a signature on work it did not intend to show.

"You've gone pale," Ruth said beside her. Casper hadn't heard her climb up. The woman moved quietly for someone carrying twenty pounds of medical kit. "What does it mean?"

"It means somebody surveyed this line recently," Casper said. "Professionally. With good equipment. In secret." She crouched, touched the ground beside the spike, and came away with soil on her fingers—dark, turned soil, mixed with fragments of blue-gray shale. "And it means somebody dug here."

"How recent?"

Casper rubbed the soil between thumb and forefinger. It was dry at the surface, faintly damp beneath, and the shale fragments still had sharp edges. Rain had come through the gorge nine days ago—Teo had cursed it, said it had muddied the water for two days. This soil had not been rained on hard. "Days," she said. "A week. Maybe less."

Behind them, Emilio had come up the bank without being asked, and he stood apart, looking not at the marker but along the grade, upstream, into the green distance where the line bent away toward the mountain. There was a quality to his stillness that Casper had learned to recognize in the last two days—the stillness of a man listening to a place he has been made to suffer in. When he finally spoke, his voice was flat and careful, each word set down like a stone on a scale.

"This is where the trucks turn off."

Casper turned. "What trucks?"

"The supply trucks. From the valley depot. They come up the logging road as far as the washout—" he gestured upstream, where the grade crossed a steep ravine on a trestle she already knew was gone, "—and then everything goes overland. Crates. Fuel. Chain." He said the last word without emphasis, which was somehow worse than if he had shouted it. "I carried chain on my shoulder for six kilometers once. Forty men, one coil each. The foreman counted the coils at both ends."

Ruth had gone very still. Teo, arriving last with the rope coil over his shoulder, looked from Emilio to the marker and back, and his weathered face did something complicated and settled into grimness.

"And the expedition?" Casper asked. "Marchetti's survey team. Did they come through here?"

Emilio's eyes came to hers, and in them was the thing she had been dreading since the burned camp, the thing that had been assembling itself in her chest piece by piece like a bridge built in the dark: the understanding that the question she was asking had an answer, and the answer had been waiting for her the whole time, patient as lichen.

"They photographed the pit," he said. "The tall one with the beard—he knelt right here, where you're standing. He wrote in a book. Then they argued. Quietly, the way people argue when they're afraid of being overheard. Then they went upstream fast, faster than sensible men travel in this gorge." He paused. "Two days later, the camp burned."

* * *

They stood in a row along the old grade like mourners who had arrived late and weren't sure whose grave it was.

Casper made herself think like an engineer, because thinking like an engineer was the only discipline she had left that had never lied to her. Facts, then. A secret survey, flagged in fresh orange, keyed to a single letter. A test pit dug within days, into shale, on the alignment of a corridor officially abandoned for eleven years. Supply traffic moving overland from a valley depot, including chain—chain, in quantity, hauled by men whose shoulders bore the evidence. A professional expedition that had found all of this, documented it, argued about it, and then vanished, its radios smashed, its camp burned by hands that had taken the trouble to destroy only the means of calling for help.

None of those facts fit together into anything innocent. She tried the innocent configurations anyway—it was a habit of honesty, testing the load-bearing capacity of the kind explanation—and each one collapsed. Prospectors? Prospectors didn't survey with stainless spikes and kilometer chains; prospectors dug holes and told lies in bars. The railway itself? The railway announced its surveys; announcements brought land claims, and land claims brought money, and secrecy cost money, and corporations did not pay for secrecy without expecting to be repaid in something worth more than money. A government project? Governments stamped their pins. Everything official in her world was stamped, dated, countersigned, filed. This was none of those things. This was a signature with no name attached, which meant the name was the one thing being hidden.

And what did a secret survey of a closed mountain corridor hide?

She looked upstream, where the grade bent into shadow below the first big tunnel—Tunnel Nine, her Tunnel Nine, bored through three hundred meters of schist by men whose names she had never known and whose faces she had seen only in a photograph on a verdict she had never accepted. Beyond it, the line climbed through the pass. Beyond the pass, the new extension was being built—the extension in all the newspapers, the triumph of engineering, the ribbon of steel that would open the interior. Everyone said the extension began at the valley railhead and climbed the eastern approach.

But railroads need more than one approach. Railroads need grades, water, timber, ballast quarries, relief lines, escape gradients for trains that lose their brakes on the down side. And a corridor already cut through the hardest part of the range—already graded, already tunneled, already paid for by men long dead—was worth more than money to anyone building the new line. It was worth years.

It was worth, Casper thought, standing on her own dead line in the alder shade, exactly the kind of thing a man would keep secret until it was finished. And secrets of that size require labor, and labor of that kind requires either wages or chains, and chains leave marks on shoulders and graves in shallow ground, and graves and photographs do not mix.

Which was why the expedition had to disappear.

The thought arrived whole, cold, and complete, and she hated it, and she could not make it leave. Ilsa Marchetti had sat across from her in a rented office that smelled of coffee and rain and said *three missed check-ins* with a face full of worry, and Casper had read that face the way she read water, and the water had said grief. But water lies about depth. A river can look like a foot of clear gravel over sand and be twelve feet of moving murder. She had known that for thirty years. She had forgotten it for exactly as long as it took a well-dressed woman to offer her double her debt.

"She sent them to find this," Casper said aloud, not meaning to say it aloud, and Ruth turned to look at her.

"Sent them to find it—or sent them to find it and then made sure they couldn't leave with it?"

"That's the same sentence," Casper said, "wearing different clothes."

Downstream, faint through the trees, came a sound that stopped all four of them at once. Not the axe. Something smaller, quicker—a whistle. Two notes, low-high, repeated. The kind of signal a man makes when he isn't alone and wants to say so without shouting.

Teo's head came up like a bird dog's. "Our friend with the axe," he murmured. "He's got company. And company means a camp, and a camp means—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. A camp meant supplies, and supplies meant logistics, and logistics meant organization, and organization meant that whatever was happening in this gorge was not one frightened prospector with a shovel. It was an operation. It had a schedule. It had a budget. And operations with budgets did not tolerate witnesses, which was a lesson the expedition had already taught them at the price of a burned camp and smashed radios.

Emilio had begun, very quietly, to shake. Ruth put a hand on his arm and he mastered himself, jaw tight, eyes fixed upstream.

"We can't go back," Dario said. It was the first sensible thing Casper had heard him say, and it cost him visibly, because going back was the thing his aunt's money had made impossible and his own mouth had made shameful. "The boat's seen. If they have a radio—"

"They have a radio," Casper said. "Everything else they have, they have a radio too. That's how the world works now." She looked up the grade, into the green dark where Tunnel Nine waited with its half-shed portal and its cold breath, and she felt the old pull of it, the terrible familiarity, the knowledge arriving in her legs before her head: *the cut above the portal is stable, the drainage ditch is silted but sound, you can walk that line blindfolded and you may yet have to.* "So we don't go back, and we don't stay here. We go up. We move on the grade, we stay off the skyline, and we reach the pass before whatever's behind us decides we're worth the trouble of catching."

"And if we're wrong?" Ruth asked quietly. "If Marchetti is exactly what she seems, and there's a rescue party already coming upriver behind us?"

Casper picked up a fragment of blue shale from the turned soil of the test pit and turned it in her fingers, sharp-edged, freshly broken, and thought about the eleven names she carried like stones in a pocket she could never empty.

"Then we'll have walked a dead line for nothing," she said, "and I'll apologize to her in person. But I've read this water a long time, Doctor, and it isn't running clear."

She dropped the shale, shouldered her pack, and started up the grade toward the tunnel, and behind her, one by one, without anyone giving the order, the others fell into step—Teo, then Ruth with her hand still on Emilio's arm, then Dario, whose radio, Casper noted without turning her head, stayed silent in his jacket pocket, transmitting nothing, which was its own kind of confession.

The gorge swallowed them single file, and somewhere far above, past the schist and the spruce and the first white smudge of the early snows, the pass waited with its mouth open.
