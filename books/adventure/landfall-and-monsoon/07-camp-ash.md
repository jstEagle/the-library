## 7. Camp Ash

Ruth smelled the camp before she saw it.

The wind had turned with the morning, sliding down from the pass, and it carried a sweetness that had no business being in that country—the charred-sugar reek of burned canvas, of tar and rope, of things that had once been someone's shelter. She had smelled it before, years ago, in a border town where a cholera quarantine had been "managed" by fire, and her stomach remembered before her mind did. She stopped on the riverbank gravel and let the others come up beside her.

"There," Casper said quietly.

The camp sat on a bench of land above the waterline where a side creek came down through the alder, flat ground, good drainage, a spring nearby—anyone who knew mountains would have chosen it, and whoever had chosen it had known. Three tents had stood there once. The center pole of one still stood, a blackened finger holding up nothing. The other two had collapsed into themselves, their fabric burned away to lace, their frames warped into shapes like ribs.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Even Dario, who had not stopped talking since dawn, even Dario kept his mouth shut and his eyes moving.

"Spread out," Casper said. "Don't touch anything until Ruth's looked at it. Teo, watch the river both ways."

They climbed the bank in a loose line, boots crunching on cinders. Ruth went first because Casper pointed her first, and because this was the part of any disaster she understood: the part where you catalogued what was broken and decided what could still be saved. She had done it in field hospitals and flooded clinics and once, memorably, in a church that had become a morgue. You put your hands in your pockets if you had to, and you walked the wreckage slowly, and you made yourself look at everything.

The fire had been set deliberately—that was clear within ten steps. It hadn't spread; it had been *placed*. The burn pattern was tight, centered on the tents, while the surrounding grass was scorched only at the margins. Someone had poured something and lit it and stood back. And around the perimeter of the camp, half-buried in ash, she found the radios.

There were three of them. Expedition-grade transceivers, the kind Marchetti's money bought, each one smashed with a care that was worse than violence. Not thrown. Not shot. Set on flat stones and beaten methodically—valve housings crushed, dials shattered, antenna sockets hammered flat until they looked like fossils of machines. Whoever did it had taken their time. Whoever did it had wanted them found this way.

"Casper." She beckoned without turning around.

Casper came over, crouched, and picked up one of the dead radios. She turned it in her hands the way she'd turn a rock sample, and Ruth watched her face do something complicated—not surprise, exactly. Confirmation.

"They didn't want anyone calling out," Casper said. "Or calling in."

"Expeditions lose radios to weather. To drops. To rivers." Ruth nodded at the nearest wreck. "Nobody loses three like this."

"No," Casper said. "This is a message. This is *make sure*."

Teo had drifted along the creek line and now called out, low. He'd found the fire's fuel source—a drum, tipped on its side, its label long gone but the smell still clinging to it, petroleum and solvent. He held up a second thing between two fingers: a boot heel, torn free, and near it a dark stain on the stones that rain had tried and failed to erase.

Dario had gone very pale. He stood at the edge of the burned tents holding his hat in both hands, and for the first time since Puerto Vado he looked less like a rich man's nephew playing at adventure and more like a young man standing in a room he'd been told was empty and finding it furnished with ghosts.

"Aunt Ilsa said they missed check-ins," he said. His voice came out thin. "She said—weather, probably. Equipment failure."

"Equipment failure," Ruth repeated, and touched the crushed radio again. Under her glove, the glass of the dial face crumbled.

It was Casper who found the note. She lifted the corner of the least-damaged tent—its floorcloth had half survived, enough to shelter whatever lay beneath—and there, weighted under a stone, was a page from a survey notebook, folded twice, the paper spotted with old rain.

Casper read it once, silently, then handed it down without a word. Ruth took it.

The handwriting was cramped, hurried, the letters of a man writing against time:

*If anyone comes — we are alive as of the 14th. Seven of us walking higher. Merlo broke his leg at the scree, we left him at the tunnel with food. We are going to the basin past the shoulder — there are photographs and ledgers and they matter more than we do now. Do not trust anyone sent from below. Do not trust the company. If you find this, the men in the camps were not volunteers.*

*— A. Ferrer*

Ruth read it twice. The second time, her eyes snagged on the last line and would not move off it.

*The men in the camps were not volunteers.*

"Well," Teo said behind her, very quietly, in the tone of a man revising a map. "That changes the arithmetic."

* * *

They might have pushed straight on after that—up the creek trail toward the tunnel Ferrer's note mentioned—but Casper wouldn't allow it. "We eat, we treat what needs treating, we search properly," she said. "People who rush past evidence miss the second body." Nobody argued. There is a kind of authority that comes from having imagined the worst thing already, and Casper wore it like an old coat.

It was Teo, checking the alder thicket along the creek for firewood, who found him.

Ruth heard the shout and was moving before it finished. She came through the alders at a half-crouch and saw what Teo saw: a hollow beneath a leaning root mass, screened by cut branches, and in the hollow a man.

He was maybe thirty, though it was hard to say—hunger and injury had sanded years off him and added others. He wore clothes that had never belonged to an expedition: rough trousers gone gray with wear, a shirt of unbleached cotton, no boots at all, his feet wrapped in strips of blanket stiff with old blood. His left leg was a ruin from mid-calf down—swollen, angry, the skin hot and shiny, a wound along the shin that had been closed with stitches of wire, of all things, twisted tight by someone with more desperation than training. Fever had soaked him and dried him and soaked him again; the branches above him were streaked where he'd clawed at them in chills.

But he was breathing. Shallow, fast, but breathing.

"Don't move him yet." Ruth was already kneeling, already pulling her kit open. "Teo—boil water. All we've got. Dario—there's a tarp in my pack, second strap, get it over this space, make shade. Casper—" she glanced up and found the engineer already there, crouched opposite, sleeves rolled, hands ready. "Hold his leg steady when I tell you. Exactly when I tell you."

The man's eyes opened at the touch of her fingers on his ankle. They were enormous in a face gone hollow, fever-bright, and for one terrible instant they fixed on Ruth with pure animal terror—he thought she was one of *them*, whoever they were, come back to finish it. He tried to pull away and couldn't, and the sound he made wasn't a word in any language, just a small scraped noise like a door in a wind.

"You're safe," Ruth said. She said it in Spanish first, then again in the trade tongue, then again in Spanish. "You're safe. My name is Ruth. I'm a doctor. No one here will hurt you."

His chest heaved. His eyes went from her face to her hands to the faces beyond her—Teo with the kettle, Dario fumbling with the tarp, Casper with her steady grip waiting—and something in him made a decision that must have cost everything he had left. He stopped fighting. He began, instead, to cry, silently, the way men cry who learned young that crying loudly gets you punished.

"All right," Ruth murmured. "All right. I've got you."

The leg was bad. The wire stitches had held the edges together but nothing else; beneath them the wound had gone deep and dirty, and infection had moved in like a squatter. The swelling told her the tissue was fighting, which meant he was still fighting too, which meant she had a chance. She cleaned, debrided, packed, and wrapped while he bit down on the rolled leather she offered and made no sound at all except at the very worst moment, when a single syllable escaped him—*mamá*, barely voiced, a boy's word from a grown man's throat—and then he was quiet again, ashamed, turning his face away.

Fever, dehydration, a wound that needed a real hospital and a real surgeon. None of which existed within a hundred miles. So she did what field medicine always does: the possible instead of the ideal. Fluids, forced. Antiseptic, generous. Willow bark and the last of her antipyretics. Splint, padded, firm. And throughout, her hands working, her voice running low and constant, nonsense syllables mostly, the sound of another human being refusing to leave.

It was nearly an hour before he could talk. When he did, the words came in fragments, between swallows of water Ruth fed him drop by drop.

"Emilio," he said. That was his name. He said it like he was checking whether he still owned it.

"Emilio. Good. Can you tell me what happened? Who did this to your leg?"

"Not them." His eyes flicked toward the burned camp. "The survey people. Ferrer's group. They found me on the grade, two days' walk down. I'd run. They carried me." His mouth worked. "They were good people. They gave me their food."

"And the ones who hurt you?"

Something changed in his face then. Ruth had seen it before, in survivors of massacres, of prison camps, of mines that ate men—the moment when the memory arrives whole and the body has to hold it. His breath went ragged. Casper's hand settled on his shoulder, light as a bird landing, and stayed.

"The extension," Emilio said. "The new line. Over the pass. They told the towns it was work. Good pay, they said. Contracts, they said." A sound that was almost a laugh. "There are no contracts. There is a gate. And behind the gate there is a quota—so many meters of grade a day, so many ties, and if the quota isn't met, no food. If it isn't met twice—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started somewhere else. "There are chains. At night, chains, so no one walks away. Men tried. The ones they caught—" He looked at his own ruined leg, and Ruth understood, suddenly and completely, how he had gotten the wound. Not rockfall. Not accident.

"You ran," she said softly.

"I ran." He said it without shame, which somehow made it worse. "Through the river. Through the rocks. Something—" his hand sketched it, the tearing, the wire stitches some camp medic had later twisted into him, "—something took the skin off to the bone. I crawled. Two days. Then the survey people found me."

Silence in the little shaded hollow. Upstream, the creek made its indifferent sound. Ruth became aware that Dario had stopped moving entirely, the tarp stake still in his hand, his face the color of the ash on the hillside.

"How many men?" Casper asked. Her voice was level, but Ruth, watching her profile, saw the effort it cost—saw the way her jaw set like someone bracing a load.

"In the main camp? Two hundred. Maybe more. There are others higher, at the cut. They don't let you count. Counting is—" he searched, "—counting is trouble."

"Two hundred men," Teo said from the edge of the hollow, "chained, on a railroad the maps say doesn't exist."

"And the survey team photographed it," Emilio said. "I heard them talking. Cameras. Notebooks. Names—they wrote down names, asked our names, said names mattered." His fever-bright eyes moved across all of them. "Then the company men came up the valley. Armed. And the survey people ran higher, into the snow country, carrying everything."

Ruth sat back on her heels. Around her the camp waited—the burned tents, the murdered radios, the note weighted under stone—and the shape of it assembled itself in her mind with the cold click of a diagnosis completing. An expedition finds atrocity. An expedition goes silent. A financier sends a rescue party up the same corridor, guided by the one person who knows the closed lines, staffed with her own nephew and a hidden radio.

Urgency had brought them this far. What replaced it, settling into Ruth's chest like cold water into a boot, was not urgency anymore.

It was horror. Horror, and the arithmetic underneath it: *if the expedition was silenced once, what happens to the second party that follows the same trail?*

She looked up and found Casper looking back at her, and she knew the engineer had arrived at the same figure.

"We rest tonight," Casper said at last, to all of them, in the voice of a woman reading a bridge load rating. "Full rest. Tomorrow we climb."

Above the treeline, far up in the blue haze of the pass, the first cloud of the front was building—white now, innocent now, stacking itself against the sky like something patient.

Ruth checked Emilio's pulse, pulled the blanket higher over his splinted leg, and did not like the sky at all.
