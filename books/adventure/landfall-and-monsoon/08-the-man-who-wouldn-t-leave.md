## 8. The Man Who Wouldn't Leave

Casper woke before the others, as she always did now, and lay in the grey half-light of Camp Ash listening to the fire tick through its coals.

The camp had been theirs for a night and most of a day—Ruth's order, over Dario's objections, and Casper had backed her without saying why. The truth was that Casper had needed the rest too, though she would not have admitted it aloud. The truth was also that she had wanted to sit with this place a while longer, to read it the way she read anything built by hands: what it had been, what had been done to it, and in what order.

Camp Ash told its story plainly enough if you knew how to ask. The main tent had burned from the inside out—the char pattern bloomed outward from a central point, which meant accelerant or a stove deliberately overturned, not lightning, not accident. The supply crates were stacked neatly at the perimeter, untouched. Whoever set the fire had wanted the camp destroyed but not the food. That was not rage. That was housekeeping.

And the radios—all three of them, smashed with a rock or a boot heel, crushed past any field repair—had been killed with care. A looter takes the radio. A panicked man drops it in the river. Someone who wanted no signal ever coming down from this valley again did what had been done here.

Emilio lay by the fire where they'd moved him, on a bedroll layered over Teo's spare tarp, his splinted leg propped on a crate of rice. He had slept badly, crying out twice in the night in a language Casper didn't speak but understood anyway—the flat, exhausted cries of a man running in his sleep. Ruth had dosed him with the last of the good analgesic and sat up with him through the worst hours, her small carbide lamp burning low like a votive.

Now he was awake, and his eyes were open, and they were fixed on Casper.

She felt the weight of his attention before she fully turned. When she did, she found him watching her with an expression she recognized from another life—from the hospital corridors after Viaduct 7, from the faces of men who had worked under her and were deciding how much of the truth they could afford to carry into a room where officials waited with notebooks. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic with his life.

"You're the engineer," he said. His Spanish was slow, slurred at the edges by fever and painkillers, but deliberate. "The one who knows the line."

"I was," Casper said. "A long time ago."

"Teo told me." Emilio shifted against the roll, and the movement cost him; his breath went thin and stayed thin for three counts before it came back. "He said you worked the mountain roads. Before they closed them."

"I surveyed them. I helped build two of them." She crouched by the fire and fed it a split of the dead camp's fuel—a chair leg, planed smooth, stamped with the expedition's mark. Even their furniture had been inventoried. "You don't have to talk. Ruth says you should rest."

"I have rested." He said it with a strange, bitter precision, as though rest were a thing that had been done to him rather than given. "I have rested for six days. In a culvert. Under a tarp made from cement bags. While my—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again from somewhere deeper. "While the others were taken up the pass. Or left behind. I don't know which is worse to say."

Dario was awake across the fire. Casper saw him pretending not to be—lying with his back to them, his bedroll arranged with studied casualness, his hidden radio no doubt cold against his ribs under his jacket. She catalogued the angle of his shoulders, the stillness that wasn't quite natural, and filed it away. Later. Everything later. First this man was going to talk, and she was going to listen, because she had spent eleven years listening to official versions of things and she had developed an appetite for the other kind.

"Then say what you can," Casper said. "Slowly. We're not going anywhere."

* * *

Teo brought water heated with pine needles and a strip of dried meat softened in the pot, and Emilio ate a little, and then he talked.

He talked the way men talk when they have been holding a story in their mouths so long it has started to dissolve—fragments first, then longer runs, the narrative assembling itself out of pieces like a rail laid across broken ground.

He had been a mason, he said. Properly trained, in the valley towns below, with papers. Two years ago the extension project had come recruiting—good wages, he'd been told, a season's work, a bonus at the end. He'd signed on with forty other men from his district. They'd ridden the maintenance cars up the closed line at night, because the closed line was not closed, that was the first thing, the thing everything else hung from. The corridor Casper had known as rusted and abandoned, struck from the maps, posted against trespass—it ran. It ran heavy traffic, most of it in darkness, most of it carrying men.

"The signs are fake," Emilio said. "The postings. A foreman showed us the paint store receipts once, drunk, laughing. The company pays for the signs itself. Closed road, dangerous, no entry. So that no one comes to see what the road is for."

Casper's hands went still around the tin cup she was holding. She had walked past those signs four days ago. She had read them as history—her own history, the closure she had lived through, the slow death of the mountain routes after the collapse. She had touched the weathered board at the junction with something close to grief.

Fresh paint underneath, she had noted. Weathering on top. She had thought: someone maintains the lie of abandonment to keep squatters off the grade.

She had not let herself think the larger thought. Now it arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do—not creeping but complete, delivered all at once like a load shifting on a crane.

They kept the line closed on paper so that no one would audit what ran on it in fact.

"And the camps?" Ruth asked quietly. She had settled cross-legged near Emilio, notebook closed for once, just listening. "The ones we passed?"

"There are nine between here and the summit works," Emilio said. "Ours was the fourth. You saw the second, maybe—I heard you pass on the river two days before they moved me." He looked at Casper. "You saw the wire?"

"We saw the wire."

"The wire is to keep us in, not anyone else out." He said it flatly, without self-pity, and somehow that was worse than weeping would have been. "At first it was a job. Hard, badly fed, but a job. Then the quotas came. Meters of tunnel per shift, ties per day, and when the quota was missed, pay was 'held'—that was the word, held—and the gates were locked at night, and there was a man with a list, and your name going onto the list was called being *sent down*, except nobody sent down ever came back up. After the third month, some of the men stopped asking about wages at all. They asked about the list instead. Whether their names were on it. That was the economy of the place. Not money. The list."

Ruth's hand had gone to her mouth. Teo stood at the edge of the firelight with his arms folded, his face carved into something old and hard, and Casper understood that Teo had known—had guessed, at least, had grown up on this river hearing the stories the mountains tell—and had said nothing, because what could a boatman say, and to whom?

"How many men?" Casper asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, in a distant way, the way you're proud of a stranger.

"In our camp, one hundred and sixty when I arrived. One hundred and thirty-one when I left it." He paused. "Not all of the difference is dead. Some were moved up to the summit works, where the tunnel is. But the infirmary tent—we had an infirmary tent, a canvas lean-to with a bench—and it filled faster than it emptied, and the doctor was a vet, actually a horse doctor, and after February he stopped coming at all."

"And you?" Ruth asked gently. "Your leg."

"A rock fall in the cut above the fourth viaduct." Something flickered across his face—there and gone, a fish under ice. "Three of us were under it. The other two didn't come out. I was dug free and carried to the camp, and the foreman looked at my leg and put me on the list."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope a branch cracked under its own snow-load, a single sharp report, and everyone flinched, and Casper saw in that flinch exactly what kind of country these men had been living in, where every sound might be the mountain or might be the list.

"They were going to send me down," Emilio said. "But the survey party came that week. Up from the river, with pack animals and cameras and a radio. Official-looking. Papers. The foreman hid the wire cutters and opened the cook tent and gave us all clean shirts and told us to smile, and the survey men walked through taking photographs of everything—the camps, the gauge, the tunnel heads, the men lined up at the mess—and one of them, a woman with grey hair and a camera on a tripod, took pictures of the infirmary lean-to, and of the list itself, nailed inside the foreman's door. She photographed the list."

Casper closed her eyes.

She could see it. God help her, she could see it perfectly—the expedition moving up the corridor she knew like her own handwriting, the cameras, the ledgers, the careful professional documentation of a crime that had been operating for two years inside a corridor officially marked *closed*. And she could see the moment the photographs became a problem. Not the taking of them. The existence of them. Film is evidence, and evidence travels, and evidence comes down off a mountain unless someone makes sure it doesn't.

"The survey party left," Emilio said. "And that night the foreman got a radio message—I saw him take it, I was in the cook tent scrounging—and after it he changed. He got quiet in a new way. The next morning they moved me and four other injured men down toward the river in a maintenance car, and the story was that we were being released, taken to the valley hospital. At the junction they took us off the car and put us in a shed, and the foreman said the release had been postponed. And then men came—six of them, armed, not railway men, hired men, the same kind who patrol the lower camps—and the foreman handed us over like freight, and the last thing he said was that we should pray the survey party lost its way in the pass."

Emilio's voice broke on the sentence, not with tears but with the effort of carrying it, and Ruth leaned forward and steadied his shoulder, and he went on.

"They marched us up-valley. Away from the river. On the second day I couldn't walk anymore—my leg had gone bad, the bone grinding—and one of the hired men raised his rifle and the foreman's cousin, a younger man, softer, argued him down. *Leave him*, he said. *The mountain will do it.* And they left me under a rock overhang with a bag of maize meal and no water, and they walked on north with the other four, and I lay there for a day and a night deciding whether to live, and then I crawled."

"Toward the river," Teo said. "Downhill. Toward smoke."

"Toward smoke," Emilio agreed. "I smelled your fire."

* * *

The camp was quiet for a long time after that.

Ruth repacked his dressing. Teo split more wood without being asked, the axe strokes steady and unhurried, a man giving his hands something honest to do. Dario had given up the pretense of sleeping and sat poking the fire with a stick, and Casper watched him watch Emilio, and watched the boy's face do something complicated—horror, yes, but underneath it a quick animal calculation, the look of a man counting exits.

Casper walked to the edge of the clearing alone.

The burned camp spread out around her, and beyond it the river ran silver-grey in the early light, and above it all the pass stood up into the sky with its new cloud building, patient, white, innocent. She stood with her hands in her pockets and let the arithmetic finish itself, because it was going to finish itself whether she supervised it or not.

Marchetti had come to Puerto Vado with a story: three missed check-ins, a lost expedition, a rescue, double the debt. Casper had read the route and refused, and the refusal had lasted exactly as long as it took a debt collector to sell her name. She had told herself, climbing into the launch at dawn, that grief was being used against her but that the mission itself was clean—find the lost, bring them home. A rescue was a rescue even if you were dragged into it.

Now she stood in the ashes of the thing she had been sent to find, and the arithmetic said otherwise.

Run the numbers the way she'd been taught to run numbers, back when her signature meant something. An expedition goes silent for three check-ins. The financier doesn't wait the standard window. Doesn't notify authorities—authorities mean records, records mean questions about what the expedition was surveying. Instead she finds a disgraced engineer who knows the closed corridor, buys her with a debt, and sends a party of five upriver with one medic, one boatman, one enthusiastic nephew, and no security worth the name. No radios capable of reaching the valley—wait. Casper turned that over. Their own set was adequate for relay through the repeater at the junction, barely. Marchetti had known the route's communications profile better than Casper did. She had chosen equipment that could whisper but not shout.

And the nephew. Reporting position nightly on a hidden set. To whom? To his aunt, certainly—but an aunt who wants a rescue found does not need nightly coordinates. An aunt who needs to know *where the film is, and whether it has been buried, burned, or recovered* needs nightly coordinates.

The expedition hadn't gotten lost. The expedition had been documenting the labor camps—photographing the list, Emilio said, photographing the infirmary—and then it had gone silent precisely when silence served the people who owned the corridor. And the rescue party marching up behind it, following its trail, was not a rescue. It was a verification. A walk-through. Confirm nothing survived, confirm nothing came down, and—Casper's stomach turned slowly over as the final piece seated itself—confirm that the guide leading the way was the one person whose ruin could be re-arranged at will, whose testimony was already worthless, who had every reason never to speak to an inspector again as long as she lived.

They hadn't sent her despite her record. They had sent her *because* of it.

Her breath came out white and slow. Eleven names in a courtroom eleven years ago, and she had swallowed the verdict because fighting it meant fighting the company with nothing but her word, and her word had been worth less than the piers she'd condemned. She had run. She had built a small quiet life in a town that forgot her name, and she had thought the price of that peace was paid in full.

It hadn't been a price. It had been a deposit. They had kept the receipt.

"Casper."

Ruth's voice, behind her. Casper turned. The medic stood at the treeline with her kit on her shoulder and her eyes steady, and behind her, at the fire, Emilio had raised himself up on one elbow to keep Casper in view, as though afraid she might simply walk into the trees and be gone.

"He's asking for you," Ruth said. "There's more. About the survey party. About where they went after the camps."

Casper walked back to the fire. Emilio looked up at her, and this time there was no arithmetic in his face. There was something plainer and harder to refuse.

"You photographed the list," he said. "In the tunnel. In Tunnel Nine—your Tunnel Nine, the big one below the summit works. There's a side gallery, sealed since the old days, and the men who've been up there two years—they go in there when they can, and they scratch their names into the rock. Names, and home towns, and dates. Like the men who built the original line did. My cousin's name is in there. He's been up there fourteen months." His voice dropped. "The survey party found the gallery. The grey-haired woman photographed every name. Every one. She told the foreman it was for the historical record. She winked at me while she said it."

Casper knelt by the fire, slowly, her bad knee cracking, until her eyes were level with his.

"Where did the survey party go," she said, "after Tunnel Nine."

"Up," Emilio said. "Toward the summit basin. With the film and the ledgers and the foreman's copy of the list, wrapped in oilcloth. And the hired men went up after them the next morning, twelve of them, with rifles and dogs." He held her gaze. "That was eight days ago."

Eight days. Three missed check-ins. A financier who arrived in Puerto Vado within a day of the third silence, already knowing the route, already knowing the name of the only engineer who could walk it.

Behind Casper, faintly, from the direction of Dario's bedroll, came a sound she had been listening for since the second night out: the soft double-click of a transmit key, and then nothing—no voice, just a short burst, coordinates compressed into a few seconds of carrier wave, sent low, sent fast, sent to someone below who was keeping a map.

Casper didn't turn around. Not yet.

Above the pass, the patient white cloud had begun, very slowly, to move.
