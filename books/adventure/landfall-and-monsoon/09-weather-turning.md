## 9. Weather Turning

Teo smelled the storm before he saw it.

It came down the wind off the high country the way bad news comes to a river town—quietly at first, then all at once, and then too late to do anything about it. He was standing in the shallows with the bow line wrapped twice around his fist, checking the launch's hull for the scrape she'd taken against a submerged log two days back, when the air changed. The warm smell of the water went thin. Underneath it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, came the scent of snow.

Snow. In what the lowland calendars still called summer.

He straightened up slowly and looked west, where the range had been building itself into the sky all week, blue and indifferent, a wall they'd been rowing toward with the stubbornness of people who had already paid too much to turn around. The wall was different now. Clouds stood on its shoulders like sentries that had stopped pacing—still, dense, gray-white, stacked in layers that meant business. And above them, streaming eastward in long plumes like smoke from a fire nobody could see, the high wind was already running.

*Föhn wind,* his grandfather would have called it, if his grandfather had known these mountains instead of only the river. *The mountain breathing out before it holds its breath.*

Teo had run this river for thirty-one years. He knew what it looked like when the sky made promises it intended to keep.

"Casper," he called, not turning. "Come look at this."

She came down the bank with her hands in her pockets, the way she did everything—like a woman conserving herself for something worse. She followed his gaze up to the range and Teo watched her face do something complicated: the flinch of recognition, then the quick arithmetic of a person who had once been paid to understand exactly this kind of weather.

"How long?" she asked.

"Depends how fast it comes off the crest." He spat into the water, an old habit that passed for precision among boatmen. "Two days if we're lucky. One if we're not. After that, the pass closes and stays closed until spring."

"And the front?"

"Front's the least of it." He pointed upstream, where the river ran narrow and fast between walls of granite, white water braiding over drowned boulders. "That comes down out of those peaks. Every drop of rain that falls up there lands in this channel inside six hours. You get a real storm sitting on the divide, this river can rise three meters overnight. There won't be a landing above the gorge. There won't be a *gorge* you'd recognize."

Casper said nothing for a while. She had a habit of silence that other people found unnerving; Teo had decided early on that it was the silence of someone listening to several conversations at once, most of them with herself.

"We're close," she said finally. "The junction is half a day up. The grade beyond that—"

"The grade doesn't matter if we drown getting to it."

"It matters to them." Her voice didn't rise, but it changed key, the way ice changes sound underfoot. "Whoever's left up there."

Teo looked at her. She was watching the mountains, but her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely—to a bridge, maybe, or a courtroom, or eleven graves she'd never visited. He had carried enough passengers to know when a person was traveling somewhere no boat could follow.

"I'm not saying leave them," he said. "I'm saying we make a decision now, while deciding is still free. Later it'll cost."

"Then let's decide together." She turned toward camp. "Get everyone."

* * *

They gathered around the cold fire ring in the lee of a fallen cedar, the five of them and Emilio, who sat propped against a root with his bandaged leg stretched out and his eyes moving from face to face with the wariness of a man who had learned that meetings like this one usually ended badly for someone like him.

Teo laid it out plainly, because plainness was the only tool he trusted. The storm. The river. The arithmetic of altitude and time. "We go up to the junction today, make camp, and tomorrow we take the old grade on foot—the launch stays tied below flood line. Or we turn back now, all of us, and come back with proper gear and proper weather and a proper search party when the passes open. Those are the honest choices. I've heard some dishonest ones too, and I want to say up front I don't like the sound of any of them."

Ruth Okafor sat with her medical kit open on her knees, repacking it out of pure nervous discipline. "Emilio can't climb in this cold. Not yet. His wound's clean but he's lost blood, and hypothermia will finish what the fever started."

"So we split," Dario said.

Everyone looked at him. He was crouched by the fire ring, feeding dead needles into a flame he'd insisted on keeping alive even though there was nothing to cook, and he spoke with the bright, brittle confidence that Teo had come to recognize as the boy's native language.

"We split. Ruth stays here with Emilio, rest of us push to the junction tonight, start up the grade at first light. We beat the front to the pass or we shelter in one of the tunnels Casper keeps talking about. It's not complicated."

"It's not complicated," Teo agreed, "the way drowning isn't complicated. Everything about it is simple right up until the moment it kills you."

Dario flushed. "My aunt's people are up there. Maybe dying up there. While we sit here debating the *weather*—"

"Your aunt's people have been silent for nine days," Casper said quietly. "If they were walking out, we'd have met them. If they were signaling, we'd have seen fires. Whatever's happening up there has already happened, Dario. The only question left is whether we get ourselves killed confirming it."

"That's a hell of a thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say. Those aren't always the same."

The silence that followed had weight. Teo watched Ruth watch Casper; watched Emilio watch Dario; watched Dario stare into his little fire with his jaw working, and thought, not for the first time, that the boy carried himself like a man reading from a script someone else had written—a script with more courage in it than the boy had been issued.

"Vote," Teo said. "All in favor of going on to the junction, at least, and reassessing there."

Four hands. Even Ruth, after a glance at Emilio, raised hers—and Emilio, unasked, lifted his own hand from where it lay in the dirt, fingers splayed, as if voting cost him something physical.

"All in favor of turning back?"

No hands. Dario looked around the circle almost triumphantly, as though unanimity proved his point rather than burying it.

"Junction, then," Teo said. "And I'm telling you now—if the sky says stop at the junction, we stop. I've spent my life learning what rivers do when storms come off the divide, and I'm not going to be argued out of my own eyes."

Nobody argued. That was the first wrong note, Teo thought later. On a crew that had been arguing since Puerto Vado, sudden agreement should have sounded like an alarm.

* * *

They made the junction by midafternoon, the launch fighting current the whole way, the water gone the color of gunmetal and running higher than it had that morning. Teo could feel the river thinking. It pushed against the hull in long, muscular surges, as though something enormous upstream had shifted its weight.

The junction itself was a place the map had forgotten—a gravel fan where a tributary slid down out of the high country to join the main current, and where, years ago, a spur line had once climbed away from the river toward the pass. Teo saw it the moment they rounded the bend: the grade, cut into the valley wall, running up through the trees like a scar that hadn't healed. Beside it, half-swallowed by brush, stood a survey marker—fresh paint, fresh numbers, wrong year.

Casper stood at the bow for a long time looking at it. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. Teo had watched her find that marker two days ago and had watched something behind her eyes close like a door.

They beached the launch above the obvious flood line—Teo insisted on three meters above the highest driftwood, and then, looking at the sky, added another meter—and began the work of making camp while the light lasted. The clouds had come down off the crest now, filling the upper valley like water filling a basin. The air had gone cold enough to see your breath.

It was while he was hauling the last of the gear upslope, alone, taking the long way around a slide of broken rock, that Teo heard the voice.

Low. Clipped. Coming from behind a shoulder of granite where the old grade curved away from camp.

"—position confirmed, yes. Junction camp. Five—" A pause. "—five plus one, the laborer. Moving onto the grade at first light. Route via Tunnel Nine, assuming—" Another pause, longer, and when the voice resumed it had dropped further, gone flat and quick, a man reciting coordinates he'd memorized rather than understood. "Grid reference follows. Mark it. Mark it *now*, we may lose signal when the front—"

Teo stood very still.

He knew the voice. Of course he knew the voice. He'd spent two weeks sharing a boat with it, listening to it joke at breakfast and complain about mosquitoes and ask Casper earnest, endless questions about trestle loads and tunnel grades. Dario Marchetti, twenty-four years old, enthusiastic as a puppy, useless with a rope and cheerful about it.

Talking into a radio. Giving their position, their numbers, their route—to someone downstream who wanted it marked *now*.

Teo's first feeling wasn't anger. It was something colder and older, the feeling of a man who has read water his whole life and finally sees the eddy he missed—the smooth place on the surface that was hiding the rock all along. *Surveillance.* He'd felt it for days without being able to name it: the boy's little errands, his absences at dawn, the way he always seemed to know what time it was. Reporting back to his aunt. All this way. Through all of this.

He stepped around the rock.

Dario was crouched with his back to him, radio handset pressed to his ear, a folded map across his knee with a pencil mark so fresh it shone. At the sound of Teo's boots he went rigid—not the rigidity of surprise but of a man caught at the exact moment he'd feared most—and then he turned, and his face did everything at once: guilt, panic, and underneath both, horribly, relief. Like a swimmer going under who finally sees a hand.

"Teo." His voice cracked. "I can explain."

"You were doing fine explaining just now." Teo held out his palm. "Give it here."

"They need to know where we are. If the storm hits and we go missing too, Aunt Ilsa needs—"

"Your aunt needs to know whether her evidence survived," Teo said. "Not whether her nephew did. Give me the radio, Dario."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment, the kind a riverman knows better than any other, the moment balanced between the rock and the wreck—Teo thought the boy might fight him. Dario's knuckles whitened on the handset. His eyes flicked past Teo's shoulder, calculating distances, exits, odds.

And then Casper's voice came from ten feet away, quiet as falling snow:

"Don't."

She had come around the other side of the rocks without a sound, which should have frightened Teo more than it did. Ruth was behind her, and beyond Ruth, leaning on a staff, gray-faced, Emilio—drawn by the voices, or perhaps by an instinct older than hearing, the instinct of a man who knows the sound of betrayal in any language.

Dario looked at all of them. The fire of his conviction, whatever script had fed it, guttered visibly. Slowly, like a man setting down something far heavier than a radio, he held out the handset.

Casper took it. She turned it over once in her hand, then depressed the transmit key and spoke into it in a voice stripped of everything but function.

"To whoever's receiving. This is Casper Sandoval. Your man in the field is done transmitting. If there are crews on that grade waiting for us—" she paused, and Teo saw her choose the next words the way a surgeon chooses an incision— "they should know the ledgers are copies. The originals went out weeks ago. Come up yourself if you want them."

She released the key. Static hissed in the cold air, formless, unreadable. Then it died.

For a long moment nobody moved. Above them the clouds finished closing the last blue seam in the sky, and the light on the valley went the color of pewter, and somewhere high on the divide the wind began to sing through a notch in the ridge—a thin, rising note, like a wire tightening.

"You lied," Dario whispered. "About the ledgers."

"Yes," Casper said.

"Why?"

"Because whoever's listening just made a decision based on false information." She handed the radio back to him—not gently, but not cruelly either, the way you return a weapon to a child along with the knowledge of what it does. "Now he knows how that feels. First light, we move. And Dario—you walk where I can see you."

She turned and walked back toward camp, and Ruth went after her, and Emilio after Ruth, leaving Teo alone on the rocks with the boy.

Dario sat down abruptly on the grade ballast, all the strength gone out of his legs, and put his face in his hands. The wind was rising now in earnest, combing the treetops, carrying the first smell of snow no longer faint but unmistakable, a promise the sky fully intended to keep.

"She sent them," he said into his hands. "Didn't she. My aunt. This whole time—I told myself it was rescue, I told myself—" He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and young. "What did I do, Teo? What did I *do*?"

Teo looked at him for a long moment. Then up at the pass, where the first flakes were coming down—not drifting, not dancing, but falling straight and hard, like something being delivered.

"You told them where to find us," he said. "So pray the storm gets there first."

Far up the valley, at the mouth of the black line of Tunnel Nine, something glinted—metal catching the last light, there and gone, like a signal, or a warning.
