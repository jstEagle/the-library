## 10. The Switchback Road

The river gave out an hour past dawn, thinning into rapids that Teo could no longer argue with. He brought the launch against a gravel bar with the tenderness of a man setting down something he loved, and when the propeller ground once, softly, on stone, he cut the engine and let the silence of the high country come down around them like a lid.

"End of the water," he said. "After this, it's legs."

Casper stood in the bow and looked up. The valley walls closed in here, granite going gray-green with lichen, and above the treeline—still far above, but visible now, undeniable—the peaks wore their first white of the season like a warning served early. Somewhere up there was the pass. Somewhere up there, if the note from Camp Ash told true, were people still breathing.

And somewhere along the eastern wall, half-swallowed by twenty years of alder and slide rock, ran the grade she had helped survey. The closed line. Her line.

"We go up," she said, and pointed to where a cut in the trees showed a hint of ballast stone gone mossy. "There."

Dario squinted at it. "That's a trail?"

"That's a railroad." She shouldered her pack and did not look at him. "Or it was. The Cordillera Extension, surveyed the year before Viaduct 7 went down. They graded eleven miles, drove two tunnels, built four trestles—and then the collapse happened, and the money dried up, and the company walked away from all of it. Nobody's run a train through there since. Nobody official."

Ruth adjusted the strap across Emilio's back, where the laborer rode slung like a wounded child, his bandaged leg trailing. He had been silent since dawn, but his eyes moved over the mountainside with a terrible familiarity, and Casper understood: he had walked out of those camps the hard way, without a road at all.

"You've been up there," Ruth said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"I drew half those plans." Casper started up the bank, testing each stone before trusting it. "So try to keep that in mind when I tell you which rocks are liars."

* * *

The grade revealed itself slowly, the way old promises do. First a suggestion through the trees—a straightness too straight for nature. Then the ballast underfoot, angular stone still locked together after two decades, refusing to become soil. Then, rounding a shoulder of the mountain, the first trestle.

It stood across a ravine where a creek ran white and loud, and Casper stopped ten meters short and made everyone else stop too.

The structure was redwood, silvered now, its timbers furred with moss. From a distance it looked intact, almost picturesque, a postcard of forgotten engineering. But Casper's eyes went where they had been trained to go—to the bents, the trestle-bents rising from the ravine floor, and to the sills beneath them, and to the way the whole skeleton leaned, very slightly, downstream.

"Don't step on it," she said.

"It looks fine," Dario said.

"It looked fine at Viaduct 7, too." The words came out flatter than she intended, and she felt Ruth glance at her, and Dario have the grace to shut up. She crouched, pulled off a glove, and pressed her palm against the near abutment. Stone, mortared, sound enough. It was the wood she didn't trust. "Water gets under the sills and stays there. Freezes every winter, thaws every spring. Twenty winters of that, prying at the joints. You can't see rot until you're standing on it, and by then you're standing on nothing."

"So how do we cross?" Teo asked.

"We don't. Not here." She studied the ravine, then the creek, then the far wall where the grade continued, a pale scar in the green. "Two hundred meters upstream the gorge narrows. There'll be deadfall or slide rock bridging it, or we find a place to drop down and climb back up. It costs us an hour. It doesn't cost us anyone."

They went upstream. It cost them nearly two hours, because the narrow was choked with a slide and they crossed it on hands and knees over wet granite, passing packs hand to hand while the creek roared below, cold enough to kill in minutes. Emilio they lowered on ropes, Ruth going down beside him with one arm hooked through the webbing, murmuring to him the whole way as though the words themselves were rope.

When they regained the grade on the far side, Dario was breathing hard and looking at Casper differently. Not warmly. But differently.

"How many more of those?" he asked.

"Trestles? Three that I remember. Tunnels, two. And the second tunnel is the bad one." She resettled her pack. "Keep walking."

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the forest changed around them as they rose. The alders gave way to pines, the pines to a sparser, colder stand, and the light came down longer and whiter between the trunks. Snowmelt dripped somewhere out of sight, a sound like slow counting. Twice Casper found things she remembered: a rusted lantern hook driven into a cut face; a stretch of rail, bent into a lazy S by some long-ago rockfall, its head worn bright where men had once walked it home.

She read the mountain the way Teo read the river—noticing what it carried, what it hid, where it turned mean. A bulge of fresh talus below a cliff meant rockfall, and rockfall meant the grade might be buried ahead, and buried grade meant route-finding through timber where the old line vanished. A seep staining the cut bank black meant frozen ground underneath, unstable. The tunnel mouths she approached the way you approach a strange dog: sideways, quietly, listening first.

The first tunnel was short, barely thirty meters, and half its roof had come down in a fall so old that young pines grew out of the rubble inside. They crossed the debris in single file, heads down, and emerged into light on the other side, and Casper allowed herself one breath of relief before she caught herself doing arithmetic again—distance climbed, hours left, the weight of the sky.

Because the sky was wrong. She'd been watching it since midmorning. The peaks above them had gone from white-capped to white-shouldered, streamers of snow blowing off the summits in long banners that pointed east, then northeast, then east again, restless as a man who can't decide which door to bolt through.

"Teo," she said. "You've seen weather up here."

He followed her gaze, and his jaw tightened. "Banner cloud. Wind's shifting. That's not a squall wind. That's a front deciding where it wants to sit." He spat, deliberately, into the dirt. "My grandmother would say the mountain is clearing its throat."

"Then we don't camp in the open tonight," Casper said. "We make the second tunnel. Shelter's already built."

* * *

The rope bridge was not on any plan she had drawn.

They found it an hour past noon, where the grade crossed a gorge deeper and narrower than the first ravine—a slot maybe fifteen meters wide, with the creek invisible far below, audible only as a hush, a breath, a suggestion of depth. The original trestle here had collapsed entirely; only the near abutment remained, a broken molar of stone. But someone, sometime within recent years, had strung a crossing: two cables anchored into the rock, wooden slats lashed at intervals, a few frayed hand-lines.

Emilio saw it and began, softly, to pray.

"Company bridge," Dario said, and his voice had gone careful. "Maintenance crews use these on the extension work. It's recent. Look at the cable—it's galvanized."

Casper was already at the anchor point, and her stomach had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with altitude. The cable was galvanized, yes. It was also anchored in a drilled hole packed with what looked like hand-tamped mortar rather than proper grout, and the near anchor sat in fractured rock that showed fresh movement—a crack running from the hole toward the edge of the abutment, hairline but alive, dust still sitting in the bottom of it like powder in a wound.

"This was rigged fast," she said. "By men who wanted it to hold today and didn't care about next month." She tested the cable with both hands, hauling sideways. It hummed, taut, and the crack in the rock ticked, very faintly, like a clock.

"Can it hold us?" Ruth asked.

"One at a time, lightly, staying off the slats where they're dark with rot—" She stopped. Recalculated. Looked at Emilio, at the packs, at the distance to the tunnel they needed to reach before dark. "One at a time. Packs ferried separately on a second pass. No stopping in the middle. I go first, then Ruth with Emilio's sling, then the rest."

"And if it fails?" Dario asked.

"Then the person on it holds onto the cable and doesn't let go, and the rest of us haul." She met his eyes. "Which is why nobody lets go. Whatever happens. Nobody lets go."

She crossed first. The bridge moved under her in a way no trestle ever should—a living sway, a breathing—but the cable held, and the far anchor, she found when she reached it, was set in sound rock, properly done. Of course it was. Men rig escapes carelessly and arrivals carefully. She clipped a spare rope to the far cable, threw the end back across, and watched them come.

Teo crossed like a man born on moving surfaces, contemptuous of the whole arrangement. Then Ruth, having slung Emilio across on the rope Casper hauled, came last-but-one, light and quick, a climber's economy in her movements that Casper filed away with everything else she was learning about her.

Which left Dario.

He stepped on with the confidence of a man who had never in his life been the last one across anything, and got four slats out before the world changed.

It didn't fail loudly. That was the horror of it. There was a small sound from the near anchor—a click, mineral, almost polite—and then the cable dropped ten centimeters with a snap of released tension, and the whole bridge swung out over the gorge like a pendulum given permission, and Dario made a sound Casper had heard once before, years ago, from a brakeman riding steel that was already gone: not a scream, just a short syllable of pure surprise, the noise a man makes when the future stops being negotiable.

"DON'T LET GO!" Casper was already flat on her stomach at the far anchor, both fists on the cable, boots wedged against the abutment stone. The cable jerked in her grip, alive, trying to leave. Behind her, Teo hit the near side and threw himself onto the sagging line, adding his weight to hold the anchor end down. The cracked rock ticked. Dust jumped from the fissure in little gray ghosts.

Dario hung with both arms wrapped in the cable, swinging over a depth that swallowed sound. His feet kicked, found a slat, and the slat exploded into brown pulp. He screamed then, properly.

"The rope!" Ruth was already moving—no hesitation, no negotiation with herself, just motion. She took the haul rope Casper had thrown back, clipped herself in with a carabiner produced from her med kit as though she'd been waiting all day for exactly this, and went over the edge of the abutment on the near side, sliding down the rope toward the sagging cable with her boots braced against rock, paying out line hand over hand.

"Ruth—" Teo's voice cracked on it.

"He can't hold. Look at his arms." She was right. Even from across the gap Casper could see Dario's forearms shaking, his grip failing by degrees, the cable burning through whatever strength he had left. "I'm lighter than any of you. I get to him, I clip him to me, you two take the strain and walk it back."

It was the worst possible plan and the only one. Casper wrapped the cable twice more around her forearms and set her heels into stone and became, for a while, nothing but tension. Teo did the same at the far end. Between them the cable steadied—not safe, nothing about it was safe, but held, a fixed thing in the air, and along that fixed thing Ruth came hand over hand, upside-down world, boots on rock, until she reached the hanging man.

What happened next took ninety seconds and lasted a season. Ruth clipped Dario's harness to her own line, shouted something Casper couldn't hear over the wind and the blood in her ears, and then they were both swinging, both falling a little, both caught, and Casper and Teo hauled with their backs and their legs and their whole furious unwilling bodies, inch by inch, the cable groaning, the cracked anchor ticking its small dry countdown, until Dario's boot scraped over the lip of the far abutment and Casper grabbed his collar and dragged him onto solid stone, and a moment later Ruth came up after him, gray-faced, her palms striped red where the rope had run through them.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The bridge swayed empty over the gorge, its near cable dangling loose from a shattered anchor, the whole rig slowly settling into the slot like a dead thing coming to rest.

Dario knelt on the ballast, shaking. Then he turned to Ruth and said, in a voice stripped down to studs, "You didn't even think about it."

"No," Ruth agreed, wrapping her hands. "Thinking's for afterward."

* * *

They reached the second tunnel as the light failed.

Casper smelled it before she saw it—cold stone and old smoke and the particular mineral breath of deep rock—and then the mouth appeared in the hillside, half its arch choked with fallen rock, the rest opening into darkness like a held breath. She made everyone stop while she listened. Water dripping, far inside. Wind, faint, moving through some hidden throat of the mountain, which meant another opening somewhere, which meant the air was breathable and moving and honest.

"In," she said. "Fifty meters, no further, until I've seen the roof."

Inside, the dark was total until their eyes adjusted, and then it was a kind of gray velvet. The tunnel ran straight into the mountain, its rails long gone, its drainage ditch still doing its patient work, a thread of water whispering along the invert. Casper walked the crown of the arch with her headlamp tilted up, reading cracks the way she'd once read them for a living—which ones were old and sleeping, which ones were awake. Near the mouth, the rock was shattered and dangerous. Fifty meters in, the arch closed to a smooth bore and the stone went quiet.

"Here," she said finally. "This section was driven through good ground. It'll hold. We camp at sixty meters, away from the mouth, out of the wind."

They got a stove going, small and blue-flamed, and Emilio drank broth and slept, and Teo sat with his rifle across his knees facing the black mouth of the tunnel and the last gray light beyond it. Ruth bandaged her own hands without being asked twice. Dario sat apart, staring at nothing, and Casper watched him and thought about the look on his face when the cable dropped—not fear, exactly. Recognition. As if something had finally become real to him that he had known in theory for a long time.

She unrolled her bedroll beside Ruth's and kept her voice low. "Your hands."

"They'll work tomorrow." Ruth flexed them, winced, flexed again. "Casper. That bridge. Company-rigged, you said. Recent."

"Within the year. Maybe within the season."

"Then the company's men come this way." Ruth said it evenly, watching the firelight move on the tunnel walls. "And they cross where we crossed. Or they know another way."

Casper lay back and looked up at the invisible roof of the mountain, forty meters of stone between her and the gathering storm, and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding all day. A rigged bridge meant traffic. Traffic meant schedules. Schedules meant someone below knew exactly how far they'd come—and how far they had left.

Outside, the wind arrived at the tunnel mouth with a long, low moan, like something enormous drawing breath to speak. The first flakes came with it, ticking against the rock at the entrance, melting, then not melting.

Snow. Early, and heavy, and a day sooner than any forecast had promised.

Casper pulled her blanket to her chin and stared into the dark, listening to the mountain count.
