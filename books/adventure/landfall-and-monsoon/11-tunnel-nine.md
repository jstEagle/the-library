## 11. Tunnel Nine

The storm found them an hour after dark, and the tunnel found them twenty minutes later.

Ruth felt it before she saw it—a change in the wind's voice, the way it stopped moaning across open rock and began to echo, hollow and doubled, against stone. Casper heard it too. The older woman raised one fist without breaking stride, and the column behind her stilled like a held breath.

"Portal," Casper said quietly. "Port side. Maybe forty meters."

Ruth couldn't see anything. The snow came sideways now, thick as static, and the headlamps they'd allowed themselves were cut down to gray coins of light that showed nothing but each other's shoulders and the endless white churn beyond. But Emilio had gone rigid at Casper's words, and his hand closed on Ruth's sleeve with a grip that had nothing weak left in it.

"Tunnel Nine," he said. His voice was a ruin—three days without water when they'd found him, frostbitten fingers, a fever they'd beaten back with Ruth's last ampoules of antibiotic—but there was something underneath the ruin now. Something that sounded almost like dread. "You don't want to sleep in Tunnel Nine."

"We don't want to sleep in the storm either," Teo said behind them. The boatman's shoulder was bound where the bullet graze had opened it again on the climb; he moved stiffly, favoring his left side, but his voice was level as ever. "Pick your poison."

Emilio didn't answer. He walked faster.

The portal was worse than Ruth expected. Half the arch had come down years ago—concrete slumped into rubble like something melted, rebar hanging out of the fracture lines in rusted curls—and the mountain above it groaned under the building wind in a way that made her medical mind catalog structural failure modes she had no training for. But Casper walked straight up to the collapse, put her palm flat against the concrete as if greeting an animal, and studied the crack pattern for a long moment.

"Shear failure," she murmured, mostly to herself. "Not blast. The crown held past the portal." She turned. "We go in thirty meters, no further. Past the second rib. If anything else lets go, it'll be at the mouth."

They went in single file, and the storm shut behind them like a door.

Ruth had been in silence before—field hospitals at three in the morning, the pause before bad news—but this was different. This was engineered silence, the kind men had carved out of living rock with drills and dynamite, and it pressed on the ears. Their lamps made small trembling circles on walls streaked with soot and mineral stain. Water dripped somewhere ahead, patient as a clock. Under their boots the old rail bed still ran true into the dark, ties rotted to black lace, rails gleaming faintly where decades of drip had polished them.

Dario counted aloud as they passed the ribs—steel hoops set into the rock every few meters, furred with corrosion. At the second one Casper called a halt, and the party collapsed into the small rituals of shelter: packs down, stove lit behind a windscreen of flattened tin, wet gloves steaming over the flame. Ruth went through her kit by touch and memory, checking Emilio's dressings first, then Teo's shoulder, then the blisters and frost-nip of the others. It was work she could do blind, and she was grateful for it, because the tunnel was doing something to her chest that she didn't have a name for yet.

It was the air. She realized that halfway through rewrapping Emilio's hand. The air tasted used. Not stale exactly—tunnels breathed, Casper had said, there was always a draft somewhere—but *occupied*. As if the space remembered being full of men and hadn't quite let go of it.

"You've been here before," she said to Emilio. Not a question.

He was sitting against the rib with his bandaged hands in his lap, watching the stove flame with an expression she'd learned to read in triage lines: the flat stillness of someone deciding whether today is the day they say the thing. "Everyone who worked the extension has been here," he said finally. "Tunnel Nine is where they kept us when the weather turned. Forty men, sometimes sixty, sleeping between the rails. No ventilation worth the name. The foreman said rock was warmer than canvas." A sound that might have been a laugh. "Rock holds its cold like a grudge."

Ruth sat down beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his. In the field medicine she'd learned from her mother, you never asked a wounded man to talk. You made yourself available and waited for the wound to speak. She waited.

Around them the camp settled. Dario had taken first watch near the portal, though watch against what, none of them said—the hired crew was a day behind at most, and everyone knew it. Teo lay on his good side with his hat over his eyes, performing sleep. Casper sat apart, as she always did, her survey notebook open on her knee, pencil moving in small precise strokes. Ruth had watched her do it every night: drawing the line from memory, grade by grade, trestle by trestle, as if committing it to paper were a form of penance.

Emilio spoke again, so low she had to lean in.

"There's a place. Off the main bore. You'd walk past it a hundred times and never know." His eyes came up to hers, and in the stove light she saw how young he was under the damage—mid-twenties, maybe, with a fine-boned face that hunger had sharpened. "The old workers found it. Before my time. A gallery, a fault seam the drillers followed and then abandoned. It goes back maybe fifteen meters and opens out at the top through a crack—you can feel the weather through it, when there's weather." He swallowed. "They scratched names in there."

"Names," Ruth repeated.

"Their own names. And the names of men who died on the extension. Men the company listed as *deserted* or *returned home*." His voice cracked on the word and steadied itself with visible effort. "There are more than a hundred. My brother's is among them."

The stove hissed. Somewhere far off in the mountain, water dripped its patient count.

"Show me," Ruth said.

* * *

They told Casper, because leaving camp without telling Casper would have been unthinkable—she'd become, in ten days of ice and fear, the fixed point everything else swung around. The engineer listened with her lamp hooded, her face unreadable, then looked at Emilio for a long moment.

"You're sure you can find it in the dark?"

"I could find it dead," Emilio said.

Casper nodded once. "I'll hold the camp. Take Ruth. Keep your lamps shaded until you're inside." And then, as they turned to go, quieter: "Ruth. If there are names, get them all. Every one. However long it takes."

The side gallery's entrance was exactly as Emilio promised—invisible. A slab of fallen rock leaned against the bore wall, and behind it a gap barely wide enough for a man's shoulders, breathing a thin cold draft that smelled of snow. Emilio went through sideways, lamp held high, and Ruth followed with her camera bag pressed to her chest, the case she had guarded since Camp Ash with the same ferocity she'd have guarded a child.

Inside, the passage widened. The fault seam ran upward at a gentle angle, floor worn smooth in places by feet, and their two lamps pushed the darkness back just far enough to reveal—

Ruth stopped walking.

The walls were covered in names.

Not scattered, not casual—*dense*, layered, overlapping, climbing toward the ceiling crack where a thread of storm-light flickered with each gust. Names cut with knives and nails and the points of survey stakes. Names in careful schoolroom letters and names in furious scrawls. Some were accompanied by dates. Some by villages. One, near the floor at knee height, had a crude ship drawn beneath it, and another a bird, wings spread, flying out of the rock itself.

"Oh," Ruth said. It wasn't a word so much as air leaving her.

"They started it the first winter," Emilio said softly. He'd gone to the wall and laid his bandaged hand below a cluster of names, not touching them—an act of reverence, like approaching an altar. "A man named Aurelio Vásquez. He was a quarryman before, from the coast. He said if the company erased them, the rock would remember. So they made the rock remember." He lifted his hand to a name midway up, cut deep and confident: **MATEO EMILIO BARAHONA**. "My brother. He fell from Trestle Fourteen in the spring. They told my mother he ran away with a woman from the valley." His voice did not break. That was somehow worse. "He was seventeen."

Ruth set down her bag with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments.

She worked by lamplight, kneeling on the smooth stone, and the camera became a kind of prayer. Frame by frame she took the wall: wide shots to establish position and scale, then detail after detail, each name centered, each date legible, each crude drawing of ships and birds and hearts captured whole. Her hands were steady. Ten years of field clinics had taught her hands to be steady while everything behind them shook. The shutter clicked and clicked, a small mechanical heartbeat in the vast dark, and with every frame she found herself reading the name aloud in a whisper—not for the record, but because it seemed obscene to take a man's name into a machine without saying it.

"Aurelio Vásquez. Tomás Iriarte. Bento Alves. Kwame Mensah—" she paused at that one, surprised, and Emilio nodded behind her. "Coastal men. They brought laborers from everywhere. Wherever the recruiters could find desperate people." 

"How many languages?"

"Four that I know. Maybe more." He pointed to a run of characters she couldn't read at all, angular and beautiful, cut with a patience that must have taken hours by lamplight. "That man taught me to whistle through my hands. He died in the fever winter. They carried him out wrapped in canvas and I never learned what they wrote on the ledger. Probably *deserted*."

Ruth photographed the angular characters twice, from two angles, making sure.

Time lost its edges. The storm above them thumped and sighed through the ceiling crack, and the little gallery held its warmth like cupped hands, and Ruth moved along the walls on her knees photographing the dead. There were names of boys who had signed themselves with an X and a friend's careful rendering beside it. There was a name cut over an older name, deliberately, violently—she photographed both, and Emilio explained without being asked: the company sent men through sometimes with chisels when rumors of inspectors came, but the workers would recut the names deeper, over the gouges, so that erasure itself became part of the record. *Look how hard they tried to forget us,* the overlaid cuts said. *And look how we refused.*

Near the back of the gallery, where the fault seam narrowed, the character of the carvings changed. These were newer—fresher rock showing pale in the cuts—and they were different in another way. Not names alone. Dates of accidents. Numbers of men. One entry, in a neat bureaucratic hand that stood out among all the raw scrawls, read: **SURVEY PARTY WAS HERE — 14 JUNE — WE SAW YOU — WE WILL TELL.**

Beneath it, four signatures.

Ruth's breath caught. "This is them. Marchetti's expedition."

Emilio leaned close, reading by her lamp. "The survey team came through in June. They were supposed to be measuring the old line for closure—demolition planning, they said. Instead they spent two nights in here." His finger hovered over the neat inscription without touching it. "One of them, the leader—he had a broken arm already, from a fall on the grade—he copied names into a book. Dozens. He said paper could be burned but photographs could be printed a thousand times. They took pictures of the camps too. Of the quotas board. Of the chains in the cook shed." He looked at her, and the stove-lit calm in his face finally fractured. "Then they went higher, and the mountain ate them, and the company told everyone they were lost to weather. And the men below—" his hand swept the wall, all hundred names, all hundred refusals— "the men below started saying maybe no one was coming. Maybe no one ever comes."

Ruth lowered the camera. For a moment she could not lift it again.

She thought of Ilsa Marchetti's face in the river-town office, all grief and checkbook: *my people, my poor people, whatever it costs.* She thought of the smashed radios at Camp Ash, supplies left intact, one wounded laborer left behind as an oversight or an afterthought. She thought of Dario's nightly transmissions, and the hired crew a day behind them, and the film canisters the ambushers had demanded by name.

*Confirm nothing survived.* Casper had said it three nights ago, and Ruth had wanted to believe it was paranoia. Standing in a room full of the erased, she believed it now with her whole clinical, unsentimental heart. The expedition had found this gallery. Had written a promise on this wall. And the promise had been answered with rifles.

"We will tell," she read aloud. Her voice came out rough. "That's what they wrote. We will tell."

"Then it falls to us," Emilio said simply. "The rock remembers. But rock can't testify."

Ruth raised the camera and photographed the survey party's inscription, and the signatures, and then—because it seemed right, because the dead deserved witnesses—she photographed the whole wall once more, end to end, slow and deliberate, a final pass like a doctor's last round.

When she finished, her knees ached and her eyes burned and the storm overhead had settled into a steady roar. She sat back on her heels in the lamplight, surrounded by a hundred lives cut into stone, and understood that something in her had changed direction. She had come up this river as a medic on a rescue mission, her conscience comfortably parked in the competence of her hands. There was no parking it anymore. Whatever waited for them below—Marchetti's lawyers, Marchetti's guns, the whole rotten arithmetic of the railroad—she was done pretending this was about survival. It was about testimony. It had been since Camp Ash. Since the first marker. Since the beginning.

"Ruth." Emilio's voice, very quiet. "There's one more thing."

He led her to the narrowest point of the seam, where the rock pinched together, and angled his lamp low. At knee height, half-hidden behind a shelf of stone, was a fresh scratch—days old, not months. Pale rock dust still clung to the cut.

Letters, shaky but legible:

**WE ARE IN THE HIGH BASIN. 9 ALIVE. FOLLOW THE WATER.**

Below it, a small drawn arrow pointing up and east, toward the roof of the range.

Ruth stared at it, her pulse suddenly loud in her ears. Alive. Nine of them alive, starving, a day's climb above them in country that was filling with snow even now—and behind them, closing fast, men whose job was to make sure that message never reached anyone.

She grabbed Emilio's arm. "Get your coat. We have to show Casper."

Behind them the lamps swung, and a hundred names watched them go, keeping their long vigil in the rock, waiting—as they had waited through every erased year—for someone, at last, to carry them down the mountain.
