## 12. Dario's Confession

The tunnel breathed.

That was the word Dario kept circling back to as he sat against the cold rock wall, watching the firelight gutter in the half-collapsed gallery. Air moved through the bore in long slow pulses, in at the south mouth and out at the rubble-choked north end, and it carried the smell of wet stone and old creosote and, faintly, iron. The mountain inhaled and exhaled around them, patient as something that had all the time in the world and no interest at all in what they did inside its throat.

He had not slept. None of them had, really, but he had slept least, because he had spent the whole night listening to Emilio's voice in the side gallery, low and cracked, naming men. And because the radio was in his pack, three feet from his hip, and it weighed more than the pack did. It weighed more than he did.

The others had gone down the passage an hour ago to photograph the names again in better light—Ruth with her camera and her careful hands, Casper with a lantern held high, Emilio shuffling along between them like a man being returned to his own funeral. Teo had stayed at the fire, cleaning the wound on his forearm, saying nothing, which was its own kind of accusation. Dario had volunteered to keep watch at the tunnel mouth. Nobody had argued. Nobody, he suspected, had wanted his company.

He turned the radio over in his hands. Marchetti's mark was still on the casing, a small gold sticker with her initials, *IM*, elegant as a wax seal. He had peeled at the corner of it with his thumbnail until the adhesive lifted, and then he had pressed it back down, and now he pressed at it again, and it would not come cleanly, and he hated it, and he could not stop.

*You're my eyes up there, Dario. That's all. Eyes.*

His aunt's voice was very clear in his memory. She had said it on the dock at Puerto Vado, the morning they loaded the launch, her hand on the back of his neck the way she'd done since he was a boy, warm and proprietary, a hand that felt like belonging and was actually a leash.

*The survey team went quiet and I need to know what happened to them before the insurance people and the railway board turn it into a circus. You'll report positions, conditions, anything you find. Twice a day, channel nine, the code we agreed. That's all. Eyes.*

He had believed her. That was the thing he could not get past, the thing that sat in his chest like swallowed stone: he had believed her, and it had felt good to be believed *in*. Dario Marchetti, twenty-three years old, the nephew who had washed out of two universities and one sailing certification, finally given a job that mattered. He had keyed the transmitter at the junction with the fresh survey marker and felt like an explorer. He had keyed it again at Camp Ash, reading the coordinates off the burned camp's remains, and felt like a rescuer. He had keyed it yesterday on the switchback grade, whispering into the wind while Casper scouted ahead, and he had told himself the tremor in his voice was the cold.

*Positions. Conditions. Anything you find.*

And her men had always seemed to know exactly where the route ran.

* * *

He heard them coming back before he saw the light. Ruth's step was even and quiet; Casper's was quieter; Emilio dragged one foot, a small rhythmic scrape that echoed down the bore like a spoon against a pot. Dario shoved the radio deep into his pack and stood, too fast, and the fire spat a spark and Teo looked up at him from across the coals with those flat riverman's eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"More names," Ruth said, coming into the firelight. Her voice was hoarse. She set her camera down and rubbed her hands together over the coals, and Dario saw that they were shaking, and that she was not embarrassed about it. "Forty-one. Emilio remembered eleven more that the rock had spalled over. He spelled them for me."

"Forty-one," Teo repeated, to the fire.

"He knew some of their mothers' names," Ruth said. "He wanted them all written down. He said—" She stopped. She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment, and when she lowered them she was composed again, a medic's composure, the kind that is built rather than found. "He said nobody up here was ever a number until the company made them one."

Casper came last, lantern in hand, and she did not look at Dario at all. She had not looked at him properly since the washout on the switchback grade, since the rope bridge, since— he could not fix the moment it changed, and that was the worst of it. Somewhere along the grade her eyes had stopped seeing a frightened boy and started seeing something else, and he did not know which transmission had done it.

She crouched by the fire, fed it a split of tunnel timber—old support lagging, dry as paper—and said, without preamble, "Storm's coming down the north face. We move at first light, no later. Emilio can't walk fast, so we walk slow, but we walk."

"We should wait out the worst of it here," Dario said. "The tunnel's shelter. The pass won't be."

"The pass won't be," Casper agreed, "but the men behind us will be, and they'll be moving in it, because they're paid to move in it. We're not waiting here to be found in a hole with one way out." She looked up then, finally, and her eyes went to him, and past him, and to the pack beside him, and stayed there one second too long. "Get some sleep, Dario. You're on second watch."

He was not on second watch. He had never been on second watch. Teo had first watch, Casper second, Ruth third, an arrangement made days ago and never altered, and Casper knew it, and he knew she knew it, and the sentence hung in the tunnel air like smoke with nowhere to go.

* * *

He did try to sleep. He lay on his bedroll with the fire dying to a red murmur and the mountain breathing over him, and he ran the arithmetic he had been running for three days, the arithmetic that always came out the same and always changed nothing.

The expedition had gone silent on the third of the month. His aunt had come to Puerto Vado on the ninth. She had already had the route mapped when she hired Casper—Dario had seen the chart on her table, the closed line inked in red, the pass marked, the distances computed. You did not compute distances along a line nobody had surveyed in years. You did not have a chart unless someone had given it to you, and the only people who could have given it to her were the people who had built the new extension through country the maps still called wilderness.

And the survey team had photographed the camps. Emilio had said it plainly, two nights ago, his burned hands wrapped in Ruth's dressings: *They came with a camera like a priest with a book. The overseer went mad. They ran them up the pass in the snow with nothing. The leader's leg was broke and still they made him walk until he couldn't.*

His aunt had known. Not suspected—*knew*. Knew about the quotas and the chains and the graves that Emilio's voice had scratched out of the dark last night. Knew, and had assembled this party of strangers and her own nephew, and sent them up the closed line after the evidence, and had asked for positions twice a day.

Not so she could send help.

So she could send *them*.

Dario lay in the dark and felt the shape of it settle over him, the whole architecture of it, load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall, and he understood at last why Casper looked at him the way she did. She had worked it out days ago. She had been counting his absences the way she counted trestles. And she had said nothing, had let him keep transmitting, had let the line stay open—why? To see who answered? To learn what his aunt's men knew? Or because she was waiting for him to say it himself, and every hour he didn't was another hour he spent on the wrong side of a line he could still, barely, cross back over.

The fire ticked. Somewhere deep in the rock, water found a path and dripped, unhurried, the way water had dripped in this tunnel while forty-one men scratched their names and were marched out into the snow.

He thought about his aunt's hand on his neck.

He thought about the eleven men on Viaduct 7, whose names he'd only ever known as a number in a newspaper, and about Casper Sandoval, who carried them like ballast in her hull, and about how his aunt had bought that number for the price of a debt and spent it like small change.

He sat up.

* * *

Teo was at the tunnel mouth, a silhouette against the grey pre-dawn, when Dario crossed the cold floor of the gallery with the radio in both hands. The others were stirring; Ruth was already awake, folding her dressings with the economy of long habit. Casper stood at the edge of the fire pit, checking the loads, and when Dario stopped in front of her she straightened and looked at him, and did not help him, and did not look away.

"I need to say something," Dario said. His voice came out wrong, high, and he made himself start again. "Before we move. Before we're on the grade with no cover. You all need to hear it from me and not— not find it some other way."

Ruth went still. Teo turned from the tunnel mouth. Emilio, propped against the wall with his bandaged hands in his lap, watched with the terrible patience of a man who had already survived the worst thing and was only interested in what came next.

Dario knelt and took the radio out of his pack and set it on the stone between them, the gold sticker catching the firelight.

"I've been transmitting since the junction," he said. "Positions. Twice a day, then more. Channel nine, coded. My aunt—Ilsa Marchetti—she gave me the set before we left. She told me it was so she'd know we were safe." He made himself look at each of them. "That was a lie. I've known it was a lie for three days and I told myself it wasn't, and I'm telling you now because men are following those coordinates. Her men. Hired men. They were behind us on the switchback grade and they'll be ahead of us on the descent, and they're not coming for the survey team."

"They're coming for the film," Casper said. It was not a question.

"The film and the ledgers and anyone who carried them out." His throat closed and he forced through it. "She knew about the camps. Before the expedition ever went upriver, she knew. The disappearance wasn't a mystery to her, it was an *opportunity*. If the team died up there, the photographs died with them, and the extension opens in the spring and nobody ever asks what the labor cost. And if the team lived—" he gestured at the radio, at the whole grey morning, "—then she needed to know exactly where the evidence was, every hour, so her men could take it and bury it and bury whoever was holding it. I'm the reason they've been a day behind us instead of a week. I'm the reason they knew the route. I did it because she put her hand on my neck and told me I mattered, and I wanted it to be true so badly I didn't look at what I was carrying."

The tunnel breathed. The fire popped. Nobody spoke for a long moment, and Dario knelt there on the cold stone with his confession sitting between them like a dropped tool, and waited to be judged.

It was Emilio who spoke first, in his cracked whisper, and what he said was: "The names. Did you tell her the names?"

"No," Dario said. "Never. I didn't know them until last night, and I'll never tell them to her."

Emilio nodded slowly, as if a ledger had been balanced, if only in one column.

Ruth said, quietly, "The wounded man at Camp Ash. The one who didn't make it. You transmitted from that camp."

"Yes."

She looked at him a moment longer, and then she picked up her camera bag and began checking her dressings, because she was a medic and there was work, and her silence was not forgiveness but it was not dismissal either, and Dario found he could breathe inside it.

Teo came over and stood above the radio. He looked at it the way he looked at dead water, at a hole in a river where the current went down and did not come back. Then he spat, deliberately, onto the stone beside it. "Your aunt's men shot at us yesterday," he said. "Grazed me. If they'd shot straight, Ruth would be carrying one more body down this mountain, and it would be on your neck along with the rest."

"I know."

"Do you." Teo crouched, eye level with him, close enough that Dario could smell woodsmoke and blood on him. "Then say what you're going to do. Not what you feel. What you *do*."

Dario looked at the radio. The gold sticker. *IM.* He had pressed at that corner for three nights and never finished peeling it, because finishing it would make it real.

He picked the set up, turned it over, and opened the battery compartment with his thumbnail. The cells slid out into his palm, cold and heavy. He stood, walked to the tunnel mouth, to the grey light and the sound of the river far below, and threw them as far out over the drop as his arm would send them. He heard nothing hit. Then he carried the radio itself to the fire pit, and knelt, and drove the heel of his boot down on the casing until the housing cracked, and again until the circuit board inside broke with a sound like a small dry bone, and he kept breaking it until there was nothing in his hands but a shattered black shell and a gold sticker, curled at the corner, finally peeling free.

He set the sticker on the stone and stepped back.

"She'll know the silence means we found something," Casper said. She had watched all of it without moving, arms folded, her face unreadable as the rock around them. "She'll know you turned. Which means she stops being careful. Her men stop following and start closing, and they'll pick the ground—probably the washout on the lower grade, the only place the treeline pinches tight enough to funnel us." She was already calculating as she spoke; he could see it moving behind her eyes, the old surveyor's habit, terrain as arithmetic. "We have maybe two hours before they stop waiting on your reports and start trusting their own eyes."

"Then we'd better be gone in one," Teo said.

Casper shouldered her pack and looked at Dario one last time, and what was in her face now was not trust—it was too early for trust, maybe it would always be too early—but it was not contempt either. It was the look of a woman re-adding a sum and finding, to her own surprise, that the total had changed.

"You'll walk in the middle," she said. "Where Teo can see you and I can see you. And Dario—when they come, and they will come, you don't hide behind any of us. You hear me? Whatever happens on that grade, you stand where the shooting is."

"I hear you," Dario said, and found that he meant it, and that meaning it felt, for the first time in three days, like standing on solid rock.

They doused the fire and moved out of Tunnel Nine into the grey and the wind, and far below them, down the switchback grade, in the pinch of the washout where the treeline closed like a hand, men who had stopped waiting for reports were already setting their positions.
