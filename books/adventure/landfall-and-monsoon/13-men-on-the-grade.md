## 13. Men on the Grade

Teo smelled the river before he heard it, and heard it before he saw the washout—a low, guttural roar where the grade dropped away into a scar of broken rock and white water. He held up a fist and the line behind him stopped, the way a good crew stops: without question, without sound. Six days on the mountain had taught them his hand signals, and he had learned theirs. Ruth's breathing, always measured, even at altitude. Emilio's limp, quieter each morning, pride rebuilding the tendon faster than the flesh. Casper's silence, which was not the silence of a woman thinking but of a woman listening.

Dario's silence was different. Dario's silence had a frequency to it, like a wire humming before it snaps.

They came around the shoulder of the grade in single file, the mountain falling away on their right into a gorge the color of gunmetal, and Teo saw the washout all at once—fifty meters of the old railroad bed simply gone, shrugged into the river by some spring flood years ago, the ties hanging out of the cut earth like ribs from a split carcass. Beyond it the grade climbed again toward a dark seam of tunnel, and above the tunnel the peaks stood white to the waist, wearing this year's snow like a warning no one had heeded.

And on the far side of the washout, in the shadow of the tunnel mouth, men were waiting.

Teo counted without moving his eyes. Four visible. Probably two more in the rocks. They had rifles slung casual across their backs, the way hired men carry weapons they've been paid to use but hope not to fire where the paymaster can hear about it. They had a fire going. They had coffee. They had been there a while.

"They knew the route," Casper said beside him, her voice pitched under the river's roar so only the front of the line could hear. "They didn't follow us. They came up the other way, from the valley. They knew where the washout was."

"Everyone knows where the washout is," Teo said. "It's the only way across."

"No." Her jaw was tight. "Everyone knows where the washout is *now*. It wasn't on any map I ever drew."

Behind them, Emilio made a small sound. Teo turned his head just enough to see the laborer's face, and what he saw there was not fear of the men with rifles. It was recognition. Emilio had seen those faces before, or faces like them, in a camp higher up the mountain where quotas were met with chains and the dead were carried down at night so the morning shift wouldn't count them.

"Back," Teo said. "Slow. Into the trees."

They were thirty meters from the treeline when the voice came, carried flat and clear across the gorge by the wind off the snowfields.

"You can stop there."

The man who spoke stepped to the edge of the washout, and Teo took his measure the way he took the measure of water: depth, current, what lay under the surface. Forty, maybe. Solid build going soft. A city man's boots on mountain ground. He held his rifle by the stock, pointed at the sky, which was its own kind of message—we don't need to aim yet.

"Six of you," the man called. "We can see the sixth. The one limping. Come out where we can talk."

Ruth had shifted without appearing to move, her body between Emilio and the rifles. Casper stood with her hands loose at her sides, and Teo, who had watched her read water, rock, and weather for a week, watched her now read men.

"What do you want?" she called back. No fear in it. That was the frightening part.

"The canisters. Film. And any ledgers, notebooks, papers the survey team was carrying. Hand them across the washout and you walk on. All of you. Even the limper."

"And if we don't?"

The man smiled, and it was a smile Teo had seen on the faces of men negotiating passage through dangerous water—confident, transactional, already pricing the loss.

"Then we come across and take them, and we can't promise what happens to what we don't want."

* * *

They fell back into the trees and stood in a knot among the pines, breath steaming, the river's roar muffled now to a pulse beneath the needle-quiet. Teo crouched, drew in the duff with one finger: the washout, the tunnel, the positions of the men, the treeline, the slope above.

"Six," he said. "Maybe seven. Two rifles we can see well enough to worry about. They'll have a man watching the gorge in case we try to climb down and ford it. We can't ford it. Nobody fords that."

"Then we go up," Dario said.

Everyone looked at him. Six days of Dario's enthusiasm had worn the others down to a tolerance that was not quite affection, and now the tolerance itself was gone, because everyone in the circle had done the same arithmetic Teo had done and arrived at the same sum.

"You've been talking to them," Ruth said. It was not a question. It had stopped being a question somewhere around the ninth day, or maybe the fourth, and only the saying of it was new.

Dario's face went through three expressions in as many seconds—guilt, defiance, and something underneath both that might, in a better man, have been the beginning of shame. "I've been reporting our position. To my aunt. That's not the same as—"

"They're here because of you," Casper said quietly. "Or they were coming anyway, and you made sure they'd be waiting at the one place we couldn't avoid."

"I didn't know they'd—"

"You didn't ask," Teo said. "That's worse."

Dario's mouth opened and closed. The mountain wind moved through the pines above them, and somewhere down the grade one of the hired men laughed at something, a short ugly bark of sound that made Emilio flinch.

"The canisters," Ruth said. "The film. If we give it up, the expedition's evidence burns with it. Every name Emilio gave us. Every photograph. The ledgers."

"And if we don't," Teo said, "we fight six armed men across fifty meters of open rock with a medic, a boy, a limping witness, and a boatman with a boat knife."

He looked at Casper. He had been looking at Casper more than he liked lately, and not because she was a woman—he'd run rapids beside women who out-thought him and out-endured him and one who had saved his life with a spare oar and a mouthful of curses. He looked at her because she was the only one in the circle who had stood on this grade before, who knew it the way he knew the river, and because her face, when she looked at the washout, held something older than the decision at hand.

"There's a way across," she said. "Not the washout. Above it. The old construction spur—there's a cut in the rock, maybe four hundred meters up. The builders used it to haul stone before the main grade was finished. It's overgrown, and the footing is bad, and if any of you fall you will fall four hundred meters into the gorge, and I will not be able to do anything about it." She paused. "But they don't know it's there. And they can't cover it and the washout both."

"Can *you* cover it?" Teo asked. "Can we cross before they figure it out?"

"No," Casper said. "We can't cross before they figure it out. So someone has to make them look at the washout while we climb."

The silence in the trees had a shape to it then, and Teo recognized the shape, because he had stood inside it once before, ten years ago, on a river in flood with a rope in his hands and a boy in the water, and the arithmetic had been the same: someone stays, someone goes, and the one who stays pays the difference.

"I'll stay," Teo said.

* * *

He would not let them argue long, because there was nothing to argue. He was the fastest on broken ground—he had spent thirty years reading rock the way other men read print—and he was the only one among them who had ever been shot at, which he did not mention, because the story of how he'd been shot at was not one that inspired confidence. He took the boat knife, the flare pistol from Ruth's kit, and two of the remaining flares, and Ruth pressed a roll of bandage into his jacket with a look that said, more plainly than words, *you will come back to have this needed of you.*

Casper stood last. She didn't thank him. She looked at him the way she looked at a trestle she was deciding whether to trust, and then she said, "Don't be a hero. Be a nuisance. Nuisance survives."

"I have been a nuisance my whole life," Teo said. "It is the one thing I am excellent at."

Dario tried to say something to him—Teo saw the boy's throat work, saw the words assemble and fail—and Teo put a hand on the back of his neck, brief and hard, the way he'd gripped the shoulders of green deckhands before their first rapid. Whatever the boy had done, he'd have to carry it up the mountain himself. But the grip said: *you will carry it, and you will live, and that is not nothing.*

Then they were gone into the trees, climbing, and Teo turned downhill alone.

* * *

He came out of the treeline a hundred meters below the washout, in the open, walking, hands visible, the flare pistol tucked at the small of his back under his jacket. The morning sun had cleared the eastern ridge and lay flat and white on the broken rock, and Teo walked into it like a man walking into a room where he already knew everyone owed him money.

The leader—the city boots, the transactional smile—straightened when he saw him. "Where are the others?"

"Gone up," Teo said. "You'll never find them in the trees. But I'll tell you where they're going, for a price."

The smile again, wider now. "And what's your price?"

"Your coffee," Teo said. "I've been drinking snowmelt for a week. It's barbaric."

It was, he would think later, the best lie he had ever told, because it was the only one that made the hired men laugh. Laughter loosens men. Laughter brings them a step closer than they meant to come. Two of them drifted toward the fire, and the leader waved Teo forward to the edge of the washout, and for perhaps forty seconds Teo stood among armed men drinking coffee that was, he had to admit, very good, and telling them about the river below in the voice he used for passengers—loud, easy, full of the names of rapids and the weights of boats—and all the while he was listening up-slope, past the leader's shoulder, to the treeline, waiting for the sound of six people climbing toward a cut in the rock that might not even be there anymore.

The first rifle shot came from behind him.

Not at him—from the trees below the grade, where there should have been no one, and Teo understood in the same instant that Casper had not left him alone at all, that she had left Ruth, or left Dario, or left some part of the plan she had never spoken aloud, because that was what she did: she read the water and she did not tell you what she saw until you were already through it.

The hired men scattered to cover. The leader's smile was gone. And Teo did the thing he had come down the mountain to do, the only thing, the whole of his job: he made noise. He shouted, he ran along the edge of the washout waving his arms, he fired the flare pistol into the rocks at their feet where the phosphorus spat and burned white against the stone, and the hired men, who had been paid to intercept a party of six and were now being charged by what sounded and looked like considerably more, did what hired men do.

Two of them fired.

Teo felt the first before he heard it—a hot slap along his ribs, as if the mountain itself had cuffed him—and then the crack of the rifle rolled up the gorge and the second shot went high, splitting the air above his head with a sound like a struck wire. He went down behind a fallen tie, the old creosote smell thick in his nose, and lay with his hand pressed to his side and looked at his palm, which was red but not running, a graze, a price, not a bill.

Above him, faint through the trees, he heard the sound he had been waiting for: a single low whistle, Casper's signal, three notes, *we're across.*

* * *

He gave them ten minutes, because ten minutes was what the climb would take, and then he began to move, low and sideways, from cover to cover, away from the washout and up into the trees, and the hired men fired twice more at the place he had been rather than the place he was, because hired men shoot at noise, and Teo had been making a great deal of it. The blood along his ribs had cooled to a sticky line. His breath came in short hitches. He climbed anyway, hand over hand, the way he had climbed a hundred riverbanks, and the trees closed behind him, and the sound of the rifles faded below the sound of the river, and then below the sound of his own heart.

He found them forty minutes on, in a fold of the slope where a spring had cut a green seam through the scree. Ruth had the bandage off his ribs before he'd finished sitting down, her fingers quick and unsentimental, and Emilio held a canteen to his mouth, and Dario stood apart with his head down like a boy at a funeral.

"You came back," Ruth said.

"I said I was a nuisance," Teo said. "Nuisances come back."

Casper crouched at the edge of the fold, looking back down through the trees toward the washout, where the smoke of the hired men's fire still rose thin and straight in the still air. Her face was the face she wore at the water's edge before a bad rapid—not afraid, but counting.

"They'll cross the washout within the hour," she said. "After that they'll pick up our trail, and they're fresh and we're not. We have the tunnel ahead—Tunnel Nine, if my memory holds—and after the tunnel the grade climbs out of the trees, and there is nowhere left to hide."

Teo pressed his hand to the bandage at his ribs and felt his own blood, warm through the cloth, the literal arithmetic of the morning. Six days ago this had been a rescue. Then it had been a search. Then a flight. Now it was a race with a price tag nailed to every meter of it, and he had just paid the first installment.

"So we don't stop," he said.

Casper turned and looked at him, and then up the grade, where the mountain went on rising into the white.

"No," she said. "We don't stop. And Teo—" her voice dropped, and for the first time since he'd known her, the engineer's certainty in it cracked, just slightly, like ice under a boot. "What's ahead of us is worse than men with rifles. I built the line over this mountain. I know what's ahead of us."

She stood, and shouldered her pack, and the party rose with her, and they climbed.
