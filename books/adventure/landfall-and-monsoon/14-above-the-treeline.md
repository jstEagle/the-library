## 14. Above the Treeline

The treeline did not end so much as surrender. One moment they were moving through stunted pines bent like old men leaning into wind that never stopped; the next the trees were gone and there was only rock, and sky the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges, and the grade climbing ahead of them in long scarred switchbacks toward a saddle that looked, from below, like a wound in the mountain's shoulder.

Casper stopped where the last tree stood—a pine no taller than she was, twisted by forty years of storms into something more knot than wood—and let the party come up to her.

"From here on," she said, "everything I know about this mountain is thirty years old."

"Then it's better than what the rest of us know," Ruth said, "which is nothing."

Teo said nothing. He stood with his hand pressed flat against his graze-dressed shoulder, looking back down the way they'd come, down the gray-green folds of the valley where, somewhere among the shadows, Marchetti's men were climbing.

* * *

They had left the washout at first light, moving fast through the trees while Teo's blood was still wet on the gravel. The ambush had cost them an hour of running and most of their composure. It had also cost Dario his rifle—he'd dropped it in the scramble into the treeline—and Emilio half his strength, because the laborer had taken a fall in the rush downhill and now walked with one arm cradled against his ribs, refusing help with a stubbornness Casper recognized from her own mirror.

Now they climbed. That was all there was. The grade rose in switchbacks cut by survey crews before Casper was born, stone retaining walls still holding in places, slumped into scree in others. She had walked this line once as a young engineer with a theodolite and a clipboard and a crew who called her *la ingeniera* without irony, and she had stood at the top of it and thought: *this is the finest work I will ever do.* Then Viaduct 7 had happened, and she had sworn never to set foot on any of it again, and here she was, thirty years later, counting her steps against the wind.

The air thinned as they climbed. By midday Emilio was stopping every hundred meters, hands on knees, breath sawing. Ruth stayed beside him without being asked, matching his pace, and Casper adjusted the whole column around the two of them without announcing it—shortening the legs between rests, choosing the gentler side of every rise.

"You're slowing us down," Dario said, not cruelly, just tiredly, somewhere past the fourth switchback. "We should send him back."

"He can't go back," Casper said. "There are men behind us who shot at us this morning. There is nowhere for him to go but up."

"He'll die up here."

"People have died everywhere on this line," Casper said. "It doesn't get to choose for him."

Dario shut his mouth. But Casper saw the look he gave Emilio—the arithmetic in it, the cold little ledger of survival—and she filed it away. Fear did that to people. She knew. She'd spent three decades doing the same math about herself.

* * *

By afternoon the weather turned from threat to fact. The cloud deck that had been building over the saddle all day came down to meet them, and suddenly they were walking inside the cloud, visibility shrinking to twenty meters, then ten, then the length of a thrown stone. The temperature fell with it. Casper could feel the snow in the air before it came—that particular pressure, that hush, the way sound went flat and close.

She called a halt at a ruined culvert where the grade crossed a dry channel, a stone arch deep enough to give them shelter from the wind if they packed into it.

"We rest here until the front passes or breaks," she said.

"And if it doesn't break?" Teo asked.

"Then we climb through it. Snow this early is usually wet and heavy. It'll slow them down worse than it slows us—they're carrying rifles and they don't know the grade." She didn't add: *and they don't love this mountain.* She didn't need to. Everyone in the culvert understood that the difference between them and the men below wasn't equipment. It was that Casper Sandoval had surveyed every meter of this route and had hated it, admired it, and memorized it in the same season of her life, and that kind of knowledge doesn't fade. It ferments.

They ate cold. Rice and dried meat and the last of the hard bread, portioned out by Ruth with a nurse's unsentimental precision. Casper watched her dole it out and did her own arithmetic and didn't like the answer. Three days of food, maybe four if they stretched it, and the descent on the far side would take two days even in good conditions, and these were not good conditions, and Emilio was eating everyone else's share of strength just by staying upright.

Dario sat apart, knees drawn up, staring out at the white nothing. Twice Casper caught his hand drifting toward his jacket pocket, where the radio lived, and twice he pulled it back empty-handed. He had sworn off transmitting after the washout—had handed the radio over, actually, and Casper carried it now against her spine like a second knife—but the habit of reaching for it hadn't died yet. Habits like that took longer than confessions.

"Get some sleep," she told him. "All of you. We move when the light does."

Nobody slept well. The wind found the culvert's mouth and played it like a bottle, a low moaning note that never quite resolved. Casper sat at the entrance with her back to the stone and watched the snow begin—fat wet flakes at first, then finer, drier, the kind that meant real cold coming—and she counted switchbacks in her head the way other people counted sheep. Eleven to the saddle. Fourteen down the far side to the basin where, if the expedition leader's note had been true, the lost survey team might still be alive.

Might be. In this. With whatever food they'd managed to carry.

She closed her eyes and let herself think, for exactly one minute, about turning back. Then she opened them again, because the minute was up, and because the men below were still climbing.

* * *

Dawn came gray and blind. The snow had laid six inches on the grade overnight and was still falling, lighter now, but steady, the sky a single unbroken sheet of pearl. Casper broke trail. Someone had to, and she was the lightest of the able-bodied, and besides—if the mountain was going to kill someone today, she wanted it to have to work its way through her first.

The switchbacks were treacherous. The old retaining walls shed snow unevenly, and twice she postholed through a drift into the gap where a wall had collapsed, sinking to her hip before catching herself on bare stone. Behind her she heard the others learning the same lesson, one curse at a time.

At the seventh switchback the wind found them properly. It came off the saddle in long gusts that hit like open hands, and Casper learned quickly to read them—to feel the pressure change a half-second before each gust and turn her shoulder into it, calling "Hold!" over her own collar so the column could brace. It was exhausting work, leading into wind. Her thighs burned. Her lungs felt scoured. Somewhere under her ribs, an old injury from the collapse—the one the doctors had called minor, the one that had never once been minor—lit up with every step, a hot wire pulled tight.

On the ninth switchback, Dario snapped.

He'd been carrying Emilio's pack since morning along with his own, and he was younger than the rest of them but not young enough for this, and when Emilio stumbled on the icy ballast and brought both of them down, Dario came up shouting.

"I can't—I'm done, do you understand? We're going to die on this mountain for a bunch of photographs and some dead men's names scratched in a rock, and for what? For *what?*"

Ruth moved toward him, palms out, the voice she used on feverish patients already in her mouth. But it was Emilio who answered. The laborer got to his feet slowly, holding his ribs, and looked at Dario with an expression that had no anger in it at all, which somehow made it worse.

"My brother's name is in that rock," Emilio said. "Second gallery. You saw it. Tomás Reyes. He was nineteen." He paused, breathing. "You want to know what the photographs are worth? They're worth him having died for something other than a quota."

Dario stared at him. The wind filled the silence.

Then Dario bent down, picked up both packs, and shouldered them, and said nothing, and started climbing. And Casper, watching him go, understood that something had shifted—not resolved, nothing on this mountain would resolve—but shifted, the way ballast shifts under a rail, settling into a truer line.

* * *

They made the saddle at what Casper's body insisted was late afternoon and the white sky refused to confirm. Eleven switchbacks. She'd counted every one.

The saddle itself was a shallow notch between two shoulders of black rock, drifted deep with snow, and beyond it the far side dropped away into cloud. Somewhere down there, fourteen switchbacks below, was the basin. Somewhere in the basin, if luck held, were starving people with film canisters and ledgers and a leader with a broken leg or worse.

Casper stood at the top of everything she'd ever surveyed and let the wind try to take her hat, her breath, her balance. Below her boots, buried under thirty years of snow and silence, ran the rails she'd helped lay—the best work of her life, abandoned, closed, erased from the maps. She had sworn never to walk it again. She had kept that oath for three decades, and keeping it had cost her exactly nothing except everything.

"All right," she said, turning back to the others. They were a ragged line against the white: Ruth steady, Teo gray-faced and silent with pain he wouldn't name, Dario hollow-eyed under two packs, Emilio upright through sheer refusal. Strangers, all of them, five weeks ago. People she'd have crossed the street to avoid, in Puerto Vado, in her other life. "One hour of light left, maybe less. We descend four switchbacks to the third tunnel—it's intact, I checked it twice in '91—and we shelter there tonight. Tomorrow we reach the basin."

"And if the expedition's not there?" Dario asked.

Casper looked at him. "Then tomorrow we keep looking. Nobody stops. Nobody gets left. That's the whole plan. That's always been the whole plan."

Teo smiled for the first time since the washout—a small, cracked thing, mostly teeth and exhaustion. "Listen to her," he said to the others. "Two months ago she wouldn't tell us her real name."

"Two months ago you were charging me triple to ride your boat," Casper said.

"Quadruple. And worth it."

They laughed—thin, gasping laughter, the kind that lives at high altitude and low hope—but they laughed, and Casper felt something settle in her chest that she had no better word for than *crew.* Not strangers anymore. Whatever waited below in the cloud, whatever Marchetti's men were doing behind them, whatever the ledgers would cost when they finally reached the valley and the law and the truth—these four were hers now, and she was theirs, and there was no version of the descent where she left any of them on the mountain.

She turned to lead them down.

And then she stopped, because the wind had shifted, and under the wind—from far below, muffled by snow and distance but unmistakable to anyone who'd spent her life listening to mountains—came the sound of voices. More than one. Climbing.

Not from behind.

From ahead. From the basin side of the saddle, coming up the far switchbacks, straight toward the third tunnel. Toward the only shelter on this face of the range.

Marchetti's men hadn't followed them over the top. They'd gone around.
