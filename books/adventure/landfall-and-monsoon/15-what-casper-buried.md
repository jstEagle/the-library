## 15. What Casper Buried

The storm had been talking to them all day.

It spoke first in the west, a bruised violet line along the shoulders of the range where the sky should have been clean and cold and blue. It spoke again at midday through the pines, a low shifting hush that made every tree lean the same direction, like a congregation turning toward a door. By the time they found the shelf of flat ground below the last ridge—a fold of granite sheltered on three sides, the kind of place Casper's old survey crews had called a *pocket*—the wind had found its voice entirely, and it was saying what it always said up here, in the language of pressure and cold: *soon.*

They got the tents up with numb hands. Teo built the fire small, the way he did everything, feeding it slivers of dead pine until it burned hot and low behind a windbreak of stones. His graze had been dressed that morning with the last of Ruth's clean gauze, and he held his arm against his ribs like something borrowed. Emilio sat closest to the flame, palms out, fingers still too stiff to curl properly around the tin of tea Ruth pressed into them.

Above them, the ridge they would have to cross tomorrow stood black against the failing light, its spine furred white already. The pass was closing. Everyone could do the arithmetic. Nobody said it aloud.

Casper took the first watch because she could not sit still, and because sitting still meant thinking, and thinking meant the thing she had been carrying since the washout, since Tunnel Nine, since the marker at the junction—since Puerto Vado, if she was honest, since the day she'd stepped off a freight car with a false name in her mouth and eleven graves behind her.

She walked the perimeter of the pocket with her compass open, not because she needed it but because the needle gave her something to look at. North by northwest, declination four degrees east, the way it had been when she was twenty-six years old and sure of everything except herself.

"You'll wear a groove in the rock."

Ruth's voice, quiet, from the firelight. Casper hadn't heard her rise. The medic moved like that—soundless, watchful, always appearing exactly where the hurt was thickest.

"Old habit," Casper said. "Checking our back trail."

"At this hour there's nothing to check but weather." Ruth came to stand beside her at the edge of the firelight, arms crossed against the cold, breath smoking. Below them the valley they'd climbed out of was a black trench full of cloud. "Emilio's sleeping. Dario's pretending to sleep. Teo is actually asleep, which after today he has earned twice over."

Casper said nothing.

"And you," Ruth went on, "are standing out here doing sums I can see from across the fire. So let me save you some time. Whatever you're carrying, it weighs more than your pack, and you've been carrying it since before the river. I've watched enough people bleed to know the difference between a wound and a weight."

The wind gusted, flung a few early flakes against Casper's cheek—dry, granular, the snow of high altitude, more sand than feather. She thought about lying. She had gotten very good at lying; it had been her trade for six years, longer than engineering ever was. *I'm fine. Go to sleep.* Two words and a smile, and Ruth would go back to the fire, and the night would pass, and the stone in Casper's chest would keep its silence one more day, and then another, forever.

But down in Tunnel Nine, men had scratched their names into rock with whatever they had—nails, belt buckles, fingernails maybe—because they wanted someone, someday, to know they had existed. *Mateo Rivas. A. Quintero. J.M. — for my mother.* They had refused to be anonymous even when anonymity was all the world allowed them.

And she had chosen hers. Chosen it like a coat, worn it like armor, hidden inside it like a hole.

"No," Casper said. "You're right. Sit down. Both of us. This will take a while, and my legs are done."

* * *

They settled against the windbreak stones, close enough to the fire that heat soaked through the canvas of her jacket, far enough that the cold kept its claim on her back. Above the pocket the stars were coming out in the gaps between clouds, hard and indifferent, and beyond them the unseen summit of the pass waited under its new snow.

"Viaduct Seven," Casper began, and stopped, because the name itself was strange in her mouth—she hadn't spoken it aloud in six years, had trained herself to route around it the way water routes around a stone, and now here it was, out in the open air, sounding smaller than the thing it named.

Ruth waited. That was the gift of her, Casper was learning. She didn't fill silences. She held them open like a door.

"I designed the piers," Casper said. "Piers three and seven, on the north abutment. The geology report we were given showed bedrock at fourteen meters. I trusted it. Then during the pour, my own borings came back—the ones I'd ordered as a formality, because I was young and thorough and wanted my numbers perfect—and the bedrock wasn't at fourteen meters. It wasn't at twenty. On pier seven it wasn't there at all, just shale and old landslide debris, packed wet, like pudding under a dinner plate."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, a load of snow let go of a tree with a soft whump.

"I wrote it up the same week. Full memo. Pier redesign, driven piles, six weeks' delay, cost estimate attached. I remember the number—two hundred and forty thousand. I remember it because the regional director read it standing up, in the doorway of my office, and then he set it on my desk and said, *Casper, the extension opens in the spring. The spring.* Like the season was an argument. Like physics cared what month it was."

"What did you do?" Ruth asked, though her voice suggested she already knew, the way a medic knows how a wound will look before she cuts away the bandage.

"I fought," Casper said. "For about nine days. I filed the memo formally. I went over the director's head to the chief engineer, who thanked me for my diligence and told me the matter was being reviewed. I learned later that *reviewed* meant a committee met once, for forty minutes, over lunch, and the company's own consulting firm produced a second geology report—clean, confident, fourteen meters of beautiful imaginary bedrock—that contradicted mine. My borings were reclassified as sampling error. Do you understand? Eleven men died because a corporation decided the ground beneath their bridge was whatever it needed it to be, and the paperwork agreed, because the paperwork worked for them."

Her hands had curled into fists without her noticing. She opened them deliberately, finger by finger, watching the firelight move on her knuckles.

"The viaduct came down on a Tuesday morning in March. Crew shift change—eleven men on the deck, checking rail alignment, the last work before ballast. Piers three and seven settled together, less than a second apart. Witnesses said it didn't collapse so much as *sit down*, the whole span folding into the gorge like a man kneeling. I was two miles away in the field office. I felt it through the floor."

Ruth's hand found her forearm in the dark. Casper let it stay.

"There was an inquiry. Official. And I want to be fair to myself for one moment, because I have never once been fair to myself, and Ruth—" she laughed, and it came out cracked, "—I am so tired of the other thing. In the inquiry I told the truth. All of it. The memo, the dates, the second report, the lunch. I had copies. I had witnesses. For eleven days I believed the truth was a machine that worked, because I was an engineer and I believed machines worked if you built them right."

"And it didn't."

"It turned out the inquiry commissioner had been the company's counsel for nineteen years. He recused himself—formally, properly—and his deputy ran the hearings instead, a man who owed his appointment to the same director who'd read my memo standing up. My borings became *inconclusive*. My memo became *insufficiently escalated*. And the finding, when it came, was exquisite—it found no corporate fault at all, and it found mine. Negligent design review. Failure to verify subsurface conditions. They took my license. They took it in a room with wood paneling, very dignified, and the director sent flowers afterward, did you know that? White lilies. To my apartment. With a card that said he hoped I'd land softly somewhere."

The wind rose and fell. One of the tents rustled—Dario turning in his sleep, or listening. Casper found she didn't care anymore. That was the strange mercy of it, arriving late: once the truth was halfway out of her mouth, the rest of it wanted out too, the way water wants downhill.

"So I ran," she said. "That's the part I've never said to anyone, not whole. Not the disgrace—I wore that, I earned some of it, I let people believe the worst version because the worst version at least had me at the center, guilty, which was simpler than the true version, which had me *powerless*. But running—I ran before the inquiry even ended. I sold what I had, I took the name off my own door, and I went south, and then further south, and I landed in Puerto Vado because it was a town where nobody looked up from the river long enough to look at you. Six years. I fixed pumps. I repaired a sawmill's drive shaft. I aligned a church bell, of all things, and the priest said God had given me clever hands, and I stood there in the bell tower wanting to tell him what my clever hands had built and what it cost, and I smiled instead. I smiled and climbed down and never rang that bell again."

"Why are you telling me now?"

Casper looked at the fire for a long moment. Emilio slept with his ruined hands open on his chest, palms up, the way children sleep. Teo breathed slow and even, his bandaged arm rising and falling. Even Dario lay still, though Casper caught the too-careful quality of his stillness and chose, tonight, to let him listen.

"Because of the names," she said. "In Tunnel Nine. Because those men scratched themselves into the rock so they wouldn't disappear, and I did the opposite—I scratched myself *out*. Every year on the anniversary I told myself I'd go back, testify, fight, and every year I found a reason, and the reasons got smaller and the silence got heavier, until the silence weighed more than the crime. And then Marchetti put my real name in front of me like a card from a deck she'd bought, and I realized I hadn't been hiding from her at all. I'd been hiding from a bridge."

She turned to face Ruth fully. The medic's face was half firelight, half dark, and her eyes were steady and unastonished, and Casper understood suddenly why people survived under this woman's hands: she did not flinch from what the body told her.

"Here's what I know now, and it's taken me a mountain to learn it. The debt I've been carrying—the one I sold my name to escape, the one I almost sold my soul to clear in that valley below—it was never mine alone. I flagged the flaw. I fought for nine days. I was buried under a report written by liars. What's mine is the running. What's mine is six years of silence while the same company dug the same lie deeper into the same mountains and chained men to it. That part I own. I'll say it in any room they put me in. But the eleven names on Viaduct Seven—" her voice caught, and she let it, "—those belong to the men who signed the reports, and the men who paid for the reports, and the man who sent lilies. And I am done carrying their share. Up here, with the snow coming, I finally put it down."

Ruth was quiet for a while. When she spoke, her voice was rough at the edges.

"In my work," she said, "there's a thing we learn early. A wound that's never cleaned doesn't heal—it just learns to hurt quietly. You've been hurting quietly for six years." She squeezed Casper's arm once, hard, almost clinical, and let go. "Tonight it's clean. It's going to hurt worse for a while. That's how you know it's healing."

Casper laughed—a real one, surprised out of her, thin and wet at the edges. "Are all medics philosophers, or just the ones stuck on mountains with me?"

"Just yours." Ruth stood, brushing frost from her trousers. "Sleep. Real sleep. Tomorrow we cross the ridge, and I need your surveyor's eyes, not your ghosts."

She went back to the fire. Casper stayed a moment longer at the edge of the light, looking north, where the pass waited under its fresh white silence—and where, somewhere beyond it, in a basin she could almost picture from her old maps, a lost expedition might still be alive, holding ledgers full of names like the ones scratched in Tunnel Nine, waiting for someone to come and make them mean something.

Behind her, a tent flap whispered. Dario emerged into the firelight, hugging his jacket closed, and stood there not quite looking at her, jaw working like a boy rehearsing a confession of his own.

"Casper," he said quietly. "There's something you should hear. About my aunt. About what she knew, and when—and about the message I sent from the switchback that you caught me sending." He swallowed. "I only sent half of it. The other half I've been carrying since the washout, and I can't carry it past the ridge."

The wind shifted, and from the west, unmistakable now, came the smell of snow.
