## 16. The Silent Camp

Ruth smelled the camp before she saw it—woodsmoke gone thin and sour, the reek of unwashed bodies and old blood, and beneath it all something sweeter and worse: the smell of people who had stopped believing anyone was coming.

She had learned that smell in the cholera camps of her residency, in the flooded districts after the levee failed, in every place where medicine arrived too late to matter much. It clung to the back of the throat. She stood at the lip of the basin with the wind pulling her hood sideways and breathed it in, and her heart did a strange double thing—leaping because they were alive, sinking because alive, this far along, meant something very specific.

"They're down there," Dario said beside her. His voice cracked on the second word. He had not slept since the tunnel, since his confession, since he'd smashed his radio against the rocks of Tunnel Nine and ground the pieces under his heel like a man crushing a spider he'd been feeding for weeks. "That's their smoke. That's—Aunt Ilsa said—"

"Your aunt said a lot of things," Teo muttered behind them. His shoulder was bound tight where the bullet had grazed him, and every hour on the climb had grayed his face another shade, but his eyes were still river-eyes, flat and reading everything. "Save your breath for walking."

Casper said nothing. She stood a little apart, the way she always did, compass open in her palm though there was no need for it now—the basin held them like a cupped hand, ringed by walls of granite scoured white with the first snows. Ruth watched her study the slopes, the way her thumb moved across the compass housing without her seeming to know it, and understood that Casper was doing arithmetic too. Not about the living below. About the weather above.

"Ruth." Casper's voice was low. "How many can you move?"

It was the question Ruth had been dreading since the washout, since Teo's blood soaked into the grade, since Emilio had begun to falter on the switchbacks and she'd started lying to him about how far was left. She looked down into the basin—at the scatter of tents sagging under new snow, at the thread of smoke rising from a fire that had more ash than flame—and made herself answer honestly, because that was the only currency she had left to spend.

"I don't know yet. Ask me again when I've counted them."

* * *

They came down into the camp slowly, hands visible, calling out in Spanish and English both, because Casper had warned them that starving people with nothing left to lose sometimes shoot first at shadows. No shots came. What came instead was a sound Ruth would carry for the rest of her life: a thin, wavering cry from the largest tent, and then another answering it, and then the sound of someone crawling.

The tent flap moved aside and a woman looked out at them. She might have been forty; starvation had carved her down to something older, cheekbones like shipwreck timber, lips split and crusted. Her eyes went from face to face and stopped on Casper's, and Ruth saw recognition move through her like current through wire.

"You're the engineer." The voice was a ruin. "Sandoval. The one who built this line."

"Casper Reyes now," Casper said quietly. "Who's in charge?"

The woman laughed—a terrible sound, two dry notes with no humor in them at all. "In charge. God. Dr. Anselmo is. Was. He fell, eleven days ago, on the scree below the col. Compound fracture, femur. I'm—" She steadied herself against the tent pole, and Ruth stepped forward without thinking, took the woman's weight against her shoulder, felt how little of her there was to take. "I'm Vidal. Elena Vidal. Surveyor. There are nine of us left. We were fourteen."

Nine. Ruth closed her eyes for half a second and did the arithmetic she'd promised herself she wouldn't do yet. Nine survivors, plus Emilio, plus their own five. Fifteen souls, one pass, and a storm front stacking itself against the western peaks like a debt coming due.

"Take me to your doctor," she said.

* * *

Anselmo lay in the smallest tent, and Ruth knew before she knelt beside him that the leg was lost. The fracture had been badly splinted—a survey tripod lashed with rope, the kind of field improvisation that bought time and spent tissue—and the wound beneath had soured days ago. The smell of it cut through even the camp-smell, sweet and foul together, gangrene announcing itself the way it always did, politely, in advance.

He was conscious. That was the worst mercy of it. His eyes were clear, fever-bright but tracking, and when Ruth cut away the filthy dressing he watched her do it with the detached attention of a man reading his own autopsy.

"You're a surgeon," he said. Not a question. She had her kit out, the good scalpel, the chloroform she'd hoarded through six hundred miles of wilderness for exactly this arithmetic.

"Yes."

"How long?"

"Since the fall?" She kept her hands moving, palpating, mapping the dead tissue. "Eleven days."

"No." His mouth twitched. "How long do I have? If you don't take it."

Ruth met his eyes. She had learned long ago that patients like this—one foot already in the country past—deserved the same honesty she gave the living. "If I take it now, cleanly, above the knee, you may keep it. If we wait, the fever decides for you, and the fever has no interest in your survival."

"And if you take it, can I walk?"

She hesitated. He saw her hesitate.

"Don't lie to me, Doctor. I've read enough charts to know what a stump means at altitude."

"You won't walk out," Ruth said. "Not on it. Not fast. Someone carries you, or you stay."

Anselmo exhaled, long and slow, and turned his head toward the tent wall where the wind pressed the canvas in like an enormous slow hand. "Then take it," he said. "And then get the others out. Vidal will tell you what we found. Vidal will tell you why they'll come for us."

"We know why," Casper said from the tent entrance. She had come in silent as snowfall. "Marchetti sent men up the grade behind us. Armed ones. They're a day back, maybe less."

Something moved through Anselmo's face—not fear. Something colder and more settled than fear. "Then you understand the order of operations," he said. "The leg. Then the ledgers. In that order or not at all, Doctor—I don't care which, but the ledgers leave this basin before I do, or before I don't. Promise me that."

Ruth looked at the man's ruined leg, at the tripod splint, at the eleven days of fever he had spent guarding a box of paper with his body while his expedition starved around him. She thought of Tunnel Nine, of the names scratched into rock by men who had known no one would ever read them.

"I promise," she said, and reached for the chloroform.

* * *

The amputation took forty minutes and cost Ruth more than any operation of her career, and not because of the surgery. Surgery, in its way, was simple: a clean field, a sharp blade, the old liturgy of clamp and ligature and saw. What was hard was the audience. Half the camp stood outside the tent in the falling snow because there was nowhere else to go and nothing else to do, and they listened—there was no way not to listen—and when it was over and Ruth crawled out on her knees to boil water over the fire, Elena Vidal handed her a tin cup of melted snow with something like reverence in her ruined face.

"He asked us to sing," Vidal said. "While you worked. Old survey song, from the coastal crews. We were terrible."

"It carried," Ruth said. Her hands would not quite stop shaking. She wrapped them around the cup and let the heat work into them. "It carried fine."

Casper crouched beside her at the fire, close enough that their shoulders touched, which Casper almost never allowed. For a while neither spoke. The snow came down thicker now, fat flakes with weight to them, the kind that meant the front had arrived in earnest and the real storm was only waiting for dark.

"Talk to me," Casper said finally. "Numbers. All of them. Don't soften it."

Ruth had been assembling the numbers all afternoon, in the gaps between tasks, the way she assembled everything. She gave them now, plainly, because Casper had earned plainness.

"Fifteen total. Anselmo can't walk—he'll need two carriers minimum, on rotation, and he'll need shelter every night, which means pace drops to maybe four miles a day if the weather allows, which it won't. Emilio's lungs are bad. He tries to hide it, but I hear him at night. Two of Vidal's people have frostbite, early stage, fingers and one foot—they can walk but they shouldn't be exposed, and exposure is the entire itinerary. Everyone else is starving. I can feed them enough to move tomorrow, barely, but starving bodies fail on descents. They fall. They make bad decisions." She stared into the fire. "Teo's shoulder is infected. Mildly. I've got him on the last of the sulfa."

"And the storm?"

"Two days of travel to the shelter cave, at full strength, in good weather. Call it four, maybe five, at our pace, in this." Ruth lifted her chin at the sky. "The front hits tonight. Real snow by morning. And Marchetti's men are a day behind us on the grade, which means the storm slows them too—but they're fresh, armed, and carrying their own supplies, and they don't have a stretcher."

Casper was quiet for a long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders and in her hair and she didn't brush it off. When she spoke, her voice had the flat calm Ruth had come to recognize as the engineer's version of terror, mastered and repurposed.

"So the honest count is this. Some of us outrun the snow and some of us don't, and every hour I spend deciding which is an hour closer for all of us."

"That's the count," Ruth said. "Unless the count changes."

"Counts change." Casper looked at her then, and there was something in her face Ruth hadn't seen before—not hope exactly, but the place hope comes from. "You told me once you became a doctor because you couldn't stand watching people do arithmetic with lives. So tell me, Doctor. What does the arithmetic say?"

Ruth looked across the camp—at Vidal distributing the last of the expedition's chocolate in slivers small enough to be a mockery, at Dario sitting apart with his head in his hands, at Emilio sleeping upright against a supply crate because lying flat made him cough, at the tent where Anselmo slept the gray sleep of the newly bled—and gave the only answer she had.

"The arithmetic says we don't leave anyone. It says we split the difference between speed and mercy until both run out. And it says—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through, "—it says somebody stays behind with the slow ones, and we both know who that arithmetic points at."

Casper followed her gaze. Teo stood at the edge of the firelight, checking the lashings on the supply sled he'd built from a broken tent frame, working one-handed, protecting his bad shoulder, saying nothing to anyone. A man already measuring distances in his head. A man who read water, and weather, and people, and had surely already read this ending for himself.

"He'll offer," Casper said quietly. "Before we even ask."

"I know."

"And we'll let him."

Ruth didn't answer. Above them the storm front slid over the col like a lid closing on the world, and the first true flakes of the front began to come down, heavy and straight and endless, erasing the trail they'd climbed, erasing the grade below, erasing everything except the small circle of firelight and the fifteen lives huddled inside it—and somewhere down in the white silence, a day behind and closing, the soundless approach of men who wanted the ledgers burned and everyone who'd read them buried with the snow.
