## 17. Snowfall

Teo smelled the storm before he saw it.

Thirty years on the river had taught him that weather arrived in the body before it arrived in the sky—a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like tin at the back of the throat, the water going strange and glassy and quiet as if holding its breath. He'd felt it first at dawn, standing hip-deep in the shallows of the high basin, washing his hands in water so cold it burned, and by midmorning the feeling had climbed into his chest and settled there like a stone.

He looked up. The peaks to the west had gone soft-edged, their summits wrapped in a gray veil that hadn't been there an hour ago. The wind had died completely. The air hung still and enormous, the whole valley waiting.

*Primer frente,* his grandmother used to say, tapping the sky with a knuckle. *The first snow doesn't fall. It arrives.*

"Teo." Casper's voice, low, from behind him. She'd come down from the ridge where she'd been reading the grade with her old surveyor's eye, and she was squinting west too, and he could see in the set of her jaw that she'd smelled it too. "How long?"

He turned his hands over, palms up, an old habit—reading weather the way other men read cards. "Twelve hours," he said. "Maybe ten if the front's moving faster than it looks. After that, whiteout for two days minimum. Three if it's a real one."

Casper said nothing for a moment. Below them, spread across the floor of the basin, the rescued expedition was doing what exhausted people do: some slept where they'd dropped, others sat in small huddles over a single stove, and Ruth moved among them all with her medic's bag and her terrible calm arithmetic face—the face of a woman counting pulses and calories and hours and finding the sums short.

There were eleven of them now who could walk. And four who couldn't.

Teo had done his own counting that morning, quietly, while the others broke camp. The expedition's leader, Voss, had a broken ankle splinted with tent poles and a fever that wouldn't break. Two of the workers they'd found were half-starved past the point of marching—one of them, a boy of maybe nineteen with a name Teo kept forgetting and relearning, could barely stand without swaying. And Emilio, their guide through the worst of it, was steady but slow, his lungs scarred from whatever the camps had put into them, each step uphill costing him what it cost other men three steps.

Four people who would turn a twelve-hour descent into twenty. Maybe more.

And the men behind them—Marchetti's hired crew, a day back on the grade, angry now and armed and led by a man named Corso whom Dario had described in a voice gone flat with shame: *He doesn't take prisoners. He takes inventory.*

"You're thinking about the cave," Casper said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm always thinking about the cave."

* * *

They'd found it two days ago, on the way up—or rather, Emilio had known it was there, the way he seemed to know everything about this country, every seam and hollow, because he'd been marched through it carrying loads no man should carry. A shelter cave in the eastern wall of the basin, above the treeline but below the col, dry-floored, with a low mouth that faced away from the prevailing wind. Miners had used it once; there were rusted pitons in the rock and the ghost of a fire ring. It would hold six people comfortably. Ten, crowded.

It would hold four people alive through a three-day storm.

The argument happened the way arguments happen in the high country—not shouted, but conducted in silences and glances and the particular way Ruth stopped packing her bag and stood very still.

"We split them," Dario said first, because Dario still believed problems could be organized into solutions. "Fast group goes now, makes the far valley by dark tomorrow. Slow group waits out the storm in the cave, comes down after."

"And Marchetti's men reach the cave during the storm," Casper said. "Or before it. Four people who can't run, alone, against Corso's crew."

"So we all stay." Dario's voice cracked on it. "We all wait out the storm together, then—"

"Then nobody makes it down." Teo said it gently, because the boy was twenty-three and learning in one week what took most men a lifetime: that the mountains didn't care how much you loved people. "Snow's coming heavy. After it passes, the cornices will be loaded, the slopes will be avalanche-prone for days, and we'll have burned our food sitting still. Every hour we delay is an hour Corso gains and a pound of rice we don't eat."

Dario looked at him. "Then what's your answer? Leave them?"

"No," Teo said. "I stay with them."

The silence that followed had weight, like snow beginning to accumulate on a roof.

Ruth came over slowly. She was a woman who wasted nothing—words least of all—and she studied him for a long moment with those clinical eyes that saw straight through a man to his blood pressure and his intentions. "You're not the slowest," she said. "You're not injured. You're volunteering."

"I'm the one who knows storms." He shrugged, and made himself smile, because if you let them see the fear it would grow roots in all of them. "I've wintered on the river when the ice took the boats. I know how to keep four people alive in a hole in a rock for three days. Rationing, heat, keeping the mind fed when the belly's empty. That's my trade, right now. Casper's trade is getting the rest of you down that mountain fast. Let everyone work."

"That's not—" Dario started.

"It is exactly how it works," Casper said quietly. She was looking at Teo with an expression he couldn't fully read—something between grief and recognition, the look of a woman who understood debts. "Teo. You don't owe these people anything. You signed on to run a boat up a river."

He thought about that. About Puerto Vado, and the launch, and the money Marchetti had promised him—which he'd already decided, somewhere on the grade after the ambush, he would never touch. About the years he'd spent ferrying strangers up and down brown water, belonging everywhere and nowhere, welcome at every landing and missed at none. About the night in Camp Ash when he'd held a lantern over Emilio's ruined back while Ruth worked, and the laborer had gripped his wrist and said, *You stayed,* in a voice full of such astonishment that Teo had felt something crack open in his chest that hadn't cracked open in forty years.

*You stayed.* As if staying were a miracle. As if no one had ever stayed for him.

"A man spends his whole life going places," Teo said. "It's good, once, to be somewhere on purpose."

* * *

They spent the last clear hours preparing, and Teo directed it all with the bossy competence of a man who has provisioned a hundred boats for a hundred crossings and never once left anything to hope.

Food went to the walking party—every calorie that could move legs needed to move legs. What remained for the cave was thin: hardtack, a slab of rendered fat, tea, a tin of condensed milk Ruth surrendered with visible pain. They cached firewood inside the cave mouth, stacked it like a wall. They filled every container with snow-melt ahead of time, because melting snow inside a cave burned fuel, and fuel was life measured in sticks.

Voss argued. Of course Voss argued—he was expedition leader, or had been, and being carried into a cave while his team walked on offended something structural in the man. Teo let him argue until he ran out of breath, then crouched beside him and said, "Jefe. I have watched you for nine days. You are a stubborn, brave, ridiculous man, and you will survive this because you are too angry to die. But today your job is to be cargo. Let me do mine."

Voss stared at him, fever-bright, and then—slowly—laughed, a dry rasp. "You're enjoying this."

"Enormously," Teo said. "It's the first order anyone's listened to since we left the river."

The boy—the nineteen-year-old, whose name was Mateo, which Teo finally fixed in his memory because of course it was, of course the universe would hand him a boy wearing almost his own name—came to him as the walking party formed up. The kid's hands were shaking, and not entirely from cold.

"Señor Teo. If the men come—if Corso—"

"Then I will talk to them," Teo said. "Old boatmen talk very well. We bore people until they leave."

Mateo didn't smile. "My brother was left behind at the second camp. When he got sick. They said someone would come back for him." His throat worked. "Nobody came back."

Teo put both hands on the boy's shoulders, feeling how slight they were, how the mountain had already begun to hollow him. "Look at me. I am not the railroad. I am not the company. I am a boatman from downriver, and I am telling you: I will be here when the snow stops. And then you and I are going to walk down this mountain together, and I am going to complain the entire way, because complaining is also my trade, and you are going to wish your brother had been the lucky one who got left with me instead of you. Do you understand?"

Something loosened in the boy's face. Not belief—not yet—but the space where belief could live.

"Yes, señor."

"Good. Now go. Walk fast, complain little, and tell Casper—" he paused, surprised by the catch in his own throat, "—tell her the river says thank you."

* * *

The walking party left at noon, eleven figures strung out along the western slope, growing small against the enormous gray sky. Teo watched them until the terrain folded them away—Casper in front with her compass, Ruth sweeping the rear, Dario carrying double weight without being asked, which was its own kind of answer to everything the boy had done and undone. Emilio went third, setting a pace the weak could hold, and just before the trail bent out of sight the old laborer turned and raised one hand. Not waving, exactly. Acknowledging. The way you acknowledge another ship on open water.

Then Teo turned to his cave, and his four charges, and the sky.

The first flakes came an hour later—fat, unhurried, falling straight down in the dead calm, as if the air itself were too tired to move them sideways. By midafternoon the far peaks were gone. By dusk the world had shrunk to the width of the cave mouth and the blue glow of the snowfield beyond it, and the sound had changed—that deep, upholstered hush of heavy snowfall, the mountain wrapping itself in cotton and wool.

Inside, they built a routine the way you build a fire: carefully, from small things.

Teo rationed by lamplight, dealing out hardtack like a card sharp, making ceremony of it so the hunger would have a shape instead of just an ache. He set watches even though no one could go anywhere—watches were less about security than about dignity, about each person having a job, a shift, a reason to be awake that wasn't fear. He got Voss drinking melted snow laced with tea, got Mateo doing exercises against the cave wall to keep the blood moving, got the two weakened workers telling stories about their villages in the lowlands—rice paddies, festivals, a dog who stole a wedding cake entire—and wrote the details down in his notebook in his crooked boatman's hand, because Ruth had taught him that names and details were ammunition, and he intended to spend every round he had.

On the second night, the storm screamed.

The wind arrived sometime after midnight like a living thing, funneling through the col above them with a sound Teo had only heard twice in his life and hoped never to hear again—the mountain itself howling, avalanches rumbling somewhere out in the white dark, deep concussions felt in the chest more than heard. Snow drove past the cave mouth horizontally, a white river flowing uphill. Inside, the temperature fell until their breath froze on the rock walls in ferns of ice, and they huddled in one pile under every scrap of fabric they owned, bodies pressed together for warmth, and Teo lay awake all night doing the arithmetic he refused to say aloud: wood, food, fuel, hours. The numbers were close. Closer than he'd told the others.

Voss, feverish, gripped his arm in the dark. "Boatman. If it comes to it—you take the food and go with the boy. That's an order."

"You're not my jefe anymore."

"I'm ordering you anyway."

"Then I'm disobeying," Teo said comfortably. "Sleep, old man. Nobody's eating anybody. Yet."

Voss laughed into the blackness, a wheezing, broken sound, and somewhere in it Teo heard the exact note of the river at flood stage—terrifying and joyful at once—and he thought: *this is what I crossed the mountains for. Not the ledgers, not the justice. This. To be pressed against strangers in the dark and find out they are not strangers.*

* * *

The storm broke on the morning of the third day, the way storms do—all at once, like a door slamming open onto silence. The sun came up on a world remade: the basin buried, the slopes smooth and blinding and beautiful and lethal, the cornices along the ridgelines hanging like frozen waves. The cold after the storm was absolute, a cold with teeth.

But they were alive. All five of them—four charges and one boatman, hungry, filthy, frost-nipped, alive. Teo stood in the cave mouth and breathed air so clean it hurt and laughed until Voss told him he'd cracked something.

They gave themselves that day to dig out and gather strength. On the fourth morning, they started down.

Teo led, breaking trail thigh-deep, choosing the line the way he read water—for the hidden current beneath the surface, the weakness under the smooth. Behind him came Mateo, then the two workers roped together, then Voss hopping grimly on his splinted leg with a makeshift crutch, slow as continental drift and twice as determined. They made perhaps two miles the first day. Teo had hoped for four. He adjusted the plan, adjusted the rations, adjusted his own expectations, and did not adjust his promise.

On the fifth afternoon, crossing a shoulder below the col, they heard voices.

Teo froze, hand up. Wind carried fragments down the slope—shouts, the ring of metal on stone. He eased forward along a rib of rock and looked down into the next basin, and his stomach dropped.

Tents. Orange and black. Six men, maybe eight, working a snow slope with probes. Corso's crew, delayed by the same storm, hunting the same ground—and between Teo's ragged column and the far valley lay three open miles of slope with nowhere to hide.

He was turning back to plan when he saw the other thing. Down-valley, tiny with distance but unmistakable: figures on the move. A strung-out line of climbers descending toward the railhead, moving fast.

And at the head of them, compact and relentless as a piston, a woman with a compass in her hand.

Casper had made it down. Which meant help might be within reach—which meant all Teo had to do was keep five fragile people invisible and alive for one more day, with hunters below and the whole white mountain listening.

He turned back to his crew, put a finger to his lips, and began, silently, to lead them up.
