## 18. The Descent

The snow did not fall so much as arrive—sideways, in sheets, as if the mountain had decided to erase itself and everything clinging to it.

Casper stood at the lip of the col with the wind hammering her back and looked down into a world with no bottom. The far side of the range dropped away beneath her boots, and where yesterday there had been a visible thread of grade switchbacking down the shoulder of the mountain, there was now only white on white on white, a blindness so complete that she could not tell sky from slope except by the way the wind tore at her face from below instead of above.

"Tell me you can see something," Ruth shouted against her shoulder, one arm hooked through Emilio's pack strap to keep him upright. The laborer's frost-nipped hands were wrapped in wool and wired shut around an ice axe he barely had the strength to swing. Behind them came the rest: the expedition's injured leader, Aldous Wren, lashed into a makeshift litter between two of his starving surveyors; Dario, gray-faced and silent since the confession; three more of the rescued party who could still walk, just barely, moving like marionettes with half their strings cut.

"I can see enough," Casper said, which was a lie, and then, because lies got people killed on a grade, she corrected it. "I can't see anything. So we're going to do this the way I learned to do it when I could see."

She turned her back to the wind and crouched, pulling the map from inside her coat—a sheet of oilcloth she'd drawn herself, years ago, from memory and instrument readings, when this corridor was still hers to survey. The ink had faded to ghost-gray, but the numbers were burned somewhere deeper than paper. She traced the line down with a mittened finger.

"The old construction road makes eleven switchbacks from the col to the tree line. Eleven. Not ten, not twelve—if we count twelve, we've drifted off the grade and onto the slide slopes, and the slide slopes will take us." She looked up at each of them in turn, making her voice carry the way she'd once made it carry across a cut forty men deep. "So here is what we do. We walk on a count. Every turn of the grade, I call the number. You repeat it. If anyone loses the number, we stop, all of us, and we find it again before another step. Nobody walks past me. Nobody wanders. The wind wants us scattered, and scattered is dead. Are we understood?"

Eleven faces, hollowed by hunger and altitude, nodded at her—or rather, leaned into the wind in a way that meant yes.

She had stood like this once before, she thought, on the morning they poured the piers for Viaduct 7, reading a crew's fear and giving it somewhere to go. She had thought that woman dead and buried under a false name in a river town. But here she was, alive after all, and the mountain was going to test whether she remembered how to be her.

* * *

The first hour was the worst, because the first hour was trust.

They roped together in pairs, thirty feet apart, Casper leading alone on a length of rope tied around her waist. She carried a bamboo survey pole—one of the expedition's own, salvaged from the litter rig—held out ahead of her like a blind man's cane, feeling for the camber of the old road bed. A graded road has a shape; even under two feet of new snow, even invisible, it keeps its bones. The crown rises gently in the middle and falls away at the edges, and if your pole tells you the ground is falling away on both sides at once, you are still on the grade. If it falls away on only one side, steeply, you have found the outside edge, and beyond that edge is air.

Her boots found the first switchback by feel before her mind confirmed it—the subtle flattening, the wide slow curve to the left that her body knew the way it knew its own heartbeat. She planted the pole, turned, and called it.

"One!"

The word came back down the rope in eleven ragged voices, some of them barely more than breath. Dario's was the loudest. He had not spoken since the tunnel, since his aunt's name had come out of his mouth like a splinter, but he repeated every number now as though the counting were penance, as though each figure called and answered might be weighed against what he'd transmitted down the mountain.

They went on. Two. Three. The wind gusted in long pulses, and between the pulses the whiteout thinned for seconds at a time—not enough to see, never enough to see, but enough to catch the darker smudge of rock ribs poking through the snow, and Casper checked each smudge against the map in her head. There: the outcrop the old crews had called the Sentinel, left side of the grade, meaning they were right of center, drifting toward the edge. She hauled the rope left, called a correction, felt the column behind her shuffle obediently through knee-deep drift.

At the fourth turn, the mountain made its first attempt on them.

It came without sound, which was the obscenity of it—a wind slab releasing somewhere above, not a roar but a *hush*, a soft exhalation, and then the grade itself began to move beneath their feet like a conveyor. Casper had been on slides before; she knew the first two seconds were everything. "Down-slope! Swim!" she screamed, and threw herself sideways and up, clawing into the crust above the fracture line, and behind her the rope snapped taut and she dug her axe in and held, arms screaming, while the snow took the lower half of the column and rolled it, tumbling, twenty feet down the face before the slide spent itself against a buried rock rib and stopped.

Silence. Wind. Her own pulse in her ears like a hammer on a rail.

"Count off," she gasped. "Everyone. Numbers."

One by one they answered—Ruth, Emilio, Dario, the surveyors, Wren's faint voice from the litter—and all eleven, all present, though one of Wren's carriers was bleeding from the eyebrow where the ice had opened it and Emilio was shaking so hard Ruth had to hold him until the shivering became something she could work with. They had lost the litter's left runner and half their remaining food, tumbled away down the face inside the slide debris, gone as surely as if the mountain had swallowed it whole.

Casper lay on her back in the snow for ten seconds and let the terror drain out through her spine. Then she got up.

"We count again," she said. "From four. We haven't lost the number. We haven't lost anything we can't afford."

Nobody believed her about the food. She could see it in their faces. But they formed up anyway, because the alternative was standing still, and standing still on this mountain was simply dying with better posture.

* * *

Five. Six. Seven.

The turns came slower now, the hours blurring into a rhythm of pole-plants and counted curves and the endless lateral shove of the wind. Casper stopped thinking in words and started thinking the way she had at nineteen, apprenticed on her first mountain line, when a grader foreman had taught her the trick she was using now: *you don't navigate a whiteout, girl, you audit it.* Count what cannot lie. The number of steps between turns. The lean of the wind on your left cheek, constant, constant, constant—because the wind pours down these valleys along the grain of the drainage, and if the wind swings to hit your back, you have turned out of the valley and into a gully, and gullies on this face end in cliffs. The dip and rise of the grade underfoot, memorized from a survey she'd walked eleven years ago with a transit on her shoulder and no idea what the numbers would cost her.

Step. Pole. Listen. Step. Pole. Call the turn.

The others had stopped being strangers somewhere above the treeline, but here, in the white blindness, they became something more than companions. Ruth moved like a metronome, tireless, always at Emilio's elbow, always the last voice to answer the count, as if she would not permit the number to be finished without her. One of the surveyors, a thin Kechwa man named Tomás who had not spoken a full sentence since Camp Ash, began calling the turns a half-beat before Casper reached them—he had walked this grade too, as a captive, hauling rails, and his feet remembered it in their own way. When his count matched hers, something passed between them in the white nothing, wordless: two people reading the same book in different languages.

Dario stayed directly behind her. She had not asked him to. He simply appeared there at every halt, close enough to grab her pack, and twice he did grab it—once when she stumbled on buried riprap, once when exhaustion put her knee down in a drift and the wind tried to peel her off the grade like a label off a bottle. He never said anything. His hands said it for him.

The second close call came at the ninth turn, in the last gray suggestion of daylight.

The grade crossed a rock bluff there—an old cut where the builders had blasted through a spur—and in summer it was a shelf with a fine view. In whiteout it was a place where the road simply ceased to exist, replaced by a traverse across bare, iced stone with a drop on the outside that the map in her head said was eighty meters and her imagination said was eight hundred. The fixed chain the old crews had bolted into the rock was still there, or part of it—she found the first link with her bare fingers, skin tearing, and nearly wept at the touch of cold iron as if it were a friend's hand.

"Chain traverse," she called back. "Hands on the rope, feet where I put mine. One at a time. Ruth, you anchor the litter. Emilio goes third—I'll be at the far end waiting for him."

She went across first, boot-spikes chattering on verglas, the void sucking at her left side, and clipped herself to the far anchor and paid out instructions turn by turn. It worked, mostly. Tomás crossed like a cat. The surveyors crossed. Wren's litter came across by inches, swaying, everyone's breath held. Then Emilio, third from last, froze.

He was halfway, and the wind chose that moment to deliver its longest gust of the day, and Emilio—who had survived quotas and chains and a burning camp and a bullet-grazed escape—simply stopped, both hands locked on the chain, forehead pressed against the icy rock, and said in Spanish, very quietly, "No puedo. Déjame aquí. No puedo."

*I can't. Leave me here.*

The wind screamed. Below him the drop filled with moving white. And Casper, anchored at the far end, heard Ruth begin to say something sensible and medical and useless, and heard instead her own voice come out of her, low and certain, carrying across the rock the way it used to carry across cuts:

"Emilio. Look at me. You can't see me, I know—but I'm here, and I am not leaving this rock until your hand lets go of that chain, so the only question is whether you cross to me or whether we both freeze to this mountain, and I have already died on one bridge, amigo, and I refuse to do it again. Ten steps. That's all that's left. Ten steps, and I will count every one of them for you."

And she counted. *Uno. Dos. Tres.* Her voice steady as a level bubble, and his boots scraping, one inch, one inch, *cuatro, cinco,* the rope trembling, *seis, siete,* Ruth's hands ready at the near anchor, *ocho, nueve*—

His hand closed on her wrist like a drowning man's, and she hauled him over the lip and onto the shelf, and they lay there together on the ice while the party breathed again behind them.

"No perdí el número," he whispered into her collar. *I didn't lose the count.*

"No," she said. "You didn't."

* * *

Ten. Eleven.

The eleventh switchback came in full darkness, navigated entirely by pole and memory and the deepening timber-smell of the air—because below them, unseen, the trees had begun. She felt them before she saw them, a change in the wind's texture, the sudden broken hush of snow-laden branches, and when she finally made out the first dark trunks looming out of the white like the masts of sunken ships, she had to stand still for a moment with her head bowed, letting the relief move through her the way floodwater moves through a culvert.

Treeline. Shelter. Slopes gentle enough that the avalanche gods lost interest. They were down.

They camped that night in the lee of a granite wall at the base of the old grade, in a hollow the original construction crews had cut for their powder magazine—she knew it was there, had known it would be there, had banked their lives on a hole in a mountain dug by men dead fifty years. They had no stove fuel worth the name and food for perhaps two more days at half rations, and half the party's feet were past the first gray borders of frostbite, and none of that mattered just then. What mattered was the arithmetic of survival had changed sign: they were no longer losing. They were descending, and every step now took them toward warmth instead of away from it.

Ruth worked down the row of feet and hands by lantern light, her face exhausted and lit from within. Teo was a day behind them with the slowest survivors, holed up in the shelter cave with a radio they could not make reach the valley—Casper had thought of him a hundred times during the descent, each switchback a small prayer sent backward up the mountain. Dario sat apart, sharpening the camp knife with slow, deliberate strokes, and when Casper settled beside him he spoke without looking up.

"My aunt will be at the railhead," he said. "Valle Hondo. If she knows the storm broke when it did, she'll have moved early. Police. Lawyers. Whatever she bought." The knife whispered against the stone. "She doesn't send lawyers unless she expects to win, Casper. And she'll know what you're carrying. She'll make you an offer. She's good at offers—it's how she got me."

"I know," Casper said.

"She offered me a railroad once." His voice cracked on the word. "A future. My name on a bridge plaque. All I had to do was hold a radio." He looked at her then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and young and older than they had any right to be. "Whatever she offers you—it will be exactly the thing you want most. That's how you'll know it's a trap."

Casper pulled her coat tighter and looked down through the black trees, toward the valley she could not see, where a railhead waited and a woman waited at it, and beyond them both, a hearing room, a set of ledgers, eleven names scratched into tunnel stone, and a verdict eleven years overdue.

"Then I suppose," she said quietly, "we'll find out tomorrow what I want most."

Below them, far down in the dark, almost too faint to hear beneath the wind, a train whistle sounded—long, low, and patient, like something that had been waiting a very long time and intended to keep waiting exactly as long as it pleased.
