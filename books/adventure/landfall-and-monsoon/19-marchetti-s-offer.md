## 19. Marchetti's Offer

The valley opened beneath them like a held breath finally let go.

Casper felt it in her knees before she saw it—the grade softening from the brutal pitch of the high country into something a body could walk without bargaining. The pines came back first, sparse and wind-bent at the treeline, then thicker, darker, until the air itself changed, thickened with resin and the wet green smell of lower ground. By midmorning they could hear water again, real water, a river with weight to it. By noon they could hear something else.

A train.

The sound carried up the valley on the cold air: the long metallic complaint of couplings, the shudder of a locomotive working hard against gradient. Casper stopped on the trail and closed her eyes, and for three full seconds she was twenty-eight years old again, standing on the deck of Viaduct 7 with a set of drawings under her arm and the whole of her future laid out in steel and stone.

Then Dario stumbled against her pack and the moment broke.

"Sorry," he said. He had not slept properly since the tunnel, since he'd told them everything, and it showed in the gray of his face and the way his hands wouldn't stay still. "I thought—I heard—"

"A train," Casper said. "The valley railhead. Which means we're close, and which means—" She looked back down the line of them: Ruth supporting Emilio, who could walk now but only in the stubborn, grinding way of a man who had decided he would never be carried again; the two expedition survivors they'd found in the basin, hollow-cheeked and light-headed but upright; herself, leading. Teo was a day behind them at the shelter cave with the slowest of the rescued workers, and every hour Casper spent walking downhill was an hour she wasn't spending thinking about that.

"Which means someone will have guessed where we'd come out," Ruth finished quietly.

"Marchetti isn't stupid," Dario said. It cost him something to say the name now; Casper watched him pay it. "She'll know the far-side railhead is the only sane descent. She'll have people there."

"She'll have more than people." Casper started walking again, setting a pace the survivors could hold. "She'll have paper. Lawyers. Police with warrants signed by judges who owe her favors. Down there, we're not heroes coming out of the mountains. We're trespassers carrying stolen documents and a dead man's photographs."

"They're not stolen," Ruth said.

"No," Casper agreed. "But watch how fast that stops mattering."

* * *

They smelled the railhead before they saw it—coal smoke and creosote and the sour iron tang of a yard—and then the trees fell away and there it was, sitting in the flat green lap of the valley like something that had always been there and always would be: a water tower, a coal tipple, three sidings choked with empty ore cars, a low station house with its paint gone gray, and beyond it all the twin threads of track running south toward the world.

And between the party and the station, waiting in the open with no attempt at concealment, stood perhaps a dozen men.

Some wore police uniforms, badly fitted, belts cinched over soft bellies. Some wore suits that fit too well. And at the center of them, hands folded in front of her coat, standing on the gravel of the yard as though she owned it—which, Casper thought, she probably did, or would by Friday—was Ilsa Marchetti.

Casper stopped. Behind her the column bunched up, and she heard Ruth draw breath to speak, and she raised one hand without turning, and the breath was held.

"I'll do the talking," Casper said.

"You don't have to—"

"Yes," Casper said. "I do."

She walked down alone. The gravel crunched under her boots, loud as gunfire after weeks of snow-hush. The wind came down the valley behind her, pushing at her back, carrying the smell of the high country with her—woodsmoke and ice and the ghost of Tunnel Nine—as though the mountain itself were standing at her shoulder.

Ten yards out, Marchetti lifted her chin. Up close, Casper could see what the weeks had done to her: new lines around the mouth, a tightness at the eyes that hadn't been there in Puerto Vado. A woman whose plan had developed a leak and who was managing the drip.

"Casper Sandoval," Marchetti said. Not Reyes. Not the false name from Puerto Vado. She said it clearly, deliberately, letting the police officers hear it, letting it land like a card played face-up. "You look terrible."

"The mountains do that."

"They also kill people. Eleven of your men, once upon a time." Marchetti's voice was pleasant, conversational, pitched for the benefit of the audience. "Or so the inquiry found. You remember the inquiry."

"I remember everything about it."

"I imagine you do." Marchetti took a half step forward, and the lawyers shifted with her like birds around a feeder. "Here is what happens next, and you may correct me if I'm wrong. You come off that mountain carrying ledgers, film canisters, and photographs of names scratched into rock. You intend to give them to a journalist—you've already sent word ahead, clever girl—or to some inspector, and you intend to stand in front of cameras and tell the story your way. And the story has two parts, doesn't it? The part about my extension. And the part about you."

Casper said nothing.

"The part," Marchetti went on softly, "where you knew Viaduct 7 was going to fail. Where you flagged the piers, put it in writing, were overruled by men above you—and then, when it came down and eleven families needed someone to blame, you let them blame you. You signed the settlement. You took the verdict. You ran to a river town and buried yourself alive rather than fight, because fighting meant testifying, and testifying meant admitting under oath that you watched those men walk onto that bridge knowing what you knew." She tilted her head. "Have I got it right? Dario tells me you confessed it all somewhere above the treeline. Very moving, apparently."

"Dario talks too much," Casper said.

"He does." Something flickered across Marchetti's face—not quite pain, but adjacent to it. She filed it away and continued. "So here is my offer, engineer, and I will make it exactly once, because unlike you I respect both our time."

One of the lawyers stepped forward with a leather folder. Marchetti didn't take it; she simply gestured, and another lawyer opened it and began to read in a dry liturgical voice: full exoneration. The verdict of the Viaduct 7 inquiry vacated. Her license restored. A public statement, drafted by the railway company, acknowledging that the original engineering warnings had been suppressed by parties unnamed. Compensation. An apology printed in the papers she owned, which was to say, most of them.

"Cleared," Marchetti said when the reading was done. "Not pardoned—pardons are for the guilty. Cleared. Your name restored in every record that matters. The eleven men's families notified that the woman they blamed was innocent. Everything you have wanted for nine years, Sandoval. Everything you ran from wanting." She paused, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was the most frightening register it had. "All it costs is a fire."

"The ledgers."

"The ledgers, the film, the photographs. Every scrap. My men will burn them here, in front of you, and you will sign a statement saying the expedition's findings were inconclusive. Then you walk away clean, rich, and restored, and those eleven names stay scratched in a rock in a tunnel nobody will ever open." Marchetti spread her hands. "Weigh it honestly. What are they to you? Men you never met. Dead before you ever heard their names. Against eleven other men whose graves you visit, whose faces you see when you close your eyes, whose deaths have been eating you alive since before some of these workers were born. One set of ghosts or the other, engineer. That's the arithmetic. Choose."

The wind moved through the yard. Somewhere down the sidings an ore car ticked as it cooled. Casper became aware, distantly, that her people had come down off the trail and were standing in a loose line behind her—Ruth with her arm through Emilio's, the survivors, Dario apart from the others, unable to look at his aunt and unable to look away.

Emilio spoke first. His English was slow, accented, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

"She knows our names," he said. Not to Marchetti. To Casper. "In the tunnel. She wrote them down. All of them. She made us say them, so she would not write one wrong."

Ruth's voice, quiet: "Two hundred and six. We counted twice."

Marchetti's composure did not crack, but Casper saw the effort it took, the way a dam shows its seams before it shows its break. "Sentiment," Marchetti said. "Very touching. It doesn't feed anyone, it doesn't free anyone, and it won't bring back your bridge, Sandoval. I'm offering you your life back. There is no one else in this valley who can offer that, and there never will be again. This window closes when I say it closes."

"You're right about one thing," Casper said.

Marchetti raised an eyebrow.

"It is arithmetic." Casper reached into her coat, slowly, and the policemen tensed, and she drew out—not a weapon, not the ledgers—a small oilcloth packet, worn soft at the folds, that she had carried over the pass and up the switchbacks and through the whiteout without ever quite deciding why. She unfolded it. Inside, creased nearly to translucence, was a single page torn from an old survey report, and on it, in her own younger handwriting, the words *PIER 3 AND PIER 5 DO NOT MEET LOAD SPEC — DO NOT CERTIFY*, and beneath it, stamped in red, the word OVERRIDDEN, and beneath that, a signature that was not hers.

"My list only ever had eleven names on it," Casper said. "Yours has two hundred and six, and counting. So let's do the arithmetic your way, Ilsa. You're offering me eleven ghosts in exchange for two hundred and six. That's not a bargain. That's me agreeing to dig the hole deeper and lie down in it." She refolded the page, returned it to her coat, over her heart, where it had lived for nine years. "You want to know the worst part? Three days ago, halfway up that mountain, I would have taken your offer. I'd have hated myself cleanly and efficiently for the rest of my life, the way I've been doing it since the bridge came down, and I'd have called that living. But then I sat in a tunnel while a man showed me where his friends scratched their names into rock so somebody, someday, would know they'd been people. And I understood something."

"Enlighten me."

"You keep calling them evidence. Paper. Canisters. Things that burn." Casper took one step closer, close enough to see the pulse ticking in Marchetti's throat. "They're not evidence. They're testimony. Evidence burns. Testimony doesn't—not once it's been spoken, not once two hundred and six names have been read aloud by people who survived to carry them. You can't buy it back, Ilsa. You can't burn it out of us. All you can buy is my silence, and my silence is the one thing in this whole valley that has already cost enough."

For a long moment Marchetti said nothing. The wind gusted, flattening the grass along the sidings, and somewhere a crow complained from the water tower.

Then Marchetti smiled, thin and cold as frost on a rail. "You understand that I cannot permit this."

"I know."

"And you understand what happens if you take one more step toward that station house. Warrants. Obstruction. Theft of corporate property. They will take those documents from you at the county line regardless, and you will spend years in court learning how little your righteousness weighs."

"Probably," Casper agreed. "But here's the thing about warrants, Ilsa—they're public. Courtrooms are public. You've just spent five minutes telling me, in front of twelve witnesses, that you'll use every tool you own to bury this. Good. Say it louder. Because the journalist I wired from the basin is already sitting in that station house, and she has been since dawn, and everything you've said since I walked into this yard has been said within earshot of a stenographer who works for me, not you."

Marchetti went very still.

Behind her, one of the policemen glanced at another, and the glance lasted a half second too long, and Casper—who had spent nine years learning to read the small betrayals of men's faces, who had once predicted a bridge's failure from a hairline crack no one else could see—saw it plainly. The uniforms were calculating. The suits were calculating. And the arithmetic, suddenly, was running the other direction.

"You should know," Casper went on, conversationally now, turning so her voice carried to the whole yard, "that copies exist. Three sets. One goes to the inspector general's office whether I'm arrested or not. One is already in the mail. And one stays with a medic named Ruth Okafor, who has treated every one of these workers and photographed every name in that tunnel, and who is, I'm told, extremely difficult to intimidate." She looked back at Marchetti. "You built your empire on the idea that the mountains would keep your secret. But mountains don't keep secrets, Ilsa. They just wait for someone willing to climb them."

Marchetti's eyes moved past her—to the line of survivors, to Emilio standing straight despite the bandages, to her nephew with his ruined face and steady gaze—and Casper watched her do the final calculation, the one that ends with a woman realizing the story has left her hands.

"This isn't over," Marchetti said quietly.

"No," Casper said. "It's finally starting."

Down the valley, around the long curve, the morning freight came whistling in toward the railhead—toward the station house, the waiting journalist, the inspector who had arrived on it, and whatever came next. Casper turned her back on Ilsa Marchetti and began to walk toward the sound.
