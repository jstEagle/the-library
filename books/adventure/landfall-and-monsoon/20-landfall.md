## 20. Landfall

The morning freight came down the valley like a promise kept.

Casper heard it before she saw it—the long low moan of a whistle bouncing off the canyon walls, doubling and redoubling until the whole gorge seemed to be talking to itself. Then the engine rounded the curve below the washout, black smoke standing up straight in the still air, and the sound resolved into something almost gentle: the rhythm of wheels on rail, the ordinary music of a line that worked, that carried mail and flour and men going home. She had not heard that music in seven years. She had forgotten how it settled into the bones, how it made a body stand straighter without deciding to.

She walked toward it along the old cart road that paralleled the river, the survivors strung out behind her in a loose, stumbling line. Emilio walked near the front now, his bandages gray with dust but his eyes clear, and when the whistle sounded again he lifted one hand toward the engine as if greeting an old friend. Maybe he was. The last train most of those workers had seen had carried them up into the mountains against their will. This one was coming to carry their story back down into the world.

The railhead at Puerto Alto was nothing—a water tower, a loading ramp, a station house with paint peeling off it in long curls—but this morning it had grown a crowd. Word traveled faster than any of them had. Half the valley seemed to have turned out: dockhands from the timber yard, women from the market with baskets still on their arms, children perched on the loading ramp who had never seen anything come down from the pass except rumor. And waiting beside the station house, exactly where the morning freight would disgorge them, stood two figures Casper had been walking toward for eleven days without knowing their names.

One was a railway inspector—she could tell by the badge pinned to his coat lapel, brass gone dull with age, and by the way he stood, weight even on both feet, hands clasped behind him, a man accustomed to waiting for other people's confessions. He was broad and gray-haired, and there was something in his face that reminded her uncomfortably of the men who had sat in judgment over her after Viaduct 7. She would learn later that his name was Aldous Renner, that he had spent thirty years inspecting mountain lines and had watched three of them close for reasons no report ever quite explained. This morning he only looked at her and waited.

The other was a journalist. Younger, a woman, notebook already open in her hand despite the wind, pencil tucked through the spiral binding like a knife through a belt. She had the restless posture of someone who had been standing in one place too long and too eagerly—shifting her weight, tapping her boot heel, watching the treeline above the station as if she expected the mountains themselves to hand her a story. Her name, when Casper learned it, was Vess. Marisol Vess. She wrote for a paper three hundred miles downstream that most people in this valley had never read, which was precisely why Marchetti had never bothered to buy her.

Casper saw all of this in the first thirty seconds, and she understood, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like gravity, what the next five minutes were going to cost her.

* * *

Marchetti had not given up. Casper had known she wouldn't—had counted on it, in a way, because a woman like Ilsa Marchetti did not lose quietly, and a quiet loss was the only kind that stayed lost. Even as Casper walked down toward the railhead, Marchetti's people were arriving ahead of them. Two cars had come up the valley road in the night; she'd seen their headlights crawling the switchbacks like slow yellow insects. Now the men stood about the station house in dark coats, casual as dockworkers, and among them Casper recognized faces from the ambush at the washout—the heavyset one with the scarred knuckles, the tall one who had put the bullet graze across Teo's arm. They had not come to fight. Not yet. They had come to negotiate, and they had brought with them the only currency they knew would matter.

They had brought her past, gift-wrapped.

She saw it assembling even before she reached the crowd: a lawyer in a good gray suit, standing slightly apart, holding a leather folder the way a man holds a thing he intends to open at the exact right moment. Casper had spent seven years learning to read men like that. The folder was thin. Thin folders held verdicts, or they held pardons, and there was only one verdict in the world anyone could offer Casper Sandoval.

Marchetti herself stood between the lawyer and the inspector, positioned with a strategist's precision—at the center of every sightline, so that whichever way the morning broke, the story would have her face in it. She wore traveling clothes that were too clean for the valley, wool the color of wet slate, and she had recovered her composure since the treeline. Whatever calculation she had begun up there, she had finished it overnight, and the answer she'd arrived at was the oldest one in the book: if you cannot bury the evidence, buy the witness.

Casper stopped ten paces away, at the edge of the gravel apron where the loading ramp met the station house, and let the crowd close around her survivors like water closing around stones. Ruth found Emilio's sleeve. Dario stood beside his aunt and did not look at her. Teo was still downriver with the slowest of the rescued workers, sheltering in the cave below the snowline, and Casper felt his absence the way you feel a missing stair in the dark—an absence with a shape, an absence she kept stepping toward.

"Miss Reyes." Marchetti used the false name deliberately, loudly, a performance for the crowd. "Or should we say—"

"Don't," Casper said. It came out flat and quiet, and it stopped the sentence the way a spike stops a rail. Around them the crowd went still, sensing the shape of something about to break.

Marchetti's lawyer stepped forward instead, opening the folder with a small practiced motion, and held it out. Inside, on the top page, Casper could read the letterhead of a court she had not stood before in seven years, and beneath it, in type she did not need to squint at, the words she had spent seven years dreaming of and dreading in equal measure: *In re the collapse of Viaduct 7 — petition to vacate findings.*

Full exoneration. Every charge dissolved. Her name restored to the rolls of licensed engineers, her record wiped clean, her exile ended—not by confession, not by truth, but by the signature of a woman whose company had built its new extension on chained men and burned camps, offered now because the ledgers in Casper's pack were worth more than eleven dead men's reputations, including her own.

"All of it goes away," Marchetti said, and her voice was gentle, almost kind, which was somehow worse than if it had been cold. "The verdict, the blame, the name they gave you. You walk out of this valley clean, Miss Sandoval. You can be an engineer again. All you have to do is give me the ledgers and the photographs, and forget you ever climbed the mountain."

Casper took the folder. She held it in both hands, and she read the top page all the way through, every clause, because she owed it that much honesty—to know exactly what she was refusing and why. The wind flipped the corner of the second sheet. Behind the exoneration order, she glimpsed a second document: a confidentiality agreement, standard boilerplate, binding silence in perpetuity. That was the real price. It always had been. Not the ledgers—they could print new ledgers, though these ones named names and tonnages and pay rates that no honest book had ever contained. The price was her mouth. Again. Forever.

Seven years ago, she had signed a different version of that same document without knowing it. When the inquiry closed over Viaduct 7, when the commission's final report named her and buried the survey memos that proved the piers had been flagged, she had told herself the fight was unwinnable, that survival was its own kind of victory, that a live engineer in exile was worth more than a dead one in a courtroom. She had run. She had changed her name and taken work hauling freight on a river and told herself every night that the running was prudence.

It had been surrender. She saw that now, standing in the gravel with the paper in her hands, seeing it whole for the first time because the mountain had stripped everything else away. Eleven men had died under her bridge because someone above her had decided the schedule mattered more than the piers, and she had let them make her the one who paid, because paying was quieter than fighting. And then she had watched a second crime get built on the same foundation—literally the same company, the same accounting, the same contempt for human life measured in tons per mile—and she had very nearly let the pattern hold.

"No," she said.

Two syllables. The crowd heard it. Marchetti heard it. The lawyer heard it, and began, smoothly, to close the folder, because men like him understood refusal instantly and moved on to the next instrument. But Casper wasn't finished. Refusal alone was just another way of running—refusal kept the choice private, kept the story safe inside her own head, and privacy was the terrain where Marchetti had always won.

So Casper turned around.

* * *

Marisol Vess had been watching from beside the station house, notebook open, pencil moving, capturing everything—the folder, the offer, the refusal—and when Casper turned to face her, the journalist's eyes went wide and then steady, the way eyes do when a person realizes the moment they trained for has actually arrived.

"You wanted a story," Casper said to her, loud enough for the crowd, loud enough for the inspector, loud enough for Marchetti's men standing in their dark coats at the edge of the gravel. "Here it is."

She unslung the pack.

Eleven days it had ridden her back—up the river, over the switchback road, through Tunnel Nine, across the basin where the snow had come early and hard. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth against the weather, were the expedition's ledgers: the pay books from the labor camps, the quota sheets written in a foreman's blunt hand, the requisition records for chain and bolt-cutters and lime, the transport manifests that matched worker counts against grave registrations. Beside them, in a tin box that had once held survey instruments, were Ruth Okafor's photographs—the wall of names in the hidden gallery, scratched into rock by men who had known no one would ever read them, photographed now by a woman who had decided someone would.

Casper set the pack on the loading ramp, opened it, and laid everything out where the morning light could reach it. Ledgers. Tin box. The survivor's note from Camp Ash. Emilio's statement, taken down in Ruth's careful handwriting during the nights in Tunnel Nine, signed with a mark because no one had ever taught him to write his own name. She arranged it all with a surveyor's precision, square to the edge of the ramp, because order mattered, because chaos was what people like Marchetti hid inside.

"My name is Casper Sandoval," she said, and the sound of it in her own voice, spoken aloud in daylight, in front of strangers, hit her like the first cold plunge of the river—shocking, then clarifying, then strangely calm. "I was the assistant engineer on Viaduct 7. I flagged the flawed piers in writing six weeks before it fell. I was overruled, and then I was blamed, and then I ran, and I have been hiding in your valley under another name ever since. Everything in this pack belongs to the public record. Take it."

Vess wrote so fast her pencil audibly squeaked. The inspector, Renner, stepped forward and put his hand on the tin box—not taking it, just touching it, establishing custody the way a man establishes a fact—and Casper watched him look at the photographs of the gallery wall, name after name after name, and saw something move across his face that she recognized from the inside: the slow, reluctant dawn of a man realizing that the system he had served for thirty years had failed somewhere fundamental, and that he was the one standing closest to the failure when the light finally reached it.

"And Ilsa Marchetti," Casper continued, turning back to face the woman in the slate-gray wool, "knew about the labor camps. Knew before the expedition ever went up the river. Sent her own nephew along to watch, and when the expedition found the camps and photographed them, she sent armed men to bury the evidence and everyone carrying it. Those are the facts. Let her lawyers spend the rest of her fortune trying to prove otherwise."

Marchetti's face did something complicated. Casper watched it happen from ten feet away: first the reflexive denial, the quick confident smile, the wave of the hand that said *this woman is unstable, this woman is a criminal, who would believe her*—and then, as the crowd's murmur shifted, as the dockhands leaned in and the market women started repeating what they'd heard to each other in low voices, as Renner's hand stayed firmly on the tin box and Vess's pencil kept moving, the smile curdled. Because Marchetti had built her life on a simple arithmetic: stories cost money, money buys silence, silence buries everything. And here was a disgraced engineer with nothing—no money, no license, no name worth having—handing the whole story away for free, in public, to witnesses who could not be bought because they hadn't been approached, to a newspaper that couldn't be killed because nobody important read it.

You cannot purchase what has already been given away. Marchetti understood commerce better than anyone alive, and in that moment she understood, visibly, that she had lost—not the war, perhaps, not the appeals and the indictments and the years of lawyers still to come, but the morning, the valley, the crowd, the light. The story had left her hands for good, and it was not coming back.

Her men scattered. Casper watched it happen with a satisfaction she had not anticipated and could not fully explain—the scarred-knuckle man first, peeling away toward the cars as if summoned by some silent signal, then the others, one by one, dark coats dissolving up the valley road. Men hired to bury things do not stay to be photographed burying them. Vess got two of their faces anyway, quick shots with the small camera she carried in her coat pocket, and Casper suspected those photographs would matter more than any testimony she herself could give.

Marchetti lingered longest. She stood in the emptying gravel with her lawyer folding the useless folder shut, and she looked at Casper with an expression that was not hatred, exactly, and not respect, and not fear—or maybe all three braided together into something Casper had no name for. Seven years ago, on the worst day of Casper's life, men like this had looked at her the same way: as a problem to be managed, a variable to be priced. Today the pricing had failed.

"This isn't mercy," Marchetti said finally, quietly, to Casper alone, or as alone as anyone could be in front of forty witnesses. "You understand that. You've traded one reckoning for another. They'll take your license proceedings, your old case, everything. You'll spend years in rooms like the one that destroyed you."

"I know," Casper said. And she did know—it was the strangest part, the part she had accepted somewhere on the switchback road in the dark, somewhere between the rope bridge and Tunnel Nine: that justice was not a door out of the fire but a door deeper in, and she was walking through it with her eyes open. "But this time I'll be in the room on purpose."

Marchetti turned and walked to her car. Nobody stopped her. Nobody needed to. The valley had already voted.

* * *

Afterward, when the crowd had thinned to stragglers and the freight had gone on down the line, Casper sat on the loading ramp with her legs hanging over the edge, the pack lighter than it had been in eleven days, lighter than it had been in seven years, and watched the inspector work.

Renner spread the ledgers across the ramp boards with a methodical care that she recognized—engineer's hands, surveyor's patience—and asked questions in a low voice that never hurried and never relented. Where was Camp Ash. How many men. What were the quotas. Who signed the requisitions. He asked about the extension's alignment, and when Casper described the grade above the treeline, the tunnel mouths, the trestle footings she had read from memory, he nodded as one engineer nods to another, and something in the nod undid her more than the exoneration offer had. For seven years no one had asked her a professional question. For seven years her knowledge had been contraband, hidden, worthless, dangerous. Now it was evidence. Now it was the spine of a case. Now it mattered.

Vess sat cross-legged nearby, transcribing, and asked her own questions—different ones, human ones. About Emilio. About the names on the wall. About Teo, still up the valley in the shelter cave with the slowest survivors and the storm coming down behind them, and Casper answered honestly: she didn't know yet. The wire at the station house was being repaired; the first word from the cave might come tonight or tomorrow or not at all. Vess wrote that down too, the uncertainty, the not-knowing, because a story that pretended to certainty was just propaganda wearing a nicer coat.

Dario came and stood at the edge of the ramp while the light went long and gold across the rails. His face was still marked where the rope bridge had tried to take him, bruised yellow-green now fading, and he looked at his aunt's empty parking place on the gravel and then at Casper, and said, quietly, "I'll testify. Everything. The transmissions, the instructions, all of it."

Casper looked at him for a long moment. He had been sent up the river to betray them, and somewhere between the washout and the tunnel he had chosen otherwise, and the choosing had cost him his family's name and gotten him half-killed on a rope bridge, and here he was offering to finish the job in a courtroom. There was no forgiveness to give—he didn't need hers, he needed the courts'—but there was something else, something she recognized from her own side of the glass: the specific relief of a person who has finally stopped lying to himself.

"Rest first," she said. "Testimony keeps. The truth doesn't spoil."

Emilio came and sat beside her on the ramp, on her left, where the sun was warmest, and put his hand on the boards between them, palm down, fingers spread, the way workingmen sit. He didn't say anything for a while. Neither did she. Then, slowly, in the fragmenting Spanish they had shared across eleven days of mountain, he said that tonight he would sleep in a bed, and that tomorrow he would go home to his village, and that when the hearings came he would stand in whatever room they put him in and say his name out loud, his full name, the one no foreman had ever bothered to learn.

"They will learn it now," Casper said.

"Yes," Emilio said. "Now they will learn it."

Down the line, the evening freight whistled at the far curve, and the sound came up the valley and wrapped around the station house, and Casper sat in it—sat inside the ordinary music of a working railroad, the sound she had loved before it broke her, the sound she had fled, the sound that had followed her home—and she understood that the morning's victory was also a summons. Renner had already told her, gently, professionally, what came next: formal statements, sworn depositions, the reopening of the Viaduct 7 file, and her own name entered into the record not as a fugitive restored but as a witness who had waited seven years to speak. There would be rooms. There would be lawyers. There would be Marchetti's people, and worse, the state's own machinery, grinding slow, asking her to account not only for what the company had done but for what she had done with her silence.

She was not afraid of it. That surprised her most of all. She had spent seven years afraid of exactly that room, and now, sitting on a loading ramp with the evidence given away and her true name spoken aloud to a valley full of strangers, she found she had no fear left to spend. It had all been spent on the mountain—on the switchbacks, on the whiteout, on the counting of turns against the wind. Fear, it turned out, was a finite supply, and she had run the account down to zero doing something that mattered.

Ruth came down from the station house with four cups of coffee from the agent's stove, handed them around—Emilio, Dario, Vess, Casper—and the four of them drank it lukewarm and too sweet, sitting on the ramp as the light died behind the western ridge, and nobody said anything important, because everything important had already been said, and what remained was only the waiting.

The telegraph key in the station office began to click sometime after dark. The agent came out onto the platform with a flimsy sheet of paper in his hand, and Casper watched him read it, and watched his face change, and watched him walk toward her across the boards with the message held out and the lamp light catching the words:

*Shelter cave reports all accounted for. Teo and party descending at first light.*

Casper read it twice. Then she folded it into her breast pocket, over her heart, where a man carries the things he cannot afford to lose, and she looked down the dark line toward the north, toward the cave, toward Teo, and she said, out loud, to no one and to everyone:

"Then we wait for him. And then we go and tell them everything."

The hearing was set for Monday.
