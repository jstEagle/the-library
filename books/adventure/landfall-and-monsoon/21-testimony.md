## 21. Testimony

The hearing room had been a customs house once, back when the river carried more freight than memory. Ruth Okafor noticed such things now—the way old buildings kept the shapes of their purposes, the way a room built for weighing goods could be refitted, with a bench and a flag and a stenographer's machine, into a room built for weighing truth. The high windows let in a cold October light that fell in long amber bars across the polished floor, and in that light the dust hung motionless, as if the air itself were holding its breath.

She had been in hearing rooms before. Field hospitals had their own tribunals—morning counts, casualty reports, the flat arithmetic of who could be saved and who could not. She had learned to speak in those rooms without flinching. But she had never sat in one where the arithmetic was moral, where the numbers on the ledger were men, where every figure had a name scratched into tunnel rock by someone who had still believed, in that final hour of scratching, that a name might outlive a chain.

She sat in the second row, behind the rail, beside Teo. His arm was still in a sling, the bullet graze at his shoulder healing into a pale seam that he touched absently, the way a man touches a scar he is still deciding whether to be proud of. He had dressed for the occasion in a white shirt stiff with factory creases, and his boatman's hands—knuckles like river stones, nails permanently darkened by tar—looked almost apologetic folded in his lap. He kept glancing at the doors.

"She'll come," Ruth said quietly.

"She always comes." Teo's voice was low, roughened by the cold he'd never fully shaken from the shelter cave. "That's the problem with her. She comes. Even when running would be kinder to everybody, including her."

The doors opened at nine o'clock precisely, and Casper Sandoval came through them in a gray coat that had been brushed clean but not new, her hair pinned back with the severity of a woman who had decided that ornamentation was a form of hiding. She walked the aisle the way Ruth had once watched her walk a decayed trestle—testing each step before committing her weight, but never stopping, never turning back. Her eyes found the second row and held there: Teo, Ruth, Emilio.

Emilio had wanted to sit closer. They had all wanted to sit closer. But the workers were seated along the far wall under the eye of a court officer, twelve of them, the twelve well enough to travel, and Emilio had insisted on standing with them before taking his place. He was thinner now than when Ruth had first seen him in the ashes of Camp Ash, but the fever was gone and the color had come back to his face, and when Casper passed him he touched her elbow—just the elbow, just for a moment—and she did not flinch from it.

Ruth had learned, over the weeks of his convalescence in the river town, that Emilio flinched from almost nothing anymore. There was a terrible freedom in a man who had already survived the worst thing. He laughed easily now. He ate like a man at a feast. And he had made Ruth promise, with a gravity that sat strangely on his young face, that she would photograph the hearing itself—not for evidence, he said, but so the workers would know they had been seen in a room with windows, in a room where men in suits had to listen.

The presiding examiner was a federal man named Corliss, dry as August grass, with spectacles that caught the window light and turned his eyes into two small blank coins. He read the charges in a voice like a clerk reading a freight manifest: obstruction of inquiry, conspiracy to conceal, the deaths at the extension camps entered into the record as counts, each one a syllable that landed in the silent room like a dropped stone into still water.

And then, separately, in a paragraph that Corliss read without inflection and the gallery received without mercy: the matter of Viaduct Seven, and the question of whether the engineer Casper Sandoval, then employed by the Cordillera & Pacific, had failed in her duty of disclosure.

Ruth watched Casper's hands on the rail of the witness stand. They were steady. That steadiness frightened Ruth more than shaking would have. Shaking would have meant the body was telling the truth the mouth might soften. This was a woman who had decided, in some interior room Ruth would never see, exactly what she was going to carry and how, and had picked it up like a pack before a long climb.

* * *

They put Marchetti on first.

Ruth had expected a dragon and got something worse: a tired, elegant woman in charcoal wool, who answered questions in a voice like silk being folded, who conceded everything small and denied everything that mattered. Yes, she had financed the survey. Yes, she had engaged men to "ensure the safe recovery of her people." No, she had no knowledge of conscripted labor. No, she had never seen the ledgers. When Corliss produced the ledgers themselves—recovered from the high basin, water-stained at the corners, Ruth's own photographs of their pages projected onto a screen in gray and white—Marchetti looked at them the way one looks at weather in another country.

"I commissioned a survey of a rail corridor," she said. "What happened in the camps is a matter for the contractors. I have been as much deceived as anyone."

In the second row, Teo made a sound low in his throat, and Ruth put her hand on his good arm.

The cross-examination went on through the morning. Marchetti's lawyer was a silver-haired man with a voice like a well-bred saw, and he worked methodically to make the whole affair sound like a dispute between companies, a disagreement about paperwork, a tragedy that no reasonable person could have foreseen. He was good. Ruth had to admit he was good. He took the horror of the camps and, sentence by sentence, wrapped it in gauze until it looked like mismanagement.

But he made one mistake. He called Emilio's testimony "the account of a single aggrieved laborer of uncertain immigration status," and he said it while looking at the jury, not at the far wall where the twelve men sat.

Ruth would remember for the rest of her life the sound that went through the room then—not a gasp, not an outcry, but a shift, a settling, like snow deciding to slide. The twelve men along the wall did not move. They simply sat up straighter, all of them at once, the way soldiers do. And the jury saw it. Everyone saw it.

* * *

Casper took the stand after the recess.

She was sworn in, and Ruth noticed that she gave her name fully, plainly—Casper Elena Sandoval—no hesitation on the surname, no softening. For three years she had been someone else in Puerto Vado, a woman of odd jobs and borrowed names, and now she handed her true name across the room like a document, like something with a weight that had to be signed for.

Corliss began with the extension. He was gentle with her, or as gentle as a dry man could be, walking her through the route upriver, the first survey marker, the burned camp, the smashed radios, the tunnel. And Casper answered in her flat surveyor's cadence, distances and bearings and times, precise as a chart. But when he asked her about Tunnel Nine—about the side gallery, about the names—her cadence broke.

Ruth had photographed that gallery. She had knelt in the cold dark with her camera and her lantern and photographed every name, every date, every crude star or cross scratched beside a name by a man who wanted to leave proof he had been a person. There were forty-one names. Ruth knew them now the way she knew the bones of the hand. She had printed them and given copies to every worker who could read, and Emilio had read them aloud, one by one, in the courtyard of the boarding house, and the other men had corrected his pronunciation and added what they knew—this one had a brother in the valley, this one sang, this one owed Emilio two cigarettes and a joke.

So when Corliss asked Casper to describe the gallery, and Casper said, "There are forty-one names," and then stopped, and the room waited, and she said, "I know them now. All of them. Emilio reads them to me. I asked him to. I wanted—" and there her voice went, not to pieces, but to some lower register, some place below the surveyor's flatness, "I wanted to be sure I never again confused a number with a man. I did it once. Eleven times."

The room did not breathe.

Corliss let the silence stand, and then, quietly: "Tell the panel about Viaduct Seven."

And Ruth, who had heard the confession once before, in a high camp above the treeline with snow coming down and the truth pulled out of Casper like a splinter, heard it again—heard it made public, made permanent, made part of the record that stenographers transcribe and archives keep.

Casper told it without ornament. She had flagged the piers. The test borings showed the gravel stratum was unstable, the footings under-specified for the flood-year loads. She had written the memo. Her supervisor had told her the schedule would not move, the financing would not move, and that if she persisted she would find her signature on a much shorter career. The memo was buried—she had proof of that now, proof that had surfaced during the investigation, a carbon copy in a dead clerk's effects. The bridge stood. The bridge fell. Eleven men.

"And you were charged."

"I was charged. The finding was that I failed to escalate. That is true. I did fail. I could have gone over my supervisor's head. I could have gone to the press. I could have refused to sign the completion certificate. I signed it. I told myself I had no choice, which is what we all tell ourselves, and then when the bridge fell and they needed someone to blame, they chose the woman who had complained, because a complainer is a convenient culprit." She paused. Her hands on the rail had gone white. "I accepted the verdict because fighting it would have meant naming the men above me, and I was afraid. I ran. I ran for three years. And I will tell this panel honestly: when Ilsa Marchetti found me, my first instinct was to refuse, because I recognized the route, and I knew what that corridor had cost before, and I knew that going back meant everything I buried would come up out of the ground with me. I refused. I was only forced up that mountain by a debt collector and my own cowardice wearing the mask of obligation."

"Is that how you see it? Cowardice?"

"I see it clearly now. I had a chance to be brave at Viaduct Seven and I chose the small survival. On the mountain, I got a second chance, and I had eleven names to teach me the difference." She looked, then, not at the panel, not at Marchetti's silver lawyer, but at the far wall, at the twelve men sitting straight as pilings. "I am not asking this panel for mercy. I am asking it to write down what I say, exactly, and to make the men above me answer for what they buried. And if my sentence must stand, let it stand. But let it stand beside theirs."

* * *

The cross-examination was a knife-fight conducted in perfect manners.

The silver-haired lawyer rose, smoothed his jacket, and began, as Ruth had known he would, with the collapse. He took Casper through the engineering reports page by page, and Casper met him blow for blow—she knew those reports better than he did, had dreamed them, had been haunted by their margins. When he suggested her memo had been "ambiguous," she recited its conclusion from memory, word for word, and asked him to explain which part of *catastrophic failure probable under seasonal loading* was ambiguous.

When he turned to the mountain—to the rescue, to the film canisters, to the ambush at the washout—he tried another blade: that she had led a private armed expedition, that men had been shot, that she had no authority. Teo stirred beside Ruth, and Ruth gripped his arm, because she could feel him composing the answer he would never be permitted to give.

Casper answered it for him, in her own way.

"Yes. Men were shot. They were shooting at us. They were shooting at men who had survived a labor camp and a burned camp and a winter they were sent up to die in, and they were shooting on behalf of the woman who sent them. I will accept responsibility for every decision I made on that grade. I would make every one of them again. If the panel wishes to charge me for refusing to hand the evidence of forty-one dead men to the people who killed them, then I invite the panel to charge me, and I will plead guilty with a clear conscience, and I will do it from a cell."

The lawyer sat down. Corliss called the recess.

In the corridor, Ruth found Casper standing at the high window, looking down at the street where the river showed between buildings, a gray gleam of it, going about its business.

"You gave them everything," Ruth said.

"I gave them what I owed." Casper did not turn. "It's strange. I spent three years learning to be no one, and it turns out being no one is the heaviest work there is. Carrying a false name is heavier than carrying a true one. I don't know why I thought otherwise."

"Because the true one had eleven men attached to it."

"Yes." Casper's reflection in the glass nodded, a ghost agreeing with itself. "And now it has forty-one more. And Teo's shoulder. And Dario's whole ruined inheritance." Her mouth twisted. "I've become a collector of the dead."

"No," Ruth said. "You've become a witness. It's different work. The dead don't need a collector. They need someone who says the names out loud where the living can hear them."

Casper turned then, and for a moment Ruth saw her not as the granite woman of the mountain but as she had been in the tunnel, lantern-lit, human, tired. "Do you believe it will matter? The hearings. The ledgers. All of it."

Ruth thought of the boarding house courtyard, of Emilio reading names while the others corrected him, of the way the men had begun, in the last weeks, to walk the town like men and not like ghosts—applying for papers, finding work, one of them already apprenticed to a carpenter, another teaching Teo's nephew to mend nets. "It already has," she said. "Justice is slow and it's ugly and Marchetti may buy her way out of half of it. But the men are in the record now. You can't un-record a man. That's the whole trick of testimony, Casper. It's not that it fixes things. It's that it makes certain things impossible to unknow."

* * *

The verdicts came at the end of the second week.

Marchetti was not destroyed—women like her were never destroyed, they were demoted, relocated, rebranded—but she was finished in the valley. Two of the contractors went to prison. The extension was halted by order, its grade left to the weather, and there was talk—real talk, printed in the papers Ruth now read over breakfast—of the workers receiving land grants and back wages, of the corridor being rebuilt honestly or not at all.

And for Casper Sandoval, the panel found what she had asked them to find: culpability, shared, proportioned at last among the men who deserved it, but not erased. Her old verdict was set aside. A new one was entered. She would serve. Supervised service, the sentence read—engineering service, public works, the mountain lines.

Teo heard it standing, his sling forgotten, and when the gavel came down he turned to Ruth with an expression she had never seen on his weathered face—something between grief and celebration, the face of a man watching a door close and a door open in the same wall.

"They're sending her back to the mountains," he said.

"They're sending her home," Ruth said.

Emilio was already moving down the row toward Casper, and the twelve workers were rising along the wall, and Ruth raised her camera—the one promise she had made—and photographed them: the woman in the gray coat standing to receive the sentence she had asked for, surrounded by men who had crossed a mountain to stand behind her, the October light falling through the high windows of a room that had once weighed goods and was now, at last, weighing something worth the scale.

But outside the customs house, on the steps, a reporter pressed a telegram into Ruth's hand—addressed to Casper, marked urgent, from a railway office in the north. Ruth broke the seal herself, read the two lines, and went cold.

They were rebuilding the high lines early. And the first bridge on the schedule was Viaduct Seven.
