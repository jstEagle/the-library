## 1. The Weight of Names

The border town was called Puerto Ceniza on the maps and something worse by the people who lived there, and Astrid Reyes had been crossing it for three days under a name that wasn't hers.

Marisol Vega. The passport was good — better than good, it was the kind of document you paid a man in Valparaíso too much money for and then stopped thinking about, because thinking about it made your hands sweat at checkpoints. *Marisol Vega, thirty-four, logistics consultant.* It even sounded like someone who belonged in a border town: a woman with nothing to declare and nowhere interesting to go.

Astrid Reyes, thirty-eight, mountain guide, had gone dark two years ago in a country that no longer issued her permits, in a second country that had revoked the ones she'd held before that, and in every outfitter's office between here and the ice fields, where her name sat in a shared blacklist database like a stain soaked through to the other side of the fabric.

She crossed the Aduana bridge at dawn because dawn was when the guards were bored rather than suspicious, and she walked the length of the riverfront with her duffel on one shoulder and her hands in her pockets, and she let Marisol Vega be boring. Boring was a skill. Boring had kept her fed and moving for twenty-six months.

Puerto Ceniza smelled of diesel and wet rope and the sweet rot of fruit barges that had come downriver from the high country and would never go back up. The Serrano ran wide and brown-green here at its lower reach, carrying snowmelt from mountains she could not see from the waterline but could feel, the way you feel an old fracture before rain. Somewhere up there — three hundred kilometers upstream, past the gorges and the falls and the closed valleys — the river began as ice.

She didn't look at the water long. Looking at water led to looking at lines on it, reading current, planning routes, and planning routes led to wanting things, and wanting things was how a person ended up standing in front of a permit office with a name everyone recognized and a file everyone had read.

*Reyes, A. Client fatality, 2016. Guide error suspected. License suspended pending review.* Then the second country, faster, no review at all: *Blacklisted. Do not contract.*

Suspected. That was the word they'd used, and suspicion turned out to have a longer shelf life than truth.

---

The job board outside the transport workers' hall was a sheet of plywood nailed to a telephone pole, layered with cards gone soft from river humidity. Astrid worked through them with the flat efficiency of a woman shopping for calories, not pleasure. Raft support, season-long, licensed guides only. Trail crew, six weeks, no experience necessary, pay that was an insult dressed as opportunity. Cargo escort, overland, references required.

Then, near the bottom, a card written in ballpoint on the back of a hotel receipt:

*MEDICAL ESCORT. Upcountry client, fragile. River + overland. 40/day + expenses. Discretion expected. Ask for Delia at the Hotel Comercio.*

Forty a day. Astrid had once been paid four hundred a day to put bankers on summits, and she'd earned it, and then she'd watched one of them walk off a ledge of rotten cornice into weather she'd told him twice they shouldn't be in, and after that the numbers stopped meaning anything. Forty a day was food. Forty a day was a bunk and another week of being nobody.

Discretion expected. That part she could deliver better than anyone alive.

The Hotel Comercio occupied the ground floor of a building that leaned slightly toward the river, as if listening for it. Inside, the air was coffee steam and flypaper and a radio playing news about mining concessions in the interior — the Cordillera Extraction Group, expanding its survey season, the announcer said, in the headwater districts. Astrid's jaw tightened without her permission. She made herself unclench it. Everyone in this half of the world had opinions about the consortium; Marisol Vega, logistics consultant, did not.

Delia turned out to be a small woman behind the desk with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair and eyes that did arithmetic on people the way accountants did it on money.

"You answered the card," Delia said.

"I read the card."

"Card says discretion."

"Card also says forty a day. I'm assuming one of those numbers is negotiable and it isn't mine."

Something moved at the corner of Delia's mouth — not quite approval, but adjacent to it. She took off her glasses, folded them, set them down. "Client's name is Voss. Efraim Voss. He's American, he's seventy-one, and his heart is finishing a conversation the rest of his body started years ago. Doctors here won't clear him for travel and doctors where he wants to go won't see him until he gets there, so what he needs is somebody to get him there alive, or mostly alive, and not ask more questions than the work requires."

"Where's there?"

"San Rafael de las Nieves. Hospital town, up the valley system." Delia named the route — river to the last navigable portage, then overland on the old survey roads — and Astrid felt the route settle into her body before her mind finished hearing it, the way a staircase settles into your legs. She knew those valleys. She'd guided in two of them, years ago, under her own name, back when the mountains still returned her calls.

"And why does a man dying of a bad heart need discretion?" Astrid asked. "Most dying men want noise. Family. Priests."

"This one wants quiet." Delia slid a photograph across the counter: an old man seated on a hotel bed, gaunt, white beard trimmed short, hands enormous and spotted and utterly still in his lap, the way hands are still when they've decided not to tremble. His eyes were the problem. They looked directly into the camera with an attention most healthy men never managed — the look of someone cataloguing you for later use.

"He was a prospector," Delia said. "Geological surveys, mineral claims, that world. He spent forty years in the headwaters. There are people who'd prefer he stayed there, and some of them have been asking after him in towns he passed through. Nothing you need to worry about. Just keep the itinerary close."

Astrid looked at the photograph a moment longer than she needed to. *There are people who'd prefer he stayed there.* In her experience, sentences like that were either salesmanship or a warning, and the difference was usually whether the speaker met your eyes afterward.

Delia met her eyes.

"When do you need an answer?" Astrid asked.

"Now. He wants to move tomorrow, ahead of the rains."

"Then I'm taking it."

---

She spent the afternoon doing what she always did before a job: inventory, route study, contingency. Not because the work demanded it — a medical escort down a well-traveled valley corridor was a milk run, the kind of job a first-season guide would yawn through — but because the ritual was load-bearing. Check the gear, check the map, check the exits. A body in motion with a checklist had no time to stand at a window looking north toward ranges it wasn't allowed to enter.

Except that evening she stood at the window anyway.

Her room above the transport hall had a view of exactly nothing — a courtyard, a dead truck, laundry lines — but beyond the rooftops, when the light went copper, she could see the first rampart of the cordillera, blue-black and impossibly far, holding the last sun the way a cupped hand holds water. Somewhere behind those walls of rock was a valley with a decree nailed across its entrance. CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY, since 1998, since the expedition that went in with eleven names and came out with none. Every guide of her generation knew the story the way sailors know a wreck. Nobody went up there. Nobody with a license, anyway.

*You're not going up there,* she reminded herself. *You're walking a sick old man downhill to a hospital. Two weeks, maybe less. You'll be paid, you'll move on, you'll still be Marisol Vega.*

It should have been enough. For two years it had been enough — enough was the whole architecture of her life now, a structure built entirely out of enough.

But grief doesn't check credentials. It arrived the way weather arrived off the glacier, without asking, and tonight it came in wearing Daniel Okafor's face.

Daniel. Twenty-nine. Venture capitalist with a grant-funded obsession and a smile that made clients forgive you anything. She'd told him twice the cornice was rotten. She'd told him a third time and started rigging the bypass line, and while her hands were busy with the anchor he'd stepped out onto it — one step, curious, weightless-seeming, the way people are right before physics remembers them — and the mountain had simply opened its hand and let him go, and she'd watched him fall nine hundred meters through weather so thick that even the falling looked gentle.

The inquiry had taken eleven months. *Guide error suspected.* Suspected because she'd been ten meters away and hadn't caught him. Suspected because the second guide on the route — a man with better connections and a worse memory — testified that Astrid had "seemed distracted." Suspected because suspicion needs somewhere to live, and a woman with no family, no firm, no country willing to vouch for her was a very convenient address.

She had replayed those ninety seconds so many times the tape had worn smooth. Some nights she caught him. Some nights the cornice held. Most nights she just stood at the anchor with the line in her hands and watched, again, because watching was the penance and the penance was somehow the point.

*Forty a day,* she thought. *Get the old man to the hospital. Don't ask questions.*

It was, she understood, the exact shape of a life she'd sworn she'd never settle for: small jobs, false names, rivers she wouldn't look at too hard. But a person could carry almost anything if she kept moving, and movement required money, and money currently came in denominations of forty.

She checked her kit one last time. Rope — yes, habit, even though the job needed none. First aid, the serious kind, trauma-grade, because a cardiac patient on a remote road was a rolling emergency and she liked her emergencies pre-empted. Satellite phone, rented under Marisol Vega's name. Maps of the corridor, commercial issue, the kind sold in gas stations, accurate to nothing smaller than a kilometer.

She did not look at the northern wall of the range again. She was proud of that, in a thin, exhausted way.

---

At seven the next morning, the Hotel Comercio's back room smelled of camphor and boiled coffee, and Efraim Voss was sitting upright on the edge of the bed fully dressed as though he'd been waiting all night for someone worth impressing.

Up close he was worse than the photograph and more alive than it. The gauntness had progressed to a kind of transparency — she could see the architecture of him, the tendons in his neck working as he breathed, the pulse ticking visibly at his temple. But the eyes were the same, direct and unhurried, and they went over her the way she went over terrain: slope, aspect, hazard, line.

"You're the guide," he said. Not a question.

"Escort," Astrid said. "I get you to San Rafael. That's the whole job."

"Delia said you were a guide." He said it mildly, testing the word's weight in the room.

"Delia says a lot of things."

Something flickered across his face that might have been amusement. He extended one of those enormous hands and she took it; his grip was dry and startlingly strong, a grip running on reserves it had no business having. "Efraim Voss."

"Astrid Vega."

The lie came out smooth. Twenty-six months of practice. But his eyes narrowed — not in suspicion, she realized, but in something closer to recognition, the way a man who has spent forty years reading country notices when a contour line doesn't quite match the ground.

"Well, Astrid Vega," he said, releasing her hand and reaching, slowly, painfully, toward the canvas bag on the chair beside his bed, "I hope you're better at your job than I am at mine. Because I've been lying about mine for forty years, and I'm nearly done."

He drew out a rolled tube of oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, and laid it on the blanket between them. And then he smiled — a real smile, sudden and boyish and terrible on that ruined face — and said:

"How do you feel about going upstream?"

* * *

Outside the window, far to the north, the cordillera stood in the early light with its closed valleys hidden behind its shoulders, and somewhere below the frost line a river that had never once given back what it took began, patiently, to run.
