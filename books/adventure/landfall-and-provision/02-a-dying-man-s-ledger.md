## 2. A Dying Man's Ledger

Astrid had heard that question before, in a dozen variations, from a dozen clients standing at trailheads with too much gear and not enough sense. *How do you feel about going upstream?* It was usually bravado. Usually it meant rapids they'd read about in a magazine and wanted to photograph themselves beside.

She looked at the oilcloth tube on the blanket between them and understood that this was not that.

"No," she said.

Voss laughed — a dry, papery sound, like wind through dead grass. "You haven't even asked where."

"I don't need to. You're dying, Mr. Voss. The contract says Puerto Esperanza, the clinic there, a doctor named Ferrán who owes me a favor. That's the whole job. Four days downriver, one night in town, and I'm done." She stood, collected her pack from against the wall, and did not put it on. "I've done my time carrying people toward things that kill them."

"Have you?" He said it mildly, without cruelty, which was worse. His eyes — gray going yellow at the rims, the eyes of a man whose liver was filing its resignation — stayed on her face with an attention she found hard to bear. "Sit down, Ms. Reyes. Or whatever your name is this week. I'll show you what you're refusing."

She should have left. Every instinct built over nine years of guiding, over two blacklists and one funeral, said *leave*. The smart move was always to leave early, before the client's dream became your problem, before their desperation got a rope around your waist.

But she sat down. She told herself it was because the bus to the next border crossing didn't leave until evening, and because the room was warm, and because her boots were still damp. All true. None of it the reason.

"Untie it yourself," she said. "My hands are cold."

* * *

The map came out of the oilcloth in sections, folded like a letter someone had written many times and never sent. It was hand-drawn — ink on survey linen, the kind of paper that survives decades folded into a coat pocket. The linework was confident, almost beautiful: contour lines stacked tight as piano keys, the river rendered as a single unbroken stroke running north into country where every other map Astrid had ever seen simply stopped.

She knew that blank space. Every guide who worked these waters knew it. The Serrano headwaters, closed by decree since 1998 — closed by decree, which was different from closed by nature. Decrees were written by men, and men wrote them for reasons, and the reasons were never printed on the map.

"You drew this," she said. Not a question. The hand was all over it.

"Efraim Voss draws everything he can't buy." He smoothed a section flat across his knees with hands that trembled only when he wasn't thinking about them. "I bought most of it, though. Bought it the way you buy anything worth having. Slowly, and from people who didn't know what they had."

The river on the map ran up through three gorges marked in Voss's cramped notation — *Redbank, Serpent Bend, the Falls (unrunnable)* — and then past a portage line, a camp symbol, and finally, near the top of the sheet, a small circle drawn twice over, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the linen. Beside it, in letters smaller than any other annotation on the map:

**La Cuna.**

"The Cradle," Astrid said.

"That's what the old trappers called it. Before the decree. A valley above the treeline where the glacier comes down to meet the river's first breath." Voss's voice had changed. She noticed it the way you notice a change in current under your boat — not with your ears but with your hands, some older instrument. "There's water under that valley, Ms. Reyes. More water than anyone has ever measured. An aquifer sealed under the moraine like a heart in a chest, and it wants out."

"And you want to measure it."

"I want to *prove* it." For a moment the tremor left him entirely, and she saw the man he must have been forty years ago — the kind of stubborn that gets people killed or rich or both. "I spent thirty years finding it. My partner spent thirty years believing me. And now the Cordillera Extraction Group is going to spend one survey season making sure no one else ever does."

Astrid looked at the map and felt the thing she had sworn off feeling: curiosity. It arrived the way weather arrives, without permission, low pressure building behind her sternum. She hated it. Curiosity was how guides died. Curiosity was why she'd once followed a client's route description into a couloir that wasn't on any map either, and carried what was left of him down on her back.

"1998," she said instead, keeping her voice flat as gravel. "That's when the valley closed. What happened?"

Voss went very still. Stillness, in a dying man, is expensive; she could see him paying for it.

"An expedition went in," he said. "Nine people. Surveyors, a journalist, two guides. They found what I found. They never came out." He began folding the map along its old creases, each fold precise, ritual. "The official record says avalanche. The decree says seismic hazard. The consortium says nothing at all, very loudly, through lawyers."

"And you say?"

He looked up at her then, and for the first time since she'd met him at the dock in Punta Roca — coughing into a red bandana, paying half up front in cash that smelled of diesel — Efraim Voss looked afraid.

"I say I'm the last person alive who knows where La Cuna is," he said, "and I am going to die in eleven weeks, maybe twelve, and if I die south of that valley, everything under that ice stays buried forever. Along with everyone who found it first."

* * *

She made herself useful after that, because usefulness was the only language she trusted. She boiled water for his tea. She counted his pills into the tin — the regimen had grown since the contract was signed, two new bottles he hadn't mentioned, and she noted the names without comment because commenting would have meant admitting she could read a treatment plan well enough to know it was palliative. She opened the window a hand's width to let the coal smoke out.

It was while she was doing this — kneeling, back to him, arranging pills by morning and night — that he said, conversationally:

"Your file says the client fell."

Her hands did not stop moving. That was the discipline, the whole discipline, nine years of it: keep the hands moving.

"My file," she said, "is a story two governments agreed on."

"Avalanche terrain, they wrote. Unroped approach. Guide error." He recited it the way he might recite coordinates, without inflection, and she understood he had read her file the way she had just read his pill bottles — thoroughly, silently, drawing conclusions. "I read it on the ferry. I also read the dissenting report. The one the second government buried. The one that says the client untied himself."

The tin lid clicked shut under her thumb, louder than she intended.

"There is no buried report," she said.

"There's always a buried report." She turned and found him watching her with that terrible mildness. "I've spent my life reading other people's ledgers, Ms. Reyes. Everyone keeps one. Most people just don't know what's written in theirs." He tapped the folded map against his knee. "You took a job escorting a stranger to a hospital for less than half your rate. A woman with your skills doesn't do that for money. She does it because she needs to be moving, and downstream is the only direction anyone will pay her to go."

"Say what you want, old man."

"I want to hire you properly. Not as a nurse. As a guide." He set the map on the blanket between them again, and this time he did not smile. "Upstream. To La Cuna. Before the survey season ends and the consortium seals the passes with equipment and lawyers and, if it comes to it, men with guns. I want to stand at the source of that river and write down what's there, in my own hand, witnessed, dated — so that it exists somewhere besides my head and this piece of linen. And then I want to come back down and give it to someone who can make it matter."

"And if you die on the way? Because you will, Mr. Voss. Maybe not in eleven weeks. Maybe in four days, on the water, in the cold." She heard her own voice go hard and was grateful for it; hardness was load-bearing. "What happens to your proof then? What happens to me, holding a dead prospector and a forbidden map in restricted territory, with my name already in two files?"

"Then you'll be exactly where you are now," he said quietly. "Carrying something that isn't yours, toward something you didn't choose, being blamed for a death that may not have been your fault." He let that sit. Outside, a dog barked twice and quit. "The difference is that this time, you'd get to decide whether to let go."

Astrid stood up. She walked to the window. Below, the street of the border town ran with meltwater, and porters moved crates under the sodium lamps, and somewhere a radio played a cumbia with more static than music. Ordinary world. Downstream world. The world where jobs ended and names could be shed and nothing ever had to be carried further than the next crossing.

Behind her, Voss began to cough — deep, wet, structural. It went on long enough that she turned, and long enough after that that she crossed the room and held the basin for him, and when it passed he was gray-lipped and grinning at her with bloodshot triumph, because he had understood something about her before she had: that she could not watch a man drown, even slowly, even honestly, even when looking away was the smart move.

"Four days to Puerto Esperanza," he wheezed. "You have four days to decide. I only need you to say yes before we pass the last fork. After that, the river decides for both of us."

"I'll think about it," she said, which was a lie, because she had already thought about it, in the way you think about a door you're pretending isn't open.

He closed his eyes. Within minutes his breathing slowed into the shallow rhythm the pills allowed him, and Astrid sat in the chair by the window with her damp boots drying and the map lying on the table where he'd left it — deliberately, she suspected, because he was a man who understood that you don't hand a curious person a locked box; you leave it in the room.

She didn't touch it. She was proud of that for almost an hour.

Then the light failed, and she lit the lamp, and the lamp threw its small circle across the table, and the edge of the oilcloth caught the glow like a shoreline catching a signal fire, and she told herself she was only going to look at the gorges again, just the gorges, just to assess the water for professional reasons, purely hypothetical.

Redbank. Serpent Bend. The Falls, unrunnable. The portage. And above them all, that circle pressed twice into the linen, hard enough to tear, the way a man presses a pen when he is writing the name of the place where his partner died.

La Cuna.

Astrid sat back. Her tea had gone cold. Somewhere below the frost line, she thought, a river was running patiently north, and it had been running for longer than her grief, longer than her guilt, longer than any decree, and it did not care what she decided — but a dying man in the bed behind her did, and tomorrow he would ask her again, and she was beginning to understand, with the reluctant, dawning horror of a woman watching her own reflection resolve in dark water, that she was going to say yes.

She blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and listened to him breathe.

In the morning, there was a knock at the hotel door — two knocks, polite, unhurried — and a voice from the corridor asking for Señor Voss in an accent that had never been within a hundred kilometers of these mountains.

Astrid was on her feet before the second knock finished, and her hand was already on the map.
