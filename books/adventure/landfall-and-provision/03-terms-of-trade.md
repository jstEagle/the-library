## 3. Terms of Trade

The fever broke at four in the morning and came back by nine.

Astrid knew the rhythm of it now, the way she knew the rhythm of weather in country she'd worked for twenty years — the way a man's skin went from furnace to ice and back again, the way his breathing changed pitch when the delirium was climbing toward its peak. She sat in the vinyl chair beside the hotel bed with a basin of water on her knees and wrung the cloth over Efraim Voss's forehead, and she thought about the arithmetic of her situation with the flat clarity that exhaustion sometimes granted.

She had been paid half up front. The other half waited in Puerto Consuelo, three hundred kilometers downstream, held by a medical transport broker who had looked at her the way everyone looked at her now — the name Reyes doing its quiet damage beneath the false one she'd signed. Two countries had struck her off their guide registries. A third would follow if anyone bothered to check the paperwork. What that meant in practice was simple: no outfitter would touch her, no insurance company would cover her, and the only work left was work like this — ferrying the dying through territory where nobody asked questions because nobody wanted answers.

Voss opened his eyes. For a moment they were clear, gray as river stone, and fixed on her with an attention that made her spine straighten.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"I slept."

"Liar." His voice was sandpaper. He tried to push himself up on one elbow, failed, and settled for turning his head to look out the window, where the morning light lay flat and white on the rooftops of San Telmo de los Andes. "How far to the hospital town?"

"Four days by road if the pass is open. Six if it isn't."

"And by river?"

The question landed wrong. Astrid set down the cloth. "There is no river route to Puerto Consuelo. Not from here."

"There's a river route to everywhere," Voss said, "if you're willing to go the long way."

* * *

He made her wait until evening. That was the first thing she noticed about how he operated — the deliberateness of it, the sense of a man rationing his remaining strength like a climber rationing rope. He ate half a bowl of soup she brought up from the kitchen downstairs, keeping down most of it, and then he asked her to close the curtains, and then he reached under the mattress.

The map came out folded into eighths, soft as cloth from handling, drawn in ink on survey paper that had yellowed along the creases. He unfolded it across the blanket between them, weighting the corners with the water glass, his pill bottle, her carabiner.

Astrid looked down and felt something in her chest go very still.

She knew rivers. She'd spent two decades reading them — the braided deltas, the gorge systems, the glacial feeds that ran milky with rock flour. This was the Serrano, or the upper reaches of it, rendered in a hand so precise it could only belong to someone who had walked every bank. But the headwaters were wrong. They were *there*, detailed, annotated with elevations and flow estimates and small careful notes in a woman's handwriting — *scour channel after melt, unreliable ford* — in a region that did not exist on any map Astrid had ever bought, been issued, or stolen. The valley above the last surveyed tributary was marked, on official maps, with the blank indifference reserved for places the government had decided not to look at.

"This is closed country," she said. "Closed since '98. There's a decree."

"There is." Voss's finger traced the river upward, past the falls, past the gorge he'd labeled *Serpent Bend* in letters gone brown with age, all the way to a small circle at the top of the page. Around the circle, in that same woman's hand: *La Cuna.* "Decrees are how you hide things. You don't fence them. You just stop printing the maps."

"Who drew this?"

His jaw worked. For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer, and then he said, "Someone who died for it," and turned the question aside like a man stepping around a hole in the ice. "Look at the annotations. Flow rates. Seasonal variation. That's thirty years of observation, Ms. Navarro" — her false name, delivered with a faint irony that told her he'd seen through it weeks ago and hadn't cared — "and none of it is in any database. Because there's something up there worth more than gold and less legal than cocaine, and the people who found it have spent a fortune making sure no one else goes looking."

Astrid sat back. Outside, the town was settling into its evening noise — dogs, motorcycles, a radio playing from the courtyard below. Ordinary sounds. She made herself listen to them, the way you check your footing on scree.

"Efraim. You hired me to take you to a hospital."

"I hired you to take me somewhere," Voss said. "I let you believe it was the hospital because I needed you to say yes first." He coughed, deep and wet, and rode it out with his eyes shut, and when he opened them again they were clear and hard and utterly without apology. "I'm dying. The doctors in Puerto Consuelo can make me comfortable for another few months, or I can spend what's left getting to La Cuna before the Cordillera Extraction Group finishes its survey season and seals that valley forever. Sealed with concrete this time. Sealed with law. After that, everything on this map stops existing — the aquifer, the evidence, the people they buried up there. It becomes a mine site and a memory hole."

"The evidence of what?"

"Murder," he said simply. "In 1998 an expedition went into those headwaters. Nine people. The official record says a serac collapse on the glacier. The official record is a lie purchased by the same consortium that's up there right now running seismic surveys behind a wall of security contractors. One person got out alive. She carried this map out with her, and she spent eleven years trying to get someone to listen, and then she drowned in the lower Serrano under circumstances the coroner called accidental." His voice cracked on the last word, and he covered it with his hand, and for a moment the dying prospector was just an old man looking at a drawing made by a dead woman.

Astrid recognized the shape of the story before she let herself recognize it. Inconvenient person. Convenient terrain. She had lived inside that story once, from the other side, and the living of it had cost her everything she'd built.

"No," she said.

Voss looked up.

"I'm a medical escort. That's the job. I get you to Puerto Consuelo alive, or as close to alive as your body permits, and I collect the other half of my fee, and we're done." She stood, folding the map along its old creases, pushing it back toward him. "I don't know what you think you heard about me, but whatever it was, it wasn't an invitation. I run one kind of trip now. Upstream into closed country against a mining consortium with private security — that's not a trip. That's a way to die somewhere new."

"They'll kill me anyway," Voss said quietly. "You know that, don't you? The moment I'm in a hospital bed in Puerto Consuelo with a name attached, some orderly with a second income adjusts my morphine drip. I've watched them do it to better men than me." He gathered the map against his chest. "I'm not asking you to be brave, Ms. Navarro. I'm asking you to be accurate. Do the math on which version of this job keeps you alive longer."

"Neither, probably." She picked up the basin. "Get some sleep. We leave for the road at dawn."

* * *

She meant it. She lay awake meaning it, staring at the ceiling of her room next door, cataloguing the reasons: the fee, the contract, the blacklisted guide's iron rule about staying inside the letter of every job because the letter was all she had left. At two in the morning she was still meaning it. At three she was listening to Voss cough through the wall, and hating herself for listening, and meaning it still.

At three-forty, she heard footsteps on the stairs.

Not the night clerk — she'd learned his tread, heavy and favoring the left knee. These were lighter, doubled, moving with the deliberate quiet of people who had done this before. Astrid was out of bed and dressed in ninety seconds, boots laced, knife in her jacket pocket, standing in the dark of her room with her ear against the door.

Voices below. Low, clipped, professional. Then the night clerk's voice, pitched high with the fear of a man being shown something in a wallet.

They were coming up.

Astrid moved. Into Voss's room, one hand over his mouth before he could speak, the other pressing the map — he'd left it on the nightstand, careless or trusting, and she'd never forgive him for either — into the canvas duffel beneath his spare clothes. "Company," she breathed. "Back window. Can you move?"

Something in his face answered her — not fear, exactly. Confirmation. As if he'd been expecting this the way you expect a storm you've been reading in the clouds all week. He swung his legs off the bed, and she got her shoulder under his arm, and together they crossed the room while the footsteps reached the landing.

The back window opened onto the flat roof of the kitchen annex, then a drainpipe, then the alley. Astrid went first, testing the pipe with her weight, then braced herself as Voss lowered himself down it in slow, agonizing increments, his breath tearing in his throat, his hands shaking so badly she finally just wrapped her arms around him from behind and took his weight in a controlled slide to the ground. Behind them, above, she heard the door of his room open. A flashlight beam swept the ceiling through the window they'd left shut.

"Hotel," a voice said above them, flat and unhurried. "Check the alley."

Astrid pulled Voss into the shadow of the woodpile as a second beam cut down into the alley mouth. Twenty meters away, the lane ended at the town's single lit street. Between here and there: open ground, a dog that would bark, a night watchman who would remember.

The flashlight swept past them once, twice, and held.

And then, from the street side, a woman's voice — clear, irritated, carrying the particular authority of someone whose credentials outrank whoever sent her — called out something in rapid Spanish about a permit dispute, about calling the regional office at this hour, about exactly who was going to be explaining themselves tomorrow. The beams wavered. The voices conferred. And in the ten seconds of confusion that followed, Astrid took Voss's arm and moved them both down the alley, low and fast, into the dark beyond the lamplight.

It wasn't until they were three blocks gone, crouched in the doorway of a shuttered feed store with Voss wheezing against the wood, that she let herself think about the voice. She hadn't seen the woman. Hadn't needed to. Anyone who could freeze two consortium security men mid-search with nothing but tone and a fabricated jurisdiction was either police, or had once been something adjacent enough to wear the voice like a key.

Or she was already following them. Which raised questions Astrid didn't have room for tonight.

"Why," she said to Voss, when she could trust her own breathing. "Why search a dying man's hotel room. If they wanted you dead, you'd be dead. They want something."

"The map." He said it without triumph, without *I told you*. Just the fact, laid out between them like a card turned face-up. "Now you understand the terms, Ms. Navarro. It's not a question of whether you come upstream. It's whether you come with me or they come for you. You've touched it. You've carried it. From tonight on, you're in the ledger too."

Astrid stared out at the empty street. Somewhere a dog was barking after all, late to the party, telling the whole town what it had missed. Her heart was still going hard, and underneath the adrenaline something was setting like cement, layer by layer, the way fear does when it has nowhere left to drain.

She had refused him cleanly. She'd had her reasons lined up like tent pegs, driven straight, load-tested. And it had taken four men with flashlights and forty seconds in an alley to show her that refusal wasn't a door she could stand behind anymore. Refusal was just a slower way of arriving at the same place.

"If I do this," she said slowly, "we do it my way. Route, pace, decisions — mine. You tell me what I need to know and nothing you feel like withholding. And when we reach this Cradle of yours, if you're alive, I put you on a plane out. No heroics at the source. Those are the terms."

Voss smiled. It transformed his face, made him briefly the man he must have been at forty, on a river, with the woman who'd drawn the map laughing at something he'd said. "Those are the terms," he agreed.

"One more thing." She shouldered the duffel. "That woman in the street. The voice. You know who she is."

The smile faded. Voss looked at her for a long moment, and the fever-light in his eyes made him unreadable.

"She's my daughter," he said. "And God help us, I don't know yet whether she's here to save us or finish the job."

Astrid closed her eyes. Upstream, somewhere in the dark, a river was running toward them out of closed country, and everything it carried was coming their way.
