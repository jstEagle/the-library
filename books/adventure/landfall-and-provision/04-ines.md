## 4. Ines

**Astrid**

The bus let them off at Puerto Ceniza an hour past midnight, and Astrid walked the last kilometer with Voss's arm over her shoulders and his weight eating into her spine like a debt.

He'd stopped talking somewhere outside of town. That was the thing about dying men — they didn't fade in a straight line. They burned bright, then guttered, then flared again at the worst possible moment, and you learned to read the flicker or you buried them on the trail. Voss was guttering now, his breath coming in shallow pulls, his boots dragging twin lines through the gravel shoulder.

"Left," he rasped. "Past the fuel depot. The blue house with the—"

"I see it."

"You don't. You see a house. I see—" A cough folded him double. Astrid braced, held him until it passed. "I haven't been here in nine years. It's smaller than I remember. Everything I did wrong is smaller than I remember."

"That's not how guilt works."

"No," he said. "It's how distance works. Guilt keeps its size."

The blue house sat behind a fence of welded rebar, a low roofline under a single yard light, a rain barrel, a rack of PVC well casings leaned against the wall like organ pipes. A hydrologist's house. There was a truck in the drive — a field truck, mud to the axles, a sediment sampler strapped in the bed — but no lights except the yard lamp, and no sound except the river, far off, running dark toward the sea.

Astrid knocked anyway. Then she looked back down the street they'd come by, at the empty road glittering under the sodium lamps, and knocked harder.

They'd made it out of San Rafael de Frontera with forty minutes to spare. She knew because she'd spent thirty-nine of them watching the lobby mirror while Voss signed the guest register under a name that wasn't his, and in the fortieth minute a gray sedan had rolled past the hotel at walking speed, twice, its passengers wearing the patient expressions of men being paid to be patient. The Cordillera Extraction Group didn't send thugs first. They sent accountants. Thugs came after the accountants confirmed you were worth the expense.

She'd gotten Voss out the kitchen door while the sedan parked.

Now, on this doorstep, she knocked a third time and heard, from inside, the deliberate sounds of someone awake who wished they weren't: a chair pushed back, a latch tested, a chain still on.

"What do you want." Not a question. A woman's voice, flat as survey paper.

"Efraim Voss is dying," Astrid said. "And two men in a gray sedan are following us. I need both facts to matter to you."

Silence. Then the chain slid, and the door opened on a woman in her thirties with her father's same heavy jaw and none of his softness — Ines Voss, though Astrid wouldn't learn the surname mattered until later, because the woman said, "He goes by Reyes tonight," glancing at Astrid with something that wasn't quite recognition and wasn't quite dismissal. "Same name as yours. Funny."

"It's not my name either."

"No," Ines said, looking at her father slumped against the doorframe, gray as ash, "I imagine nothing about either of you is what it says on paper. Come in before the street sees you."

**Ines**

Nine years, and he showed up on her doorstep at one in the morning looking like a man three weeks past his own funeral, propped up by a stranger with a guide's forearms and a fugitive's eyes.

Ines put him in the kitchen chair because it was the sturdiest furniture she owned, and because she refused — refused — to carry him to the couch like a child. He weighed almost nothing. That was the part that undid her. Not the face, not the voice, not the way he said "Ines" like a man testing ice. The weight. Her father had once carried her four kilometers down a trail with a dislocated shoulder of his own, singing to keep her calm, and now she could have lifted him with one arm and set him on a shelf.

She took his pulse without asking. Clinical. Wrist, watch, count. Ninety-four and irregular, skin cold and clammy, lips going the color of old brick. She'd seen this presentation in field clinics along the watershed — cardiac, probably, something secondary eating his lungs — and she knew with professional certainty that he was measuring his remaining time in days.

"Who's your friend," she said, nodding at the stranger.

"Astrid Reyes. Guide."

"Blacklisted," Efraim said, and coughed. "Twice. Best rope hand on the Serrano drainage, and blacklisted twice, which tells you everything about how this country treats—"

"I didn't ask for the résumé."

"Ines." Her name in his mouth. Nine years since anyone had said it that way, with that particular apology already folded inside it, and she hated that some animal part of her responded to it like a dog hearing a whistle.

She turned to the sink and filled a glass. Water was the one language she spoke fluently. She could tell you what any water in this valley was doing, where it had been, what it carried, how long it intended to stay. She could not tell you why her mother had gone upriver with Efraim Voss in 1998 and come home in a box with the cause of death listed as *exposure, misadventure, unconfirmed*, and she had stopped asking around age twenty-two, when asking got her a slammed door and a nine-year silence.

"Drink," she said, putting the glass in his hands and closing his fingers around it. "Slowly."

He drank like a man who'd forgotten how. The stranger — Reyes — stood by the door with her back to the wall, watching the windows, and Ines found herself cataloguing her the way she catalogued anything new in a watershed: source, flow rate, what it might be carrying. Guide's boots, resoled twice. A jacket too good for her current circumstances. Hands that had done rope work recently enough to leave grooves in the calluses. And eyes that kept going to the window every eleven seconds, which meant the gray sedan story was true, which meant Ines's quiet life at the end of a dirt road had just acquired a use-by date.

"There are men following you," Ines said.

"Two that we know of. Mining consortium. They want something your father has."

"Of course they do." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "He's spent his whole life carrying things other people want. Why break the pattern?"

"Ines—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "You do not get to say my name like we're mid-conversation. We are not mid-conversation. Our conversation ended in 2016 when you told me my mother's death was nobody's fault and then never mentioned her again in your presence for nine years. So." She pulled a chair backward, sat astride it, arms folded on the backrest. "Here's what happens now. You drink your water. Your guide friend tells me exactly what kind of trouble is parked outside my house. And then you both leave, and I go back to bed, and tomorrow I file a report with the provincial water authority about strangers on my property, which will be the only honest paperwork involved in this entire night."

Efraim set down the glass. His hands were shaking, and not only from the illness.

"The map," he said. "You've seen it. When you were small. The headwaters."

Something went cold down Ines's spine, all the way to the base, where nine years of silence lived.

"You swore to me," she said quietly, "that you'd burned it."

"I lied. I lie professionally. It's the family disease." He fumbled at his coat, and Reyes moved — fast, fluid, a hand on his wrist, stopping him, and the two of them exchanged a look Ines couldn't parse, some negotiation conducted entirely in eyebrow and grip.

"Not here," Reyes said. "Not with the curtains open."

"Then close them," Efraim said, "and listen, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to sleep for a week or die, whichever comes first. Marisol drew that map. Before she died. She sent it to me in pieces, over two years, and I assembled it, and I have carried it ever since, and there are men outside this valley right now who killed her partner and would kill her daughter to make sure no one ever follows it upstream."

The kitchen went very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Out in the dark, the river kept talking to itself.

Ines became aware that she was standing. She didn't remember standing.

"My mother," she said, "died of exposure."

"Your mother died because she found something the Cordillera Extraction Group has spent twenty-seven years making sure stayed lost, and because she tried to bring it out." His breath caught; he rode through it. "The boot you're standing next to, Ines — the flood debris, the '98 expedition wreckage that washes down every spring — that's not misadventure. That's evidence. And I am the last man alive who knows where the rest of it is, and I am dying, and I need—"

"You need." She heard her own voice crack and despised it. "You need. Of course. Twenty-seven years, and when you finally need something from me, it turns out to be the one thing I can't refuse, because it's *her*."

Reyes shifted by the door. "Ma'am. If the men are tracking him, they'll track him here. You should decide fast whether you're in this or not."

"I'm not in *this*." But even as she said it, Ines was moving — pulling the curtains, killing the yard light, checking the road through the gap in the boards with the practiced eye of a woman who had grown up with a prospector father and had learned surveillance before she'd learned algebra. The street was empty. For now. Gray sedans traveled in pairs; everyone in the extraction corridors knew that; the second one would be on the far side of town, logging plates.

Her mother's map. Assembled in secret. Carried for twenty-seven years while he let his daughter believe her mother had simply — wandered, misjudged the weather, become terrain.

"You could have told me," she said. "Any year. Any single year of nine."

"And you'd have done what? Filed it with the water authority?" He almost smiled, and it broke something in her to see how much of her own face was in that smile. "You'd have done exactly what you're doing now. Which is the right thing. Which is why I waited until I had no strength left to argue with you."

* * *

**Astrid**

Astrid stood by the closed curtains and watched the two of them fight, and thought: *there it is.* Grief doesn't look like crying. Grief looks like a hydrologist sitting rigid in her own kitchen, arms crossed, defending herself against a dying man with data points. Grief looks like a father spending his last currency on the truth because it's the only coin his daughter will accept.

She'd seen versions of it before. She'd been a version of it before — Daniel's mother on the courthouse steps, asking Astrid questions Astrid still couldn't answer, seven years ago now, the incident report sealed, the blacklist descending like weather. *Inconvenient people erased by convenient terrain.* She didn't know yet whose story that phrase belonged to. She only knew it fit more than one grave.

Voss was fading fast. She could see it in the gray of his lips, the way his sentences were shedding their endings. Whatever happened next, it would happen tonight.

"He needs rest," Astrid said. "Real rest, horizontal, and if there's a doctor in this town who doesn't file reports, he needs one by morning."

"There's a clinic two streets over that doesn't ask names. There always is, in towns like this." Ines hadn't uncrossed her arms. "That doesn't answer my question, which is: what exactly are you proposing? Because I can hear a proposal assembling itself in this room, and I want it stated plainly so I can refuse it properly."

"Upstream," Voss said.

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. I know what upstream means. Upstream means the headwaters, the closed valley, the place that killed my mother, and you want to go there *dying*." She was on her feet now, voice low and furious, the fury of a woman keeping volume down for the neighbors. "You want to be carried up the Serrano by a disgraced guide and whatever boat you can steal, into a valley with no exit, pursued by a company that murders people, so you can — what? Touch the source? Die at the coordinates? Write your own ending like a man in a novel?"

"Yes," Efraim said simply. "All of that. Except the novel. Novels have endings. This has a purpose. There's a difference, and the difference is what I'm leaving behind when I go."

"The only thing you're leaving behind is me, and you've had practice."

The words landed. Astrid saw them land — saw the old man take them the way a hull takes a rock, absorbing, cracking somewhere internal. And she saw something else, something that made her stomach drop: Ines was already losing the argument and knew it. Because underneath the fury, underneath nine years of silence, the woman wanted the same thing her father wanted, and wanted it for the opposite reason. He wanted the source. She wanted answers. Same river. Same coordinates. Two people rowing toward the same wreck from different ends of their lives.

"I'll need to think about the boat," Ines said abruptly, to the window, to no one. "And I'll need to think about whether I hate you enough to let you die without me. Give me an hour."

She left the kitchen. They heard a door close down the hall — not slammed. Closed. With control.

Voss exhaled, long and shuddering. "She'll come."

"People usually surprise you?"

"No. People usually do what the dead require of them. She just hasn't admitted yet that Marisol requires it." He looked up at Astrid, and his eyes were clear for the first time that night, terribly clear. "You could still walk away, Ms. Reyes. Take the fee, disappear. No one would blame you."

"Everyone blames me," Astrid said. "It's the one thing I'm never short of." She checked the road through the curtain gap. Empty. Eleven seconds. Empty. "Sleep. I'll wake you if the sedan comes."

* * *

**Ines**

She didn't think. Thinking was for people with time.

In the back room she kept her field gear in a state of permanent readiness — a habit from the years of solo survey work, living out of a duffel, answering to whichever watershed flooded next. She packed by touch and muscle memory: instruments, notebooks, the waterproof case, spare batteries, the photograph she had sworn she'd thrown away and had not thrown away, because she was her father's daughter and lied professionally too.

Her mother's face looked up at her from the photograph, squinting into sun on some ridge twenty-eight years gone. Marisol Voss, née Quispe, field hydrologist, dead at thirty-four of *exposure, misadventure, unconfirmed*. Ines had built her entire career on top of that phrase like a house built on a sinkhole. Every river she'd measured, every aquifer she'd mapped, every report she'd filed — she'd told herself it was science. It was not science. It was a search party that had never disbanded.

And now the search had coordinates.

She sat on the edge of the cot with the duffel between her knees and let herself feel the full shape of it, the way you let a wound breathe before packing it. If she went upstream, she went with a dying man she hated loving and loved hating, guarded by a stranger with a blacklist, hunted by a corporation, toward the place that had eaten her mother. Every rational instrument she owned read the plan as fatal.

Every rational instrument she owned had also read her mother's death certificate as accurate, and that had been a lie for twenty-seven years.

So: terms. She was not going as a daughter. Daughters forgave, daughters reconciled, daughters held hands at deathbeds. She was going as a witness. She would keep him alive — her medical training, such as it was, her field-clinic hours — long enough for him to stand at the source of the Serrano and answer for Marisol Quispe in front of whatever was left of her. Answers first. Absolution later, if the river allowed it, which rivers generally didn't.

She zipped the duffel. In the kitchen, the guide was standing at the window again, counting seconds, and her father had actually fallen asleep in the chair, mouth open, breathing in that wet, labored rhythm that Ines's clinical mind automatically began timing and her human mind refused to hear.

"An hour's up," Astrid said, without turning.

"I'm coming." Ines set the duffel down harder than necessary. "But understand the arrangement. I'm not doing this for him. I'm doing it so he lives long enough to tell me everything — every piece of it, all of it, what really happened in '98, what she found, who decided she was expendable. And when he's told me, if he's still alive, then maybe we discuss whatever it is fathers and daughters discuss. Maybe. Don't either of you mistake this for forgiveness."

Astrid nodded slowly. "Understood."

"Do you? Do you understand, Ms. Reyes? Because you're carrying him, literally carrying him, and I've watched people confuse gratitude with obligation and obligation with love, and it always ends with someone drowning." She picked the duffel back up. "I keep people alive. That's my job. Keeping *him* alive is not the same as keeping him."

Outside, far off down the valley road, an engine changed gears — once, twice, unhurried — and then faded without approaching. Or it didn't fade. Sound lied at night, over water. Astrid was at the curtain again, counting.

"We leave before dawn," the guide said. "There's a riverport downstream called Bahía Sucia. If we're going upstream, we need a boat, and boats need pilots, and pilots need to be desperate enough not to ask questions."

"I know Bahía Sucia," Ines said. "Everyone who works water knows Bahía Sucia. It's where boats go to be forgotten." She looked at her father, asleep in the chair, and felt the old braided thing in her chest pull tight — the resentment and the love, indistinguishable now, load-bearing together. "Wake him at four. Dying men travel best in the cold. Less time to change their minds. Ours or theirs."

Astrid almost smiled. "You're going to be useful."

"I'm going to be merciless. Useful is a side effect." Ines shouldered the duffel and went to check the road one last time, and in the glass, faint under the yard light she'd shut off, she saw it: a gray sedan, far down the street, parked with its lights off, patient as silt.

They had until dawn. Probably less.

"Ms. Reyes," she said quietly. "Start the truck."
