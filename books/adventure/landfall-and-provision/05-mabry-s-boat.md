## 5. Mabry's Boat

Mabry smelled the river before he saw it — diesel and silt and the sweet rot of the fish market, all of it braided into the particular stink of Puerto Hondo at the end of the dry season. He'd been smelling that smell for eleven years now, ever since the consortium's lawyers took his license and the river took everything else. A man could go blind in a place like this. Some did. Mabry just went to work.

He was patching a seam on the Provision's portside pontoon when they came down the ramp — three of them, moving like people who'd learned recently that the world was watching. The tall one he didn't know. The woman moved like a guide, weight low, eyes doing the work of a surveyor. And behind them, half-carried between two strangers' shoulders, an old man wrapped in blankets against a heat that would kill him faster than the cold ever could.

Mabry knew that face.

Not from life. From a photograph, ten years old, pinned inside a ledger he'd helped load onto a consortium barge and had never been able to forget: Efraim Voss, prospector, missing, presumed drowned, reward for information. The face in the photo had been angry. The face coming down the ramp was past anger, past most things, held together by will and the two strangers bearing his weight.

Mabry set down his torch and waited. In Puerto Hondo, waiting was a profession.

* * *

The tall one did the talking. His name was Voss too — no, not Voss, the old man was Voss; the tall one was somebody else, a pilot named Mabry, which made Mabry laugh until he coughed.

"You're the one they banned," the man said. "Ran supply runs up the Serrano for Cordillera Extraction. Three seasons."

"Four," Mabry said. "They only caught me on three."

"So you'll run for anyone with money."

"I ran for them because they paid and nobody else asked questions." He looked at the woman. She hadn't spoken. She was reading his boat the way he'd once read weather — hull lines, freeboard, the patched seams, the engine cowl tied down with wire. Judging. Finding him wanting. Fair enough. The Provision *was* wanting. She was forty years of rust holding hands with thirty years of luck, and she floated because the river hadn't gotten around to finishing her yet.

"You're banned," the woman said finally. Her voice was flat, tired, accented from somewhere north. "Both licenses. River and mountain."

"And you're Astrid Reyes," Mabry said. "Which means we're both famous in rooms we never want to enter again."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a flinch. Recognition of a kind. "You know who I am."

"Everybody on this river knows who you are. They also know what the consortium says about you isn't the same as what happened. Rivers keep better records than courts." He spat over the side, into water the color of strong tea. "What's the job?"

The old man stirred in his blankets. "Upstream," he said, and his voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "To the headwaters. La Cuna."

Mabry felt the word land in his chest like a dropped anchor. He'd carried crates marked with that word once, sealed crates, heavy ones, up through the Redbank gorge in fog, and had been told never to ask, and hadn't, and the not-asking had sat in him ever since like a swallowed hook.

"That's closed water," he said. "Decreed since '98. There are patrols. There are sensors. There are men whose whole job is making sure nobody sees what's up there."

"There's a dead woman up there," the old man said. "And a lie the size of a valley. And me, dying fast enough that I don't have time for your price haggling. Name a number."

Mabry looked at the Provision. At the rust. At the luck. At eleven years of dockside repairs and ferrying tourists who wanted their danger pre-packaged and their rivers tame. He thought about the sealed crates. He thought about the hook.

"Fuel, food, and my ban lifted when this is done," he said. "That's the number. No money changes hands."

The woman's eyebrows rose a fraction. The old man laughed, a wet, tearing sound. "A pilot with a conscience."

"A pilot with a debt," Mabry said. "I've been paying interest for ten years. Time to collect on the principal."

* * *

They loaded at dusk, which was stupid and necessary. Medical supplies the woman had bought with cash that smelled of desperation. A folding cot for the old man. Crates of fuel. Rope the tall one — his name was Delgado, quiet, competent, hands like a rigger — coiled with a care that told Mabry he'd lost someone to bad rope once.

The last one aboard was a young woman who came down the ramp alone, carrying nothing but a field case and a face like closed doors. Hydrologist, the old man said. My daughter. The way he said *my daughter* was the way a man names a wound.

Ines Voss didn't look at her father. She looked at Mabry, and her look was a question he couldn't answer yet: *are you worth the risk of hoping?*

"No," he said aloud, surprising himself. "I'm not worth it. But the boat is. Sometimes."

For the first time since the ramp, something that wasn't grief crossed her face. It might even have been a smile. It was gone before he could be sure.

* * *

They cast off at full dark, running without lights through the port's outer channel, and Mabry felt it happen — the thing that always happened, the thing he'd never found another name for. The moment the current took the hull and the shore began to slide, the guilt loosened its grip by one degree. Not gone. Never gone. But motion was a kind of penance, and the Provision was built for penance. She'd carry whatever he put in her.

Efraim Voss lay on the cot under the canopy, breathing in a rhythm Mabry recognized from other boats, other years — the shallow, counting breaths of a man measuring out what was left. The daughter sat beside him not touching him. The guide stood at the bow doing what guides do, which is worry professionally so nobody else has to.

Mabry took them wide around the first bend, into the main channel, and let the river have them.

It was nearly midnight when he smelled the smoke.

He heard it first, actually — the sound of Puerto Hondo burning carries, especially the sound of a dock going up, tarred planks and creosote pilings, a sound like the world clearing its throat. He cut the engine and drifted, and they all listened together while the orange glow bloomed against the clouds behind them, painting the water the color of a wound.

"The berth," Delgado said quietly. "Where we bought the fuel."

"They torched it," the guide said. "They're telling us they can reach us anywhere."

Mabry said nothing. He was watching the glow, and thinking about the men who'd lit it, and thinking that they were professionals, and professionals don't burn docks to send messages unless the message matters more than the noise. Which meant the map mattered. Which meant the old man's valley mattered. Which meant Mabry had finally, after eleven years, signed on to something worth burning down a dock over.

Ines Voss spoke without turning from the fire. "My father's sleeping."

"He should see this," Mabry said. "Proof they're scared."

"He's seen enough fires." She turned then, and in the light of the burning dock her face was terrible and calm. "How long to Redbank?"

"Two days, if the water's kind."

"And if it isn't?"

Mabry restarted the engine, low, and pointed the bow upstream into the dark. "Then the river gets a vote."

Behind them the dock collapsed in a fountain of sparks, and the Provision climbed the black water toward the gorge, carrying four living people, one dying one, and every debt Mabry had spent a decade trying to drown.
