## 6. Rapids at Redbank

The gorge announced itself a mile before they reached it, as a sound like distant applause that never stopped. Astrid stood at the bow of the Provision with one hand on the rail and listened, and what she heard made her wish she were deaf.

"That's Redbank," Mabry said from the helm. He said it the way a man names a grave he dug himself.

She'd seen it on the survey map Voss had given her — a narrow notch where the Serrano dropped forty meters through basalt no wider than a road. The mapmaker had drawn the gorge as a black slash and written nothing beside it, which on a hand-drawn map was its own kind of testimony. Rivers got names where people survived them. This stretch had no name but Redbank, and the name referred to the color the water turned when it ran high.

It was running high.

"Portage?" Astrid asked.

Mabry didn't answer right away. He was watching the water the way other men watched women they'd wronged — with longing and dread in the same face. "There's no portage. The banks close in. It's the gorge or it's back to Serpent Bend and the patrol boats, and we both know what's waiting there."

"Then we run it."

"We," Mabry said. "You keep saying we like it's a small word."

Ines came forward from the stern, where she'd spent the morning with her father, spooning electrolyte solution into him between the long gray lapses where his breathing went shallow and his eyes tracked something none of them could see. She looked at the gorge, then at Astrid, and said nothing, which was its own kind of vote. Nine days on the river had taught Astrid to read Ines's silences the way she read water: the flat calm that meant consent, the standing wave that meant argument. This one was consent. Ines wanted her father alive long enough to answer for her mother, and she would ride anything to keep that appointment.

Voss himself lay in the shelter of the cargo tarp, propped against the fuel drums, and when Astrid went back to check his straps he opened his eyes and smiled at her with terrible warmth.

"Tomás," he said. "You came back for me."

"I'm not Tomás," Astrid said. It was the fourth time in two days she'd said it. "I'm Astrid. Your daughter's here. We're taking you upriver."

"Tomás always said we'd run Redbank together." His hand found her wrist and gripped it with a strength that surprised her, that came from somewhere deeper than his failing body. "Ninety-eight. He said the headwaters were worth any water between here and there." His grip loosened. His eyes went to the gorge and stayed there. "He was wrong, of course. But you can't tell a man that until after."

Astrid pulled the strap tight across his chest. "Hold on to something when I tell you," she said. "That's the whole job."

He laughed, a dry rustling sound, and for a moment he was lucid enough to look at her — really look, the way he had that first night in the hotel when he'd spread the map across the bed like a man laying out a winning hand. "You've run water like this before."

"Water like this, yes. This water, no."

"That's all any of us get," Voss said, and closed his eyes.

* * *

Mabry backed the Provision into the eddy above the drop and killed the engine, and in the sudden silence the gorge filled the world. Astrid stood on the bow deck and read it the way she'd read a thousand rapids on a thousand rivers, in the years before the years after: the tongue of green water entering left of center, the hole below the first drop eating its own spray, the lateral wave off the right wall that would slam anything light enough to float. The river narrowed to maybe thirty meters, and where it narrowed it fell, and where it fell it folded, three drops in a quarter kilometer, each one hiding the next.

"You see the line?" Mabry called up.

"I see a line." She pointed. "Green tongue, hard left, thread the seam between the hole and the wall, then hold center for the second drop. Third one you just point it straight and pray."

"Praying's your department. I lost my religion to this river years ago." He wiped his palms on his trousers, and she noticed — filed away, the way she noticed everything now, everything being potentially the last thing — that his hands were shaking. Not fear exactly. Something adjacent. "I ran this in ninety-one," he said. "High water, like this. I lost a man off the back of the boat. Consortium surveyor. They didn't even dock me for it — they docked me for what I was carrying, later. But I heard him hit the third drop." He looked at her. "I've heard it every night since."

"Then hear something else," Astrid said. "Nobody goes in the water. Nobody. We tie everything that floats, including ourselves. If the boat swamps, we swim the eddy below the second drop, river left, and we walk out. You hear me? The boat is replaceable. We are not."

Ines had come aft to stand over her father. She'd tied herself in with a length of climbing webbing Astrid had rigged that morning, and she'd tied her father in beside her, and her hands were steady on the straps. She caught Astrid looking and gave a single nod. Trust, in its smallest available denomination. Astrid took it.

"Engine on standby," Mabry said. "On my mark."

Astrid clipped into the bow line, coiled the throw rope across her shoulder where it wouldn't foul, and looked at the green tongue of the entry current one more time. The river didn't care what they'd survived to get here. That was the thing she loved about it, had always loved, even now, even with everything: the river was the only honest party in any of their arrangements.

"Mark," Mabry said, and gave it throttle, and the Provision went in.

* * *

The entry was clean. The green tongue took them and the bow rose and Astrid felt the whole boat become a single held breath. Then the first drop, two meters of falling water, and the gorge walls closed in and the sound went from applause to artillery.

The seam was there, exactly where she'd read it — a lane of broken green between the hole's brown boil and the wall's reflected surge, maybe two meters wide, maybe less. Mabry held them in it with hands that had stopped shaking the moment the water took the boat. For eleven seconds the world was spray and noise and the smell of cold stone, and then the lateral wave hit.

It came off the right wall like a door slamming. Astrid saw it, called it — "RIGHT" — and Mabry was already correcting, but the boat was light with fuel burned and the wave took the stern and swung them. The Provision spun a hundred and eighty degrees in the seam, and now they were backing toward the second drop, and the second drop did not care what direction you arrived from.

"Engine!" Astrid shouted.

"Dead!" Mabry had the throttle wide and the prop was cavitating, chewing air where the stern had lifted. "We're sideways, we're — "

The second drop took them quartering, and the world went white.

Astrid came up inside the spray curtain with the throw rope still across her shoulder and the boat still under her, half-swamped, wallowing in the eddy behind the second drop's pillow. The river left. She'd said river left. She looked for the eddy and found it and found, too, the thing she'd spend the rest of her life not quite seeing when she closed her eyes: Ines in the water.

The lateral wave had taken her off the stern. The webbing had torn free or been cut — later Astrid would find the frayed end and understand that the strap had parted clean, a flaw in the weave, bad luck wearing the costume of bad gear — and Ines was in the current below the drop, in the boil line, and her head was down.

Astrid didn't remember deciding. She remembered the cold. The Serrano ran off glaciers and the cold went through her like a verdict, and then her hands were doing what twenty years of rivers had taught them. The throw rope was across her shoulder because she never stowed it in the gorge, never, and she paid it out with her left hand and threw with her right, a flat sidearm under the spray, and the bag landed a meter upstream of Ines's shoulder.

"INES." Her voice came out of some register below thought. "TAKE THE ROPE."

The boil line dragged Ines under again. One second. Two. Astrid fed rope, feeling for the weight, the way you feel for a fish, for a pulse. Then the line came taut and she leaned back against the rail with her boots braced and hauled, hand over hand, the rope burning through her gloves, and Ines came up gasping and found the line and held it, and Astrid pulled her along the eddy fence to the boat's stern, and Mabry was there, on his belly on the swamped deck, reaching down with both arms, and together they hauled her over the rail like a netted fish.

She came up coughing the river out of herself, and Astrid held her by the jacket with both fists and looked at her face — blue-lipped, wide-eyed, alive — and said the only thing there was to say.

"Breathe. You're in the eddy. Breathe."

Ines breathed. Then she said, "My father — "

"Strapped in. Mabry, check him."

Mabry was already aft. Voss lay against the drums with water sheeting off the tarp above him and his eyes open and fixed on Astrid with an expression she would carry longer than the cold.

"Tomás," he said, wonderingly. "You threw the rope. Just like the Yana Mayu. Just like — " He stopped. His face worked. "He didn't catch it," he said, to no one, to the gorge. "Nobody ever catches it."

"I caught it," Astrid said. She said it firmly, the way you talk a hypothermic person back across the line. "I'm Astrid. I caught it. Ines is safe."

He looked at her a long moment, and something in him reorganized, and he said, "Ines," in a voice full of a grief so old it had gone smooth as river stone, and turned his face to the tarp.

* * *

They ran the third drop pointed straight, as promised, and the Provision surfed out of the gorge into a long braided valley where the sun was still shining, absurdly, as though nothing had happened. They bailed the boat in the shallows. Mabry's engine, miraculously, caught on the fourth pull. Ines sat on the fuel drums wrapped in the spare tarp, shivering, and Astrid sat beside her and did the only competent thing left, which was to make her drink hot water with sugar in it and keep drinking it.

"You threw it flat," Ines said eventually. Her voice was wrecked and she used it anyway. "Sidearm. Under the spray. Everyone throws overhand. It sails. You threw it flat."

"You watch a lot of rope work?"

"I grew up on this river. My mother could put a throw bag through a car window at forty meters." Ines looked at the water going by, the water that had almost kept her. "I haven't thought about that in years. I haven't let myself." She turned and looked at Astrid directly, and Astrid understood that something had changed in the arithmetic between them — not trust exactly, not yet, but the removal of one obstacle to it. "You didn't hesitate. Everyone hesitates. You didn't."

"Hesitation is just arithmetic you do too late," Astrid said. "I did mine years ago."

Ines almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile in nine days, and Astrid counted it the way she counted everything now.

* * *

They camped that night on a gravel bar below the gorge, in the lee of a driftwood pile the river had been building for decades. Mabry rigged the stove under the tarp and cooked rice with too much salt because salt was what the body wanted after a swamping. Ines slept — actually slept, the deep collapsed sleep of the nearly drowned — and Astrid sat up with Voss because someone had to, and because his breathing scared her.

He surfaced from it near midnight, in the dark, and found her by the fire.

"Tomás," he said. Gently. As if greeting an old friend across a table.

"I'm Astrid."

"I know who you are." He lowered himself onto the driftwood beside her, moving like a man negotiating with his own skeleton. "I know exactly who you are. That's what's so — " He searched for the word. "Convenient," he finished, and laughed at himself, a small dry sound. "When the fog comes, I call you Tomás. Because Tomás is who should be here. Tomás would know what to say to you." He looked into the fire. "You want to know what happened at the Yana Mayu?"

"I know what the reports said. Your partner drowned running a rapid. You were the only witness."

"The reports said he drowned." Voss's voice had gone very quiet. "Tomás was the strongest waterman I ever knew. He wouldn't have drowned in that rapid. He wouldn't have been *in* that rapid — we'd portaged it that morning, both of us, agreed on it over coffee. And then that afternoon the survey team's boat went through, and Tomás went after them, and I threw him a rope and he didn't take it." His hands were shaking, and not from cold. "He looked right at me. Forty meters. Flat throw, under the spray. And he looked at me and he turned away and the river took him. Because of what he'd found upstream. Because of what they'd do to keep it found." He looked at her then, and his eyes were clear, horribly clear, the fog burned off by whatever the confession cost. "So when I call you Tomás, understand — it isn't confusion. It's hope. It's me hoping that this time, the rope gets caught."

Astrid sat with that. The fire popped. Upstream, faint and constant, the gorge went on applauding.

"Your daughter thinks you're using us," Astrid said. "As cover. For an obsession."

"She's right." He said it without flinching. "I am. I'm also dying, and the two facts are not in conflict. A man can be selfish and correct at the same time. Ask any martyr." He pulled the blanket tighter. "She'll understand one day. Or she won't, and she'll be right about that too."

"Which is it?"

"Ask me at the source," Voss said, and closed his eyes, and this time when he spoke again his voice had already begun to fog. "Tomás. Tell Ines her mother kept the coordinates in her boot. Left boot. She never trusted pockets."

Astrid sat up all the way. "Efraim. What boot? Whose boot?"

But he was asleep, or somewhere past sleep, breathing in that shallow ragged rhythm, and the fire burned down, and the Serrano ran dark and constant toward the headwaters, carrying everything it kept.

Astrid looked at the sleeping camp — Ines curled in the tarp, Mabry sitting his silent watch at the boat, one hand resting on the hull like a man touching a scar — and thought about left boots, and about the flooded timber they'd pass tomorrow, and about the river's long memory, and did not sleep at all.
