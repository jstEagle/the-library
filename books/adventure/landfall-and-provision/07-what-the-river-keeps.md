## 7. What the River Keeps

The river rose overnight.

Ines woke to the sound of it — not the steady conversation they'd been running beside for three days, but something deeper, a bass note under everything, like a truck idling beneath the gravel bar. She unzipped her bag and saw the water had come up two feet in the dark, brown now instead of green, carrying whole trees past their camp like matchsticks.

"Pack up," Astrid said. She was already standing, already dressed, scanning the bank with that flat professional attention Ines was beginning to recognize as the woman's only register. "We're moving to high ground. Flooded timber, maybe half a kilometer up. It'll hold us until this drops."

Mabry was lashing the Provision's lines to a spruce trunk, muttering to it the way he muttered to everything — apologies, curses, endearments, impossible to tell which. Her father sat propped against a log with his eyes closed, gray as driftwood, and for one bad second Ines thought he'd died in the night and nobody had noticed.

Then his eyes opened and found hers, and she looked away first.

They carried him. That was the morning's first humiliation, or hers anyway — Mabry took the shoulders, Astrid the feet, and Ines walked ahead carrying the packs because someone had to and because her arms needed work to do or they were going to do something else entirely. The timber came up around them like a crowd: alders and cottonwoods standing in water to their waists, their roots braided into islands of silt and debris. Astrid picked a shelf of ground where the flood hadn't reached, a dry pocket ringed by trunks, invisible from the river unless you knew to look.

"Here," she said. "Nobody sees us from the water. We wait out the rise."

"How long?" Ines asked.

"A day. Maybe two." Astrid dropped the packs and started rigging a tarp between two trees without waiting for agreement. "Floods like this burn fast when they're snowmelt. The mountain decides, not us."

*The mountain decides.* Ines wanted to say something sharp about how much of this trip seemed to be decided by things that weren't any of them, but her father made a small sound — not quite a word — and she swallowed it.

She got him settled on the tarp, got water boiling over the little stove, measured out the antibiotics Astrid had been rationing like a miser. His fever was back. She'd known it before she touched him; you could see heat coming off a person when it climbed past some threshold, a shimmer in the air above the skin like road glare. Nine years since she'd touched him at all, and now she was doing it hourly, pressing the backs of her fingers to his temple, charting his decline in tenths of degrees.

"Efraim," she said. "Drink."

He drank. His eyes tracked her face with an expression she couldn't file anywhere — gratitude, maybe, or the particular confusion of a man who kept expecting to see someone else.

"You have her hands," he said.

Ines set down the cup harder than she meant to. Water slopped onto the tarp.

"Don't," she said.

"I'm allowed—"

"You're allowed nothing." She heard her own voice come out flat and cold and was briefly grateful for it. Cold was useful. Cold kept the other thing underneath from getting out. "You don't get to invoke her. Not while I'm keeping you alive. You can do that after. If there is an after."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he did the thing he'd been doing for nine years, which was nothing. He turned his face away and closed his eyes, and the silence closed over the subject like water over a stone.

* * *

The flood gave them time, and time was the one thing Ines didn't want.

There was nothing to do in the flooded timber but exist. Mabry slept off his watch in the boat, which they'd hauled up and tied among the trees like a strange wooden animal bedded down for winter. Astrid checked the lines, checked the sky, checked the river, in that order, every hour, a ritual so consistent Ines could have set a clock by it. And Ines — Ines walked the debris line.

That was what the flood left along the margins of the timber: a tidemark of everything the river had ever taken and given back. Plastic jugs gone opaque with sun. Fishing net rotted to lace. A boot. A section of corrugated roofing. Logs stripped of bark, pale as bone. She walked it because walking was better than sitting, and because some part of her — the part trained by ten years of hydrology to read what water leaves behind — couldn't stop reading.

It was on the second circuit, near the upstream edge of their island, that she found the boot.

She knew immediately that it wasn't ordinary flotsam. Ordinary boots rot; this one had been preserved. It stood upright in the silt, half-buried, small — a child's size, six or seven years old maybe — and its leather had gone stiff and dark but intact, the stitching still holding, the sole still vulcanized and firm. Something about the way it sat there upright, patient, expectant, made the hair stand up on her arms.

She crouched beside it. Turned it over with a stick.

Stamped into the heel, in letters worn nearly smooth but legible if you tilted it against the light: **EXPEDICIÓN SERRANO '98**.

The world went very quiet. Even the flood seemed to lower its voice.

She knew the date the way you know your own birthday. Everyone in her field knew it. The 1998 Serrano expedition: eleven people, government-sanctioned survey of the headwaters, vanished somewhere above Redbank Gorge. No bodies recovered. No wreckage found. The valley sealed by decree the following spring — *environmental protection*, the decree said, though no environmental study had ever been published — and the whole drainage dropped off the maps like a held breath. Her father had been on that expedition. Her mother had led it.

Her mother had *led* it.

Ines knelt in the mud with the boot in both hands and felt nine years of carefully maintained ignorance begin to crack along fault lines she'd drawn herself. Because here was the thing she had never let herself think through: expeditions didn't vanish. Rivers didn't swallow eleven adults and leave nothing. Boats washed downstream. Radios washed downstream. *Boots* washed downstream. Somewhere below whatever happened in that valley, the river had been carrying pieces of it for twenty-six years, depositing them along its banks like evidence in a case no one had opened.

And her father had come back. Alone. With a story she'd never gotten out of him, and a silence she'd inherited like debt.

"Ines." Astrid's voice, low, behind her. "You've been gone two hours. We need to—" She stopped. Ines didn't turn, but she heard the guide read the scene: the kneeling, the boot, the stillness. When Astrid spoke again her voice had changed, gentled by exactly one degree, which from her was a speech. "What is it?"

"It's from the expedition." Ines heard her own voice and was surprised by how level it came out. "Ninety-eight. There's more." She nodded upstream along the debris line. "Look."

Astrid looked. Twenty meters on, snagged in the root wad of a fallen spruce, hung a shape that had fooled the flood into thinking it was just another branch — until you got close and saw the casing, the corroded dial face, the antenna snapped to a stub. A field radio. Military surplus, the kind survey teams used before satellite units got cheap. Green paint gone to white scale, battery compartment open and empty, the whole thing furred with mineral deposits. Dead for decades. But stamped on the side, same as the boot: **EXPEDICIÓN SERRANO '98**.

Astrid crouched next to it. She didn't touch it right away. Ines watched her look at it the way she'd watched her look at rapids — reading, calculating, deciding.

"This is why the valley's closed," Astrid said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"This is why the valley's closed," Ines agreed. "Or it's why they *say* it's closed. Eleven people go in, eleven people don't come out, and the response is to seal the watershed and erase it from the maps. You know what that is? That's not grief. That's liability."

"And your father was on it."

"My father walked out." The words tasted like copper. "My mother didn't."

* * *

She went back to the camp with the radio under her arm because she could not make herself leave it in the mud, and she set it down in front of her father like a subpoena.

He was awake. He'd been drifting all day, surfacing and sinking, but he surfaced now, and his eyes went from the radio to her face and she watched him understand exactly what she'd found and exactly what she was asking, all in the space of a single breath.

"Where," she said, "did my mother die?"

"Ines—"

"*Where.*" The word cracked despite her. Fine. Let it crack. "I was fourteen. You came down off that river alone and you told me the water took her, and you told me the survey data was lost, and you told me never to ask again, and I was fourteen so I obeyed you. I'm not fourteen anymore. I've spent nine years not asking, and I am done. So you're going to tell me what happened up there, Efraim, or I'm going to put you in the boat and run us back downriver right now, fever and all, and you can die in a hospital answering questions to people who don't love you."

The last word escaped before she could catch it. *Love.* Past tense, present tense, she didn't know anymore, and neither, from the look on his face, did he.

Mabry had appeared at the edge of the clearing, drawn by the noise, and frozen there. Astrid stood behind her father's head, arms crossed, saying nothing, watching. Let them watch. Let the whole drowned forest listen.

Voss looked at the radio for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was thin and rusted, but it held.

"We found water," he said. "That's where it starts. Nineteen days in, above the falls, we found the aquifer. Marta flagged it first — your mother, she read the terrain like other people read street signs. An artesian system under the glacier, pressurized, enormous. Enough fresh water to—" He stopped. Coughed. Started again. "Enough to matter. To cities. To countries. To men who measure things in billions."

"The consortium," Astrid said. Not a question either.

"They didn't exist yet. Not by name. But there were men attached to the expedition — funders' representatives, two of them, quiet men with good boots. And when Marta filed the preliminary numbers, they understood what she'd found before she did. Water's the next gold, mija. Oil you can dig around. Aquifer you can own." His hand moved, vaguely, toward the radio. "She wanted to publish. Full disclosure, international review, protected status. They wanted a private survey and a sealed basin. She refused. She was—" something moved across his face, too fast to name, "—she was never any good at refusing quietly."

"What happened?" Ines asked. Her voice sounded far away to herself.

His eyes drifted. Fever or memory, she couldn't tell which had him. "There was a night. Rain. The camp was above the gorge, on the east bench. There was an argument — I heard parts of it, I was in the instrument tent. Then there was shouting. Then there was—" His hand made a gesture, open, empty. "The river came up. They said the river came up. A flood surge, out of season, out of nowhere. Took the lower camp. Took the boats. Took—" His voice failed and rebooted. "Eleven people, Ines. The river doesn't take eleven people in one night. Not that river. Not in that reach. I've run water my whole life and I'm telling you the numbers don't work."

"But you survived."

"I was checking a gauge station downstream." Flat. Recited. A sentence he'd polished on the inside of his skull for twenty-six years until it was smooth and meaningless. "When I came back up, there was no camp. There was debris. There was—" He looked at the boot in Ines's hands, and whatever he saw there finished him. He turned his face to the tarp. "I searched for weeks. Alone. Then the decree came down, and the soldiers came, and a man in a good suit explained to me what would happen to my daughter if I kept searching. And I stopped. I took my map and my guilt and I went underground, and I stayed there, and I told myself I was keeping you safe, and maybe I even believed it."

"That's not an answer," Ines said. "That's a eulogy. Who killed her?"

"I don't know." And the terrible thing, the thing that made her want to shake him until his teeth rattled, was that he sounded like he meant it. "I know what I heard. I know what the numbers say. I know your mother drew a map in the last week of her life and hid it where only I would find it, because she trusted me with it, and I sat on it for twenty-six years like a coward. But names? Proof? That's up there. In the ice. Where they sealed it. Where she sent me to look."

"Where *we're* going," Astrid said quietly.

Voss's eyes came back to Ines. They were wet. She realized distantly that she wasn't crying at all, that some mechanism in her had locked down completely, and that this was probably bad.

"She named the source," he whispered. "Did I tell you? La Cuna. The Cradle. She said the whole valley sat in the mountain's lap like a child." His breath caught, held, released. "She said the river remembers everything. She said that's why they'd never get away with it. She believed the water would testify."

Ines looked down at the boot in her hands. Small. Patient. Twenty-six years in the current, and it had come ashore two meters from where she slept.

*The river keeps what it's given,* her mother used to say, in another life, kneeling at the edge of some sampling station, showing a small girl how to read a gauge. *Your job is to be worth telling.*

"Rest," Ines said finally, and heard how much it cost her to say anything gentle at all. "We'll talk more when the fever breaks."

"There isn't time for the fever to break," Voss said, and closed his eyes, and this time she let him.

* * *

Night came down on the flooded timber, black and dripping. Astrid took first watch. Mabry rolled into his bedroll with the groan of a man whose body had become a list of complaints. Ines sat by the dead radio and cleaned it with a rag, working corrosion out of the dial housing, not because it could ever live again but because her hands wanted a task and her mind wanted a door and the radio was both.

She was still at it when Astrid's hand closed on her shoulder.

Not hard. Just there. Ines went still.

Downstream, faint but unmistakable, an engine. Not the loose slap of a fisherman's outboard — something bigger, steadier, running against the current's grain with purpose. And then light: a white blade sweeping through the trees across the channel, swinging along the far bank, climbing, sweeping again. Floodlights. Methodical. A search pattern.

Astrid was already moving, killing the stove's ember, pulling tarps dark, waking Mabry with two fingers pressed to his shoulder. Ines gathered her father's head into her lap and held him down gently when the fever made him murmur, and together the four of them became part of the flooded forest — four shapes among ten thousand shadowed trunks, breathing shallow, while the light walked the water below them.

The patrol boat slid past their island close enough that Ines could hear voices on it, clipped and bored, and see the consortium's mark stenciled on the wheelhouse: a stylized peak over crossed lines. The floodlight raked the debris line where the boot had lain. It passed over the place where she'd knelt. It kept going.

For a full ten minutes after the sound faded, nobody moved.

"They're not looking for us," Mabry breathed at last. "Not yet. That's a sweep pattern. They're looking for something *in* the river."

"Or making sure nothing comes out of it," Astrid said.

Ines looked down at her father's sleeping face, then upstream, into the black where the river ran cold and fast toward a valley that had been holding its breath for twenty-six years.

*The water will testify,* her mother had believed.

Tomorrow the flood would drop. Tomorrow they'd run upstream, toward the lights, toward the source, toward whatever the ice had kept.

She tightened her arms around the man who had lied to her for nine years, and waited for morning.
