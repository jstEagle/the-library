## 8. The Portage

The falls announced themselves a mile before they arrived, a sound like the sky tearing along a seam. Mabry cut the engine and let the Provision drift while they listened, and nobody said what they were all thinking, which was that no boat on earth was going through that.

"Salto Grande," Mabry said finally. "Runs a hundred meters over basalt. In '09 a consortium survey crew tried to line their gear down the gorge." He spat over the side. "We found the gear. That's all I'll say about the rest."

"So we carry," Astrid said.

"Three days," Mabry said. "Maybe four, the way Voss is."

Voss lay under the tarp in the bow, his breathing a wet, laborious music. Ines sat beside him with her fingers on his wrist, counting a pulse that seemed to arrive less and less often, like a guest who kept forgetting the appointment. When Astrid looked at her, she shook her head — not *worse*, exactly, but not better, and on this river *not better* was a currency losing value daily.

They put in at a gravel shelf below the gorge's throat and began the ugly arithmetic of portage. The Provision came apart into what Mabry swore were designed pieces and what Astrid suspected were just pieces: hull sections, the outboard, fuel drums, the gear. Two hundred kilos of it, give or take, and three kilometers of trail that wasn't a trail so much as a rumor of one along the gorge's northern lip.

Astrid did the math the way she did all her math, alone and without flinching: Voss weighed maybe fifty-five kilos now, down from whatever he'd been, and he could walk perhaps two hundred meters at a stretch before his legs went to strings. That meant carrying him. That meant either a litter and two bearers, or relays, or both. Mabry's shoulder had been wrong since Redbank — she'd watched him favor it, watched him hide it, and she'd said nothing, because on a boat there was nowhere to send a man to heal. Ines was strong but small, built for sampling stations and lab benches, not for hauling a dying man over scree. And Astrid's own left knee had been singing since the second gorge, a low hot note she kept turned down where nobody could hear it.

Four people. Two hundred kilos. Three days.

*Do the arithmetic,* her old instructor used to say. *Then do it again, because the first time you'll have lied to yourself.*

* * *

The first day they made eleven hundred meters.

It was Voss himself who insisted on walking the first stretch, and it was terrible to watch — the old man lurching along with an arm over his daughter's shoulders, his boots dragging twin furrows in the mud, his face the color of tallow. He talked the whole time, though not to them. He talked to Ruiz, his dead partner, pointing out features of the gorge as if Ruiz were walking beside them, as if the falls were a landmark on a trip they'd planned together decades ago and were only now keeping the appointment.

"There's the bench, Ruiz. See it? Told you we'd camp on the bench." His head swung, searching the empty air. "You never had patience for portages. That was always your — " He stopped. His hand found Ines's shoulder and gripped it, and for a moment his eyes cleared and he saw her, actually saw her, and something moved through his face like weather. "You have her hands," he said. "Marisol's hands. I never told you that."

Ines said nothing. She walked him the two hundred meters and then she lowered him onto the litter they'd lashed from paddles and pack frames, and she stood looking down at him with an expression Astrid couldn't file under any single name.

They carried him in relays after that. Astrid took the head, Mabry the feet, Ines the gear in two loads, shuttling. The trail climbed through flooded timber — dead trunks standing in standing water, the 1998 flood line still marked in gray on their bark — and then broke onto open scree where the sun came down like a hammer and the gorge roared below them, constant, indifferent, a sound that got into the bones and stayed.

At the second relay point Mabry set down his end of the litter a half-second late. Astrid saw it — the fractional delay, the way he caught himself against a boulder, the way his right arm hung afterward like something borrowed. She said nothing. She marked it. Leadership, she had learned, was mostly a discipline of things not said at the wrong moment, filed for the right one.

They camped on the bench Voss had promised Ruiz. Ines fed him sips of rehydration salts and he drank like a child, trusting, and then he slept, and Ines sat at the edge of the firelight sharpening her knife on a stone with slow, even strokes, and Astrid pretended not to understand what she was seeing: a woman keeping her hands busy so they wouldn't do something else.

"You should sleep," Astrid said.

"When he sleeps, I sleep." A stroke of the stone. "That was the deal. I keep him alive long enough to answer."

"And after he answers?"

The knife paused. In the gorge below, the falls went on tearing the sky. "I don't know," Ines said, and it was the first honest thing she'd said since the hotel, and Astrid found she respected it more than any plan.

* * *

The second day, the beacon.

Astrid found it by accident, which was the only way anyone found anything anymore. They'd broken camp at gray light and she'd gone forward to scout the trail where it narrowed along a rock rib, and there, wedged under a boulder at the rib's base, was a cairn. Not an old one. Fresh. Stones stacked in the last week — she could tell by the lichen scars still bright on the undersides.

Nobody stacked cairns on the Serrano. The river was closed by decree; there was no one to stack them for.

She crouched. The stones were placed the way her own instructors had taught her to place them, decades ago, in a training program run by an outfit she no longer named: a marker for a line of advance, sighted from the air. She stood up slowly and looked at the sky, at the hard blue emptiness of it, and thought about the patrol boat at the flooded timber, and the helicopter she'd heard two nights ago, a sound she'd told herself was distance and wind.

Then she went back to the boat and found their own beacon.

It was Mabry's — a satellite tracker bolted under the console, standard kit, the kind every licensed pilot on the Serrano carried because the consortium required it, because the consortium wanted its pilots findable. It had a battery life of ninety days and a position report every six hours, and it had been reporting their position, faithfully, every six hours, since the dock fire. Since before the dock fire. Every gorge, every camp, every relay point, delivered to whoever was stacking cairns.

She didn't say anything right away. She did the arithmetic first.

Option one: leave it running. They'd be found, probably tomorrow, probably at the worst terrain on the portage, by men with rifles and a mandate. Option two: remove it and run. But the moment it went silent, the silence itself would be a signal — a flare saying *here, exactly here, come look* — and they'd have perhaps a day's grace before searchers converged on their last known position. Option three: make it lie. She had no way to make it lie. It wasn't hers, she had no code, no cable, nothing but a screwdriver and a rock.

So: option two, with a modification. Kill it here, at the top of the portage, where the trail forked — one branch along the north lip, one dropping to an old slue on the south side — and let the silence buy them a day while the searchers sorted through two possible routes. Then navigate blind.

Blind. She stood there holding the thought up to the light. Below the falls the Serrano braided into a maze of channels through forty kilometers of flooded forest, unmarked, uncharted since the closure, shifting every flood season. Mabry knew it — Mabry had run supplies through it for three years — but Mabry's shoulder was failing and Mabry's memory was three years old and rivers do not keep still for anyone's memory.

She thought about the client. She thought about Diego, whose death had ended her career, and how the inquiry had found that she had made, in their words, *a series of defensible decisions that produced an indefensible outcome*. She thought about how every one of those decisions had been defensible alone and indefensible together, and how the arithmetic of leadership was always done alone, at altitude, in the wind, with incomplete numbers, and how the ledger came due later, in a hearing room, with the wind long gone.

Then she went and got the rock.

Mabry watched her unscrew the beacon's housing with the flat of the screwdriver and didn't stop her, and when she lifted the unit out — green light still blinking, faithful to the last — he said only, "Consortium'll want the deposit back."

"They can bill me."

"Where's it go?"

"Bottom of the slue on the south side. Let them find it drowned. Let them spend a day dragging water instead of walking trails." She weighed the unit in her hand. It was warm from the sun and heavier than its mass. "Then we run the braid blind."

Mabry was quiet a moment. "I know the braid," he said. "As of three years ago."

"Rivers change," Astrid said. "People don't. You'll remember what matters." She said it like she believed it, because a leader's first job was believing out loud, and her second job was doing the arithmetic that belief would cost.

She carried the beacon to the slue herself. It sank without ceremony, one green blink swallowed by brown water, and she stood a moment watching the surface close over it, feeling the specific loneliness of a decision no one had argued with her about because she hadn't let them. That was the trick nobody put in the manuals: the loneliest part wasn't deciding. It was deciding *first*, alone, so that everyone else only ever had to agree.

* * *

They ran the south branch that afternoon and camped where the slue met the braid, on a sandbar that wouldn't exist next flood season. The Provision was reassembled by firelight, Mabry working one-armed and pretending otherwise, and Voss woke once, clear-eyed in the way the dying sometimes were, and looked at the stars through the broken canopy and said, "Ruiz would have liked this. He always said the river kept its own books."

"Does it?" Ines asked. Her voice was flat, but she'd asked, and Astrid, watching from the fire, understood that the asking was the whole conversation, the entire nine-year estrangement compressed into two words.

"He said it forgives nothing," Voss said. "But it forgets nothing either. He said that's the same thing, if you live long enough." He closed his eyes. "I'm tired, Marisol."

Ines went still. Then she pulled the tarp over her father and lay down with her back to him, and Astrid saw her shoulders, and did not go to her, because some weights you carry by not touching them.

Astrid took first watch. The river ran dark past the sandbar, split and split again into its blind channels, and somewhere behind them — north, she judged, across the falls — a light moved along the gorge rim. A flashlight, or a headlamp, sweeping slowly. Methodical. Men doing a grid search around a drowned beacon, exactly as the arithmetic predicted.

It worked. That was the thing about the arithmetic: it usually worked, and it never once felt like winning.

She woke Mabry for second watch and lay down beside the cold side of the fire, and her knee throbbed in time with the falls, and she thought: *three more days of this, and then the braid, and then whatever's at the source.* She thought: *Voss won't make the source.* She thought it without pity and without cruelty, the way you think a number, and then she hated herself a little for the clarity, and then she slept, because tomorrow the numbers would need her again.

At gray light they pushed off into the maze. Mabry stood at the console with his good arm and read the water like a man reading a letter from someone he'd wronged, and the Provision slid into the first channel, and the flooded forest closed behind them like a door.

It was Ines, at the bow, who saw it first: fresh sawdust on the water, and tied to a drowned trunk ahead, a plastic surveyor's ribbon, consortium orange, streaming in the current.

Someone had marked this channel too. And the ribbon was pointing downstream — toward them, or past them, into water deep enough to hide anything.

Astrid raised her hand for silence, and the only sound was the river, and the river was saying nothing at all.
