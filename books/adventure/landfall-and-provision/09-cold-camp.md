## 9. Cold Camp

The storm came down the valley like something with intent.

Mabry had watched weather his whole life — from wheelhouses, from riverbanks, from the wrong side of too many horizons — and he knew the difference between weather that happened to you and weather that was after you. This was the second kind. It rolled off the icefields in a brown-grey wall, ate the light, and turned the Serrano from a river into a rumor. By the time they'd hauled the Provision into the lee of a granite shelf and thrown the tarp over her, the rain was coming sideways and the temperature had dropped ten degrees in an hour.

They were two days below the falls portage, four days out from Serpent Bend, and they were not moving.

"Three days," Mabry said, crouched under the overhang, watching the river eat its own banks. "Maybe four. After that it breaks."

"You can't know that." Ines stood with her back to the rock, arms crossed, water dripping off her hood.

"I've run this river eleven years. I know her moods better than my own."

"That's not reassuring," she said, "given what I know about your decisions."

He let that one go. There wasn't enough dry wood in Patagonia to waste on an argument.

Voss lay in the bow of the boat where they'd dragged it up, wrapped in every layer they owned except the ones Astrid and Ines were wearing. His breathing had a rattle in it now, like gravel shifting in a sluice box. He'd stopped eating yesterday. He drank when they made him drink, small sips of melted snow sweetened with the last of the sugar, and each time he looked at them with an expression Mabry recognized from other dying men: apology and defiance in equal measure, both refusing to say sorry.

Astrid crouched at the stern, doing arithmetic with their food bags. Mabry watched her hands move through the inventory without touching anything, counting by sight. She did everything that way now — measured, economical, like a woman spending coins she couldn't replace.

"Talk to me," she said without looking up.

"Nine days of food if we stretch it. Five people—" He caught himself. Four. Voss didn't count anymore, not against the rations; he took nothing and gave back worry. "Four eating. Two days stormbound minimum. Then the gorge above Serpent Bend, which is a full day even in good water, and this isn't good water. We'll come out of it hungry or we won't come out of it."

"And behind us?"

Mabry looked upriver, into the grey. The patrol boat had passed them yesterday evening, floodlights sweeping the banks, close enough that they'd killed their stove and sat in the dark listening to diesel engines work against the current. It hadn't seen them. Or it hadn't cared yet.

"Behind us is a company with helicopters and no reason to hurry," he said. "We're the only thing on this river moving slower than they are."

* * *

The fracture came on the second night, as fractures do — not all at once but along old lines, the ones already drawn.

The stove had finally gotten enough dry fuel going to melt snow, and they huddled around its blue flame while the wind tried to peel the tarp off the shelf above them. Voss was sleeping, or something near it, his breath whistling. Astrid was rationing silence the way she rationed food. And Ines sat with her knees pulled up, staring at her father's face in the stove-light, and whatever she'd been holding since the flooded timber camp — since the child's boot, since the fragments of confession — finally came apart in her hands.

"He knew." Her voice was low, but it carried under the tarp like a blade finding a gap. "He knew what was up there. He's known for nine years, and he waited until he was dying to say anything, because a dead man's map can't be questioned and a dead man doesn't have to finish the fight."

"Ines," Astrid said.

"No. I want it said out loud, once, where he can hear it or not hear it, I don't care which." She turned toward the bow. "You used us. You used me. A hydrologist looks legitimate on a permit application. A daughter looks sympathetic. You didn't come find me because you wanted forgiveness — you came because I'm cover, same as she is—" a jerk of her chin at Astrid, "—same as the pilot, same as everyone. We're not a crew. We're camouflage."

The stove hissed. The wind screamed somewhere above the shelf.

Mabry found himself looking at his own hands. Because the accusation had a shape he recognized. He'd been cover once too — running supplies for Cordillera Extraction, telling himself he was just a boat, just freight, just a man who moved things and wasn't responsible for what the things were for. Until the night he'd understood what the crates on his deck were for, and by then the river between him and clean hands was wide.

Voss opened his eyes.

It took him a long time, and Mabry watched him do it — watched the man haul himself up out of wherever he lived now, hand over hand, like climbing a rope out of deep water. When he spoke, his voice was thin and cracked, but it was his.

"You think I don't know that?" he said. "You think I haven't done the sums? Every person on this boat is a cost, girl. I've counted you all. I count you every morning when I wake up and see what my dying is costing you." He drew a breath that seemed to cost him a week. "I brought you anyway. That's the truth. Not cover. Not redemption. I needed you, and needing people is the one sin I never learned to stop committing."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what it is." His eyes found hers, and there was something terrible in them, something clear. "Your mother needed to be believed, and I didn't believe her until it was convenient. Now I need to be carried, and you carry me. We are a family of debts, Ines. All I'm asking is that you collect mine at the source instead of here."

Ines stood. For a moment Mabry thought she was going to walk out into the storm, and part of him — the honest part — thought maybe she should, maybe distance was the only mercy left in this boat. But she just stood there, shaking, rain hammering the tarp overhead like applause from a hostile crowd.

Then she sat back down, farther from her father than before, and said nothing, and the nothing was louder than the accusation had been.

Astrid met Mabry's eyes across the stove. In hers he saw the thing he was beginning to understand about her: she didn't look away from broken things. She looked straight at them and started calculating how to carry them.

"We turn back," Mabry said quietly. It was his turn. He'd been holding it two days. "That's what I keep coming back to. Storm costs us two days, gorge costs us one, ambushes cost us what they cost. We've got a dying man, nine days of food, and a company that wants the map more than it wants us alive — for now. Turn around, run downstream with the current, we're at the delta in three days. Voss dies warm instead of cold. We live."

"You want to quit," Ines said.

"I want to survive. It's different." He fed a stick to the stove. "I ran supplies for these people. I know how they think. Right now we're a curiosity. The day we become a threat, we stop being chased and start being handled. Turning back isn't quitting. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Astrid repeated. She said the word like she was weighing it.

"Everything out here is arithmetic," Mabry said. "Food, distance, weather, luck. Trust most of all. Trust is the only supply you can't resupply. Spend it wrong and no amount of courage gets it back."

Astrid looked at Voss. Voss looked at the ceiling of tarp and storm. And Ines looked at her father with an expression Mabry couldn't read — fury and grief braided so tight he couldn't tell which was holding which together.

"Nobody's turning back," Astrid said finally. "Not tonight. Sleep. We decide at dawn, and we decide together, and nobody decides alone."

But her voice, Mabry noticed, had already decided.

* * *

The delirium came in the smallest hours, the way it does.

Mabry had first watch, huddled at the mouth of the shelter with the river roaring past close enough to touch. Around three, the quality of Voss's breathing changed — the rattle smoothed out, went almost peaceful — and Mabry turned to check on him and found the old man sitting half upright, staring at the storm with eyes gone bright and somewhere else.

"Efraim." Mabry moved to him. "Lie back. You're burning up."

Voss didn't seem to hear. He was smiling. It was the worst thing Mabry had seen in years, that smile — a young man's smile wearing a dead man's face.

"There it is," Voss whispered. "There it is, Tomás. Do you see it?"

Mabry froze. Tomás. The partner. The name Voss had been calling Astrid for days now, confusing her, grieving her.

"The walls," Voss went on, his hand rising, tracing shapes in the air. "Blue ice all the way round, like a bowl God set down and forgot. And the river comes out of the middle of it — not from a glacier, Tomás, from *under*, from the aquifer, boiling up cold and clean enough to drink forever. La Cuna." He laughed, a dry rustle. "La Cuna. The Cradle. They named it right and then they buried the name."

"Efraim—"

"They built the camp on the moraine. Ninety-eight. We were so careful, we were so *quiet*—" The smile collapsed. His hand dropped. "Tomás, they're shooting. Why are they shooting, we only came to *measure*—"

Ines was awake. Mabry heard her before he saw her, the small sound she made, and then she was there beside him, and neither of them touched the old man, because touching him felt like it would break something that was already breaking.

"He's naming it," Ines whispered. "The source. He's actually—"

"La Cuna," Voss said again, softer now, sinking. "Tell her. Tell my girl the river remembers where it's born. Tell her that's worth carrying." His eyes drifted closed. "Worth carrying upstream..."

The words trailed off into the rattle again, and then into sleep, real sleep or its counterfeit.

For a long time nobody moved. The storm hammered. The river roared. Somewhere up-valley, beyond two gorges and a waterfall and a hundred miles of country no map admitted existed, a bowl of blue ice held a dead camp and a living secret, and a dying man had just given it a name.

Mabry looked at Ines. Her face in the stove-light was wet, and he understood it wasn't only grief. It was the sound of a question being answered after nine years of asking — and the answer arriving from a man who might not survive to know he'd given it.

"He believes it," Mabry said quietly. "Whatever's up there — he believes it all the way down."

"Belief isn't evidence."

"No. But it's a heading." He poked the stove. "And headings are all we've got."

Dawn came grey and grudging, the storm still working but thinner, the way storms get when they've spent their anger. Astrid stood at the edge of the shelter studying the river, and Mabry came up beside her and read her face the way he read water — looking for the line, the channel, the way through.

"Still want to turn back?" she asked.

He thought about Voss tracing the shape of a hidden valley in the air. About Ines's face. About trust, spent like coin, and what it bought.

"No," he said. "But somebody should write down that I said yes first."

Astrid almost smiled. Almost.

"Get the boat ready," she said. "Storm's breaking. And Mabry—" She nodded upriver, toward the gorge, toward the bend with the serpent's name, toward the Cradle. "We're not the only ones who heard him last night. Keep your ears open."

Mabry followed her gaze into the grey and felt, beneath the fear, the old pull of the river — the one that had gotten him banned, betrayed, and banished, and had never once let him go.

Somewhere ahead, engines were waking up too.
