## 10. The Ambush at Serpent Bend

Astrid smelled the smoke before she saw the boat.

She was on the bow line, checking the lashings on the gear Mabry insisted on calling cargo and Ines called ballast, when the wind shifted off the water and carried it to her — diesel and something sweeter underneath, two-stroke exhaust. She straightened slowly, the way you do when you don't want to startle whatever's watching you.

"Mabry."

He was at the tiller, one hand on the throttle of the Provision's ancient outboard, squinting into the morning glare off the Serrano. "I smell it."

"How far?"

"River says a bend. Bend says half a klick." He glanced back at her. "Serpent Bend. I told you about Serpent Bend."

"You told me it was suicide."

"I told you it was suicide *in flood*. This is only high water." He said it like that made it better. It didn't. The river here ran fast and green through a canyon whose walls leaned in from both sides, polished granite streaked with quartz, and the bend itself — according to Mabry, according to every pilot who'd ever lost a boat there — doubled back on itself so sharply that the current hit the far wall and rebounded into a standing wave the locals called the Snake's Tongue. Boats went in. Some came out. None came out dry.

Ines emerged from under the tarp where Voss lay propped against the duffels, his breathing a wet rattle they'd all stopped commenting on because commenting didn't change it. "What is it?"

"Company car," Mabry said.

Voss opened his eyes. He'd been drifting for hours, in and out, sometimes calling Astrid by his dead partner's name — Tomás, Tomás, check the gauge — and sometimes surfacing sharp and lucid enough to frighten her. He surfaced now. "They're not here for us," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Not yet. They want the map."

"They can have my half of it," Ines said flatly.

"They want it *intact*," Voss said. "That's the difference. A dead man's map is worth nothing. A burned map is worth nothing. They need it whole, and they need it from someone who knows how to read it, which means—" he coughed, and the cough went on too long, and Astrid looked away from the blood-flecked cloth he pressed to his mouth— "which means they need us alive. For now."

"For now," Astrid repeated. She'd heard that construction before, in other countries, in other jobs gone wrong. *For now* was what men said when they hadn't yet decided which end of the transaction you were on.

The bend came up faster than she wanted. And around it, nose-out from a gravel bar where no boat had any business being moored, sat a gray-hulled patrol skiff with an outboard twice the size of theirs and three men aboard wearing the kind of jackets that didn't have logos because logos could be traced.

* * *

Mabry killed the engine and let them drift, and the silence was worse than the noise had been. The canyon amplified everything: the water hissing over submerged rock, the creak of the Provision's hull, Voss's breathing, which had become the loudest thing aboard.

"Options," Astrid said quietly.

"We go back." Mabry's jaw worked. "Two days upstream to the last takeout. They'll run us down before we make a kilometer. That boat does forty."

"Then we land and portage."

"Around this? Look at the walls, Reyes. There's no bank. There's rock going straight up for two hundred meters. We'd be climbing with a dying man and a boat on our backs while they pick us off like—" He stopped himself, but everyone heard the rest of the sentence anyway.

Ines had her binoculars up. "Three men. One at the bow with a rifle slung, not shouldered. Two aft. There's a fourth on the gravel bar, by the fire. They're not hiding."

"Because they don't think they need to," Astrid said. Her heart was doing something strange — not racing but slowing, each beat deliberate, the way it did in the last minutes before a rope length or a river pitch, when her body understood before her mind did that fear was a luxury and arithmetic was free. "They're set up as a checkpoint, not an ambush. They expect us to see them and stop."

"So we stop," Ines said. "Talk. You're good at talking your way past people. You talked us past the fuel dock at Puerto Cobre."

"The fuel dock wasn't carrying rifles."

"The fuel dock man wanted more money than we had. Different problem, same shape."

Astrid almost laughed. Almost. Below them, maybe four hundred meters, the gray skiff sat patient as a spider, and beyond it the canyon wall curved away and the river bent out of sight toward the Snake's Tongue, and Mabry was looking at that curve the way a man looks at a door he swore he'd never open again.

"No," Astrid said, because she saw it forming in his face. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"It's not suicide in this water."

"You said—"

"I said a lot of things when I was drinking." He wiped his palms on his thighs. "The Tongue is a rebound wave. It looks like a wall. It isn't. You punch through the left third where the return current cancels the main flow, and if you're still floating after that, the rest is just reading water fast. I've run it twice."

"And?"

"And once I made it." He reached under the console and came out with a battered life vest, threw it to Ines, then another to Astrid. The third he buckled onto Voss himself, gently, the way you buckle a child in, and Voss let him without protest, which frightened Astrid more than the rifles did.

"Mabry," she said. "If we miss the left third—"

"Then we were dead anyway. They just hadn't gotten around to it yet." He pulled the starter cord. The outboard caught on the second pull, that ragged two-stroke bark echoing down the canyon like a gunshot announcing itself.

The men on the skiff stood up.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, and afterward Astrid would remember it as fragments stitched together by sound.

Mabry opened the throttle and the Provision surged downstream, and for ten seconds the gray skiff did nothing, because its crew genuinely believed no one was insane enough to keep coming. Then the man with the rifle raised it, and the first shot cracked off the canyon wall somewhere above Astrid's head and the report came back a half-second later, doubled, tripled, the granite throwing their own violence back at them.

"Down!" She shoved Ines flat behind the gunwale, dropped beside her, felt the deck shudder as rounds slapped the water astern — walking closer, the shooter correcting, patient, professional. A round punched through the transom near Mabry's knee and wood splinters stung her cheek.

"They're shooting at the *boat*," Ines shouted over the engine. "Why are they shooting at the boat?"

"Because they want the map dry!" Voss, propped against the duffel, laughing — actually laughing, a terrible sound with blood in it. "You can't shoot a man holding the thing you need! Aim low, boys, aim low!"

Another burst. Something important-sounding clanked. The engine note stuttered, caught, held. Mabry hunched over the tiller like a man sheltering a flame with his body.

"Serpent Bend!" he bellowed. "Everybody hold onto something that loves you back!"

The canyon turned. Astrid got one look at it — the river slamming into the far wall, piling up, the white chaos of the Tongue standing across the channel like a gate — and then she stopped looking at the river and started looking at Mabry, because his hands on the tiller were the only part of this she couldn't do anything about.

He didn't go left. He went left-*ish*, a line that looked wrong, that flirted with the rebound current, and Astrid understood a half-second too late that he was threading the seam between the main flow and the return, the place where the water argued with itself and canceled out. The Provision hit the seam and the world went vertical.

They climbed the Tongue. For one suspended moment the boat was pointed at the sky, Voss sliding down the deck until his boots hit the transom, Ines's hand finding Astrid's wrist and gripping hard enough to leave marks, and then the crest broke beneath them and they dropped into the trough on the far side with a impact that folded Astrid over the gunwale and knocked the breath out of her in a white grunt.

The engine screamed. Kept screaming. Mabry was still upright, still steering, reading the water below the drop at a speed that should have been impossible, calling rocks before they appeared — "left, LEFT, hold right — brace—" — and Astrid crawled forward on her stomach and threw her weight where he pointed, ballast with a brain, and the Provision threaded the needle twice more and then the canyon opened its hands and spat them out into a long, calm, braided reach where the water spread wide and shallow over gravel bars and the sun came down warm and stupidly gentle.

Behind them, around the bend, the gray skiff did not follow.

* * *

They grounded on a shingle bar two kilometers down and took stock, the way you do after a fall — inventory of bodies first, gear second, pride last.

The hull had taken three rounds. One had passed clean through the transom; one had gouged a furrow along the starboard chine; the third had entered the storage locker and destroyed, with what Astrid could only regard as editorial precision, the case of condensed milk. Mabry patched the holes with epoxy putty and a strip of rubber sheet while Ines checked Voss, who had come through the rapids flushed and bright-eyed and terrifyingly alive-seeming, adrenaline papering over the failure underneath the way fresh snow papers over a crevasse.

Astrid walked the bar alone, because someone had to, and because her hands wouldn't stop shaking and she preferred they do it privately.

That was when she saw the radio.

It was snagged in the willows at the head of the bar, half-submerged, military-green, a handset coiled on its lanyard. Prisoner's radio, she thought — no. Not prisoner's. Abandoned. Ditched. She followed the story backward: scuffed boot prints in the mud above the willows, a cigarette butt still damp, and the unmistakable churn of an outboard that had backed off the bar in a hurry. Someone had been waiting here. Downstream of the bend. The second net, in case the first one failed.

And they'd fled. Left a working radio in the willows and run.

She carried it back to the boat. Mabry looked at it, then at her, and read the whole conclusion off her face.

"They ran," he said.

"They ran."

"From *us*?" Ines came around the transom, Voss's pulse still under her fingers. "Four of us in a bullet-holed bathtub?"

"Not from us." Astrid turned the radio over. Stenciled on the casing, half worn: CORDILLERA EXTRACTION GROUP — SURVEY DIVISION 3. And beneath that, a unit designation she recognized from the hotel in Puerto Cobre, from the men who'd searched their rooms while she pretended to sleep. "From whoever's upstream of them. Division Three doesn't spook easy. Something ahead has them pulling back." She looked at Voss. "How close are we?"

He smiled. It transformed his face, made him briefly the man Ines must have remembered from before, from the photographs. "Close enough that they're nervous."

Mabry keyed the radio. Static, then a scatter of voices — clipped, coded, professional. He worked the dial, hunting, and caught a fragment clear enough to freeze them all:

"—negative on interdiction, repeat, negative. Hold position at the falls. Nobody goes above the falls until the survey team confirms containment. If the package surfaces, do *not* engage. Observation only. I say again, observation only. Director wants it intact and quiet. Quiet trumps fast."

Silence on the bar. The river murmured over the gravel, indifferent.

"*Package*," Ines said finally. "They're calling my father a package."

"They're calling the map a package." Voss's voice had gone thin again, the adrenaline spending itself. "Which means they know exactly what I'm carrying and exactly what it proves, and they've decided I'm more useful alive than dead. Congratulations, señorita. You've just been demoted from target to evidence."

Astrid crouched at the waterline, washing the grit from her split knuckles, and let herself feel it — really feel it, for five seconds, the cold terror of those rounds cracking past her head, the way her body had wanted to curl small and instead had thrown itself over Ines like a tarp over gear. Five seconds. Then she stood up and put the feeling away, because there was work to do and a river to run and a promise sitting in the waterproof tube inside her jacket that three armed men and a director somewhere wanted intact and quiet.

Here was the thing she understood now, standing on the shingle with her ears still ringing: somewhere between Puerto Cobre and this gravel bar, the job had changed shape. She had signed on to ferry a dying man to a hospital. Then to carry a map upstream. But this — putting her body between bullets and a stranger's daughter, running rapids she'd have refused as a paid guide, feeling that fierce, irrational, unprofessional surge when Ines's grip found her wrist on the Tongue — this wasn't escort work anymore. This wasn't even the promise. This was something older and less negotiable, and it scared her more than the rifles did, because it meant she had something to lose again.

She'd spent six years since Tomás's death making sure she had nothing to lose. Nothing to lose was safe. Nothing to lose floated light.

"Load up," she said. "We run until dark, then we hide the boat and walk the next stretch. If they're holding at the falls, the falls are our window — we portage through their checkpoint blind spot tonight, while they're watching the water for a boat that isn't coming."

Mabry raised an eyebrow. "Walk. With Voss."

"With Voss." She looked at the old man, already sagging against the duffel, spent. "Can you walk?"

Voss opened one eye. "I've been walking toward that valley for twenty-six years, señora. A few kilometers on my feet is nothing."

"He'll say that until he collapses," Ines said quietly, to Astrid, not to her father.

"Then we carry him," Astrid said. "Same as the boat. Same as everything else worth carrying."

She pushed the Provision off the bar and took the bow, and the current took them, and somewhere upstream a director who wanted things intact and quiet was about to learn that quiet had never once been on offer.
