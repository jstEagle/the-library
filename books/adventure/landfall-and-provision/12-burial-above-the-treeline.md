## 12. Burial Above the Treeline

Ines knew her father was dead before she reached the tent.

It wasn't a sound. It was the absence of one — the particular silence that settles over a camp when the person who has been breathing raggedly through the night, the person whose breathing you have been counting without admitting you're counting it, simply stops. She stood outside the orange nylon with her hands full of firewood and let the moment arrive the way cold water arrives, all at once and then everywhere.

Inside, Astrid was already kneeling beside him. She had her fingers at his throat and her other hand flat on his sternum, and she wasn't moving, and that stillness told Ines everything the fingers would have told her anyway.

"He went in his sleep," Astrid said. Her voice was level, the guide's voice, the voice that had carried them through Redbank and the portage and the ambush at Serpent Bend. "Sometime in the last hour. He wasn't in pain. I was awake — I would have heard."

Ines set the firewood down outside the tent flap, carefully, the way you set down anything when your hands might betray you. Then she crawled in.

Efraim Voss lay on his side, facing the tent wall, his map case tucked under his arm like a child's stuffed animal. His face in the gray pre-dawn light was finally, completely at peace, and Ines found that she hated it — hated the peace, hated that he had gotten to choose his exit, hated that the last thing he'd done was sleep while she had spent the night awake two tents away, rehearsing the questions she would never get to ask.

She had nine years of questions. She had a mother's death. She had a childhood spent being told the river took her, when all along it had been men who took her, men with survey stakes and burn barrels, and one of those men was lying here with his mouth finally closed.

She should have felt relief. She had told herself she would feel relief.

What she felt was the specific weight of a hand she had never held.

* * *

They buried him above the treeline because that was where the ground allowed it, and because Mabry said — and no one argued — that a man who had spent forty years looking up at mountains should spend eternity looking down at his river.

The moraine rose off the eastern bank a half-mile above their camp: a long spine of glacial debris, granite and till and the pale bones of a landscape the ice had chewed and abandoned. Lichen the color of old blood crusted the stones. From the top, the Serrano appeared and disappeared through the valley's bends, a ribbon of gray-green glacial milk, and somewhere up that ribbon, two days' hard travel, lay La Cuna.

They carried him up in the map case's waterproof groundsheet, because there was nothing else, and because Ines said he would have appreciated the economy. Astrid took the head of the improvised stretcher. Mabry took the feet. Ines walked beside and did the thing her body insisted on doing, which was to steady the load every time it swayed, even though it didn't need steadying, even though both of the others were stronger than she was and had been carrying things — boats, packs, dying men — for longer than she'd been alive.

The digging took two hours. The soil above the treeline was a thin scab over stone, and they had to work around boulders the size of refrigerators, levering them with the boat's spare oar until Mabry's bad shoulder made him swear in three languages. Astrid dug the way she did everything: without complaint, without wasted motion, her breathing the only sound she allowed herself. Ines noticed, in the way you notice things when your hands are busy and your mind is trying to escape, that Astrid favored her left leg on the downstrokes, and that she caught Ines noticing, and that neither of them said anything.

The grave, when it was done, was not deep. Nothing above the treeline is deep. It was a long, shallow trench in the moraine, lined with what stones they could move, and they laid Efraim Voss in it with the dignity available to them, which was not much, and which was everything.

Ines had thought about what to say for the entire two hours of digging. She had drafted eulogies in her head the way she drafted grant proposals — ruthlessly, structurally, cutting every adjective that didn't earn its keep. She had a paragraph about his brilliance as a field hydrologist. She had a sentence about his terrible jokes. She had a whole passage about her mother, Elena, and how the two of them had loved this river in the same greedy, consuming way, and how one of them had died for it and the other had spent thirty years dying of it.

She stood at the head of the grave with all of it assembled behind her teeth.

Then she looked at the river.

It came down out of the north, out of the closed country, out of the valley the decree had sealed and the consortium had bought and her mother had bled on. It didn't know about any of that. It just ran, the way it had run before the 1998 expedition and the burn barrels and the lies, the way it would run after the Cordillera Extraction Group was a footnote in someone else's dissertation. It ran because that was what water did, and it carried everything — sediment, history, the dissolved chemistry of a continent — and it gave nothing up until someone came and measured it.

Her mother had been a measurer. So was Ines. So, in his crooked, obsessive, cowardly, magnificent way, had been Efraim Voss.

Ines took the map case from where Astrid held it and drew out the map — the hand-inked thing her father had carried for twenty-six years, the sole survivor of a murdered expedition, drawn in Elena Voss's own hand on paper that had gone soft as cloth from handling. Ines had studied it every night since they'd taken it from the hotel safe. She knew the contour lines the way she knew her own pulse. She knew the notation in the upper corner, in her mother's slanted engineer's hand, the one her father had never been able to bring himself to explain and that Ines had finally decoded herself two nights ago, alone, by lantern light, with her heart going like a trapped bird.

She folded the map closed.

"No eulogy," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "He wouldn't have wanted one. He hated being summarized."

Mabry took off his cap. Astrid put her hand on Ines's shoulder, light as a landing bird.

Instead, Ines read the coordinates.

She read them the way you read coordinates in the field: slowly, precisely, twice. Latitude to the second. Longitude to the second. Elevation, estimated from the barometric record her mother had kept in the margins. She read them loud, into the wind that came down the valley off the ice fields, and she watched the numbers leave her mouth and go out over the water the way smoke goes out over water, dispersing, becoming part of the air that the river breathed.

"Anyone who comes looking," she said, when she was done, "anyone at all — a surveyor, a prosecutor, a journalist, a hydrologist with a grudge — can find him here. And anyone who finds him here will find these numbers. This grave is a waypoint now. That's the only eulogy he gets, and it's the only one he'd have wanted. He's not resting. He's pointing."

She knelt and put her palm flat on the turned earth, once, briefly, the way you check a reading.

"I'm not forgiving you," she said quietly, so that only Astrid, closest, could hear, and maybe not even her. "But I'm going to finish what you started, and I'm going to do it right, and that's worth more to both of us than forgiveness would be."

Then she stood up, and Mabry and Astrid filled the grave, and they built the cairn — a rough cone of moraine stone, waist-high, anchored with the largest boulders three of them could roll. Astrid set the last stone. It was a flat slab of granite with a seam of white quartz running through it like a surveyor's line, and she placed it with a care that Ines understood was not about her father at all, but about the dead woman Astrid had once failed to bring home off a mountain, and about every dead person every guide carries, and about the fact that some weights you don't put down, you just learn to walk with.

Mabry said a few words in the low river dialect of the port slums, words Ines didn't fully catch, addressed not to Voss but to the water. Then he wedged the cap he'd taken off onto the top of the cairn, where it looked absurd and exactly right, and said, "He'd have hated that. Good."

* * *

They came down off the moraine in single file, and the camp, when they reached it, had the stripped-down silence of a place that has already begun to be abandoned.

There was no discussion about whether to go on. Ines had expected one — had, in the honest dark of the last week, half hoped for one, because a part of her had wanted someone else to make the argument that would let her stop. But Astrid was already repacking the Provision's dry bags with the methodical economy of a woman who had made her decision somewhere around the second hour of digging, and Mabry was down at the boat running his hands along the hull seams, muttering about caulking, and when Ines stood in the middle of camp holding her father's map and said, "We're still going," neither of them even looked up.

"Obviously," said Astrid.

"Obviously," said Mabry, and spat over the side in the traditional manner of rivermen marking a departure.

But smaller and quieter — that was true, and Ines felt it settle over the three of them like the valley's own weather. They were three now instead of four, and the arithmetic of the journey had changed in ways that went beyond rations and watch rotations. A dying man, however much he weighed on a boat, is a kind of cargo; cargo gives a voyage its excuse. Now there was no excuse. Now it was three people and a map, going up a closed river toward a valley that a consortium with helicopters was actively trying to keep empty, and every one of them knew it, and every one of them had chosen it anyway, and that knowledge sat in the boat with them like a fourth passenger who didn't row.

Ines spent the afternoon doing what she was best at, which was work. She inventoried the food with a hydrologist's unsentimental precision and did not like the number she arrived at. She checked the water filters, the rope, the medical kit, the fuel. She re-read her mother's marginalia on the map until the lantern gutters low, and found, in the margin of the third sheet, beneath a contour line that marked the valley's northern wall, a notation she had missed before — a date, *14.02.98*, and beside it two words in Spanish that made her close the map and sit very still for a long time:

*El acuífero respira.*

The aquifer breathes.

Her mother had known. Not suspected — known. Had stood at the source of the Serrano and measured something so large, so impossible, that she had written it down the way you write down a miracle, and then she had rowed home to tell the world, and the world had killed her for it, and her husband had spent twenty-six years carrying her handwriting around like a wound he refused to let close.

Ines looked across the fire at Astrid, who was sharpening the machete with long, even strokes, her bad leg stretched out on a pack, her face in the firelight unreadable and utterly reliable.

"The northern wall," Ines said. "There's a route marked. My mother drew it. It's not on any survey."

Astrid's strokes didn't pause. "How bad?"

"Bad. But she was five months pregnant when she mapped it, and she graded it *moderate*."

Something moved through Astrid's face — not quite a smile, something older and harder, the expression of a guide meeting a dead colleague across a quarter-century. "Then it's doable," she said. "We go at first light. Before the weather, and before whatever the consortium has left in this valley finds us first."

Mabry, from the boat, without turning around: "There's a helicopter on the far side of the divide. Heard it an hour ago. Heading north."

The fire cracked. Up on the moraine, barely visible in the last light, the cairn stood with its absurd cap, pointing north the way its occupant had pointed for twenty-six years.

Ines folded the map, put it inside her jacket, against her chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat, and began, with steady hands, to break camp.
