## 13. The Ascent

The Provision wouldn't go any farther.

Mabry beached her in a gravel fan where the Serrano split itself into braids too shallow for a hull, and stood at the bow looking upstream the way a man looks at a wall he's been paid to climb. The river came down out of the mountains in white threads, braiding and rebraiding across a valley floor of gray stone, and beyond it the country simply stopped pretending to be navigable. Scree. Ice. A horizon with teeth.

"End of the line," Mabry said.

"End of the boat's line," Astrid said. She was already repacking the rope.

They buried what they couldn't carry under a cairn at the treeline — spare fuel, the heavier tent, most of the food they'd have wanted over the next four days and would now do without. Ines knelt at Voss's grave first, thirty meters up the moraine, and touched the stones they'd stacked there two mornings ago. She didn't say anything. She'd said the coordinates aloud at the burial, once, flat as a survey reading, and that had been the whole eulogy, and Astrid understood it was also the whole inheritance.

"Four days up," Ines said, coming back down. "Two days back, if we're empty-handed."

"If we're anything-handed," Mabry said.

Astrid shouldered her pack and tested her left leg against the slope before she committed her weight to it. It held. It always held, in the morning, when it was cold and honest and hadn't yet been asked to lie all day.

They climbed.

* * *

The country above the braids was country no map admitted to. Astrid had seen the survey sheets — the consortium's, the ones Voss had stolen copies of — and on them this valley was blank paper, a polite nothing between the river gauge stations. No contours. No trails. Just the cartographer's white silence that meant either *nothing here* or *we don't want you to look*. After nine days of being hunted by men with helicopters, she'd stopped believing in *nothing here*.

The moraine gave way to talus, and the talus to a glacier's dirty snout, and by midday they were walking on ice that groaned under them like an old ship. The air thinned faster than the ground rose. Astrid counted her breathing without meaning to — she'd done it for twenty years, a guide's habit, matching clients' pace to their lungs — and caught herself counting her own like a stranger's.

Her left knee had been bad since 2016. Everyone knew that; it was in the incident report, the one that had ended her career: *guide sustained ligament damage during recovery operation.* What nobody knew was that it had never healed right, that she'd managed it the way you manage a bad debt, rolling it forward, paying minimums, and that somewhere below Redbank Gorge the debt had come due. Three days ago, hauling Ines out of the water, she'd felt something in it shift and settle wrong, like furniture moved in a dark room. Since then it had been speaking to her in a language of small hot signals, and she had been answering in the only dialect available: *not yet.*

She set the day's rhythm so that she was always first onto a slope or last off one — never in the middle, where someone might watch her take a step. It was old craft. You hid a limp the same way you hid fear: by controlling where people's eyes went.

"You're favoring it."

Ines. Of course. The woman read water for a living; of course she read bodies too.

"I'm distributing load," Astrid said. "It's called technique."

Ines looked at her for a long second, the way she looked at a gauge station whose numbers didn't match the river. Then she let it go, which was worse than arguing. Arguing meant she cared enough to spend the breath.

Behind them, far downstream and impossibly loud in the thin air, a helicopter crossed the valley mouth.

They all three froze, three dark shapes on white ice, and watched it hang there a moment — a black insect at this distance, patient as a question — then swing away north along the range front. Not searching the valley. Searching the edges. Yet.

"They're not following the river anymore," Mabry said quietly. "They're cutting ahead."

"Then we stop being on the river," Astrid said, and turned uphill.

* * *

By late afternoon the glacier steepened into a wall of blue ice split by a rock rib, and Astrid earned her keep the way she'd earned it her whole life: forty meters of rope, three anchors, and a voice kept level while her hands did arithmetic. She led the pitch, placed the protection, brought them up one at a time. Her knee screamed on the final traverse and she made no sound at all, because sound travels, and because Ines was ten meters below watching her boots for placement.

At the top there was a col, and beyond the col — the valley opened like a held breath finally released. A hanging basin ringed by peaks, glacier-fed, green-black in its lower folds with a forest that shouldn't have existed at this altitude, sheltered and secret, and at its head, half-hidden in cloud, a mountain shaped like a cupped hand.

Voss's map had called it La Cuna. The Cradle.

"That's not possible," Ines said. "That basin is a heat trap. Look at the aspect — it would hold meltwater all year. That's why the aquifer—" She stopped herself, but her eyes were shining with something that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite triumph. Her mother had died trying to show the world this view, and here it was, unguarded, unphotographed, real.

"It'll still be there tomorrow," Astrid said. "We camp here. Light's going."

It was the right call and she knew it was the right call and she hated making it, because every hour they waited was an hour the helicopters gained. But she'd buried one client already by pushing past the margin, whatever the official report said about terrain, and she would not do it twice. Not with these two. Whatever else she'd lost, she still had the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said: *cold camp, early start, descend into the basin at first light.*

They pitched the tent in the lee of the rock rib. Mabry melted snow and they ate half-rations and watched the light die off the peaks one color at a time. Astrid sat with her left leg stretched out straight, which she could pass off as relaxation, and pressed the heel of her hand into the swelling above the kneecap where no one could see.

The joint was hot. Properly hot. Hot the way it had been in 2016, in the hours after, when she'd carried a dead man's weight down a scree slope on a leg that had already quit and she'd done it anyway because the alternative was leaving him for the ravens. The body remembered that too. The body kept its own ledger, and it was not a ledger you could trade away, not even for a dying man's promise.

"How bad?" Mabry said, low, beside her. He didn't look at her. He was watching the valley mouth, where the last light was going out.

"Manageable."

"I ran supplies for those people for six years," Mabry said. "You know how I got good at my job? I learned to know when the engine was lying to me. Gauge says fine, temperature says otherwise — believe the temperature." He finally turned his head. His face was all weathered planes and regret. "Believe the temperature, Reyes."

"In the morning I'll tell you the truth."

"That's what the engine always says."

But he dropped it, and she was grateful, and gratitude made her feel like a fraud, because she had no intention of telling him the truth. The truth was that the leg had maybe one hard day left in it, and the source was two days away, and the descent afterward would be a separate negotiation with a separate God. She'd make the ascent. What came after belonged to the woman who survived the ascent, and that woman could handle her own debts.

Ines crawled out of the tent and sat on Astrid's other side, knees drawn up, watching the stars come out over La Cuna. For a while none of them spoke. The glacier ticked and settled beneath them, ice doing its slow bookkeeping in the dark.

"My mother used to say the mountains are honest," Ines said eventually. "That's why she liked them better than people. A mountain never pretends to be smaller than it is."

Astrid looked at her own left leg, laid out straight and swollen in the starlight, lying to everyone including itself.

"No," she said. "They don't pretend. They just wait for you to catch up."

* * *

The helicopters came at dawn. Two of them.

Astrid woke to the sound before she was awake to anything else — that flat heavy thrum bouncing off the cirque walls, directionless, everywhere — and had the tent flap open with her boots half-laced while Ines was still surfacing from sleep. The machines were low, running the valley floor below them, flying the pattern searchers fly when they've narrowed the box. One passed directly beneath the col, close enough that she could see the survey pod slung under its belly, the sensor array, the man in the door with binoculars.

She pulled Ines back from the lip by the collar, gently, and put her eye to the gap instead.

The helicopters worked the basin for twenty minutes. Swept the forest edge. Hovered over something near the valley head — the old expedition camp, maybe, or the outflow stream — then rose together and flew south, toward the range crest, and the sound faded until it was just another thing the wind might have made.

"They know we're close," Mabry said. He'd come out without his pack straps even undone. "That wasn't a patrol. That was a sweep. Next pass won't be a sweep."

Astrid studied the basin. The descent route she'd picked — the glacier's eastern flank, a ramp of moraine and old snow — was exposed to the valley for its entire length. Two hundred meters of open ground with nowhere to hide and a helicopter that could arrive in ninety seconds.

Or the western side: steeper, crevassed, a route she'd have to find and lead on sight, with a knee that had filed its formal complaint. Slow. Very slow. The kind of slow that let the next pass catch them in the open anyway.

There was a third option and she refused to look at it. The third option was turning around, and the third option didn't exist.

"West side," she said. "We rope up. Single file. Nobody moves until I say."

"The east ramp is—"

"The east ramp is a shooting gallery." She was already coiling rope. "West side. Move."

The descent took everything. The snow bridges were rotten with the season and she probed every step with the axe, placed pickets where the ice allowed, and talked constantly — *weight on the rope, test, commit* — because talking kept her from listening to the joint. By the fourth hour the knee had stopped sending signals and started sending verdicts. Each flex was a bright wire pulled through the joint. She found a rhythm that used the right leg for braking and the axe for everything else, and if it looked strange from behind, well, nobody behind her was a guide.

Except Ines was a scientist, and scientists notice patterns.

Halfway down, on a rest stop that Astrid disguised as a navigation check, Ines crouched beside her and looked — openly, clinically — at the left boot, at the way Astrid had unconsciously wedged it into a crack to unload it.

"You should have told me."

"Told you what? I'm standing on a glacier. Everything hurts."

"Astrid." First time she'd used the name since the burial. "If your leg goes on this ice, we both go. That's not courage, that's physics."

For a moment Astrid almost told her. Almost said: *in 2016 I carried a man's body down a mountain on this leg and the leg has never forgiven me and neither have I, and I am afraid, genuinely afraid, not of falling but of stopping, because stopping is what I did after, for six years, and I will not stop again.* The words were right there, loaded, ready.

What she said was: "I'll make it down. Get on the rope."

Ines held her eyes one more second — that hydrologist's stare, measuring flow behind the surface — then clipped in. And as she turned, Astrid saw her glance back at Mabry, and saw Mabry give the smallest nod, and understood with cold clarity that they had already discussed it. That her secret had become their project. That the ledger had a second set of books now, kept by other hands.

Fine. Let them watch. She'd get them to the source, and then the leg could say whatever it wanted.

They reached the basin floor at midafternoon, dropping into the green shadow of that impossible forest, and the temperature fell and the air thickened and for a hundred vertical meters it was almost easy, almost kind. Old-growth trees, moss to the ankle, a silence so complete Astrid could hear her own pulse in the bad knee like a second heartbeat keeping dishonest time.

Then the sound came again. Both helicopters. Close. Coming up the valley from the south, low, deliberate — not searching now. Arriving.

And through the trees ahead, where the forest broke at the basin's head, Astrid saw what they'd come nine days and one death to see: the pale scar of the 1998 camp, tents collapsed under decades of snowmelt, a cache barrel split open, and beside it — unmistakable, absurd, intact — the drill rig and core boxes of a survey that officially never happened.

Between them and it, on the open gravel flat, a figure in consortium orange stepped out of the treeline and raised one hand. Not a weapon. A wave. An invitation.

Behind them, the helicopters settled onto the flat like wasps coming home.

"Reyes," Mabry breathed.

"I see it," Astrid said, and reached for the rope, and put her weight on the leg that had promised her nothing, and started forward.
