## 14. Whiteout

The storm came down on them like a verdict.

Ines heard it before she saw it — a sound in the glacier above them, a deep structural groan, the mountain clearing its throat. Astrid stopped mid-step and held up one fist, and Ines had learned enough by now to stop when that fist went up, to stand still with her boots planted and listen.

"What is it?"

"Spindrift." Astrid's face was turned into the wind, eyes narrowed against the first hard grains already hissing across the snow. "It's coming off the icefield. We need wall, now."

There was no wall. There was the cliff face they'd been traversing all morning, a rampart of gray granite rising out of the white like the hull of a ship, and at its base a shallow overhang where the rock leaned out over its own shadow. It wasn't shelter. It was a suggestion of shelter. But Astrid looked at it the way she looked at everything — as an arithmetic problem — and said, "That's what we have," and started toward it.

Mabry carried Voss's ashes now, in a drybag lashed inside his pack, because burying him on the moraine hadn't meant leaving him behind entirely; it had meant leaving most of him, and taking the rest the last distance to La Cuna so he could see it, in whatever way the dead see things. Ines had argued about this for exactly one minute before realizing she didn't actually want to win. Her father was going to the source one way or another. She just wanted credit for not wanting it.

Now she took the front of the sled rope while Mabry hauled behind, and they dragged their gear into the overhang as the storm arrived in earnest — not gradually, not building, but all at once, the way weather works above the treeline, the sky simply deciding to end.

* * *

By the time they'd anchored the tarp across the mouth of the hollow, using pitons Astrid hammered with the flat of her axe and guy lines Mabry rigged from boat strap and bootlace, the world beyond three meters had ceased to exist. The wind found every gap in the rock and screamed through it in different registers, a choir of complaints. Snow came sideways, then upward, then in spirals that seemed to mock the very concept of down.

Ines sat with her back against stone that was cold enough to burn through two layers, and did the inventory she'd been doing since the moraine, when the arithmetic had changed.

Food: four protein bars, half a bag of rice, a tin of sardines Mabry had been saving "for an occasion." Fuel: enough for maybe six melts of snow, if they were disciplined. They had planned for five days of climbing. The storm would take at least two. Which meant they would come out of this hollow with nothing, and there were still — she checked the altimeter on her watch, a habit from fieldwork that had become a tic — nine hundred vertical meters between here and the valley, plus whatever the glacier route demanded.

"We're short," she said. Not a question. She wanted it stated, on the record, between the three of them.

Astrid didn't flinch. "We're short."

"How short?"

"Two days short, if the storm holds. One day short if we're lucky."

"And if we're not lucky?"

Astrid met her eyes across the dim space, the light through the tarp gone the color of old milk. "Then we make different decisions."

Ines wanted to ask what decisions. She didn't. Some questions you saved until you needed the answer, because answers spent early went stale.

Mabry, wedged deepest into the hollow where the rock curved, said nothing at all. He'd been quieter since the burial — not brooding exactly, but listening to something internal, some frequency Ines couldn't pick up. Once, passing him the rope on the scree, she'd caught him murmuring to the drybag. She'd decided not to have seen it.

They melted snow in shifts. The stove hissed under the tarp edge, and the wind answered it, and Ines drank water that tasted of ash and granite and thought about how her mother had died of thirst, technically, in the end — dehydration written on the death certificate as a contributing factor, as if the river that killed her had at least offered her a drink.

* * *

The first night, nobody slept much. The wind found a new note around midnight, lower, almost conversational, and Astrid crawled out twice to check the anchors, coming back each time with frost in her eyebrows and her jaw set tighter.

It was during the second night that Ines asked the question.

She hadn't planned to. That was the thing about darkness and confinement — they dissolved the membranes people kept between themselves. She'd been watching Astrid's silhouette against the faint gray of the tarp, the way the woman sat perfectly upright even resting, spine never touching the rock, as if leaning back were a luxury she'd forfeited along with everything else.

"The client," Ines said. "The one who died. You've never told me what happened."

The silence lasted long enough that Ines thought she wouldn't answer. The wind filled it, pressing at the fabric, testing the knots.

"His name was Tomás Elgueta," Astrid said finally. "He was sixty-one. A retired dentist from Temuco who'd decided he wanted to stand on a summit before his heart gave out, which — he told me this himself, laughing about it — was scheduled to give out any year now. He hired me through an outfitter that shouldn't have taken the booking. I told them that. I told them his cardiology clearance was four years old. They wanted the fee."

"You took him anyway."

"I took him anyway." Astrid's voice was flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone who had said these words before, to investigators, to lawyers, to herself in hotel rooms at three in the morning. "Because I'd done harder routes with older clients. Because I was arrogant about my own margins. Because the season had been thin and rent was real."

"What happened on the mountain?"

"He slowed down on day two. Not dangerously — just slower than the plan. And I had a choice. I could cut the route short, take the shoulder instead of the ridge, and get him down a day early. Or I could push, because the weather window was good and the ridge was right there and I'd promised him a summit." She paused. "I pushed. He made the top. He cried, actually. He was so happy. And then on the descent his heart — or something, the autopsy was ambiguous, altitude complicates everything — and he sat down in the snow ten minutes below high camp and didn't get up."

Ines waited. There was more. There was always more.

"I did CPR for forty minutes at four thousand meters," Astrid said. "Alone. I broke two of his ribs and I kept going, because that's what you do, you keep going, and the whole time some part of my brain was doing the math — how long to the nearest help, what the weather was doing, whether anyone even knew where we were — and the math said no one was coming. The math said it was always going to be me and him and the snow." Her silhouette shifted. "The inquiry cleared the route. The outfitter blamed my judgment. His family — his daughter — she said she didn't blame me. She sent a letter. Very kind. 'You stayed with him.' As if staying were the thing. As if staying weren't the minimum."

"Do you blame yourself?"

Another silence. Then, quietly, in a voice stripped of the guide's authority: "Every day. Not for pushing. For believing I was the kind of person who could carry someone else's life and not drop it."

Ines sat with that. Outside, the storm threw itself against the mountain, indifferent, and she understood suddenly that this was the same indifference that had taken her mother — not malice, not conspiracy, just terrain and timing and the fact that the world does not audit its accidents.

Except that wasn't true either. That was the thing she'd been circling for weeks.

"My mother wasn't killed by terrain," she said. "Not really."

* * *

Astrid turned toward her. In the dark she could feel the attention like heat.

"She drowned in the Serrano. That's the official record. Capsized at the confluence during a solo survey run, body recovered downstream, ruled an accident. Closed file." Ines heard her own voice go careful, the way it did when she handled data she didn't trust. "But she wasn't running that section solo. She had a partner on the survey — a man named Delgado, from the provincial water office. He came back alone. He said the raft hit a hole and flipped and she was swept. And everyone accepted it, because rivers kill people, because hydrologists know the risk, because convenient terrain erases inconvenient people."

"You think it wasn't an accident."

"I think my father knew it wasn't an accident, and chose the river's version anyway, because the river's version let him keep working, keep prospecting, keep dreaming, without becoming a problem. I think Delgado got promoted. I think the consortium's survey season opened the following spring in a valley that had been closed by decree for eleven years, and no one asked why the closure quietly ended." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "And I think my mother saw something in that valley worth dying for, and the men who killed her counted on the mountain to do the paperwork."

The wind gusted. The tarp cracked like a whip and settled.

"That's why you came," Astrid said. "Not for him." Meaning Voss. Meaning the ashes in Mabry's pack. "For her."

"For the record." Ines's throat was tight. "She has a line in a provincial database. Cause of death: drowning. Contributing factors: none. That's all she is. One line. And I'm a hydrologist — I spend my life making sure water gets measured correctly, that the numbers are honest, because bad numbers kill people downstream. And my own mother's number is wrong. It's the one measurement I can't fix, and I have spent nine years—" She stopped. Breathed. "Nine years not speaking to him, because he let them write it."

"And now he's dead, and you're carrying him to the place where the truth is."

"Where the truth was," Ines corrected. "If it's still there. If the ice kept it."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, rougher, the recitation-voice gone.

"They're the same story, aren't they. Tomás and your mother."

"How do you mean?"

"Inconvenient people erased by convenient terrain." Astrid said it slowly, tasting it. "Tomás was inconvenient because he was old and slow and his death embarrassed people who'd taken money they shouldn't have. Your mother was inconvenient because of what she saw. The mountain doesn't care which kind of inconvenience you are. It just provides the cover story. Heart failure at altitude. Drowning at the confluence. Act of God, case closed, next client, next survey season." A bitter breath that might have been a laugh. "I used to think the wilderness was honest. That it was the one place without lies. But it's worse than a liar. It's an accomplice. It'll swear to anything."

"Don't say that." It came out of Ines sharper than she intended. "Don't you dare say that."

"Why not? You just—"

"Because the river didn't lie. People lied and used the river. There's a difference, and if I lose the difference I lose—" She pressed her palm flat against the cold stone floor of the hollow, grounding herself the way she grounded instruments. "My mother loved that river. She trusted it. She read it better than anyone alive, probably. If I let them turn it into an accomplice, then they win twice. They kill her and they poison the only clean thing she had left."

Silence again. But a different quality of silence — Ines felt Astrid reconsidering, felt the other woman's guilt shift and settle into a new shape beside her own.

"You're right," Astrid said at last. "The terrain doesn't choose. People choose, and hide behind the terrain." A pause. "Which means the record matters. The numbers matter. What happened to Tomás is in a report somewhere with a conclusion I've never been allowed to read. Maybe someday—"

She didn't finish. Ines finished it for her, silently, in the dark: *maybe someday someone will dig up the file the way we're digging toward the ice.* Two guilts, she thought. Two closed cases. Two women sitting in a hole in a mountain, keeping each other company inside the same wound.

* * *

The second day, the storm did not break.

They ate the last of the bars at dawn, splitting them with a knife into precise quarters, and Ines watched Astrid give Mabry her own quarter and lie about having eaten. Mabry, who noticed everything about boats and nothing about people, or perhaps everything about people and pretended otherwise, ate it without comment and later split his sardines three ways without being asked, keeping none for himself, and when Ines protested he said, "I ran supplies for those bastards for two years. I can run on empty for two days. It's penance. Let me have it."

The fuel ran out mid-afternoon. After that they ate snow, small handfuls, letting it melt in the mouth because Astrid warned them that eating it fast would drop their core temperature, and the cold became a presence in the hollow, patient, thorough, working its way through sleeping bags and clothing and the spaces between people. Ines shivered in waves and timed her breathing to the wind and thought about hypothermia the way she thought about river stages — a curve you could read, a threshold you could forecast, and somewhere past a certain point the system stopped being recoverable.

Somewhere past midnight, her teeth chattering too hard for sleep, she heard Mabry talking to the drybag again. Low, apologetic, a man renegotiating terms with the dead.

"Hold on, old man. Nearly there. Nearly."

And Astrid, awake too, staring at the tarp, whispered something Ines almost missed:

"Tomás, if you're keeping score — I'm still carrying people. I don't know if that helps you. It's all I've got."

Ines reached across the dark and found Astrid's hand, gloved, frozen-stiff, and held it. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say that the holding didn't say better.

* * *

On the third morning, the wind died.

Not gradually — it simply stopped, between one hour and the next, the way a machine stops, and the silence afterward was so total that Ines could hear her own blood moving. Astrid unzipped the tarp and light poured in, blinding, the whole world refrozen into brilliance, and they crawled out into a landscape remade: a meter of new snow over everything, the cliff above them hung with feathers of powder, the air so clear that the peaks across the glacier seemed close enough to touch.

Astrid stood up stiffly, tested her weight on her left leg — Ines saw the wince, the half-second delay before the woman masked it, and filed it away without comment — and scanned the route ahead.

Then she went very still.

"Mabry," she said. "Look at the icefall."

Across the glacier, where the route bent toward the hidden valley, the seracs gleamed white and blue and untouched. But above them, hanging in the windless sky, two black specks moved in a slow orbit. Helicopters. Holding position at the edge of hearing, patient as vultures, marking the line of the only route to La Cuna.

"They waited out the storm too," Ines said.

"Yeah." Astrid shouldered her pack and settled the straps with hands that didn't quite tremble, and her voice came out level and cold and certain. "So we stop hiding and start racing. We move now, we move fast, and we get to the source before they land."
