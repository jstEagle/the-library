## 15. La Cuna

The glacier gave them the last mile the way a miser gives: grudgingly, in small change of crevasse and blue ice, demanding payment at every step.

Astrid went first. She had been going first for six days now, since the boat came apart against the shore below the icefall and they'd shouldered what remained — rope, tent, the sealed aluminum case Voss had made Ines swear to carry, Mabry's ruined shoulder wrapped in a sling of pack straps. Six days of climbing through country that no map admitted to. The limp in her left leg had gone from companion to passenger to something closer to crew, always there, always taking up space she couldn't spare. She'd been managing it with ibuprofen and lies. The lies were getting thinner.

Behind her, Ines moved like her mother must have moved — Astrid was sure of it now, sure in the way you become sure of things you have no right to know. Economical. Patient with the mountain. She placed her feet as if the ice were a document she was reading.

Mabry came last, breathing in wet rags, refusing help twice an hour on principle.

The valley opened without warning.

One moment they were cutting steps across a glacier's shoulder, heads down against wind that came off the ice field like a debt collector; the next, the shoulder fell away beneath them and there it was.

Astrid stopped so abruptly that Ines walked into her back.

"Madre de Dios," Mabry said behind them both, and then, because he was Mabry: "Well. He wasn't lying."

La Cuna. The Cradle.

It was a hanging valley, a bowl of stone cupped between two ridgelines that ran up into peaks still unnamed on any map Astrid had ever seen — and she had seen most of them, once, before the names stopped mattering to anyone who signed her paychecks. A glacier poured into it from the north and simply *stopped*, calving nothing, advancing nothing, held in place by some accident of shadow and temperature, as if the ice itself had agreed to keep a secret. Below the glacier's snout, a lake lay green-gray and utterly still, and out of the lake's far end, a river began — small, absurdly small, a thread of meltwater no wider than a kitchen doorway.

The Serrano. All of it. Every rapid they had survived, every gorge, every floodlit night, every kilometer of fear and rusted boat and burned dock — all of it began here, in water you could step across.

"Voss drew it true," Ines said quietly. She had the map out, the hand-drawn thing that had started everything, holding it up against the valley as if checking a forgery against the original. "Every contour. He never saw this. His partner did. And his partner's drawings came from—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. They all looked at the same place at the same time, because there was nowhere else to look.

At the base of the glacier, where the ice met the lake, stood a camp.

*Stood.* That was the only word. Three tents — canvas, expedition-grade, the kind they hadn't manufactured since the nineties — upright and intact, their colors bleached but their shapes perfect, preserved in air cold enough to stop time. A cache of supply crates sat beside them in a neat row. A radio mast leaned against a boulder, guy wires taut. A cook shelter, its tarp still rigged. Everything wearing eighteen years of silence like a second skin.

Nobody spoke. The wind did the talking, moving down off the ice field and across the lake, and for a moment Astrid could almost believe she heard voices in it — the dead expedition still at their chores, still waiting out weather that had never lifted.

"We should approach from the east," she heard herself say, and her guide's voice came out steady, which surprised her, because inside her chest something was coming apart. "Rockfall zone on the west flank. Stay off the lake ice near the snout. Single file."

Ines looked at her. "You can do that? Switch it on? Just — be a guide again, right now?"

Astrid met her eyes. "It's the only switch I've got left."

They descended.

* * *

The camp was worse up close than it had been from above, and better, too. Worse because the details accumulated: a boot by a tent door, laced, as if its owner had stepped out of it and meant to come back. A mug on a crate, frost inside. A pair of gloves tucked into a belt, fingers curled. Better because the cold had done what cold does — it had kept everything honest. There was no rot here, no scattering, no scavenging. Whatever had happened in this valley in 1998 had happened quickly, and then the valley had closed its hand around the evidence and held it.

The tents were empty. Astrid checked each one, and Ines watched her do it, and neither of them said what they were both thinking: *empty is not the same as nobody died here.*

It was Ines who found the survey tent.

It stood apart from the others, larger, ridge-poled, and when Astrid unzipped its stiffened flap the smell that came out was paper and metal and deep cold — the smell of an archive. Inside, crates were stacked to the canvas walls. Astrid pried one open with her knife. Field notebooks, dozens of them, their covers furred with frost. Another crate: film canisters, sample bags labeled in faded ink. A third, heavier, and when she lifted the lid her breath stopped.

Core samples. Cylinders of sediment and rock in sealed tubes, each tagged with coordinates and dates. August 1998. September 1998. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a bound report — typescript, maybe eighty pages, with a title page that read:

**HYDROGEOLOGICAL SURVEY: SERRANO HEADWATERS — PRELIMINARY FINDINGS. CONFIDENTIAL.**

Ines took it from her hands like a person taking a newborn.

She read. Astrid watched her read — watched the hydrologist surface through the exhausted daughter, watched her lips move over figures, watched her face change as the numbers assembled themselves into meaning.

"There's an aquifer under this valley," Ines said at last. Her voice was very flat, which Astrid had learned was how Ines sounded when she was closest to breaking. "A big one. Confined, pressurized, artesian. This report estimates recoverable volume in the — I can't even — the numbers are *absurd*. This isn't a mine site. This is a water field. This whole valley sits on top of enough fresh water to—" She stopped. Looked up. "Do you understand what this is worth?"

"More than gold," Mabry said from the tent mouth. He hadn't come inside. He was standing with his back to the light, watching the ridgeline. "Water's the next gold. Everybody downstream knows it. That's why the Cordillera boys want this valley surveyed, sealed, and forgotten until they're ready."

Astrid turned pages. The report was meticulous — a professional's work, annotated in two hands, one cramped and technical, one looser, more urgent, marginal notes pressing against the edges of the page like a voice rising. She recognized the second handwriting immediately. It matched the map. It matched the annotations Voss had made on the map in his final weeks, copying from memory the hand he'd loved.

The margins told a different story than the body of the report. The body said: *significant resource, extraction feasible, further study required.* The margins said: *they know. Tomás flagged the discrepancy in the drilling logs. R. says the consortium men asked about our radio schedule. We are leaving early.*

*R.* Rosa. Ines's mother.

"Ines." Astrid held the report out. "Read the last twenty pages. Read the appendices."

Ines read. The tent was very quiet except for the tick of cooling fabric and, distantly, the groan of the glacier settling.

"Appendix C," Ines said finally. "It's a log. Radio transmissions, dated October ninth through eleventh, 1998. Requests for evacuation. Requests for medevac — someone was hurt, a fall, then the entry says the injury was *not consistent with a fall*. Then—" Her finger stopped on the page. "Then a transmission logged as received but unanswered. From this camp. To anyone. Then nothing. The report just ends. No conclusion. No signature block. Someone pulled it mid-write."

"They killed them," Mabry said. Not a question. He'd known. Astrid realized he'd probably known for years, in the way men who run supplies for bad people know things — sideways, deniably, at a price they kept promising themselves they weren't paying. "Or had them killed. The ones who wouldn't sign off on burying the survey. The ones who might talk."

"The sole survivor finished the report," Astrid said slowly, assembling it the way she'd assemble a route, hold by hold. "Rosa. She got out — or was let out, or escaped — tried to expose it, and died on the river trying. Voss spent his life putting together what she left behind. And this—" she touched the case Ines carried, the aluminum case with the map folded inside it, "—this was the key that tells anyone who finds this camp that it's real. That it's not a story."

"It's proof," Ines said. "It's *forensic* proof. Chain of evidence, dating, the works. You can't fake permafrost."

Outside, the light changed.

Astrid noticed it before she understood it — a wrongness in the quality of the afternoon, a flicker at the edge of vision. She stepped out of the survey tent into the flat white glare and looked up, and the awe that had carried her down the moraine curdled in her chest like milk.

Two dots. High over the southern ridge. Moving.

"Helicopters," she said. "Get out. Get out now."

* * *

They scattered the camp's cover back over the crates — not well, there wasn't time — and moved for the boulder field at the valley's eastern wall, where the shadows were deep and the rock would break up any thermal signature. Astrid drove them hard, harder than altitude allowed, and paid for it in her leg, which had stopped aching and started burning, a hot wire running from hip to heel.

The helicopters came over the ridge at 3:40 in the afternoon. Two of them — white-bodied, no markings she could read at this distance, flying the way consortium aircraft flew: fast, flat, unhurried, the confidence of machines whose owners owned the sky they used.

They circled the valley twice. Wide, slow orbits. Looking.

Astrid pressed herself into a crack between boulders with Ines beside her and Mabry wedged into shadow above, and she watched the aircraft and did the arithmetic she hated doing, the lonely kind, the kind that had gotten her blacklisted twice because she kept doing it out loud.

Two helicopters. Minimum four seats each, plus fuel range that meant a staging point within two hundred kilometers — the survey camp on the lower plateau, the one Voss had warned about. If they'd spotted the party's tracks on the glacier — and tracks in snow this high lasted weeks — they knew exactly how many people were down here. Four. Or three, if they'd lost count in the icefall.

If they wanted the camp destroyed, they'd have brought incendiaries and been done with it. They hadn't. Which meant they wanted to see who else had found it first.

Which meant the map had leaked after all. Or the beacon. Or Voss himself, in a delirium, in a hospital town, before Astrid had ever taken the job — there was no way to know, and knowing wouldn't change the arithmetic.

"Astrid." Ines's whisper was barely breath. "The report. The samples. If they land—"

"I know."

"If they land, we can't let them—"

"I *know*."

Below them, the lead helicopter slowed. Dropped. Its shadow slid across the dead camp like a hand across a table, and it settled onto the gravel bar by the lake with a roar that rolled around the valley walls and came back doubled. Doors opened. Figures dropped to the ground — four, five, six, dark gear, rifles slung but present, moving with the loose competence of professionals toward the tents.

The second helicopter held overhead, orbiting wider now, its shadow sweeping the scree slopes. Sweeping the boulder field.

Sweeping toward them.

Astrid put her mouth against Ines's ear. "When it comes around again, we move. Ten meters at a time, between sweeps, into the gully. There's a route up the east wall — Voss marked it, a col at the head of the valley. We take the evidence and we climb."

"Climb *where*? We're in a bowl. They're in the sky."

"High ground buys time. Time is the only currency we've got left." She looked at Ines, and found Ines looking back, and for one stripped-bare moment neither of them pretended. They were four days' travel from anywhere, out of food, out of boat, out of luck, sitting on top of the truth of nine murders while the men who'd committed them dismounted forty meters below.

"Mabry," Astrid breathed upward. "You copy the plan?"

From the shadow above, a whisper: "Copy it. Don't love it." A pause. "The case stays with me on the climb. My shoulder's shot anyway — can't fight, can't run fast. But I can hold a strap."

"No," Ines started.

"It wasn't a request," Mabry said, and there was something in his voice Astrid hadn't heard before — not guilt, not irony, but resolve, plain and load-bearing. "I ran supplies for these people for six years. I've been waiting the whole river to give something back. Let me carry it."

The orbiting helicopter came around. Its shadow crawled toward the gully like a sundial marking the end of something.

Astrid counted the sweep. Five seconds of shadow. Fifteen of clear ground.

"Now," she whispered, and they moved — three ghosts in a dead valley, carrying the weight of the drowned years uphill, while below them the men in dark gear reached the survey tent and stopped, and one of them raised a hand, and pointed, not at the camp, not at the ice —

At the tracks. Their tracks. Leading straight up the eastern wall.

And the second helicopter banked, and began to climb.
