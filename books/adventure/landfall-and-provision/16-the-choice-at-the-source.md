## 16. The Choice at the Source

The valley opened beneath them like a held breath.

Astrid had stood in a lot of places that maps lied about — places drawn as blank space that turned out to be canyons, or plateaus, or nothing much at all. La Cuna was the opposite of all of them. The map had promised a basin, and the basin was there, ringed by walls of blue ice, but no cartographer's line could have prepared her for what lay inside: a floor of ancient glacier, wind-scoured to glass, and rising from it, half-submerged, the shapes of tents.

Not their tents. Not anyone's.

The 1998 camp sat in the ice like an insect in amber. Two dome shelters, collapsed but whole, their nylon gone brittle and gray-green with age. A sledge. A stack of fuel drums fused into the surface by twenty years of freeze and thaw. And scattered between them, small and dark against the white, things she didn't want to look at too closely.

"Stop," Ines said behind her, and Astrid realized she'd already stopped, three steps onto the glacier, her breath coming in ragged pulls through the thin air.

Mabry came up on her left, breathing hard, his bad shoulder cradled against his chest. He'd carried Voss's field notebook against his skin since the burial, wrapped in plastic, and now he took it out and looked at it as if checking whether it still counted for anything up here.

"It's real," he said. "God help us, it's actually real."

Astrid knelt. The ice under her palm was smooth as river stone, and through it, dimly, she could see color — a rectangle of faded orange, a crate lid, something that might have been a boot. The glacier moved, she knew. A few centimeters a year, maybe less in a sheltered basin like this. It had taken two decades to swallow this camp and another two decades to give it back, and now it was giving it back all at once, a warm summer's ablation stripping the surface down to the bones of what happened here.

"Ines." She kept her voice level. "We have maybe an hour before we're not alone."

Ines wasn't listening. She was walking toward the nearest tent, her crampons ringing on the ice, and when she reached it she went down on her knees beside a shape at its entrance — a body, or most of one, mummified by cold, dressed in expedition gear so old-fashioned it looked like a costume. She didn't touch it. She just knelt there, and Astrid saw her lips moving, and understood that Ines was reading the name tape on the jacket.

She couldn't hear it from where she stood. She didn't need to.

* * *

They worked fast after that, because Astrid made them work fast.

The documents were where Voss's map said they'd be — not in the tents but in a steel ammunition box sunk into the ice near the center of the camp, its location marked on the hand-drawn sheet with a small, patient X. Mabry chopped it free with the ice axe while Astrid stood watch at the mouth of the valley, scanning the sky. Ines pried open the lid with shaking hands.

Inside: survey reports in plastic sleeves. Core sample logs. Aerial photographs annotated in three different hands. And a ledger — an actual ledger, columns of dates and figures — that Ines opened, read one page of, and closed again as if it had burned her.

"The aquifer," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "It's all here. The flow rates, the reserves, the — " She stopped. Turned a page. "This is a kill order. Dated August 1998. Signed with initials."

"How many copies?" Astrid asked.

"This is the original. There might be duplicates in consortium files, but — " Ines looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "Nobody keeps evidence like this unless they plan to use it. They never used it. They buried it. This is the only proof my mother died for."

Astrid heard it then.

At first she thought it was the glacier settling — the deep groan of ice under tension that she'd been hearing since Redbank, the sound she'd learned to ignore. But this was different. Rhythmic. Thumping. Coming from over the eastern wall, growing louder.

Rotor wash.

"Down," she said. "Get it back in the box. Now."

They were still scrambling when the first helicopter crested the ridge — a black utility bird, unmarked, flying heavy. Then a second behind it. They didn't land in the valley; there was no room. They set down on a shelf of rock above the eastern rim, and men began rappelling down the ice wall on fixed lines, six of them, eight, ten, descending into La Cuna like spiders on silk.

Astrid pulled Ines behind the wrecked sledge and put Mabry next to her.

"Options," Mabry whispered. His face had gone the color of old tallow. "I count eleven. Automatic weapons. We've got your pistol, my rifle, and about nine rounds between them."

"I know."

"So we're negotiating."

"We're negotiating."

The mercenaries fanned out across the glacier with a discipline that told her everything about what they were — not local muscle, not hired porters with guns, but professionals. They didn't rush. They walked the perimeter first, sealed the valley mouth, and only then did one of them — taller than the rest, unhurried, his weapon slung rather than raised — come toward the sledge.

He stopped ten meters out. Close enough to talk. Far enough to be safe from desperation.

"Astrid Reyes," he said.

Her blood went cold. Not because he knew the name — she'd given it up somewhere around Serpent Bend, in her own head, as a cost she'd already paid — but because of how he said it. Not like a threat. Like a file he'd memorized.

"And you're the man who burns things," she said.

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. "Commander Sosa. Cordillera Extraction Group, security division. I'm going to make this simple, because I respect your time and you've clearly earned some professional courtesy." He gestured, casually, at the steel box between Ines's knees. "That material is corporate property. Stolen property, technically, though I understand none of you knew that when you started. You're guides. Pilots. A grieving daughter. Nobody here is a criminal."

"Except whoever signed that ledger," Ines said.

Sosa didn't even glance at her. "Mrs. Voss. My condolences on your father. He was a stubborn man, and stubborn men die badly in mountains. You've seen it." He let that sit. "Here is the offer. The documents go into the stove we brought for exactly this purpose. You walk out of this valley. All three of you. Helicopter ride to the river, boats waiting, no charges, no questions. Your blacklists stay whatever color they are. You go home."

"And if we don't?" Astrid asked.

Sosa spread his hands, taking in the valley — the ice, the tents, the shapes in the ice, the vast indifferent walls. "Then you're buried with it. That's not a threat, Ms. Reyes. That's just what this place does. Ask the last people who wouldn't leave." He nodded at the mummified figure by the tent. "Twenty-six years, and nobody's ever come looking. Nobody will come looking for you either. The difference is, this time we'll be sure."

* * *

Astrid had made a lot of decisions in bad places. Rope decisions, weather decisions, the arithmetic of who goes first and who carries what and who might not make it if the plan goes wrong. She'd learned to make them fast and live with them slow.

This one she could feel arriving like weather.

Because part of her — the part that had spent seven years eating rejection letters and border refusals, the part that had crossed into this country under a false name because no outfitter on two continents would hire her — that part heard Sosa's offer and wanted it with a hunger that shamed her. Walk away. Live. Let the past stay buried; it had stayed buried fine for twenty-six years. What was one more grave? What was her name worth, really, weighed against her pulse?

And then she looked at Ines.

Ines was kneeling behind the sledge with the ledger clutched to her chest, and she was looking at Astrid with an expression Astrid had seen once before — on a rope team, years ago, on a client whose life had just become her responsibility. Total transfer of trust. Not because Astrid had earned it in that moment, but because there was no one else, and the human animal will hand its weight to whoever's standing closest to the anchor.

*Don't,* Ines's eyes said. *Don't you dare take this from me too.*

"We need a minute," Astrid said.

"You have until my men finish setting the charge," Sosa said.

Astrid frowned. "What charge?"

He pointed, without looking, at the base of the eastern ice wall, where two of his men were working with something that gleamed. "Thermite. Standard practice for site remediation. When we leave, the burn goes through the camp, the box, and forty meters into the ice shelf. By next season there won't be a crevasse field here. There'll be a lake." He checked his watch. "You have four minutes. I'd spend them deciding, not talking."

* * *

They talked anyway. Fast, low, heads together behind the sledge while the smell of thermite primer drifted sharp and chemical across the ice.

"He's lying about letting us walk," Mabry said. "You know that. Witnesses are witnesses."

"Probably," Astrid said.

"Definitely. I ran supplies for these people, Astrid. I know how they close a site." His jaw worked. "But if we fight, we die here, and the box burns anyway, and it was all for nothing."

"Not necessarily nothing." Astrid's mind was running the terrain the way it ran rapids — reading the water ahead, counting the holes. "The box. How heavy?"

"Forty kilos, maybe fifty with everything in it."

"And the ice between here and the west wall?"

Mabry stared at her. "It's a glacier. It's all ice."

"There's a crevasse field along the western margin — I saw it when we came in. Old meltwater channels, bridged snow. Unstable." She was already pulling her rope off her shoulder. "Which means it's unstable for them too."

"Astrid." Ines's voice cracked like a whip. "Tell me you're not thinking about running for it."

"I'm thinking that a fifty-kilo box doesn't cross broken ice fast, and neither do we, and eleven armed men don't cross it at all if we break the bridges behind us." She met Ines's eyes. "I'm thinking we run west and we make them choose between chasing us and shooting us, and either way some of this gets out."

"And if it doesn't?"

Astrid didn't answer that. Because the honest answer was the one she couldn't say: *then I traded your mother's grave for your life, and I'll hate myself quietly for the rest of mine.*

She never got to make the choice cleanly. The mountain made it for her.

* * *

It started with a sound — the same groan she'd heard earlier, but wrong now, deeper, and coming from underneath them. Thermite, she thought wildly. No. Too far away. Then she understood: the helicopters. Two aircraft, landing and idling on a rock shelf above a glacier that hadn't borne that kind of weight in ten thousand years. Vibration travels through ice like a voice through a wire. Somewhere below them, a twenty-meter bridge of snow over a meltwater channel finally gave up the argument.

The glacier opened.

Not near the mercenaries. Near Ines.

She'd shifted her weight — just stood up, just turned toward Astrid — and the snow under her dropped away with a sound like a door closing. One instant she was kneeling; the next she was gone, down into a slot of blue darkness, and the sound she made wasn't a scream, it was worse — a short, punched-out grunt of impact, and then silence.

"Ines!"

Astrid was moving before the thought finished forming. Rope off her shoulder, anchor — the sledge, iron-shod, frozen solid into the ice — clove hitch, throw. She went to the lip of the crevasse on her belly, which was the only correct way, and looked down into blue light and depth.

Ines hung twelve meters down, snagged against the far wall, one arm twisted above her, the ledger still somehow pressed against her chest with the other. Blood on her forehead. Her eyes found Astrid's and they were clear, which meant conscious, which meant alive, which meant every second mattered differently now.

"My arm," Ines called up, teeth gritted. "I think it's broken. I can't climb."

"Hold the rope. Don't move. Mabry — haul team, now!"

Behind her, the world had gone loud. Shouting in Spanish. Sosa's voice cutting through it, sharp with a new anger, because his clean solution had just developed a complication: a woman dying in a hole changes the arithmetic for everyone. She heard him snap orders — and then, incredibly, heard boots on ice coming *toward* them, two of his men, weapons slung, because whatever else Sosa was, he hadn't planned on a death today, and dead hostages negotiated poorly.

Astrid stopped hearing any of it.

There is a state she'd been in maybe five times in her life, always at altitude, always when someone's life hung on her hands — the world narrowing to a corridor of pure task. Rope friction. Haul angle. Mabry's good shoulder against the load, his ruined one braced on the sledge runner. Ines's weight coming up meter by meter, the rope burning through her gloves, her arms screaming, and below her the blue light brightening as Ines rose toward her.

Halfway up, the second collapse came.

The crevasse mouth widened with a crack like a rifle shot, and the sledge — her anchor — lurched, slid thirty centimeters toward the widening gap, and stopped. Held. Barely. Snow hissed down past Astrid's head into the dark.

And across the glacier, the thermite caught.

She smelled it before she saw it — that fierce chemical blaze — and then she saw it: a bloom of white-orange fire at the base of the eastern wall, spreading with unnatural speed across the old camp, nylon flaring, fuel drums beginning to sing. Sosa's men had lit it early. Whether by order or panic, she'd never know. The fire moved toward the steel box like it had a grudge.

*The box.* Fifty meters away. The ledger's siblings, the core samples, the photographs, the signatures — all of it sitting in a steel coffin that was about to become an oven.

Ines's hand broke the lip of the crevasse.

Astrid grabbed her wrist — the good one — and hauled, and Mabry hauled, and together they dragged her out onto the ice, gasping, bleeding, alive. For one full second Astrid allowed herself nothing else. Then she looked up.

Fire on one side. Broken ice on the other. Eleven men regrouping between them. Ines on her knees in the snow, half-conscious, unable to walk unaided.

One person could maybe reach the box and back before the fire did. One person could not carry a wounded woman and a fifty-kilo box through a collapsing crevasse field pursued by mercenaries. The arithmetic was brutal and it was instantaneous and it was, for once, no arithmetic at all.

She got Ines's arm over her shoulders and stood up.

"Astrid—" Ines rasped, seeing where her eyes had gone. "The box—"

"Is steel," Astrid said. "Steel doesn't burn. People do."

"But the documents—"

"Are paper." She tightened her grip and started moving, west, toward the broken ice, away from the fire, away from the box, away from the promise she'd made a dying man on a hotel bed in another lifetime. "Paper remembers. So do we. Move."

Behind them, La Cuna burned — twenty-six years of ice and silence giving way to flame, the old tents going up like signal fires, the steel box glowing at its heart. Ahead of them, the crevasse field waited, blue and broken and, for the moment, unguarded.

Mabry fell in on Ines's other side, and Astrid saw him glance back once at the fire — a long, unreadable look — before he turned his face forward and ran.

* * *

They reached the first snow bridge as it began, behind them, to be contested — shouts, a single wild burst of gunfire that kicked up ice chips a meter from Mabry's heel, Sosa's voice roaring something about the margin, about the lake, about *let them go, the burn handles it.*

Let them go.

Astrid ran anyway, Ines limping against her side, the fire painting their shadows huge and flickering on the western wall, and she did not look back again — not at the flames, not at the box, not at the men who had offered her a bargain the mountain had refused on her behalf.

She would remember, later, exactly one thing about those minutes with perfect clarity: Ines's heartbeat against her ribs, fast and stubborn and alive, keeping time like a river.

Everything else — the smoke, the sirens of rotors lifting off, the glacier groaning shut behind them like a book — blurred into motion.

What she could not have known, running blind into the broken ice with nothing saved and someone else's daughter breathing against her shoulder, was that the fire had not taken everything.

Nothing ever took everything. Not if somebody was willing to steal.
