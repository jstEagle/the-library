## 17. Provision

Mabry had spent thirty years learning what a river sounded like when it lied, and the Serrano at Serpent Bend had been lying all morning — a low, conversational murmur over stones that meant nothing good downstream. Now, pinned in the narrows below La Cuna with smoke drifting down from the burning cache and mercenaries shouting on the ice shelf above, he heard it again. The river was telling him something. It always was. The trick, he'd learned too late in his life, was listening before you needed to.

He crouched behind the Provision's gunwale where the boat lay beached in the willow scrub, fifty meters below the valley mouth, and watched Astrid Reyes make the only choice she was ever going to make.

He'd known it since Redbank, if he was honest. Since the moment she'd gone into the water after Ines without hesitating, rope singing off her hip, and come up coughing with the girl's collar fisted in her hand. Some people did arithmetic their whole lives and arrived at the same answer every time. Astrid's answer was people. Not maps, not names cleared, not the redemption she claimed she didn't want. People.

So when the crevasse took Ines — when the ice shelf groaned like a dying animal and swallowed her to the shoulders, and the commander of Cordillera Extraction's hired guns stood there offering fire or freedom — Mabry hadn't even bothered watching Astrid's face. He'd watched her hands. They were already moving for the rope.

"Reyes!" the commander shouted, voice carrying flat across the ice. "Last time I ask. The documents burn, you walk. All of you. Or nobody walks."

Astrid didn't answer him. She answered Ines, who was screaming somewhere down inside the blue throat of the glacier, and the answer was her boots hitting the ice at a dead run.

The mercenary nearest the cache — a heavy man with a red windbreaker and an incendiary canister already cracked open — looked from Astrid to the documents spread on the tarp like a man deciding which sin to commit first. Then he threw the canister.

Flame went up off the old expedition papers with a sound Mabry felt in his teeth, a soft whump of sixty years of evidence becoming light and heat. The 1998 survey. The aquifer charts. Voss's partner's name written in a dead man's hand. All of it curling at the edges, going brown, going black.

And nobody was watching Mabry.

That was the thing about being the old man, the disgraced pilot, the hired boat. Thirty years of running supplies for men like the ones on that ice shelf had taught him exactly how invisible a person could make himself when he stopped mattering to the story. The consortium had bought his silence once with a ban and a blacklisted license, and they'd assumed the purchase held. Everyone always assumed the boat man wasn't listening.

But Mabry had listened. And three days ago, camped in the cold below the moraine while Voss died quietly in his sleeping bag, Mabry had done his own quiet arithmetic.

The core sample sat in its aluminum shipping tube where they'd cached it with the rest of the findings, wrapped in Ines's spare shirt. Forty centimeters of glacial ice drilled from the aquifer seam, dated, labeled in Efraim Voss's careful prospector's hand: *SEAM SAMPLE 7 — DO NOT THAW.* Beside it, the field notebook — not Voss's, but the one that mattered more. Ines's mother's notebook, recovered from the 1998 camp, water-stained and legible, every coordinate and contamination reading and date of every meeting between the expedition leaders and the consortium's surveyors recorded in a hydrologist's precise script. The document that named names. The document that would hang them.

The fire was eating the rest. The tarp, the duplicate charts, the photographs of the preserved camp, the copies of everything — all of it meant to be found, meant to be undeniable, meant to be too much to burn. The commander had understood that. That was why he'd brought fire instead of patience.

Mabry understood something else. You didn't beat men like this by giving them everything to destroy. You beat them by keeping one true thing they didn't know existed.

He moved low along the willow scrub, knees cracking, breath sawing, and reached the cache site's edge where the heat came off the flames in waves that smelled like history ending. The two nearest mercenaries were turned toward the crevasse, toward Astrid hauling on the rope hand over hand, toward the shouting. The red-windbreaker man was feeding the fire another armful of charts, thorough, professional, bored.

Mabry's hand closed on the aluminum tube. Cold through his glove. He pulled it against his chest, rolled away from the light of the fire into the shadow of a serac, and knelt there a moment with his heart slamming, feeling like a twenty-five-year-old smuggler again and hating how much of him liked it.

Then the notebook. It lay at the tarp's edge where the wind had pushed it, half under a rock, untouched by flame so far. He snatched it, shoved it inside his jacket against his ribs, and was moving before the guilt could catch up — because there was guilt, God help him, there was always guilt. Stealing from Astrid's promise. Taking the choice out of her hands. She'd chosen Ines over the truth, chosen clean over redeemed, and here he was muddying it, keeping a copy she'd have burned herself if she'd known.

But he'd watched her limp up that last stretch of scree, hiding the leg, lying with her body the way he'd lied with his licenses for thirty years. He'd watched Ines read coordinates over her father's grave because a eulogy would have broken her open. He'd watched Voss die whispering about absolution arriving late.

None of them got to be clean. That was the whole lesson of the river. But somebody had to carry the true thing out, and Astrid had already paid enough. Let this one be his sin. Let it sit on his ledger next to all the supplies he'd run for the consortium, next to the men who'd drowned because his cargo helped buy the guns that killed them. One theft in the other direction. One act of faith with cold hands.

*Faith,* his father would have said, *is just stubbornness with better lighting.*

Mabry stuffed the tube into a dry bag, lashed it under the Provision's forward thwart beneath the bail bucket and the tangled spare line, and covered it with the moldy foam pad nobody ever moved. Then he went back for the others, because faith that doesn't row is just opinion.

* * *

Astrid came down the ice slope with Ines half-conscious across her shoulders, and Mabry saw the cost of it immediately — the way her left leg dragged on the final steps, the gray in her face, the blood frozen in a dark stripe down her forearm where the ice had opened her. She laid Ines in the boat's bottom among the gear and looked up at him with eyes that had nothing left in them but function.

"They'll follow," she said. "Downriver. They know we can't climb out any other way."

"Then we don't give them a slow target." He was already pushing the Provision's bow into the current. "Get her under the tarp. Out of the wind."

Ines stirred, mumbled something — a coordinate, Mabry thought, or maybe her mother's name — and went under again. Astrid wrapped her in both sleeping bags and wedged herself beside her, one hand pressed flat against the girl's sternum as if she could hold her heart beating by main force.

"The evidence," Astrid said. Her voice was ash. "It's gone. All of it."

Mabry kept his eyes on the water. "Yeah," he said. "Gone."

It wasn't even a lie, not quite. What was in his dry bag wasn't evidence yet. Evidence needed authentication, chain of custody, a hydrologist willing to stake her name on it. Right now it was a tube and a notebook and an old man's secret, and secrets sank boats. He'd decide later whether he'd done the right thing. That was the arrangement he'd made with his conscience decades ago: act first, audit never.

The gorge took them.

La Cuna's outflow ran narrow and fast between walls of blue ice, and the smoke from the burning cache followed them down like a flag of surrender, streaming through the notch in the valley wall, visible for miles — visible, Mabry knew, from the helicopters circling high above. Let them look. By the time anyone descended to the river level, the Provision would be ten kilometers gone, riding flood-stage water toward the canyon country where the consortium's own dams and survey equipment made witnesses dangerous to *them*.

He ran the first rapid blind, the way he'd run a hundred rivers with cargo worth more than his life, reading the water by sound and the boil lines on the surface. The boat slammed a wave train, shipped half a ton of freezing water, and Astrid bailed without being asked, one-handed, the other still flat on Ines's chest.

Behind them, faint now, a helicopter dropped toward the valley floor. Mabry didn't look back. Looking back was how you hit rocks.

* * *

They made the lower canyon by dusk, running water the color of hammered tin, and camped in a slot of gravel where the cliffs cut the wind. Ines woke properly around midnight, shivering hard, lucid, asking what happened in a voice scraped raw. Astrid told her the truth — most of it. The trade offered, the fire, the collapse, the escape. She told it flat, without self-pity, and ended with: "I chose you. I'd choose you again. I'm sorry about your mother's work."

Ines stared into the coals for a long time. Then she said, "She'd have done the same. For me. That's why they killed her — she wouldn't stop, even after I was born. Even when it was dangerous." A pause. "Burning it doesn't unkill her."

"No," Astrid said. "It doesn't."

Mabry sat apart, mending a torn oar shaft with cord and a splint of willow, and said nothing, and felt the dry bag's weight against the thwart like a second passenger. Tomorrow, maybe. Or the day after. When the girl was stronger. When the river was kinder. When he'd figured out how to say *I stole your inheritance back from the fire* without it sounding like what it was — an old smuggler's superstition that some things refuse to burn.

Near dawn, Astrid slept at last, and Ines sat awake watching the water, and Mabry heard the helicopter again — distant, upstream, searching the wrong valley. He allowed himself one long breath.

Then he saw the patrol boat.

It came around the bend below them with its floodlights off, running dark, moving fast against the current — which meant it wasn't patrolling. It was waiting. Someone had guessed where the river would deliver them, and had set the table accordingly.

Mabry put his hand on the dry bag and began, very quietly, to wake Astrid.
