## 18. Descent

The river had doubled overnight.

Astrid saw it in the way the water climbed the banks, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees like matchsticks. The Serrano at flood stage wasn't a river anymore. It was an argument the mountains were having with the sea, and they were running straight down the middle of it.

"Two meters above normal," Mabry said, reading the water the way other men read faces. "Maybe three. The Provision will handle it." He patted the wheel housing with a hand that still trembled from the portage. "She's handled worse."

"She hasn't," Astrid said.

"No," Mabry agreed. "But I have."

Behind them, maybe forty minutes back, the mercenaries' boat was doing what their boat did — only newer, faster, and full of men who didn't have to worry about hull integrity or fuel gauges or the woman bleeding through her boot where the scree had opened her ankle on the descent. Astrid had wrapped it that morning with tape and a strip of Ines's shirt, and she could feel every step of it now as a hot wire pulled tight each time the Provision slammed off a wave.

Ines sat forward with Voss's map spread across her knees, weighting the corners with rocks from the bilge. She'd been quiet since La Cuna. Quiet since the fire. Quiet since Astrid had hauled her out of the crevasse with two fingers hooked into her collar while the evidence of nine murders went up in black smoke behind them. There was a particular silence a person carried after something like that, and Astrid knew its weight because she'd been carrying one herself for eight years.

"The canyon starts at the next bend," Ines said. "Cañón de los Testigos. My mother marked it on her field notes. *Do not run at high water.*"

"Your mother also drew a map that got people killed trying to keep it secret," Mabry said. "No offense to the dead."

"Some taken," Ines said, but there was no edge in it. Just exhaustion.

Astrid looked over her shoulder. Far downstream, around a gray shoulder of rock, a white wake stitched itself across the brown water. They weren't even hiding anymore.

"They'll follow us in," she said.

Mabry shook his head slowly. "They can't. That's the thing about Los Testigos — the consortium spent eleven years and probably half a billion keeping that canyon exactly the way it is. Untouched. Unsurveyed by anyone but them. There's a dam survey pinned to those walls, Astrid. Water rights, mineral claims, the whole future of the Cordillera Extraction Group sitting in a canyon nobody's allowed to touch. If they chase us in and we wreck — if *anything* wrecks in there — there are investigators, environmental assessors, satellite passes. A wreck means outsiders. Outsiders mean eyes."

"So they'll wait at the far end," Astrid said.

"They'll wait at the far end," Mabry confirmed. "Which means we come out of that canyon directly into their arms, unless—" he squinted at the water ahead, jaw working— "unless we come out moving so fast they can't get set. Or unless we don't come out at all."

"That's not a plan," Ines said.

"It's a river," Mabry said. "Plans are for lakes."

* * *

The canyon swallowed them at 10:40 in the morning.

One moment they were in open floodplain, the Serrano spread wide and shallow across a braided gravel bed; the next, the walls closed to thirty meters and rose six hundred, sheer and wet and dripping with meltwater that fell in silver threads from heights the eye couldn't comfortably hold. The river compressed between them and turned from brown to green-gray, deep and cold and suddenly, horribly fast.

Astrid stood at the bow with a rope coiled at her feet, because that was the only place she knew how to be. The Provision bucked under her like a living thing. Spray came over the gunwale in sheets. Somewhere behind her, Mabry was making sounds — half words, half prayer, all engine — feeding throttle into waves that stood up taller than the boat was wide.

The first rapid hit them before Astrid had finished naming it to herself.

It wasn't a rapid so much as a place where the entire river dropped three meters through a gap barely wider than the boat, and they went through it sideways, and for four full seconds Astrid was airborne inside the hull, her shoulder striking the bench, the rope tearing out of her hands, Ines screaming something that might have been her name or might have been her mother's. Then they were through, wallowing, shipping water, and Mabry was laughing — actually laughing, a wild cracked sound — bailing with his hat.

"Hull's holding!" he shouted. "Mostly!"

"Don't tell me mostly!"

"You don't want to know! Look alive — eddy fence ahead, left side!"

Astrid looked left and understood why the consortium needed this canyon intact. No road crew on earth would build here. No survey team would survive a season. The walls leaned. The water moved through them like something with intent. And threaded along both banks, half-submerged in the flood, were the stumps of an old forest drowned when some ancient slide dammed the river for a decade — dead snags rising out of the current like the masts of a fleet that had gone down standing up.

An eddy fence was bad anywhere. An eddy fence made of drowned trees at triple volume was a death sentence with good posture.

"Mabry—"

"I see it." His voice had gone flat and professional, which frightened her more than the laughing had. "I need you on the line. If we broach, you throw for the right bank snag, not the left. Right bank. Say it back."

"Right bank."

"Ines — down in the bilge, hands on the ring bolts, and whatever happens, whatever you see, you do not let go."

Ines went down without argument. Astrid caught one glimpse of her face — white-lipped, eyes fixed on nothing, hands finding the steel rings and locking — and then the river took the bow and swung it toward the snags and everything became noise.

They didn't broach. What happened instead was almost worse: a wave took them broadside into the eddy line itself, that invisible seam where the current split, and the Provision rolled up onto her chine and hung there — hung, weightless, the whole world tilted ninety degrees, Astrid looking straight down at green water and a drowned tree sliding past six inches below her boots — and then Mabry did something with the throttle and the trim and forty years of banned, disgraced, unforgivable skill, and the boat slapped back down and tore free.

For a long moment nobody said anything. The canyon roared around them. Water ran out of the bilge plug holes in twin streams.

"My mother ran this in a kayak," Ines said finally, from the bottom of the boat. Her voice was strange. Almost wondering. "Her notes said she portaged."

"Maybe she portaged at lower water," Astrid said.

"Maybe she lied," Ines said, "so no one else would try it."

Ahead, the canyon bent, and the light changed, and Astrid felt rather than saw the last drop coming — a long, rolling, mile-long staircase of water that no one had ever named because no one who mapped it had lived to file the report. Mabry saw it too. She watched him take his hands off the wheel for one second — one second only — and crack his knuckles like a man stepping up to a table where he owed everything.

"Hold on to something that loves you," he said.

Then he drove the Provision down the throat of the Serrano at flood stage, and the world became white.

* * *

They came out the far end at 12:15, according to Astrid's watch, though time had stopped meaning much somewhere around the third drop.

The canyon released them the way a fist releases a bird — all at once, into blinding open light, the river fanning wide again across a delta plain already smelling of salt and silt. The Provision limped. One bilge pump had died. The transom leaked in a way that Mabry pronounced "structural" and then refused to discuss further. But they were through, and floating, and alive, and the white wake of the mercenary boat was nowhere to be seen.

Because they couldn't come in after us, Astrid thought. Witnesses are dangerous there. She looked back once at the canyon mouth, at the dark slot in the earth where the consortium's entire future sat waiting for a dam that would never now be built quite the same way, and she understood that they hadn't outrun the mercenaries. They'd simply passed through the one place on the river where killing them would cost more than letting them go.

It didn't feel like victory. It felt like being handed back.

Mabry cut the engine to a low burble as the channel shallowed, and the three of them sat in the settling spray, none of them moving, listening to the water and the gulls and the distant, impossible normality of the coast.

"We need a radio," Ines said at last. Her voice was hoarse from river water. "We need to call someone. The prosecutor Voss named, the journalist — anyone. Before the consortium writes the ending for us."

"There's a fuel dock at Puerto Hambre," Mabry said. "Fuel docks have radios. Fuel dock operators have ears, but at this point—" he gestured at the ruined boat, the burned map's ashes still ghosting the deck, the three of them soaked, starved, and hunted— "secrecy is a luxury item."

They ran the last twenty kilometers in silence.

* * *

Puerto Hambre wasn't a town so much as an apology for one: a fuel dock, a cannery shed, a bar built from three shipwrecks, and a scatter of houses leaning into the wind. The delta spread out beyond it in a thousand braided channels, brown water meeting gray sea, the whole landscape flat and stunned after the vertical violence of the interior.

Mabry fueled. Astrid found the dock operator — a woman with forearms like dock pilings and the unimpressed gaze of someone who had seen every kind of fugitive the river produced — and traded cash for ten minutes on the radio and no memory of their faces.

The call took less than five minutes. Ines did the talking, crisp and precise, reciting coordinates, dates, names, the accession numbers of the core samples sewn into Mabry's jacket lining, the field notebook wrapped in plastic against her skin. When she finished, the voice on the other end was silent for a long moment.

"Say the aquifer number again," the voice said.

Ines said it again.

"And you're telling me this came out of the headwaters. Out of the closed zone."

"Out of La Cuna," Ines said. "Out of the ice. I'm a hydrologist. I've authenticated the data myself. And in eight hours, the people who own that valley are going to tell you it never existed."

The voice said it would call back within the day. Then the line went dead, and the three of them stood in the fuel shack smelling of diesel and glacier melt, and Astrid felt the strangest sensation — the feeling of having done everything right and won nothing. Voss was dead under a cairn on a moraine. The evidence had burned. Whatever happened next belonged to lawyers and satellites and strangers with microphones. The river part was over. The river part, the only part she had ever been any good at, was finished.

The radio crackled back before the hour was out. Not their contact.

It was the cannery's shared band, the one every boat in the delta kept tuned for weather. The dock operator turned it up without being asked, because everyone in the shack wanted to hear it.

"—repeat, all vessels advised that the upper Serrano drainage remains closed following this morning's announcement by the Cordillera Extraction Group. Company officials confirm a catastrophic survey accident in the headwaters region, citing a glacial collapse and the loss of one research facility. A company spokesperson stated that all personnel were safely evacuated and that independent verification is, quote, 'obviously impossible given terrain conditions.' Rescue authorities have declined to comment. In related news—"

The dock operator clicked it off.

"A survey accident," Ines said quietly. "Nine people murdered in 1998, my mother among them, and today they call it a glacial collapse."

"They're fast," Mabry said. He was looking at Astrid. "Faster than our contact. Which means right now, officially, there's nothing up there but ice. And officially, the three of you are the only witnesses that ice ever held anything at all."

Astrid looked out the shack window at the delta channels, at the fishing boats going about their evening, at the ordinary world resuming around them as if the last three weeks had happened on another planet. Survival, she thought. This is what it looks like. You get to the end of the river and you're still breathing and it counts for nothing yet.

The dock operator's radio phone buzzed. She answered, listened, and held it out to Ines without expression.

"It's your journalist," she said. "And she says — her words — 'Tell the hydrologist she has until morning, and tell whoever's chasing you that they just ran the wrong canyon.'"

Ines took the phone. Astrid watched her straighten, watched the hydrologist come up through the grief like spine through water, and outside, at the fuel dock, a car with government plates rolled slowly past the cannery shed and stopped.

Its hazard lights came on.

Someone inside was watching the fuel shack, and they weren't consortium.

"Get ready," Astrid said softly. "Whatever this is — it starts now."
