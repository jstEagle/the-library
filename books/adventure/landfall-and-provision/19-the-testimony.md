## 19. The Testimony

The capital smelled of diesel and jacaranda, and Ines hated how easily her body remembered it. Nine years she'd lived in field stations and gauging camps, in places where the only clocks were river stages and snowmelt pulses. Now she stood on a sidewalk outside the Palacio de Justicia watching taxis slide through heat haze, holding a drybag against her chest like a child, and the city's noise pressed on her the way water pressure presses at depth.

They had come down from the delta on a fuel barge, three people and a dead man's map folded into Astrid's jacket pocket. The news had broken while they were still on the water — *survey accident in the Serrano headwaters, two consortium geologists missing* — and Mabry had listened to the radio broadcast with his one good shoulder against the wheelhouse door and said nothing for an hour. That silence had frightened her more than the helicopters ever had.

Now, in the capital, he finally spoke.

"Not here," he said, when Astrid suggested going straight to the prosecutor's office. "You walk into that building with a story, you're a crank. You walk in with a journalist already holding your documents, you're a source." He nodded across the street, toward a café with a faded awning and a man at a corner table who'd been nursing the same espresso since they arrived. "And I know somebody."

"You know somebody," Astrid repeated flatly.

"I ran supplies for these people for six years, Reyes. I know everybody. Most of them would shoot me. This one owes me." Mabry's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Her name's Camila Duarte. She wrote the piece about the 2016 mine collapse that got her exiled to the culture desk. She's been waiting nine years for someone to hand her the Cordillera Extraction Group's throat."

Ines looked at the drybag in her arms. Inside it, wrapped in a fleece jacket and a garbage bag and every layer of protection Mabry could improvise aboard a burning boat: a core sample sealed in wax and foil, and a field notebook with a water-stained cover and handwriting she recognized before she was ready to.

She hadn't opened it yet. She couldn't. Not yet.

* * *

Camila Duarte turned out to be a small woman with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair and the unnerving habit of not blinking when something interested her. She listened to Mabry's account without interrupting — the ambush at Serpent Bend, the fire spreading through the ice cave, the crevasse, Astrid going back for Ines while the evidence burned — and then she looked at the drybag and said, quietly, "Open it."

The core sample came out first: a cylinder of ice and sediment, maybe forty centimeters long, labeled in grease pencil along its length. Coordinates. Date. Depth. And a notation Ines read twice because her hands were shaking too badly to trust her eyes the first time:

*AQ-1 CONFIRMED. CONFINED AQUIFER, ARTESIAN HEAD PRESSURE. DO NOT FILE.*

"Do you understand what this is?" Ines heard herself say, and her voice came out in her professional register, the flat lecture-hall tone she used for students, which was the only way she could keep it steady. "This isn't ore. This is water. A confined aquifer under the whole valley floor, artesian pressure, probably feeding the Serrano's base flow through subglacial channels. They didn't murder eleven people over minerals. Minerals get mined out. An aquifer like this—" she stopped, made herself say it plainly— "an aquifer like this is worth more than the mine would ever be. Control the water, you control every town on three hundred kilometers of river. Forever."

Camila had gone very still behind her glasses. "The decree," she said. "The 1998 closure. Everyone assumed it was environmental."

"It was hydrological," Ines said. "It was always hydrological. My mother found the water, and they killed her for finding it, and then they closed the valley so no one else ever would."

She said *my mother* out loud for the first time in the capital, in front of strangers, and the sentence sat in the air between them like something that had been waiting nineteen years to be said.

Then she took out the notebook.

* * *

She worked through it the way she'd work any field record — systematically, coldly, because that was the discipline her mother had taught her before everything else, before the silences and the leaving: *data first, grief later, data will still be true when grief has finished with you.*

Elena Voss's handwriting. Her mother's handwriting, slanted left-handed, the same hand that had written grocery lists and birthday cards Ines still kept in a shoebox she'd never told anyone about. Page after page of survey notes: piezometer readings, tracer tests, discharge measurements at the glacial outlet streams. And threaded through the science, dated entries in a smaller, tighter script — the entries of a woman who had begun to understand what she was looking at and what it would cost.

*March 14. R. asked me today why we're mapping the aquifer boundary if the contract says mineral survey. Told him to stop asking questions he doesn't want answers to. He laughed. God, he laughed.*

*April 2. Two men came up from the company camp. Not geologists. Efraim says don't sign anything. Efraim says a lot of things.*

*May 9. If anything happens to this expedition, the notebook goes downstream. I've sewn it into the lining of my pack. Whoever finds it: the numbers are real. Check them. Someone has to check them.*

Ines set the notebook down on Camila's table, in the back room of the newspaper office where they'd moved once Camila saw the first page, and she pressed both palms flat against her thighs and breathed until the room stopped tilting.

"Whoever finds it," she said. "That's us. Nineteen years later. That's us."

Astrid put a hand briefly on her shoulder — two seconds, no more; Astrid Reyes rationed touch the way she rationed rope on a bad pitch — and said, "Check them, then. You're the one who can."

So Ines checked them.

For six days, in a borrowed office with a secondhand laptop and Camila's archivist digging through twenty years of land registries and shell-company filings, Ines Voss did the work she had trained her whole life to do and never once believed would matter. She reconstructed the aquifer's boundaries from her mother's piezometer data and cross-referenced them against modern satellite gravimetry. She matched the tracer-test chemistry against published baseline studies of the Serrano's tributaries. She built the model her mother had died with unfinished, and when it converged — when every line of Elena Voss's nineteen-year-old numbers snapped into agreement with data gathered by instruments that hadn't existed in 1998 — she printed the correlation charts and laid them in front of Camila Duarte and said, "It's real. All of it. It was always real."

Camila looked at the charts for a long time. Then she looked up.

"There's a prosecutor," she said. "Deputy prosecutor, anti-corruption unit, named Herrera. He's been circling Cordillera Extraction for three years with nothing but rumor. He needs this authenticated by someone with standing — not a journalist, not a fugitive guide. A credentialed hydrologist willing to sign her name to the analysis under oath." She paused. "You understand what signing means. Once your name is on this, you're not a witness anymore. You're a party. They will come after you the way they came after your mother."

Ines thought about the shoebox of birthday cards. About nine years of not answering the phone. About a dying man on a moraine above a river, buried under stones with nothing but coordinates for a eulogy.

"My mother signed her name to her findings," she said. "It killed her. But the numbers survived anyway, because she wrote them down and trusted the river to carry them." She picked up the pen. "I'll sign."

* * *

The meeting with Deputy Prosecutor Herrera happened in a basement conference room with no windows, which Ines suspected was deliberate — no windows meant no angles for a rifle, or perhaps just no light for anyone to see what justice actually looked like while it happened. Herrera was a heavy, careful man who read everything twice and asked questions like a man laying stones across a creek: one at a time, testing each before weight went onto it.

He took them through the chain of custody with forensic patience. Where the sample was taken. Who held it. How it traveled. Mabry answered in his laconic river-pilot cadence, admitting freely and without drama that he had taken the materials from a burning site without authorization. "Steal's a strong word," he said. "Salvage. From a crime scene the owners were busy setting on fire."

Herrera almost smiled. Then he turned to Astrid, and Ines watched the guide go carefully blank in the way she went blank whenever anyone approached the subject of her past.

"And you, Ms. Reyes. Your involvement began as a medical escort?"

"A job," Astrid said. "One job."

"Your file indicates you've been barred from guiding in two countries following a 2016 incident on the Río Blanco. A client fatality."

"I know what my file indicates."

Herrera studied her for a moment longer than was comfortable, then closed that folder and opened another, and Ines saw — because she was sitting close enough, because she'd spent weeks learning to read Astrid's silences — the exact moment the deputy prosecutor's expression changed. Something flickered there. Recognition, maybe. Or the particular satisfaction of a man who has just found a stone that fits.

"Ms. Reyes," he said slowly, "in the course of preparing this case, our investigators obtained the complete Cordillera Extraction internal archive. Including incident reports filed by their contracted security division." He slid a single photocopied page across the table. "You should look at this."

Astrid didn't move. "What is it?"

"The 2016 Río Blanco incident report. As filed by Cordillera's security contractor, whose personnel were operating in the area that season under a mineral exploration permit." Herrera tapped the page once. "Read it."

Ines watched Astrid read. Watched her lips part, watched the color leave her face and then return unevenly, watched her hand come down flat on the paper as if to hold it still against some interior shaking. For a long time she said nothing at all, and the windowless room hummed with its fluorescent light, and Ines understood that whatever was on that page had been carried for eight years the way Ines had carried a shoebox of birthday cards — secretly, unbearably, alone.

"The rockfall wasn't natural," Astrid said finally, and her voice was stripped down to bare wire. "I always knew. I *knew*. The scarp was fresh-cut. I said so at the inquiry and they called it trauma-induced speculation. They blacklisted me for saying it." She looked up at Herrera. "Their own security team logged it. They cut the slope to seal a side channel — a *water* channel — and my client was underneath when it let go. And they filed it, and buried it, and let me carry it."

"They filed it because the site supervisor was sloppy," Herrera said. "Bureaucracies are careless even when they're murderous. It has sat in their archive for eight years, unremarked, until our investigators pulled every document with the word 'Serrano' within ten pages of it." He leaned forward. "Ms. Reyes, this report doesn't merely corroborate the pattern. It exonerates you. Completely. The inquiry's conclusion was based on testimony supplied by the same contractor. You weren't wrong. You were silenced."

Astrid stood up. Sat back down. Stood again, and this time walked to the wall and put her forehead against it, and Ines got up and went to her — not touching, just standing near, the way you stand near someone on a narrow ledge — and heard her say, so quietly it was barely language:

"I built my whole life around being guilty. Every choice. Every border. Eight years of believing I'd killed him." A breath. "And the proof was in a filing cabinet the entire time."

* * *

The story broke on a Thursday.

Camila Duarte's byline ran above the fold: *THE WATER THEY KILLED FOR.* Beneath it, the correlation charts Ines had signed. The photograph of the core sample. Elena Voss's notebook pages, facsimile-reproduced, her left-slanted handwriting suddenly everywhere — on screens, on street corners, in the mouths of anchors who nineteen years ago wouldn't have printed her name.

By Friday there were warrants. By Saturday the consortium's chief executive was detained at the airport trying to board a plane with a diplomatic passport, and the footage played on loop in every bar in the capital, and Mabry watched it from a stool with a beer he wasn't drinking and said, "Huh," in the tone of a man watching a river drop away beneath a boat that had somehow held together.

Ines gave testimony for eleven hours over three days. She explained artesian pressure to lawyers. She explained tracer chemistry to judges. She stood in a hearing room and read her mother's May 9th entry aloud — *someone has to check them* — and watched a stenographer type it into a permanent record, into the official memory of a country that had let Elena Voss become a footnote, and when she finished reading, the hearing room was silent, and the presiding judge said, "Thank you, Dr. Voss," and Ines walked out into the corridor and cried for four minutes against a wall, exactly four minutes, and then washed her face in the bathroom and went back in.

Astrid testified last. Ines sat in the gallery and watched her do it the way she did everything — spare, exact, no ornamentation. When the counsel for the victims' families asked her whether she forgave herself, now that the record was corrected, Astrid was quiet for a long moment.

"I don't think exoneration works like forgiveness," she said. "I think it works like weather. It changes what's possible. What you do with the possible is still yours."

* * *

Afterward — after the hearings adjourned and the headlines moved on to the next scandal and the capital resumed its diesel-and-jacaranda indifference — the three of them ended up on the roof of Mabry's cousin's building, eating takeout out of paper cartons while the city glittered below them like a circuit board.

"So," Mabry said. "Reyes is un-blacklisted. Voss is Dr. Voss with a chair offer from the university. And I'm a material witness with legal fees and a boat with a bullet hole in the hull." He raised his beer. "To sideways justice."

"To my mother," Ines said, and they drank to that instead.

Later, when Mabry had gone downstairs to argue with his cousin about rent, Ines found Astrid standing at the roof's edge, looking north, toward the mountains she could not see but clearly felt — Ines could tell she felt them; it was in her stance, the slight forward lean, like a dog scenting wind.

"They're going to reopen the inquiry formally," Astrid said. "Clear the record. Offer restitution." She was quiet a moment. "Everyone keeps asking what I'll do now. Like the answer should be obvious. Reputation restored, licenses reinstated, big outfitters knocking." She shook her head slowly. "I spent eight years thinking belonging was something you lost when they took your name off the list. Turns out I don't want the list back. I want—" she gestured vaguely northward— "the river."

Ines stood beside her. Below, the traffic flowed, patient and endless, a river of lights running through concrete.

"He's still up there," she said. "Under the cairn. With nothing but coordinates for company." She hesitated, then said the thing she'd been carrying since the hearing, since before the hearing, since the moraine. "We should go back. Not for the hearings. For him. Put a proper marker. Make it a waypoint people can find."

Astrid turned and looked at her, and in the city glow Ines saw something in her face she'd never seen there before — not the blankness, not the rationed care, but something open and unguarded and almost young.

"When?" Astrid said.

Ines smiled, tired and real. "Spring melt. When the water's high and the fish are dumb and Mabry can't think of an excuse." She bumped her shoulder against Astrid's. "Besides — you owe me a season of guiding. And I hear you're finally licensed."

Below them the city went on glittering, indifferent and forgiven, and somewhere far north under the ice, the water they had killed for ran clean and free toward the sea, carrying everything, forgetting nothing.
