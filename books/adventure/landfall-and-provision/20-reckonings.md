## 20. Reckonings

The hearings lasted eleven days, and Astrid attended all of them in a blazer she'd borrowed from a public defender's wife, feeling like a client in her own life.

The chamber in the capital was smaller than television made it look — a horseshoe of desks, a microphone that squealed whenever anyone leaned too close, a stenographer whose fingers never seemed to stop moving. Astrid sat at a folding table with Ines on her left and a lawyer named Quintanilla on her right, and she answered questions for six hours a day while the country learned, in installments, what had happened above the treeline of the Serrano.

The core sample did most of the talking. Mabry had turned it over to the prosecutor's office in a dry bag, wrapped in his spare shirt, and when the technicians cracked it open on the fourth day and read out the aquifer's estimated volume, the chamber went quiet in a way Astrid had only ever heard in high country — the silence of people doing arithmetic they didn't like. Forty years of water. Enough to make the consortium's survey permits a theft, and the 1998 expedition's disappearance a line item.

The murders took longer to land. Documents are one thing; a corroded radio with a bullet hole through its casing is another. Ines handled that part. She stood at the witness table in a blouse she'd clearly bought for the occasion, her hair pulled back, and she read her mother's field notebook into the record in a voice that never wavered until the last page, where it wavered exactly once. Astrid watched the stenographer's hands slow down, as if even the machine wanted to give the words room.

*Lucía Voss de Arrieta, hydrologist, 1971–1998. Cause of death: exposure, per original finding. Cause of death, per revised finding: homicide.*

Nine years of silence between a father and a daughter, and it took a courtroom to say the mother's name out loud. But it got said. It got said into a microphone and transcribed and printed, and by the second week it was on the front pages under headlines Astrid couldn't read without looking away: *THE RIVER KEEPS ITS LEDGER*. *WHAT THE CONSORTIUM BURIED*. *THE WOMAN WHO DREW THE MAP*.

Her own name surfaced too, though the papers got it wrong at first — *Astrid Reyes, the guide who survived*, as if survival were the salient fact. Then the 2016 incident report surfaced with everything else, pulled from the consortium's internal files because it turned out the company had quietly commissioned a second investigation after her blacklist and buried the findings when they came back the wrong way.

She heard it read aloud on the ninth day. *Guide error: not supported. Client conduct: contributory. Weather event: unforecastable. Recommendation: no action against guide.* Four sentences, drafted eight years ago by an investigator whose name Astrid never learned, filed in a cabinet in a building in a city she'd never been to, and left there because the truth was inconvenient to men who needed a scapegoat more than they needed accuracy.

Eight years of blacklists. Two countries. Every outfitter's door closed, every job board silent, her name passed between permit offices like a contagion. And the whole time, four sentences sat in a drawer.

When the chairwoman read the recommendation into the record and the chamber murmured, Astrid felt nothing she'd been promised she would feel. No flood. No unburdening. She'd imagined this moment on bad nights for eight years — imagined standing up, imagined saying *I told you so* to a room, imagined the weight lifting. Instead she sat at a folding table in a borrowed blazer and thought about the rope work at Redbank, and how Ines's hand had felt when Astrid hauled her out of the water, and how none of that had ever been in question. The river had ruled on her a long time ago. The chamber was just catching up.

* * *

The verdicts came in the spring.

Three consortium officers were indicted; two fled before the warrants landed, and one, the survey director whose signature was all over the 1998 cover-up, stood trial with the posture of a man who still believed the terrain would absorb him. It didn't. The aquifer documents were too clean, the notebook too specific, and Ines — credentialed now in a way her mother had never been permitted to be — dismantled his expert witnesses one by one from the stand, politely, in the language of flow rates and recharge zones, until the man's own survey data convicted him.

Restitution was ordered. It was the kind of number with too many zeroes to mean anything — damages to the Voss estate, damages to the families of the 1998 expedition, a public trust established to protect the headwaters in perpetuity. The headwaters would stay closed. Not hidden this time. Closed, patrolled, protected. Astrid testified for that clause specifically, and when a senator asked her whether the valley should be opened to tourism, she said: "Some places should be allowed to remain what they are. We already know what happens to them when we decide otherwise."

Her blacklist dissolved by administrative order on a Tuesday. She got the letter in the morning and stood in the doorway of her rented room holding it for a long time, reading the bureaucratic language — *reinstatement of full guiding privileges, all jurisdictions, effective immediately* — and waiting for the feeling to arrive.

It didn't arrive. What arrived instead was an afternoon: she walked to the outfitters' district for the first time in eight years, past the shops that had turned her away, and found that two of them had changed hands and one had closed, and the one that remained, Halvorsen's, put a job offer on the counter before she'd finished saying hello. Good wages. Prime season. Her pick of routes.

She stood there in the smell of rope wax and boot leather, in the shop where she'd bought her first harness at nineteen, and felt like a ghost being offered a room in her own house. She thanked him. She said she'd think about it. She walked out with the offer folded in her pocket and sat on the seawall for an hour, watching the harbor, and understood that the thing she'd been carrying for eight years — the name, the ban, the exile — had become, without her noticing, the shape of her life. She had built herself around the wound. Now the wound was closed, and she didn't know what filled the space.

Vindication, it turned out, was a document. Belonging was something else, and no one was issuing letters for that.

* * *

Ines called her in June. No preamble — she'd never used one in all the months Astrid had known her.

"The grave," she said. "The anniversary's next week. I'm going up. Mabry's bringing the boat as far as the lower takeout."

Astrid looked at the job offer in her pocket. "I'll be there."

She almost expected the climb to feel different — the same moraine, the same river, but with her name cleared, her body unburdened. It didn't feel different. The trail was as steep, the air as thin, her knee as honest about the weather. The country hadn't read the newspapers. It didn't know she'd been forgiven. It only knew whether she could still walk, and the answer, gratifyingly, was yes — slower now, with a brace she'd stopped hiding, but yes.

The cairn stood where they'd left it, a year of weather having softened its edges, lichen already claiming the north faces of the stones. Ines had brought a new marker — a small bronze plate, survey-grade, with the coordinates stamped deep — and the three of them set it without much talk, Mabry doing the lifting, Ines doing the leveling, Astrid packing the base. It was, Astrid thought, the kind of work they were good at together: each knowing the job without being told, the way a crew knows after enough water.

When it was set, Ines read the coordinates aloud. Same as the burial. No eulogy. The numbers went out over the moraine and the river took them downstream, and Astrid thought: *a waypoint*. The man was a waypoint now. Anyone navigating by this marker was navigating by him.

They ate lunch sitting against the cairn, passing a thermos around, and it was Ines who finally said it, looking out at the valley rather than at either of them.

"I got the Halvorsen offer too," she said. "And two universities. And the trust wants to hire me to run the monitoring station at the headwaters."

Mabry snorted. "Monitoring station. They'll build a hut and a helipad and call it protection."

"They'll build a hut," Ines agreed. "That's how protection works. You put people in a place or you lose it." She turned the thermos cup in her hands. "I haven't decided. I keep thinking about what my mother's notebook says on the last page. She wrote that the river was the only honest witness she'd ever had, and that someone should stand witness back."

The wind moved through the scree below the moraine, that high thin sound like breath drawn through teeth. Astrid looked at the two of them — the hydrologist with her mother's stubborn jaw, the pilot with his rebuilt shoulder and his stolen-and-returned conscience — and felt the thing she'd been unable to name since the verdict, the hollowness inside the vindication, suddenly take a shape she recognized.

It wasn't grief. It was a question. *What do you belong to, when the thing that exiled you lets you back in?* She'd belonged to the exile. She'd belonged to the river, and the river didn't care about her name either way. What she'd never belonged to was people — not since 2016, not since the client whose face she still saw in white water, not since she'd decided that competence was safer than company because competence couldn't die on her.

Except it had turned out that company could survive her. These two had. Through rapids and portages and a crevasse and a fire, through the worst country either of them would ever see, they had not died on her, and she had not left them, and somewhere in all that the arithmetic had changed.

"Halvorsen's offering me prime routes," Astrid said. "Best money I've ever been offered."

"And?" Mabry said.

"And I keep thinking the best money was never the point." She turned her cup, watching the coffee skin over in the cold. "I keep thinking about the clients nobody wants. The ones the big firms turn away — too old, too green, too much trouble, wrong kind of story. I've been that client. I know what it's like to be a liability on paper."

Ines looked at her. "You want to guide again."

"I want to guide *differently*." She heard herself say it and felt the rightness of it land in her chest like an anchor set. "Lower reaches. The water nobody runs because it's not spectacular enough for the brochures. Small boats. People the industry gave up on. And when we take them upriver—" she nodded at the cairn, at the closed valley beyond it, "—we take them as witnesses, not tourists. People who'll understand what they're looking at."

Mabry was quiet a long moment. Then: "The Provision could carry six. Eight, if nobody's proud."

"Nobody's proud," Ines said. "That could be the company name."

"It could not," said Mabry. "I have a reputation."

"Your reputation is a stolen core sample and a burned dock."

"Which is why I'm sensitive about it."

Astrid laughed — the first real laugh since the hearings, she realized; maybe since the source — and the sound went out over the moraine and came back off the glacier's shoulder, small and human in all that stone.

They didn't sign anything. There was nothing to sign. But walking down, the three of them spaced out along the trail the way crews walk, Astrid understood that the decision had been made the way the important ones always got made — not in a chamber with a stenographer, but on a moraine, over a thermos, by people who had carried each other this far and intended to keep carrying.

At the lower takeout, the Provision sat at the dock looking exactly as she had — half rust, half luck, the bullet graze on the hull painted over in a green that didn't quite match. Mabry went to check her lines. Ines stood at the rail, looking upstream toward the closed valley, toward the monitoring station she hadn't said yes to and the mother whose name was in the record now, forever, in ink that couldn't be filed away.

"Astrid," she said, without turning. "The client you lost. In 2016. What was his name?"

No one had asked her that. Not the investigators, not the hearings, not the headlines. They'd asked what happened, whose fault, how much. Never the name.

"Tomas," she said. "His name was Tomas Elu." And saying it out loud, on this river, a year and a lifetime after she'd carried it alone, she felt it set down — not gone, never gone, but *carried together* now, which was a different thing entirely.

Ines nodded once, the way you acknowledge a coordinate. Then she pointed upstream, where the afternoon light had found the water and turned it to hammered tin, and where, far off at the takeout's edge, a small boat was just coming into view around the bend — a client boat, running the lower water alone, badly, its pilot clearly in over his head and clearly about to learn what the Serrano did to people who didn't ask for help.

Astrid was already moving.
