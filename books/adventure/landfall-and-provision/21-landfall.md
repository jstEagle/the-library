## 21. Landfall

The season's last clients came down the river on a Tuesday in late March, three of them: a retired schoolteacher with a bad knee, a teenager whose mother had paid extra for "character building," and a quiet man from the capital who had read about the Serrano hearings and wanted to see the water for himself before it got famous.

Astrid met them at the takeout below Serpent Bend, where the Provision sat low on her trailer with a season of scratches written across her hull like handwriting. Mabry was already there, hosing silt out of the bilge, whistling something tuneless and old. Ines stood at the edge of the gravel bar with a clipboard she didn't need, because after a year she knew every client's name and every rapid's mood without writing either down.

"Any carnage?" Mabry asked.

"Two swimmers at Redbank," Astrid said. "Both recovered. The kid cried for ten minutes and then asked if he could do it again."

"Character built," Ines said, and made a mark on the clipboard anyway, because some rituals hold even when they're useless.

The outfit had no name on its boats. The paperwork called them Reyes-Voss River Services, which was a mouthful and a compromise, like most things that lasted. Clients called them the small outfit, or the one that takes anybody, and both descriptions were accurate enough. They ran the lower reaches only — the forty kilometers of brown-green water between the delta towns and the first gorge — and they took the clients the big firms rejected: the overweight, the frightened, the recovering, the old, the ones with prosthetics and pacemakers and court dates. Astrid had a rule she never wrote down but enforced without mercy: nobody gets told they can't come because of what their body or their history says about them. The river would make its own assessment. It always did.

The consortium's officers had fallen through the winter, one resignation at a time, like ice going out of a lake. Two were under indictment. One had fled to a country that didn't ask questions. The headwaters above the falls were closed now by decree — but a different decree, signed by a different government, the word *protected* where *forbidden* used to sit. There was talk of a research station at La Cuna. Talk of a memorial. Talk moved slowly, and Astrid had learned to let it.

She drove the schoolteacher and the boy into town while the quiet man lingered at the boat ramp, looking upstream at the white line of the far gorge.

"You've been up there," he said. Not a question.

"Once."

"What's it like?"

Astrid considered the honest answers. Cold. Loud. Like standing inside the throat of the world. Like finding your own name carved somewhere you didn't put it.

"Like a place that doesn't need us," she said. "That's the best thing about it."

He nodded slowly, satisfied or not, and went to find his bag.

* * *

The anniversary came four days later, and they'd planned it the way they planned everything now — around weather, around water levels, around who could carry what.

They went up on foot, the three of them, because the moraine above the treeline allowed no other approach. Mabry carried the marker wrapped in a tarp across his bad shoulder, complaining the whole climb in the comfortable way of a man who complained so he wouldn't have to say anything else. Ines carried the drill and the epoxy. Astrid carried food, rope, and the small bronze plate that had taken six weeks and two letters to a foundry to get right.

April on the moraine was wind and lichen and the smell of stone remembering winter. Voss's grave sat where they'd left it — a cairn of river stones on the ridge overlooking the valley, unmarked by anything but the shape of grief. A year of freeze and thaw had settled the rocks into themselves, the way a body settles into sleep. Grass had begun, stubbornly, at the base.

Ines knelt first. She always touched the top stone before anyone worked, a hydrologist's superstition: check the structure before you trust it.

"He'd hate this," Mabry said, setting down the tarp. "All this fuss."

"He'd pretend to hate it," Ines said. "Then he'd inspect our work and tell us we set the capstone crooked."

"It is crooked," Astrid said.

"Then it's authentic."

They drilled the anchor point into living rock below the cairn — not on it; the grave itself would stay as they'd built it, hands and stones and nothing else. The marker went beside it, waist-high, angled toward the river so that anyone standing at it would face downstream, the direction Voss had spent his life trying to go and his last months fighting to reverse. Astrid held the plate steady while the epoxy cured, and read it once more in the flat gray light:

**EFRAIM VOSS**
**PROSPECTOR**
**HE FOUND THE WATER**

Ines had insisted on that last line. At the foundry stage, Astrid had suggested *he loved this river*, and Ines had looked at her with an expression that was nine years of silence compressed into a single second, and said: *Loving it wasn't the point. Finding it was. He spent his whole life being told the water wasn't there.*

So: HE FOUND THE WATER. Four words. It sounded like an epitaph and a joke at once, which Astrid suspected was exactly how Efraim Voss would have wanted to be remembered.

Mabry unpacked the flask. He always brought a flask to graves; Astrid had stopped asking what was in it, because the answer changed and none of the answers mattered. He poured a measure onto the stones, poured three more into dented tin cups, and they drank whatever it was — something peaty and brutal that Ines said tasted like a fuel spill and Mabry said tasted like character building.

"To the old man," Mabry said.

"To my father," Ines said. She still stumbled slightly on the word, like a runner catching a root and keeping stride.

"To the client who fired me," Astrid said, and they laughed, because it was true — Voss had technically terminated her medical escort contract on day three when she refused his map, and rehired her on day four when the agents searched the hotel, and had never let her forget either transaction. "Worst employer I ever had."

"Best one too," Ines said quietly.

"Yes," Astrid admitted. "Best one too."

They sat against the cairn while the wind came up the valley, carrying the sound of the river far below, a sound Astrid had learned over twenty years the way other people learn a language they dream in. Below them the Serrano ran green through its gorge, running down toward their town, their ramp, their battered boat, their clients with bad knees and rebuilt lives. Running toward landfall. Everything on this river eventually made landfall. That was the deal the water offered: you went where it took you, and if you survived, you arrived somewhere.

It had taken her most of her life to understand that arriving somewhere wasn't the same as belonging there.

* * *

She'd grown up believing belonging was a document. A license, a permit, a name cleared off a list. For seven years after the 2016 incident she had carried her blacklist like a second skeleton — not visible, but structural, determining everything she could stand up under. When the exoneration came, folded into Mabry's stolen file like a letter she hadn't known was addressed to her, she'd expected to feel the skeleton dissolve. She'd expected lightness.

What she'd felt instead, reading the incident report in a prosecutor's office in the capital, was a strange, disorienting grief. Because the report proved what she'd always half-known: that Daniel Okafor's death — her client, her dead man, the weight she'd carried through every border town under false names — had been caused by a failed anchor bolt the outfitter's owner had ordered not replaced. Not by her rope work. Not by her judgment. By a man in an office who had never stood where she stood, deciding her margin of safety was someone else's profit.

She'd walked out of that office cleared, vindicated, free — and stood on the capital's sidewalks feeling like a woman whose house had burned down and been declared structurally sound.

It was Ines who'd named it, later, in the whiteout voice they used for true things: *You thought the punishment was the belonging. You thought carrying it was what made you one of us — the people the mountains keep.* And Astrid had understood, in the dark of that storm-bound ledge, that she'd built her whole identity on a debt, and that forgiveness, however earned, was also a kind of eviction.

So she'd gone back to the river. Not to the big firms that now offered her contracts with her real name printed in bold. To this: a rusted boat, a partner with a ruined shoulder, a hydrologist with a clipboard, and a client list made entirely of people the industry had decided weren't worth the liability.

Home, it turned out, wasn't a cleared reputation. It was chosen company. It was Mabry's terrible coffee and Ines's crooked capstones and a teenager crying at Redbank and then asking to do it again. It was a grave on a moraine with a joke for an epitaph.

* * *

They came down off the mountain in the long gold light of late afternoon, and at the trailhead there was a truck waiting that shouldn't have been there.

Astrid saw it first — old habit, checking lots and roads the way other people checked mirrors. White pickup, government plates, a woman leaning against the fender with a field jacket and the patient posture of someone who had waited two hours and would wait two more.

"Parks service," Ines said, squinting. "I know her. Delgado. She was at the hearings."

The woman pushed off the fender as they approached and offered her hand to each of them in turn, firm and dry. "Marisol Delgado, Headwaters Protection Office." She said it with the faint self-consciousness of someone reciting a title that hadn't existed eighteen months ago. "Sorry to ambush you up here. I tried the office phone twice."

"We don't answer phones during season," Mabry said. "Phones are how trouble finds you."

"Then I'll be brief." Delgado pulled a folder from the truck cab. "The protection decree has a management plan attached. Public comment closed last month. The commission wants a licensed outfit to run the sanctioned access program — limited expeditions, upper river, research support and guided passage. Three permits a year. Educational, scientific, memorial." She paused. "Nobody local will bid. The big firms think it's poison — too much scrutiny, too much history. And frankly, the commission won't give it to them anyway. Too much history."

She held out the folder. Astrid didn't take it yet.

"The headwaters are closed," Astrid said. "Protected. We fought for that."

"Closed isn't forgotten," Delgado said. "Closed means managed. And management needs people who know that country and have reasons to protect it." Her eyes went to the ridge above them, where the new marker caught the last light. "People with reasons. That was the phrase in the commission minutes, actually. I didn't write it."

Ines took the folder. She opened it against the truck's hood, and Astrid watched her read the way she read everything — fast, technical, hungry — and then slow down at one page, and slower at another, until her finger stopped on a line and stayed there.

"They want the La Cuna site surveyed," Ines said. "Mapped properly. Documented. Before the ice retreat takes the rest of the '98 camp." She looked up. "They want my mother's camp recorded. Officially. Her name in the site designation."

"Elena Voss Research Access Point," Delgado said. "Pending approval. Your testimony carried a lot of weight, Dr. Voss."

Ines turned away for a moment, toward the river, and Astrid gave her the privacy of pretending not to watch. Mabry didn't pretend at all; he just stood close, shoulder almost touching hers, the way he'd stood through every hard mile of the year past.

"Three permits," Astrid said finally. "A year. Upper river."

"First one would be next spring. Survey team, plus guides." Delgado shrugged. "The commission assumes you'll say no. Everyone assumes you'll say no. You spent a year getting that valley sealed."

Astrid looked upstream. The gorge had gone blue with evening, and somewhere beyond the line of sight, past the falls and the portage and the flooded timber and Serpent Bend, the river rose out of the glacier at La Cuna, cold and clean and carrying everything it remembered toward the sea.

They'd buried Voss facing downstream. But everything worth reaching on this river was upstream.

"No," Astrid said, and Delgado's face began to fall, and Astrid took the folder out of Ines's hands and held it up. "We didn't spend a year getting it sealed. We spent a year getting it *kept*. Somebody should stand at the source and make sure it stays kept." She looked at Mabry, who was grinning like a man being offered a suicide run he'd secretly dreamed of. She looked at Ines, who had gone very still, the stillness of a woman hearing her mother's name spoken aloud in official language for the first time.

"Somebody with reasons," Astrid said.

Delgado exhaled. "I'll tell the commission you're considering it."

"Tell them we're bidding," Astrid said. "Tell them the Provision runs in spring."

The truck pulled away down the valley road, taillights shrinking toward town, and the three of them stood a moment longer in the dusk with the folder between them and the river talking its endless language below.

"Upper river," Mabry said. "In spring. In flood season." He shook his head, delighted. "The old man is laughing at us right now. I can hear him."

"He found the water," Ines said. "Somebody's got to keep it."

They walked down toward the trailhead together, three shadows lengthening in the last light, and behind them on the moraine the new marker held its angle against the wind, facing the river, pointing home.
