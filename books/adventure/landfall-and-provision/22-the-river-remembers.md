## 22. The River Remembers

The morning the new client arrived, I was re-caulking a seam on the Provision's hull with my hands in cold water up to the wrists.

That was how most mornings started now. Mabry had taught me that a boat is never finished, only maintained — that the river takes its toll daily and you pay it daily or you stop floating. He said it like scripture, standing hip-deep in the shallows behind our shed, and I'd come to believe it applied to more than boats.

"Reyes." His voice carried from the office, which was really the front room of Ines's house with a desk crammed into it and a hand-painted sign: *Serrano Provision Outfitters — Lower Reaches*. "You've got a walker."

A walker meant someone who'd shown up without a referral, without a booking, without the quiet vetting we did on everyone who wanted to hire us. We took clients the big firms rejected — the ones with thin wallets and thick histories, the ones other guides had blacklisted for reasons fair and unfair alike. I knew something about both kinds of list. But walkers were rare. Walkers were either desperate or dangerous, and after everything, I'd learned to tell the difference faster than I used to.

I dried my hands on my thighs and went inside.

He was young. That was my first note. Mid-twenties, maybe, with the sunburned neck of someone who'd spent a season outdoors and the restless hands of someone who hadn't figured out what to do with his life yet. He stood in the middle of the room like he wasn't sure he was allowed to take up space in it, and when his eyes found mine they did the thing I still hated: they flicked down, quick, to check my limp before they came back up to check my face.

"It's fine," I said. "Everyone looks. You're supposed to. If your guide's leg gives out on a portage, you want to know about it before you're carrying her pack."

His ears went red. "Sorry. I didn't—"

"You did. It's fine." I leaned against the doorframe. "Name?"

"Tomas. Tomas Aldana." He said it like he was testing whether the name would hold weight here. "I heard you take people nobody else will."

"We take people who are honest about why nobody else will take them," Mabry said from the desk, not looking up from the ledger. He kept the books in a cramped, beautiful hand, all his numbers marching in straight lines, as if order on paper could atone for the disorder of his past. "Which are you?"

Tomas looked at the floor. Then at me. Then, surprisingly, he answered straight.

"I got a client hurt last season. Not killed. Broken ankle, bad one, set wrong because I panicked about the weather window and pushed us down a scree slope I shouldn't have touched." His jaw worked. "Two outfitters won't return my calls. The third offered me a job shoveling out their stables. I figured if I'm going to keep guiding, I need to learn from somebody who's been where I am."

Ines came through from the kitchen with a pot of coffee and three chipped mugs, and I watched her take the measure of him the way she took the measure of water — quietly, completely, missing nothing. She poured without asking if anyone wanted any. Nobody ever wanted any until it was poured; then everybody drank.

"And why us?" she asked. "There are cheaper outfits on the lower river."

"There aren't honest ones." He hesitated. "My father worked survey seasons for Cordillera Extraction. Before. He never talked about the headwaters, but he wouldn't go near them either. When the news broke last year — the aquifer, the expedition, all of it — he cried. I'd never seen him cry. I've been trying to understand this river ever since."

The room went still in the particular way it did whenever the consortium came up, which was less often now than it used to be, but never never. Mabry's pen stopped. Ines set the pot down with a small deliberate click.

"The hearings are public record," she said finally. "The prosecutor's summary runs four hundred pages. You could read it and save yourself the fare."

"I read it." Tomas's voice steadied. "It didn't answer my question."

"Which is?"

He turned to me. Maybe because I hadn't spoken yet, maybe because something in the way I was leaning there told him I was the one whose answer would cost something. People always seemed to know that about me, eventually. It was the one talent my disgrace had left me.

"Do you think the wilderness forgives?" he asked. "What happened out there. What people did. Does it — let it go?"

* * *

I made him wait while I thought about it, which was only fair, since I'd been thinking about it for a year.

We'd planted the cairn marker on Voss's moraine three weeks ago, on the anniversary of his burial. All four of us — Mabry, Ines, me, and the boat, hauled as far up the lower trail as two stubborn people could drag her, because it seemed wrong to make the pilgrimage without her. The moraine overlooks the river he loved, exactly as Ines promised it would, and the cairn is nothing special: river stone, mortar mixed by hand, a brass plate Ines had engraved with coordinates instead of words. A waypoint, not a monument. Anyone navigating by those numbers would find themselves standing over Efraim Voss looking down at the Serrano, and if that wasn't a eulogy, none of us could write a better one.

Ines had brought a flask of coffee and poured some out on the stones, an offering to a man who'd drunk enough terrible camp coffee in his life to appreciate the gesture. Mabry had said nothing at all, which from Mabry was a full testimony. And I had stood there with my bad leg aching in the cold and felt — not peace, exactly. Something adjacent to peace. The feeling of a debt acknowledged rather than forgiven. Of a load carried far enough that setting it down didn't feel like quitting.

The headwaters were closed now. Protected, not hidden — the decree rewritten, the valley sealed under conservation law instead of corporate silence. No one ran the Serrano above Redbank. The 1998 camp stayed in its ice, catalogued and photographed and then left alone, which was what Ines had fought the hardest for in the restitution hearings: not access, but rest. Her mother's name sat in the official record now, spelled correctly, attached to the map she'd drawn and the truth she'd died carrying. *Marisol Voss, cartographer and survivor, source of the Serrano survey.* Ines had checked the spelling eleven times.

And me? My file was clean. The 2016 incident report, preserved in Mabry's stolen core sample box alongside the aquifer data, had exonerated me in the same breath it condemned the men who'd arranged Daniel Okafor's death and dressed it as terrain. Blacklist dissolved. Licenses restored. Two countries' worth of outfitters suddenly very sorry, very eager, offering sums that would have fed me for a decade.

I'd turned them all down and gone back to caulking hulls.

People thought that was bitterness. It wasn't. It was arithmetic — the same lonely arithmetic I'd learned on the portage, when I smashed the beacon and chose blind navigation over being found. Some things you carry because carrying them is who you are. I had tried belonging to the big firms once, back when I thought belonging was something granted by institutions, stamped on credentials, revocable by committee. Then I lost it, and learned what it actually was: three people and a rusted boat, choosing each other every morning.

"So?" Tomas prompted, careful, because he could see the silence had weight.

"No," I said.

He blinked. Behind him, Mabry's pen resumed its scratching, and Ines hid whatever her face was doing behind her mug.

"The wilderness doesn't forgive," I said. "Forgiveness implies it noticed you needed it. The river doesn't hold grudges and it doesn't grant absolution. It just keeps everything. Every rope cut too early, every body the current took, every lie told on its banks — it keeps it all, the way ice keeps a camp for twenty years. That's not mercy. That's memory."

"That sounds worse than forgiveness."

"It's better." I pushed off the doorframe and crossed to the window, where you could see the Serrano through the cottonwoods, low and green-gold in the morning light, running the way it had run before any of us and would run after. "Because forgiveness is cheap. Someone says the word and the debt evaporates and nobody has to do anything. Memory makes you work. If the river remembers, then you remember, and if you remember, you get to decide what to do about it. Carry it upstream. Bury it under a cairn. Write it into a record so the next person doesn't have to die finding it. Forgiveness ends a story. Memory continues one."

Tomas was quiet for a long moment. "And you? What do you carry?"

"Daniel Okafor," I said, and his breath caught, because yes, he'd read the file, he knew the name. "Every day. Not as guilt anymore — the report settled that. As a client I failed in every way except the one that mattered, and as the reason I guide the way I guide now. Slower. More honestly. With more coffee stops than any itinerary strictly requires." I shrugged. "He'd have complained about the pace. He complained about everything. I count that among the things I miss."

"And the others?" He glanced around the room, including them without naming them. "Voss. Your—"

"Her mother," Ines said, level. "Efraim. Yes. Those too. We don't travel light here." She set down her mug and looked at him with the flat assessing gaze that had terrified three separate prosecutors' assistants. "One more question, Tomas Aldana. Why guiding? Truly. Because a man who wants to understand his father can read a book. A man who wants to guide is trying to become something. What?"

I watched the boy wrestle with it. To his credit, he didn't rush.

"Because when I panicked on that scree slope," he said slowly, "my client trusted me anyway. Even falling, even screaming, she grabbed for my hand. And I've been trying to earn that ever since, and I don't know how to do it anywhere except out there." He gestured vaguely toward the river, toward all of it. "I figure trust is like — like a route. You can't fake it. You can only walk it, one honest step at a time, and hope the ground holds."

Mabry snorted. Ines almost smiled. I recognized the sentiment because I'd once heard a dying prospector say nearly the same thing about maps: that a promise is a route you walk, not a document you sign.

"He'll do," Mabry said to me, in the tone of a man filing a verdict.

"He'll do," I agreed, "if he bails when he's told to bail and asks questions before rapids, not during. Provision leaves Thursday, six days, lower reaches to the delta and back. You'll row. You'll cook. You'll learn the seam caulk, because God knows it needs doing weekly." I stuck out my hand. "Welcome aboard, Tomas Aldana. One rule above all others."

"Which is?"

"We tell the truth on this boat. About the river, about ourselves, about what we're afraid of. Lies sink faster than holes."

He shook my hand. His grip was stronger than his confidence, which was the right way around.

* * *

Thursday came gray and clean, the kind of morning that smells like rain it hasn't committed to yet.

We loaded the Provision the way we always did — Mabry checking the engine with the tenderness he showed no living person, Ines sealing the instrument case with her hydrology kit and, tucked inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, the field notebook she still carried everywhere though every court in two nations had certified its contents. I stowed the food. Tomas, to his credit, asked where things went instead of guessing, and remembered the answers.

At the dock, Ines paused with one foot on the gunwale and looked back at the house, at the sign, at the whole small improbable life we'd built on the bones of a larger catastrophe. "Voss would hate this," she said. "All this safety briefing. All these rules."

"Voss would love this," I said. "He'd just pretend to hate it. Same as you."

She laughed — short, real — and stepped aboard.

Mabry took the tiller. I took the bow, where I could read the water first, and Tomas sat amidships with his hands folded like a man in church, which in a sense he was. The current took us the moment we cast off, gentle here in the lower reaches, deceptively gentle, the way rivers are near their mouths when they've already done their raging upstream where no one watches.

The town fell away. The cottonwoods closed in. Somewhere ahead the Serrano bent toward country we knew by heart now — every shoal, every eddy, every place the water remembered what we remembered — and beyond that, far upstream and closed forever, the glacial valley where a dead man's promise had been kept and a living woman's name restored.

"Ms. Reyes?" Tomas called forward, over the engine's mutter.

"Astrid."

"Astrid." He tried the name out. "The thing you said — about carrying things upstream. How do you know when you're done? When you can put it down?"

I watched the water slide past the bow, brown-green and patient, holding everything it had ever been given.

"You don't put it down," I said. "You just get strong enough that it weighs nothing. Keep rowing."

And the Provision pushed off into the current, running easy toward the delta, carrying us all — the guilty and the cleared, the grieving and the grinning, one new hand learning the ropes and three old ones that no longer knew how to let go — downstream, together, into the morning.
