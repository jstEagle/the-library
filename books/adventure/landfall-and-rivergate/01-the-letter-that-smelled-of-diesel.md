## 1. The Letter That Smelled of Diesel

The letter arrived the way most bad news arrives in the lowlands: late, damp, and smelling of someone else's fuel.

Paloma Whitlock found it wedged under the tarp of the supply sled at the edge of Site Fourteen, where the salvage crew had been pulling a drowned trading post out of the silt for six weeks. The envelope was university stock — heavy cream paper, the kind that cost more per sheet than her crew made in an hour — but it had traveled in a mail barge with the diesel drums, and now it reeked of them, a greasy hydrocarbon ghost that would not wash off her fingers after she'd handled it.

She stood at the sled for a long moment before opening it. Ten years of practice had taught her to read the shape of correspondence from her father's world before reading the words. Thick envelope: grant paperwork, or a conference invitation she'd decline. Thin envelope: a bill, or worse.

This one was thick. She almost left it for the tide of mud creeping up from the river channel.

"Whitlock!" Okafor's voice carried across the site, roughened by cold and pipe smoke. "You going to stand there all morning or are you going to help us lift the east sill? The water's coming back."

"The water always comes back," she said, but she pocketed the letter unopened and went to work, because the water did always come back, and the sill — a blackened beam from the old post's foundation, preserved by a century of anaerobic muck — would be gone by noon if they didn't get it onto the cradle first.

The work took everything, the way good work did. Six of them in the channel, hip-deep in water the temperature of melted snow, hauling on slings while the pump fought the seep. Paloma called the counts, watched the beam's angle, felt through the sling lines whether the wood was holding or starting to delaminate along the grain. It was the kind of problem that filled the whole skull, leaving no room for anything else, and she had chosen this kind of problem deliberately, ten years ago, with the same care she chose her boots.

By midday the sill was out, strapped down under wet burlap, and the crew broke for food around the stove in the site shack. Paloma ate standing up, as usual, and only then remembered the letter.

She took it outside, into the flat gray light off the river, and opened it with her belt knife because the envelope had swollen with damp.

Two pages. A letterhead she knew too well — the Department of Riverine Archaeology, Whitmore College — and beneath it, a signature she knew better: Dean Harrow, who had signed off on her father's last four expeditions and had never once, in ten years, written to his daughter.

*Ms. Whitlock.*

Not *Dr.* Not even *Ms. Paloma Whitlock.* Just the surname, stripped bare, as if there were only one Whitlock who mattered and everyone knew whose name it really was.

*It is my difficult duty to inform you that the Nakamura-Whitlock River Survey has missed two scheduled radio checks from its position above Landfall Rapids. The expedition's last confirmed transmission was received on the 3rd. Weather in the upper gorge is deteriorating ahead of seasonal norms. The University is organizing a search party to depart from Rivergate Station within the week.*

*Given your documented familiarity with the Rivergate passes — familiarity which, we note, you have declined to make available to this department until now — the University extends the following offer. We require a guide with proven competence above the rapids. You are, according to every record we hold, the only such guide currently licensed. In consideration of your service, the Department is prepared to provide the field reference required for your pending salvage permit application, reference 44-C, upon successful completion of the search.*

*We trust you understand the urgency.*

*Yours sincerely,*
*R. Harrow*

Paloma read it twice. Then a third time, standing in the cold with the diesel smell rising off the paper like a second message.

They hadn't even used her father's name right. *Nakamura-Whitlock River Survey* — the hyphenated title he'd fought the university over for two years, back when she was nineteen and still answering his letters, still believing that if she worked hard enough, learned fast enough, became useful enough, he'd ask her along someday. He'd won the naming fight. He'd never once asked her on an expedition. And now the survey bore both their names like a marriage neither of them had agreed to, and the university was writing to her because the man whose name came first in it had gone quiet above the worst water in the range.

Missed two checks. Her father did not miss radio checks. She could hate him — she did, in the practiced, load-bearing way she hated him — but hatred required accuracy, and the accurate fact was that Elias Nakamura ran his expeditions like clockwork wound by hand. He'd missed one check in twenty years, the season she'd turned twelve, and he'd spent three days afterward apologizing to the committee in language so formal it read like a legal deposition. Two missed checks meant the radio was broken, or the camp was gone, or the men were dead, and none of those possibilities were things she wanted to be thinking about while standing in the mud of somebody else's drowned history.

"Bad news?" Okafor had come out of the shack, steaming mug in hand, watching her face with the frank interest of a man who'd spent thirty years reading weather and people and found them similar.

"It's nothing."

"You're holding that letter like it bit you."

"I'm holding it like it costs money." She folded it along its creases. "Which it does."

Okafor grunted. He knew about the permit. Everyone on the crew knew about the permit — 44-C, the deep-salvage license that would let her run her own contracts instead of hiring herself out to other people's digs, the piece of paper she'd been assembling documentation for across three seasons and two provinces. Every report filed, every hour logged, every signature collected except one: the academic field reference, which no working archaeologist would give her because she'd walked away from the profession's front door ten years ago and the profession had long memories.

And the only institution positioned to give it to her was the one that employed her father.

"They want something," Okafor said.

"They always want something." She looked out at the river, brown and patient, already reclaiming the pit they'd pumped dry. "My father's expedition has gone quiet above Landfall."

Okafor said nothing for a moment. Even a man who'd never met Elias Nakamura knew the name; it was stenciled on half the survey equipment in the territory, on the monographs in the college library, on the plaque at the ferry landing commemorating the flood study of '09. "That's bad water," he said finally. "Above Landfall."

"It's the worst water in the range. That's why nobody runs it without him." She heard the bitterness come out sideways and didn't bother to catch it. "He's mapped it twice. There are maybe four people alive who know the Rivergate passes well enough to take a search party up there in October, and I'm one of them, and the other three are dead or seventy."

"So they've asked you."

"They've offered me a trade. My permit reference for my father's river." She laughed, short and ugly. "Ten years I've kept clear of that name. Kept it off my applications, kept my own work clean of it, built something that was mine. And the first time they write to me, it's to sell me back my own qualification in exchange for dragging me into his story."

"What are you going to do?"

"That depends." She turned the folded letter over in her hand. "On how much of what I feel is anger and how much is something else."

* * *

She didn't decide that night. She decided the way decisions actually happen in the field — slowly, in the background, while the hands do other work — and then all at once, at some unremarkable moment, when the accumulated weight tips over.

The unremarkable moment came two days later, at dusk, at the edge of the site, when she went down to the channel to check the cofferdam and found the river dropping the season's first real frost on the gravel bars. Winter was early everywhere this year. Up in the high country, above Landfall, it would already be snowing on the col, and every day that passed was a day the search window narrowed, and somewhere above the rapids — if he was alive, if any of them were alive — her father was learning what October in the gorge did to a man without shelter.

She crouched at the water's edge and let herself think about him properly for the first time in years, which felt less like grief than like opening a crate she'd sealed carefully and marked DO NOT OPEN, knowing full well she'd open it eventually.

Elias Nakamura, teaching her to read water when she was eight, crouched beside her on a rock with his hand flat on the current: *Feel that? The river tells you where it's deep. It lies about everything else.* Elias Nakamura, waving from the deck of the expedition barge as it pulled away from the Landfall dock, her mother's hand on her shoulder, her father already turning toward his maps before the boat cleared the pilings. Elias Nakamura, at her mother's funeral, standing at the grave for exactly as long as protocol required and then walking to the truck to take a call from the university. Elias Nakamura, in the one letter he'd sent her in ten years — a single page, four years ago, offering her a position as *field assistant, junior grade*, on a survey she'd have been leading if she'd stayed inside his walls — and her reply, which had been one line: *I don't work for people who hire their family last.*

She had meant it. She still meant it. That was the trap of it — the resentment wasn't a phase, wasn't a wound that time would close. It was a conclusion, reached honestly, from evidence.

But conclusions were for warm rooms and safe rivers. Above Landfall, in October, with the radio silent, conclusions didn't matter. What mattered was that a man who never missed a check had missed two, and that she was the only guide who could go find out why, and that the universe — which had always had her father's sense of humor, cruel and precise — had arranged it so that saving him and saving herself required the same trip.

She stood, wiped her hands on her thighs, and walked back up to the site shack where Okafor was banking the stove.

"I need the skiff tomorrow," she said. "Early. I'm running down to the telegraph office at Millbrook, and then I'll need two weeks' leave. Unpaid."

Okafor looked at her for a long moment. "The university letter."

"The university letter."

"And the permit?"

"If I don't go, they don't sign. If they don't sign, I lose three years of work." She fed a split of birch into the stove and watched the flame take it. "So I'm going to go up that river, find whatever's left of his camp, bring back whoever's left of his crew, and collect my piece of paper. And then I am never going to think about Elias Nakamura again, and this time I'll mean it."

"You've said that before."

"I've said a lot of things before. This time I'll have earned it."

Okafor nodded slowly, the way he did when he'd decided not to argue. "Take the skiff. Take the good compass out of the shed — the brass one, not the plastic. And Whitlock." He waited until she looked at him. "Whatever's up there, whatever happened between you two — the river doesn't care. It'll kill you both the same. Go up there as a guide, not a daughter. Guides come home."

She thought about telling him that was exactly what she intended. She thought about it and found she couldn't say it, because saying it would make it a promise, and promises made in warm rooms had a way of drowning in cold ones.

Instead she said, "Thanks for the skiff," and went to pack.

* * *

She packed the way her father had taught her, which was the only inheritance she'd ever accepted willingly: everything essential, nothing sentimental, weight balanced low and centered. Rope. First aid. The repair kit. Dry rations for six days beyond the planned five. Her own charts of the Rivergate passes — drawn by her own hand, from her own runs, corrected against his published maps in red ink wherever he'd been wrong, which was more often than the journals admitted. She hesitated over the notebook shelf, then took a blank waterproof book and three pencils, because whatever they found upriver, someone would need to write it down.

Last, she took the letter out of her jacket and read it once more by lamplight, and this time she noticed what exhaustion and anger had let her miss.

*Given your documented familiarity with the Rivergate passes — familiarity which, we note, you have declined to make available to this department until now.*

Harrow hadn't needed to write that. It served no purpose in an offer letter except to remind her that they'd been keeping file on her, that her refusals had been recorded, catalogued, held in evidence like prior convictions. And beneath it, another thing, smaller: the offer specified the reference would come *upon successful completion of the search*. Not upon guiding it. Upon completing it. Which meant the university had already decided what success looked like, and it wasn't a rescue. It was a recovery. They were wording it like an insurance claim.

Or like a cleanup.

She sat with that for a while, the lamp hissing, the frost ticking against the shack's tin roof. Then she folded the letter away, and found she was no longer sure which she dreaded more: finding her father dead, or finding him alive and discovering what he'd done to make two radio checks disappear.

Outside, the river ran dark and constant, lying about everything except where it was deep.

She doused the lamp. At dawn she'd point the skiff downriver, toward Millbrook, toward the wire she'd send accepting terms she despised, and after that — Rivergate Station, the resupply depot at the head of the rapids, the last place on earth with a roof before the gorge swallowed everything.

She fell asleep rehearsing the route and woke once, near midnight, certain she'd heard a barge engine in the dark below the site. But when she got up and looked, the river was empty, black water sliding past black banks, carrying nothing visible toward the sea.

The engine sound, if there had ever been one, was gone.
