## 2. Rivergate Station

The river told you things before people did. That was the first lesson my father ever taught me, standing on a gravel bar when I was nine years old, and it was the last thing I wanted to be thinking about as the charter plane's pontoons kissed the brown water off Rivergate Station and taxied toward the dock like a tired animal coming home to a barn it didn't trust.

Rivergate hadn't changed in ten years, which was its own kind of lie. The same corrugated buildings crouched on stilts above the high-water line, rust bleeding down their faces in long orange tears. The same diesel smell hung over everything — generators, boats, the fuel drums stacked in their cage of chain-link. The same hand-painted sign over the dock office: RIVERGATE SUPPLY & OUTFITTING — LAST STOP BEFORE LANDFALL. Somebody had added a smaller sign beneath it since my last visit, laminated and official-looking: *Consortium concession area. Authorized personnel beyond this point.*

I stood on the dock with my duffel and my survey case and let that second sign sink in. Ten years ago there had been no consortium here. Ten years ago this had been a place where anyone going upriver stopped to buy rope and kerosene and gossip, and the gossip was free.

"You Whitlock?" The man who came out onto the dock was broad, gray-bearded, wearing a canvas jacket with the sleeves cut off despite the cold coming down off the range. His forearms were a map of old scars and newer ink. "Charter said one passenger."

"Paloma Whitlock." I offered a hand. He took it briefly, his palm dry and rough as bark. "You're Dekker?"

"Dekker, yeah. Station keeper." He picked up my duffel before I could protest, which I suppose answered the question of whether he was staff or management. There was nobody else. That was the other thing about Rivergate — in the old days there'd been a dozen people working the station during survey season. Now there was Dekker, and behind him, through the office window, a young woman at a radio desk who didn't look up.

Inside, the office smelled of coffee gone bitter on a hotplate and the sweet rot of damp plywood. A woodstove ticked in the corner. On the wall behind the counter hung the thing I'd come three thousand kilometers to see, though not for the reason the university thought.

The freight board.

Every expedition running the Rivergate passes posted its manifest there — crates in, crates out, dates, signatures. It was how outfitters kept each other honest and how guides like me knew what was moving on the river. My father's name was on it in his own blocky handwriting, the letters I'd grown up watching him carve into tent stakes and cache lids: **E. WHITLOCK EXPEDITION — UNIV. SURVEY 12 PERSONNEL — INBOUND 14 CRATES — OUTBOUND 2**.

Inbound fourteen. Outbound two.

Dekker set my duffel down behind the counter and poured me coffee without asking. It was terrible. I drank it anyway, because my hands needed something to do besides shake.

"When did they come through?" I asked. "My father's party."

He didn't answer right away. He looked at the coffee pot instead, at the window, at the freight board — anywhere but at me. When he finally spoke, his voice had the flatness of a man reciting something rehearsed. "Came through on schedule. Resupplied. Went on up toward Landfall."

"On what date?"

"Eleventh."

"And the last radio check?"

"Nineteenth. Routine position report. All normal." He said *all normal* the way a witness says *to the best of my knowledge*, which is to say: technically true, spiritually hollow.

"That's eight days of silence," I said. "On a river where your own posted rules require daily contact above the rapids."

"Radio's temperamental up there." He shrugged. "Weather fronts off the range play hell with the signal. You know that. You've run these passes."

"I have." I set the cup down. "So let me see the resupply crates."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, involuntary, like a fish turning under ice. "Crates?"

"The fourteen inbound. University property. If the expedition's overdue, standard procedure is an inventory check so the search party knows what equipment went up with them. Radios especially. Serial numbers." I'd invented none of this. It was all true, all procedure, and all beside the point. What I actually wanted was to stand next to those crates and read them the way my father taught me to read rivers.

"They're in the warehouse," Dekker said. "Sealed. I'm not authorized—"

"Authorized by whom?"

The pause lasted half a breath too long. "Consortium holds the concession now. Their logistics office signs off on warehouse access."

"Their logistics office is four hundred kilometers downriver."

"Then I guess you'll want to radio them." He smiled without any warmth reaching his face. "Take a while for an answer, though. Weather."

*Weather.* The word was doing a lot of work in this building.

* * *

I walked out along the shore path while Dekker pretended to do inventory in the office, and I did what I'd been doing since the plane lifted off from the lowlands: I read the river.

The Kestrel ran wide and green-gray here, braiding around gravel islands, deceptively calm in the way of big water gathering itself for violence downstream. Landfall rapids were twenty kilometers up, where the valley pinched between granite shoulders and dropped forty meters in a chaos of standing waves. Every guide who worked this stretch knew the rhythm of traffic on it. Barges went up loaded and came down light. Survey crews went up in aluminum skiffs with outboards roaring and came down the same way, loud, visible, accounted for.

But the night barge — that was different.

I heard about it within the hour, because Rivergate was too small to keep secrets and too scared to tell them straight. The young woman at the radio desk, whose name tag said MIRELLE, mentioned it in passing while I pretended to study the tide-and-flow charts pinned by the door. "Barge came through Tuesday night," she said, not looking up from her logbook. "Heading downriver. You hear anything about a ride down, that's who'd take it."

"At night?" I said. "Downriver? Nobody runs the lower channel at night except the mail boat, and the mail boat has running lights and a schedule."

Mirelle's pen stopped moving. For just a moment she looked at me — really looked, the way one person looks at another when they're both standing in a room with something dangerous in it — and then her eyes went back to her logbook. "Station policy is we don't track private cargo," she said. "That's what Dekker says."

"What do *you* say?"

She turned a page she hadn't written on. "I say the generator needs diesel, and I'm off at six."

* * *

The warehouse sat fifty meters back from the dock on its own pilings, padlocked, with a consortium decal on the door that hadn't been there ten years ago — a stylized mountain over crossed pickaxes, blue on white, cheerful as a travel poster. I circled it once in the gray afternoon light, hands in my pockets, looking like a bored guide killing time, and I found what I was looking for on the landward side: a ventilation gap under the eaves, wide enough for a cat, and below it, stacked against the wall where someone had been in a hurry, the crates.

Fourteen of them should have been inside. Six were outside, shoved into the shadow of the loading ramp under a tarp that had blown half off. And they were wrong in every way a crate can be wrong.

They were unopened.

I want to be precise about that, because unopened is a specific condition. These were university expedition crates — my father had specified the design years ago, marine ply with steel banding and stenciled contents lists, each one sealed with numbered tamper tags logged at origin. An opened crate gets re-banded, re-tagged, its stencil smudged with handling. These six had never been touched. The tamper tags sat pristine in their crimps. Rain hadn't even spotted the stenciling, which meant they'd been under the tarp or inside until very recently — after the eleventh, after the expedition supposedly *resupplied*.

I crouched and read the stencils by the light leaking under the tarp edge. **CRATE 07 — RADIO SPARES (2) HF/SSB. CRATE 08 — MEDICAL, FIELD SURGICAL. CRATE 09 — SAMPLE ARCHIVE, SEALED.**

Sample archive, sealed. My father never left a sample archive behind. He used to say an archaeologist who trusts the river with her samples deserves what the river gives back, which was usually nothing. And radio spares — a crew running silent for eight days, and their backup radios sitting under a tarp at the last outpost?

You don't lose equipment like that. You leave it. Or someone makes sure you leave it.

I stood up slowly, my heart doing something cold and deliberate in my chest. Behind me, gravel crunched.

Dekker stood at the corner of the warehouse with a flashlight he wasn't shining at me yet, and behind him, half in shadow, a man I hadn't seen before — trim, mid-forties, city jacket under a rain shell, the kind of clean hands that don't belong anywhere near a river. He wore no badge, but the consortium mountain-pickaxe logo was embroidered small and neat on his chest pocket, like a country club crest.

"Dr. Whitlock," the clean-handed man said. "Paloma, isn't it? Corben Voss. Consortium field liaison." He said *liaison* the way some men say *lawyer*. "We heard you'd be coming through. Terrible business, your father's expedition. We're cooperating fully with the university's search."

"Were these crates part of the resupply?" I asked him directly, because I've learned that guilty men answer direct questions worse than clever ones.

Voss glanced at the tarp, unhurried. "Surplus inventory. The expedition elected to lighten its load before the upper river. Common practice."

"Elected to go upriver past Landfall without spare radios and a surgical kit."

"Experienced crews travel light." His smile was small and professional. "Your father especially. Legend, they say."

The word *legend* in his mouth sounded like something being measured for a coffin.

"Who ran the night barge?" I said.

And there it was — the half-second hitch, the weight shifting in his stance, the flashlight coming up an inch. Not guilt exactly. Calculation. Voss was too controlled for guilt; what crossed his face was the rapid arithmetic of a man deciding how much I already knew and how much it would cost to find out.

"Private cargo moves on private schedules," he said. "If you'll excuse us, Dr. Whitlock, the warehouse is a restricted area under the concession terms. I'd hate for your search effort to get tangled in paperwork." He stepped aside, gesturing back toward the station with the flashlight, courteous as a maître d' showing you the exit. "You'll want to hire your boatmen early. Weather window's closing. First snows are early this year, they say."

*They say.* Everyone at Rivergate spoke in quotations of other people, a whole outpost talking in borrowed voices so no voice could be held responsible.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the bunk room Dekker grudgingly rented me — the only guest room, because Rivergate no longer expected guests — with the lamp low and my father's last postcard propped against the basin. It had come six weeks ago, forwarded twice, a tourist card of the Landfall falls with a line scrawled across the back in his handwriting: *Found something on the terrace you'd have loved. Wish you were here. — Dad.*

*I'd have loved.* Past tense, and he wasn't dead yet when he wrote it. Something in him had already started writing his own eulogy, or his own confession, and I'd thrown the card in a drawer and told myself I'd answer it after permit season.

Now I lay listening to the river work the pilings under the floorboards, and I made myself do the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the dock.

An expedition of twelve doesn't vanish. Twelve people make noise — stove smoke, latrine trenches, generator hum, the orange glare of tarps on satellite photos. Eight days of silence means either catastrophe or containment, and catastrophes leave debris, and the river below Landfall had shown me nothing on the flight in but clean water. I'd spent the whole approach scanning the banks, the eddies, the drift lines, the way I'd scan a site for disturbance. Clean. Swept. Rivers don't sweep themselves.

And the crates. Fourteen in, and at least six never opened, and a night barge going *downriver* heavy enough to justify running black water at night, and a station keeper who couldn't look at me, and a radio operator quoting policy like scripture, and a consortium liaison materializing within minutes of me finding the tarp — as if he'd been watching the warehouse. As if he'd been waiting for someone to come looking.

My father had found something on the terrace. Something he thought I'd love. And somewhere between that postcard and the eleventh of the month, the thing he'd found had become the thing somebody needed to make disappear — not lost, not drowned, not scattered by weather. Disappeared the way you disappear a ledger, a burial ground, a man's name from a freight board. Professionally. With tarps and tamper tags and a smiling man in a city jacket.

I got up and looked out the window at the river. Moonlight lay on the current in broken silver bars, and far upstream, where the valley narrowed toward Landfall, the dark of the gorge seemed to breathe.

Tomorrow I'd hire boatmen and run the rapids. Tonight I stood at the window and admitted what I'd known since the tarp blew back: I hadn't come for the permit reference. I'd told myself the money and the career math and the resentment, and all of it was true, and none of it was why I'd gotten on the plane within nineteen hours of the call.

Somewhere up that black gorge was my father's camp, and whatever was left of it had been arranged for me to find — or arranged so I never would. Either way, the river had already testified. Unopened crates. A barge at night. A swept story with too many hands holding the broom.

I packed my duffel in the dark, and in the morning, when I asked Dekker for names of boatmen willing to run Landfall, he gave me two and wouldn't meet my eyes, and the younger of the two, when I shook his hand at the dock, leaned close and said, quiet enough that the wind almost took it:

"Whatever you're looking for up there — the barge crew wasn't consortium. They were scared. Big men, scared. And they weren't carrying crates down."

He glanced once at the warehouse, once at Voss's empty window.

"They were carrying them *up*."
