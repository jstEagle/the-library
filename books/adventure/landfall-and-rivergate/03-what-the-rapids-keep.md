## 3. What the Rapids Keep

We put in below the station at first light, because the light mattered. Landfall in the dark was a rumor you could die of; Landfall at dawn, with the sun still behind the ridge and the water lit from the east, showed you its lines the way a scar showed a surgeon where to cut.

The two boatmen the university had wired money for were named Dekker and Anselm. Dekker was broad and gray-stubbled and had run the lower gorge for twenty years without drowning, which he mentioned twice. Anselm was younger, quiet, with rope-scarred hands and a way of looking at the river like it was an argument he intended to win. Neither of them asked why the daughter of Elias Whitlock was running his own rapids to find him. Dekker asked how much the salvage bonus was. Anselm asked nothing at all.

I gave the safety talk anyway, standing on the gravel with the boats loaded and the mist coming off the water in slow ropes.

"Landfall is a mile and a half of river that drops forty feet," I said. "It doesn't have a single line. It has three, and they braid, and they braid differently every season depending on how the ice went out. The left channel is the postcard line — fast, clean, one hole at the bottom you punch through. The center is where the river keeps its teeth. The right is a lie that looks like a shortcut." I looked at each of them. "We run left, we eddy out at the split rock, we scout the bottom third on foot before we commit. If anyone tells you they know Landfall, they're lying or they're dead."

"You've run it?" Anselm asked. First words since the station.

"Twice. Once with my father, once without him."

Dekker spat into the water, which is a thing boatmen do to say *I'm ready*, and we pushed off.

* * *

The first third of Landfall is a lie told slowly. The river widens, flattens, goes glassy over gravel bars, and you can almost believe the maps exaggerate. The banks close in — black spruce leaning over gray water — and the current picks up the way a sentence picks up speed before the bad news. I rode the stern of the lead boat with the sweep oar and watched the water read itself to me: the V of a submerged rock, the smooth green tongue of the main channel, the boil lines where deep water argued with shallow.

"Left fork," I called, and Dekker swung us left, and Anselm followed.

The river narrowed. The walls came up. And Landfall stopped pretending.

The sound arrived before the water did — a long, rolling concussion, like the earth clearing its throat — and then we were in it. The left channel is a chute, a quarter mile of standing waves stacked shoulder-high, the whole river funneled between rock walls close enough to touch with an oar. I have been in water that loud before. It never stops being a physical thing, the roar, pressing on your chest, eating your own voice. Dekker rowed like a man praying in a language only his arms spoke. Spray came over the gunwale in cold sheets. Somewhere behind us Anselm's boat was either there or it wasn't, and there was no looking back, because looking back in Landfall is how you join the rocks.

The hole at the bottom is called the Punchbowl, a backwash the size of a barn where recirculating water holds whatever the river gives it. You hit it straight, you punch through, you come out the other side into the eddy below the split rock, gasping, alive, absurdly triumphant.

We hit it straight. We punched through. The eddy took us and spun us and set us down against the gravel like a hand setting down a cup.

Anselm's boat came through six seconds later, sideways, shipping half the river, both of us screaming at him to straighten out — and he did, barely, and then we were both in the calm water below, and Dekker was laughing the high, cracked laugh of a man whose body has just informed him it isn't dead yet.

I didn't laugh. I was already looking at the water.

Below the Punchbowl the current slackens and the river spreads, and everything the rapids have taken — and Landfall takes things every season, every decade, every generation; canoes, barges, men, and once, according to my father, a piano — everything settles into the slack water and the eddy lines and the gravel shoals downstream. The river keeps what it keeps, but it also files it, sorts it, lays it out for anyone patient enough to walk the banks.

"Beach them," I said. "We walk the shoals."

* * *

The salvage line below Landfall is a quarter mile of gray gravel and driftwood, and I walked it the way my father taught me to walk a dig: slow, in lanes, eyes down, then up, then down. Dekker came along because the salvage bonus was per item. Anselm stayed with the boats, which suited me.

The river had been generous. There was the usual detritus of a working river — fuel drums, half a dock, a boot that had gone past being anyone's boot. And then, tangled in a sweep of deadfall at the high-water line, a panel of hull planking, four feet long, painted the expedition green that I would have known anywhere, that I had grown up seeing on crates in our garage, on the skiff he taught me to row in, on the sign over the boathouse at the university boathouse where I had stopped going when I was twenty-two.

I crouched next to it and did not touch it for a long time.

The wood was splintered along one edge, torn, not cut — driven against rock with force. The paint was fresh enough. The green was *his* green, mixed dark the way he mixed it because he said bright boats attracted bears, which was nonsense, which was the kind of nonsense I had spent ten years missing and hating myself for missing.

"Whitlock's boat?" Dekker asked from ten feet away, and I heard him already recalculating the bonus.

"His survey skiff." My voice came out flat and strange. "This is the bow section."

A skiff does not shed its bow section in gentle water. A skiff sheds its bow section when it meets rock at speed, or when it meets rock at all, or when someone puts an axe through it, and I made myself think that last thought and hold it, because the station keeper's face at Rivergate had been the face of a man who knew something, and unopened resupply crates were the kind of detail that didn't happen by accident.

I stood. I kept walking the lane.

Twenty minutes later, wedged in the root ball of a toppled spruce where the flood debris had piled it, I found the notebook.

It was the orange waterproof kind he'd used for thirty years, the kind that costs too much and works, bound with a cord through the spine. It was swollen with water but intact, because it was built for exactly this — to go into rivers and come back out. His name was on the cover in his blocky drafting capitals: E. WHITLOCK, RIVER SURVEY, SEASON 9. Below that, in smaller letters he'd added at the start of every season like a superstition: *ALL FINDINGS TO THE PUBLIC RECORD.*

I sat down on the gravel with it and opened it and read my father's handwriting for the first time in four years.

The early pages were survey work — soundings, bank profiles, the meticulous little cross-sections he drew of every terrace, every cut bank, every gravel bar, with depths and dates and the tiny annotations that made his maps the best in the territory. *Gravel bar unstable, do not camp.* *Spring seep here, good water.* *Ilsa says the fish run late this year — she's usually right.* Ilsa. I filed the name.

Then, three days before the last dated entry, the handwriting changed. It got smaller. It got faster. The neat drafting capitals broke into a cursive I'd only ever seen in his old letters to my mother, back when there were letters.

*Terrace above the right fork, 40 m elevation. Not a natural formation. Cultural layers. Fire-cracked stone in situ. Bone — small, bundled, deliberate. This is a burial ground. Generations of it. This is exactly what Marrow Consortium's assessment says does not exist.*

*Called the university. Called the consortium's office. Told them I'd amend the heritage assessment. They were very polite. That's the part I don't like.*

*If anything happens to me or this survey, the record is the thing. The record is the thing. The record—*

And then nothing. The last three pages had been torn out. Not ripped in haste — torn carefully, close to the spine, by someone who wanted the pages and wanted no fragments left behind. The stubs of them were still visible at the gutter, orange paper edges like a wound.

I sat on the gravel with the river talking its low talk behind me and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in the chest, that my father had known he might not come back down this river, and had written that down, and that someone had taken the pages anyway, and that the someone had left the notebook in the river — because a drowned man's notebook in the drift line tells a story all by itself. *Lost. Swept away. Grief, accident, tragedy, closed file.*

Except they'd missed the panel. And they'd missed the one thing he'd taught me that they couldn't have known mattered: he never trusted a single copy of anything. *The record is the thing,* he'd written, and my father's records had copies the way rivers have tributaries. Somewhere upstream there was a camp, and a camp had a site ledger, and ledgers get copied, mailed, hidden. He'd taught me that too, without ever once, in my whole childhood, teaching me how to say *I need you* in a way that either of us could hear.

"Found something?" Dekker called.

I closed the notebook and held it against my ribs, under my jacket, against my skin, like something with a heartbeat.

"His survey notes," I said. "We keep moving."

* * *

The landing below the rapids was a gravel shelf and a lean-to, a day's poling above the survey camp. We made it by late afternoon, running the easy water with the current doing the work, the two boats riding low with salvaged gear and one salvaged hull panel wrapped in a tarp like a body.

I cooked rice over the spirit stove and divided it three ways and nobody talked much, which is the way good river people talk. The light went orange on the ridge across the water. Somewhere up there, in the black-green folds of the terrace country, my father was either dead or held, and I had stopped being able to pretend those were different weights. Grief had come early. I kept trying to send it back. It kept refusing.

It was Anselm who broke the silence, turning his tin cup slowly in his hands.

"The right fork," he said. "You said it's a lie that looks like a shortcut." He looked at me across the fire. "The hull panel. The way the wood's torn — that's not the left channel. Left channel's all water, no wood to hit. That's rock-on-rock damage. That's the right fork, or the terrace shallows above it."

Dekker snorted. "So the man ran the wrong line. Happens."

"Whitlock doesn't run the wrong line," Anselm said. "Everyone knows that. It's why they pay him." He was still looking at me. "And it's why I'm not going up to the camp tomorrow."

The fire popped. I set down my cup.

"Say that again."

"I'll take the boats back down to Rivergate with the salvage. That was the contract — run the rapids, assist the recovery, done at the landing." He said it evenly, without apology, and I realized he'd been building to it since the Punchbowl. "I've got a family. I ran Landfall because it's Landfall and I wanted the line. But whatever's up at that camp, it isn't a search anymore. You read that notebook on the gravel bank where you thought we couldn't see your face." He stood, unhurried, and began coiling his rope with the neat economy of a man who had decided something and would not be argued with. "Men who tear pages out of notebooks don't leave witnesses, and I'm not going to be one."

"Anselm." I stood too. "I can pay—"

"You can't pay enough to make the right fork safe," he said. "Nobody can. That's the whole point of it." He slung his coil over his shoulder and paused, and for a moment something moved behind his face — not sympathy exactly, something more like recognition. "My brother worked a concession camp up the Kettle once. Same consortium, different river. He came back different, and then he didn't come back at all, and everyone official said accident." He looked upriver, where the dark was coming down between the ridges like a lid. "You're going up there anyway. I can see it. So I'll say one thing, free of charge, and then I'm sleeping."

"Say it."

"The right fork isn't a lie," he said. "It's a door. Old river people used the terrace above it as a crossing for a hundred years before the maps came. My grandmother told me. If your father found what I think he found up there, then the consortium didn't erase a camp." He shouldered his pack. "They erased a graveyard. And graveyards, Ms. Whitlock, are the one thing this country doesn't forgive."

He rolled into his bedroll with his back to the fire. Dekker looked at me, looked upriver, and began, with great concentration, to clean a rifle he had not mentioned at the station.

I sat with the notebook in my lap and read the torn stubs again by firelight, running my thumb over the cut edges, and made myself a promise with a deadline attached: whatever the last pages said, I would find them. Up at the camp. Up on the terrace. In whatever hands had torn them out.

Above the landing, in the last light, the river went on talking to itself, and it sounded less like water now and more like testimony.
