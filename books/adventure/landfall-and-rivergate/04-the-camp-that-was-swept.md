## 4. The Camp That Was Swept

The camp sat in the crook of the river where the valley widened into a gravel terrace, and from two hundred meters out, Paloma knew.

She had grown up reading camps the way other children read weather. Her father's camps had a signature: the cook tent pitched with its back to the wind, the fuel drums ranked like soldiers, the survey stakes planted at exact intervals, string lines taut as harp strings. Order was his religion. He'd taught it to her before he'd taught her to swim — *a sloppy camp is a sloppy mind, Paloma* — and she had spent ten years building deliberately sloppy camps just to spite him.

This camp was not sloppy. This camp was *nothing*.

She stood at the tree line with the notebook waterproofed inside her jacket and let the silence come at her in layers. No generator hum. No radio chatter. No smell of diesel or coffee or the sweet rot of a latrine dug too close to the water table. The tents were gone. Not collapsed, not shredded by weather — gone. The ground where they'd stood was pale rectangles of compressed earth, four of them, arranged in the neat geometry she would have known anywhere.

Someone had swept this place like a floor.

"Stay behind me," she told Ilsa, though her voice came out thinner than she intended. The girl had barely spoken since the landing, since the boatman Marta had pushed off downstream alone rather than go one meter further upriver, and Paloma couldn't blame her. The river below Landfall was honest about what it wanted to do to you. This terrace was lying about something, and lies were worse.

They crossed the open ground fast, low, the way you cross ground that might be watched, and Paloma made herself look at everything because looking was the only thing she could still do for him.

The fire pit had been burned hot and then raked. She crouched at its edge and sifted ash between her fingers: metal fragments too melted to identify, glass fused to slag, and — her stomach tightened — the warped spine of what had been a field binder, its cover stamped with the university's crest half-legible under the char. He'd burned his own records. Or someone had burned them for him and made him watch.

The latrine trench had been filled and capped with fresh-cut sod, which took hours, which meant patience, which meant professionals. The cache pit where any expedition kept its reserve food and fuel had been dug out, lined with new plastic sheeting, and left empty — not looted, *emptied*, the way you empty a site when you want whoever comes next to know there is nothing here, there was never anything here, stop looking.

But they'd missed things. They always missed things, because men who sweep camps are men who work from checklists, and checklists don't know rivers.

Paloma found the first miss within ten minutes: a tent stake driven deep into the gravel bar's edge, university-stamped, overlooked because the grass had grown up around it. The second was better. Behind the stone that had served as the cook's prep table, wedged in a crack, a tin cup with a dent she recognized — her father had carried that dented cup on every expedition since she was nine years old, had drunk his morning coffee from it while she pretended to sleep in her bag and listened to him murmur coordinates to himself like prayers.

She held the cup and did not cry. Something past crying had opened in her chest, something colder and more useful.

"They didn't leave," Ilsa said behind her. "They were taken."

Paloma turned. The girl was standing over the pale rectangle where the fourth tent had been, and her face had gone the color of the gravel.

"How do you know?"

"Because I watched them pack it." Ilsa's voice was flat, reciting. "I've been up there." She pointed at the scree slope above the terrace, where a dark seam showed between two boulders. "Eleven days."

* * *

She'd hidden the night the men came, Ilsa said, sitting with her knees pulled up on the cold stones of the old fire ring while Paloma built a small smokeless fire in a hollow they'd lined with wet earth. Eleven days on granola bars and meltwater, eleven days watching the terrace through a gap in the rocks, waiting for a rescue that kept not coming.

"I heard the barge first. After midnight. No running lights." Ilsa wrapped her arms around her knees. "Dr. Whitlock heard it too. He came out of his tent with the site ledger under his arm and he told us — me and Tomas and Dr. Okafor — he said, *whatever happens, the assessment doesn't change.* Like he knew. Like he'd been expecting them."

Paloma fed a twig to the fire and said nothing. Her father's voice in another man's memory, saying the kind of stubborn thing he'd say. It hurt worse than silence.

"The men came up from the landing. Six of them. Consortium badges — the blue diamond, the one from the Kestrel Range concession. They weren't rough. That's what I keep thinking. They were polite. One of them shook Dr. Whitlock's hand." Ilsa laughed, a short ugly sound with nothing in it. "He shook his hand. And then he asked for the ledger, very reasonably, like he was asking to borrow a pencil. And my father—" she stopped, corrected herself, "—*Dr. Whitlock* said no. He said the ledger was already filed. He lied. He hadn't filed anything; we were three weeks from done. But he said it so calmly, and the man smiled, and then two others went into his tent and started packing it while everyone watched."

"Where were Tomas and Okafor?"

"Gone already. Two days before. They went downriver to Rivergate for resupply and never came back." Ilsa stared into the fire. "I thought maybe the barge had missed them. Now I think the barge *was* the point."

The men had worked through the night, Ilsa said. Methodical. Tents struck, equipment crated, the crates stenciled with consortium markings before they even left the terrace. Toward dawn, one of them had walked the perimeter with a rake and a can of accelerant, and the cook tent and the records box had gone up together in a fire so clean it barely smoked.

"And Dr. Whitlock?"

"He argued. All night he argued — not shouting, worse than shouting, *reasoning*, citing the heritage act, section numbers, like the law was a wall they couldn't walk through." Ilsa's voice cracked and steadied. "At the end, the one who'd shaken his hand — big man, gray beard, soft voice — he said something I only caught part of. He said, *the tribunal convenes in six weeks, Doctor, and the terrace will be a parking lot by spring either way. You can be a witness or you can be a footnote.* And then they put him on the barge. Upriver. He wasn't struggling. He looked—" she searched for it, "—he looked like a man being escorted somewhere he'd already decided to go."

Paloma fed the fire another twig and watched it eat.

Upriver. Past the head of navigation, into the country her father had mapped and she had refused to follow him into, toward whatever the consortium kept up there — a forward camp, a holding place, a hole to put inconvenient doctors in until the permits renewed and the bones could be bulldozed legally.

"You should have gone with them," she said quietly. "You'd be safer."

"With them?" Ilsa almost smiled. "I'm twenty-three. I'm a second-year field tech. If I'd gotten on that barge, I'd be a signature on a missing-persons form by now, same as Tomas and Okafor probably are. Out here I'm evidence." She looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed but level. "You're Paloma Whitlock. You're the guide the university sent. Which means somebody finally came. So I'm going to tell you everything I saw, and everything Dr. Whitlock found, and you're going to get it downriver to people who can use it. Aren't you."

It wasn't a question, and Paloma didn't treat it as one. She took out the waterproof notebook — her father's, fished from the rapids, its final pages torn away — and laid it on the stone between them.

"This was in the river below Landfall. His last entries are gone."

Ilsa went still. Then, slowly: "He tore them out himself. The last morning. I saw him doing it at breakfast — he was copying something, writing fast, and then he tore the pages out and folded them small and put them somewhere inside his coat, and he wouldn't say what. He just said, *if anything happens to me, the ledger isn't the only copy.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere upvalley, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhale, and Paloma felt the cold thing in her chest turn over and open its eyes.

Her father had known. Had copied the ledger, torn the pages free, and mailed them — or hidden them — or done whatever careful, infuriating, methodical thing a careful, infuriating, methodical man does when he expects to disappear. And the men who took him had searched his tent and burned his records and still hadn't found them, because they were looking for a ledger, and he had sent them a decoy.

"He always said a sloppy mind loses things," Paloma said, mostly to herself. "He never once said what a tidy one hides."

Isla frowned. "What does that mean?"

"It means he wanted them found." Paloma closed the notebook and stood, looking upriver, where the valley narrowed and the water went loud and white over stones, and beyond it the range stood up against the sky with snow already dusting the high shoulders — weeks early, the boatmen had said, weeks early. "Not by them. By whoever came next. He just couldn't know it would be me."

She looked at the scree slope, at the dark seam where a twenty-three-year-old had lived for eleven days on granola bars and stubbornness, and made the decision the way her father would have — completely, immediately, and without asking anyone's permission.

"We're not going downriver," she said. "We're going up. Tonight, while the light holds. Because whatever he hid, he hid it ahead of us, and the first snows aren't waiting, and neither am I."

Ilsa followed her gaze to the mountains, and Paloma saw the fear arrive in the girl's face and stay there — and saw her nod anyway.

Above the terrace, the wind shifted, and for a moment it carried something that did not belong to the valley: the faint, wrong smell of woodsmoke from a fire that wasn't theirs.

Someone else was on the river.
