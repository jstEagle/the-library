## 5. Ilsa's Testimony

The girl — Ilsa, she'd said, Ilsa Vann, junior field tech, second season with the survey — wouldn't come down from the tree line until Paloma banked the fire and stood back with her hands visible.

Smart, Paloma thought. Whoever had swept this camp had done it like men who expected witnesses.

Ilsa came the last fifty meters bent double, using the alder for cover even though there was nothing left to hide from. She was maybe twenty-three, all elbows and sunburn, wearing a rain shell two sizes too big with the sleeves rolled six times. Her eyes went to Paloma's face, then past her shoulder to the burned camp below, and something in her expression collapsed and reassembled itself twice before it settled into a kind of grim arithmetic.

"You're Whitlock," she said. "The daughter."

"I'm the guide the university sent." Paloma kept her voice level. "You radioed?"

"Three days ago. From the ridge, on a handheld I wasn't supposed to have." Ilsa crouched at the edge of the clearing, not entering it, as if the raked gravel were still hot. "Nobody answered. So I stayed up there and watched them finish."

"Watched who?"

"Four men. Consortium badges on their jackets — Meridian Consolidated, the blue chevron. They came upriver in the night on that barge, the one that doesn't run after dark. They spent nine hours here." She said it flatly, reciting, the way people recite things they've made themselves stop feeling. "They didn't steal anything worth stealing. They took paper. Ledgers, sample bags, the heritage assessment drafts, my supervisor's correspondence file. Then they burned what wouldn't carry and raked the ash into the fire pit and screened the pit through a flour sieve so there'd be no fragments with print on them." She looked at Paloma directly for the first time. "Whoever they are, they've done this before."

Paloma felt the cold arrive in her hands first, then her chest. She'd seen looted sites before — careless, greedy, opportunistic. This was different. This was hygiene.

"Where's the crew?" she asked, though she already knew the shape of the answer. "Where's my father?"

Ilsa's jaw worked. "They took him upriver. Dr. Whitlock and the site ledger. The rest of us they put on the barge going down — me and Tomas and the cook. But the current was wrong for loading at the landing, too shallow since the drought, so we beached below the bend to wait for the water." She paused. "I ran. I'm not proud of it. I told the others to run too, but Tomas has a mother in Landfall he sends money to, and the cook said nobody bothers the kitchen staff. So it was just me in the brush, and by the time I worked up the nerve to go back for the satellite phone, they'd found it and smashed it."

"You did the right thing running."

"I did the scared thing." Ilsa said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "It happened to be right."

* * *

They made camp upstream of the ruin, on a gravel bar with good sightlines and two escape routes, because Paloma had learned long ago that you never sleep in a place someone bothered to erase. She got a stove going behind a windbreak of driftwood, and Ilsa ate like someone who'd been living on fear and berries for three days — methodically, everything, no wasted motion.

Then Paloma laid her father's waterproof notebook on the crate between them, open to the torn stubs where the final pages had been ripped out.

Ilsa went very still.

"That's his," she said. "He carried it in an inner pocket. He used to say if the camp burned, the notebook walked out on its own legs."

"It did. Someone caught up with its legs." Paloma turned it toward her. "These pages are gone. Whatever he wrote last is gone. You were his junior tech. What was he writing?"

Ilsa set down her spoon. For a long moment she looked at the fire instead of at Paloma, and when she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, more careful, the voice of someone testifying rather than talking.

"You should understand something about your father first. The way the crew talked about him, I thought he'd be arrogant. Famous man, famous name, all those expeditions. But he wasn't. He was careful. Obsessively careful. He made us bag and tag everything twice. He photographed every unit before and after close. He said — I wrote it down because it struck me — he said, 'The river keeps better records than we do, but the river can't testify.'"

Paloma's throat tightened. That was him. That was exactly him, down to the cadence, and hearing it secondhand from a stranger hurt worse than any memory.

"The survey was supposed to take eleven days," Ilsa went on. "Corridor mapping for the access road, some core samples on the terrace above the oxbow, standard pre-clearance work. Meridian holds the concession option on the whole upper basin. The permits renew in the spring, and renewal requires a final heritage clearance — a signed statement that no significant cultural materials will be affected. Your father's signature was the last one needed. Everyone else had already signed."

"And?"

"And on day six we opened Unit 4 on the terrace." Ilsa's hands had started moving as she talked, shaping the ground in the air between them, and Paloma realized the girl was seeing it, really seeing it, the way diggers do. "Two meters down we hit a stain layer. Then charcoal. Then — " she stopped, swallowed — "then small bones. Infant and child burials, flexed positions, wrapped in bark. And beads. Trade beads, glass, and older stuff beneath them — shell, copper. Dr. Whitlock called the whole crew over and made us stand around the unit in silence for a full minute. He said, 'This is a cemetery. This is generations. This is the oldest continuous occupation site anyone has ever documented on this river.' He was shaking. Not scared-shaking. Something else."

Paloma closed her eyes. She could see him doing it — could see the exact angle of his shoulders, the way he'd push his glasses up with the back of his wrist when his hands weren't steady enough to trust.

"What did he do next?"

"He stopped the survey. Flagged the whole terrace as protected under the interim provisions — that's automatic once human remains are confirmed, it freezes all ground disturbance pending tribunal review. He started drafting the heritage assessment that same night. The real one." Ilsa's mouth twisted. "And that's when the calls started."

"Calls from whom?"

"A man named Corben. Project director for Meridian's basin development. First call was friendly. Very friendly. He congratulated Dr. Whitlock on the find, said how important rigorous science was, said Meridian wanted to fund a proper excavation — a full season, unlimited budget, a real team. All very generous." She shrugged. "Dr. Whitlock thanked him and said the terrace would need to be documented before any development timeline could be discussed, and that documentation would take years, and the permit renewal couldn't proceed in the meantime. The second call was less friendly. By the fourth call, Corben was saying words like 'inaccuracy' and 'professional liability' and asking whether Dr. Whitlock was sure about the depth of the remains. Whether they might not be, say, nineteenth-century. Whether a flexible reading of the stratigraphy might place the burials outside the protected period."

"Which would make them legally irrelevant."

"Which would make them legally irrelevant," Ilsa agreed. "Except they're not. Dr. Whitlock ran the dating himself — charcoal association, bead typology, soil micromorphology he sent out on the resupply barge. The deepest layer is four hundred years old, minimum. Maybe older. Those are somebody's ancestors under that terrace. River people. The same families that still fish the oxbow." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "Meridian isn't just trying to build a road. They're trying to build a road through a graveyard and call it empty land. And they needed your father to sign the paper that made it empty."

Paloma stared into the fire. Ten years of lowland contract digs, of telling herself she'd chosen the small honest work over his grand expeditions — and here was the proof that the difference between them had never been ambition. It had been this. A hole in the ground full of small bones, and a pen, and what a man does when someone offers him money to unsee it.

"He refused," she said. It wasn't a question.

"He refused." Ilsa almost smiled. "Politely at first. Then not politely. On the last night, he told us at dinner — the whole crew — that he'd finished the assessment and filed a copy with the tribunal registry by satlink, and that if anything happened to him or the camp, the record existed and would speak for itself. He said it looking at the river, not at us. Like he knew who was listening."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the water, a loon called, long and descending, and Paloma thought: the river keeps better records than we do.

"But the registry copy needs corroboration," she said slowly, working it through. "A filing alone won't void a concession. The tribunal will want the original ledger, the field notes, chain of custody. Which is why the fixers took the ledger and took him — not to kill him. To hold him. A missing lead archaeologist who can be produced later, cooperative, signing whatever revised assessment they put in front of him. Dead men can't sign. Disappeared ones can, eventually."

Isla flinched at the word *fixers*. "You think he's alive, then."

"I think dead men make martyrs and martyrs make hearings, and hearings are the one thing Meridian can't afford before spring." Paloma heard her own voice go cold and flat, the guide's voice, the voice that read water. "They don't need him dead. They need him tired. And they need the physical evidence gone, because a filing without the ledger is just an allegation, and allegations lose to lawyers every day of the week."

"So we get the ledger back."

"We get the ledger back." Paloma turned the ruined notebook over in her hands. "And we figure out what he tore out of this, because my father doesn't tear pages out by accident. If he removed them, he removed them for someone to find."

* * *

Later, while Ilsa slept in shifts the way frightened people do — light, armed with a boat hook, facing the water — Paloma sat up with the notebook and let herself feel it, finally, fully: not grief exactly, but its engine, fury. The consortium hadn't just taken her father. They'd taken the terrace, the bones, the four hundred years of river families who'd buried their children in bark wrappings above the oxbow, and they'd done it all with sieves and rakes and a flour sifter, professionally, the way you clean a site you intend to deny existed.

She had spent a decade telling herself his world was vanity — headlines, funding galas, rivers named after sponsors. She had built her whole life on the assumption that walking away from him meant walking away from compromise.

But he hadn't compromised. He'd stood in a hole full of ancestral bones and said *no* to a project director with a billion-dollar timeline, knowing exactly what men like that do to obstacles, and he'd mailed the truth to a tribunal registry anyway.

Her eyes stung. She let them, briefly, then wiped them on her sleeve and got out the map case.

Because there was one more thing Ilsa had said, half-asleep, an hour ago, that Paloma couldn't stop turning over:

*"He told the cook to send his notes downriver on the resupply barge. Double-wrapped, addressed to himself. He said, 'If I can't carry it out, it'll wait for me at Rivergate.'"*

Rivergate Station. The outpost with the unopened crates and the evasive keeper and the barge that moved at night.

Her father had mailed the evidence ahead of them — and somewhere between here and Rivergate, four men with blue chevrons were making sure nothing waited for anyone.

Paloma checked the sky. Low cloud building in the northwest, smelling of snow too early in the season. Rising water in the river, brown and fast.

Whatever window existed, it was closing. She woke Ilsa with a hand on her shoulder.

"Get your boots on," she said. "We're going upriver."
