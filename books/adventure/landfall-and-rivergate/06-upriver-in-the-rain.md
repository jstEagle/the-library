## 6. Upriver in the Rain

The rain started an hour after we left the landing, and it did not stop.

It came down in that gray, patient way of mountain rain — not a storm but a condition, something the river simply lived inside. Within two hours the Landfall had risen a hand's width against the gauge rock my father had drilled and marked himself, twenty years ago, when he still believed the river would always be there to measure. The water had gone from green to the color of steeped tea, carrying pine needles and slurry and one whole birch trunk that spun past us like a slow torpedo.

"Too high," Ilsa said from the bow. She said it the way you'd report weather to someone who already knew.

"It's always too high." I set the pole against the bottom and pushed. "That's why nobody comes up here."

We were in the survey camp's spare freighter — a flat-bottomed work skiff, sixteen feet of riveted aluminum that handled like a church pew. My father's boat. His name was stenciled on the transom under a coat of someone else's gray paint, and I could feel it there every time my hand brushed the gunwale, the way you can feel a scar through a shirt sleeve.

Poling is not rowing and it is not paddling. It is argument. You plant the pole, lean your whole weight into the current's shoulder, and hold the conversation until the river gets bored. It is also the only honest way to move upstream through water this shallow and this fast, and it is brutal work. By midday my palms were raw in both gloves and my lower back had filed a formal complaint. Ilsa took turns at the bow pole, and she was better at it than I expected — small, steady, no wasted motion, the economy of someone who had spent her life being underestimated by large men.

She hadn't talked much since the camp. That was fine. There was a particular kind of talking a witness does in the first day after, spilling everything to anyone, and I wanted her testimony intact for people whose job was listening — a tribunal, a magistrate, anyone but me. So we worked in silence broken only by the pole knocking stone and the rain ticking on the water around us.

* * *

We made the first bend by late afternoon and camped on a gravel shelf under an overhang my father had called the Smokehouse, because the soot of some trapper's fire fifty years gone still blackened the ceiling stone. I got a tarp rigged, a stove lit, tea going. Ilsa sat with her knees drawn up, watching the river rise past the willows.

"They'll know we're coming," she said finally. "Voss's men. They left trackers."

"I'm counting on them knowing."

She looked at me then, and there was something new in her face — not fear exactly. Recalculation. "You want them to come?"

"I want them to be where I can see them." I fed another stick into the stove. "A man hunting you in open country picks the ground. A man who thinks he's ambushing you walks into yours. It's the same principle as the rapids. You don't fight the current. You read it, and you let it do the work."

"And if they're better at reading than you are?"

I almost smiled. "Then I'll learn something."

She didn't laugh, but something in her shoulders came down half an inch, and I understood that was the whole point of saying it out loud. Not tactics. Permission to be afraid without stopping.

We ate rice and smoked fish from the station keeper's stores — bought at triple price, from a man who couldn't look me in the eye, which told me more than his answers had. Ilsa slept first. I sat up with my father's notebook under the stove light, reading pages I'd already read four times, looking for what wasn't there.

The final entries were careful, professional, and wrong. Depth soundings along the terrace reach. Sediment notes. And then, in the last legible line before the torn pages: *Stratigraphy does not support the consortium's site assessment. Recommend full—*

Full what. Full review. Full excavation. Full stop. The rest was gone, ripped out clean, and every hour I turned the absence over in my hands like a stone with a hollow side.

He'd found them. The bones. The graves. Generations of river people laid into that terrace above the flood line, the ones the mining company needed never to have existed, because a burial ground meant heritage protection, and heritage protection meant the permits didn't renew, and the permits were worth more than any of us. He'd found them, and then men with consortium badges had burned his camp, taken his ledger, and taken him upriver.

And now I was following the same water they'd used to carry him away.

* * *

The second day the river fought us properly.

Above the second bend the valley narrows — the Rivergate proper, where the walls close in and the channel braids through gravel shoals that shift with every flood. In low water it's a maze. In rising water it's a trap. We poled, dragged, lined the boat through the worst shallows with ropes and swearing, and lost two hours to a chute I misread and had to back out of with the current shoving the hull sideways like a hand on the stern.

Ilsa held the line fast without being told, boots braced, rope wrapped twice around her forearm, and when we finally won the boat into slack water above the shoal she said nothing about it, and neither did I, and that felt like the beginning of trust — the kind you build out of shared labor instead of words.

It was mid-afternoon, rain thickening again, when I saw the cairn.

A stack of stones on the right-bank bluff, waist-high, fresh — stones still dark with unweathered faces, no lichen, no moss. Someone had built it within the last week. Below it, nailed to a spruce at eye level, a strip of orange flagging tape.

"That's theirs," Ilsa said quietly. "Route marking. They mark their supply lines."

I stood in the boat a long moment, pole planted, letting the current press against me while I looked at the tape. Orange, industrial, the kind sold by the case. The consortium's color. They'd been running gear up this channel regularly enough to need trail markers, which meant a forward camp, which meant weight, which meant they'd expected to stay.

Or they'd expected him to stay.

"We're close," I said.

"How close?"

"Half a day. Maybe less, if the water lets us cheat."

Ilsa looked upriver, where the valley bent away behind a wall of mist, and I watched her decide something. When she spoke again her voice had changed — flatter, harder, the voice of the woman who had hidden in a root cellar for three days while men burned her camp.

"Good."

* * *

We found the forward camp at dusk on the third day, and it was wrong in every direction.

First, the smoke. Woodsmoke hanging in the spruce, sweet and recent, when the clearing ahead should have been cold. Then the boats — two aluminum freighters pulled up on the bank, uncovered, taking rain. Nobody leaves boats uncovered unless they left in a hurry or intended to come straight back. Then the clearing itself: tents standing, tarps rigged, a gear line strung between trees with wet clothing still clipped to it, turning slowly in the wind like a row of hanged men.

Nobody home. Everybody coming back.

"Hold here." I backed water and put us into the eddy behind a downed spruce, thirty meters short of the bank. "We look first. Always."

Ilsa nodded. Her hands had gone white on the gunwale.

We came ashore downstream, quiet, and I went in first with the hatchet from the tool kit — not subtle, but I'd rather have it and not need it. The camp opened up around us: three large expedition tents, a cook fly, crates stacked under a tarp. Consortium stencils on the crates. RIVERGATE MINERALS — SURVEY DIVISION, the letters crisp and official, the division of the company that officially did not operate above the rapids.

The stove under the cook fly was warm. Not cooling-warm. Warm-warm. I put my palm near the flue and felt heat still climbing off it, and my stomach dropped through the mud of the clearing, because that meant minutes, not hours. Somebody had fed that stove and walked away from it while the kettle — there was a kettle on the plate, still faintly ticking as the metal contracted — was barely cool.

"They left in a rush," Ilsa whispered.

"No." I looked around again, slower. The gear line. The wet clothes. Two boats uncovered. A stove still warm. "They left in a panic. Rush is organized. This is—" I stopped, because I'd seen it before, once, on a salvage dig in the lowlands when a crew hit something in the spoil heap they weren't supposed to find and the company man shut the site down in forty minutes. Fear looks like this. Not scattered. Abandoned mid-sentence.

We searched anyway, fast and systematic, because evidence doesn't care whose fear made it. Sleeping bags rolled or crammed. Food stores half-packed, a crate of cans left open. And in the lead tent — the big one, the command tent — a table with maps weighted by stones, and on the map, the terrace reach circled in grease pencil, with grid squares drawn over it and annotations in a hand I recognized.

My father's handwriting. On their map.

They'd made him show them. That was the thought that arrived, cold and complete: they hadn't just taken him upriver. They'd sat him at this table in the rain and made him draw the grids, mark the terraces, tell them exactly what he'd found and where, so they could go dig it out themselves — or bury it deeper. The grease pencil circles were his. The annotations were his. And beside one square, in his cramped engineer's script: *intact — do not disturb — legal significance.*

Legal significance. He was fighting them with paperwork even as their prisoner. Even terrified, the old man's instinct was documentation. Something in my chest cracked along an old seam, and I let it, and kept working.

Under the table's edge, wedged where it had slid during whatever hurried departure, I found the satellite phone.

Dropped, not packed. A ruggedized handset, consortium asset tag on the back, screen cracked but lit. I thumbed it awake. Signal bars: none — we were deep in the rain shadow of the ridge, and the sky was doing its best to keep it that way. But the call log was there, and the last outgoing call was timestamped nineteen hours ago. The last incoming, fourteen hours ago. After that, nothing. A phone this important doesn't get left behind by accident.

Which meant whoever carried it had left in such a state they'd dropped it and not noticed. Or hadn't cared. Or—

"Paloma." Ilsa was at the clearing's edge, very still, pointing at the mud of the trail leading north from camp. "Look."

Boot prints. Many. Heading upriver, toward the terrace, spaced wide — moving fast. And overprinting them, fresher, darker in the wet clay, a smaller set of prints heading the same direction. Two sets. Maybe three. Hours apart at most.

And below the trail, half-filled with rainwater, a single print pointed back the way we'd come. Toward Rivergate. Toward us.

Someone had gone downriver. Recently. Someone who would walk into our camp tonight or tomorrow and find a warm stove of our own, and a boat, and a trail of our own making leading straight to where we stood.

I felt the shape of the situation turn over, the way a river turns a boat. For six days I had thought of myself as the hunter — reading sign, following current, closing distance. Standing in their camp with their warm stove breathing on my face, I understood the arithmetic had changed. They knew this country from the air and the ledger book. We knew it from the water and the game trails. But they had radios, and we had a dead phone, and they had numbers, and we had two pairs of hands, and somewhere ahead of all of us, on a terrace above the flood line, lay the only thing in this valley that mattered.

"They're pulling everything to the terrace," I said. "Whatever they found there scared them enough to abandon this. Or whatever my father told them did."

"Then we follow?"

I looked at the boot prints filling with rain, at the stove ticking itself cold, at the map with my father's handwriting holding the line the only way he knew how.

"We follow," I said. "But not on their trail. There's a route on the far bank — game trail, high line. My father mapped it. If they're watching the river path, we'll come at the terrace from above, from the side they'll think is impossible."

Ilsa stared at me. "In this rain? On a trail nobody's cut in twenty years?"

"He cut it," I said, and heard, for the first time since the letter arrived, no bitterness in my own voice at all. "It'll hold."

We stripped the camp of anything useful — fuel, food, dry socks, a coil of good rope, a first-aid tin — and left the rest standing exactly as we'd found it. Let them wonder. Let them count what was missing and guess at who.

We crossed in the last gray light, poling hard against a river that had risen another foot since noon, and the rain came down on us like it intended to go on forever, and maybe it did. Behind us the consortium's stove ticked cold in its empty camp. Ahead, somewhere in the dark above the far bank, a trail waited that only two people alive had ever walked — and one of them had taught it to the other without ever meaning to.

At midnight, sheltering under a cutbank with the boat tied and the phone dead in my pocket, I checked the call log one last time.

There was a new entry. An incoming call, missed, timestamped eleven minutes ago.

From a number the log labeled with a single word:

**TERRACE.**
