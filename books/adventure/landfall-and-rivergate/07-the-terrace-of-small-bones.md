## 7. The Terrace of Small Bones

The rain quit an hour before dawn, and Paloma woke to a silence so complete it felt like a held breath. She lay in the lee of the overturned survey skiff, listening to the river work the gravel bar below, and understood that today was the day. Not because any sign told her so — because the water was dropping, and dropping water meant the consortium's boats could run again, and boats meant men with badges and bolt cutters and the patience of professionals.

She'd have one day on the terrace. Maybe less.

Ilsa was already awake, sitting with her knees drawn up, watching the light come gray into the eastern gorge. She'd slept badly; Paloma had heard her turning all night, and neither of them had mentioned the shapes they'd both seen across the water at dusk — the far bank's cutbank slumping where it shouldn't slump, as if something heavy had been dragged there and covered.

"Show me again," Ilsa said. "What he found."

Paloma took out the waterproof notebook. The final pages were gone, torn close to the spine, but the surviving pages held his hand: contour lines in confident strokes, depth notations, a sketch of the terrace itself rendered in cross-section like a cathedral drawn by someone who loved cathedrals. His handwriting was small and exact. She had spent ten years refusing to look at that handwriting, and now she couldn't stop looking at it.

"He marked this bench," she said, tapping the page. "Third terrace above the flood line, south-facing, above the worst of the ice scour. He wouldn't have flagged it unless the stratigraphy was clean. Undisturbed." She closed the notebook. "Which means what's under us has been sitting here since before anyone drew a map of this river."

They climbed.

* * *

The terrace announced itself the way good sites always did — quietly, to anyone who knew how to listen. A change in the ground cover first: the moss thicker, darker, fed by centuries of accumulated soil instead of raw flood silt. Then the stones. Not cobbles, not river-throw, but placed stones, half-swallowed by earth, running in a shallow arc along the bench's lip like the ribs of something enormous sleeping just under the skin of the world.

Paloma stood at the edge of it and felt her anger change key.

She had come upriver furious — furious at the burned camp, at the raked ground, at the ledger stolen from under Ilsa's hiding place, at her father for whatever silence had let men with consortium badges walk him off his own site. That fury was still there. But standing over the arc of stones, she felt something older and larger move underneath it, the way bedrock moves under a landslide. This wasn't about her father. It had never been about her father. It was about whoever had carried their dead up this bench, generation after generation, when carrying them must have cost everything — the climb, the cold, the weight of grief made physical — because this place mattered enough to spend that.

And the consortium wanted it erased so a permit could renew.

"Start here," she said, kneeling at the arc's midpoint. "We document everything. Photographs, drawings, measurements. We don't lift anything we don't have to. We're not excavating — we're *witnessing*. There's a difference, and the tribunal will know the difference."

Ilsa nodded and got out the camera, the one Paloma had taken from the forward camp along with the satellite phone. Its memory card had photos already on it — Voss's, presumably, or one of the other fixers'. Paloma hadn't looked yet. There hadn't been time, and some part of her had been afraid of what she'd find.

Now she slid the card into her own camera and scrolled.

The first images were logistics: boats, crates, the forward camp's cook tent. Then, abruptly, the terrace — shot from three angles, methodical, competent, the framing of a man who knew documentation protocol. And in the fourth image, exposed where frost heave and the consortium's own hurried grading had peeled back the turf: bone. Small bones. An infant's cradle burial, flexed, wrapped in what the photo showed as stained matting, lying against a copper ornament gone green with age.

Paloma sat back on her heels.

Voss had seen this. Voss had photographed it, which meant he'd been ordered to destroy it or ignore it, and instead he'd knelt down in the mud and documented it like an archaeologist. She thought of the man's flat voice on the satellite phone, the careful neutrality of his terms, and revised her estimate of him upward by a degree she didn't yet trust.

"He photographed them," Ilsa said softly. "Why would he—"

"Because some people can't help themselves," Paloma said. "Doesn't make him innocent. Makes him useful." She copied the images to her second card, then handed the original back. "Keep that somewhere he can't find it if he comes looking."

Then she picked up her trowel, and went to work.

* * *

A day is nothing. Every archaeologist knows it. A proper excavation of a cemetery terrace — even a test program, even a salvage documentation — wants weeks: grid stakes, datum lines, screens, bags, labels, the patient arithmetic of layers. What Paloma did that day was violence of a different kind, controlled and necessary, and she hated every centimeter of it even as she made it speak.

She opened three units.

The first was at the stone arc, forty centimeters square, and it came down through black organic soil into a lens of packed ochre within the first hour. Ochre meant intention. Ochre meant ceremony. Beneath it, without her even needing to ask the ground, lay a burial: an adult, extended, hands arranged at the sides, the skeleton stained rust-red from the pigment that had once soaked the wrapping. Around the skull, a ring of pierced shells — river shells, gathered from gravel bars exactly like the one below them, carried up here one handful at a time.

"They lived on this river," Ilsa whispered. She was holding the camera, and her hands were shaking. "The shells are from right here."

"Generations," Paloma said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Look at the depth intervals between these two. That's not one event. That's a *practice*. Families coming back to the same bench, century after century, burying their dead above the flood line where the water could never take them back." She sat in the mud beside the unit, trowel forgotten, and looked out over the gorge. "You don't do that unless this is yours. Not claimed. Not permitted. *Yours.*"

The second unit found the children.

It was Ilsa who saw the first one — a scatter of tiny vertebrae in the profile wall, and Paloma had to talk her through it while the younger woman cried without noise, the way you learn to cry on expeditions, silently, efficiently, because noise costs heat and heat costs judgment. Cradle burials. Infants, wrapped, tucked into the earth with small gifts: a carved bird of antler. A bead of trade glass so old its color had gone the deep violet of very early contact. One child buried with a fishhook still in its small fist, as if someone had hoped the fishing would continue on the other side of whatever door death was.

Paloma documented each one. Photographs, scale bars, measured sketches, provenience notes in her own cramped field hand — and beneath her notes, unbidden, her father's phrasing kept surfacing in her head, his old teaching voice: *record what you see, not what you feel, and the feeling will keep.* She had sworn for a decade never to think in his sentences. Now they were the only sentences that fit.

By midday the pattern was undeniable, and Paloma made herself say it aloud, for the recorder, for the tribunal, for the record that might outlive all of them:

"Burial terrace, multi-generational, minimum four distinct occupation horizons visible in section. Estuarine and riverine shell inclusions consistent with local procurement. Material culture spanning pre-contact through early trade period. This is a recognized ancestral cemetery of the river peoples. Any resource extraction, grading, or ground disturbance on this bench constitutes desecration of a burial site. End notation."

Her voice didn't shake until the last word, and then it shook a lot.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon with the desperation of people racing a tide. Paloma opened the third unit at the terrace's eastern edge, where her father's sketch showed a possible cairn, and there it was: a stack of flat stones over a deeper interment, an elder, buried with a paddle laid across the body — a real paddle, lashed and bladed, preserved in the anaerobic soil as if set down yesterday. Someone had given their dead the means to travel. Up here, above the rapids, above Landfall, where the water killed boats for sport — someone had sent their grandfather onward with a boat in his hands.

Paloma knelt there a long time.

She thought about her father, twenty years ago, teaching her to read a section wall on some lowland contract dig, his hand warm on her shoulder, before the awards and the headlines and the slow estrangement that had calcified into a decade of silence. She thought about the torn pages of his notebook, and what kind of pressure tears pages out of a man's records. She thought about the concession maps she'd seen copies of, the neat polygons of the mining claim, and realized with a clarity that felt like cold water that the claim's boundary ran precisely along this bench — that somewhere in a consortium office, a lawyer had drawn a line around a cemetery and called it overburden.

That was the word. She'd seen it in the assessment draft Ilsa had memorized. *Overburden.* The material to be removed before the valuable thing underneath could be accessed.

Except here the valuable thing *was* the overburden. Generations of river people, filed as waste rock.

"Paloma." Ilsa's voice, sharp. "Across the river."

Paloma looked up. On the far bank, at the treeline, something moved — a glint, then another. Glass or optics. Two figures, maybe three, working downstream along the cutbank, unhurried, the way men move when they believe no one is watching them.

The water had dropped all afternoon. She'd known what that meant.

"We're done here," she said, and began backfilling the units with quick, careful hands, tamping the soil down, replacing the turf plugs, erasing their own work the way the consortium had tried to erase the site — but leaving the bones exactly where generations had put them, undisturbed, safe under her restored earth. Some excavations were also fortifications. She packed the documentation — cards, notes, the scale photos — into the waterproof bag with her father's notebook, cinched it, and slung it across her chest.

Ilsa was staring at the filled units, at the invisible graves. "That's it? We just leave them?"

"That's the whole point," Paloma said. "They were never ours to move. We came to prove they're here. Now everyone who matters knows." She looked east, gauging the light — three hours, maybe four, before dark, and the range beyond the gorge catching the first hard white glare of weather building high up. Snow in the peaks. Early, the way the station keeper had warned. "And now we get over that pass before the snow does."

They dropped off the terrace's north side onto the game trail — the faint, braided path her father's map marked in pencil with a single annotation she'd ignored for two days and now read three times: *Ridge route. Steep. Water at the col only after melt. Trust the trail, not your eyes.*

His handwriting again. His voice again, arriving exactly when she needed it, ten years late and right on time.

Behind them, across the river, the figures had stopped moving. One of them raised something to their face — binoculars, or a rifle scope, and at this distance it didn't matter which.

Paloma turned uphill and started climbing, Ilsa's breath loud behind her, the documentation bag tight against her spine like a second heart, and the first flakes came down just as the trail bent away from the river and into the trees — fat, slow, deliberate flakes, falling straight down through windless air, the way snow falls when it intends to keep falling for days.
