## 8. Fixers on the Ridge

The crow came first.

Not a real crow — Ilsa's word for it, hissed low from the alder screen where we'd been cataloguing the last of the terrace all morning. *Crow at ten o'clock.* I didn't ask what she meant until I followed her finger up the far bank and saw it: a glint of glass on the ridgeline opposite, where no glass had any business being. Binoculars catching the midday sun. Then a figure, brief as a struck match, dropping behind a granite shoulder.

I'd been so careful. We'd approached the terrace through standing water, worked under canopy, buried our fire ash in the river gravel every night. But you can't hide a day of excavation from anyone who knows what disturbed earth looks like from above. The trench was a scar. The screening frames were white flags. Everything we'd done to honor the dead had announced us to the living.

"Two," Ilsa said. "Maybe three. They've been there since before noon — I heard a radio squelch when the wind shifted."

My hands were steady. That surprised me. Somewhere over the past two days they had stopped being my father's daughter's hands, shaking with grief and fury, and become something else: a guide's hands, doing the next thing, and the thing after that. I looked at the ridge again and made myself count like a surveyor instead of feel like prey.

"They'll come down the scree chute," I said. "It's the only fast line off that shoulder. Forty minutes if they're good, an hour if they're cautious." I turned back to the terrace, to the small bones laid out under the birch bark sheets we'd cut, to the copper pendant I'd photographed forty times because I couldn't stop photographing it. "We have thirty."

"We can't just leave them." Ilsa's voice cracked on *them*. She'd spent yesterday learning to trowel around vertebrae with her breath held, and she'd crossed some line inside herself that I recognized because I'd crossed it too. These weren't artifacts anymore. They were people.

"We're not leaving them. We're leaving them exactly where they are, which is where they've been for four hundred years, and which is the one place the consortium can't touch without committing the crime in front of witnesses." I started rolling the documentation into its oilcloth sleeve — the photo log, the stratigraphy sketches, my father's notebook with its torn gut. "The bones are safe. The paper isn't. Paper is what they want, and paper is what walks out of here with us."

She stared at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic every digger eventually does: the site is not yours to save. The record is. Her jaw set, and she began packing.

* * *

We went north, because north was wrong.

Every instinct in me screamed for the river — downriver, toward Landfall, toward the tribunal, toward people. But the river was a corridor and corridors are where you get caught. My father's route notes, the ones I'd been reading by headlamp like scripture, had a line I'd initially skimmed past: *N of terrace, game trail climbs to bench system, then goat paths W toward the range. Not on any concession map. Found it chasing a wolverine in '09. Nobody hunts where nobody knows to look.*

Nobody hunts where nobody knows to look. He'd written it like a boast. It read now like a letter he'd mailed eleven years early, addressed to a daughter he hadn't met yet.

The game trail started behind a fallen spruce and was invisible until you were on it — a thread of compressed moss, a rub mark on bark at shoulder height, a switchback cut so old the trees had grown over the edges. Ilsa kept looking back. I didn't. Looking back is how you trip, and tripping is how you die on ground like this, and besides, I already knew what was behind us. Behind us was the terrace getting smaller, and above the terrace, sooner than my forty minutes, men in consortium gray.

We heard them before we saw them — not voices but the specific silence of people trying not to make noise, which carries its own signature if you've grown up around hunters. A jay stopped calling. That was the tell. The forest went professionally quiet along our backtrail, and I pulled Ilsa off the trail into a stand of young hemlock, and we crouched among the needles while three figures came down the switchbacks below us.

They were good. I'll give them that. They moved the way my father's best field men moved, weight low, rifle slung muzzle-down, eyes working the terrain in quadrants. Not soldiers — fixers, the kind the mining companies keep on retainer for exactly this, men whose job descriptions exist nowhere in writing. The lead one paused at the point where our trail left the game path, crouched, and touched the moss.

He was touching our prints. Fresh ones. Hours old at most.

I put my hand slowly over Ilsa's wrist because she'd gone rigid, and I could feel her pulse hammering against my palm like a bird in a box. Below us the lead man straightened, spoke two words I couldn't catch, and pointed — not up our trail but across the slope, west, cutting the angle. He thought we were running for the river. He thought everyone ran for the river.

Let him think it.

We held still for twenty minutes after they passed, long enough for the cold to soak through both knees, and then we climbed.

* * *

The bench system was everything his notes promised and nothing a map would show you: a staircase of flat shelves stacked up the mountainside, each one hidden from the last by rock ribs and krummholz, so that you could move a thousand vertical feet without once being skylined. He'd found this chasing a wolverine. Of course he had. My father had never once in his life taken the direct route when an interesting animal was involved, and every one of those detours had been folded into his maps like letters he never sent.

I climbed with the notes open in a plastic sleeve strapped to my thigh, checking his hand-drawn profiles against the actual mountain, and the eerie part — the part I didn't say aloud — was how well they matched. Eleven years of erosion and two brutal winters hadn't moved a boulder he'd marked. He'd drawn this country the way other men drew their children: accurately, obsessively, from memory.

"You knew these trails before this week," Ilsa said at the second bench, breathing hard, sweat freezing at her hairline. "You said you'd never been up here."

"I've never been up here." I coiled a length of cord, eyes on the slope below. "But I grew up reading his maps the way other kids read comics. I could draw the Rivergate passes from memory at age nine. It annoyed him. I think it also pleased him, and he never knew what to do about that."

"What happened between you?" She asked it plainly, the way she asked everything — no tact, all nerve. "Ten years. What does that even look like?"

I considered not answering. The honest answer was too big for a mountainside and too small for the truth: nothing happened. That was the whole catastrophe. No fight, no betrayal, just a man who loved rivers more than dinners and a girl who counted his absences until the counting hardened into a policy. He'd missed my graduation for a flood stage reading. I'd missed his sixtieth for a contract dig I could have rescheduled. Two people conducting a decade-long argument conducted entirely through silence, each waiting for the other to file the opening brief.

"He chose the work," I said finally. "And I decided that choosing the work was a verdict on me. It wasn't, probably. It was just who he was. But I was nineteen and nineteen-year-olds don't do *probably*." I stood, shouldered the pack. "Ask me again when we're somewhere warm. And ask him. He owes you the answer more than me."

Above us the trees gave out. Above the trees, the range.

* * *

We made the high bench at midafternoon, and from there the world opened like a book falling open. South and east, the whole drainage lay exposed — the silver thread of the river, the dark smear of the terrace's forest, and beyond it, tiny with distance, the smoke of the consortium's forward camp. Three columns now. They'd reinforced overnight. Somebody with authority had decided we mattered.

"Paloma." Ilsa's voice had gone flat, which I'd learned meant frightened. "Smoke. There — not their camp. Our side of the valley."

I found it. A thin gray plume rising from the treeline near the base of the scree chute, maybe two kilometers south and eight hundred feet below. A second team. They'd split — one group on our backtrail, one sweeping the flank, and between them a net closing on the only two directions a sane person would run.

Except we weren't running those directions. We were standing on a shelf of stone that existed on exactly one map in the world, and the man who drew it was either dead or in a consortium truck, and either way he wasn't talking.

"There," I said, and pointed west, where the bench narrowed into a ramp of broken granite climbing toward a col I'd memorized from a profile sketch labeled, in his cramped engineer's hand, *GOAT ROUTE — BAD WEATHER HOLE BEYOND — DO NOT ATTEMPT IN WIND.*

"How bad?"

"Bad enough that he wrote a warning on his own map, and he once poled a boat through Landfall at flood stage because he wanted the data." I checked the sky. High cirrus, building from the southwest. Weather coming, maybe tonight, maybe tomorrow. Weather was a threat and weather was a door — it would slow the fixers faster than it slowed us, because they had radios and timetables and bosses, and we had nothing but the mountain's permission. "We cross the col before the wind comes up. After that, we're ghosts."

We climbed. The ramp was everything *bad* promised — frost-shattered blocks that tilted underfoot, exposure on the right that went down and down to a talus field the color of old bone. I went first, testing each hold, passing the good lines back to Ilsa in a vocabulary we'd invented on the river: *solid, sketchy, don't trust it, trust it.* Fear sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, and I let it sit, because fear sharpens. Fear was telling me exactly where my feet were. Fear was doing its job.

Halfway up the ramp I heard it — a sound I'd know anywhere, the flat crack of a radio transmission bouncing off rock, distorted to gibberish but unmistakable in shape. Then, fainter, a human voice shouting something with the cadence of a command.

They'd seen us. Or seen movement, which amounted to the same thing. I glanced back once — allowed myself one glance — and picked out two figures on the lower bench, absurdly small, gesturing. One raised binoculars. Even at this distance I could feel the lens finding me, framing me, measuring my pace against their options.

I turned around and climbed faster.

Here is the thing I understood in that hour, moving over stone with my lungs burning and my father's route notes pressed against my thigh: I had spent ten years being angry at him for the wrong reason. I'd been angry that he loved the work more than us. The truth, climbing toward the col with men hunting me, was worse and better — he hadn't loved the work *more*. He'd loved it *differently*, in a language I refused to learn until it was the only language that could keep me alive. Every skill saving my life today was his. The route was his. The habit of counting escape lines before danger arrived was his. I was his map, walking.

At the top of the ramp the col opened ahead: a notch in the ridge with wind screaming through it already, early, ahead of schedule, streaming snow-flecks off the crest like smoke from a chimney. Beyond it, the notes promised a sheltered hollow — *bad weather hole* — and beyond that, the descent country, and beyond that, Rivergate, and a dead-letter crate, and whatever my father had mailed himself in the last days of his freedom.

Ilsa came up beside me, gray-lipped, and shouted over the wind: "What if they follow us onto the col?"

"They won't." I nodded at the sky, where the cirrus had thickened to slate and the light had gone the color of pewter. "Look at that. They're professionals. Professionals don't cross a col in building weather for two women they can find later." I pulled my hood up and stepped into the wind. "And if I'm wrong about that, we'll hear them coming, and the col will do the rest."

The crossing took forty minutes that felt like forty years. On the far side, in the sudden hush of the lee, we dropped into the hollow his map had promised — a wind scoop beneath an overhanging slab, floored with dry pine duff, invisible from three directions and defensible from the fourth. Home, for as long as home lasted.

I got a fire going small and hot, screened by the slab, and while Ilsa melted snow I spread the route notes on my knees and studied the next stage by the orange light. His pencil marks marched down the north side in confident little dashes. At the bottom of the page, beneath the final descent profile, he'd added a note I hadn't noticed before, small, almost an afterthought:

*If anyone ever needs this route in a hurry — I'm sorry about the scree field at the bottom. Take the left rib. Trust me.*

Trust me. Two words, addressed to no one, to everyone, to me.

Below us, far down the valley, the consortium's camp smoked its patient smoke. Above us, the storm kept arriving. And somewhere downriver, in a station full of bought men, a crate waited with my name on it and my father's handwriting on the label — the ledger, the copies, the truth, sitting in the dark like a fuse.

One more pass. One more river. I fed the fire another stick and started planning the morning.
