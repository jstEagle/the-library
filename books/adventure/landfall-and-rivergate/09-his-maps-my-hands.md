## 9. His Maps, My Hands

The cache was where the map said it would be, which was its own kind of shock.

Paloma had spent three days telling herself the old trapper's marks were stale — that a man who'd been gone from her life for a decade couldn't still be trusted to put a roof over her head. But there it was, exactly as drawn: a seam of split rock above the third bend of the game trail, half-hidden behind a curtain of alder, with a door of peeled spruce poles so weathered it had become the hillside. She pulled the alder aside and felt the cold breath of the interior on her face — dry, dark, smelling of spruce resin and old smoke and something faintly chemical, like the inside of a tackle box.

"Is it big enough?" Ilsa asked from below, one hand pressed to her ribs where she'd taken a fall on the scree.

"It's big enough." Paloma heard her own voice come out flat, controlled, and hated how much it sounded like his.

Inside: a bunk frame with a rotting pad, a rusted woodstove no bigger than a breadbox, a crate of split kindling, and shelves built into the living rock. On the shelves, dust lay thick as snowfall — but beneath it, order. Cans of fuel. A tin of matches sealed in wax. Dried fish wrapped in birch bark. And maps. Dozens of them, rolled in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, each labeled in a hand Paloma knew better than she wanted to.

*UPPER RIVERGATE — WINTER ROUTE.* *GAME TRAILS ABOVE LANDFALL — NOT FOR SURVEY USE.* *CACHE 7 — SEE NOTES.*

She stood with the first roll in her hands for a long time before she untied it.

Outside, the light was failing early behind cloud, the way it did up here even before the real storms came. Ilsa got the stove lit while Paloma unrolled the maps across the bunk frame, weighting the corners with stones from the doorway. Her father's hand filled every margin — not the tidy cartography of the published expedition reports, all clean lines and institutional confidence, but this: cramped, slanting, alive. Arrows. Little annotations in pencil gone soft with age. Sketches of landmarks that only made sense if you'd stood where he'd stood.

*"False col — do not trust. Looks passable from east; drops 40 m into a slot."*

*"Water here even in August. Good camp. Mosquitoes biblical."*

*"If caught out in whiteout, follow the creek down. It will lie to you twice and then take you home."*

Paloma read that last line three times.

It will lie to you twice and then take you home.

She could hear him saying it. Not the voice from the phone calls that had dwindled to nothing, not the voice from the award dinners she'd watched online from dig sites with bad signal — the other voice. The one from before, when she was small enough to ride in the bow of the survey skiff and he narrated the river like a story he was making up as he went. He'd always talked to the water like it was a person with opinions. She'd forgotten that. Or she'd buried it, which amounted to the same thing, and now the maps had dug it up without asking permission.

"You're doing the thing," Ilsa said.

"What thing."

"Where you go somewhere else. Your jaw does this—" Ilsa made a motion at her own face. "You've been reading those notes for an hour and you haven't blinked."

"They're route notes," Paloma said. "They matter. If we're going over the range ahead of whatever Voss's people are doing, we go the way he mapped, or we don't go at all."

"That's not what I asked."

Paloma looked up. Ilsa sat cross-legged by the stove with a strip of dried fish she wasn't eating, twenty-three years old, burned-out eyes, three days away from the men who'd torn apart her camp and taken her boss upriver. She had earned the question. That didn't mean Paloma had to answer it.

But the storm was coming — you could smell it in the way the wind had turned, feel it in the pressure sitting on the valley like a thumb — and they might be in this cache for a day or three days, and questions kept in the dark fester worse than wounds.

"He worked," Paloma said finally. "That was the estrangement. Everyone wants it to be more dramatic than that. Some blowup, some betrayal you can point to. It was just — he worked. Expeditions, seasons, contracts. My mother used to say he loved the river more than any place he actually lived, and when she died he stopped pretending otherwise. I was sixteen. He sent me to stay with her sister in the lowlands and went back upriver six weeks after the funeral. Six weeks." She heard the edge come into her own voice and let it. "He asked me to come along the next summer. I said no. Then I said no for ten years. I dug contract sites in the flats and told myself I was building something he never bothered to build — a life with walls and a door that locked."

"And then they offered you the permit reference."

"And then they offered me the permit reference," Paloma agreed. "And I told myself that was why I came. Money and paper. Clean reasons."

Ilsa turned the fish strip over in her fingers. "Wasn't?"

"I don't know yet." It was the truest thing she'd said in days. "I keep waiting for it to resolve. Either I'm here for him or I'm here despite him, and it won't pick one."

"Maybe it doesn't have to." Ilsa nodded at the maps spread across the bunk. "Those are pretty good reasons too."

Paloma almost smiled. She rolled the top map closed and opened the next one, and that was when she saw it — tucked inside the oilcloth, folded to the size of a cigarette packet, a single sheet covered front and back in the same slanting hand. Not a map. A letter, or the bones of one, unsent.

*P —*

*Found something on the terrace today that changes everything and I don't know who to tell anymore. The consortium's people were very clear about what the heritage assessment needed to say. Very clear. I keep thinking about teaching you to read stratigraphy off a riverbank — you were eight, you thought it was magic. It is magic. It's the only honest magic there is. The ground doesn't lie. People do.*

*I should have come to the lowlands. I should have come ten years ago. I tell myself the work excuses it and the work has never once excused anything.*

*If you ever read this, I hope it finds you somewhere the ground is good.*

Her father's handwriting deteriorated near the bottom, the letters pressing harder, the pen tearing slightly:

*— and I know you'll do it right. Whatever they took from you, you'll do it right. That's the inheritance. Not the name. The eye.*

Paloma set the page down on the bunk with the care you use on something that might still be alive.

Ilsa had gone quiet. When Paloma looked over, the younger woman was watching her with an expression she couldn't parse — pity, maybe, or recognition. "What is it?"

"A letter he never sent." Her voice came out strange, sanded flat. "To me."

"What does it say?"

Paloma picked it up again and read it aloud, because some things get smaller if you keep them in your chest and larger if you let them into the air. When she finished, the stove ticked, and outside the wind had begun working at the alders with long deliberate pulls, the sound of a storm rehearsing.

"The eye," Ilsa repeated. "Not the name."

"He spent thirty years building the name," Paloma said. "Expeditions. Headlines. The Whitlock surveys. And at the end, hiding in a cache like a criminal, he writes that the name was never the point." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Ten years I refused to be his daughter, and it turns out he was sorry about being my father."

"Maybe both things are true," Ilsa said. "Like your reasons for coming."

Paloma looked at her. "You're twenty-three. Where do you get this stuff?"

"Bones don't lie either," Ilsa said. "I spent two months cataloguing that terrace before anyone knew what it meant. You learn things, sitting with the dead. Mostly you learn that everyone's story is unfinished, and the ones who pretend theirs isn't are the dangerous ones." She fed a stick into the stove. "Your dad pretended his was finished. The consortium pretends the terrace isn't there. Same disease."

The storm arrived an hour after full dark.

It came the way her father's marginalia had promised storms came to this stretch of the range — not gradually but all at once, a wall of sound moving up the valley, and then snow, fat and horizontal, hammering the spruce-pole door until the whole cache seemed to breathe with it. Paloma wedged the door tighter, banked the stove, and spread the route maps out under the lamplight for the work that mattered: choosing their line over the col before the weather chose it for them.

His notes were merciless and magnificent. He'd marked the safe crossing with a triple underline and a date — *crossed here, Oct 9, clear until noon* — and marked the tempting false route with a sketch of a man falling into a crevasse, rendered with genuine artistic commitment. He'd noted wind direction by season, snow-load patterns, the exact spot where the trail bent north around a rock face he'd named, in pencil, *the Argument,* for reasons the note did not explain and Paloma found herself smiling at anyway.

*Take the high line past the Argument even if the low line looks open. The low line is a liar. Trust the cairns; I rebuilt them in '09; they'll hold.*

She traced the line with her finger, committing it to memory the way she'd once committed stratigraphic profiles — layer by layer, feature by feature. This was the wound and the tool in the same object. Every annotation was a year he'd chosen the river over her. Every annotation might also be the reason she and Ilsa lived through the next three days.

"You can hate the map and still follow it," she said quietly, to no one.

"To no one" except Ilsa, who looked up from her corner. "What?"

"Nothing. Sleep. We move when the wind drops, not before."

But sleep came slowly, and when it came it brought the river — the lowlands river, warm-water, brown, the river of her childhood — and her father standing hip-deep in it with a hand-drawn chart, showing eight-year-old Paloma how the current wrote its history in silt bars. *Read it like writing,* he'd said. *Everything leaves a record. Your job is to be the person who can still read it.*

She woke once in the black hours to the storm screaming against the door and Ilsa asleep sitting up, and Paloma sat for a while with the unsent letter in her lap, rereading the last line by the last of the lamp oil.

*The eye. Not the name.*

When the light finally came, gray and sourceless through the seams of the door, the storm had settled into its long middle age — still blowing hard, but steady now, the scream gone out of it. Paloma dressed, checked the maps one final time, and was folding the unsent letter back into its oilcloth when Ilsa spoke from the bunk, awake after all.

"There's someone outside," she whispered. "Been standing down-slope for ten minutes. Just — standing."

Paloma killed the lamp. Through the gap between the spruce poles, down among the alders where the game trail broke from the rocks, she could see the shape of a man, alone, no pack visible, making no attempt to hide. Snow gathered on his shoulders. After a moment he raised one arm — slowly, deliberately, the way you signal a boat from shore — and called up through the wind in a voice roughened by days of weather:

"Whitlock! It's Voss. I'm alone, and I need to talk before the others close the gap!"
