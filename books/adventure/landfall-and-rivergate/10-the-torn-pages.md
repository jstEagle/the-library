## 10. The Torn Pages

The storm found them before they finished the tea.

It came down the valley like something poured — not falling snow but a gray-white slurry of air and cloud that erased the treeline in minutes, then the near pines, then the woodpile Ilsa and I had stacked against the cache door. One moment I could see the ridge where we'd spotted the fixers' cookfire smoke two days ago; the next there was nothing but the window of frosted glass and our own reflections ghosted on it, two women in a room the size of a coffin stood on end.

"Good," Ilsa said. "Now nobody moves."

"That's the idea." I fed another split of spruce into the little iron stove and watched the draft pull the flame sideways. The trapper's cache was older than either of us — a root cellar really, dug into the shoulder of the slope and roofed with peeled logs and a yard of earth, the kind of thing my father had marked on his maps with a small triangle and the notation *usable*. He'd been right. It was usable. It was also cold at the edges, dark except for the stove's glow through its mica window, and full of the smell of old fur, creosote, and mouse.

We had food for six days if we were careful. The pass would not open for four, maybe five, if it opened at all this side of December. Which meant that for the first time since Rivergate Station, since the rapids, since the burned camp and the terrace of small bones — I had nothing to do but think.

And there was one thing left to think about.

I took my father's notebook out of the dry bag where it lived now, wrapped in a wool sock like something sick. The waterproof paper had done its job through the river and the rain and the climb; the ink was as sharp as the day he'd pressed it into the page. Survey grids. Depth soundings off the terrace bench. A sketch of a burial cist drawn from above, the stones rendered in that quick confident hand I'd spent ten years refusing to look at closely because looking at it meant admitting how much of it I'd inherited — the same hand, nearly, that filled my own field books.

The last legible entry ended mid-sentence on the third-to-last page: *Consortium rep arrived at camp today with revised terms. Told him the heritage assessment stands as written. He said—*

Then nothing. Three pages gone, torn close to the spine, deliberate, by someone who knew exactly what they were removing. I'd turned the notebook over forty times since What-the-Rapids-Keep, held it to lamplight, run my thumb along the stubs of the missing leaves. Whoever tore them had been careful. Careful men still leave traces.

I'd been telling myself since the camp that the pages were gone, that whatever my father had written in those last hours — the ledger numbers, the names, the offer they'd made him — had gone upriver in a consortium man's pocket or downriver in the belly of some stove. Grief makes a good excuse for giving up early. I'd had practice.

But last night, half asleep against my pack, I'd remembered the lining.

* * *

The cache walls were lined with old newspaper, layered three and four sheets thick against the log walls, tarred over in places, yellowed everywhere else. Trappers did it for insulation. My father had noted caches like this one in his route notes going back twenty years — *cache below moraine, lined, dry* — and I'd always assumed the notation was about the structure.

I knelt at the wall nearest the stove and put the light to it.

Newspaper, yes. Weeks-old issues of the Landfall Courier, the kind of paper that gets used to wrap fish and line cellars and is forgotten. But beneath the top layer, where the tar had peeled away from a corner and a strip of the lining hung loose like scabbed skin, the paper wasn't newsprint.

It was graph paper. Waterproof graph paper, the expensive kind survey outfits buy, with a faint blue grid and handwriting showing through from the reverse side.

My hands went very still. Outside, the wind climbed a register and the whole hillside seemed to lean.

"Ilsa."

She looked up from the map she'd been copying. She'd gotten good at reading contour lines in three days flat, faster than most first-year students I'd taught, and she studied terrain the way she studied everything now — like a person who has learned what terrain can hide.

"Come hold the light."

I worked the loose strip free with my knife tip, slow, the way you lift a fossil out of matrix. The lining came away in a sheet the size of a spread hand, and on its underside — pressed against the log, hidden from anyone who hadn't gone looking — columns of figures marched in my father's hand. Dates. Tonnage. Permit numbers. And across the top of the fragment, underlined twice:

*RIVERGATE — GENERAL DELIVERY — HOLD*

"He mailed it to himself," Ilsa said.

"Not mailed." I turned the fragment toward the stove light. "Copied. He copied the ledger onto loose sheets and then—" I stopped, because the shape of it was assembling itself in my head the way a site assembles when you've cut enough of the overburden to see the feature. "He lined the cache with them. No. Wait."

I pulled more of the lining down. More fragments. Some were blank on the hidden face; some carried only a few lines; one had a corner torn diagonally, as if someone had grabbed the sheet and torn it free in haste — or as if it had been torn deliberately, into pieces small enough to disappear into a wall of newspaper, where no searcher raking a camp for documents would ever think to look.

"He didn't mail it yet," I said. "He was going to. He wrote the address on the top sheet — Rivergate general delivery, hold for pickup — and then he didn't send it. Or he couldn't. Or they came before he could get downriver." I sat back on my heels. The stove ticked. Somewhere under the floorboards a mouse resumed its work, indifferent to archaeology. "So he hid it here instead. In the one place in the range he trusted, on a route only he mapped, and then they took him upriver anyway, and the pages have been sitting in this wall waiting for—"

"For you," Ilsa said simply. "For whoever came looking. He'd know you'd come. Eventually. Even if it took years."

I didn't answer that. There wasn't an answer that didn't open something I couldn't afford to open in a cellar during a whiteout with fixers on the ridgelines. But my throat did something complicated, and I let it, briefly, in the dark where Ilsa was polite enough to study her map.

Then I got to work.

* * *

Reconstruction is patience disguised as obsession. I'd spent a decade doing it with potsherds — fitting rim to base, profile to profile, letting the curve of one fragment tell you where its neighbor had to be. Paper obeys the same logic. Tearing is a violent act but a patterned one; fibers match along tear lines the way fracture surfaces match in bone. You don't guess. You test, and the fragments tell you no until suddenly, with a small dry click of fit, they tell you yes.

We stripped the lining wall by wall. Ilsa pulled sheets down while I sorted, laying every fragment that wasn't true newsprint on the cache's single table, weighted at the corners with stones, arranged by the color of the ink — my father had used two pens that season, a black fine-liner for headings and a blue ballpoint for figures, and the difference showed even in candlelight. By midnight the table looked like the aftermath of a document explosion, which is essentially what it was.

By two in the morning, we had sentences.

The ledger itself was the easy part. My father had been a surveyor before he was anything else, and he'd copied the concession ledger in neat ruled columns, page references included: tonnage moved under salvage permits versus tonnage actually extracted, the gap between them running to hundreds of crates a season, all of it routed through the night barge, all of it signed off by a name I recognized from the permit filings — the consortium's operations director, the man whose signature would be sitting on the renewal application right now, somewhere downriver, waiting for a tribunal stamp. Ilsa read the columns aloud in a low voice while I matched fragments, and each number landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. We heard the splash long after.

The harder fragments were the letter.

Not a letter — a statement, drafted and redrafted, in my father's hand, addressed to no one and everyone: *To the land tribunal, Landfall district.* Four drafts, overlapping, torn apart and recombined. In the earliest he was defensive, procedural, a man building a legal wall brick by brick. By the third draft the wall had come down and what was underneath was rawer:

*I told them the terrace was ancestral and protected and they told me permits were ancestral too, older than any grave, and that I should decide which inheritance I wanted to leave my daughter.*

I stopped matching fragments for a while after that one.

"He wrote about me," I said finally, not meaning to say it aloud.

Ilsa glanced over. "You're in the record now," she said. "Might as well be in it standing up."

The final draft was short. He'd learned, somewhere between draft one and draft four, that tribunals don't read grief — they read evidence. The last version listed the ledger, described the terrace, named the cist field, cited the heritage statute by section number, and closed with instructions: *Copy enclosed. Original ledger retained. Deposit copy at Rivergate post, general delivery, hold for P. Whitlock. If I cannot deliver the original myself, she will know where the rest is buried.* Then, smaller, in the margin, in the hand I knew better than my own:

*P — I should have shown you the maps years ago. Forgive an old man his cowardice. The terrace is real. Make them look at it.*

Outside, the storm hammered the earth roof like a fist on a door. Inside, I sat with four drafts of my father's conscience spread across a trapper's table, and I understood at last what he'd been doing in those final days at the burned camp: not deciding whether to tell the truth, but deciding how much of himself it would cost, and mailing the cost ahead of him to a post office box like a man sending his heart on ahead by freight.

"He never sent it," Ilsa said quietly.

"No. They reached him first." I gathered the fragments into sequence, weighted them, photographed each stage with the battered camera from the forward camp — Voss's abandoned satellite phone had died, but the camera still had film, and film doesn't need a signal. "But the copy exists. All of it. Ledger, statement, names, numbers."

"And the original?"

"The original went upriver with him. Which means it's either on the consortium's barge, in their director's safe, or—" I didn't finish. Didn't need to. Ilsa had seen the camp. We both knew what professionally erased could include.

"So we go to Rivergate."

"We go to Rivergate." I checked my watch, an old habit that felt absurd in a hole in the ground. "General delivery holds mail thirty days. After that it goes to the dead-letter crate behind the station — I've seen it, it's a wooden box the keepers never empty because the company that owns the station changes every few years and nobody wants the paperwork. If his parcel's already lapsed into dead letters, it's still there. Sitting in a crate. Waiting."

"How long does the hold run?"

"Depends when he posted it. If he managed to get the copy down to Rivergate before they took him — and the address on the top sheet says he intended to — then the thirty days started sometime in the last three weeks." I did the arithmetic and didn't like the remainder. "We could be inside the window. We could be one day outside it. Either way, the clock started without us."

Ilsa looked at the storm-black window, then at me. "How fast can this pass open?"

"Fast as the wind decides. Two clear days and the col's walkable. Five and we're climbing over the top in spring." I fed the last of the spruce into the stove and watched the flames take it. "Which means the plan is simple and terrible. The minute this storm breaks, we move. No rest day, no careful route-finding down the game trails. Straight for the col, straight down the north side, straight to Rivergate — and we pray the dead-letter crate is as neglected as it looked."

"And if the consortium's people reach the station first?"

I thought about the evasive keeper at Rivergate, the unopened resupply crates, the barge that came through at night. I thought about men with badges raking a camp clean.

"Then we hope the postal service is more honest than everyone else on this river," I said. "And we get there first."

Ilsa nodded once, the way crew nod — no argument, just the weight shifting forward. She rolled into her bag. I sat up a while longer with the reconstructed pages under a stone, reading my father's column of stolen numbers by stove-light, learning at last, ten years late, the exact size of what he'd found and what it had cost him to try to give it away.

Somewhere above us, under a meter of moving snow, the pass waited to decide which of us it would let through.

I meant to be the first.
