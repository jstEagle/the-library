## 11. First Snow on the Col

The snow did not fall so much as arrive — a gray wall coming down the valley from the north like something with intent.

Paloma saw it first from the shelf of rock they'd been climbing for two hours, a bruise-colored mass swallowing the peaks above the col. She stopped, one hand on the cold granite, and did the arithmetic she'd been dreading since Rivergate. The pass was supposed to hold open another week. The old survey records said so; her father's marginalia said so, in his cramped confident hand: *Col holds to Oct 20 most years.* Most years. The mountain had never cared much for what men wrote about it.

"That's not weather," Ilsa said behind her. "That's a decision."

"It's an early winter." Paloma shrugged her pack higher. "We make the col before it does, or we don't make it at all."

They climbed. The game trail her father had mapped thinned to a suggestion, then to a memory, then to nothing but cairns he'd stacked thirty years ago, lichen-crusted and leaning, each one a small argument that someone had come this way before and believed it passable. Paloma touched each one as she passed. It was the closest thing to conversation she'd had with him in a decade.

The first flakes came while they were still below the saddle — fat, unhurried, almost gentle, the way a fist is gentle on its way somewhere. By the time they crested the col itself, a notch of shattered stone between two white shoulders, the gentleness was gone. Wind drove the snow sideways with a sound like sand poured over tin. Visibility collapsed to twenty meters, then ten, then the length of your own arms.

"We can't go down the north face in this," Ilsa shouted. "We'd walk off a cornice without ever seeing it."

Paloma turned into the wind, squinting. Somewhere below them, she knew, the fixers were climbing too — better boots, better tents, radios that worked. If the storm closed the pass for both parties equally, it was only fair. Nothing about this chase had been fair yet.

"Cache," she shouted back. "My father marked a trapper's cache half a mile east along the ridge. Stone roofed. We hole up until it breaks."

"And if it doesn't break?"

"Then we learn patience." She said it lightly, and hated how much of her hope it cost. "Come on."

* * *

The cache was exactly where the map promised, which still startled her every time. A low structure of dry-stacked stone against a granite outcrop, roofed with split slabs, hidden behind a curtain of stunted pine. Inside: a floor of packed earth, a rusted woodstove, half a cord of gray old wood, and a trapper's leavings — a cot frame, a coil of wire, three glass traps gone green with age, and a smell of fox musk and pipe smoke so old it had become part of the stone.

Ilsa got a fire going while Paloma wedged the door and unpacked their food with the grim precision of a woman counting ammunition. Seven days of rations if they were careful. Five if they were honest.

Outside, the storm built itself a cathedral. Snow hissed against the stone. The wind found every gap in the old walls and played them like pipes, a rising note that never quite resolved.

Ilsa sat close to the stove, hands open toward the heat, and watched the fire with an expression Paloma was beginning to recognize — the look of someone running a film behind their eyes that they couldn't stop. The camp. The men with consortium badges. Her father being walked upriver between two of them, not fighting, which was somehow worse than if he'd fought.

"They'll be warm tonight," Ilsa said. "The men following us. Down-valley there's timber, shelter. They'll have good gear."

"Yes."

"And we're sitting in a hole in a rock eating oatmeal."

"Yes," Paloma said again, and fed the stove another stick. "But they don't know where the cache is. And they don't know these trails. And when the storm lifts, they'll be guessing and we'll be moving." She looked up. "Patience isn't the same as losing, Ilsa. It just feels worse."

Ilsa almost smiled. "Did your father teach you that?"

"No," Paloma said. "The river did."

* * *

Two miles below the col, in a dome tent pitched in the lee of a granite shoulder, Tomas Voss listened to the same wind and thought about bones.

He'd stopped thinking about much else in the last two days, which was a professional problem. He was paid to think about routes, supply margins, radio schedules, and the disposition of two women who were carrying something his employer wanted very badly buried. He was not paid to think about small skulls arranged in rows under river silt, or the way the older ones were worn smooth as river stones, or the fact that he had stood on that terrace with a camera in his hand and taken photographs he was supposed to take and then more photographs he was absolutely not supposed to take.

He checked the satellite phone again. No signal worth the name — the storm ate the satellites whole. His team of four was pinned below the treeline with him, gear rated to minus fifteen, morale rated considerably lower. Renner had already asked twice whether they could turn back and report the women lost to weather. Report it, collect the fee, let the mountain do the ugly work.

Voss hadn't answered. That was its own kind of answer.

He unzipped the tent flap and looked north through the white roar. Somewhere up there, past the col, past the north face, was the tribunal town — five days' travel in clear weather, unreachable now for anyone without wings. The storm had done his job for him. All he had to do was sit here, keep his people alive, and wait for the mountain to finish.

It should have felt easy. Instead he kept seeing the terrace. Kept hearing Whitlock — the old man, before they'd taken him upriver — saying, quietly, not pleading, just stating it like a man reading coordinates: *You can't bury people twice, Mr. Voss. Well. You can. But it doesn't hold.*

Voss zipped the flap shut and lay back and did not sleep.

* * *

Day two of the storm, the wood ran low.

Paloma rationed the fire to an hour at dawn and an hour at dusk, and they learned to live in the cold dark between, wrapped in everything they owned, talking to keep their core temperatures up. Ilsa talked about the survey camp — the good weeks, before the end. The way Whitlock senior had hummed while he worked, badly, some folk song from a country neither of his children had ever seen. How he'd made the junior crew measure everything twice because "the river forgives nothing and neither should you."

"He talked about you," Ilsa said, in the blue hour before dawn on the third day. "Once. He said he had a daughter who read water better than any man he'd ever hired. He said it like an apology."

Paloma fed the stove a stick and didn't answer for a long time. The firelight moved on her face.

"He used to say the river forgives nothing," she said finally. "Like it was a virtue. Like it was wisdom." She poked the coals. "It's not wisdom. It's just what you tell people instead of sorry."

Ilsa was quiet, then: "Are you doing this for him? The ledger, the tribunal. For him?"

"I'm doing it for the dead people under the terrace," Paloma said. "He's incidental." She heard how it sounded even as she said it, and knew Ilsa heard it too, and neither of them said anything else, and outside the wind went on building its cathedral.

* * *

On the fourth day the storm paused — not broke, paused, like a held breath — and Paloma climbed the ridge behind the cache to look.

South, back the way they'd come: white chaos, no movement. North, down the far side: the clouds had torn open along the valley just enough to show her the world below, and what she saw made her stomach drop through the mountain.

Smoke. Three threads of it, rising from the treeline two miles down the northern approach. Campfires. They'd gotten around the col — or through it, or they'd known another route, or they'd simply started earlier and been luckier. Either way, they were below her, between her and the tribunal, warming themselves at fires she couldn't afford.

She counted figures through the field glasses. Four. One standing apart from the others, taller, not sitting, looking up at the ridge. Even at this distance she knew what he was doing. He was reading the terrain the way she read water — looking for where a person would have to go.

She slid back down to the cache and told Ilsa.

"So we're besieged," Ilsa said. "They're below us, the pass is closing again, and we have three days of food."

"Two and a half."

"Wonderful." Ilsa pulled her knees up. "What do they want? Really? If they wanted us dead, why haven't they come up in the night?"

Paloma considered that. It was the right question, and the fact that Ilsa had asked it meant the girl was thinking clearly, which was worth more than food. "Because coming up here costs them something. Because dead guides who vanished in a storm are a story, and stories get investigated. What they want is the ledger. Quietly. Which means—" she stopped.

"Means what?"

"Means somebody will eventually climb up here to offer us a trade." She said it flatly, but her skin prickled. "Safe passage for the book. Money, maybe. It's cheaper than killing us and it looks cleaner."

"And would you take it?"

Paloma looked at the stove, at the last of the wood, at the girl who had crawled out of a burned camp and followed her up a mountain on nothing but borrowed nerve.

"No," she said. "But I'd listen. Listening costs nothing, and it tells you what they're afraid of."

* * *

In the camp below, Voss stood apart from his men and looked up at the ridge he couldn't see, and made a decision he had probably made on the terrace among the small bones and had spent four days refusing to say out loud.

Renner came to stand beside him, breath steaming. "Weather window tomorrow morning, maybe six hours. Enough to go up and come back."

"Go up, maybe," Voss said. "Come back, we'll see."

"You're not sending all of us."

"No." Voss watched the smoke of his own fire flatten in the wind. "This one I do alone."

* * *

The fifth dawn came clean and hard, the sky scoured blue, the world rebuilt overnight in white. Paloma was awake before light — habit, and hunger — and was melting snow when Ilsa touched her arm and pointed.

A figure was climbing the ridge toward the cache. Alone. Unarmed, as far as she could tell, hands visible, moving slowly and openly the way a man moves when he wants very badly to be seen coming.

Paloma put the field glasses on him. Tall. Gray at the temples. Consortium-issue parka with the badge already stripped off the sleeve — she could see the paler rectangle where it had been.

She set the glasses down. Her heart was going like a paddle wheel, and she made herself breathe the way you breathe before running rapids: slow, deep, committed.

"That's him," she said. "The one from the ridge. He's coming to bargain."

Ilsa's hand found a rock the size of a fist. "And if it's a trick?"

"Then we're two against one on our own ground," Paloma said, and stepped out into the blinding morning to meet him.
