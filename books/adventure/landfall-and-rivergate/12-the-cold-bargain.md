## 12. The Cold Bargain

The storm broke at dawn the way storms do up there — not gradually, but all at once, like a hand lifting off the tent. Paloma crawled out into a world scoured white and blue, the col glittering, the air so clean it hurt to breathe. Three days they'd been pinned under it, three days of melting snow for water and rationing the last of the smoked fish, listening to the wind try to peel the flysheet off its poles.

Ilsa was still asleep, curled against her pack with her mittened hands tucked under her chin like a child. Paloma let her sleep. She stood in the entrance and scanned the slope below out of habit, and that was when she saw him.

A single figure on the glacier tongue, maybe four hundred meters down, climbing. Alone. No rope team visible behind him, no camp, no sled track except his own, a single stitched line across the white. He moved well — deliberate, economical, the walk of someone who'd done winter travel but wasn't native to it. He carried a pack and nothing else she could identify as a weapon.

She woke Ilsa without a word, just a hand on her shoulder, and put a finger to her own lips. They watched from behind the windbreak of their snow wall as the figure climbed the last pitch below the col, unhurried, stopping twice to rest in a way that looked almost theatrical. Letting them see he was tired. Letting them see he was alone.

"He's coming to talk," Ilsa whispered.

"Or coming to get close enough not to have to."

But nobody climbed to four thousand meters alone, through a storm's aftermath, to do something stupid. Stupid was cheap. This was expensive. And men who spent consortium money didn't spend it on gestures.

Paloma slung her ice axe and went out to meet him, leaving Ilsa at the tent with instructions she knew the girl would ignore if things went wrong. The snow squeaked under her boots, that dry cold squeak that meant the temperature had fallen hard overnight. She stopped where the slope steepened and let him come to her.

Up close he was younger than she'd expected — mid-thirties, maybe, with a face weathered unevenly, sunburned across the cheekbones and pale around the eyes where goggles had sat. His parka was expedition-grade, consortium-issue by the cut of it, but worn like someone had actually lived in it for weeks rather than posed in it for a brochure. He stopped six meters away, which was a polite distance, and set his pack down on the snow between them like a man laying cards face-up.

"Whitlock," he said. Not a question.

"Who's asking?"

"Voss. Marcus Voss." He said it flatly, the way you say a name you've stopped being proud of. "Security contractor. Meridian Consolidated." Then, before she could answer: "Your father is alive."

The words landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there, hot. She kept her face still. Ten years of practice at keeping her face still around anything that touched Elias Whitlock.

"I know," she said. "Ilsa told me. Men took him upriver."

"We took him upriver. Yes." He didn't flinch from the *we*. She noted that. Most men would have said *they*. "And then we lost him, or he was moved, and I've spent eleven days being lied to about which. So now I'm here, spending my own calories, climbing your mountain, because there's a conversation that has to happen and my employers have decided it can't happen honestly."

"You're a long way to come to lie to me yourself."

"I'm here to offer terms." He crouched, unzipped the pack, and took out a thermos, two metal cups, and a foil brick of chocolate — real chocolate, lowland stuff, the kind that cost more per gram than the fish they'd been eating. He poured without asking. Steam rose and shredded sideways in the wind. "Drink. You look like you've been eating glacier for a week."

She didn't move. He set one cup on a flat rock anyway, equidistant between them, an offering neither accepted nor refused, and straightened up.

"The terms are simple," he said. "You give us the ledger. The copied pages, the notebook, whatever documentation you recovered from the terrace. In exchange: safe passage down the north side, transport to Landfall, and a payment that would fund your own survey company for five years. Cash, wired anywhere you name. No strings after that. You go back to contract digs in the lowlands and never think about this river again."

"And my father?"

Voss was quiet for a moment. Wind combed the surface of the snow between them, lifting little ghosts of powder.

"My orders were to recover the ledger and ensure Dr. Whitlock's cooperation," he said. "The word *ensure* was doing a lot of work in that sentence. What I can tell you is that as of nine days ago he was alive, ambulatory, and angry enough to break a man's nose over a radio schedule. After that—" He spread his hands. "After that I was pulled off the detail and reassigned to finding you. Which tells me either he's been moved somewhere my clearance doesn't reach, or the people holding him decided I ask too many questions. Both possibilities should worry you. Neither should surprise you."

"You're selling me my father's life for a stack of paper."

"I'm telling you what the paper buys." He met her eyes steadily. "I'm not going to insult you by pretending the ledger is sentimental to anyone. It's leverage. It's the only thing standing between Meridian and a renewed concession, and everyone on this river knows it. Right now you're a woman on a mountain with a piece of paper and no way to enforce it. Come spring, when the tribunal sits, you'll be a witness with documentation and legal standing. Meridian needs the ledger gone *before* the permits renew. That window is closing, and they know it, and desperate companies do arithmetic instead of ethics. The payout is real. The passage is real. Take it, and you live, and you're rich, and your father—"

"Is worth exactly as much to them as the paper in my pack."

"Yes," Voss said simply. "That's the honest version. There isn't a better one. Anyone who offers you one is lying harder than I am."

* * *

They stood there while the wind rose and fell, and Paloma made herself think it through the way her father had taught her to read water — not the surface, the current underneath.

Because that was the thing about the offer. It was good. It was *too* good. A fixer with a mandate to erase evidence didn't climb alone into the open and lay out terms; he waited until dark and took the pack off a sleeping woman. Everything about Voss's posture was wrong for the job he claimed to be doing. He'd shown his face. He'd given his full name. He'd told her, unprompted, that his own employer was lying to him.

And there was something else. Something she'd been circling since he started talking.

"You said you were reassigned," she said slowly. "From the detail holding my father. To finding me. Why does a security contractor care whether I'm found alive? Meridian's problem ends if I fall in a crevasse with the ledger in my pack. Cheaper, too."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes. Not guilt exactly. Something older and more tired than guilt.

"Can I show you something?" he said. "It's why I came. The terms were the cover story. My own cover story, if I'm honest — I needed a reason to make this climb that I could live with."

He reached inside his parka, carefully, telegraphing every movement, and drew out a camera. An old field camera, film, the kind her father had used for twenty years. He held it out.

"Don't take my word for anything," he said. "Look."

She took it. Her hands were steady in a way that surprised her. She thumbed back the film counter and began advancing through the shots, shielding the viewfinder from glare.

Terrace photographs. Shot in flat overcast light, professional framing, the kind of documentation a heritage assessor would kill for. The burial terrace — *her* excavation, the trenches she'd dug in that one desperate day, the stone cists and the ochre staining and the small grave goods laid out in rows. But these weren't her photos. These were taken weeks ago, before she ever reached the terrace. Before the camp was burned. Before any of it.

And they showed what her father had found first. The terrace intact, undisturbed, the stratigraphy exposed in a fresh cut bank: layer upon layer of occupation, generations deep, the river people's dead lying in ordered rows beneath the very ground Meridian's concession maps marked as sterile gravel bar.

The last frame stopped her breath. It was a wide shot of the whole terrace with a man standing at its edge for scale — tall, gray-bearded, wearing a surveyor's vest she'd last seen hanging in a closet in Landfall ten years ago. Elias Whitlock, looking directly into the lens with an expression she recognized from her childhood: the look he wore when he'd found something true and knew it was going to cost him.

"That was taken the morning he refused to sign," Voss said quietly. "I was on the detail. My job was to photograph everything he found so it could be — managed. Dismissed. Reclassified as recent interment, inconsequential, outside heritage protection. That was the assignment. Document it well enough to erase it credibly." He paused. "I did the assignment. All of it. And then I got back to camp and couldn't sleep, and the next night I couldn't sleep again, and on the third night I printed a second set of negatives and hid them in my boot liners, and I've been carrying them ever since like a stone."

Paloma lowered the camera. The wind filled the silence.

"You saw them," she said. It wasn't a question. "The bones."

"I saw the bones." He looked past her, up the slope toward the col, at nothing. "I've cleared sites before. Logging camps, old traplines, a mission graveyard once — that was bad enough. But this was different. This was hundreds of years of people, buried proper, with care, generation after generation, right where Meridian's maps say nobody ever lived. And I stood there watching your father measure it and I understood that my job wasn't to guard a site. It was to be part of making those people never have existed. Because a mining company needed them not to." He turned back to her. "I want to tell you I don't know how I justified it for as long as I did. But I do know. The money's good and the work is steady and you tell yourself everyone else would do the same. And then one morning you're looking at a photograph of an old man who risked everything to write down the truth, and you realize you're the reason he's in a room somewhere wishing he hadn't."

"Why give it to me? Why not just walk away? Desert. Go downriver and disappear like the sensible ones do."

"Because walking away leaves the ledger as the only evidence, and ledgers can be stolen, and women carrying them can have accidents on glaciers." He nodded at the camera in her hands. "That's a second record. Independent. Dated. With your father in the frame. If something happens to you, it still exists. If something happens to me, it exists. Two copies in two hands is the difference between a story and a fact."

He crouched again and, from the bottom of the pack, beneath the thermos and the rations, drew out a slim bundle wrapped in oilcloth. He didn't offer it. He just let it sit there in the snow between them, visible.

"My field log," he said. "Dates, locations, names, orders received and orders followed. Including the order regarding your father that I refused to carry out. Written in my own hand, signed, witnessed by nobody, which means it proves nothing in a court and everything to a tribunal that wants to understand how Meridian operates. It's yours. Not traded. Not bargained. Given."

"And the terms?" Paloma said. "The payout, the safe passage?"

"Forget the terms." He almost smiled; it didn't reach his eyes. "I had to offer them. If I'd climbed up here and immediately defected, you'd have shot me, and you'd have been right to. The offer was the price of admission." He straightened, and for the first time he looked tired — genuinely tired, the fatigue of a man setting down something heavy. "Here's the truth, Ms. Whitlock, free of charge. Meridian's second team is two days behind me, better equipped, and they don't have my doubts. Their orders won't include terms. The storm bought you a week; you've spent most of it. The pass closes for real within days, and after that the only way down is the way they'll be watching."

Paloma looked at the oilcloth bundle. At the camera in her hands. At this man who had climbed into the wind carrying his confession like ballast he finally wanted rid of.

"If I take your log," she said, "and we go down that mountain together, and the tribunal asks me why it should believe a Meridian fixer — what do I tell them?"

Voss considered it.

"Tell them I saw the bones," he said. "Tell them that's the whole of it. Everyone on this river has a price, and mine turned out to be a row of graves under a gravel bar. I looked at what they paid me to unmake, and I couldn't do the arithmetic anymore."

Behind her, she heard boots in the snow — Ilsa, ignoring instructions exactly as predicted, coming up the slope with her axe held wrong and her chin set. Voss watched her come and made no move at all, hands loose at his sides, and something in his stillness decided it for Paloma.

She picked up the oilcloth bundle. It was lighter than she expected. Most truths were, once you finally picked them up.

"Two days' lead," she said. "Then we descend together, and you break trail, because you're fresher and heavier and if there's a crevasse bridge that won't hold, I'd rather it found out on you."

This time the smile reached his eyes.

"Fair terms," Voss said. "Better than the ones I brought."

Paloma turned toward the col, where the clouds were building again over the far range — soft, white, deceptively gentle, the way weather lied before it closed a pass for the season. One clear day, maybe two. The north face beyond it, and the river below, and Rivergate, and the dead-letter crate with her father's mailed ledger waiting in it like a heart in cold storage.

They would need every hour of the light there was.

Because down in the valley, faint but unmistakable on the wind, she heard it: the distant stutter of a chainsaw, and under it, engines — many engines — starting to move.
