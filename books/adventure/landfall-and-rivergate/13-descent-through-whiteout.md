## 13. Descent Through Whiteout

The storm broke at dawn the way a dam breaks — all at once, and with no respect for what stood downstream.

We had spent two days in the trapper's cache listening to it. Two days of wind screaming across the col like something with a grudge, snow packing against the door until Ilsa had to dig us out twice just to reach the latrine trench. Two days of Voss sitting by the stove with his hands flat on his knees, saying nothing, while I studied the torn fragments of my father's notebook and tried to decide whether the man across from me was a prisoner, a defector, or the most patient trap ever set.

On the third morning the wind stopped so abruptly that the silence woke me.

I pushed the door open into a world gone white and still. The sky was the color of old milk. Snow lay knee-deep on the shelf outside, unbroken, unmarked — no tracks below us, which meant the fixers hadn't climbed past the cache in the storm. It also meant that somewhere down there, they were moving again, because men like Voss's former colleagues didn't stop for weather. They waited it out, the way we had, and then they came on.

"We go today," Voss said behind me. He'd read my shoulders before I'd said a word. "The storm bought us a day, maybe two. The north face will hold fresh snow for another twelve hours before it crusts. After that, every print we leave is a signature."

"And after that?"

"After that we're signatures too."

Ilsa stood in the doorway beside me, breath steaming, squinting down at the slope that fell away beneath the cache in long, timbered ribs. She'd been quiet since Voss arrived alone under his white flag of surrender — quieter than fear made her, quieter than grief. This morning she looked almost hungry.

"The game trail," she said. "Your father's route."

I nodded. The notebook fragments had given me enough: *north rib, third drainage, stay off the open slabs until the tree line thickens.* My father's handwriting, cramped and certain, written by a man who trusted the mountain more than he trusted people. For ten years I'd resented that trust — the way he gave it to rivers and ridgelines and never once, as far as I could remember, to me. Now I was betting all three of our lives on it.

"Compass work is yours," I told Voss. "Trail-breaking is mine. Ilsa keeps the interval between us and calls out if she loses sight of either." I looked at him. "You navigate toward the river, not away from your own people. If I see one bearing that bends toward their camp, I leave you on the mountain with your story."

He didn't argue. He'd stopped arguing somewhere on the second night, when I'd asked him why a man who photographed bones carried them like a wound instead of a weapon, and he'd said, *Because I thought I was doing survey work, Ms. Whitlock. Nobody tells you what you're erasing until you've already erased it.*

That answer had bought him this much: a rope, a bearing, and my back turned to him on the descent.

* * *

The first hour was the easy part — if you could call chest-deep snow easy. I went first, breaking trail in a rhythm I'd learned on contract digs in the lowlands: step, sink, pack, step. Behind me Voss held our compass course off the north rib, calling corrections in a flat voice that carried strangely well in the dead air. Behind him, Ilsa counted our intervals aloud, her voice thin but steady, a metronome against the whiteout that kept trying to close over us.

Because that was the thing about the storm's aftermath nobody warns you about. The wind had stopped, but the cloud hadn't lifted. We descended inside the belly of it, a whiteness so total that up and down became opinions rather than facts. Ten meters away, the mountain simply ceased to exist. Twenty meters, and Voss himself was a gray suggestion, a coat and a voice.

"You'll want to trust your feet," he called forward. "Don't. Trust the slope angle and the trees. Feet lie in powder. Slopes don't."

"I know how to read terrain," I said.

"I know," he said. "I'm telling myself as much as you."

We crossed the first open slab with ropes on, moving fast because fast was safe — the danger wasn't falling, it was lingering where a slab might settle. The snow there was different underfoot, denser, wind-loaded, and it made a sound I didn't like: a low, hollow drumming through my boots, like walking on the skin of something full. Voss heard it too. I saw him change course without being told, bending our line toward a band of rock where the slab thinned to nothing.

"My father mapped this face," I said, mostly to hear a voice that wasn't the wind's ghost. "Nineteen years ago. He called it the Kind Face in his notes because it looked gentle from the valley."

"And it isn't."

"It isn't anything," I said. "It's just rock and gravity. The names are for the people who have to come back."

He was quiet for a few steps. Then: "He wrote that?"

"He wrote everything. That was the problem." I said it harder than I meant to, and the mountain took the echo and gave it back to me twice.

* * *

The second drainage was where it went wrong.

My father's notes had been clear — *third drainage, stay high, the bottom funnels into a drop* — but drainages look alike under a meter of new snow, and the whiteout ate our landmarks whole. Voss caught it first. He stopped so abruptly Ilsa nearly walked into him, and when I turned back up the line, he was standing with the compass in his palm and an expression I'd learned to dread on other people's faces: the arithmetic of error.

"We're in the fourth," he said. "Or the fifth. The rib we crossed should have bent east. It bent west."

"Then we climb back."

"If we climb back, we burn two hours and leave a trail a blind man could follow. Or—" He looked downslope, into the murk. "There's a way through here. Steeper. The bottom drops into a gorge, but there's a bench partway down, and the bench runs west to rejoin the third drainage."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I helped survey it." He said it without flinching, which was its own kind of honesty. "Two seasons ago. Consortium route reconnaissance. We were mapping access corridors for equipment hauls." His jaw worked. "I told myself it was just lines on paper. Everything was just lines on paper, right up until the terrace."

Ilsa spoke for the first time in an hour. "Paloma. The fixers are behind us or they aren't. Standing here doesn't decide it."

She was right, and we both knew she was right, and I hated the geometry of it: trusting the navigation of a man who had helped draw the roads for the people hunting us, because the alternative was the open slope and the storm's fading mercy.

"Bench," I said. "But I break trail. You call the bearings. If the bench isn't where you say it is—"

"It's where I say it is."

"—then the next conversation we have will be very short."

* * *

The bench was where he said it was. I'll give him that forever.

It appeared out of the white like a promise kept — a shelf of snow-covered granite maybe four meters wide, angling down along the gorge wall, with the void on our left dropping away into a darkness the eye couldn't finish. We roped tight and moved along it single file, and for twenty minutes the world narrowed to boot placements and the sound of three people breathing.

Ilsa was behind me. I could hear her breathing change before anything else — the shortening, the catch. Then a sound like a green branch twisting.

She went down without a cry, which frightened me more than a scream would have. She sat in the snow with her left leg extended and her hands wrapped around her ankle above the boot, and her face was the color of the weather.

"Rolled it on rock under the snow," she managed. "It's — give me a minute. It's fine."

It was not fine. When I eased the bootlace loose, the joint beneath had already begun to swell, hot through two layers of wool. Not broken — I'd set enough fractures on salvage crews to know the difference — but sprained badly, the kind of injury that turns a descent into a negotiation.

"Can you bear weight?"

She tried. She got halfway up, put weight on it, and made a sound through her teeth that I felt in my own jaw. She sat back down. "Ask me again in an hour," she said, "and I'll lie better."

Voss crouched, unhurried, and began repacking the rope. "Then we change the plan," he said. "She rides the middle. We shorten the interval, take the bench slow, and below the tree line we build her a splint and a staff. There's a stand of birch where the bench meets the drainage — I marked it on the consortium map because it was the only decent anchor point on this whole face."

"You marked trees for them."

"I marked everything for them." He looked up, and for the first time since the col, something moved behind his eyes that wasn't calculation. "That's the job, Ms. Whitlock. You reduce a living place to points and bearings so that someone who's never stood here can take it apart. I was good at it." He tied off the knot. "I'm going to be good at undoing it, too. Starting with getting her down this mountain."

Ilsa laughed — a small, cracked sound, half pain. "Listen to him. Two days ago he was the enemy."

"Two days ago I was useful to the wrong side," Voss said. "The mountain doesn't care which side I was on. Neither does that ankle."

We built the splint at the birch stand, exactly where he'd promised it would be, and I tried not to think about what it meant that his memory of this face was better than my father's notes. Maybe it meant nothing. Maybe competence was competence, wherever it had been rented out. But as I cinched the last wrap around Ilsa's ankle and watched Voss cut a birch staff and fit it to her grip with a carpenter's patience, I understood that something had shifted in the arithmetic between us. Not trust. Trust was a currency I wasn't ready to mint. But shared risk does something that argument can't: it makes a man's hands legible. And his hands were steady, and they were careful, and they were working for us.

"From here," he said, "it's six hundred meters of timbered slope to the river flats. The snow will start crusting within the hour — cold snap coming, I can feel it in the air. Our tracks will set behind us like plaster casts." He glanced at the gray ceiling overhead. "Which means whatever we do, we do it now."

Ilsa got upright on the staff, tested the ankle, and swore with feeling. Then she squared her shoulders. "Lead on," she said. "I've been carried enough for one lifetime."

* * *

We came down through the timber as the light failed, three shadows roped together in a forest gone blue and silent. The crust formed as Voss predicted; by the last hour each of our steps rang faintly on the frozen surface, and behind us our trail hardened into a record none of us could erase. Somewhere above and south of us, men with consortium badges were reading that record or would be by morning. We had perhaps a night's lead. Perhaps less.

The river announced itself before we saw it — a black gleam through the trees, then the low mutter of open water at the edges of the ice. And then, as we pushed through the last alders onto the flats, Ilsa grabbed my sleeve and pointed.

Downstream, maybe two kilometers, a light was moving.

Not a headlamp. Not a campfire. A running light, riding low on the water, sliding downriver through the dark with the patient confidence of something that owned the channel. As I watched, it passed behind a wooded point and emerged again, and I caught the shape of it: flat-decked, wide, running without any cargo lights at all.

The barge. The same night barge the station keeper had lied to me about at Rivergate, a lifetime ago — the one that moved in the dark and answered no radio calls. It had come upriver heavy, I'd guessed. Now I watched it heading down, riding higher in the water than a loaded barge should ride, and the guess curdled into certainty.

Empty going down? No. Lighter, yes — but not empty. Crates ride lighter than ore. My father's terrace, packed and palletized, going to market.

"They're moving it tonight," Ilsa breathed. "Before the tribunal — before anyone—"

"Of course they are." My voice came out flat and cold, and I barely recognized it as mine. "Storm closes the pass, everyone assumes we're dead on the mountain, and the river's still open below the rapids. It's the only road they have left."

Voss stood beside me, watching the light shrink downstream, and said quietly, "Rivergate station sits at the narrows below the lower rapids. Anything on this water has to pass it. And Rivergate has a dead-letter crate, doesn't it? Your father's mailing?"

I looked at him. He looked back, and I saw that he'd already done the math I hadn't let myself do — that the ledger my father had mailed to himself was waiting in that station, and that the barge, the fixers, and we were all converging on the same narrow stretch of water from three directions.

"That's two days' walk," I said. "Three, with her ankle."

"Then we'd better start," Ilsa said, leaning hard on her birch staff, "before they figure out we're not on the mountain anymore."

Behind us, the north face vanished into the dark, holding our tracks in its frozen skin like a confession. Ahead, the river ran black and busy, carrying stolen generations down to the sea.

I tightened the rope on my shoulder and started for the water.
