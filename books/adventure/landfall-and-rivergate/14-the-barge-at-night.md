## 14. The Barge at Night

The river found us before we found it.

We heard it first — a low, continuous hush through the trees, like a crowd murmuring behind a wall — and then the ground tilted and there it was below us, wide and black and moving with that patient, indifferent strength that makes every other sound in the world seem temporary. The north face had taken everything out of us. Two days of whiteout descent, Voss reading compass bearings by feel when the needle froze, me breaking trail until my arms shook, Ilsa hopping on one good leg between my shoulder and a cut alder staff she'd sharpened herself and refused to give up. We came down off the last moraine in the blue hour after sunset, and the river was there, and it was enormous, and for a long moment none of us said anything at all.

"We made it," Ilsa said finally. She said it like she didn't believe it, like the words were something she was testing for load-bearing capacity.

Voss crouched at the treeline and studied the water. "We made it down," he corrected. "That's not the same thing."

He was right. The river wasn't an ending; it was a road, and roads run both ways. Somewhere above us, back up on the col, the rest of Voss's team would be finding our cold camp and working out which direction we'd gone. Somewhere downriver, past the lower rapids, sat Rivergate Station and the tribunal office and the only hearing that mattered. And somewhere between here and there —

"There," Ilsa said.

She pointed with her staff. Downstream, maybe two kilometers, where the river bent around a gravel bar and slowed into a long eddy, there was a light. Not a campfire light. A work light, white and steady, mounted high enough to throw a flat disc of illumination across the water. And under it, riding low against the current, was a shape I recognized even at this distance, because I'd been picturing it since Rivergate Station, since the station keeper's evasive shrug and the unopened resupply crates and the story about a barge that came through at night and didn't stop.

The same barge. Or its twin. Either way, it had come back upriver, and now it was going home.

* * *

We worked our way downstream through the timber, fast when we could, slow when the ground demanded it. Ilsa's ankle was worse than she'd admitted — I could hear it in her breathing on the descents, a small involuntary hiss she thought she was hiding — but when I offered to leave her with the packs and go ahead alone, she looked at me like I'd suggested leaving her memory behind instead.

"I'm the witness," she said. "You're the guide. Neither of us works without the other."

Voss said nothing, but he adjusted our route without being asked, swinging us wide of the gravel bar and putting a low bluff of spruce-covered rock between us and the work light. He knew how these people operated from the inside, and he moved like a man defusing his own past: carefully, and with a certain grim respect for what it could do to him.

We reached the bluff as full dark settled, and lay on our stomachs among the roots, looking down.

The barge was moored against the bar, bow upstream, riding so heavy in the water that the freeboard was barely a hand's width. It was longer than I'd guessed from the station keeper's description — forty feet, maybe more, a flat-decked work barge with a pusher tug lashed alongside and a canvas crew shelter aft. Floodlights on poles lit the deck like a stage. And on the stage, six men were loading crates.

Not loading. *Stowing.* There's a difference, and you can see it from a hundred meters if you've spent your life around people moving things they shouldn't. These men weren't handling the crates like cargo. They were handling them like evidence — padding them, wedging them, lashing them down under tarps as if the river itself might try to subpoena them.

"Count them," Voss said quietly.

I counted. Wooden crates, roughly a meter square, maybe thirty of them along the deck, each stenciled with the same marking: a consortium logo I'd known since childhood — my father's old client, the client whose name had been on the permit applications he used to leave face-down on the kitchen table — and beneath it, in fresh paint, the word that made my jaw tighten:

**SALVAGE.**

Salvage. As in recovered. As in rescued. As in legally cleansed. Thirty crates of river-bottom history, dredged or dug or simply stolen from the terraces above Landfall, stamped with a word that turned theft into paperwork.

"That's not salvage," Ilsa breathed. Her voice had gone strange. "That's — Paloma. That's the terrace. Some of those are from the *terrace*. I saw the dig marks. I saw—"

"I know." My mouth was dry. "I know."

Because I did know. In one desperate day on the burial terrace I'd documented what my father had found and been taken away for finding: generations of river people laid to rest above the flood line, trade goods and carved paddles and the small bundled bones of children, a cemetery older than any claim the consortium could paper together. The whole legal architecture of their concession rested on that ground being empty. Empty ground could be mined. Occupied ground — *ancestral* ground — triggered review, and review meant delay, and delay meant the permits might lapse before the money started moving.

So they hadn't just erased the site. They'd *harvested* it. Dug it themselves, crate by crate, and shipped it downriver labeled as salvage — artifacts recovered from the riverbed, fair game, no heritage protection attached. If anyone ever asked where the collection came from, there would be bills of lading. There would be a paper trail. There would be nothing at all pointing upriver to a terrace of small bones.

"They're not hiding the site," I said slowly, working it through. "They're *relocating* it. They're moving the proof into private hands where no tribunal can reach it. By the time the hearing convenes, the physical evidence will be in a vault in the capital, owned by a company that doesn't have to let anyone look."

Voss was silent beside me for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was flat in a way I'd learned to read as anger held under pressure, like water behind a dam.

"I loaded three of those crates myself," he said. "Three weeks ago. At the forward camp. I was told they were core samples." He paused. "I didn't ask. That's the job. You don't ask, and the not-asking feels like professionalism right up until the night you see what's actually in the boxes, and then it feels like what it is."

"What is it?" Ilsa asked.

"Theft," Voss said. "With extra steps."

* * *

We watched for an hour. It was Ilsa who insisted on the hour, and she was right to — you don't act on a first reading of anything, my father used to say, and for once his voice in my head was useful instead of painful. In that hour we learned the shape of the operation. A foreman with a clipboard checked each crate against a list. Two men with rifles stood at the gangplank, bored, stamping their boots against the cold — guards more ceremonial than alert, because who was going to rob them out here? The pusher tug idled low, its exhaust plume standing straight up in the still air. And once, clearly, across the water, we heard the foreman say a name.

"Rivergate by tomorrow midnight. Kessler wants it off the books before the hearing clerk opens on Monday."

Kessler. The director. The man whose signature had been on the letter that hired me, buried in the university's boilerplate like a tick in fur. Hearing him named aloud, casually, over the sound of the river, did something to the whole enterprise — shrank it, somehow, made it finite. Men. Crates. A schedule. A deadline. Things with edges could be fought.

"Tomorrow midnight," I repeated. "That means they run the lower rapids at night."

Voss nodded. "Full moon's gone. They'll run dark, on the pilot's memory. It's done all the time — the cargo's worth more than the risk."

"And the pilot knows the channel."

"The pilot *is* the channel," Voss said. "Half these night runs, the man steering has run that water since he was twelve. The consortium doesn't hire pilots. It hires them away."

I lay back among the roots and looked at the canopy, and let the plan assemble itself the way a river assembles a channel — not by deciding, but by finding the lowest path and following it. We couldn't take the barge. Six men, two rifles, and Ilsa hopping on a stick. We couldn't sink it; the crates would go to the bottom, and while part of me found that deeply satisfying, the bones deserved better than a second drowning. We couldn't follow it downriver — not with Ilsa's ankle, not with Voss's team somewhere behind us closing the gap.

But we didn't need to take it. We needed to *witness* it.

"Voss," I said. "Your field log. The photos."

His head turned toward me in the dark. "What about them?"

"You photographed the terrace before you could make yourself stop. Show me the camera."

A pause. Then, slowly, he pulled it from inside his jacket — a ruggedized field unit, scuffed, its screen glowing faintly when he woke it. He thumbed through without letting me take it, and I understood: this was his confession, and he would be the one to turn the pages. Terrace shots. Dig faces. The neat rectangular scars where crates' worth of burial goods had been lifted out. And then — my breath caught — a shot of the staging area at the forward camp, timestamped, showing crates stacked under tarps, and the stencil visible on the nearest one.

**SALVAGE.**

"Timestamped," I said. "Geotagged?"

"Every frame. It's a survey unit. It logs position automatically." He looked at the screen, then at the barge below us, lights blazing, men working. "You want me to photograph the barge."

"I want the scale," I said. "Anyone can argue about a terrace. Bones are emotional; emotions get disputed. But thirty crates of 'salvage' sitting on a consortium barge, geotagged, timestamped, three days before a permit hearing — that's logistics. Logistics don't get disputed. Logistics get *prosecuted*."

Ilsa made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "You're going to document them documenting their crime."

"I'm going to do what my father wouldn't," I said, and heard the bitterness in it, and let it stand. "He found the truth and let them carry him off with it. We're going to find the truth and *mail* it."

* * *

Getting close was the easy part. The floodlights that let the crew work also blinded them; beyond the edge of the light disc, the night was absolute, and we came down through it along the shadow line of the bluff, Voss leading now because this — approaching men with guns without being seen — was the trade he'd actually practiced. Ilsa stayed at the treeline with the packs, lying flat, her staff across her knees like a rifle. Before we left her she gripped my wrist.

"If it goes wrong," she said, "don't come back for me. Go downriver. The testimony matters more than—"

"Stop." I put my hand over hers. "Nothing goes wrong tonight. Tonight we're ghosts. Save the martyrdom for the hearing."

She almost smiled. "You sound like him."

"I know," I said, and it hurt less than I expected. "Come on."

We reached the tail of the gravel bar, where the eddy curled back on itself and the water ran quiet over sand. Twenty meters of open ground separated the last spruce from the barge's stern line, and Voss took it in a low crouch, fast and silent, and I followed counting my own heartbeats. The stern of the barge rose above us, barnacled with weed to the waterline, close enough to touch. From here the deck was a wall. We pressed into the shadow under the overhanging transom, between the hull and the bank, and the river breathed cold against our necks, and above us men talked and laughed and dragged crates, oblivious.

Voss raised the camera. The shutter made no sound at all — silent mode, of course, a fixer's camera — and the screen flickered as frame after frame recorded itself: the stern registration number, painted large and official. The crate stack under its tarp corner, stencils facing outward as if begging to be read. The manifest clipboard hanging on a hook by the wheelhouse door, close enough that I could read the column headers upside down: *ITEM / ORIGIN / CONDITION.* And in the origin column, over and over, in the foreman's tidy hand: *RIVERBED — SEC. 7. RIVERBED — SEC. 7.*

Section seven. The terrace. Lying in ink, in triplicate.

Then Voss lowered the camera, and I felt him go rigid beside me.

Above us, boots crossed the deck toward the stern. A man leaned over the rail directly overhead — close enough that I could smell cigarette smoke rolling off him — and spat into the eddy, the glob of it landing in the water a hand's width from my cheek. He stood there a moment, a silhouette against the work light, rifle slung, gazing out at the darkness as if it owed him money. Then he straightened, muttered something to someone farther forward, and walked away.

We didn't move for a full minute. When Voss finally exhaled, it came out shaking.

"One more," he whispered. "Then we're done."

"One more what?"

He angled the camera up along the hull, toward the bow, where a small cabin superstructure sat above the crate deck — and on its roof, lashed under a grey tarp that covered it badly, was a shape I knew. A shape I'd grown up under. An aluminum survey boat, twelve feet, dented port gunwale, the ghost of a name still legible under a coat of grey primer where someone had tried and failed to paint it out.

*LANDFALL AND RIVERGATE SURVEY — W-3.*

My father's workboat. His third one. I'd helped him patch that dent when I was nineteen, the last summer we spoke without fighting, both of us kneeling in the shallows with epoxy on our hands, laughing about something I could no longer remember.

They hadn't just taken him. They'd taken his boat, and they were shipping it downriver as loot, and nobody had stopped them, and the river had carried all of it — the bones, the boat, the man — past every check-in point that was supposed to mean something.

"Paloma." Voss's voice, very quiet. "The camera's full. We should go."

I looked at the boat one more second. Then I touched the hull — just once, fingertips on cold wet aluminum, the way you'd touch a shoulder — and we backed away into the dark.

* * *

Ilsa was waiting at the treeline, and she read our faces before we said a word.

"It's bad," she said.

"It's worse than bad," I said. "It's organized." I told her — the manifest, the section numbers, the barge's schedule, Rivergate by tomorrow midnight. Her face went through fury and arrived somewhere colder and steadier, and I realized I was watching her become the person she was going to be for the rest of her life. Some people meet injustice and flinch. Ilsa met it and set her jaw.

"So we beat it there," she said.

"On one ankle?" I glanced at her leg. "Downriver, past the lower rapids, ahead of a powered launch?"

"Not past the rapids." She pointed her staff at the barge, at the pusher tug, at the whole lit machine of it. "*On* it. Or near it. Paloma — they're stopping at Rivergate anyway. Midnight tomorrow. And the station keeper's already bought, you said so yourself. Which means whatever they're doing at Rivergate, they're doing it comfortable, unhurried, with the landing to themselves." Her eyes were bright and hard. "Which means that's where they're weakest."

Voss grunted. "Or most guarded."

"Both," I said. "Both is fine. Both means we know where to look."

Because that was the thing the night had given us, lying in the cold under my father's stolen boat: the enemy had shown us its timetable. Everything — the crates, the manifest, the ledger copies, whatever else Kessler wanted off the books before Monday's hearing — was converging on Rivergate Station, tomorrow, midnight. One place. One window. One chance to intercept the paper trail at the same chokepoint where the physical trail passed through.

And I had one more card, the one I'd been carrying since the trapper's cache: my father's notebook, its torn pages reconstructed from fragments of the cache lining, telling us he'd copied the concession ledger and mailed it to himself. To Rivergate. To the dead-letter crate of a station keeper who was probably too scared or too bought to ever open it.

Everything was at Rivergate. The ledger. The barge. The men with badges. Maybe, if the fates were feeling generous, even the truth.

We withdrew upstream along the bluff until the work light faded behind the bend, and made a cold camp in a hollow above the water, no fire, sharing a single ration tin and the last of the tea Ilsa brewed with snowmelt in a dented cup. Voss took first watch. I lay awake listening to the river, rehearsing the next forty-eight hours, and somewhere past midnight Ilsa's voice came softly out of the dark.

"Paloma. The boat. Your father's boat, on their deck."

"Yeah."

"When we get it back—" she paused, and I heard her choose the words carefully, "—what will you do with it?"

I stared up at the stars through the spruce limbs for a long time.

"Run it," I said finally. "Down the river he never finished mapping. With his maps and my hands." I closed my eyes. "Sleep, Ilsa. Tomorrow we go to war with a station."

Below us, unseen now, the barge rode at anchor with its thirty crates of stolen centuries, waiting for midnight and the current. And downriver, past the dark miles and the lower rapids, Rivergate Station sat on its pilings with its dead-letter crate and its bought keeper and its watchmen — holding, whether it knew it or not, everything we had left to fight for.
