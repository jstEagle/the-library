## 15. Rivergate Under Watch

They came down out of the trees an hour before dusk, when the light went long and amber across the water and every shadow on the landing stood out like a signature.

Paloma lay in the alder brush above the station with the binoculars pressed so hard to her eyes they ached, counting men. Four at the dock itself, two of them armed with shotguns slung casual as fishing rods. One on the porch of the station house, talking to the keeper — Marta Vey, sixty years on this stretch of river, who had once fed Paloma smoked char off her own plate because Paloma's father had pulled her son out of a whirlpool eddy below Landfall. That was eleven years ago. The woman on the porch now wouldn't meet the fixer's eye. She kept her hands busy with a coil of rope that didn't need coiling.

Two more men sat in the consortium's fast launch, tied up where the mail barge usually moored, engines cold but fuel drums stacked on the dock beside them like a promise.

"Six," Ilsa whispered. "You said four."

"I said four yesterday." Paloma lowered the binoculars. "They've been reinforced. Which means word came down from the col. Which means Voss is late."

"He'll come."

"He'd better come tonight. The mail barge runs tomorrow at first light whether the consortium likes it or not, and if that ledger isn't out of the dead-letter crate before it does—" She stopped herself. Saying it aloud made it real, made it fragile. "It has to be tonight."

The plan had been Voss's, worked out in whispers during the last day of descent while Ilsa's ankle swelled inside her boot and the whiteout chewed the north face into static. *The station keeper knows your face,* he'd said. *And mine. But nobody down there knows what I look like anymore except as a man who climbed a mountain for them. So I walk in the front. I make noise. You go through the back, where the freight comes in.*

*And if they take you?*

He'd shrugged, that half-smile he had, the one that never reached all the way up. *Then you owe me a drink in a town with electricity.*

She hadn't asked him again after that. Asking twice would have meant she thought he might not do it, and she needed — for her own balance, her own nerve — to believe he would.

* * *

Voss left them at the treeline while there was still enough light to see the path by.

He'd cleaned himself up as best the wilderness allowed: shaved with a knife against a tin mirror, buttoned his jacket to the throat, scraped the worst of the mud from his boots. He looked less like a man who'd spent nine days in the high country and more like one who'd spent a bad weekend. It was deliberate. He wanted to be read as competent, not desperate. Desperate men got searched immediately.

"You're sure about the crate," he said to Paloma, one last time.

"Dead-letter goes under the counter, third drawer, behind the parcel shelf. My father used it for years. Anything he didn't want found until he wanted it found, he mailed to himself care of Rivergate Station and picked it up in person." She'd watched him do it when she was nineteen, the summer before everything between them went bad. He'd winked at her over the counter like a boy hiding cigarettes. *Best post office in the territory, Pally. Nobody checks the dead letters. Nobody wants to think about them.* "Marta will have kept it. Whatever else she's done, she keeps things."

"And if she's sold that too?"

"Then we're finished anyway, and it doesn't matter how we find out."

Ilsa touched his sleeve. "Come back," she said simply, and Voss looked at her hand on his arm for a long moment before he nodded.

Then he stepped out of the trees and became, in the space of twenty yards, just another tired man walking down to the river.

* * *

From the brush, Paloma could see only fragments of what happened next, and for years afterward she would fill in the gaps from what Voss told her, stitching their two views of that evening together like the torn pages of a notebook.

He came down the path whistling — actually whistling, some flat minor tune — and the man on the porch straightened first, then the two at the dock. A shotgun came off a shoulder not quite casually enough.

"Hold it there."

"Easy." Voss raised both hands, palms out, the universal grammar of harmlessness. "Edmund Voss. Consortium field operations. You've got my name on a wire from base camp, or you should."

The porch man — Paloma couldn't hear the exchange, only see its shape — sent one of the dock men up the steps. There was a short conference, low voices, the kind of conversation conducted entirely in suspicion. Then the launch engine coughed alive and someone keyed a radio, and Paloma understood: they were verifying him. Calling up the chain. Every minute of it bought her time and cost him credibility, because somewhere upriver or over the ridge, the men who'd hired him were learning that Edmund Voss had walked off the mountain three days ago and hadn't reported in.

She slid backward into the alders and began to move.

* * *

The back of Rivergate Station was all freight and forgetting. A loading ramp worn hollow by decades of crates, a door propped by a stone, a smell of diesel and dried fish and old paper that Paloma had known since childhood and that hit her now like a hand on the chest. Her father had carried her through that door once, asleep, after a crossing from the lower camps. She remembered nothing of it. She remembered being told about it, which amounted to the same thing in a family built on stories.

Inside, the freight room was dim, lit by one bulb burning brown through a fly-spattered shade. The counter ran along the far wall, and beyond it the shelves rose to the ceiling: parcels, tools, dry goods, the sediment of forty years of river commerce. And beneath the counter, exactly where memory said it would be, the dead-letter drawer.

The problem was Marta.

The old woman was in the front room, visible through the doorway, still pretending to coil rope while the fixer stood over her. Paloma could hear them now, clearly, the wall between the rooms being the thin plank construction of a building that predated insulation as a concept.

"—just asking when the barge comes in, Mrs. Vey. Routine."

"Barge comes when it comes. River decides."

"The river decided Tuesday. Barge didn't run." A pause. "We're looking for mail. Certain mail. Anything addressed to a W. Whitlock."

Paloma closed her eyes. Of course. Of course they knew his name; they'd taken the man himself. They'd had ten days to learn his habits, and somewhere in the burned camp or the forward station or the mouth of a frightened junior surveyor, someone had mentioned the dead-letter trick, or maybe they'd simply guessed, because men who erase things professionally assume everyone hides things the same way.

Her father's name, spoken in that room, in that voice, was worse than any threat. It was proof they were close.

"I don't hold mail for people who don't come get it," Marta said. "Rivergate's not a vault."

"No. But you're careful, Mrs. Vey. Careful people keep things." The voice dropped, and though Paloma couldn't make out the words that followed, she heard the register change — the register men used when courtesy stopped being worth the effort. She heard Marta's rope-coiling stop.

There was no more time for cleverness. Paloma came around the end of the counter on cat feet, knelt, and pulled the drawer.

It stuck. Forty years of river damp had swollen the runners, and it gave with a wooden groan that sounded, in the silence of the freight room, like a shout.

In the front room, the talking stopped.

Paloma froze, crouched, one hand inside the drawer, feeling past bundles of yellowed telegrams and a child's shoe and a tin of rusted hooks until her fingers found the shape she was looking for: a manila envelope, thick, wrapped in oilcloth, addressed in her father's block capitals — *W. WHITLOCK, CARE OF RIVERGATE STATION, HOLD FOR CALLER.* Postmarked eleven days ago. Mailed from the terrace country, from whatever passing boat or floatplane he'd flagged down in the last hours of his freedom.

He'd done it. He'd actually done it. Even with men coming upriver behind him, even knowing what they wanted, he'd sat down and copied the ledger page by page and mailed it to himself, because some habits run deeper than fear.

Footsteps crossed the front room toward the doorway between rooms.

Paloma took the envelope, pushed the drawer shut with her hip — gently, gently, the groan swallowed this time — and looked around the freight room for anywhere to be that wasn't here. The window over the loading ramp was nailed shut. The cellar hatch was in the open, too exposed. And then she saw it: the old grain locker at the back, door standing open six inches, big enough for a body, smelling of mouse and mold.

She was inside it, pulling the door to a finger's width from closed, when the flashlight beam swept the freight room.

* * *

Later, wedged in the dark among the ghost-smell of barley, listening to her own heart, Paloma would catalog what she heard with the terrible precision of someone memorizing evidence.

Two sets of boots. The freight room searched methodically, shelf by shelf — she could track them by sound, crates lifted and set down, the particular scrape of the dead-letter drawer being hauled open again. A long silence at the drawer. Then:

"It's been opened." The fixer's voice, tight. "Recently. Look at the dust on the runner."

"Marta?"

"I didn't touch it," Marta said, and her voice was steady, and Paloma could have kissed her. "Nobody's called for dead letters in years. Maybe mice."

"Mice don't pull drawers."

"Big mice on this river."

A chair scraped. Someone sat down heavily. "Where is he?" the fixer asked, and the question wasn't for Marta anymore. It hung in the room, general, rhetorical, aimed at the walls. "Three days off the grid. His radio's dark. He walks into a station crawling with our people and asks for a bed for the night like he's on holiday." A pause. "He's not right, Mrs. Vey. Something happened up there."

"Men come off that mountain wrong all the time," Marta said. "Mountain does what it does."

"This isn't the mountain."

Paloma put her forehead against the cool wood of the locker and breathed through her mouth. Because she knew what the fixer didn't yet know he knew: Voss had seen the bones. He'd stood on the terrace with a camera in his hand and the whole weight of his paycheck on the other side of the scale, and the bones had won. He was not right. Something had happened up there. And now he was sitting in the front room of Rivergate Station buying time with a cup of tea he didn't want, letting himself be verified and questioned and slowly cornered, because every minute he held their attention was a minute the freight room stayed empty of searching hands.

Sacrifice offered without being asked. She turned the phrase over and found she had no answer for it. Only a debt, and a deadline.

Through the crack in the locker door she could see the window going fully dark. Outside, somewhere near the dock, the fast launch's running lights came on — no, not the launch. A different boat, upstream, running down without lights until the last bend: the mail barge, early, pushing hard, as if its pilot also had reasons to be quick tonight.

The fixer heard it too. She saw him rise through the doorway, silhouette against the front windows. "That's early," he said. "Watch the dock. Watch everything. If Voss moves, hold him. If anyone comes for mail—" he paused, and Paloma felt the sentence arrive like a hand closing around her wrist— "hold them too."

Boots moved toward the front. Boots moved toward the dock. The freight room emptied.

Except it didn't. One man stayed — she could hear him, settling onto a crate by the counter with the patience of a man being paid by the hour, a shotgun barrel catching the brown light of the bulb whenever he shifted.

Between Paloma and the back door, twelve feet of open floor, sat a man with a gun and nothing to do but wait.

And outside, growing louder, the mail barge came down the last bend with its searchlight sweeping the water, and somewhere on that deck was a mail sack, and somewhere in that sack, if the fates had any decency left, was the one thing that could turn a stolen ledger into a delivered one — and Paloma Whitlock crouched in a grain locker with her father's oilcloth envelope pressed against her chest, understanding with total clarity that getting out of this room was going to cost something she hadn't planned to pay.

The man by the counter lit a cigarette. The match flared, illuminating, for half a second, the face of the youngest of the dock crew — barely older than Ilsa, nervous, thumbing the safety of his gun like a boy with a worry-stone.

The barge horn sounded at the dock. Once. Twice.

And then, from the front of the station, Voss's voice rang out, loud and drunk-sounding and utterly calculated: "Mrs. Vey! Another tea! And bring the good bottle — I'm celebrating!"

Every head in the building turned toward the sound.

Almost every head.
