## 16. What Voss Carried

The lamp in the back room of the Rivergate station burned kerosene and gave off a smell like a machine shop in winter. Paloma sat at the keeper's scarred table with her elbows on the wood and her hands flat, because if she didn't pin them down they would shake. Through the wall she could hear the muffled argument at the landing — Voss's voice, level and unhurried, and two other voices doing most of the talking. The consortium men had come up from the launch an hour after dark, and they had taken him at the door of the station without ceremony, one hand on each arm, walking him toward the fuel shed as if he were luggage.

She had watched it happen through the gap in the curtain. She had done nothing. That was the arrangement, and the arrangement was holding.

Ilsa sat across from her, sewing a torn strap on the pack with small furious stitches. Neither of them spoke. The dead-letter crate sat under the table between Paloma's boots — a dented steel box the station keeper used for mail that outlived its addressees — and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, was the envelope her father had mailed to himself eleven months ago. Inside that, copied out in his cramped, slanting hand, were the pages of the concession ledger: payments, dates, initials, the whole architecture of the lie that the burial terrace did not exist.

It should have been enough. A ledger proved motive. It proved knowledge. It proved that men had signed their names to erasure.

It did not prove the terrace itself.

Paloma had thought about this all afternoon while Ilsa splinted the firewood and the rain came down in ropes. Documents could be called forgeries. Her father's handwriting could be dismissed as the work of a disgraced man with a grudge. Ilsa's testimony was the word of a junior crew member against a consortium with three lawyers for every river bend. Even the photographs on the satellite phone — if the phone still worked, if the battery held, if anyone believed images hadn't been staged — even those were thin.

What she needed was something that came from inside the enemy's own hands.

The argument outside stopped. Boots crossed the gravel. A door closed somewhere near the fuel shed, and then there was only the rain and the generator next door coughing through its evening hour.

Ilsa looked up. "They'll hold him until the barge comes through."

"I know."

"Will he talk?"

"He talked to me on the col," Paloma said. "That's what frightens them."

* * *

He came to her window past midnight.

She heard him before she saw him — not footsteps but the absence of them, the particular silence of a man who had spent years moving through country where noise cost money. Then a soft knock, two knuckles, once, and she was across the room with the lamp turned low and the window open before her mind finished agreeing with her body.

Voss stood in the rain shadow beneath the eave. His jacket was dark with water. One eye was swelling shut where someone at the fuel shed had expressed an opinion about his loyalty, and he was smiling anyway, a thin crooked thing that had more exhaustion in it than humor.

"You're supposed to be detained," Paloma whispered.

"I am. This is me being detained very badly." He worked something from inside his jacket — a flat package wrapped in a plastic survey bag, sealed with electrical tape. "Take it."

She took it. It had weight. Not paper weight. Something denser, squared, cornered.

"What is it?"

"My field log." He glanced over his shoulder at the black rectangle of the fuel shed, where a lantern still burned behind its boarded window. "The real one. Not the summary I filed with Meridian. The one I kept for myself, because I'm a coward with a filing system." His breath fogged and tore away in the wind off the river. "There's a camera in my kit. Geological documentation, that's what I told myself it was for. Strata, exposures, soil profiles. Standard practice." He laughed, quietly, horribly. "I stood on that terrace for four hours telling myself I was photographing soil profiles."

Paloma's hands tightened on the package. "You were there."

"Before your father was taken upriver. Before the burn team came in. I surveyed the terrace for the heritage assessment — the real one, the one that never got filed." His good eye found hers, and in it she saw the same thing she'd seen on the col at twelve thousand feet, when he'd offered terms he clearly didn't want her to accept: a man standing in the wreckage of his own decisions. "I documented it properly. Grid squares. Scale bars. Context numbers. Four hundred years of river people buried in terraced layers, and every photograph timestamped and geotagged by equipment Meridian issued me themselves." He shook his head slowly. "They paid for the evidence of their own crime. They handed me the camera."

"Why give it to me now? Why not walk away? You could still—"

"Walk away to where?" He said it without bitterness, which made it worse. "I've been walking away for six months. You don't sleep in that job, Whitlock. You wait. There's a difference, and I finally learned which one I was doing." Rain ran off his jaw. "They're putting me on the morning barge downriver. Officially I'm being rotated out. Unofficially—" he touched the swollen eye, "—unofficially this is the conversation where they find out whether I'm rotated or retired."

"So we get you out. We go downriver tonight, all three of us, the skiff—"

"No." He said it flatly, and it ended the sentence. "If three of us run, they chase three of us, and they have a fast launch and you have a patched hull. If I stay, they watch me sit in a shed, and you go downriver tomorrow with a head start and everything you need." He stepped back from the window, into the rain. "Besides. Somebody has to be on that barge counting crates. You saw what's in them. Somebody who's going to be in the room when the tribunal asks questions ought to know exactly how many grave goods left this valley in private cargo."

"Voss—"

"Whitlock." He almost smiled again. "Your father wrote in his route notes — chapter and verse, margins full of it — that the river forgives nothing. He was right. But it keeps everything. Every hull panel, every ledger page, every bone. It keeps it all and gives it back to whoever's patient enough to stand on the bank." He tapped the plastic bag against her window frame, once, like a man sealing a contract. "Be patient. Be loud. And when they ask you how you got the log, tell them the truth. Tell them a compromised man couldn't stop himself. It'll be more believable than any cover story either of us could build."

Then he was gone, into the dark between the buildings, and thirty seconds later she heard the fuel shed door open and close, and the lantern behind its boards went out.

* * *

Ilsa slept. Paloma did not.

She trimmed the lamp wick until the flame stood no taller than a thumbnail, set the field log on the table, and cut the tape with her belt knife. Inside the plastic bag was a hardbound notebook, water-stained at the corners, and a padded envelope heavy with memory cards, each one labeled in Voss's precise engineer's hand: T-01 through T-14. Terrace, she guessed. Fourteen cards. She turned the notebook open under the light.

The first pages were exactly what he'd said — geology. Grain sizes, slope angles, stratigraphic columns drawn in ruler-straight lines. She read three pages of it and felt the strange intimacy of reading a stranger's diary: here was a mind that took comfort in measurement, that built itself little rooms of certainty and lived in them. She recognized the architecture. Her father had the same rooms. Maybe everyone who worked rivers did — you measured what you could measure because the rest of it, the water, the weather, the men with badges, would not be measured.

Then, forty pages in, the columns stopped.

In their place: sketches. A terrace plan. Grid squares laid out in pencil over the contour lines, each square numbered, each number keyed to entries in a running log. And beside the plan, in handwriting that had begun controlled and deteriorated page by page into something rawer, the context notes.

*Layer 2, Sq. C4: flexed burial, adult, river-cobble cairn above cranium. Textile fragment, preserved by mineral staining. This is not seasonal. This is not isolated.*

*Layer 3, Sq. B7: juvenile interment with carved antler toggle. Same tradition as L2. Depth consistent with 300–500 yrs BP.*

*Note: Meridian's submitted assessment describes this landform as "recent alluvial deposit, no cultural material observed." I walked this deposit for four hours. Whoever wrote that sentence never stood here. Or stood here and lied.*

Paloma turned pages with fingers that had gone careful, the way they went careful around bone.

He had photographed everything. The card labels matched the grid: T-03, central terrace; T-07, cairn detail; T-11, textile macro shots. She slid the first card into the satellite phone — hers, the dropped one from the forward camp, charged on the station's generator all afternoon — and the screen lit and filled with an image so clear it hurt.

A burial cairn, half-excavated, photographed straight down with a scale bar laid beside it. Timestamp in the corner. GPS coordinates burned into the file metadata by the phone itself. Coordinates that put the image, beyond argument, beyond forgery, on the terrace above Landfall rapids — the same coordinates written into her father's stolen notebook, the same coordinates the consortium's heritage assessment declared empty.

She scrolled. Cairn after cairn. Layer after layer. A child's antler toggle resting on a foam pad with a coin for scale. A textile fragment spread on gray board, its pattern still visible after centuries in the ground — river people, generations of them, laid into the terrace their descendants still fished below. The last card held wide shots of the whole landform, and in the corner of the final frame, accidentally, the edge of a consortium survey stake, flagged orange, driven into the burial layer itself.

She sat back. The lamp hissed. On the table, the ledger in its oilcloth and the field log in its plastic lay side by side, and she understood, with the slow cold clarity of a river dropping into a single channel, what she was holding.

The ledger proved they knew. Voss's log proved it was there. Each one alone could be argued with, explained away, buried in motions and counter-expertise. Together they were a closed circuit: motive and corpus delicti, the signature and the grave, each authenticating the other. His photos carried Meridian's own GPS metadata. Their ledger carried Meridian's own initials. There was no version of the story, told by any lawyer in any hearing room, in which both documents were true and Meridian was innocent.

"They built it themselves," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the rain. "Every piece of it. They built the case against themselves and paid for the privilege."

Ilsa stirred in her bunk. "Is it enough?" she murmured, half asleep.

Paloma looked at the photographs glowing on the little screen — the child's toggle, the cairns, the orange flag in the bones — and thought about her father writing these same coordinates in a notebook whose last pages had been torn out by men who understood exactly what paper can do. He had tried to carry the truth out alone and been carried off instead. She was carrying it out with a defector's log and a dead-letter envelope and a junior crew member's testimony, and none of it clean, none of it pure, every piece of it handled by compromised hands.

Justice, she thought, wasn't assembled from clean hands. It was assembled from whatever hands were willing, stitched together in lamplight, and sent downriver before anyone could stop it.

"It's enough," she said. "It has to move tomorrow."

She repacked the cards, wrapped the log and the ledger together in the oilcloth, and sewed the bundle into the lining of her jacket with Ilsa's needle and thread, stitch by stitch, while the rain kept on and the river kept saying what it always said.

It was nearly dawn when she heard the barge horn on the water — long, low, twice — and under it, thinner, the sound of a fast launch coming upriver against the current, running lights cutting the fog.

Two boats. Coming from opposite directions. Both looking for her.

Paloma blew out the lamp.
