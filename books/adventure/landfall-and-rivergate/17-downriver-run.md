## 17. Downriver Run

The skiff was a liar. That was the first thing Paloma decided about her, standing in the gray before dawn with her boots in six inches of river and her hands on a hull that had been patched more times than it had been built. The boatman who'd sold it to them — traded, really, for Voss's satellite phone and a promise neither of them intended to keep — had called her sound. He'd said *she'll hold* the way men say things they want to be true.

She'd held so far. Two days downriver from Rivergate Station, through water that ran brown and fast with the melt nobody had asked for, and she'd held. But Paloma could feel every seam of her talking through the floorboards, a low mutter of flex and complaint, like an old woman being made to run.

"You're going to sink us," Ilsa said from the bow. She sat with her sprained ankle propped on a crate, both hands locked on the gunwale, her face still carrying the green-gray cast it had worn since the whiteout on the north face.

"Not today."

"That's what you said about the pass."

"The pass held too." Paloma pushed off the gravel bar and took the stern oars. "We just didn't enjoy it much."

Behind them, upriver where the channel bent around the tail of Coalbank Island, the morning was still dark enough to hide things. That was the whole arithmetic of the day: dawn was their margin. The consortium's fast launch had been tied at Rivergate when Voss walked into the landing to be a distraction, and a fast launch burned fuel and needed a crew, and crews slept, argued, second-guessed. Every hour of darkness was another hour of lead.

But the ledger was sewn into the lining of Paloma's jacket, against her ribs, and it weighed more than paper had any right to weigh. And somewhere behind them — hours behind, maybe one hour, maybe less — the launch was coming downriver after it.

Voss's field log rode in the same jacket pocket as her compass. She'd read it twice by lamplight in the dead-letter room at Rivergate, once for the bones and once for the man. Forty-one photographs. Dates, coordinates, his own handwriting admitting what he'd seen on the terrace before he could make himself look away. *I was sent to erase a cemetery,* he'd written, in the flat bureaucratic hand of someone trained to write reports, *and instead I have become its witness.* She hadn't known a man like Voss could write a sentence like that. It had rearranged something in her chest, and she hadn't had time yet to figure out what.

"Paloma." Ilsa's voice came low and tight. "Look at the water."

Paloma looked. The lower rapids began without ceremony — no roar announcing them, just a change in the river's texture, the smooth brown skin of the channel suddenly wrinkled and broken, a long diagonal seam of white water running bank to bank half a mile ahead. Below Landfall, the river forgot its manners. Everyone knew that. It was why the towns existed where they did, why the barge traffic stopped at the landing, why the whole economy of the valley ended at this line in the water like a sentence with a period.

The fast launch couldn't shoot these rapids. Nobody shot these rapids in anything with an engine, because the rocks at the bottom of the chute would open an aluminum hull like a tin can, and everyone knew that too.

Which meant the launch would put in above the rapids, and its crew would come down the portage trail on foot, and they'd be waiting at the bottom when Paloma and Ilsa came through.

Unless.

She'd been turning the idea over since midnight, testing it the way you test ice, putting weight on it slowly. Now, with the seam of white water growing in front of her and the sky going from charcoal to dirty silver at her back, she let herself say it out loud.

"I'm not going to run away from them," she said. "I'm going to run into them."

Ilsa turned around carefully, favoring the ankle. "Explain that like I'm not exhausted."

"There's a shallows below the chute. Gravel bar, braided channels, maybe two feet deep at this flow. Fishermen work it in the season. There's a fish camp there — smokehouse, drying racks, three or four families. Marta Kowalczyk's people; I guided her sons through the passes two summers ago." Paloma kept her eyes on the water while she talked, reading the current the way her father had taught her to read it before he'd taught her anything else about himself. "If we wreck in front of witnesses, we're not fugitives anymore. We're a story. Stories travel faster than launches."

"You want to crash on purpose."

"I want to crash *visibly.*"

"And if you misjudge it?"

Paloma fed the oars through and felt the current take the bow, gentle now, coaxing, the way a con artist is gentle right up until he isn't. "Then I'll have a lot of time underwater to reconsider my choices."

* * *

The chute took twenty minutes of her full attention and gave nothing back.

She ran it the way her father's maps said it wanted to be run — left of the first seam, hard right past the fang rock that showed only as a boil of disturbed water, then straight down the throat of the channel with the walls of spray closing in on either side. The skiff screamed. Every patch on her hull sang a different note. Twice the river picked the stern up and moved it sideways like a hand correcting a child's grip, and twice Paloma hauled it back with arms that had stopped hurting an hour ago because they'd gone past hurt into something else.

Ilsa didn't scream either. She just said "left" and "right" in a small clear voice, calling the water ahead of them, and Paloma understood that the junior crew member who'd hidden in a root cellar for nine days while men with consortium badges raked her camp had found, somewhere between the terrace and the col, a spine of pure iron.

Then the chute spat them out, and the river went wide and shallow and confused, splitting into braids across the gravel shoals, and the fish camp was there in the early light exactly as Paloma remembered it — smokehouse leaning, racks hung with the last of the autumn catch, nets spread on frames, and three figures already out at the water's edge because fishermen were always out at the water's edge at dawn, because fish didn't care about anyone's schedule.

And there, pulled up on the bar at the head of the shallows with its engine ticking as it cooled, sat the consortium's fast launch.

They'd beaten the rapids by portaging in the dark. Of course they had. They had money and motors and men who carried things, and Paloma had given them a night's head start by stopping to sleep. Four men stood on the gravel near the boat, and one of them was pointing, and even at two hundred meters Paloma could see the gesture's meaning plain as speech: *there, that's them, take the near channel.*

"Paloma—" Ilsa started.

"I see them."

The near channel was a lie. She knew it the way she knew her own hands. At this flow it looked like the obvious line — wide, smooth, direct, running straight at the camp — but underneath its calm surface a gravel shelf waited, the kind of bar that had grounded a hundred drunk fishermen and one very sober surveyor's daughter. The deep water ran the far braid, close under the cut bank, narrow and fast and honest.

The men were wading toward the near channel's mouth. One of them carried a boat hook. Another had something slung across his back that Paloma chose to believe was a rifle case and refused to think about further.

She swung the skiff's bow toward the near channel anyway.

"What are you doing?" Ilsa's voice cracked upward. "That's where they are—"

"Exactly." Paloma shipped the oars and took the steering sweep. "Hold on to something that loves you."

The current grabbed them and the shallows opened up ahead — broad, glittering, deceptively kind in the new light, the far bank lined with drying racks and smokehouse and the three fishermen who had stopped working and turned to watch, because a boat running the lower rapids at dawn was not a thing you saw every week, and human beings were built to watch disasters the way plants were built to turn toward sun.

Two hundred meters. The men in the channel mouth were shouting now, waving the boat hook, trying to flag her down or warn her off or both. A hundred meters. She could see faces — hard, tired, hired faces, and one of them she recognized from the ridge above the terrace, a big man with a beard who'd fired no shots but had stood at the edge of the trees all afternoon like a fence post with opinions.

Fifty meters. The gravel shelf rose to meet them.

Paloma put the skiff's port side onto it at speed.

The sound was enormous — a grinding, tearing crunch that went up through the hull and down through her wrists and teeth, and then the world tilted, and the river came aboard in a cold sheet, and the skiff slewed sideways across the bar with her keel opening along the gravel like a zipper. Paloma went into the water. It wasn't deep — waist-deep, then knee-deep, then she was on her feet with the current shoving at her thighs and the wrecked skiff lying against the shelf beside her, half out of the water, dying loudly.

She surfaced sputtering, and the first thing she did — before checking her own body, before looking for Ilsa — was clap a hand to her jacket. The ledger was there. The log was there. The compass was gone, spinning away downstream, and she watched it go without regret. She wouldn't need directions again.

"Ilsa!"

"Here." Ilsa stood thigh-deep ten feet downstream, soaked, furious, upright, holding her crate of documentation above the waterline with both arms like a mother lifting a child out of traffic. "You are *insane.*"

"Probably." Paloma got a shoulder under the skiff's gunwale and shoved, rolling the hull higher onto the bar so it wouldn't wash off, making the wreck permanent, making it *exhibit A*. "Help me beach her. All the way up."

The four men had stopped at the edge of the deep channel. They'd come expecting to intercept a fleeing boat, to board it, to take whatever it carried, quiet and quick, before anyone downstream was the wiser. Instead they were standing in rising daylight in front of a fish camp full of witnesses, watching two women climb out of a deliberately wrecked boat in the middle of the most public shallows on the river.

The bearded man recovered first. "Whitlock!" His voice carried flat across the water. "You're done running. Hand over what you took from Rivergate and this stays simple."

Paloma waded to the bar and stood beside her ruined skiff, water streaming off her, and she made herself smile. It wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile she'd learned from watching her father work a room full of donors — the one that meant *you have already lost and don't know it yet.*

"Simple," she repeated, loud enough to reach the smokehouse. Loud enough to reach the three fishermen, who had come down to the water's edge now, and the two more who'd emerged from the smokehouse itself, and Marta Kowalczyk's oldest son, who stood with his arms crossed and a filleting knife still in his hand, watching the men in consortium jackets with the particular patience of a man deciding how many problems he was willing to have today. "That's funny. Let me tell you what's simple. This is the Kowalczyk fish camp. These are eleven witnesses, counting the kids in the smokehouse. You've been chasing two women across a hundred miles of river to take a piece of paper that says what your company buried on the terrace above Landfall." She patted her soaked jacket, and the ledger inside it made a soft wet slap. "It's evidence now. It has witnesses. Everything you do next happens in front of them."

The bearded man's jaw worked. Behind him, one of the others had begun, very quietly, to edge back toward the launch.

"You wrecked your own boat," he said.

"I did." She let him hear how little shame there was in it. "Ask yourself why a woman would rather drown her own skiff than let you touch what's in her jacket. Then ask yourself what a tribunal's going to ask you."

Marta Kowalczyk's son spat into the water, unhurried, deliberate, a punctuation mark. "These women eat breakfast with us," he said to the bearded man, in the tone of a man stating the weather. "You can wait on the bar."

* * *

They ate breakfast with the Kowalczyks.

Smoked whitefish, strong tea, bread that Marta's daughter-in-law pressed on them while telling Ilsa, in the unanswerable way of grandmothers everywhere, that she was too thin and had clearly been through something and would say no more about it because it wasn't polite to ask. Outside, the four consortium men waited on the gravel bar beside their launch, and the waiting had a different quality than pursuit. Pursuit had momentum. Waiting had doubt. Paloma watched them through the window over the tea kettle and saw the doubt working in them like water working a seam — the youngest one sitting down on a crate, the one with the rifle case setting it down and not picking it up again, the bearded man talking on a satellite phone with his back turned and his shoulders climbing toward his ears.

"He's getting instructions," Ilsa said.

"He's getting *contradictions.*" Paloma turned her cup slowly. "Somebody upstream is telling him to take the paper. Somebody legal is telling him what happens to a company whose fixers assault witnesses in front of a fish camp the week before a permit hearing. He's caught between orders and consequences, and consequences are winning."

Voss had told her how it worked, in the cache on the mountain, in the voice of a man confessing a mechanism he'd served for years. *Nobody down the chain knows anything. They know routes and names and what they're paid. The rot is all at the top, and the top is terrified of paper.* She hadn't fully believed him. Watching the bearded man's shoulders now, she believed him completely.

A boy of maybe ten came in from the bar and reported, breathless, that the angry men were arguing, and that one of them had thrown his hat in the water, and that the launch was being loaded — but not to chase anybody. To leave. Upriver.

Paloma set down her cup.

"They're not going back for orders," she said. "They're going back because there's nothing left to take." She stood, and her whole body announced its opinion of the previous forty-eight hours, and she ignored it. "Get your pack. We're walking to Landfall."

"Walk? It's thirty kilometers."

"Down a road, past three farms, arriving mid-morning with a fish camp full of witnesses who watched armed men chase us." She shouldered her pack and grinned, and this time the grin surprised her by being real. "Ilsa, we're not running anymore. We're arriving."

At the door, Marta Kowalczyk caught her arm. Her English was slow and precise, chosen like stones. "My son will radio ahead," she said. "To the tribunal house. They will know you are coming." Her eyes went to Paloma's soaked jacket, to the shape of the ledger beneath the fabric, and back up. "My mother's family is from the terrace country. Before the surveys. Before the companies." She squeezed once, hard. "Tell them everything."

Outside, the morning had gone fully bright, and the river ran on past the wrecked skiff on the bar, carrying its cold cargo of light toward the sea, and Paloma Whitlock stepped onto the road to Landfall with the truth against her ribs and, for the first time in ten years, nowhere left to hide and no reason left to try.
