## 18. The Tribunal at Landfall

Landfall smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, the way river towns smell in late autumn when everyone is pretending winter is a rumor. Paloma came down off the water at midmorning with Ilsa limping beside her and Voss's field log zipped into her jacket against her ribs, and she did not stop. Not for the dockmaster's questions, not for the woman selling fried dough on the boardwalk, not even to look back at the gray water where two boats lay wrecked in the shallows behind them, still leaking diesel into the current like a signature.

The tribunal hall sat at the top of the town's single hill, a squat stone building that had been a customs house before it was anything else, its lintel carved with scales worn nearly smooth by a century of rain. A banner had been strung above the doors: *CONCESSION RENEWAL HEARING — UPPER RIVERGATE TERRACES — PUBLIC SESSION.* Beneath it, men in consortium windbreakers stood smoking, their breath pluming white, their eyes tracking every person who climbed the steps.

Paloma counted four of them. She let them count her.

"You're bleeding," Ilsa said quietly.

"It's from yesterday."

"Then you should have washed it off." Ilsa's ankle was wrapped in strips of Voss's spare shirt, and she had refused a crutch since the landing, walking with a stubborn hitch that dared anyone to offer help. "You want them to see you like this?"

"I want them to see me exactly like this." Paloma pushed open the door.

Inside, the hall was warmer than she expected and more crowded. Folding chairs in rows, a clerk's table, a raised bench where three tribunal officers sat beneath a faded portrait of some founding magistrate whose face had gone the color of weak tea. The room hummed with the particular energy of people who had come to watch something break — outfitters, homesteaders, a delegation of river families in their good coats, and along one wall, a cluster of consortium lawyers with leather folders arranged on their knees like ammunition.

At the front, a clerk sat behind a small desk with a brass bell he never seemed to use. Paloma went to him directly, before her nerve could reconsider.

"I'm filing evidence," she said. "In the matter of the concession renewal."

The clerk looked up. He was young, with the careful blankness of someone who had been warned about this exact moment. "Filing closes at noon."

"Then I'm early." She set the ledger on his desk first — her father's copy, reconstructed page by page from the fragments lining the trapper's cache, wrapped in oilcloth and dry despite everything the mountain had done to them. Then the documentation from the terrace: photographs, her field notes, the stratigraphic sketches she'd made in one desperate day while fixers watched the ridge. Last, Voss's log, still smelling faintly of the man who'd carried it up a frozen col to offer terms and then, somewhere between the col and the river, changed sides without ever saying so out loud.

The clerk weighed each item with his eyes. "Name?"

"Whitlock. Paloma Whitlock."

Something moved through the room then — not loud, but total, the way a current moves under ice. *Whitlock.* The name meant expeditions, headlines, a man whose face had been on the funding posters in every university hallway for twenty years. It also meant, as of three weeks ago, missing.

A lawyer near the wall leaned toward another and murmured. The murmur spread.

"Exhibits are entered," the clerk said, stamping each folder. "You'll be called if the panel wishes to hear you."

"They'll wish," Paloma said.

She took a seat in the last row, Ilsa beside her, and only then allowed herself to look around properly. The hearing had already begun — an officer reading procedural language in a voice like gravel poured slowly, counsel for the consortium droning through a summary of "sustained economic benefit" and "responsible stewardship." The words were familiar. They were the same words her father had once used over dinner, years ago, explaining why he'd signed on to survey the terraces: *someone has to map it honestly, Pally, and better me than a company man.*

She had believed him. That was the wound under all the others — not that he'd lied, but that she'd stopped checking whether he had.

The morning crawled. Witnesses for the renewal testified: a logistics manager, a safety officer, a man whose entire statement was a recitation of employment figures. Each time the doors opened, cold air swept down the aisle and Paloma turned to look, and each time it was only a fisherman or a clerk or a child sent in from the cold.

It was past noon, after a recess nobody wanted, when the doors opened again and stayed open, and two men walked in flanking a third who walked like a man being carried by his own skeleton.

Paloma stood up before she knew she'd decided to.

Her father had lost weight the way rivers lose banks — gradually, then all at once. His beard was wrong, uneven, grown in captivity. He wore a consortium-issued parka with the logo half peeled away, as if he'd started removing it himself and been interrupted. His hands were in his pockets. His eyes found the floor and stayed there, cataloguing cracks, until the escort guided him to the witness chair and he had no choice but to lift his head.

He saw her in the last row.

Paloma had rehearsed nothing for this moment. Ten years of rehearsals — arguments composed in tents, letters drafted and burned, the whole architecture of resentment she'd built to keep herself upright — and none of it survived the actual sight of him: alive, diminished, looking at her across forty feet of provincial linoleum like a man looking at shore from very far out.

His mouth formed her name. No sound came out.

She did not sit down. She did not go to him either. She stood in the aisle with Ilsa's hand suddenly gripping her sleeve, holding both of them steady, and she nodded once — the smallest possible acknowledgment, the kind you give across a rapid to say *I see you, hold your line* — and then she sat, because the hearing was not finished and neither, apparently, were they.

* * *

The consortium's counsel produced him like an exhibit of their own.

"This witness," the lead lawyer said, smoothing his tie, "was recovered from a remote worksite following what we understand was a disorientation episode. Dr. Whitlock has served this territory with distinction—"

"Objection to characterization," said the tribunal chair, a woman named Ondar with gray braids and the patience of permafrost. "Dr. Whitlock will speak for himself. Dr. Whitlock, do you understand why you're here?"

Her father's hands came out of his pockets. They were shaking. Paloma watched him look at them as though they belonged to a stranger, and then fold themselves flat on his knees.

"I understand," he said. His voice was hoarse, rusted from disuse. "I was asked to sign a heritage assessment stating that the terrace site above Landfall rapids contained no significant cultural remains. I was told the assessment was a formality. When I completed my actual survey, I found that it was not a formality. There are remains. Generations of them. Burial terraces, intact, extensive." He paused, and the pause cost him visibly. "I refused to sign the false version. After that, I was not permitted to leave."

"Permitted," Ondar repeated. "By whom?"

"Men employed by the concession holder. They held me at a forward camp upriver. They took my crew's ledger. They burned my camp and raked the ash." He said it flatly, a man reading coordinates off a chart, because that was the only way left to say it. "One of my crew, a junior surveyor, escaped into the bush. Her name is Ilsa Vannet. She is in this room."

Every head turned. Ilsa lifted her chin and did not flinch, and Paloma felt something in her chest crack slightly — pride, maybe, arriving sideways.

"The ledger," Ondar said. "Do you know where it is?"

"I copied it," her father said. "Page by page, before they took the original. I mailed the copy to myself at Rivergate station. I didn't trust—" his voice caught, steadied, "—I didn't trust anyone closer than the post office."

Paloma's hand went to the clerk's desk without her deciding to move. "Exhibit one," she said, and her voice carried further than she intended. "Filed this morning. The copied ledger, complete, in Dr. Whitlock's hand. Along with photographic documentation of the burial terrace, taken eleven days ago, and the field log of Marcus Voss, formerly of the concession holder's security detail, which includes photographs of the same terrace taken before the attempted erasure."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere near the consortium lawyers a folder snapped shut like a jaw.

Ondar looked at Paloma for a long moment. "And you are?"

"Paloma Whitlock. Contract archaeologist, lowlands district. I led the recovery of the evidence and I witnessed the terrace site directly." She paused, and then gave them the rest, because the rest was the point. "My father is my estranged parent. I took this job for a permit reference, not for him. Everything I've filed stands independent of anything he says."

Someone in the river-family delegation laughed — short, surprised, approving. Her father closed his eyes briefly, and Paloma would have sworn, later, that it looked almost like relief. Like a man setting down weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

"Dr. Whitlock," Ondar said, turning back. "Why did you hide the copy instead of reporting it?"

"Because I didn't know who was bought." He said it simply. "I do now. Some of them are in this room."

* * *

They recessed at dusk. The panel would reconvene tomorrow; the consortium's counsel requested time to review the new exhibits, and Ondar granted it with the thin smile of a woman watching lawyers discover that paper cuts both ways.

Outside, the temperature had dropped hard. Frost furred the tribunal hall's iron railing, and the streetlamps came on one by one down the hill, lighting the river below in broken orange pieces. Paloma stood on the steps breathing steam, Ilsa beside her on the low wall, and tried to feel triumphant and mostly felt hollowed out, the way a dig site feels at the end of a season — exposed, unfinished, waiting for weather.

"He looked at you," Ilsa said. "When he came in. Before anything else."

"I noticed."

"What are you going to do about it?"

"I don't know yet." Paloma turned her hands over, studying the rope burns and the split knuckles, the archaeologist's calluses layered over the guide's. "Ten years I built a case against him. It was a good case. Now he goes and testifies against himself in front of the whole territory, and the whole thing collapses, and I'm supposed to just—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He didn't do it for me. He did it because it was true. That's the only reason I can believe any of this, and it might be enough. It might be all there is."

Ilsa bumped her shoulder, gently, on the bad-ankle side. "For what it's worth, he looked at you like a man reading a map he thought he'd lost."

"That's exactly the problem with him," Paloma said. "Everything's a map."

The door opened behind them. Footsteps on frost-brittle stone, unhurried, and then her father's voice, close and ruined and careful:

"Pally."

She didn't turn right away. She let the old nickname land, let it hurt, let it be true. Down the hill the river ran dark toward the rapids, carrying the last light out of the valley, and somewhere upstream the burial terrace slept under its first snow, patient as it had been for five hundred years, keeping everyone's names.

"I heard your testimony," she said finally, turning. Up close he was worse and better — worse in the face, better in the eyes, which were clear in a way she hadn't seen since she was a child. Sober eyes. Accountable ones. "It was good testimony."

"It was late testimony." He glanced at Ilsa, and something passed between them — the debt of a life, acknowledged without ceremony. "Miss Vannet. You got out. I'm glad."

"She got us both out," Ilsa said, and hopped off the wall. "I'm going to find food. You two have ten years to get through and I refuse to watch."

She limped off down the hill, leaving them alone in the lamplight with the frost and the river sound rising faintly from below.

"I'm not going to ask if you're all right," Paloma said. "I watched what they did to your camp. I know what they did to you."

"No. You know some of it." He tucked his shaking hands under his arms, an old habit, hiding weakness the way he'd hidden everything. "There are things I haven't said. Things I did before they ever came for me — calls I took, money I accepted for the first survey season before I understood what it was buying. If the tribunal digs, they'll find it, and it should be found. I won't have you defending a clean version of me. You've earned the real one. You especially."

Paloma looked at him for a long time. The anger was still there — she could feel it living in her like an organ, functional, permanent — but beside it now sat something else, smaller and harder to name. Not forgiveness. Recognition, maybe. The understanding that maps are drawn by flawed hands and still, sometimes, get you home.

"Tomorrow," she said, "when they try to make me a trespasser, you stay seated. Let me fight my own fight."

"And after?"

"After," she said, "we'll see. One day at a time. Same as the river."

Behind them, the tribunal hall doors banged open, and a clerk came out into the cold with a lantern, calling down the steps: "Miss Whitlock? Miss Whitlock — the panel reconvenes tonight, emergency session. The consortium's director has arrived on the evening barge." He caught his breath. "And he's asking to testify."
