## 19. Hearing Day

The tribunal hall in Landfall had been a cannery once, and it hadn't tried to be anything else. The corrugated walls still sweated rust in the corners, the ceiling beams still carried the hooks where salmon had hung by the thousand, and the smell of brine and old diesel lived in the timber the way a stain lives in bone. They'd set up folding tables on the raised loading dock at one end — three tribunal members behind them, a clerk with a laptop that kept losing its signal, and a flag of the territory hanging crooked from a nail. The public benches were church pines salvaged from somewhere, and they were full.

Paloma counted the room before she let herself feel it. River people in their good coats. Two women from the fish co-op who'd fed her and Ilsa the night they came downriver. A schoolteacher she didn't know taking notes like this was a civics lesson he'd waited his whole life to teach. Voss's lawyer, a sharp-faced man named Okonkwo who'd arrived on the morning flight with two boxes of documents and an expression of permanent mild surprise. And along the back wall, arms folded, faces arranged into expressions of patient reasonableness: the consortium's people. Six of them. Their director, Aldous Merrick, sat apart from even his own delegation, in the front row on the aisle, gray-suited, silver-haired, hands loose on his knees like a man watching weather he'd already decided to enjoy.

Her father sat beside her at the petitioners' table, and Paloma was aware of him the way you're aware of a fire in the next room.

They'd shaved him. Given him a clean shirt. The university's counsel had argued all morning yesterday about whether he could testify at all — coerced testimony, compromised witness, chain of custody of his own mind — and the tribunal had split the difference: he would speak, but only after Paloma, and only within her filing. He was Exhibit F, essentially. A human attachment to a document.

He hadn't looked at her yet. Not really. He'd looked at the table, at his own hands, at the ledger lying between them under a plastic sleeve. When the clerk called the session to order and everyone stood for some territorial formula recited in a bored voice, he stood too, half a beat late, like a man remembering the customs of a country he'd been deported from.

"The tribunal will hear petition nineteen," said the chairwoman. Her name was Magda Reyes, and she had the flat, unhurried voice of someone who'd spent thirty years listening to men explain why the rules shouldn't apply to them just this once. "Concession renewal, Kettle Range holdings, twelve thousand hectares above Landfall rapids. Petitioners have filed for emergency review." She looked down. "Ms. Whitlock. You'll proceed."

Paloma stood. Her legs worked, which surprised her. She'd rehearsed nothing on purpose — Ilsa had told her, on the barge dock at dawn, *don't perform it, just say what happened, they can smell performance* — and so she opened her mouth and said what happened.

She started with the letter that smelled of diesel. She gave them the dates: the missed radio checks, the resupply crates unopened at Rivergate Station, the station keeper's eyes going anywhere but hers when she asked about the night barge. She told them about running Landfall with two boatmen and pulling her father's notebook out of the eddy below the third chute, its last pages torn out. She watched the room while she talked, because that was the trick of it — not watching the tribunal, watching the room. The river people leaned forward when she described the camp that had been swept. Burned, raked, lime-dusted. Professionally erased. She saw an old man near the back mouth something to his neighbor, and the neighbor nod, and she understood that they had known. Not the details. But they had known that something upriver had been bought and buried, the way you know a dog has been kicked without seeing it happen.

Then she came to the terrace.

"I'm a contract archaeologist," she said. "Ten years of salvage digs in the lowlands. I know what a burial ground looks like, and I know what it looks like when someone decides it doesn't look like that anymore." She laid out the photographs — hers, then Voss's, side by side on the document screen the clerk managed to get working. The terrace above the river bend. The stone cairns. The small bones. "Four generations minimum, by the stratigraphy we documented in a single day under pursuit. River people. The ancestors of people in this room." She let that sit. "The consortium's heritage assessment, filed eighteen months ago, describes this location as 'sterile gravel bench, no cultural material.' That assessment was signed off on the strength of my father's survey. His real survey said otherwise."

Merrick shifted in his seat. It was the first movement he'd made, and it was very small — a man adjusting his weight, nothing more — and Paloma understood that everything he did today would be exactly that small. He wasn't going to bluster. He was going to be reasonable to death.

"Your father's real survey," Reyes said. "You've filed the reconstructed ledger."

"I have. He copied the concession ledger — the payments, the directives, the instruction to reclassify the terrace — and mailed it to himself at Rivergate Station rather than hand it over. It's been in the dead-letter crate since August. The postmark is verifiable. The handwriting is his. I'll note for the record that I verified it against forty years of his field notebooks, which I grew up reading."

"And your father confirms this?"

"He'll speak for himself," Paloma said. "When you're ready for him."

Reyes made a note. "We'll come to him. First — Mr. Merrick's office has filed a motion challenging the admissibility of all petitioner evidence on grounds of trespass. Your documentation was gathered, I believe, on concession land, without permit, during a period when the concession holder claims exclusive access rights." She looked up, and there was something almost apologetic in it. "The motion will be heard. You should respond to it now if you intend to."

*There it is.* Paloma had known it was coming since the pass, since Voss first warned her on the cold scree: *they won't fight the bones, they'll fight you. You're easier to bury than a cemetery.*

"May I respond at length?" she asked.

"You may."

She turned to face the room instead of the bench, because the motion wasn't really aimed at the bench. It was aimed at the pines full of river people, and she intended to answer it where it landed.

"Mr. Merrick's motion says I trespassed," she began. "That's true. I walked onto land his company leases, without permission, and I dug in it, and I took photographs, and I ran from his employees through a snowstorm. Every word of it is accurate, and I'd like to thank him for putting it in writing, because it saves me the trouble of establishing jurisdiction over my own legs."

A ripple went through the pines. Somewhere a woman laughed once, sharply, and stopped herself.

"But notice what the motion does not say. It does not say the terrace isn't there. It does not say the bones are fake. It does not say the ledger is forged, or that my father's signature was obtained honestly, or that the night barge out of Rivergate was carrying anything other than what we found in its hold — which, for the record, is catalogued in Exhibit D: ninety-one crates of funerary goods labeled as 'salvage aggregate,' bound for a private buyer downcoast." She turned, finally, and looked directly at Merrick. "The motion says I had no right to be there. It does not say his company had a right to do any of what I found there. Those are different questions, and I'd respectfully suggest the tribunal notice that only one of us wants to change the subject."

"Ms. Whitlock," said the second tribunal member, a heavy man named Brandt who hadn't spoken until now, "the trespass question isn't frivolous. Evidence gathered illegally—"

"With respect, sir, it's gathered illegally or it isn't gathered at all. That's how it works upriver. The consortium holds exclusive access to twelve thousand hectares. No independent archaeologist can get a research permit inside that boundary — I checked, for six years, and was declined four times, and I'd love to know why, and I suspect this hearing will tell me. The heritage assessment that justified the original concession was performed by the concession holder's own contractor. There was no one else *allowed* in. So when the only people who can lawfully see a crime scene are the people committing it, the rest of us have a choice between trespassing and pretending. I chose trespassing. I'd choose it again. And I'd note that the territory's own Heritage Act, section four, provides that discovery of burial sites creates a reporting duty on *any person*, permit status notwithstanding. I didn't violate the Act. I performed it. The consortium paid my father to make sure no one ever got the chance."

Silence, then, of a particular quality — the silence of a room doing arithmetic. Paloma sat down. Her father's hand moved on the table, an inch toward hers, and stopped, and she pretended not to see it, and hated herself a little for how much she noticed.

Reyes consulted her colleagues. "The motion is noted and will be ruled on with the finding. Mr. Merrick, you have the floor for response."

Merrick rose. Up close, at the front of the room, he was smaller than his reputation — a compact man, neat, with the kind of face that aged well because it had never done anything strenuous. He thanked the tribunal. He expressed, sincerely, his respect for Ms. Whitlock's evident passion. He used the word *passion* the way other men used *hysteria*. He regretted, deeply, the tragedy of a family estrangement being aired in a public forum. He noted that Dr. Edmund Whitlock had been, at the time of the disputed survey, under significant personal and financial strain — there were records, medical records, which the consortium had obtained and would share with the tribunal confidentially — and that a man in such strain might sign things he later misremembered signing.

Paloma felt her father go rigid beside her, and she put her hand flat on the table between them, not touching him, just present, the way you'd steady a table in an earthquake.

Merrick addressed the bones themselves with exquisite care. Yes, there appeared to be remains on the terrace. Remains were not automatically ancestral; the region had a long history of transient camps, of travelers, of deposits that predated any living community's claim. The consortium had, in fact, commissioned a second opinion — he produced it, a slim report from a firm in the capital — which suggested the terrace material might date outside the protected period entirely. He said the word *might* four times. He said *preliminary* six. He built, in eleven unhurried minutes, a structure made entirely of hedges, and then he sat down inside it.

It was masterful. Paloma had to give him that. He had taken everything she'd brought — the ledger, the photographs, the barge, her father — and reframed it as a family dispute with archaeological garnish. A daughter acting out. A sick old man. A question of dates.

Reyes looked at the petitioners' table. "Dr. Whitlock. The tribunal will hear you now."

*

Her father stood, and the room changed around him.

Paloma had spent ten years building a version of Edmund Whitlock in her head — tall in memory, loud in argument, certain as a compass needle. The man who stood up at the petitioners' table was none of that. He was thin. He'd lost weight in ways that wouldn't come back. His hands, when he gripped the edge of the table, shook until he consciously stilled them, and she recognized the effort because she'd seen him do it in the trapper's cache, steadying himself against the memory of whatever they'd done to him upriver in those weeks the fixers held him.

But when he spoke, his voice was level.

"My name is Edmund Whitlock. I've surveyed rivers in this territory for thirty-eight years." He paused, and Paloma realized he was waiting — not for courage, but for permission from himself. "I want to be precise, because precision is the only thing I have left that they couldn't take."

He told it in order, and the order itself was damning. The consortium's approach, two years ago: generous funding, full autonomy, *we just need the corridor assessed*. The terrace, found in the second week. His initial assessment, filed honestly: extensive ancestral burial site, protected class, recommend rerouting the entire eastern corridor. Then the meeting in the capital. Then the second meeting. Then the phone calls, and the phrase they'd used — he repeated it flatly, without theater — *"reclassify or lose the contract and the reference."* The same lever they'd pulled on her, Paloma thought. The same lever, pulled on both of us, at both ends of a decade.

"They had my daughter's permit file," he said. "I didn't know that until later. They knew her name the whole time. They knew what the reference was worth to her." His voice cracked on *her*, just barely, and he pressed on. "I signed the false assessment. I want that in the record plainly: I falsified a heritage document. I'm not here to excuse it. I'm here to tell you what it cost, and what it was worth, and who set the price."

He described the ledger copy. The mailing. The torn pages — his own tearing, at the rapids, when the fixers boarded the survey boat and he had thirty seconds to decide what they'd find on him. "I kept the cover pages because a notebook with no missing pages looks innocent. I sent the substance ahead of me." He looked, for the first time, at Paloma. "I taught her that. Documents travel better than people. I just never expected her to be the one who came looking."

Then he said the thing Paloma hadn't known, the thing that rearranged the whole season in her chest.

"When they took me upriver, they didn't threaten me. That was the worst part. They explained. Patiently. For days. That the terrace was an inconvenience, that the river people would be compensated, that progress required flexibility, that I was making it *personal* when it was merely *procedural*." He turned to the pines, to the river people in their good coats. "They never once believed you were real. That's what I understood, sitting in that tent. It wasn't malice. It was that to them, you were a line item with feelings attached. And I had signed a paper agreeing with them."

Nobody in the pines moved. The schoolteacher had stopped taking notes.

"I can't give back what I signed away," her father finished. "I can tell you the terrace is real, the burials are ancestral, the assessment I signed was false, and every word of instruction in that ledger came from the consortium's director of operations, in writing, over eight months. Ask them to produce their internal correspondence. If I'm lying, they'll have the emails that prove it." He sat down slowly, like a man setting down something very heavy. "They won't produce them."

*

The deliberation took four hours, and the hall emptied and refilled twice, and Paloma spent most of it on the cannery steps with Ilsa, eating fried bread from a cart and not tasting it.

"He did all right," Ilsa said.

"He did more than all right."

"No. I mean—" Ilsa wiped her fingers on her jeans. "He said your name in there. Twice. Like it cost him and he paid anyway." She shrugged. "That's not nothing. From what you've told me, it might be the most expensive thing anyone's said in that room today."

Paloma didn't answer. Across the street, Merrick stood on the hotel balcony talking on a satellite phone, unhurried, gray against the gray sky, and she watched him the way you watch a river you're about to run — looking for the line, the safe channel, knowing there might not be one.

The bell rang. They went back in.

Reyes read the finding standing up, which Paloma learned later was unusual — she did it when she wanted the room on its feet.

"The tribunal finds as follows. One: the emergency review is granted. Two: the concession held by Kettle Range Holdings is voided effective immediately, pending full heritage review of the entire lease area by an independent assessor appointed by this tribunal, with costs borne by the concession holder. Three: the trespass motion is denied — the petitioner acted within her statutory reporting duty under section four. Four: the matter of the falsified assessment is referred to the crown prosecutor. Five—" and here Reyes looked directly at Merrick's delegation, and her voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse for them, "—the consortium is directed to preserve all internal correspondence relating to the Kettle Range survey. All of it. Failure to preserve will be treated as spoliation, and this tribunal has broad discretion in drawing conclusions from spoliation."

She sat down. The clerk's gavel cracked once against the loading dock, and the sound came back off the corrugated walls like a shot up a canyon.

The pines didn't cheer. River people didn't cheer, Paloma was learning — they exhaled, all together, a long collective breath like a tide going out, and then the old man near the back began, quietly, to sing something low and rhythmic, and others joined, and it wasn't a celebration, it was older than that. A naming. A claiming-back.

Merrick was already gone. His delegation packed boxes with practiced speed, and Okonkwo caught Paloma's eye across the room and touched two fingers to his temple — *we're not done, but we won this round* — and her father stood at the table gathering his papers with shaking hands, and she found herself standing beside him, helping, their shoulders almost touching, neither of them saying anything, the silence between them no longer a wall but a kind of unfinished sentence.

Outside, the light was going gold on the water, and the first hard frost of the season was coming down the valley — you could smell it, iron and cold. Her father zipped his bag and looked upriver, at the rapids, and said, quietly, mostly to himself:

"They'll appeal. And the review will take a year. And the ground will freeze in three weeks." He glanced at her sideways, and for the first time in ten years there was something in his face she recognized from her childhood — the look he got before proposing something reckless to a crew. "Spring excavation permits open Monday. First-come queue at the lands office. In this town, that means camping on the steps tonight."

Paloma looked at the lands office two streets down, at its wide stone stairs, already filling with dusk.

"How early are you thinking of getting there?" she asked.

He smiled — small, cracked, real. "How early are *you*?"

Behind them, unnoticed, a courier in consortium colors crossed the lobby of the lands office carrying a sealed application envelope — filed, stamped, and dated before the gavel had even cooled.
