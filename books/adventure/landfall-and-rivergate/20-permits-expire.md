## 20. Permits Expire

The permits expired at noon.

Not at dawn, not with ceremony, but at twelve o'clock exactly, when the clock over the land tribunal's registry office ticked past the minute and the Meridian River Consortium's claim on the upper watershed became, in the language of the statute, *void and of no further effect*. Paloma had read the clause enough times in the last three weeks to recite it in her sleep. She'd wanted to be standing somewhere meaningful when it happened. In the end she was standing in the registry office doorway, holding two coffees, watching the clerk stamp the last of the filing copies while the second hand swept up to the top of the dial.

The stamp came down. The clerk — a narrow woman named Okonkwo who had processed Paloma's own salvage permits for a decade without ever once looking up from her desk — glanced at the clock, then at Paloma, and said, "That's that."

"That's that," Paloma agreed.

She handed her one of the coffees anyway. Okonkwo took it with the expression of a woman accepting tribute she had long since earned.

Outside, Landfall was doing what Landfall always did in late autumn: hauling barges, arguing about freight rates, smelling of diesel and woodsmoke and the green rot of the levee willows. But there was something different in it today, a looseness, the way a town breathes out after holding itself taut through a hearing season. Word had traveled downriver faster than any official notice. The consortium's concession was dead. The fast launch that had chased them through the shallows sat impounded at the fuel dock under a tribunal writ, its hull still bearing the scar of where Paloma had put it into the gravel bar at full throttle. Men in consortium jackets had been seen loading their gear onto the afternoon freighter instead of into survey boats. Nobody in Landfall was mourning them.

Paloma walked down to the levee with her coffee cooling in her hand and let the river look at her for a while.

It had been six weeks since she'd run Landfall rapids with two hired boatmen and a splintered hull panel. Six weeks since she'd pulled her father's notebook out of an eddy with its final pages torn away. Six weeks since Ilsa had crawled out from under a collapsed supply tent with river mud in her hair and a story that had turned Paloma's grief into something with an edge on it. Some mornings it felt like years. Some mornings it felt like yesterday, and she could still feel the cold of the terrace stones through her gloves, the small bones arranged in their generations, the patience of people who had buried their dead above the flood line for four hundred years and never once needed a permit to do it.

Her father had found them. Her father had been paid to unfind them. And he hadn't been able to do it — that was the part Paloma was still learning how to hold. He had refused to falsify the heritage assessment, and they had taken him upriver for it, and he had copied the ledger and mailed it to himself at Rivergate like a man hiding a confession inside his own name. All those years she'd spent digging in the lowlands to get away from him, and it turned out the man had been carrying the same weight she was, just in a different pocket.

"You're going to wear a groove in that boardwalk," said a voice behind her.

Ilsa came down the levee steps with her ankle still favoring the left side, six weeks of healing not quite enough to make the sprain a memory. She'd traded the expedition's ruined field clothes for a coat from the Landfall thrift shop, brown wool, sleeves too short, and she wore it like armor. She stopped beside Paloma and looked at the water.

"Is it done?" she asked.

"Done at noon. Fourteen minutes ago."

Ilsa nodded slowly, as if checking the news against something internal. "My mother used to say the river keeps its own ledger." She sipped at nothing; she hadn't thought to buy coffee. "I didn't believe her until this year."

"The river kept nothing. You did. Voss did. Okonkwo's stamp did." Paloma shrugged. "The river just watched."

"That's what she meant."

They stood together while a barge ground past, loaded with winter freight, its wake slapping the pilings. Upstream, invisible beyond the first bend, were the rapids, the camp, the terrace. Come spring, if the tribunal's review went the way the evidence said it should, there would be a sanctioned excavation there. Paloma had already drafted the proposal. She'd written it in three nights, sitting in the trapper's cache on the mountain with her father's maps spread around her like a deck of cards she was finally learning to read.

Speaking of which.

He was waiting outside the registry office when they got back — not hovering, because Aldous Whitlock did not hover, but positioned, the way he'd always positioned himself at dig sites and press conferences and family dinners: at the exact center of whatever room he was in, radiating the assumption that the room existed for his convenience. Six weeks of recovery had rebuilt some of the flesh on his frame. Nothing was going to rebuild the rest. There was a new hesitancy in his hands, which had once moved like instruments, and when he saw Paloma coming he straightened in a way that cost him something.

"Paloma." A pause, weighing. "Ilsa."

"We watched the clock run out," Paloma said. "You missed it."

"I was signing my deposition addendum." He held up a folder as proof, as if the paper excused him. "Okonkwo has opinions about ink color. Apparently the tribunal does too."

For a moment none of them said anything. This was how it went now, the three of them: careful sentences, loaded pauses, everyone handling the past like cracked ceramic. Paloma had told herself, on the whiteout descent and again on the dawn run and again in the hearing room where her father had testified in a voice sanded down to gravel, that reunion and reconciliation were different animals and only one of them had shown up. She'd been right. But the other one was circling closer than she'd expected, and some days that was harder than distance.

"I have something for you," her father said. "If you'll walk with me."

* * *

He led her not into the registry office but along the block to the old surveyors' hall — a stone building with a brass plaque worn nearly illegible, where the territorial mapping bureau had kept its offices back when maps were drawn by men with frostbitten fingers. It was mostly storage now. Her father had a key. Of course he had a key; Aldous Whitlock had keys to half the institutional architecture of the watershed, acquired across thirty years of being the man everyone called first.

Inside, among shelving units of rolled charts and dead instruments, he unlocked a flat steel case and opened it on a worktable.

"My expedition name," he said. "Whitlock Surveys. Registered forty-one years ago. It's carried eleven major expeditions, two hundred-odd publications, and — " his mouth twisted — "one very large mistake that we are currently paying for in public."

Paloma looked at the case. Inside lay the registration certificates, the original charter, the seal, the whole genealogy of the name that had hung over her childhood like weather. She had grown up underneath that name. She had spent ten years digging contract sites in the lowlands specifically so she wouldn't have to stand in its shadow, and here it was, packed in steel wool, being offered to her like a crown.

"It's yours," her father said. "It should be yours. The name is worth more than the sum of everything I did with it, and you're the one who proved that. Take it. Rebuild it. By spring nobody will remember the consortium's version."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere a pipe ticked as the building cooled.

Paloma picked up the original charter. Heavy paper, fountain-pen signatures, her father's name at the bottom in handwriting she recognized from a thousand field notes — including the ones in the trapper's cache, the route notes she'd read by lamplight while Ilsa slept, hearing his voice in the margins for the first time in years. *Cut high on the east wall before the rain fills the gullies. P. would find the line faster. She always reads water better than I do.*

He'd written that eight years ago. Eight years of silence, and he'd been writing her into his maps the whole time.

She set the charter down.

"No," she said.

Her father blinked. In forty-one years, the word had probably been said to him fewer times than she could count on one hand, and never by her.

"The name isn't worth what you think it is," Paloma said. "I know what you mean to offer. You're offering me the clean part — the reputation, the publications, the thing that opens doors. And you want me to take it because taking it means you've given me something, and giving it means—" She stopped, made herself go on. "But I'd be spending the rest of my career explaining whose name it was. Answering for the gap year where it worked for the consortium. Every permit application, every tribunal filing, every time someone at a conference squints at me and says *any relation?* — I'd be carrying your story alongside mine forever, and I've already done my time carrying it."

"Paloma—"

"Let me finish." She pulled the folded papers from her jacket pocket — she'd carried them all morning, waiting for this, though she hadn't admitted it even to herself until this moment. "I filed this at nine o'clock."

She laid the certificate on the worktable beside his charter. Fresh ink, fresh seal. **Rivergate Survey & Heritage Services. Principal: P. Whitlock.** Filed under her own name, her own company, built on nothing but her own record and the season that had just ended.

Her father stared at it for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly strangled, but real. "You registered it *before* I offered?"

"I've been drafting the articles since the cache on the mountain." She tapped the certificate. "You want to know what I inherited from you? Not the name. The maps. The mistakes. The habit of reading water like a confession. The stubbornness that makes a man refuse to falsify an assessment even when he knows what it'll cost him. That's the estate. I'm claiming all of it." She met his eyes. "The name I'm leaving in the case. It served you. Let it retire with honor."

He looked down at the two documents side by side — his charter, yellowed and grand, and hers, crisp and plain — and something moved through his face that Paloma couldn't fully read. Grief, maybe. Relief, maybe. The particular pain of a man discovering that the best thing he ever built was not the thing he built.

"Rivergate," he said quietly. "After the passes."

"After the station where the resupply crates sat unopened and the keeper lied to my face." She almost smiled. "And after the dead-letter crate where you mailed yourself the truth. Seemed fitting. The whole company runs on the principle that things people try to bury come back."

Her father reached out and touched the edge of her certificate, lightly, the way he touched fragile artifacts. "Will you at least let me be your first client?"

"You can't afford me."

"I have settlement money coming and no reputation left to spend it on." He straightened, and for a moment — just a moment — the old expedition leader stood in the dusty surveyors' hall, the man from the lecture slides of her childhood. "The tribunal review will need independent documentation of the terrace. Full stratigraphic survey, proper excavation protocol, chain of custody on every bone. They won't trust anything with my name attached, and they shouldn't. But a new firm, clean record, principal credited with recovering the evidence in the first place..." He spread his hands. "I'm told she reads water better than I do."

Paloma looked at him for a long time. The pipe ticked. Outside, a gull argued with the harbor.

"Spring season," she said. "Contract terms. You report to my crew chief, same as anyone."

"Who's your crew chief?"

"Ilsa. If she'll take it." She raised her voice slightly toward the door, where she'd noticed, twenty minutes ago, a brown wool coat pretending to study a chart of the old portage routes. "Which brings us to the actual reason I came downtown."

* * *

They found Ilsa in the hallway, examining the portage chart with the intense concentration of someone who had heard every word through the door and was going to pretend otherwise until formally relieved of the effort.

"Crew chief," Paloma said. "Terrace excavation, spring season. Sanctioned, funded, properly provisioned. You'd be running the field crew, the catalog, and the schedule. And keeping me honest, which based on recent evidence is a full-time position."

Ilsa turned. Her eyes were bright in a way she was clearly trying to manage. "I'm a junior crew member. I was junior on a survey that got burned down."

"You're a junior crew member who survived six days alone in erased country, kept her head down, kept her observations straight, and delivered testimony that held up under cross-examination from the consortium's counsel — a man who eats witnesses for breakfast." Paloma shrugged. "In my company, that's a résumé. In my company, that's the *whole* résumé."

"And if the tribunal review goes badly? If the director appeals, drags it out another year?"

"Then we wait, and we keep the site documented, and we outlast them. That's also the company principle." Paloma held out her hand. "Well?"

Ilsa looked at the hand. Then, unexpectedly, she stepped past it and hugged her — quick, fierce, careful of the ribs that were still mending from the descent. "Yes," she said into Paloma's shoulder. "Obviously yes. But I'm negotiating my rate."

"Everyone negotiates their rate. My father taught me that."

"He did not teach you that. You learned it spitefully, on your own, out of pure contrariness."

"That's also inheritance," Paloma said.

* * *

Evening came down on Landfall the way it always did, gold going to copper going to grey, the harbor lights waking one by one. Paloma walked the levee alone one more time, past the impounded launch, past the freighter that would carry the consortium's men out on the morning tide. At the rail opposite the rapids' far roar — faint now, a sound like the town's own pulse — she stopped and let the day settle over her.

Somewhere upriver, under snow, the terrace waited with its generations. Somewhere in a steel case in the surveyors' hall, a famous name slept off its long career. Somewhere in a boarding house, her father was writing out a client inquiry in careful fountain pen, addressing it to a company that had existed for seven hours.

Her radio crackled.

"—any station, any station, this is *Kestrel*, upstream of the Forks, do you copy—" A pause, static, then again, thinner: "We've lost our guide to a broken leg at the second portage. Looking for anyone who knows the Rivergate passes. Willing to pay. Repeat, willing to pay—"

Paloma looked at the radio for a moment. Then she keyed the transmit switch.

"*Kestrel*, this is Rivergate Survey. I know the passes."
