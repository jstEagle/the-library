## 21. Back to the Terrace

The river gave us six weeks of good weather, and I spent every one of them on my knees.

Not the way I'd spent the last decade on my knees — in contract pits, scraping pipeline corridors and shopping-mall foundations, digging other people's garbage out of other people's ground. This was different. This was a grid I'd staked myself, with strings pulled taut between cedar stakes and numbers painted on aluminum tags, and a crew that showed up at dawn because they wanted to, not because the permit money was the only money left in the valley.

Ilsa ran the day-to-day. That had been her condition for coming back, and I'd agreed before she finished asking. She'd earned it twice over — once for surviving the winter the consortium's men made of her first season, and once for what she'd done at the tribunal, sitting in the witness box with her hands folded like a woman reciting scripture, telling twelve strangers about the night the badges came upriver. She was twenty-four and she managed nine people older than her without raising her voice. When she corrected my elevations, I wrote them down.

"Unit seven's producing," she said that morning, crouching beside me at the terrace edge. "Small bones again. Same layer."

I brushed soil from a rib the size of my thumbnail. Below us the Landfall rapids kept their argument with the rocks, the same conversation they'd been having since before anyone had a name for them. My father used to say the river was the oldest witness in the valley. He'd said it in lectures, in grant applications, in the margins of maps. He'd never once said it to me.

That was the arithmetic of the spring: some things came back, and some things didn't, and you learned to tell the difference by touch.

* * *

The tribunal's ruling had come down in February, three weeks after the hearing. Concession voided pending full heritage review; the burial terrace granted protected status under my survey company's stewardship — *Whitlock River Surveys*, registered in my name, one word changed from his, which felt like both a rebellion and an apology. The consortium's director had appealed, then withdrawn the appeal when Voss's field log went into the public record. Photographs don't shout. They just sit there, dated and geotagged, being true.

Voss testified from the record only. He never came back to the valley. His deposition ran forty pages, flat and precise, a man describing his own complicity the way he might describe weather systems. At the end of it there was a single handwritten line, scanned in crooked:

*I saw the children's graves before I understood what I was sent to erase. I should have walked out that day. I am sorry it took me until the ridge.*

Ilsa read it aloud to the crew our first morning, because she believed — and I had come around to believing — that we should know exactly whose compromised hands had built the ground we stood on. Justice assembles itself from damaged parts or not at all.

* * *

We found them in layers, the way you find everything.

The top layer was river silt, two thousand years of floods laid down like pages. Under that, charcoal and ash — hearths, cooking fires, a village that had watched these same rapids. Under that, the burials themselves, and this was where I made everyone stop and start over slower.

They were small. That was the thing no report could carry, the thing my father's torn-out notebook pages had only gestured at in his cramped hand: *terrace burials, multiple generations, infant and juvenile remains predominant, wrapped in bark, oriented to the water.* Generations of river people who had carried their dead up this bank and laid them facing the current, so the river would know them. Beads of traded copper. A carved pendant shaped like a fish. One grave, deeper than the others, holding a woman buried with a paddle worn smooth as driftwood.

Ilsa named the excavation protocol herself: no remains lifted without community oversight, everything documented in place, the elders from the downriver settlements invited to walk the grid whenever they wanted. Three of them came the second week. The eldest, a woman called Marta who spoke more English than she admitted, stood at the terrace edge for a long time before saying anything.

"My grandmother said people lived above the loud water," she told me. "Before the sickness. Before the barges." She looked at the small bones emerging from the earth like sentences being finished. "Everyone said it was a story."

"It was a record," I said. "It was just written somewhere nobody was reading."

She put her hand on my shoulder, briefly, and I understood I'd been given something no tribunal could issue.

* * *

He came on a Tuesday in the fourth week.

I knew before Ilsa told me. There's a way a camp changes when someone arrives who doesn't belong to its rhythm — a hush that runs through the screens of the mess tent, the way conversation folds itself politely around a stranger. I was in unit four, on my knees in the ash layer, when I heard his voice ask someone a question about parking the skiff.

I stayed in the pit longer than I needed to. Long enough to be childish about it, if I'm honest. Then I climbed out, stripped my gloves, and walked over.

He'd aged a winter's worth of winters since Landfall. The coercion had done something to him that the rescue hadn't undone — he moved carefully now, like a man carrying water across ice, and he'd stopped performing. For thirty years my father had been a performance: the laugh, the anecdotes, the map-flourish, the way he'd hold a compass up to a room like a relic. All of it gone. What stood at the edge of my excavation was a thin old man in a clean shirt, holding a paper bag of oranges like an offering at a shrine he wasn't sure he was welcome in.

"I heard the site got protection," he said. "I read the ruling."

"You could have called."

"The university has my number. You have my number." He looked at the grid, the tags, the screens, the crew pretending not to watch. "This is better than anything I drew."

It wasn't forgiveness. We both knew it wasn't. It was two people standing at the edge of a hole in the ground that contained the truth neither of us had been allowed to speak for years, and the truth doing the work that we couldn't do for each other yet.

"They were buried facing the river," I said. "All of them. Every generation."

His eyes went bright and he blinked it away, and for once he didn't turn the moment into a lecture. "Your mother's people did that too," he said quietly. "Coastal side. Facing the water." He'd never told me that. Ten years of silence, and he'd been saving that one sentence the whole time, or maybe he'd only just found it.

He stayed two days. He didn't touch the artifacts. He sat where visitors sat, ate what the cook tent served, and on the second evening he took out a pencil and asked Ilsa's permission before sketching the terrace profile — not as the celebrated August Nakamura of the expedition brochures, but as a visitor making a picture of something beautiful. When he left, he shook my hand. A handshake. We stood there after, hands still clasped a beat too long, neither of us knowing the protocol for whatever this was.

"Come back in the fall," I said. "When we do the reburial consultation. They'll want descendants' voices at the table."

"I'll come back," he said. "As a visitor."

"As family," I said, and immediately regretted how much it cost me to say, and he nodded like a man accepting terms he'd spend the rest of his life trying to deserve.

* * *

The last week of the season, we found the fish pendant's twin.

Ilsa brought it to me at dusk, cradled in a finds tray, and we sat on the terrace edge with our boots hanging over the drop while the rapids went gold and then grey below us. Two pendants, two graves, separated by maybe ninety years of burials. Someone had carved the same fish twice, taught the pattern to someone else, and the pattern had survived everything — the sickness, the barges, the consortium, my father's silence, my own.

"Do you ever think about what happens after the report?" Ilsa asked.

"All the time. The report is the easy part. It's just the truth, typed."

"And after the truth?"

"After the truth," I said, "the river keeps going. And somebody in fifty years opens a new trench and finds our grid stakes and wonders about us." I turned the pendant in the light. "I hope they wonder kindly."

She laughed, and the sound carried down the bank, and somewhere below us the river answered the way it always does — not kindly, not cruelly, just continuing.

* * *

On the final morning, I packed the last of the documentation crates myself and stood a long time looking upriver, toward the pass, toward the col where the snow had nearly ended us, toward everything the winter had taken and the spring had handed back with interest.

The radio on the mess table crackled. Static, then a voice — a young expedition leader, nervous, formal, asking for Rivergate Station, asking whether anyone knew a guide who knew the passes above Landfall.

Ilsa raised an eyebrow at me. I picked up the handset.

"This is Whitlock River Surveys," I said. "Who's asking?"
