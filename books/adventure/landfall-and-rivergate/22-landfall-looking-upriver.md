## 22. Landfall, Looking Upriver

The season ended the way it always did at Landfall: not with a decision but with a sound. One morning in late October the river ran a half-tone lower, and the willows along the bank went the color of old brass, and the boatmen who had worked the lower rapids all summer began pulling their skiffs out of the water without being asked, the way animals move to higher ground before anyone tells them to. Paloma Whitlock stood on the rock shelf above the falls with a mug of coffee going cold in her hands and listened to the river say what it said every year: *not yet, but soon.*

Below her, the town was doing its autumn arithmetic. The tribunal building had gone quiet again after the hearing, its windows dark, but the effects of it were everywhere — in the survey stakes the consortium crews had abandoned along the terrace road, leaning at angles like drunk sentries; in the new notice board outside the land office, papered with the heritage review schedule in three languages; in the way people on the street still looked at her, sometimes, with a curiosity she hadn't earned and couldn't spend. Six weeks since the hearing. Long enough for the story to flatten into something tellable. Not long enough for her to know what it had cost, or what it had bought.

Her father had gone south ten days ago, on the mail barge, with a canvas duffel and a doctor's appointment in the city and a handshake that had lasted a beat too long to be casual and a beat too short to be anything else.

"Take care of the maps," he'd said, and she'd said, "They were never mine to take care of," and he'd looked at her then — really looked, the way he used to look at a river gauge, reading the numbers and not liking them — and said, "That's the trouble with you, Paloma. You always did the math before the feeling."

She hadn't answered. There hadn't been an answer. But she'd stood on the dock until the barge cleared the bend, and she'd found afterward that something in her chest had unclenched by maybe a degree, which was more than she'd had in ten years, and she supposed that was how repair worked. Not a reconciliation. A truce with a long horizon.

* * *

The survey office she'd registered in September occupied the back half of a building that had been a chandlery, then a flophouse, then nothing, and now was hers — one room with a scarred plank desk, a drafting table Ilsa had found at a farm auction and carried three miles on her back out of pure stubbornness, and a wall of maps. His maps. She'd had them reframed under glass, all eleven of the Rivergate passes and the terrace country, drawn in his small, ruthless hand, elevation lines like fingerprints. On the worst days she caught herself standing in front of them the way you stand at a grave. On the good days she stood in front of them the way you stand at a workbench.

Today was a workbench day. She was redrawing the approach to the terrace — the sanctioned approach, the one the heritage review had approved, with the trail rerouted off the burial soil entirely — when the radio on the shelf behind her crackled.

She almost didn't turn around. All season the radio had been mostly weather and mostly silence, the two things the high country produced in abundance. But the voice that came through now was young and clipped and trying very hard to sound professional, and it was saying: *"—any station on this frequency, this is the Alder Creek expedition, we're staged at Rivergate Station, we're looking for a guide for a spring approach through the passes, repeat, we're— "*

Paloma set down her pen.

Rivergate. The word landed in her the way it always did now, a stone dropped down a well. She could see the station without closing her eyes: the sagging dock, the dead-letter crate with its rusted hasp, the keeper Marta's face the day Paloma had come back downriver with the ledger in a dry bag and the whole town's attention swinging toward her like a searchlight. Marta had wept, actually wept, standing in the doorway of the station house, and Paloma had not known what to do with that and had done nothing, and Marta had seemed, somehow, to understand.

*"—any station, do you copy—"*

She keyed the mic. "Alder Creek, this is Whitlock Survey, Landfall. I copy. Say again your position and party size."

A pause — the particular pause of someone who hadn't expected an answer. Then: *"Whitlock Survey. Are you — sorry, is this — we heard about the tribunal. We heard a Whitlock ran the Rivergate passes last fall."*

"You heard right. Party size and objective, Alder Creek."

*"Six of us. University-backed, small survey, heritage assessment work on the upper tributaries — the same country, we think, as the burial terrace."* Another pause, and then, with the honesty of someone too green to know better: *"Honestly, we've had two guides cancel. Everyone's heard what happened to the last expedition that went up there."*

Paloma looked out the window at the river. The light was doing the thing it did in late October, coming in low and copper-colored, laying a bright road on the water all the way up the channel toward the rapids, as if the river were pointing at something.

"What happened," she said, "is that a mining consortium paid a survey crew to erase a burial ground, and when the crew's lead wouldn't do it, they took him upriver and burned his camp, and it took his daughter, a junior crew member, and a fixer with a conscience to put it back together. That's what happened. If that's the story that's scaring off your guides, you should know it accurately."

Silence on the frequency. Long enough that she checked the set.

*"That's — yes,"* the voice said finally. *"That's the story. The accurate one. Our director read the tribunal record twice."* A breath. *"He said the passes need guides who know what's at stake up there. He said — he said ask for you by name."*

She should have felt something at that — pride, or its opposite, or the strange vertigo of being asked for by name in the country where she'd spent a decade being nobody's daughter and nobody's guide. What she felt instead was the river. The sound of it through the window, patient and enormous, taking the light and giving nothing back.

"When?" she said.

*"First clear window in April. We'd want two weeks in the field, Terrace approach and the upper tributaries. We pay union rate. We — Ms. Whitlock, are you there?"*

"I'm here." She looked at the drafting table, at the reroute she'd drawn that morning, at the elevation lines running up the page like a pulse. "I'll need to see your permits. I'll need your heritage protocol in writing, and I'll want it to be stricter than the tribunal requires, because the tribunal's requirements are the floor, not the standard. And I'll want your director to understand that if anyone on that expedition touches that terrace without a sanction number, I will walk them out of the passes myself, in whatever weather is available, and they can explain the schedule slip to their funding board."

Another silence, and then a laugh came through the static — startled, relieved, the laugh of someone who'd been afraid of getting a yes and had gotten something better. *"Understood. Understood, ma'am. April first window. We'll send the paperwork downriver on the next barge."*

"Whitlock Survey out."

She set the mic down and stood there for a while, not moving, the coffee finally cold enough to taste like nothing.

* * *

Ilsa found her an hour later on the rock shelf above the falls, because Ilsa always found her there when the radio had been talking. Ilsa was twenty-four now and crew chief in everything but the paperwork, which would come in spring, and she had the particular weathered competence of someone who had spent a year learning that survival was a skill and not an accident. She sat down on the cold rock without being invited, the way family does.

"Alder Creek?" she said.

"You were listening."

"I was listening." Ilsa pulled her knees up. "You said yes."

"I said I'd read the paperwork."

"You said yes." Ilsa grinned at the river. "Six weeks after the hearing and you're already taking expeditions up the same passes where they burned a camp. You know the consortium's appeal is still pending. You know there are still people in this town who think Voss should be in a cell instead of testifying from the record."

"I know all of it."

"Do you know what you want?"

Paloma considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, which was more than she'd given most questions for most of her life. What did she want. The permit money had been the answer once, the lie she'd told herself on the dock at the lowlands dig, and it had stopped being an answer somewhere on the river between Landfall and the terrace — she'd never been able to pinpoint the day, only that by the time she'd stood among the small bones with a trowel in her hand and her father's notebook open on her knee, the wanting had changed shape entirely. It wasn't the permits. It had never been the permits. It was this: the country, and the work, and the truth of it kept like a fire is kept — fed, tended, never assumed.

"I want," she said, "to be the guide who knows the passes. Not his daughter. Not the woman from the tribunal. Just the guide."

Ilsa nodded slowly. "And when Alder Creek asks why you know the passes so well?"

"Then I'll tell them about my father's maps." She said it and heard, with a small internal surprise, how little it cost. "All of them. The mistakes included. Especially the mistakes."

* * *

That evening she walked down to the dock, because there was one more thing the season required of her and she had been putting it off. The barge from upriver had come in at dusk, the same night barge, or its successor — the consortium's private cargo operation had been seized under the tribunal's interim order, and the crates of 'salvaged' artifacts had gone into a bonded warehouse in the river town, catalogued, photographed, waiting for repatriation. But the barge that ran the river now was a working boat again, carrying mail and flour and diesel and the ordinary freight of ordinary lives, and it had brought up a parcel for her.

She signed for it at the landing. The clerk didn't say anything, but his eyes went to the return address, and then to her, and then away, the way people's eyes had learned to move this autumn.

She carried it up the hill to the office and opened it under the lamp. Inside: a single sheet of paper, and beneath it, wrapped in a wool sock, a brass compass. His compass. The one from every photograph of every celebrated expedition, the one she'd grown up thinking was less a tool than a talisman. The paper was a page torn from a field notebook, and the handwriting was his, smaller now, less ruthless, the hand of a man writing carefully because his hands were not what they had been.

*Paloma —*

*The tribunal sent me a copy of your survey registration. Whitlock Survey. I notice you didn't take the expedition name, and I notice I'm not surprised, and I notice that both of those noticings are my own fault, which is a sentence I have been composing for ten years and could only finish after a fixer carried my field log down a mountain for me.*

*The compass is yours. Not as inheritance. Inheritance is what you get when someone dies or gives up. This is equipment. It goes to the person who will use it, and that was always going to be you, I just didn't know it until I watched you do it without it.*

*The passes in April will have a late winter. The Alder Creek people will not believe you when you tell them. Tell them anyway.*

*— Your father*

*P.S. The terrace reroute you drew is correct. I checked it against my old notes. I was wrong about the eastern line in '09. You weren't there to see me be wrong, so I'm telling you now.*

She read it twice. Then she set the compass on the drafting table, next to the reroute drawing, where its needle swung once and settled, pointing — as it always pointed, as it had pointed through every celebrated expedition and every silence and every year of the distance between them — north. Toward the passes. Toward the terrace. Toward the work.

She did not cry. She had done her crying on a river, in the rain, at an age and in a place where no one could mistake it for weakness, and she found she was mostly finished. But she sat for a long time with the letter flat under her hand, and outside the window the river ran dark and full toward the falls, and somewhere above the rapids, under the first early snow already falling on the col, the terrace of small bones slept under its sanctioned protection, and the river people who had lain there for generations lay on, and the country held all of it — the bones, the mistakes, the truth — the way it held everything: without forgiveness and without malice, without memory and without forgetting, asking only that you come back in the spring and do the work properly.

* * *

On the last day of the season, the radio crackled again.

She was locking the office, dusk coming down blue over the water, when the set behind her hissed to life — not Alder Creek this time, not university English, but the flat, careful cadence of the river people from the tributary settlements, the old voice of the upper country that she'd learned to hear in her father's tapes and then in person, standing on the terrace with Ilsa translating the place-names one by one.

*"—Whitlock Survey, this is Kettle Landing. Do you copy."*

She crossed the room and keyed the mic. "Kettle Landing, this is Whitlock Survey. I copy. Go ahead."

*"We have heard you are guiding for the university people in April."* A pause, the length of someone choosing words the way you choose footing on scree. *"The elders have talked. We do not object. But we ask one thing."*

"Name it."

*"You take our man with you. A young one. Denali — he is nineteen, he knows the tributary water, and he wants to learn the passes the way your father learned them, from the ground, not from a permit."* The voice paused again, and when it came back there was something in it that might have been the ghost of a smile. *"The elders say the country does not forgive. But they say also that it teaches. They say you are the one who learned. So we send him to you, and you teach him what the river taught you."*

Paloma stood in the blue dusk with the mic in her hand and the compass on the table behind her, needle steady, pointing north, and she thought about the girl she had been at nineteen, digging other people's contract sites in the lowlands, telling herself the distance from her father was a choice and not a wound. She thought about what the river had taught her, and what it had cost, and whether any of it could be handed to a nineteen-year-old in a single season or whether it could only be learned the way she'd learned it — the hard way, the whole way, all the way up.

She thought: *the wilderness forgives nothing.* She thought: *good.*

"Tell the elders yes," she said into the mic. "Tell Denali to bring his own boots. And tell him the first thing he learns is that the passes don't care who his father was."

She set the mic down. Outside, the last light was going off the water, and upriver, past the falls, past the station, past the terrace, the high country stood white and silent under its early snow, waiting — as it always waited, as it would wait long after all of them — for the spring, and the work, and whoever was foolish enough, and lucky enough, to answer when it called.

She turned off the lamp and locked the door behind her, and the river ran on in the dark, saying what it always said: *not yet, but soon.*
