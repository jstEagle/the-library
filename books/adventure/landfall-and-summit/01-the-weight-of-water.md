## 1. The Weight of Water

The liaison arrived at low tide, which Noor Ashgrove took as an omen. Men who came at high water wanted passage; men who came when the river was pulling back, baring its ribs of mud and rope-scarred pilings, wanted something else entirely.

He stood on her dock in a coat too fine for the Saltmere waterfront, gray wool gone dark at the hem where the damp had climbed it, and he held his hat the way a man holds a thing he's afraid someone will take from him. Behind him the river ran brown-green and patient, carrying its usual freight of silt, alder leaves, and secrets.

"Captain Ashgrove." He said the name carefully, as if testing whether it would hold his weight. "My name is Verel. I'm with the Office of Surveys and Boundaries."

"Noor," she said. "Ashgrove was my grandmother."

Something flickered across his face—satisfaction, maybe, or relief that he'd guessed right about which name she'd answer to. "I need to hire your boat."

"My boat is hired every day. Coal, timber, pilgrims going up to the shrines at Candlerock. What I don't do is passengers who won't say where they're going."

"The headwaters."

Noor set down the coil of line she'd been mending and looked at him properly for the first time. He was younger than his coat suggested, thirty perhaps, with the pale indoor skin of a man who worked by lamplight, and there was a tremor in his hands that he thought his gloves hid. The headwaters. Everyone on the river knew what that meant. The upper gorge where the maps went blank—not unexplored, but *unmapped*, deliberately, the survey offices having inked over their own knowledge three generations ago with a flat wash of white and a printed legend: TERRA INCOGNITA BY ORDER OF THE CROWN.

"I don't go above the Falls," she said. "Nobody does. That's not superstition, Mr. Verel. That's arithmetic. The current up there eats boats, and the Crown eats anyone who asks why."

"That's precisely why we need you." He glanced over his shoulder at the dockside street—the fishwives, the boys gutting eels, the old men who watched everything from beneath their hats—and lowered his voice. "Six weeks ago we sent a survey team upriver. Nine people. Instruments, provisions, a military escort. They were to chart the upper gorge and report back by packet boat at intervals. The first two packets came down on schedule. Then nothing. Three weeks of nothing."

"So send soldiers."

"We sent soldiers. They came back reporting fog, bad footing, and no sign of the team." His jaw tightened around something bitter. "They came back too quickly, Captain. And they reported things in an order that suggests they'd rehearsed the report."

Noor leaned against the rail and let the silence work on him. It was an old trick and it never failed: give a frightened man room and he'll fill it with the truth.

Verel filled it. "There are people in the Office who believe the team found something they weren't meant to find. And other people in the Office who believe those people should be silenced before they report it. I can't investigate my own agency. But you—" he gestured at her, at the boat, at the whole salt-stained world she moved through—"you owe us nothing. You have no loyalties we can trace. You have—" he hesitated, then said it anyway, because he'd come this far—"a reputation for finding things and saying nothing."

"There it is," Noor said. "The real job. You want me to find them and lose them again."

"I want you to find out what happened. Report to me and no one else. And if what happened is best left unreported—" he produced a purse, heavy enough that it clinked when it landed on the rail between them, "—then you'll be paid either way. For the search or for the silence. Your choice, made far from here."

She looked at the money for a long moment. It was more than the *Kestrel* earned in a season. It was debts paid, the hull re-coppered, a winter without counting candle-ends. It was, most dangerously, freedom—the kind you could only buy by agreeing to see nothing.

Every instinct she owned told her to take it. She had spent eleven years learning to walk away from things: from the family estate sold out from under her father's coffin, from creditors who wore her mother's face like a mask, from the name Ashgrove itself, which opened doors she didn't want opened and closed others she did. Walking away was her trade, same as boating. She was good at both.

"All right," she heard herself say. "I'll go up as far as the last packet station. If I find nothing, I find nothing. If I find something—" she picked up the purse, weighed it, and made herself smile, "—we'll discuss rates."

Verel exhaled like a man setting down a load he'd carried too far. He gave her the last known coordinates, a description of the team's boat—a government longboat, green hull, white superstructure, the Office's compass rose painted on the bow—and left the way he'd come, hugging the shadows along the dock as though the river itself might be taking notes.

Noor stood a while after he'd gone, watching the tide turn. The water began its slow climb back up the pilings, erasing the mud, the ribs, the evidence of what lay underneath. That was the river's whole philosophy, she thought. Cover everything. Keep moving. Never let anything be seen resting.

She untied the *Kestrel* within the hour.

* * *

Three days upstream, past Candlerock's shrines and the coal landings, past the last farms where children ran the banks waving, the country narrowed and went quiet. The hills drew in close and forested, the air thickened with the smell of wet stone and pine resin, and the river changed its voice—from the broad murmuring drawl of the lowlands to something swifter, colder, more private. Noor passed the Falls at dawn, running the gap on the north side where the water folded over itself like a struck bell, and after that she was alone with the current and the white space on every map she owned.

She found the longboat on the fourth day, moored in a side channel so still it might have been a held breath.

Green hull. White superstructure. The compass rose faded but legible on the bow. It sat exactly where a careful crew would leave it—warped to the bank, lines doubled, fenders out—with the neatness of men who expected to return by evening.

It was the emptiness that was wrong. Not the dramatic emptiness of violence, but the tidy emptiness of a room left for lunch. Their gear was intact: instrument cases stacked and lashed, tents rolled, a spirit stove, tinned rations enough for weeks. A rifle rack with all four rifles present and oiled. Noor boarded her, checked the bilge (dry), the stores (sealed), the logbook (last entry six days old, routine soundings, signed by the chief, a hand called Maren Voss). Nothing taken. Nothing disturbed. Nothing, apparently, worth leaving in a hurry for.

She stepped onto the bank to look for tracks. The near shore was soft alluvial mud, the kind that recorded everything—a heron's footprint, a dropped leaf, the print of a boot. There were no boot prints. None leading in, none leading out. She walked the channel's length twice, then crossed in the tender and walked the far bank, which was gravel and gave up its secrets grudgingly but not completely. Gravel remembers weight, if nothing else. It remembered nothing.

Nine people had walked off a moored boat into trackless country carrying none of their equipment, and the ground had agreed to forget them.

Noor stood in the middle of the empty camp with her hands in her pockets and felt the old familiar pull at her heels, the one that said *this is not your trouble, the money is already half yours, turn the boat around*. She had gotten very good at hearing that voice and obeying it. It had kept her alive. It had kept her free, or whatever passed for free when you were running.

Then she saw the toy.

It was tucked into the corner of the stern locker, behind a coil of signal line, small enough that she'd missed it twice. Carved wood, river-worn smooth, shaped like a little boat with a crooked mast. Someone had whittled it badly and lovingly—knife marks visible, the hull uneven, one side burned with a clumsy attempt at letters: *IDR*. Not a child aboard, then. A child ashore. Some junior member of the team, some boy or girl years back, carving a boat for a kid who waited at home—or, she thought, turning it over in her palm, feeling how the edges had been rubbed soft by anxious thumbs, some young man who'd brought his childhood aboard himself and held onto it the way sailors hold onto charms.

Her grandmother had carved her one once. A different boat, a better one, from driftwood off the landfall claim at the river's mouth, before the name became a debt and the debt became a wall. *So you always know which way the water runs,* her grandmother had said. Noor had lost the toy somewhere between the funeral and the flight, and had told herself for eleven years that losing things was simply what she did.

She stood in the empty camp holding a stranger's carved boat, and the voice that said *walk away* went very quiet, and under it she heard another voice, older, rougher, hers: *nine people don't vanish. Someone knows which way the water runs.*

Noor put the toy in her coat pocket, cast off the government longboat, and towed it behind the *Kestrel* into the narrowing gorge, toward the white space on the map, where the river kept what it kept—and, she was beginning to suspect, where it was keeping a boy named Idris.
