## 2. What the Current Keeps

The river above the drowned boat changed its manners.

Noor felt it before she could name it—the water running colder, quicker, as if it had somewhere to be and resented her for slowing it down. For three days she poled the *Magpie* against the current through country that grew steeper and quieter, where the banks narrowed to gray stone and the trees leaned in overhead like witnesses conferring. No villages. No smoke. Not even the litter of fisherman's line or a rusted tin that marks the edge of other people's lives.

She had told herself, back at the mooring with the child's carved heron in her fist, that she would take the liaison's money and report nothing. That was the plan. It was always the plan. Take the coin, keep the mouth shut, keep the boat moving.

But she had not turned back. She had put the heron in her coat pocket, where it knocked against her ribs like a small impatient heart, and she had gone upstream.

On the third evening she found the quarry.

She smelled it first—wet chalk and iron, a mineral breath rising from a cut in the hills where men had once clawed out limestone and then, when the vein failed, simply walked away and let the river in. The flood had turned the pit into a lake of improbable blue, water so still and so pale it looked like a held breath. Terraces of white rock descended into it, step by step, each shelf flooded to its own precise line, and at the bottom, if the old charts were right, lay the drowned workings with their galleries and their dark mouths.

Noor tied off in the lee of a spoil heap and stood in the shallows a long time, listening.

Wind in the scree. Water ticking against stone. And beneath it, faint and wrong, the sound of a man trying not to cough.

She didn't call out. Calling out was for people with nothing to lose. Instead she built a small fire on the lowest terrace, set her kettle on it, and made tea for two, leaving the second cup empty and steaming on the rock where anyone hiding in the flooded galleries could see it. Then she sat down, took out the carved heron, and turned it in her fingers until the light went soft.

The cough came again. Then a scrape. Then, after a silence long enough for her to drink half her tea, a voice—thin, cracked, young.

"That cup's a trap."

"Everything's a trap," Noor said, not turning around. "This one has tea in it."

A long pause. Then footsteps on wet stone, careful, favoring one leg, and a boy stepped into the firelight—or not a boy, she corrected herself, a young man starved down to the architecture of himself. Mud to the knees. A survey satchel across his chest, clutched the way other people clutched children or scripture. His eyes did the arithmetic of her—her boat, her build, her hands—looking for the answer to a question he was afraid to ask.

"Noor Ashgrove," she said. "Riverboat. I was hired to find your team."

He flinched at *hired* as if she'd struck him. "Then you should report me and collect the rest."

"Should I?"

"They'll pay more for me than for the others." He said it flatly, a fact already chewed to nothing. "Dead or delivered."

Noor looked at him across the fire. She had spent eleven years becoming a woman nobody could owe anything to and from whom nobody could take anything, and here was a creature who had learned the same grammar at half her age.

"Sit down before you fall down," she said. "The tea's not poisoned. If I wanted you dead I'd have saved the fuel."

He sat. He drank. His hands shook so hard the cup chattered against his teeth, and she watched him fight it, watched him will himself steady, and something in her chest that had been locked since her grandmother's funeral turned over once in its sleep.

"Idris," he said at last. "Idris Vey. Junior cartographer. Which means when the senior men made their decisions, I was furniture." He looked up. "You found the boat."

"Moored and empty. Gear stowed. No tracks on either bank."

"Because they didn't leave by the banks." His voice dropped, and the quarry seemed to lean in around the fire. "They left by the water. All of them except me."

**—from the field journal of Survey Chief Aldous Ren, recovered with the team's effects:**

*Day 19. The source is not what the Ministry's maps show, and the Ministry knows it. Vey's soundings confirm it: the spring does not feed the river. The river feeds the spring. The water runs backward at the head, a slow tidal breath, and what it carries up from below—*

*Struck through. Struck again.*

*Day 21. Corvane has ordered the instruments sealed and the soundings destroyed. I asked on whose authority. He asked me, very gently, whether my daughter still boarded at the Kessington school. That is the whole of the conversation. That is the whole of the Ministry.*

*Day 22. I have begun keeping this record twice. The copy I will hide where a junior man with soft principles might find it. Forgive me, Idris. I am a coward in the large things and honest only in the small ones.*

* * *

Idris talked until the fire burned to coals, and what he told Noor came out in the order of his nightmares rather than the order of events.

The survey had found the source in the second week. That was the job; that was the map. But the source was wrong. The water at the head of the river breathed—a slow, tidal pulse, in and out, like something sleeping—and the soundings showed a channel beneath it that no geology could account for, running deeper than the instruments could reach and *warm*, impossibly warm, the whole winter through.

"Corvane sealed the site within a day," Idris said. "Not for science. For ownership. He sent a rider downriver with sealed orders, and three days later men came up who weren't surveyors. They had boat-hand's calluses and army boots and they called the chief *sir*." His cup had gone still in his hands. "Ren argued. Ren kept a journal he thought nobody knew about. And then—"

He stopped. The fire ticked.

"Then one morning the camp was packing to descend, and Ren's tent was empty, and Corvane said he'd gone down ahead. But Ren's kit was there. His boots were there." Idris's voice went very level, the levelness of a person holding a door shut against something. "I'd been copying his soundings into my own folio all week because he asked me to—said a map should live in more than one place. Corvane found out. He told me I'd ride down with the second party and be commended. And I understood, the way you understand a current under a calm surface, that commended meant *disappeared*, the same as Ren. So at night I took the folio and I went into the water."

"Into the quarry."

"It floods off the old workings. There are galleries—air pockets, whole dry rooms under the terraces. My grandfather worked this pit. I grew up swimming them." A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "The one place in this country Corvane's men can't map."

Noor was quiet a long time. Above the quarry rim the stars came out hard and cold, and somewhere downriver, days away, the *Magpie* rocked at her mooring, and somewhere further downriver than that was a mouth, and a claim, and a name she had spent a decade sanding off herself.

"Your grandmother," she said. "The one who worked the pit."

"Died when I was nine." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him—she saw the wince in his ribs. "I don't belong to anyone now. That's rather the point of me."

"Is it," Noor said. It wasn't a question, and they both heard that it wasn't.

* * *

**—journal of Aldous Ren, second copy, in a different, hurried hand:**

*They came for me at the third bell. Corvane watched from the spring. He wanted me to see the water before I went into it. I will write the one thing they cannot drown: the source breathes, and beneath the breathing there is a door of worked stone, and the stone is carved with a name. I could not read it. But I know a landfall mark when I see one, and I have seen that mark before—on the old claim-plaques, the Ashgrove plaques, at the river's mouth.*

*The source belongs to a dead woman's claim. That is why it must be buried. That is why the boy must be buried with it.*

*If anyone reads this: find an Ashgrove.*

* * *

They left the quarry before dawn, because Idris's leg was worsening and because Noor had heard, in the last hour of dark, the thing she had been listening for since the mooring: engines. Two of them, upstream, running quiet, coming down.

"Survey launches," Idris whispered, white-faced at the terrace edge. "Corvane keeps two on the headwater stretch. If they're moving, they know the folio exists. They know *I* exist."

"Then we don't take the river." Noor was already breaking camp, her hands moving with the cold competence that had kept her alive through eleven years of other people's emergencies. "We cut overland through the slate country and come back to the water below the gorge. My boat's moored at the bend—she's fast, and she draws nothing."

"They'll watch the bends."

"They'll watch the bends they know about." She slung his satchel over her own shoulder, folio and all, and felt its weight settle against the carved heron in her pocket. "Idris. Look at me. I have spent my whole adult life being excellent at not belonging to anyone, and I am telling you it is a skill, not a virtue. Right now we belong to the same problem. That's enough to start."

He looked at her—this stranger who had come upriver out of reluctance and stayed out of something she hadn't named—and something in his starved face steadied.

"Ren's journal," he said. "The second copy. I hid it where I hid myself. There's a gallery under the third terrace with an old tool chest. But Noor—" his voice cracked and he pushed through it, "—the last entry. He wrote about the source. He wrote that the stone at the spring is carved with a landfall mark. He said—" Idris swallowed. "He said it was an Ashgrove mark."

The engines on the river grew a fraction louder.

Noor stood very still, the dawn coming up gray behind the quarry rim, and felt eleven years of running arrive all at once at a door she had nailed shut from the outside.

"Get your leg under you," she said. "We're going to need to move fast, and then, I think, we're going to need to move home."

* * *

**—journal of Aldous Ren, final entry, water-stained, recovered from the flooded workings:**

*If the boy is reading this, I am sorry, and I am proud, and the map lives because you do. Corvane's men are coming at the third bell. The mark on the stone is an Ashgrove mark. Find an Ashgrove. Make her stop running.*

*The river remembers what we bury. It keeps everything. It is keeping me.*
