## 3. Headwaters

The river turned against them on the third morning.

Noor had known it would. Thaw never came gently to the high country—it came like a mob, all at once, with the snowfields giving up their winter in a single furious week. The Ashgrove, which had run shallow and conversational for days, now ran brown and shouting, hauling ice the size of coffins downstream with a sound like furniture breaking. She stood at the tiller of the *Marrow*, the little boat she'd traded her good compass for, and felt the current shove the hull sideways every time she corrected, as if the river were a horse testing a new rider.

"Is it supposed to sound like that?" Idris called from the bow. He had the survey's stolen journal strapped across his chest like armor, and both hands locked on the rail with the grip of a man who had learned, recently and permanently, that the world could take things from him.

"No," Noor said. "It's supposed to sound worse. Sit down."

He sat. He'd been sitting when she told him to for six days now, which was more obedience than she'd gotten from any crew in eleven years of running river, and she didn't know what to do with it. Trust was a cargo she'd never learned to stow properly. It shifted. It made the boat ride wrong.

They passed the last marker at noon—a leaning post with the government's faded brass plate, the one that said WATERSHED BOUNDARY: ENTRY PROHIBITED in letters that had been weathered past menace into mere suggestion. Idris read it aloud anyway, his voice thin over the water's roar. "They put that up the year I was born. My grandfather fished above it his whole life."

"Then your grandfather was a criminal," Noor said, "and so am I. Congratulations. It runs in the best families."

She didn't tell him it ran in hers. Not yet. Not until she'd decided what she was going to do about it.

The valley narrowed. The banks rose from mud to stone to walls of granite furred with moss, and the river between them went from brown to a strange, glassy green, the color of bottle bottoms, cold enough that she knew a man in the water would have perhaps four minutes to change his mind about everything. The air smelled of snowmelt and wet iron. Somewhere ahead, under all that noise, was the source—the spring the survey team had been sent to map and forbidden to describe, the thing Idris had seen his chief do murder to protect.

"Tell me again," she said, because the repetition kept them both from thinking about the cold. "What did Vessale find."

Idris had told her four times. He told her a fifth. "It isn't a spring. Not the way the reports say. The water comes up under pressure—artesian, from a fractured aquifer—and the flow has been increasing for years. The survey's real purpose wasn't mapping. It was appraisal. Vessale wasn't measuring a river, he was measuring a *claim*. Whoever controls the source controls every town downstream, every mill, every ferry. And the land around it—" He stopped. He'd stopped here every time, and every time she'd made him go on, and every time he looked at her like he was handing her something heavy. "The land around it has an owner on record. A lapsed claim. Ashgrove."

The name went through her the way the cold water would have. She kept her eyes on the channel. "My grandmother's," she said finally. "The landfall claim. Mouth of the river. I thought it was all rivermouth shacks and silt."

"The claim runs the whole watershed. Head of navigation to source. It's in the old grant language—your grandmother's grant language. It lapsed when she died because nobody filed succession. But lapsed isn't extinguished. A living Ashgrove can seal it. Or sell it." He looked at her, and she felt the look like a hand on her shoulder. "That's why they sent for *you*, Noor. Not for your boat. Vessale needed a living Ashgrove to sign the source over to the agency before the claim could be contested. The missing survey team was never the emergency. *You* were the emergency."

The river took a bend and the *Marrow* took it badly, slewing toward the rock wall, and Noor let the near-miss eat the three seconds she needed before she trusted her voice. Eleven years. Eleven years of outrunning a name she'd thought was worth nothing but debt, and it had been the whole time a fortune with her face on it, and men had died deciding what to do about that.

"Then we're not running from them anymore," she said. "We're running *to* something. There's a difference. It matters."

* * *

They reached the headwaters basin at dusk, and it was nothing like she had imagined.

She had imagined a trickle, a seep, something apologetic. Instead the spring burst from a seam in the mountainside like a wound that had never learned to close, a column of water wide as a barn door, and the pool it filled was so clear it looked like a hole in the world—she could see forty feet down to pale stone, and the stone was moving, shivering, with the pressure from below. Snow came down around it in fat, slow flakes that melted the instant they touched the water. The air was full of the sound of the river's beginning, which was not gentle at all. It sounded like a crowd. Like a crowd that had been shouting for ten thousand years.

Idris stood at the pool's edge with the journal open in his hands and his face wet, and Noor couldn't tell if it was snowmelt or something else. "It's beautiful," he said, in the voice of a man apologizing to it.

"That's how it gets you," Noor said. "Water's a con artist. Sounds like peace right up until it's got you under."

She heard the other boat before she saw it—engine noise where no engine had any business being, upstream of everything, at the top of the world. She got Idris behind the granite shoulder of the spring's outcrop and had the *Marrow*'s line in her hand, ready to run, when the survey launch came around the last bend with its running lights on, obscene in the wild dark, and Vessale stepped onto her bow with a lantern held high like a man arriving at his own coronation.

"Noor Ashgrove," he called across the water. His voice carried the way voices carry in high thin air, perfectly, horribly. "There she is. The heir."

"There's no heir," she called back. "There's a boat captain with a fare to drop."

"There's a signature," Vessale said. "Yours. One page. You seal the claim, the agency compensates you beyond anything you'd make in fifty years of hauling cargo, your young friend gets a full pardon and a fresh name, and everyone walks out of this valley breathing. That's the offer. It doesn't get made twice, and it doesn't get made to corpses, which is the alternative and I'll be honest, Noor, it's the one I've been budgeting for."

The snow thickened. Noor felt the storm arriving the way sailors feel a squall—a pressure in the ears, a wrongness in the light. Behind Vessale, two of his men stood with rifles not quite pointed anywhere, which was its own kind of pointing.

"You killed four men to get to this page," she said.

"I *lost* four men," Vessale said, "to a river that doesn't care who owns it. Which is rather my point. The claim exists so that someone does care. Someone with a name and a stamp. Sign, and the chaos becomes order, and order is the only thing that's ever saved anyone."

She thought of her grandmother then—really thought of her, for the first time in years, not as a debt or a name but as a woman who had stood at the river's mouth and put her mark on paper and meant it. *Landfall,* the old grant was called. The place where you stop running.

"No," Noor said.

Vessale sighed, and the lantern light made his face gentle and terrible. "I was afraid you'd say that."

The storm chose that moment to arrive in earnest. The wind came down the valley like a landslide of air, the snow went horizontal, and the world went white, and in the white Noor heard the thing she would hear in her sleep for the rest of her life: Idris, no longer beside her, shouting her name from somewhere below and to the left, and then the sound of the pool taking him.

She went in after him.

The cold was not a sensation. It was a decision the water made about her, instantly and completely, a fist closing on every muscle she owned. She'd been in thaw rivers before; she had never been in water that came up from under the earth, water with the winter still in it, water that had never once been warm. She opened her eyes in the clear terrible green and saw him below her, ten feet down, the journal still strapped to his chest like an anchor, his hair streaming, his eyes open and fixed on her with a calm that was worse than panic.

She got an arm under him and kicked for the light, and the water pulled them back down, the spring's outflow dragging everything toward the seam in the mountain, toward the river's throat, and Noor understood with a clarity the cold gave her that the pool was not trying to kill them. It was trying to *keep* them.

Her lungs went to fire. Her shoulder found the outcrop rock and she used it—shoved off it, hard, sideways, out of the outflow's line, and the current's grip tore loose all at once and they came up into the storm gasping, Idris coughing the river back out of himself, the journal's oilcloth wrapping streaming water but sealed, sealed, *sealed*—she'd made him double-wrap it in the quarry and he'd thought she was being fussy.

Vessale's launch was gone from the bend. She heard it later, or told herself she did—the engine note climbing, desperate, turning tail down-valley through the storm. It didn't matter. Nothing he did upstream mattered now, because Noor stood in the shallows with a half-drowned boy coughing in her arms and made the choice that had been waiting for her her whole life.

She didn't keep the journal. She didn't hide it, didn't bargain with it, didn't hold it over anyone. She waded out onto the flat rock at the pool's lip, where the outflow ran fast and clean, and she fed it to the river—page by page, the surveys, the appraisals, the orders in Vessale's own hand—letting the water that had started all of it carry the truth down to the delta, down to Sefa and the dockworkers and the harbor clerk who owed her, down to every soul downstream who drank from this and had a right to know what had been done in their name.

The river took it all without comment. Rivers were good at that. Rivers kept everything.

Idris, shaking, teeth chattering through what he was trying to say, finally got it out: "They'll say we made it up. There's no proof now."

Noor looked down the valley, where the storm was breaking apart and the first hard stars were coming out over the water, all of it flowing one direction, toward the sea, toward the mouth, toward the landfall claim with her grandmother's name on it and her own name waiting underneath like a boat under a tarp.

"There's one kind of proof they can't burn or buy," she said. "A living Ashgrove, standing on the land, filing the claim. Come on. We're going to the mouth of this river."
