## 4. Landfall

The river mouth smelled different in the weeks after the storm. Noor couldn't have said what had changed—salt and silt and tar, the same as always—but she noticed it the morning she walked up from the dock with the claim papers rolled inside her coat, dry against her ribs like a second heartbeat. The air tasted cleaner. Or perhaps it was only that she was breathing differently, standing still long enough to notice anything at all.

The land office at Greywater Landing occupied a former chandlery at the top of the tide stairs, its windows fogged with salt bloom, its clerk a narrow man named Pell who had known her grandmother and, Noor suspected, had loved her in the quiet way clerks love people who bring them interesting paperwork. He looked up when she entered, and his eyes went first to her face, then to the roll of paper in her hands, and something in his expression shifted—recognition, then caution, then a kind of careful hope.

"Noor Ashgrove," he said. Not a question.

"Captain Ashgrove," she said, and heard her own voice steady on the name for the first time since she'd been old enough to hate it. "I'm here about the landfall claim. Section nine, mouth of the Vell. My grandmother's."

Pell drew out a ledger so old its spine had been mended with fishing line. He turned pages with the reverence of a man handling scripture, and there it was—the Ashgrove entry, ink gone brown as river water, the lapsed seal stamped beside it in faded red. *Unclaimed forty-one years. Reversion pending.*

"Forty-one years," Pell murmured. "Half the survey men who came through here asked after this section. Asked why nobody held it. I told them the truth—that the last Ashgrove died, and her granddaughter wouldn't so much as say the name." He glanced up. "They stopped asking after a while. Started measuring instead."

"I know what they measured." She unrolled the papers on his counter: her grandmother's death record, her own birth line, the chain of descent written out in Idris's precise cartographer's hand. "I'm claiming it. Today. Before your reversion clock runs out, before anyone else files, before—" She stopped herself. *Before they can stop me* hung in the room anyway, unsaid.

Pell read everything twice. Then he took out a fresh pen, and the scratch of it against the register was the loudest sound Noor had ever heard.

When it was done—when the seal pressed into wax and the wax cooled and the ledger closed over her name like a hand closing over a stone—she walked back down the tide stairs on legs that felt borrowed. She had spent twenty years imagining this moment as a surrender. It felt nothing like surrender. It felt like setting down a load she'd forgotten she was carrying, and discovering her shoulders still worked.

* * *

The house at the end of Dock Row had been hers by inheritance too, though she'd let it stand empty, shutters banging, letting the weather argue with it year after year. Now there were lights in every window.

Idris had taken the ground-floor room that faced the water and turned it into a map room. Charts covered the walls—not just the Vell but every tributary, every flooded quarry, every bend where the survey team's gear had surfaced or hadn't. In the center, pinned under glass, was the map he'd been carrying when Noor found him: the headwaters, drawn in his small exact letters, with the spring itself marked not as the agency's charts had marked it—a blank, a void, an absence—but with a single word in red ink. *Witnessed.*

"They can't unsee what I saw," he'd said the night he pinned it up. "And now neither can anyone else. That's what a map is, Captain. It's testimony you can fold."

The strays had found them the way strays do, without being sought. Old Sefa from the eel boats, whose knees had finally quit on her, now kept the kitchen and cooked as if feeding an army. A boy called Tam who'd been running cargo for men no better than the survey chief slept in the loft and asked endless questions about rivers. And Brannic—the liaison who had hired her all those months ago, nervous, sweating through his collar—arrived one gray evening with a satchel full of documents and no official sanction whatsoever, and simply never left.

It was Brannic who brought the news down the hill that first winter week, breath steaming, boots loud on the boards.

"The tribunal's seated. Three magistrates, none of them appointed by the agency." He dropped the satchel on the table among the supper dishes. "Your evidence made it downstream. All of it. The journals, the sealed orders, the copies of the source surveys Idris smuggled out. They're calling it the Headwaters Inquiry."

"And the survey chief?" Noor asked.

"In custody. Not quietly, either—he's naming names, trying to buy his way lighter. Every name he gives drags another man down with him." Brannic allowed himself a thin smile. "He gave up yours, Captain. Told them you refused his offer at the spring. Refused a fortune, in front of witnesses, in a storm. The magistrates liked that."

Idris looked up from his charts. "And my name?"

"Cleared. Formally. The order's signed." Brannic slid a paper across the table. "You're not a fugitive anymore, boy. You're the inquiry's star witness. They want you to testify—draw the source as you saw it, mark where the team went, show the court what the agency buried."

Noor watched Idris take the paper. His hands, which had been steady enough to draw flood lines in a moving boat, shook now. She understood. It was easier to survive than to be seen surviving—to stand in a bright room and say *this happened to me, and I remember all of it.*

"You don't have to do it alone," she said. "I'll be sitting behind you. Sefa will feed the whole courtroom if that's what it takes. Tam will heckle."

That earned a wet laugh. "You'd come ashore for me? Permanently?"

"I live here," Noor said, and the strangeness of it still surprised her, saying it aloud. "I filed papers. There's a seal with my name on it in a ledger in Greywater Landing. I'm told that means I've stopped running."

* * *

The night before the tribunal convened, Noor walked the length of her claim.

She started at the dock, where the *Kestrel* rode at anchor—her boat, her old escape, now moored like a dog that had learned to stay. She followed the tideline past the rotting pilings and the new ones Sefa's nephews had driven, past the eel traps and the drying nets, all the way to the boundary stone her grandmother had set a lifetime ago: a squat granite post carved with the Ashgrove mark, half-swallowed by moss.

Noor knelt and cleared the moss away with her thumb. The carving was crude, worn, unmistakable. Her grandmother's hands had cut it. Her grandmother's stubbornness had planted it here, at the edge of the water, facing down the whole length of the river as if to say: *everything upstream answers to someone, eventually.*

For years Noor had believed the claim was a chain—her family's name wrapped around her ankle, dragging her toward obligations she never chose. Standing at the stone now, she understood it differently. The claim wasn't a weight. It was an anchor. And an anchor only holds if you want to stay.

Behind her, up the slope, the house glowed. She could hear Tam laughing at something, Sefa scolding him fondly, Idris's pen scratching late at his maps. Strays, all of them. Her crew, whether she'd ever formally signed them on or not.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold granite, once, the way you'd touch the shoulder of an old friend.

Then she turned and walked back up toward the light—and stopped halfway, because there was a lantern coming down the tide stairs, fast, and a voice calling her name. Pell's voice, breathless, urgent.

"Captain! Captain Ashgrove—you need to see this. A rider just came through from upriver." He thrust a folded paper at her, his hand unsteady. "The thaw's early this year. The Vell's rising faster than any gauge on record—and at the headwaters, where your witness marked the spring—" He swallowed. "The water's coming up *red*, ma'am. And there are boats on the river that aren't answering any hail. Boats flying no colors at all."

Noor unfolded the paper. Idris's red-inked word stared up at her from the copy Pell had brought—*witnessed*—and beneath it, in a stranger's hand, a single line:

*The source isn't finished giving up its dead.*
