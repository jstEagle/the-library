## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The river had been trying to tell me something all morning, and like most men who make their living off other people's misfortunes, I wasn't listening.

I'd been on the *Marrow* since before dawn — my dredge barge, forty feet of rusted stubbornness anchored over the wreck of the steamer *Coriander's Pride*, eleven fathoms down in the silt-brown throat of the Vell. Sanctioned site, stamped papers, a government seal that said I was allowed to pick at this particular corpse. The navy had sunk her sixty years back, and the navy had signed off on her salvage, and somewhere between those two facts there was supposed to be nothing but honest work.

Honest work. That's what I told my crew when they asked why a man my age still went down himself instead of hiring younger lungs. The truth was simpler: I liked the quiet down there. No one talks at eleven fathoms. No one lies, either, because there's no breath to waste on it.

"Visibility's soup," said Oskar Brenn, my tender, leaning over the rail as I checked my harness. He was twenty-three, built like a dock crane, and permanently convinced I was going to drown just to spite him. "Current's up too. River's moody."

"The river's always moody," I said. "That's what makes it honest."

I spat over the side — for luck, though luck and I had an arrangement more of tolerance than affection — and went down the ladder into water the color of steeped tea.

* * *

Down there, the world narrowed to the circle of my lamp and the rasp of my own breathing through the regulator. The *Coriander's Pride* lay on her starboard side like a woman asleep in a doorway, hull furred with sixty years of river weed, paddle wheel collapsed into itself like a folded fan. I'd been working her for three weeks. Most of what came up was disappointment: rivets, crockery, a child's boot, coils of rope gone to black mush. Cargo manifests said she'd carried refugees upriver when she went down. History agreed. History and cargo manifests are usually right, or at least agreeable enough that nobody checks.

I was cutting free a tangle of chain near the forward hold when my lamp caught something that didn't belong.

Not metal exactly — metal was everywhere down here, eating itself patiently. This was shape. A box, maybe chest-sized, half-swallowed by sediment, sitting where the hold's collapse should have crushed it flat. It hadn't been crushed. It sat in its pocket of silt like an egg in a nest, patient as only lead can be.

I worked around it for twenty minutes before I let myself touch it, which is either professionalism or cowardice depending on how you count such things. When I finally cleared it, my gloves came away gray with silt and my heart was doing something unprofessional.

Lead-sheathed. Sealed. And on the lid, under an inch of grime I wiped away with my thumb, a crest: a tower above crossed keys, struck through with a single diagonal bar.

I knew that crest. Everyone past fifty knew that crest. It had flown over the Republic of Velmark until Velmark stopped existing — not conquered, not dissolved, just *erased*, borders redrawn at some conference table by men who'd never smelled the river. They'd taken the flag down and the maps were reprinted and within a decade schoolchildren couldn't have found the place on a globe if you'd paid them. A whole nation filed under *former*. The navy that sank the *Coriander's Pride* had done it wearing the victor's confidence of men erasing a chalkboard.

And here was the chalkboard's answer, sealed in lead, sitting in the belly of a ship the same navy had put on the bottom.

I've measured my life in miles. That's the diver's arithmetic — miles of river surveyed, miles of cable run, miles between wrecks, between contracts, between the women and harbors I'd left behind with varying degrees of grace. Miles are comfortable. Miles don't ask anything of you except more of themselves.

But kneeling on that drowned deck with my thumb on a dead nation's seal, I felt the units change. The river had just measured me in consequences, and I could feel them stacking up like water pressure, fathom by fathom, waiting for the moment I surfaced.

I brought the chest up anyway. Of course I did. Curiosity is the occupational disease of salvage men; we're the people who can't walk past a locked drawer.

* * *

Oskar hauled me aboard and then helped me wrestle the chest onto the deck plating, both of us grunting like it owed us money. Water sheeted off the lead. In daylight the crest looked worse — not faded, but *accusatory*, the way old things get when they've waited too long to be seen.

"That's Velmark," Oskar said. Not a question. His voice had gone careful, the way voices do around things that might be listening.

"It's Velmark," I agreed.

"They said she was carrying refugees."

"They say a lot of things." I crouched, ran my palm along the seam. The seal was intact — poured lead, official weight, the kind of closure you use when whatever's inside has to survive neglect, disaster, and decades. Somebody had wanted this chest found eventually. Or wanted it *not* found ever, and the river had simply outvoted them. Rivers are good at that. Rivers hold everything and give it back on their own schedule, and no manifest, no treaty, no navy has ever successfully appealed their rulings.

"Boss," Oskar said slowly, "should we open it?"

"No."

He blinked. "You're the most curious man I know. You opened a rum barrel once to check if it was empty. It was full. You drank it."

"I did," I admitted, "and I learned from that experience that some containers are better left as mysteries." I stood, wiped my hands. "This one's sealed under authority. Lead seal means somebody official closed it. We log it, we report it, we let the paperwork decide."

Oskar looked at me like I'd started speaking in tongues. In fifteen years he'd never heard me volunteer for paperwork. Neither had I. But something about the crest made my mouth dry, and when a man's instincts start shouting, the smart move is to hand them the wheel.

We logged it. Time of recovery, position, description. I wrote *lead-sealed strongbox, crest of former Republic of Velmark* in my own square handwriting and felt, absurdly, like I was signing a confession.

* * *

The navy launch arrived fifty-one minutes later.

I know it was fifty-one minutes because I'd just checked my watch when Oskar said, "Boat coming," and I remember thinking that was fast — faster than any salvage report travels through bureaucracy. Bureaucracy moves at the speed of sediment. This had moved at the speed of intent.

She came around the bend gray and low, engines running quiet, no ensign flying, which on a naval vessel is its own kind of statement. She slid alongside the *Marrow* without hailing, and four men came over the rail in order, like beads on a string. Three of them were young, broad, and dressed like they'd been told to look civilian and hadn't quite managed it. The fourth was older, gray at the temples, uniform jacket under a plain oilskin, and he looked at my deck the way an auditor looks at a ledger — already certain something was wrong, merely confirming the amount.

"Petra Halloran," he said. Not a question either. It was going to be that kind of afternoon.

"Commander," I said, reading the rank off his collar, because two could play at that game.

"Ashe." He didn't offer a hand. "You recovered an item this morning. Lead-sealed. Where is it?"

Straight to it, then. I gestured at the chest, sitting where we'd left it, dripping quietly onto my deck. Commander Ashe crossed to it and stood over it for a long moment, and I watched his face do something very small and very controlled — the expression of a man finding a debt he'd hoped was written off.

"You logged it," he said.

"I log everything."

"Of course you do." He turned. For the first time he really looked at me, and I got the full weight of those eyes, pale and unhurried, the eyes of a man who'd spent his career making problems disappear and had gotten good at deciding which ones deserved effort. "Halloran. Your license covers recovery, not retention. The item is state property. Underwater cultural heritage statute, section nine. You'll be compensated for your time."

"It's my find," I said. "Sanctioned site. My permit says—"

"Your permit," Ashe said mildly, "is a renewable document."

There it was. Not a threat, exactly. Threats are loud; this was quiet, the way deep water is quiet, and quiet things are the ones that kill divers. Around us his three young men had arranged themselves into a loose geometry that included my chest, my rail, and my crew, and Oskar had gone very still by the winch, the way people do when they realize the conversation has stopped being a conversation.

I thought about arguing. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this: I considered it. I ran the numbers the way I'd run them a thousand times — depth, current, odds, cost. And the numbers came back the way they always come back when the other man owns the harbor, the license office, and apparently the response time of gunboats on my stretch of river.

"Compensated," I repeated.

"Fairly." Ashe signaled, and two of his men lifted the chest between them with the reverence of pallbearers. At the rail he paused, one boot on the gunwale, and gave me a last look that was almost — almost — apologetic.

"For what it's worth, Halloran, you found nothing today. That's the cleanest outcome available to you. I'd take it."

"And if I'd rather know what I found?"

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Interest, maybe, which is worse. "Then you're a man who measures his life in miles," he said, "and I'd hate to see you start counting in other units."

They dropped down to the launch, and she pulled away around the bend, gray into gray, taking a dead republic's secrets with her.

I stood at the rail a long time after she was gone. The river kept moving the way rivers do, indifferent and total, carrying everything toward the sea. Sixty years ago it had swallowed a ship and held its tongue. Today it had handed the tongue to me, and I'd let a man in an oilskin carry it off, and told myself that was prudence.

Prudence. Self-deception's favorite coat.

Oskar came and stood beside me. "We're just letting them take it?"

"We're letting them take it."

"And that's it?"

I watched the water, brown and patient, already closing over the wake. "That's it," I said, and hated how easily it came out.

It wasn't it, of course. Nothing on a river is ever *it*. Two days later, a letter arrived on navy stationery smelling faintly of brass polish and old paper, requesting my presence at the Harbor Master's annex regarding "matters arising from your recent operations." No threat in it. No promise either. Just an appointment, the way the river makes appointments — quietly, downstream, whether you show up or not.

I read it twice, folded it into my pocket, and caught myself doing the old arithmetic. Miles to the annex: four. Miles I could put between myself and all of this, if I ran tonight: hundreds.

I didn't run. I should have. Instead I told Oskar to mind the barge, and I walked into town to keep my appointment, measuring every step in consequences.
