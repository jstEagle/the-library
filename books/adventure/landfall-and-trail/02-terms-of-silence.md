## 2. Terms of Silence

The summons came the way bad weather comes to a river man: not as a question but as a condition of the day.

I'd been home eleven hours. Long enough to hose the silt off my gear, short enough that my ears still rang with the river's low conversation. The knock on my door was naval in character — three raps, evenly spaced, patient as a man who already knew I'd answer. The ensign standing on my step couldn't have been older than the chest I'd pulled up, and he had the same sealed quality about him.

"Mr. Halloran. Commander Ashe requests your presence at the Admiralty annex. Nine o'clock."

"Requests," I said.

He didn't blink. "Nine o'clock, sir."

The annex wasn't the main naval headquarters — that was granite and flags and tourists taking photographs of sentries. This was a narrow brick building two streets back from the harbor, the kind of place where paperwork went to be forgotten on purpose. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs took my name without looking up, and I sat in a corridor that smelled of brass polish and old paper, that particular sweetness of documents decomposing in their files. Forty minutes of it. Long enough to count the water stains on the ceiling and decide there were nine of them, arranged like islands on a chart nobody would ever sail.

At ten past nine — because even courtesy is a form of command — a door opened and a woman in civilian clothes said, "Mr. Halloran. Please."

* * *

Commander Ashe's office held a desk, two chairs, a window with a view of a wall, and more brass than a chapel. He was maybe fifty-five, built like something designed to survive pressure, with gray hair cut so precisely it looked surveyed rather than grown. His hands were folded on the blotter when I came in, and they stayed folded for the first five minutes, which told me everything about how much he trusted them not to do something else.

"You recovered an item yesterday from the *Vespera* site," he said. "A lead-sealed chest."

"I recovered a lot of items. Bottles, chain, a ship's bell, somebody's boot—"

"The chest, Mr. Halloran."

"It's logged. It's in my salvage hold under seal number—" I gave him the number, and watched him write it down even though I'd have bet my boat he already knew it by heart. That's the thing about men like Ashe. They make you perform knowledge they possess absolutely, just to establish who holds the pen.

"The crest on that seal belongs to a nation that no longer exists," he said. "Its dissolution was formalized twenty-two years ago. Its archives were dispersed. Some were destroyed." He unfolded his hands, refolded them. "Some were lost in transit."

"In transit," I said. "On a steamer."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise, exactly. Recognition that I wasn't going to be stupid, which he seemed to regard as a complication rather than a relief.

"The *Vespera* was carrying government materials when she went down," he said. "That's all you need to know."

"With forty-one passengers aboard."

"That is also true." He said it the way a man confirms the weather. Rain expected. Forty-one passengers. Both facts, filed side by side.

I looked at the wall out the window instead of at him. Brick, mortar, a drainpipe running down it like a plumb line. "I've dived that wreck six times over four years. Sanctioned site. Permitted work. Nobody ever mentioned government materials."

"Because until yesterday, no one had found any."

"And now?"

"Now the item has been located, and its disposition is a matter of state interest." He opened a drawer — unhurried, deliberate, a man who had rehearsed this opening of drawers — and set a folder on the desk between us. "You're regarded as the best river pilot working the upper reaches. Your wreck certifications are current. Your discretion, we're told, is professional."

"Told by whom?"

"By people whose opinions we pay for." He let that sit. "We are mounting a survey expedition. Hydrographic, cartographic, upper river, above the Kestrel Rapids. It departs in nineteen days. We require a guide who knows the channels, the portages, and the calving windows on the Serac Glacier. You will be paid triple your standard rate. You will sign the enclosed confidentiality instrument. And upon completion, your permits will be renewed for five years without review."

I didn't touch the folder. "And if I don't?"

Ashe leaned back. For the first time he looked almost comfortable, which frightened me more than anything he'd said so far.

"Then the salvage license becomes subject to review," he said. "Review finds irregularities. Irregularities find penalties. And separately — and I say this with genuine regret, Mr. Halloran, because I've read your file and I think you're a serious man — the recovery of state materials from a protected site, followed by their unreported retention, is a matter that could interest prosecutors. Retention being the operative word. You did log the chest. But you also opened the seal before logging it. Didn't you."

My mouth went dry. I hadn't told anyone that. Not the harbormaster, not my deckhand, nobody. The seal had been cracked along one edge when I'd pried the lid loose on the barge deck, alone, at midnight, because I'm a man who measures things and I wanted to measure what was inside. Papers. Bundled papers, water-swollen but legible, stamped with that dead republic's crest, and one line of handwriting across the top page in faded iron-gall ink that I could still read in the dark behind my eyelids.

"How do you know that?" I asked.

"Because the seal was photographed at the recovery site, and again at your hold, and the photographs differ." He said it gently, which was worse. "I'm not here to threaten you, Halloran. I'm here to offer you the only door out of the room you're standing in. It happens to be upriver."

* * *

I walked the harbor for an hour after that, because I needed brick walls at my back instead of in front of me, and gulls screaming, and the smell of diesel and fish doing their honest work on my senses. Triple rate. Five-year renewal. Or a courtroom where the navy owned the judge the way it owned the annex, the clerk, and probably the gulls.

Here's the arithmetic I did, standing at the rail watching tugs nudge a freighter into her berth: I have spent my whole life measuring distance. Miles of channel. Fathoms of depth. The span between a hull and the rock that wants it. It's clean work, measurement. Numbers don't lie to you; they just tell you the truth about the water and let you decide what to do with it.

But the river had measured me back yesterday. It had put a lead-sealed box in my hands and said: *here are your consequences, diver. Take their weight or take their price.* And now a man with surveyed hair was offering to convert that weight into wages.

I told myself it was simple. I told myself: it's a paid run. High water season, good money, a few weeks of playing nursemaid to navy surveyors who can't read a current. I've done harder jobs for worse clients. I told myself the chest was just cargo, that governments lose paper all the time, that whatever was in those bundles had been underwater thirty years and could wait another month under military lock. I told myself that signing a piece of paper wasn't the same as meaning anything by it.

I want to be clear that I knew, even then, that I was lying. That's the part I keep coming back to, years later. Not the signing — the knowing. There's a moment on every dive when you pass below the light line, where the sun stops reaching and the water goes from green to black, and you feel it in your chest, that small cold announcement: *you are now somewhere you can't turn around quickly.* I felt that same announcement in the annex office, sitting in a chair that smelled of someone else's pipe smoke, and I signed anyway.

The pen was navy-issue, heavy as a tool. My signature came out steady, which surprised me. Steady hands, dishonest heart. There's a phrase for sailors who navigate by instruments they know are broken. We call it *dead reckoning*, and we say it without irony, and that morning I was the term made flesh: moving forward on a position I no longer trusted, trusting the motion itself to save me.

Ashe countersigned without reading it, which told me he'd read it a hundred times. Then he stood, and offered his hand, and his grip was dry and exact.

"Departure briefing in nineteen days. You'll meet the full complement then — surveyors, a cartographer, security detail." He paused at the word *security*, letting me hear the weight he'd hidden inside it. "One more thing, Halloran. The chest travels with us. Sealed. If it should happen to open again — by accident, by curiosity, by any cause whatsoever — that event will be treated as a breach of your instrument. Am I understood?"

"Perfectly," I said. "Though I'll point out that curiosity is how you found me."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment of a hit scored by both sides.

"Yes," he said. "It is. Mind it doesn't kill you."

* * *

Nineteen days later I stood on the deck of the expedition steamer — a converted gunboat called the *Meridian*, all fresh paint over old bones — and watched the last crates come aboard under tarpaulin. One crate was longer and heavier than the others, and the two sailors carrying it walked like men handling a coffin, and I didn't need x-ray eyes to know what slept inside it.

The crew assembled on the aft deck for introductions. Surveyors mostly, young men with the pale look of people promoted above their talents. Two of Ashe's security detail, who introduced themselves by surname only and stood the way furniture stands. And at the end of the line, a woman with wind-chapped cheeks and a leather map case slung crosswise, who shook my hand with a grip that had hauled survey equipment through real country.

"Ines Varga," she said. "Cartography. You're the guide? Petra Halloran?"

"I'm the guide."

"They said you raised the first artifact off the *Vespera*." Her eyes were brown and level and missed nothing, and I felt them go over me the way I'd go over a stretch of water before committing the boat to it — checking for rocks under the surface. "What was it like? Finding something from her?"

"Just mud and metal," I said. "Mostly."

"Mostly," she repeated, softly, filing the word away somewhere I couldn't see.

Ashe appeared at the rail and called the assembly to order, and the *Meridian* cast off into the brown morning current. I went forward to my wheel, and through the glass I watched Ines Varga stand at the stern rail, watching the harbor fall away, wearing the expression of a person who hadn't come to map a river at all.

I turned upstream and told myself, once more, that it was just another paid run.

The river, ahead of us, said nothing. Rivers never do. They just wait to see what you'll do with what they've given you.
