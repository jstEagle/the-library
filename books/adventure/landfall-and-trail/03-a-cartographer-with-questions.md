## 3. A Cartographer with Questions

My grandfather taught me that a map is a confession. Every line you draw admits what you know, and every blank space confesses what you don't. He said it the way other men say grace — automatically, without expecting anyone to listen. I was seven and I listened anyway, because he was the only adult in my life who treated blank spaces like promises instead of threats.

He went into one when I was nine. The *Corvessa*, lost on the Vell with all hands, or nearly all hands, depending on which ledger you believed. The navy's ledger said all. My grandmother's grief said otherwise for years, until it wore itself down to a smooth stone she kept on the windowsill and touched when she thought no one saw.

I became a cartographer because of that stone. I have never once told an interviewer this, and I am telling it now only to myself, in the privacy of my own skull, where it can't be used against me.

* * *

The naval office smelled of brass polish and paper gone soft with age, and the man across the desk from me had the particular stillness of someone who had learned to let other people fill silences. Commander Ashe. Grey at the temples, grey in the eyes, grey everywhere except his voice, which was warm the way a stove is warm — useful, and dangerous to lean on.

"Miss Varga. Your survey work on the Lower Vell came highly recommended."

"I'm flattered," I said, which was true, and also a lie of omission so large it had its own weather system.

"The expedition requires a cartographer for the upper river. The charts are forty years old and worthless. You'd be mapping territory no survey has recorded since before the war." He slid a contract across the desk. "The pay reflects the inconvenience."

I read it twice. It was generous, and it was strange: a clause about communications discipline, a clause about returning all materials, a clause about discretion that used the word *indefinitely*. Expeditions don't usually ask you to keep secrets from people who aren't born yet.

"Who else is going?" I asked.

"A salvage diver named Halloran will guide us through the river proper. A small crew. Naval escort." Something moved behind his eyes when he said Halloran's name — not warmth, not contempt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man holding a leash and knowing exactly how much slack was in it.

I signed. Of course I signed. Somewhere under the Vell's cold water, in a wreck the navy suddenly cared about forty years too late, there might be a locker, a letter, a body wearing my grandmother's ring. You don't refuse a door like that. You walk through it and apologize later to whoever you were before.

* * *

I met Petra Halloran on the dock at Graywater Landing, where the expedition's boat — a converted gunboat called the *Tern*, all new paint over old scars — sat low in the water like something pretending to be innocent.

He was easier to read than Ashe, which is to say he was readable at all. Big hands, diver's forearms, a face that had been weathered by rivers rather than sun. He looked at me the way men in his trade look at everything: assessing weight, estimating what it would cost to move.

"You're the mapmaker," he said.

"Ines Varga. You're the guide."

"That depends who you ask." He almost smiled. It didn't survive the trip to his eyes. "Welcome aboard the Tern. Mind the third stair. It lies."

I liked him immediately, which annoyed me, because I hadn't come here to like anyone. I had come here with my grandfather's last known position plotted on a chart I kept folded inside my jacket, and with a list of questions I intended to ask carefully and never directly.

The chest changed everything.

* * *

They loaded it aboard the second morning, under guard, which told me more than any briefing did. A salvage crate, iron-banded, lead-sealed, hauled up out of the *Corvessa* herself — I knew the provenance within minutes of hearing the crew talk, because salvage crews gossip like laundresses and I had made a study of listening.

And I watched Petra Halloran see it.

Here is what I know about watching people: most of them perform their reactions. They feel something and then they decide how to show it, and the gap between the two is where liars live. But some reactions happen in the body before the mind can dress them. A flinch is honest. A flinch is the truth arriving early, uninvited.

Petra stood on the dock while two sailors wrestled the chest up the gangplank, and when the morning light caught the seal — a crest stamped in lead, some heraldry I didn't recognize, a double-headed bird or a broken star, worn nearly smooth — his whole posture changed for half a second. Shoulders dropping, chin tucking, the micro-crouch of a man who has heard a shot fired somewhere behind him.

Then it passed, and he was just a diver supervising cargo, bored and competent.

But I had seen it. And once you've seen a man flinch at a piece of metal, you can't unsee it. Every time he walked past the hold after that — and he walked past it often, always with a reason, checking lashings, checking bilge, checking nothing — I watched. He never touched it. That was its own kind of tell. Salvage men touch things; it's how they think. He kept his hands in his pockets around that chest like a man passing a dog he knows bites.

* * *

We made the first anchorage above the falls on the fourth night, and I got him alone the way I get everyone alone eventually: by being useful and undemanding. I spread my working charts on the deck table under a lamp and started correcting them, and correction is bait no navigator can ignore.

He took it, as I'd hoped. Leaned over my shoulder, pointed out a sandbar that had migrated a mile downstream since the old survey, traced the channel with one blunt finger.

"Your hand shakes," I said.

"It does that when I haven't been diving. Too much blood in the alcohol, my old instructor used to say." He straightened. "Good charts. Better than the ones Command gave us."

"Command's charts are forty years old."

"So's most of what we're doing." He said it lightly, but his eyes went to the hatch that led down to the hold, and I followed the glance the way a compass follows north.

"The wreck," I said. "The Corvessa. You raised the chest yourself?"

Something closed in his face — not a door slamming, more like a curtain drawn by someone very careful about cords. "I raised a chest. Provenance is the navy's business now."

"It was a passenger steamer."

"It was a lot of things." He picked up one of my pencils, turned it over, set it down. "Why do you care? You're here for the upper river. Blank spaces. Isn't that what you mapmakers chase?"

It was a deflection, and a decent one, and I decided to spend a little truth on him to buy more. "My grandfather was aboard her. Tomas Varga. Able seaman. The navy lists him as lost with all hands." I watched his face while I said it, and this time there was no flinch, no curtain — there was something worse. There was *care*. A quick, pained attention, like a man hearing a name he already knew belonged to a grave.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, and hated meaning it.

"You knew the ship's story before you dove her."

"Everyone in salvage knows the Corvessa. She's famous."

"Famous for what?"

"For sinking." He gathered his charts — my charts — off the table with unnecessary tidiness. "Get some sleep, Miss Varga. We run the narrows tomorrow, and the river doesn't care whose grandfather was aboard anything."

He left. I sat with the lamp and my maps and drew a slow line along the reach where the *Corvessa* had gone down, and then, below it, in the margin where cartographers keep their confessions, I wrote three words in pencil:

*He knows more.*

* * *

I want to be fair to him. This matters to me — fairness, accuracy, the whole professional religion of it. So let the record show that Petra Halloran was, in the days that followed, unfailingly decent to me. He taught me the river's moods. He warned me before every set of rapids with the gravity of a man reading last rites. When the portage trail rotted out from under a crewman and Petra hauled him back from the edge by his collar, I saw genuine fear for another person's life, unperformed, unpriced.

But decency isn't transparency. Some of the most careful liars I've ever mapped were kind men. Kindness is excellent ballast; it keeps a lie stable in rough water.

And the signs kept accumulating, the way silt accumulates — grain by grain, invisible daily, undeniable monthly. The naval escorts conferred in whispers and stopped when I approached. The chest stayed locked in the hold under guard, and once, passing the hatch at night, I heard Ashe's voice inside, low and level, saying words I couldn't make out to someone who answered in monosyllables I suspected were Petra's. The crewman named Doss, who according to the cook had "got curious with a pry bar" during the second week, was transferred off the boat at the next landing with a speed and silence that no ordinary reassignment ever travels with. Nobody would meet my eyes when I asked about him. Nobody would meet my eyes about much of anything anymore.

And Petra watched me. Not the way men usually watch me — I'm accustomed to that and mostly immune — but the way a handler watches a dog that's begun sniffing along a fence line. Interested. Calculating. Deciding whether I was a problem or merely a nuisance.

Fine. Let him calculate. I had been raised on blank spaces, and I had learned the family trade: you don't get the truth by demanding it. You get it by making the truth inconvenient to withhold.

So I worked. I mapped the banks, sounded the channels, corrected the charts, and every evening I sat in the lamplight transcribing my grandfather's letters — the salvaged bundle my grandmother had kept for forty years in a biscuit tin, water-stained, half-legible, written in a shorthand code the navy had never bothered to decode because the navy had never bothered to care. I decoded them openly, on deck, where the watchers could see me doing it. Curious girl, sentimental about her grandfather. Harmless.

Petra stopped by the third night. Stood at the edge of the lamplight with his hands in his pockets, looking at the pages.

"That's an old cipher," he said. "Navy merchant code, pre-war."

"You recognize it?"

"I've salvaged enough pre-war wrecks to recognize the alphabet." A pause. The river muttered against the hull below us. "Does it say anything?"

"Not yet." I looked up at him and let him see me looking. "But it will. Letters always do. That's the trouble with writing things down, Mr. Halloran — paper floats, and divers raise it, and granddaughters read it. Everything sinks eventually except the truth. That's the one thing buoyant enough to come back up."

He held my gaze for a long moment. In the lamplight his eyes were the brown of river water over gravel, and behind them something was turning over, heavy, like a chest shifting its weight in a flooded hold.

"You should be careful," he said quietly. "Not everything that comes up should have."

And he walked away into the dark, leaving me with my grandfather's coded pages and a certainty that had finished sharpening itself into a point:

Whatever the *Corvessa* carried, whatever that lead seal meant, Petra Halloran had known before he ever hooked the chain to it — and Commander Ashe had sent me aboard this boat believing I would never find out why.

The narrows waited for us in the morning. So did the answers, somewhere upriver, in a valley the charts left blank — and I intended to fill them both in.
