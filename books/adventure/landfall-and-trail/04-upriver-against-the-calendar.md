## 4. Upriver, Against the Calendar

The river above Kessel Bend doesn't forgive. It tolerates, briefly, the way a landlord tolerates a tenant he's already decided to evict.

We'd been running it four days when I understood that Commander Ashe had never once looked at the water. He looked at his watch, at his maps, and at me — in that order, and always with the same arithmetic in his face, as if I were a line item that kept threatening to go over budget.

"Your schedule says we clear the Serrat rapids by tomorrow noon," he said that morning, standing on the *Marta Kay*'s deck while the mist came off the water like breath off a dying man.

"My schedule says that if the river agrees," I said. "Rivers don't read schedules, Commander. They write them."

He smiled the way a filing cabinet smiles. "Then persuade it."

I didn't tell him what I was actually thinking, which was that the calving season gave us eleven days — maybe twelve if the weather turned kind, and the weather up here had never once been kind to anybody who needed it to be. The glacier route through the Vhalen valley opened for a window each year, forty-eight hours of passable trail along the ice face before the meltwater swelled and the seracs started shedding house-sized chunks into the river below. Miss the window and you waited a year. Ashe knew this. He'd built the whole expedition around it, which told me something: whatever he wanted in that valley, he wanted it badly enough to bet naval money on a glacier's mood.

* * *

The chest rode in the hold under tarpaulin and lock and two of Ashe's men who never seemed to sleep at the same time. I'd stopped looking at it. That was the arrangement — my silence for my license, my license for my living — and a man can get very good at not looking at things. I'd had practice. There were other chests, other wrecks, other mornings when the river gave up something somebody powerful would rather have stayed down.

But not-looking is a skill that rusts.

It rusted on the fifth day, at the Corvin portage, where the old trading trail along the north bank had rotted back into forest. We hauled the longboat overland on rollers cut from spruce, forty men and a cartographer and a commander who supervised without lifting anything heavier than a clipboard. The portage took eighteen hours. By hour fourteen, one of Ashe's men — a broad, quiet fellow named Doss — had wrenched his shoulder, and I put him on light duty, which meant sitting by the chest while the rest of us bled and swore.

That was how it happened. Not conspiracy. Just a man with a wrenched shoulder, an afternoon of nothing, and a lock that some previous century had made cheap.

I found out about it the way you find out about most disasters: afterward, from the silence. Doss wasn't at supper. Neither were his kit or his hammock roll. When I asked, one of the other navy men said, without looking up from his tin plate, "Reassigned. Launch picked him up at the last beacon. Back downriver."

"At night? In this water?"

"He had orders."

Nobody had orders that good. No launch ran the lower Serrat after dark unless it was carrying something more urgent than a sailor with a sore shoulder. And no sailor got pulled off an expedition mid-portage for anything less than what he'd seen inside a sealed chest bearing the crest of a nation that hadn't existed for thirty years.

I did the math I didn't want to do. A man peeks. Within a day, the man is gone — extracted cleanly, quietly, before anyone could ask him what he'd seen. Which meant they'd known he'd peeked. Which meant someone was watching the watchers, or watching all of us, and doing it well enough that I couldn't tell who.

I looked across the fire at Ines Varga. She was working by lamplight, as she always was, her survey sheets weighted with river stones, drawing the upper channel in fine, patient lines. She glanced up — she had a way of catching me looking, or maybe I had a way of being caught — and held my eyes for a second longer than coincidence allows.

She'd noticed Doss's absence too. I could see it in the set of her mouth, the question she was keeping behind her teeth. She was a woman who asked questions for a living; I could feel them accumulating against my hull like pressure against a dam.

* * *

The Serrat rapids nearly killed us on the seventh day, which was, in fairness, right on schedule.

They run three miles through a granite chute, and thirty years of flood seasons had rearranged every rock I remembered from my last run. I stood at the bow and called the lines while Halloran — no relation, a stolid deckhand who'd signed on for wages and gotten an education — worked the sweep beside me. The *Marta Kay* went through standing on her beam ends twice, shipped half a ton of green water, and came out the bottom with her paint scraped down to honest wood and everyone aboard suddenly devout.

Ashe appeared at the rail as we drifted the calm below, wringing river out of his coat, and for one unguarded moment he looked almost human — pale, wide-eyed, a man who had just learned what the river thought of his schedule.

"Well," he said.

"Well," I agreed.

"We're behind."

"We're alive," I said. "Behind is negotiable. Alive is the asset."

He didn't laugh. He never laughed, though once, near the drowned village of Ostrek, I'd gotten a sound out of him that might have been laughter's taxidermied cousin. Instead he consulted his watch again, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd been doing, and I watched him arrive at a number that satisfied him, because men like Ashe are never behind — reality is merely early.

But something else happened at Serrat, something smaller and worse. While the crew bailed and cursed, one of Ashe's officers went below with a lantern, ostensibly to check the bilge. He was down there too long. When he came up, the lantern was out and his hands smelled of lamp oil, and later I found the tarpaulin over the chest had been retied in a knot I didn't tie.

Somebody was checking on it. Regularly. The way you check on a prisoner.

And that word — *prisoner* — sat with me through the evening watch and wouldn't leave. I'd been telling myself a story since the naval office, the story I'd signed my name under: *it's just another paid run.* Salvage men tell themselves stories the way other men drink; it takes the edge off. But stories have to survive contact with evidence, and the evidence was stacking up like driftwood against a bridge piling. A chest guarded like ordnance. A peeking sailor vanished overnight. An officer visiting the hold with a lantern and a lie. A commander who cared about a valley settlement abandoned thirty years ago with an intensity that had nothing to do with hydrography.

You don't escort a cover-up knowing you're escorting it. You realize it gradually, the way you realize you're lost — first the landmarks stop matching, then the compass starts lying, and then one evening you look around and admit that every direction you've walked has been wrong, and that you passed the point where turning back was simple somewhere miles ago.

I passed it somewhere around the Corvin portage. Maybe earlier. Maybe in the office that smelled of brass and old paper, when I signed.

* * *

On the ninth night we tied up below the Grey Stair, the last serious water before the valley approach, and the cold came down off the ice fields hard enough to make the rigging sing. The crew turned in early. Ashe's men drew lots for night watch, which they'd done every night, always among themselves, never once offering the rotation to my people — a small discourtesy that had grown from odd to ominous.

I couldn't sleep. I went up on deck with coffee gone bitter from sitting too long on the stove, and found I wasn't alone.

Ines was at the stern rail, wrapped in a blanket, watching the moonlight come down the glacier's shoulder eight miles upstream — a white gleam like a blade laid flat against the dark.

"You should be sleeping," I said. "Tomorrow's the Stair."

"So should you." She didn't look at me. "Do you ever count them?"

"Count what?"

"The days." She nodded toward the ice. "Until the window opens. Until it closes. Everyone on this boat counts something different — wages, miles, days to payday. But you count the glacier. I've watched you do it. You look upstream every morning like a man waiting for a verdict."

She was more right than she knew. "Eleven days until it opens," I said. "Two days open. Then it shuts for a year."

"And after it shuts?" Now she looked at me, and there it was — the question behind the questions, the one she'd been carrying since the day she joined at Kessel Bend with her rolled charts and her eyes that missed nothing. "Whatever your commander wants in that valley. After the ice closes, nobody comes back out. Nobody goes in. Whatever happens in there stays in there. Isn't that convenient?"

"Ines—"

"I saw the seal on that chest." Her voice stayed low, level, but underneath it was something moving fast, like current under smooth water. "At Kessel Bend, when they loaded it. You flinched. Don't tell me you didn't — I was ten feet away. You looked at that crest like a man looks at a grave marker with the wrong name on it."

The cold came off the rail and into my hands. I thought about Doss, gone in the night. I thought about the retied knot. I thought about the launch that ran black water after midnight, and about the contract folded in my chart drawer with my signature at the bottom and my freedom stapled to it.

"I'm a salvage man," I said finally. "I raise what the river gives up. What's in it isn't mine to read."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that keeps us both paid."

Something crossed her face — disappointment, or its close cousin, pity — and she turned back to the glacier. For a long moment neither of us spoke, and the river talked instead, the way it always does, patient and indifferent, running its gray mile after gray mile toward a sea that forgets everything.

"Thirty years ago," she said quietly, "a steamer went down in that river with my grandfather aboard. The histories say refugees. The manifests say cargo. My grandmother says nothing at all anymore, because she cried herself past words sometime in the fifties." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I'm not on this boat to map the upper channel, Petra. You know that. And I think you know more than you're saying, and I think it's costing you sleep, because you're up here drinking bad coffee at midnight instead of being warm."

She left me with that, and the cold, and the white blade of the glacier eight miles upstream, opening its window soon, closing it sooner.

I stood at the rail a long time after she'd gone below. Eleven days. Two of them usable. And somewhere ahead, in a valley that had been keeping its own counsel for thirty years, whatever the chest had been carrying — or whatever it pointed to — was waiting under the ice for us to arrive, with a commander who intended to document it and bury it deeper than the river ever had.

I finished the coffee. It tasted like every decision I'd made since the naval office.

Tomorrow we ran the Grey Stair. Tonight, for the first time in twenty years on the water, I was afraid of what waited at the top of the river more than what waited in it.
