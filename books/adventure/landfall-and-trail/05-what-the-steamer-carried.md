## 5. What the Steamer Carried

My grandfather wrote in a cipher he invented for love letters, which tells you everything about the kind of man he was. Anton Varga, second engineer's mate of the *Meridian Star*, spent his watches below deck encoding his heart into columns of numbers, and somewhere above him the river was already composing its reply.

I had three of his letters. They'd come to me in a lacquered box from my mother, who'd gotten them from her mother, who had never once opened them — because grief, in my grandmother's house, was treated like fine china. Displayed. Never used. So the letters sat for forty years while I learned to read contour lines and river gradients, and then Petra Halloran went down into the *Star*'s ribs with a crowbar and a flashlight and came up with a chest stamped with a dead republic's seal, and I sat in my bunk that first night with my hands shaking so badly I dropped the cipher key twice.

Here is what nobody tells you about decoding: it is slow, and it is intimate, and by the third hour you know a man you never met better than you know most people alive. Anton was methodical but sentimental. He repeated certain number-pairs at the ends of paragraphs — I decided they meant *your eyes* — and he made small arithmetic errors when he was excited, which meant I could track his moods across the pages like weather fronts.

The first letter was courtship. The second was homesickness, with a passage about the smell of the engine room that made me laugh out loud alone in a tent on a riverbank, which earned me a strange look from one of Ashe's men through the canvas.

The third letter was dated nine days before the sinking.

* * *

I worked on it during the day like everyone else — soundings, bank surveys, the tedious liturgy of mapmaking that gave me a legitimate reason to be aboard this floating argument between a navy patrol boat and a salvage barge. But at night, when the generator coughed off and the expedition settled into its watch rotations, I spread Anton's pages under my headlamp and did the arithmetic of a dead man.

Nine days out. He wrote fast, the errors clustering, excitement or fear, hard to say. The column-cipher broke open paragraph by paragraph:

*They tell the passengers we carry them to Corvane, to the camps there, to safety. The manifest says two hundred souls. I have counted the boats going out to us at night. There are no passengers, Ines-mira. There are crates, and the crates are cold, and the men who load them do not speak above a whisper.*

I set the page down. My breath fogged in the cold of the tent. Somewhere outside, the river said its one long sentence over and over.

*I have seen inside one crate. I will not write what I saw. I will only write that the Republic did not order this, because I helped file the Republic's manifests for six years and I know their hand, and this seal is right but the paper beneath it is navy stock. Someone has dressed a lie in our colors.*

*If we are sunk, it will not be an accident. If you receive this, take it to no one in uniform. Take it to a schoolteacher, a priest, a printer. Anyone whose living does not depend on the flag.*

There was more — names, coordinates, a reference to "the valley ledger" that meant nothing to me yet and would come to mean everything — but I read those lines perhaps thirty times. The history books said the *Meridian Star* sank with refugees aboard, a tragedy of the border war, the drowned republic's last convoy of the displaced. Every anniversary, wreaths on the water. My grandmother had gone to every one of them, standing very straight, not crying, holding her box of letters like a woman holding a verdict she couldn't yet read.

Two hundred souls. There had been eleven bodies recovered. Eleven. I had told myself for years that the river kept the rest. Rivers do keep things. But rivers don't keep two hundred people without a trace, and they especially don't keep them when the survivors' accounts all mention how quickly the navy cordoned the wreck, how divers came before the salvage crews were licensed, how the site stayed closed for six years for reasons given as "ordnance" and then quietly unspoken.

The steamer hadn't been carrying refugees. It had been carrying evidence — of something done in the valley upstream, something the drowned republic never sanctioned, packed into crates with forged seals and sent downriver to disappear. And when someone decided the crates might surface anyway, the navy had put the *Star* on the bottom of the river with her crew, and then written two hundred ghosts into the passenger manifest to make the sinking unforgivable to investigate.

You could not ask questions about a mass grave of refugees. That was the genius of it. Grief was the perfect sealant.

And now the navy wanted that chest so badly they'd bought a salvage diver's silence with a contract and a threat, and were hauling an armed survey team upriver ahead of the calving season, racing the ice to a valley that appeared nowhere in any official record except, apparently, in the margins of my grandfather's cipher.

I sat with that until my headlamp battery dimmed, and I understood two things with total clarity. First: I was going to need help, and there was exactly one person on this expedition who had touched that chest and flinched. Second: whatever I felt building toward Petra Halloran — and I'd been pretending for two weeks that I didn't notice the way he watched water the way other men watch faces — was about to become secondary to what I needed from him. Or maybe not secondary. Maybe fused. I wasn't ready to examine the weld.

* * *

I found him on the aft deck near midnight, which surprised me less than it should have. Petra didn't sleep much; I'd noticed that too, cataloguing it among the other things I'd noticed and refused to act on. He stood at the rail with his hands wrapped around a tin mug gone cold hours ago, watching the black water slide past, and when my boots hit the deck he didn't startle. He just turned his head enough to confirm it was me and turned back.

"Can't sleep either?" he said.

"I can sleep fine." I leaned on the rail beside him, close enough that our sleeves nearly touched. "I just found sleeping less interesting than a question."

"That sounds like a cartographer's problem."

"It's a granddaughter's problem." I let that sit. He didn't move, but something in him went very still, the way water goes still over a deep spot. "Anton Varga. Second engineer's mate, *Meridian Star*. You pulled up his ship last month. You pulled up his ship wearing gloves, like it was evidence, and then the navy arrived within the hour, and you signed papers I'm not cleared to see, and now here we all are, racing a glacier."

Petra took a slow sip of cold coffee. "You've been busy."

"I decode things for a living. Maps are just ciphers with better manners." I turned to face him. "Three letters, Petra. Ciphered. His own system. And the third one says the *Star* wasn't carrying refugees. It says she was carrying proof of something the navy did upstream, dressed in a stolen seal, and that if the ship went down, it wouldn't be an accident."

The river filled the silence. He didn't deny it. That was the thing I would remember later — he didn't perform confusion, didn't reach for the comfortable lies a man under contract reaches for. He just looked at the water and said, quietly, "What else does it say?"

"'Take it to no one in uniform.'"

That got a short, dry almost-laugh out of him. "Your grandfather sounds like he'd have gotten along with me."

"He died at thirty-four because men like your Commander Ashe needed him at the bottom of a river." I heard my own voice go sharp and didn't rein it in. "So I'll ask plainly, and you can decide how much of your contract survives the asking. What's in the chest?"

For a long moment he studied me, and I watched him weigh it — I could actually see the scales working behind his eyes, loyalty against something else, the professional habit of silence against whatever it was he thought about at midnight with a cold mug in his hands. When he finally spoke, his voice was low, pitched under the engine noise.

"You're asking me to break a contract that has my license and probably my freedom stapled to it."

"I'm asking you to tell me whether my grandfather's death was a line item or a crime."

"And if it's a crime?"

"Then every mile we go upriver, we're carrying the cover-up deeper into the one place the ice hasn't finished hiding yet." I held his gaze. "And you know it. That's why you're out here instead of asleep. That's why you flinched at that seal. You've known since the day you brought it up, and you've been telling yourself it's just another paid run ever since, and it isn't working, is it? The self-deception. It never does. I've tried it myself."

He looked down at the mug, turning it in his scarred hands. "Ashe's orders are to document the valley and seal it. Whatever's there gets recorded for the archive and buried for everyone else. Forty-eight-hour window, in and out, and the route closes behind us." A muscle worked in his jaw. "He showed me photographs. Of the site. Before I signed."

"So you knew."

"I suspected. Now I know the shape of it." He finally looked at me fully, and there was something raw in his face that the wry diver's mask usually covered. "Ines — if I tell you what I know, you can't unknow it. And if Ashe finds out I told you, it stops being his problem and becomes ours. Both of ours. There's a man aboard, Kovac, who peeked into that chest at the salvage dock. He got reassigned within a day. Reassigned to what, nobody will say."

"Then we should be careful," I said. "I've been careful my whole life. It's boring."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first honest thing either of us had produced all night. "Your grandfather," he said slowly, "the third letter. Does it mention a ledger? A valley ledger?"

The cold walked up my spine. "How do you know that word?"

"Because I've seen the chest's inventory manifest. Navy paper, decades old, filed under ordnance recovery." He set the mug down on the rail with a soft click. "One item listed. 'Valley ledger, sealed.' And Ines — the item number on that manifest matches the serial stenciled on the chest I raised. Which means whatever your grandfather was trying to warn your grandmother about—"

"—is sitting in a naval strongroom right now," I finished, "or locked in the hold of this boat, twenty feet below where we're standing."

He nodded once. Above us, a door opened on the bridge — footsteps, a lantern beam sweeping the deck rail, pausing. We both went quiet, two silhouettes leaning on a rail, doing nothing suspicious, saying nothing at all. The light moved on. The door closed.

When it was safe, Petra spoke again, barely above the river. "Tomorrow we make the last portage before the glacier trail. After that, there's no turning around — the calving season closes the lower route whether we've finished or not." He glanced at me sideways. "Whatever you're planning to do with those letters, decide fast. And Ines — keep them somewhere Kovac's replacement can't find them."

"They're memorized," I said. "Every number. My grandmother made me recite ciphers as a child. She said a girl should always know at least one language the men in charge can't read."

This time he did smile, brief and real, and it did something to my chest that I firmly scheduled for later examination. Then his face sobered, and he nodded toward the dark water sliding past, toward the valley waiting somewhere upriver in the ice.

"One more thing," he said. "The night watch changed rotation today. There's a man aboard now who wasn't on the original roster — new face, navy issue, introduced as a hydrographer. He doesn't take soundings. I've watched him for two days." Petra picked his mug back up, casual as anything, a man discussing nothing. "He stands outside the chart room whenever you're in it."

I looked at the dark windows of the chart room, where my maps lay spread across the table, and felt the expedition close around us like a hand.

"Then tomorrow," I said, "we both start lying beautifully."
