## 6. The Valley Before the Ice

The glacier announced itself the way bad news does — a sound you feel in your teeth before you understand it. A crack like a rifle shot rolled down the valley at dawn, and I lay in my bedroll doing arithmetic: forty-eight hours of open trail, maybe less if the calving came early, and eleven miles of ice-scoured ground between us and whatever the navy had sent me up here to bury.

"Route's open," I told Ashe, because that was the job. Guide the expedition. Say the words. Keep my mouth shut about everything else.

He was already dressed, which told me he hadn't slept either. Commander Ashe had the kind of face that looked rested even when it wasn't, a face built for tribunals and press photographs. "Then we move," he said. "Full kit. Nobody straggles."

Nobody straggled. For once the man's paranoia was good seamanship.

* * *

The trail along the glacier was the kind of thing you don't forget and can't describe without lying. The ice wall stood two hundred feet on our left, blue in its depths like something drowned, and every hour or so it shed a chunk of itself into the meltwater with a boom you felt through your boots. On our right the valley climbed away in terraces of scree and old moraine, and somewhere up there, according to the charts Ines wouldn't show anyone but me, sat the settlement.

The drowned nation's last town.

I'd been running rivers for twenty years. I'd measured my life in miles — miles dredged, miles run, miles between me and every bad decision I'd ever made. But walking that trail with the ice groaning overhead like a ship's hull under pressure, I understood we were measuring something else now. Hours. And hours are just consequences with better posture.

Ines walked beside me for a stretch, her survey case slung across her back, her eyes doing what they always did — reading the ground like a page she didn't trust.

"You've been here before," she said. Not a question.

"I've never been here."

"But you knew about this valley." She kept her voice level, conversational, the tone of a woman discussing rations. "When Ashe recruited you. You knew what was up here."

I watched a serac lean farther out over the meltwater and decided not to think about it. "I knew there was a route. That's what they hired me for. Routes."

"Petra." She said my name the way she said everything — precisely, like placing a mark on a chart. "My grandfather sailed on that steamer. I'm going to find out what happened to him whether you help me or not. But I'd rather you helped me."

Ahead of us, one of Ashe's men — Kessler, the quiet one with the camera case he never opened near the rest of us — turned and looked back. Just looked. Then faced forward again.

"Not here," I murmured.

She followed my glance, understood, and said nothing more. That was the trouble with Ines Varga. She never pushed when pushing would cost her. She just waited, patient as water, for the ground to give way.

* * *

We reached the settlement at midafternoon, and I will tell you honestly that I stopped walking without deciding to.

It sat in a hanging valley above the glacier's shoulder, a scatter of stone houses with their roofs long collapsed, a chapel with its bell still hanging, a well with its sweep-arm frozen mid-tilt like a clock stopped at some particular minute. Forty years of winters had been gentle to it. The dry cold had preserved what rot would have eaten elsewhere — door frames, fence rails, a child's sled leaning against a wall as though its owner had only stepped inside for supper.

Nobody spoke. Even Ashe's men went quiet, and those men could stand watch in silence for six hours without noticing.

"It wasn't burned," Ines said softly. She'd moved ahead of all of us, her professional caution gone, her cartographer's hands hovering over a doorpost where someone had carved a row of small marks — heights, I realized. A parent measuring a growing child, year by year. "History says the valley was evacuated. That they left."

"They left," Ashe said behind us. "That's the record. Let's keep it that way until we know otherwise."

But we already knew otherwise, because Kessler had found the first body an hour later, and then we found the rest.

They were in the long barn above the village, and the ice had come for them the way ice comes for everything — slowly, thoroughly, without malice. A rockslide in the winter after the massacre, Ines guessed, or an avalanche off the upper slopes; the barn's roof had collapsed and the snow had packed in and never fully melted, cycling freeze and thaw until the whole interior set like a cellar of grey glass. And in it, preserved the way glaciers preserve things — the men, women, and children of the valley, perhaps sixty of them, huddled together in the dark the way people huddle when they've been driven somewhere and told to stay.

Some of them wore uniforms. Not the uniforms of the drowned republic. Ours.

I've seen drowned men pulled from rivers. I've seen what thirty years of water does, and I thought I was past flinching. This was different. Water takes things apart. Ice keeps them. There was a woman with a wedding band still bright on her finger, and a boy — I made myself look, I made myself count him as real — a boy of maybe ten holding a wooden horse, and the horse's paint was still red.

"Well," said Commander Ashe, into the silence. His voice had gone flat and official, a drawer closing. "Document it."

Kessler raised his camera. Ines stood with her hand pressed flat against the barn's frozen doorway, and I saw her lips moving — counting, or praying, or both.

* * *

Ashe worked fast after that, and I'll give him credit for competence. He always did his worst work competently.

By nightfall the site had been photographed from every angle, sketched, sampled, logged into a ledger that would go into a naval safe and never come out. He directed it all from the village square with his collar up against the cold, precise as a man defusing a mine. Which, in his way, he was.

"The calving season closes this route in two days," he told us around the cook fire. "After that, this valley is sealed by geography itself. No road. No river access above the falls. Nothing comes in or out except ice." He let that sit. "Our report will state that the settlement shows evidence of abandonment consistent with the historical record. Any findings suggesting otherwise are preliminary, unverified, and compromised by site degradation. Is that understood?"

Nobody answered, which he took for agreement, because powerful men always do.

I caught Ines's eye across the fire. She looked away first. Good, I thought. Don't fight him here. And then I hated myself for thinking it, because what I meant was: *don't fight him yet.*

Later, when the camp had gone down to watch-fires and breathing, I walked back up to the barn alone. I don't know why. I do know why. I wanted to see if it would be less terrible without the others standing there making it official.

It wasn't.

I stood in the doorway with the moon coming off the ice field and lighting the barn's interior a faint corpse-grey, and I thought about the chest sitting in the expedition hold below — lead-sealed, crest-stamped, carried upriver on my boat under my name. I thought about the crewman who'd peeked into it at the second portage and how he'd been reassigned before breakfast, and how *reassigned* was such a clean word for what had actually happened to him. I thought about a nation that no longer existed, whose only remaining citizens were lying in a barn under forty years of ice, waiting for someone to say their names.

And I thought about Ines's grandfather, who was not among them. She'd have checked the faces by now. I'd seen her checking. He died on the steamer, then — or worse, he lived long enough to know what the steamer was carrying, and what it was carrying was the proof of this.

A shape moved at the edge of my vision. Ines, wrapped in her coat, standing ten yards off in the moonlight, watching me watch the dead.

"They were our uniforms," she said quietly. "Petra. Some of them were wearing *our* uniforms."

"I know."

"Do you?" She came closer, and her breath hung white between us. "Because you signed his contract, and you guide his route, and tomorrow you'll walk out of this valley and let him seal it behind us like a grave he gets to write the epitaph for. So I'm asking you plainly. When we get back down — whose side are you on?"

Out on the glacier, something calved. The boom rolled up the valley and broke against the peaks and came back smaller, the way echoes do, the way truths do when governments get hold of them.

I looked at the barn full of the dead, and then at the living woman in front of me, and I heard myself say the first honest sentence I'd spoken since the naval office.

"Ask me again tomorrow night," I said. "And bring your notebook."
