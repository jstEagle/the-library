## 7. Broken Compass

My grandfather used to say that a map is a promise you make to strangers. Draw the river true, he'd say, and you've told every soul who follows you: *you will not drown here, not if I can help it.* He was a sailor, and then he was a name on a naval ledger, and then — for fifty years — he was nothing at all. A line in a column marked *lost with all hands.* The navy had kept his promise for him, and their version read: *you will drown here, and no one will ask why.*

I had spent three nights learning why.

The valley settlement sat under the glacier's shoulder like a thing the ice had swallowed and politely declined to digest. Ashe's survey team had documented it with the thoroughness of men building a coffin — measurements, photographs, samples, all of it logged, sealed in oilcloth, and stacked in the strongbox that traveled everywhere with two armed escorts. Officially, we were mapping an abandoned settlement of archaeological interest. Unofficially, I had watched Commander Ashe stand in front of a wall of ice that held, frozen mid-flight, the last hour of four hundred people, and order it covered with canvas before the enlisted men could look too long.

Four hundred people who had run from soldiers wearing their own nation's colors.

The letters had told me the shape of it. My grandfather's hand, cramped and salt-stained, describing crates loaded at night, orders given in whispers, a steamer turned around mid-river by gunboats flying the flag of the republic that no longer existed — the flag the chest bore, the flag the navy had spent decades teaching schoolchildren to associate with piracy and plague. They hadn't been carrying refugees. They'd been carrying witnesses. Depositions, photographs, muster rolls of the massacred valley below us right now, preserved in ice like flies in amber. And the navy had sunk them for it, and then built a career, a doctrine, a *nation's memory* on top of the wreck.

So I copied everything.

* * *

It was slow, careful work, done in the hours when the camp slept and the glacier groaned like something dreaming badly. I couldn't take the originals out of the strongbox — but Ashe, in his efficiency, had ordered duplicate field notes made, and duplicates passed through the cartographer's tent for inclusion in the survey maps. Nobody suspects the mapmaker. I was furniture. I was a woman with a steady hand and ink-stained fingers, and I used both to transcribe deposition after deposition into the margins of my own working charts, in a cipher of my own devising disguised as elevation notations. To anyone flipping through my folio, it was contour lines and soundings. To me, it was four hundred names climbing out of the river at last.

The photographs were harder. The expedition's camera was inventoried; every plate accounted for. But I'd brought my own — a small folding Kodak, officially for "reference shots of terrain," unofficially because I had never trusted an expedition I hadn't packed for myself. Each night I took it into the ice field where the evidence lay under its insulting canvas, and I photographed what the navy wanted buried twice over: once by water, once by tarpaulin.

On the fourth night, the canvas was already pulled back when I got there.

I froze, one hand on the ice axe I'd started carrying more out of habit than sense. Moonlight came off the glacier in a hard white sheet, and standing in the middle of it, holding the edge of the tarpaulin like a man deciding whether to cover a body or uncover one, was Petra Halloran.

He didn't turn around. "You walk loud for a cartographer," he said.

"I walk exactly as loud as I intend to."

That earned me half a smile, there and gone. He let the canvas drop and faced me, and I saw he wasn't carrying his rifle. That meant something. On this expedition, an unarmed man at midnight was either a fool or a decision, and Petra Halloran had never struck me as a fool.

"How long have you known?" I asked.

"Since the chest." He said it simply, the way a man admits to a scar. "The seal. I knew what it was the second the mud came off it. I've been knowing things ever since, and hating every one of them."

"You signed Ashe's paper."

"I signed Ashe's paper." He looked past me, up the glacier, to where the seracs stood like a jury that hadn't finished deliberating. "I told myself it was just another paid run. You tell yourself things, Ines. It's how a man gets through a life he's not proud of."

"And now?"

"Now I watch a woman sneak out of camp every night with a camera smaller than her fist, photographing things that could hang a fleet admiral, and I ask myself what I'm going to do about it." His eyes came back to mine, and they were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "And I find I don't want to do anything about it except hold the light."

I hadn't realized I'd stopped breathing until he crouched and picked up the lantern I'd set down, unlit, and struck it alight himself. He raised it so the glow fell across the ice — across the dark shapes pressed into it, the bundled records, the bundle of uniforms folded with terrible neatness by men who had expected to answer for them someday and never got the chance.

"Hold it steady," I said, and took out the Kodak.

We worked together in silence after that, and the silence was the strangest part — not hostile, not awkward, but *conspiratorial*, warm as a shared coat. He angled the lantern. I shot the plates. When a wind came down off the ice and threatened the canvas, he weighted it with stones rather than letting it blow away and draw attention. When my hands shook — they shook, once, over the muster roll with my grandfather's name in it, written in his own living hand — Petra Halloran reached over and steadied the frame without a word, and I understood that whatever line he'd been standing behind his whole careful salvage-diver's life, he had just stepped over it, boots and all, for me.

For me, or for the truth. By then I honestly couldn't tell the difference, and I don't think he could either.

"There's a watcher," he said quietly, as we packed the last plates. "Corporal Reyes. Ashe posted him on the supply tents three nights ago. He rotates past the ice field on the hour."

"Then we have—"

"Less than that. He cuts the corner early when it's cold." Petra took the Kodak from my hands and slid it inside his coat, against his chest, where the cold wouldn't crack the plates. "Give me your folio. The cipher charts."

"If Ashe searches my tent—"

"He won't search yours first. He'll search mine, because I'm the hired muscle and hired muscle is always the first suspect." He held out his hand. "Which means they need to be somewhere he'll never think to look."

"Where?"

He almost smiled again. "In the one place on this river nobody would ever hide anything worth finding." He tapped the deck of my chart case. "Inside the official survey. Your maps go to naval headquarters regardless of what happens to any of us. Contour lines, Ines. Nobody reads contour lines."

I stared at him. "You're hiding the massacre inside the navy's own paperwork."

"I'm hiding it where it'll outlive us." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse — or better. "I've spent twenty years pulling dead men's property out of rivers and handing it to whoever paid. This is the first time I've ever put something *back*."

* * *

We made it thirty steps toward camp.

Reyes came around the glacier's shoulder with his collar up and his rifle slung, and the moonlight did what moonlight does — it made everything honest. He saw us. We saw him see us. There was a long, crystalline moment in which three people stood on ancient ice and each of us understood exactly how much trouble we were in, and then Reyes did the arithmetic aloud, in the flat voice of a man reporting weather:

"Commander said anyone near the site after hours gets reported."

Petra didn't move. Didn't reach for a weapon he wasn't carrying. He just looked at the corporal the way he must have looked at wrecks from the deck of a salvage barge — measuring, patient, certain of what lay underneath.

"She's taking terrain fixes," Petra said. "Night shots for stellar navigation. I'm escorting her. Ask the commander if you like — in the morning, in writing, through me."

Reyes's eyes went to me, then to the bulge of the camera inside Petra's coat, which was doing a poor job of being invisible. He was young. That was the worst of it. He had a young man's face, still soft, still capable of being surprised by the world, and I watched him weigh a career against a suspicion, and I watched the career start to win.

"I'll report what I saw," he said finally. "Just what I saw."

"Just what you saw," Petra agreed pleasantly. "A cartographer and her escort, walking back from a navigation fix. Put that in your log, Corporal. Word for word."

Reyes hesitated — and in that hesitation I saw the crack, the hairline fracture in the navy's perfect silence, the same crack I'd been widening all week with pen and film. Then he touched his cap and walked on toward the supply tents, and neither of us breathed properly until his footsteps faded.

"That buys us a day," Petra said. "Maybe two. After that, Ashe starts wondering why his corporal's report sounds rehearsed."

"Then we have a day," I said. "Maybe two."

He looked at me then, really looked, and under all that river-worn caution I saw something kindle that I suspected had been waiting years for permission. "You're not frightened."

"Oh, I'm frightened." I tucked my arm through his, partly for warmth, partly because I was done pretending I didn't want to. "But my grandfather spent his last hours carrying the truth down a river toward people who needed it, and the navy sank him for it. I can carry it the rest of the way. It's the family business, apparently."

We walked back toward camp along the glacier's edge, two small figures under an enormous indifferent sky, and behind us the ice held its dead and my photographs held the proof and Petra's coat held the camera, and ahead of us the tents of the expedition glowed with lamplight — Ashe's lamplight, Ashe's order, Ashe's version of events, all of it soon to be worthless.

At the edge of the firelight Petra stopped and turned to me, and his voice dropped to that diver's murmur he used when the water was deep and the margin for error was thin.

"When we run the river home," he said — *when*, not *if*, and I loved him a little for the grammar — "Ashe will try to stop us. So before that day comes, you and I are going to need more friends on this crew than he has."

I looked past his shoulder, at the enlisted men warming their hands at the fire, young faces like Corporal Reyes's, men who had stood that afternoon in front of the canvas-covered ice and felt something move in them that orders couldn't quite reach.

"Then tomorrow," I said, "I start drawing them a map worth following."

Inside the command tent, a lamp flared, and Commander Ashe's shadow rose long and sharp across the canvas — and stayed there, unmoving, far longer than a sleeping man's would.
