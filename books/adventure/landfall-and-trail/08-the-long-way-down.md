## 8. The Long Way Down

I have run rivers that tried to kill me. I have never before run one while a man with a sidearm and a grudge stood on my deck deciding whether I was cargo or crew.

It started, as most disasters do, with someone being polite.

Ashe came aboard the *Marrow* at first light, boots polished, face arranged into something he probably believed was friendly. Behind him stood Lieutenant Corrigan — the watcher, the one who'd seen Ines and me on the ice with her camera — holding a canvas satchel like it was a communion plate.

"Miss Varga's services are no longer required," Ashe said. "There's been a change in mission scope."

"Is that so."

"She'll be returning downriver on the launch. Her effects will be searched as a matter of routine." He said *effects* the way other men say *contraband*, and I understood in one cold drop of the stomach that they knew. Not suspected. Knew. The photographs, the copied pages, all of it sitting in the false bottom of her chart case not six feet from where Corrigan was standing.

Ines came up from below on cue — we'd rehearsed this, God help us, we'd actually rehearsed it — wearing the blank professional calm of a woman who has never once thought about her grandfather or massacres or anything but contour lines.

"Routine," she repeated.

"The navy is very fond of routine," Ashe said. "It keeps men honest."

I looked at the river. The river looked back, gray-green and patient, and for once it seemed to be offering advice: *you've got maybe four minutes of this conversation left, Halloran, spend them well.*

* * *

Here is what I had going for me: forty-eight hours early, the calving season had opened the lower trail wider than anyone expected, and the meltwater was running high enough to make the Serpent's Teeth — the untested rapids below the valley, the ones every pilot's manual marked with a little skull-and-crossbones flourish the cartographers probably enjoyed drawing — technically passable. Technically. The way a coffin lid is technically a door.

And here is what I had going against me: eleven souls aboard, two of them Ashe's men, one of them a woman whose whole life fit inside a chart case, and a navy launch moored thirty yards downstream with an engine already warm.

So when Ashe said, "Halloran, you'll stand aside while we conduct the search," I did what any reasonable man would do.

I dropped the anchor winch lever with my hip.

The clang was enormous. Every head turned — sailors are superstitious about sudden noise, and even navy men flinch when a boat complains — and in that half-second of collective attention I said, loud and flat, "Ice groan. Off the south wall. Everyone topside, now, look at it."

They looked. Of course they looked. You don't survive upriver by ignoring the ice when it talks. And in the shuffle of bodies, Ines slipped past Corrigan like smoke through a keyhole and went over the starboard rail into the shallows, chart case strapped tight, wading toward the tree line where our spare dory sat cached under brush since Tuesday.

By the time Corrigan figured out that the woman he'd been sent to detain was ankle-deep and accelerating, I'd thrown off the mooring lines and put the *Marrow*'s nose into the current.

"Halloran!" Ashe's voice cracked across the water like a whip. "You are stealing naval property!"

"No, Commander," I called back, hands full of wheel, heart full of something that wasn't fear exactly and wasn't courage either, some third thing made of both. "You are. I'm just taking it home."

The launch engine roared behind us. Ahead of us lay sixty miles of river that no survey had ever blessed, and the Serpent's Teeth, and whatever the water decided we deserved.

* * *

Running rapids you've never run is a peculiar kind of prayer. You read the water the way Ines reads maps — the smooth green tongues where the channel runs deep, the white fangs where rock waits just under the surface pretending to be foam — and then you commit, because commitment is the only instrument you have. Hesitation kills faster than rocks do. Rocks at least are honest.

We took the first set of teeth sideways and lived. The second set nearly folded us against a midstream boulder the size of a chapel, and I heard the hull groan along its whole length like an old man getting out of bed, and then we were through, spinning, shipping water over the rails, and the crew was bailing and laughing in that high unhinged way people laugh when they've just found out they're still alive.

The launch didn't follow into the Teeth. I saw it holding above the drop, circling, and I knew what that meant: they'd portage, or they'd radio ahead, or both. We hadn't escaped. We'd bought time, and time on a river is spent fast.

Which is when Okafor said, "Petra. Where's Brandt?"

I counted heads. Ten. Eleven had gone in.

Brandt was one of Ashe's men — big, quiet, competent, the kind of sailor who fixed things without being asked and asked nothing back. He'd been loyal to the commander the way a dog is loyal to whoever feeds it, which is to say completely and without philosophy. He must have gone overboard in the second set. In all that chaos, in all that spray, nobody had seen him go.

We ran the boat into an eddy and searched until dark. I won't write down everything we found and didn't find. I'll say only that the river gives things back on its own schedule, and it gave Brandt back near dusk, downstream among the willow roots, and that I was the one who pulled him out because I was closest and because some debts you pay in person.

He was twenty-six years old. He had a photograph of a girl in his breast pocket, water-swollen, her face gone soft as a memory. I sat on the bank holding it and understood that I had killed him — not with malice, not with intent, but with a lever dropped by my hip and a lie shouted at the right moment. Ashe's men had fired no shots. Nobody had touched anybody. And still there was a body cooling beside my river, and my name on it somewhere, written in a hand I couldn't read yet.

Ines crouched beside me. She didn't say *it wasn't your fault*, because she was too honest for that and loved me too much to lie. What she said was: "He chose to be on that boat. So did you. So did I."

"It doesn't feel like choosing," I said. "It feels like arithmetic. One man dead, one truth alive. I keep trying to make the sum come out clean and it won't."

"Nothing comes out clean," she said. "It comes out true, or it doesn't. Clean is for people who never get wet."

I put the photograph back in Brandt's pocket, buttoned the coat over it, and let the willows have him until morning, because the ground upriver was iron and the river itself was the only grave that would take him tonight.

* * *

We buried him at dawn above the flood line, under a cairn we built from river stones, and I spoke the few words the service requires and a few more it doesn't. Then I stood in front of the crew — ten of us now, soaked, exhausted, half of them looking at me like a captain and half like a criminal, which was fair, since I was both — and told them the truth.

All of it. The chest. The seal. The massacre the drowned republic never sanctioned and the navy that sank the steamer to bury the record of it. The photographs in Ines's chart case. The reason Ashe wanted her detained and the reason I'd run the Teeth instead of letting it happen.

Silence, after. The long kind.

Then old Maren, who'd been piloting these waters since before I was born, spat over the rail and said, "About time somebody said it out loud. My father worked the steamer route. He always said the official story stank of paint over rot."

One by one, the others nodded, or shrugged, or simply went back to their stations, which in sailor language is a signature on the dotted line. Even the two men who'd started the voyage under Ashe stayed. Maybe they'd done their own arithmetic overnight. Maybe they'd looked at Brandt's cairn and decided loyalty belonged to the dead man's truth instead of the living commander's convenience.

"We can't outrun them forever," Ines said quietly, beside me at the wheel. "The launch will be waiting at the mouth. They'll have officers, lawyers, guns."

"I know."

"So what's the plan, Captain?"

I watched the river braid itself around the bars of gravel ahead, silver and green and utterly indifferent to navies, and I felt something settle in me that had been loose since the day the dredge brought up that lead-sealed chest. Fear, probably. Or its opposite. With me they've always been hard to tell apart.

"They want the chest," I said. "Fine. Let them have it — in front of everyone. Journalists meet the mail steamer at the mouth every Thursday; the *Corvane* docks at noon. We arrive at noon. And when the officers come aboard to seize their precious evidence, I'm going to open it myself, on my own deck, and read what's inside out loud until every ear on that dock has heard it. You can't confiscate a thing the whole world has already heard, Ines. That's the one lock no navy ever picked."

She looked at me for a long moment, and there was grief in her face for Brandt and for her grandfather and for the girl she'd been before the letters, and underneath all of it, steady as bedrock, something that looked a great deal like hope.

"And if they shoot you first?" she asked.

"Then you read," I said. "You're better with words anyway. I just measure miles."

The *Marrow* took the current, and the last bend of the river opened ahead of us, and far off, hazy in the morning light, I could see the masts of the mail steamer at the mouth — and the gray shape of a navy launch riding at anchor beside her, patient as a verdict.

Noon was coming. So were we.
