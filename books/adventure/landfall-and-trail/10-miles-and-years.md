## 10. Miles and Years

They gave me a chair. That was the first surprise of the tribunal — a real chair, oak, with arms, positioned in the center of a room that smelled like floor wax and institutional dread. I'd expected a stool in a cell. Instead they sat me down across from three officers in dress whites and a civilian assessor who kept looking at me like I was a horse he was deciding whether to buy or shoot.

Commander Ashe sat to one side, immaculate, hands folded, wearing the expression of a man who had rehearsed his disappointment in a mirror.

The charges were read aloud in a voice like a train schedule: theft of naval property, breach of contract, obstruction of an official survey, aiding and abetting unauthorized disclosure, and — my personal favorite — "conduct unbecoming a civilian contractor." I hadn't known civilians could be unbecoming. Apparently we could. It's in a regulation somewhere.

I answered their questions honestly, which is not something I'd recommend as a general strategy but which felt correct that week. Yes, I opened the chest. Yes, I read from it. No, I did not regret it. The assessor asked me if I understood the gravity of what I'd done, and I told him I understood the gravity of what the navy had done considerably better, and that the chest had been doing the understanding for sixty years.

Ashe testified for two hours. He used words like *context*, *operational necessity*, and *the fog of history*. He said the word *stability* eleven times. I counted. When you're sitting in a chair waiting to learn how many years of your life someone else is about to spend for you, you count things.

But the room had already been decided before anyone entered it. Ines had seen to that.

* * *

Here is what happened while I sat in custody drinking coffee that tasted like punishment: Ines Varga took the copies she'd made in the ice valley — the photographs, the transcriptions, her grandfather's letters decoded line by line — and she did not leak them. Leaking, she said, would have let them bury it again under six months of denials and a reorganized press office. Instead she walked into the offices of three newspapers on the same morning, laid everything out on tables, and answered questions until midnight. She brought surviving crew members as witnesses. She brought the manifest. She brought the names — all forty-one of them, including a sailor named Tomas Varga whose last letter home described, in a boyish hand, cargo he'd been ordered never to mention.

Her grandfather had been twenty-three years old. He'd written his mother that the river was beautiful and that he'd learned to make a kind of bread from the ship's cook. Two pages later he wrote about what he'd seen from the rail at a place called Kessel Bend, and after that the letters stopped.

She stood in front of the tribunal too, though technically she wasn't charged with anything. They couldn't figure out what to charge her with. Unauthorized cartography wasn't a crime yet, though I suspect someone drafted legislation that afternoon.

When public outrage arrived, it arrived the way the calving season arrives — first a distant cracking, then the whole face of the thing coming off at once. Editorials. Questions in the assembly. Old women laying flowers at the harbor rail where the steamer had docked. A nation that had spent sixty years telling itself a tidy story discovered the story had forty-one bodies underneath it, and no amount of naval varnish would cover the smell.

So the tribunal declined to prosecute. The chairman announced it with the enthusiasm of a man prescribing a suppository. National interest, he said. Extraordinary circumstances, he said. The public record having been, ah, materially altered by recent disclosures, prosecution would serve no constructive purpose.

Then he took my salvage license anyway.

That was the trade, and everyone in the room knew it. Truth gets out; Petra Halloran doesn't dive again. They didn't say it plainly — governments rarely do — but the assessor's report used the phrase "unsuited to further certification," which is bureaucratic for *we can't hang you so we'll take your lungs*. Sixteen years of reading rivers with my hands, and it ended in a sentence in a report nobody would read.

Ines was furious on my behalf. I wasn't, particularly. A man who guides covert expeditions upriver to bury massacres shouldn't really complain when the profession closes its doors to him. There are other ways to earn a living near water. You can always smell it, even inland.

* * *

We published the book in the spring.

*Landfall and Trail*, we called it — her title, because she said every map begins with a landing and ends with a road, and everything true happens in between. We wrote it in a rented house above the estuary with a roof that leaked directly over my side of the bed, which Ines claimed was character-building. She wrote the chapters about the letters and the ice. I wrote the ones about the river. Ashe got a chapter too, though we were careful with the facts and let his own testimony damn him; he'd retired quietly two months after the tribunal, reassigned to some desk job involving logistics, which is where navies put men who have become embarrassing.

The crew came to the launch party. Most of them. Radek came, which cost him something — he'd lost his brother-in-law to the river on the run downstream, the man loyal to Ashe who drowned in the untested rapids, and for a long time Radek couldn't look at me without both of us flinching. But he came, and he shook my hand, and he said the river keeps what it keeps and gives back what it gives back, and neither of us gets to audit the ledger. I've thought about that sentence most days since.

The book sold better than either of us expected. Not spectacularly — books about drowned nations don't make anyone rich — but enough. Enough that the valley settlement was declared a memorial site. Enough that the forty-one names went up on stone at Kessel Bend, cut deep so the ice couldn't take them. Enough that Ines's grandfather got a paragraph in the official histories instead of a footnote saying *lost*.

Lost, it turns out, is just a word people use when they haven't looked hard enough.

* * *

It took me a year to stop measuring things in miles.

You don't notice you're doing it until it stops. For sixteen years my life had been distances — the run from the wreck field to the harbor, the portage at Devil's Elbow, the depth of the channel below the falls, how far upstream before the ice. Miles were how I kept the world at a manageable size. A mile has no feelings. A mile doesn't ask you what you saw at Kessel Bend or whether you'd do it again.

But standing on the riverbank where the dredge had first brought up that lead-sealed chest — Ines beside me, the water running brown and patient the way it always does in October — I caught myself counting something else. Not distance. Duration. Three years since the tribunal. Two winters in the leaking house. One book. Four hundred and eleven mornings of watching her draw maps at the kitchen table, tongue between her teeth, redrawing the upper river from memory because the official charts still lied.

Years. That's the unit now. Years, and seasons, and the slow accumulation of ordinary days that don't require anyone's silence.

The navy sent a letter last month. Official stationery. They wanted to know if I'd consider consulting on a new hydrographic survey — the river changing course, they said, new channels forming where the glacier retreats. My knowledge of the upper reaches would be, and I quote, *invaluable*.

Ines read it over my shoulder and laughed until she had to sit down on the porch step.

I haven't answered yet. The letter sits on the kitchen table under a mug, next to her ink bottles and my tide tables, and some evenings I pick it up and some evenings I don't. Part of me wants to write back a single word. Part of me wants to frame it. And part of me — the part that still dreams about green water going black over a wreck site, the part that will always hear the river calling measurements in its sleep — wonders what the new channels look like, whether the valley route has widened, whether the ice is giving back more than it takes these days.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about peace: it isn't the absence of the river. It's learning to stand on the bank instead of in it, and finding that the view is good, and the company is better, and the water keeps moving whether you're in it or not.

Ines came out just now with two cups of coffee and stood beside me at the rail, shoulder against mine, looking at the same brown water I've been looking at all my life.

"You're going to answer them," she said. Not a question. She reads me the way she reads contour lines.

"I might," I said.

"Petra."

"Ines."

She handed me the coffee. Steam rose off it into the cold air, and downstream the gulls were working the shallows, and the light came off the river the way it does in late autumn — gold and low and honest.

"Whatever you decide," she said, "we decide it together. That was the deal."

"That was the deal," I agreed.

And I looked at the letter, and I looked at her, and I realized I already knew which way the current ran. Some rivers you run for money. Some you run for silence. And once — only once — you run one for the truth, and it costs you everything, and it turns out everything was mostly ballast anyway.

I set the letter down. Tomorrow, maybe, I'd write back. Or maybe I'd write something else entirely — a second book, about the new channels, about what the retreating ice uncovers, about a cartographer and a diver who finally had time.

The river went on measuring itself in miles. I had stopped.

I had years now.
