## 1. The Debt Collector's Compass

The thief left boot prints in the alkali crust like a man writing his own confession, and Hazel Sandoval followed them the way he did everything these days: patiently, resentfully, and because he had no other choice.

They were cheap boots, the prints said. Left heel worn to the nail. A man who walked a lot and paid for it out of his own pocket, which meant he wasn't consortium, wasn't cartel, wasn't anybody with money enough to matter. Just another desert rat gone desperate in the fourth year of the drought, which by the ledger of this country made him guilty of the only crime that still got prosecuted: being poor at the wrong time.

Hazel crouched and touched the print. The edges were still crisp. An hour old, maybe less.

"Come on, friend," he murmured. "Make it easy."

The flats stretched out ahead of him, white as bone under a sky the color of an old bandage. Heat shimmer stood up off the ground in slow columns, and somewhere out there a jackrabbit was having a better morning than either of them. Hazel straightened, worked the sweat-stiff collar of his shirt away from his neck, and started walking.

His badge rode against his chest on a lanyard under the shirt, where it always rode — not because he was ashamed of it, but because out here a ranger's tin was worth exactly what you could defend it for, and flashing it early only told people where to shoot. He'd learned that from Vasquez, back when Vasquez was alive and teaching instead of dead and remembered. *The badge is a tool, Sandoval. Not a personality.* Vasquez had a lot of sayings. Most of them were true. All of them had gotten cheaper since the funeral.

Two miles on, the boot prints bent toward a dry wash choked with mesquite, and Hazel allowed himself the small luxury of knowing how the morning would end. The wash funneled into a slot canyon half a mile long with one way in and one way out, and every desperate man in the territory had used it at least once, because desperation was not famous for making good maps. The thief would hole up in there, tell himself nobody could corner him in the narrows, and be wrong.

Hazel had been wrong about bigger things. He didn't judge.

He thought about the debt while he walked, because he thought about the debt most of the time, the way a man with a stone in his boot thinks about the stone. Nineteen thousand, four hundred and twelve dollars. He knew the number the way other men knew their own birthdays. It had been Eli's number first — Eli, who could talk a banker out of his shoes and a riverboat pilot out of his cargo, who'd borrowed against everything including, in the end, his life, though the loan officers hadn't required that collateral so much as accepted it gratefully when the fever took him. The lenders had come to the funeral. That was the part Hazel still couldn't get past. They'd stood at the graveside in their clean clothes with their hats in their hands, wearing faces like folded paper, and afterward one of them had shaken Hazel's hand and said, *We understand you're the executor,* and that word — executor — had sounded for a moment like something noble, some Roman office, before it resolved into what it actually was: a man who pays.

Four years now. He sent the money downriver on the first of every month, a third of a ranger's salary wrapped in brown paper and entrusted to a freight mule train that charged him a fee for the privilege of carrying it. What remained bought beans, lamp oil, ammunition, and once a year a new pair of boots, which was why his own left heel was worn to the nail too. He and the thief would have gotten along, under other circumstances. Under other circumstances they might have been the same man.

The badge kept him honest. That was the joke he didn't tell anyone: honesty was the most expensive thing he owned. A ranger couldn't take side work. Couldn't run cargo, couldn't look away from a smuggler's boat for a consideration, couldn't do any of the hundred small crooked things that turned survival into comfort out here. Every month the choice came again, plain as arithmetic: stay clean and broke, or get comfortable and dirty. And every month he stayed clean, partly from principle, mostly from stubbornness, and a little — he admitted this only on bad nights — because if he was going to spend his whole life paying for his brother, he wanted the paying to mean something. He wanted the ledger to balance to more than zero.

So far it balanced to zero.

* * *

He found the thief in the slot canyon, exactly as predicted, sitting with his back against the warm red rock and a stolen pocketwatch open in his palm like a little brass heart he'd cut out of somebody.

"Don't," the thief said, when Hazel's shadow fell across him. He didn't reach for the knife at his belt, which put him a rung above half the men Hazel arrested. "I heard you coming for a mile."

"You walk loud."

"I walk tired." The man looked up. Young, maybe twenty-five, sunburned to the color of the canyon walls, with the particular hollowed-out look of someone who hadn't eaten properly since the rains failed. "It's just a watch."

"It's somebody's watch." Hazel held out his hand. "That's the whole law, friend. Everything is somebody's."

The man looked at the watch a long moment — and here was the thing Hazel would remember later, though he wouldn't know why until much later, when remembering it would feel like reading a letter written to him across years: the thief looked at the watch the way a man looks at a thing he's already lost. No fight in it. He set it in Hazel's palm almost gently.

"There was a compass too," he said. "Brass one, heavy. I sold it already, day before yesterday, to a tinker down at Mile 40. Didn't know it was in the bag till I was clear." He shrugged, a small defeated motion. "Figured you'd want to know. You seem like the kind that wants the whole inventory."

Hazel blinked. In eleven years of chasing petty thieves across the flats, not one had ever volunteered the rest of the manifest. "You always confess the parts I haven't caught yet?"

"Only the parts that don't matter anymore." The man smiled without any humor in it. "The watch mattered. It was going to buy my mother's medicine for two months. The compass never mattered. Some surveyor's toy."

Something cold moved through Hazel then, faint as a fish under ice. "Surveyor?"

"The river crew. The ones camped up past Serpent's Neck doing their measuring." The thief squinted up at him. "Why? You looking for them?"

"No," Hazel said slowly. "But I'm about to be."

He cuffed the man — loosely; there was no need for anything else and both of them knew it — and walked him back out of the narrows into the blinding flat light, and neither of them spoke for a long time, because there was nothing to say that the country itself wasn't already saying. The drought said it. The empty river gauge at the last crossing said it. The burned homesteads along the lower bank, abandoned by families who'd sold their water rights for pennies to men in clean boots, said it loudest of all.

At the skiff tied up in the shallows — the thief's skiff, confiscated now, a flat-bottomed river boat with a cracked windshield and an engine that had probably been stolen twice — Hazel radioed it in. The dispatch desk at Fort Calloway crackled, popped, and gave him the superintendent's clerk, a woman named Reyes who had the flattest voice in three territories.

"Sandoval. Tell me you have the Mile 40 theft."

"Have him. Watch recovered. He also lifted a brass compass off the same party — already sold to a tinker. I can recover it."

"Skip the compass." Papers rustled through the static. "Sandoval, listen. The survey expedition. Okafor's team, the hydrographic outfit working the upper river past the Neck."

"What about them?"

Reyes paused, and Reyes never paused. Her pauses were rationed out to her by the bureau, and she spent them like coins.

"They've gone quiet," she said. "Nine days. Last scheduled transmission was the ninth, routine position report, and then nothing. Superintendent wants eyes on it."

"So send eyes. We've got six rangers on the roster."

"We've got three rangers not assigned to escort duty for the land-title assessors, one ranger on medical leave, and you." Another pause, shorter. "And the upper river's outside our jurisdiction anyway. This isn't official. There's no budget line for it. The superintendent wants someone to go up there, look around, and file a report that says whatever keeps the bureau from having to do anything expensive."

Hazel looked at the thief, who was watching him with the expression of a man following a tennis match played entirely over his head.

"And if I find something expensive?" Hazel asked.

"Then you'll want to describe it very carefully," Reyes said, "in your report."

The radio clicked off. Downstream, forty miles away, the freight mule train was carrying nineteen thousand, four hundred and twelve minus one month's payment, and upstream, past Serpent's Neck, nine days of silence sat on the water like a stone.

The thief cleared his throat. "Does this mean I'm walking home?"

"It means you're riding in your own boat," Hazel said, untying the line, "as far as Mile 40, where you're going to show me which tinker bought that compass. After that we'll see about the walking."

"That's not how arrests usually go."

"Nothing," Hazel said, pushing the skiff off the mud and swinging aboard, "is how it usually goes. That's the problem with this job. It's all holding pattern until suddenly it isn't."

He opened the throttle. The engine coughed twice, caught, and the skiff climbed up onto the plane, throwing spray that caught the light and fell apart into rain. Behind them the flats receded, white and patient, keeping all their secrets the way they always had. Ahead the river narrowed into the first low shoulders of the canyon country, and the current — thin, brown, exhausted after four rainless years — pulled them upstream toward whatever had stopped talking.

Hazel rested his hand on the throttle and found, to his surprise, that his heart was beating faster than the engine.

He'd been waiting four years for something to happen. He just hadn't expected it to sound like silence.
