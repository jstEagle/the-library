## 2. Orders Upstream

The superintendent's office smelled like old paper and older coffee, and Superintendent Ada Reyes sat behind a desk that had been new when the territory was, which is to say it was older than both of us and looked it.

"Sit down, Sandoval."

I sat. The chair wobbled on a bad leg, and I'd been sitting in that same wobbling chair for six years, long enough to know that when Reyes used my last name instead of my first, the conversation was going to cost me something.

She slid a folder across the desk. Thin. Ranger folders were never thin unless the department wanted them thin.

"What's this?"

"The Halvorsen survey." She said it flat, the way you'd read a weather report for a county nobody lived in. "Hydrological team contracted out of Corridor City. Six people. They went upriver eleven days ago to chart seasonal flows above the Kestrel Narrows."

"And?"

"And they've gone quiet. Last scheduled transmission was nine days ago. A routine position report, everything normal. Nothing since."

I turned the folder over. There wasn't much in it. A crew manifest with names I didn't recognize, a supply requisition stamped APPROVED by somebody's rubber stamp, a map of the upper river with a red pencil line tracing their intended route up into country where the red pencil line just sort of gave up, the way maps do when the mapmakers stop believing in the land.

"So send somebody after them," I said.

"I'm sending you."

"Reyes." I leaned back, and the chair creaked its complaint. "You're sending me. One ranger. Upriver. Into canyon country where the radio doesn't work past Serpent's Neck, to find six people who may or may not want finding, in a district I cover alone on a good week."

"You have a boat now."

"I confiscated a skiff from a man who stole it from a fisherman. It has a hairline crack in the hull and an outboard that smokes like a campfire serenade."

"It floats?"

"Barely."

"Then you're equipped." She folded her hands on the desk, and I saw her do the thing she did when she was about to hand me something rotten and call it bread: she looked me straight in the eye and didn't blink. "This isn't official, Hazel."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning there's no budget line for it. Meaning if you file mileage and per diem, accounting will bounce it. Meaning the department's position, if anyone asks, is that the Halvorsen contract is a private matter between the survey company and its insurers, and ranger resources are stretched too thin for—"

"For what? For looking for dead people?"

"For looking for anybody." Her voice didn't change, but something behind it did, some small pressure like water against a dam. "There are interests upstream, Hazel. Mining interests. Water interests. Men whose lawyers can tie this department up for a decade over a single unauthorized inquiry. If you go, you go as a private citizen conducting a personal errand. Your badge stays in your pocket unless you need it, and if you need it, you'd better be able to explain why in a room full of people paid to make you regret it."

I looked at her for a long moment. Six years I'd worked for Ada Reyes, and in six years she'd never once sent me anywhere without paperwork, without backup protocol, without at least the fiction of institutional cover. That was the whole architecture of the job — the badge, the forms, the chain of command. Strip those away and all you had was a man in a boat.

"Why me?" I asked.

"Because you know the river better than anyone on payroll. Because you're cheap." She almost smiled. Almost. "And because you're the only one who won't ask questions I can't answer."

"That's not true. I'm asking them right now."

"Yes, you are." She pushed the folder another inch toward me. "And I'm telling you I don't have answers. Nine days of silence from a six-person crew in country where silence usually means exactly what you think it means. Somebody has to go look. Nobody official can afford to care. So it's you, or it's nobody."

Outside the window, the flats shimmered in the late-morning heat, and somewhere out there was the thief I'd caught yesterday, sitting in a holding cell because he'd stolen a boat worth less than what it would cost to prosecute him, and somewhere beyond him was my brother's grave and the ledger of debts buried with it, and I thought: *this is what my life is. A holding pattern. Circles over the same ground, catching the same small criminals, paying the same interest on a debt I never signed for.*

"Who's the survey company?" I asked.

Reyes hesitated. That hesitation told me more than the folder did.

"Corridor Consolidated," she said. "Mining and development."

"Mining." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "A mining consortium hires a hydrology team to chart water flows, the team goes silent, and the department can't officially look for them because the mining consortium might get *uncomfortable*."

"Hazel."

"No, it's fine. It's fine. I understand completely." I picked up the folder. It weighed almost nothing. Six names, and it weighed almost nothing. "Unofficial errand. Personal capacity. Badge in my pocket. Anything else, or am I free to go drown in a river on my own time?"

"One more thing." She reached into her desk drawer and set something on the wood between us. A roll of bills, rubber-banded. Maybe two hundred dollars. Maybe less. "Expense fund. Don't itemize it."

I stared at it. Two hundred dollars wouldn't buy fuel for the trip, let alone food, let alone whatever a person buys when they expect trouble.

"This is coming out of your own pocket," I said.

"Don't itemize it," she repeated, and that was the end of the meeting.

* * *

The general store in Perdition Flats was run by a woman named Odile Fontaine who had forgotten more about provisioning river expeditions than I would ever know, and who charged accordingly.

"Upriver," she said, when I told her how far. "Past the Narrows?"

"Past the Narrows."

"How many days?"

"Ten, twelve, depending on current and luck."

"Luck." She wrote the word down on her pad as though pricing it. "You'll want hardtack, salt pork, coffee, rope, lamp oil, spare spark plugs, a tarp, and a prayer. The prayer's free. Everything else comes to two hundred and forty dollars."

I put Reyes's crumpled roll on the counter. Odile counted it with the speed of a woman who'd been counting money since before I was born, then looked up at me with an expression I'd seen before, usually on creditors.

"Forty short," she said.

"Put it on my account."

"Your account, Mr. Sandoval, currently stands at sixty-one dollars and eighty cents outstanding, which I have generously refrained from mentioning for four months."

"I appreciate the generosity."

"Do you?" She tapped the pad against her palm. "Here's what I'll do. You take the supplies. You leave the forty as a marker. And when you come back down that river — *if* you come back — you settle the whole account inside thirty days, or I sell your debt to the collection house in Corridor City, and you know what they're like."

I knew what they were like. I'd spent three years learning exactly what they were like, one payment at a time, on a debt that started at four thousand dollars and somehow never got smaller no matter how faithfully I fed it.

"Thirty days," I said.

"Twenty-five."

"Twenty-eight."

"Twenty-six, and I throw in the spark plugs." She stuck out a hand across the counter, dry and strong as a fence post, and we shook on it, and that was that. In Perdition Flats a handshake outweighed any contract, mostly because contracts required lawyers and lawyers required money and money was the one thing the Flats had never produced in quantity.

I loaded the supplies into a wheelbarrow and pushed it down to the river landing, sweating through my shirt, doing arithmetic I didn't like. Fuel for the skiff: thirty dollars, minimum, if the current cooperated. Food for two weeks: already mortgaged. Lamp oil, rope, the tarp: gone into Odile's total. What remained of my own cash, after the forty-dollar marker: eleven dollars and change.

Eleven dollars and change, and a debt that grew like kudzu, and a badge I couldn't officially use, going upriver after six strangers because nobody with money could be bothered to care whether they were alive.

My brother Danny would have loved it. That was the bitter joke of it. Danny Sandoval, who never met a risk he didn't propose to, who borrowed against everything he owned and half of what other people owned, who died with his boots off in a hospital bed in Corridor City leaving me a ledger instead of a eulogy — Danny would have called this an adventure. He'd have stood on the landing with his arms spread wide at all that brown water and said, *Haze, this is the kind of thing a man remembers.*

Danny remembered nothing now. I remembered everything. That was the difference between us, and it was the whole difference, and I hated him a little for it every single day, and I missed him more than I hated him, which was its own kind of debt.

* * *

The skiff sat at the landing where I'd tied it, riding low and looking apologetic. Eighteen feet of riveted aluminum, someone else's paint job flaking off in gray scales, the name *PILGRIM* stenciled on the bow in letters that had once been white. The thief — his name was Cordero, and he was twenty-two and scared and not very good at crime — had stolen it from a fisherman named Ansel, and Ansel had signed the confiscation papers with the resigned air of a man who knew he'd never see the boat again either way.

I checked the hull first. The crack I'd noted in my incident report ran along the port chine, hairline, weeping a slow bead of water when I pressed it. Epoxy would hold it for a while. Not forever. Nothing held forever; that was the first lesson of the river and the second lesson of debt.

While the epoxy cured I went through the boat, because a confiscated vessel is a confession written in objects, and I wanted to know what kind of man Cordero was besides a bad one.

Under the stern bench: a bedroll, damp, smelling of woodsmoke. A tin plate, a fork with two tines. A coil of fishing line and three hooks. Under the forward bench: a canvas satchel, and inside the satchel —

I stopped.

Photographs. A dozen of them, cheap prints, curled with damp. A young woman — the same young woman in most of them, laughing on a dock, squinting into sun, asleep against a rail. And a boy, maybe four years old, gap-toothed, holding up a fish nearly as big as he was.

On the back of one photograph, in pencil, in careful block letters: *MARA AND LUIS. WHEN I COME BACK WITH THE BOAT WE GO SOUTH.*

I sat in the thief's stolen boat holding the thief's whole life in my hands, and I thought about the cell back in town, and the charges I'd filed, and the fact that Cordero had run because a man with a sick family and no work will always run, and the fact that in about a year, if the math held, I'd be running too, from something, toward something, for reasons just as desperate and just as illegible from the outside.

I put the photographs back in the satchel and the satchel under the bench, and I didn't write any of it in my log.

Some things aren't evidence. Some things are just the world, being the world.

* * *

By late afternoon the epoxy had set and the fuel was aboard and the light on the water had gone the color of hammered brass. I cast off the lines, and the Pilgrim swung out into the current, and the outboard coughed twice, smoked like promised, and caught.

Perdition Falls dropped away behind me — the store, the jail, the superintendent's office with its wobbling chair and its two hundred dollars of secret mercy. Ahead the canyon walls rose up out of the evening shadow, closing in, narrowing the sky to a bright ribbon overhead. The river ran fast and brown-green and indifferent, carrying me north the way it carried everything, without opinion.

I stood at the tiller and let the cold air work at my face, and I made myself say it plainly, the way Reyes hadn't:

Six people were dead or dying or hiding, and I was the only one looking, and I was looking in an unofficial capacity with a cracked hull and eleven dollars, and if I found trouble up there, real trouble, the kind that wore suits and hired lawyers and left nine days of silence behind it — then there would be no department behind me, no backup, no paperwork trail leading home. Just a man in a boat, and a badge in his pocket getting heavier by the mile.

Fine.

I'd been broke before. I'd been alone before. I'd spent three years being both at once, and I was still here, still upright, still moving. Whatever was waiting upriver, it couldn't collect anything I hadn't already paid.

The first stars came out in the ribbon of sky. Somewhere ahead, around a bend I couldn't see yet, the canyon narrowed toward the Kestrel Narrows, and beyond them the country went empty and high and strange, and somewhere in all of that silence were six names in a thin folder.

I opened the throttle. The Pilgrim smoked and complained and held together, the way most things do, and we went upriver into the dark.

It was full night when I saw the fire.

A single point of orange light, high on the eastern rim, well above the flood line, well above anywhere a sane traveler would camp — burning steady in the black mouth of a side canyon, close enough to the survey route that it couldn't be coincidence, far enough from the water that it couldn't be comfort.

Nobody camps away from water in desert country. Nobody, unless they're hiding from whoever might come by the water.

I cut the engine and drifted, listening to the current talk against the hull, watching that small stubborn flame burn in the dark like a signal only meant for eyes that knew how to look.

Someone was alive up there.

And someone, I understood suddenly, coldly, completely, did not want to be found by just anyone.
