## 3. Water Where Nothing Grows

The canyon began on the third day, and the river changed its mind about being a river.

Hazel had grown up on water that behaved — the slow brown crawl of the lowland channels, wide enough to turn a barge, lazy enough to swim in as a boy before his brother taught him better things to do with his hands. This was different. Up here the water ran green-white and fast over shelves of black rock, talking constantly to itself in a language of pressure and stone, and the walls closed in on either side like a book deciding how much of its story you were allowed to read.

He poled the skiff through the morning shade, because the current was too strong for the little outboard to fight and too shallow in places for anything but a pole and patience. The thief's skiff. That still sat wrong with him. He'd confiscated it off a man named Denny Foss who'd stolen less than Hazel was now spending per day on fuel, and somewhere back at the last fuel dock he'd signed a chit that put him deeper into his own arithmetic of debt. His brother's debt had a number attached. This one didn't yet. Those were always the worst kind.

The radio had said nothing useful in two days. Static, mostly, and once — he couldn't be sure — something that might have been a voice, or might have been wind finding a crack in the canyon and whistling through it like breath across a bottle top. He'd called the survey expedition's frequency every six hours out of discipline rather than hope. *Survey Two, Survey Two, this is Ranger Sandoval, do you copy.* The silence answered with silence.

Superintendent Ochoa hadn't wanted to send anyone at all. That was the part that kept circling back to Hazel as he worked the pole, planted it, leaned, lifted, planted again. *Unofficial,* Ochoa had said, meaning unfunded, meaning unrecorded, meaning if Hazel vanished up this canyon the paperwork would read *personal leave.* A ranger missing upriver was a problem nobody downstream wanted to own. There were reasons canyons like this stayed empty.

He made camp that night on a gravel bar shaped like a half-moon, dragged the skiff above the waterline, and ate cold beans because a fire felt like an invitation. He told himself that was superstition. He built a small fire anyway, because cold beans after ten hours of poling is a punishment no one has earned, and he kept it small, cupped in a hollow of rock where its light would have to work hard to escape.

That was when he found the first sign.

Not the tent — the tent came later, and worse. This was smaller: a strip of surveyor's flagging tape, the fluorescent pink kind they used to mark instrument stations, snagged on a willow whip at the high-water line. It had been there long enough to fade from pink to the color of an old scar. Someone had tied it in a deliberate knot. Not torn, not blown — *tied*, a double hitch, the kind you tie when you want a marker to survive weather and time.

Surveyors marked their stations going forward. They didn't usually mark their trail coming back.

Unless they weren't coming back. Unless someone was leaving a trail for whoever followed.

Hazel crouched over the knot for a long time, longer than the knot deserved, while the river talked and the fire ticked down to orange coals. Then he untied it carefully and put it in his breast pocket, which was where he put things he wasn't ready to think about.

* * *

The fourth day the canyon narrowed until he could have thrown a stone at either wall, and the light only reached the water for two hours around noon. He passed a side gorge so deep and dark that the air coming out of it was cold, actual cold, in a country where cold was a rumor from other places. The river felt watched. He knew how that sounded. He'd spent six years learning to trust exactly this feeling — the prickle at the neck that arrived before evidence did — and six years learning that the feeling was right more often than the evidence was early.

Midday, he smelled smoke.

Old smoke, days old, soaked into stone the way rain soaks into adobe. He pulled the skiff against the current into an eddy behind a fallen slab and climbed. The smell led him up a talus slope to a shelf of flat ground forty feet above the water, and there it was: the supply cache. Or what remained of it.

Someone had stacked the expedition's spare drums and crates in the open, doused them, and burned them thoroughly — not a campfire accident, not lightning, not a cook stove gone wrong. The aluminum had melted into silver puddles. The plastic had become black lace. Whatever paper had been there was ash finer than flour, and when Hazel crouched and sifted it with two fingers, the wind took most of it away before he could learn anything except that someone had wanted nothing left to read.

He walked the perimeter slowly, the way he'd been trained and the way he'd taught himself after the training stopped covering what the job actually was. Boot prints in the baked mud at the edge of the shelf. At least three sets, maybe four, overlapping and re-overlapping — people standing in a group, facing the cache, watching it burn. One set of prints was different. Narrower. Lighter heel strike. And those prints didn't stand and watch.

Those prints came up the slope from the water, stood at the edge of the scene for exactly as long as it takes to understand what you're looking at, and then went away — fast, long-striding, heading *up* the talus, away from the river, into the rocks.

Away from the water. In a desert. Where the whole logic of survival runs the other direction.

Hazel stood a long time at the edge of that shelf, reading the ground the way other men read letters from home. Somebody had come to this cache expecting supplies and had found a funeral pyre instead. Somebody had understood, faster than Hazel himself was understanding it, that the fire wasn't carelessness. It was a message. *We were here. We are erasing ourselves. Do not wait to be next.*

And somebody had run inland, alone, toward thirst and heat and stone, because inland was the one direction the fire's authors would not look.

He went back down to the skiff and sat in it without starting anything, hands loose on the pole, letting the eddy turn him in slow circles. The radio hissed on its bracket. He thought about calling it in. *Ochoa, I've got a destroyed cache and sign of a survivor fleeing on foot, requesting backup.* And Ochoa would say what Ochoa always said, which was that there was no budget, and what Ochoa sometimes said, quieter, which was that there was no *want*, and Hazel would be alone up here either way. He already was alone up here. The difference between *alone* and *abandoned* was mostly paperwork.

He pushed back into the current.

* * *

The tent was the fifth day, and by then he'd stopped trusting the river to be empty.

It was a dome tent, expedition grade, one of four that should have been traveling with Survey Two according to the manifest Ochoa had grudgingly photocopied for him. It was pitched — badly, hastily, by someone who knew tents but not this terrain — on a bench of sand above a bend, and it had been torn open along one whole side. Not cut from outside. Torn from within, the fabric ripped in long diagonal pulls, the kind of damage that happens when a person leaves a shelter in a hurry and doesn't bother with the zipper because bothering with the zipper costs seconds and seconds are the only currency that matters at moments like that.

Inside: a sleeping bag, empty. A field journal, water-swollen to the density of a brick, its ink run to gray ghosts. Hazel turned pages until he found one legible fragment — a sketch, actually, a cartographer's hand, precise even drowned — showing the river bending around a formation labeled in careful print: *the narrows below the gorge — anomalous readings intensify.* Below that, in different ink, pressed hard enough to score the page: *they are not readings.*

No date. No name. The rest was water.

He sat with the journal in his lap and the torn tent breathing softly behind him in whatever wind moved through the canyon, and he made himself think it through like a ranger and not like a man whose skin had gone cold. *They are not readings.* Written by whom? Pressed hard — fear does that, or fury. Added later than the sketch — different ink, different pressure. Someone had drawn a map and then someone, maybe the same hand, maybe another, had looked at what the instruments were saying near the source and had written down the truth underneath the data.

And then a tent had been torn open from inside, and someone had run.

He searched the sand around the camp with the sun going long and copper behind the western wall. Footprints everywhere, weeks old, half-eaten by wind. But at the base of the bench, where the sand met stone and stone held a print longer than sand ever would, he found them again: the narrow prints, the light heel strike. Coming down to the water once. Going away from it twice. The second set of departures deeper-toed than the first, someone running harder, someone who had come back here — why? For the journal? To leave it? — and then fled inland again, higher this time, toward a ramp of broken rock that switchbacked up the canyon wall toward a skyline of ruined towers where nothing grew and nothing lived and no sane person goes.

Hazel stood at the bottom of that ramp as the light died. Above him the canyon wall went up and up into shadow, and somewhere up there, in the heat and the thirst and the dark, a person was alive. Had been alive. Was maybe still alive, holed up in some cave, listening for boots on stone, holding onto whatever it was that made them write *they are not readings* and then run.

Every instinct he owned said climb. Every practical fact said night, and gear, and a skiff that couldn't defend itself, and a canyon that had been burning caches and tearing tents for somebody else's reasons.

He climbed thirty feet up the ramp anyway, just far enough to see the switchbacks continue beyond the reach of his flashlight, and he stood there in the rising dark with his hand flat against warm stone, and he said it out loud, quietly, the way you talk to someone you can't see but are sure can hear:

"Ranger service. I'm coming back at first light. Stay alive till then."

The canyon took the words and gave back nothing. But the wind moved through the narrows below, and for a moment — he'd swear this later, under oath if it ever came to that — the sound it made was almost like an answer. Almost like a voice. Almost like someone up in the ruins of the dark, exhaling.

He went down, anchored the skiff, and lay awake under the stars with the rifle across his chest and the swollen journal zipped inside his jacket, over his heart, where he carried the things he wasn't ready to think about.

At first light, he climbed.
