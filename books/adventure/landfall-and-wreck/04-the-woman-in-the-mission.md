## 4. The Woman in the Mission

The compass had been pointing at the door for three days.

Wren Okafor sat with her back against the flooded nave's stone wall, knees drawn up, the rifle across her thighs, and watched the brass needle tremble toward the broken archway where the river came in. It had been doing that since the water rose — swinging off true north like a dog straining at a leash, dragging its whole small brass body around to face the flood.

*He's still here,* the needle said. *Or it is. Or something is.*

She didn't trust it anymore. She didn't trust anything anymore, except the rifle and her own arithmetic: eleven rounds remaining, one canteen half full, four days since she'd eaten anything that hadn't come out of a can or the river itself. Four days since Mateo died. Six since the others. She was running the numbers again when she heard the boat.

Not a motor — she'd have heard a motor from a mile off, and she'd have had time to choose her ground. This was an oar, muffled, patient, someone sculling who knew how to be quiet on water. Which meant either a fisherman, which this stretch of dead river hadn't supported in years, or the men from the gorge, which meant she was about to die in a church.

Her body made the decision before her mind caught up. She was moving through the waist-deep water of the transept, rifle held high, boots silent on submerged flagstones, folding herself into the shadow behind the collapsed confessional. The compass stayed in her breast pocket, and even through the fabric she could feel it pulling, absurdly, toward the entrance like a compass-shaped heart beating against her ribs.

The sound grew. The soft knuckle-knock of wood on water. Then light — a lantern, hooded, sweeping low across the flooded floor, and beneath it a voice, low, talking to himself or to the boat or to God, she couldn't tell.

"—should've turned back at the narrows," the voice muttered. "Should've turned back at the depot. Should've—"

The lantern swung into the nave.

Wren put the rifle's muzzle through the gap in the confessional panel and said, "Don't move."

The man froze so completely that for one strange second she thought he might be a statue, some saint the flood had missed. He stood shin-deep in the shallows where the floor rose, one hand on the gunwale of a battered skiff, the other raised, palm out. The lantern hung from his belt and lit him from below, which made his face look carved and older than it probably was — thirty-something, sun-cured, a jaw that had been broken once and set by somebody in a hurry. Desert clothes gone stiff with dried sweat. A ranger's badge on his chest, dull as an old coin.

"Evening," he said.

"Don't." Her voice came out cracked. She hadn't used it in two days except to count. "Hands where I can see them. Both of them."

"They are."

"The other one. Off the boat."

He lifted it slowly. Water dripped from his fingers. "I'm going to say something," he said, "and you're going to want to shoot me anyway, so I'll say it fast. My name is Hazel Sandoval. District ranger, out of Corriente Wells. I'm looking for the Halvorsen survey expedition. Six people, went quiet nineteen days ago."

The name hit her like cold water down the back of the neck. *Halvorsen.* Ingrid Halvorsen, who'd hired Wren because her charts were the cleanest in the territory and paid her half up front, which should have been the first warning, because nobody pays half up front for a job they expect you to survive.

"You're lying," Wren said.

"I'm tired," he said. "Those aren't the same thing, but tonight they look alike. Look — badge. You can see it. Come see it if you want. I'm not going to reach for anything."

She didn't come see it. She kept the muzzle steady and studied him instead, the way she'd study a contour line that didn't match the terrain — looking for the error, the place where the drawing and the world disagreed. His hands stayed up. His breathing was controlled but shallow; he was scared, actually scared, not performing it. And he'd come alone, in a skiff worth maybe forty dollars, with a lantern and no visible escort, which no ambush party in history had ever managed.

"Nineteen days," she said.

"That's what the wire said."

"They've been dead sixteen."

The lantern flame shivered. Something moved through his face — not surprise exactly. Confirmation. She watched him arrive at it, watch the arithmetic land behind his eyes, and whatever he'd been telling himself on the long row upstream, he let go of it right there, standing in the water of a drowned church.

"I figured," he said quietly. "Burned cache at Mile Forty. Tent torn open. Boot prints running away from the water." He looked at her then, really looked, past the rifle. "That was you. Running inland."

"Running anywhere," she said. "Inland was just the direction nobody would think to look."

"And then you stopped."

"And then I stopped." Her arms were shaking. She noticed it with a kind of distant irritation, the way you notice weather. "Because I ran out of country to run to, and because—" The words snagged. She pushed past them. "Because the thing I took is worth more than my life, and I got tired of dying badly in installments."

"What thing?"

"Show me your warrant first," she said. "Your orders. Whatever paper sent you upriver alone in a stolen skiff."

"It's not stolen. It's confiscated. From a thief named Denny Marsh, who stole it from a smuggler named Marisol, who will presumably kill me for it later, which feels fair." He shifted his weight, and the rifle tracked him, and he went still again. "No paper. Unofficial. My superintendent wants plausible deniability more than he wants answers. I bought supplies I couldn't afford and pointed myself upstream. That's the whole of it."

It was insane. It was the most insane thing she'd ever heard, and she had spent nineteen days listening to armed men discuss aquifers and asset timelines over the bodies of her colleagues. Insane, and it rang true the way forged money never does — too worn, too human, too stupid to be a cover story.

Slowly, keeping her left hand visible, she reached into her pocket and brought out the compass.

Hazel Sandoval looked at the little brass instrument the way a starving man looks at a locked pantry.

"That's not pointing north," he said.

"No."

"Where's it pointing?"

"At the source of the river," Wren said. "At what killed six people. At what they're going to wall up and bury under a hundred feet of concrete if nobody stops them." Her voice was doing something strange now, climbing, fraying, and she hated it, hated that after everything she couldn't keep it level. "They shot them, Ranger. They shot all of them. Ingrid last, I think, or maybe I just saw hers clearest. And I ran. I took Mateo's compass and I ran, and I have been sitting in this church for four days waiting for those men to find me, and every boat I heard I thought was them, and now you're here and you're either the law or you're the best acting I've ever seen, and I need you to be the law, do you understand? I need it so badly I could weep, and I don't weep, I chart, that's what I do, I make the world legible, and none of this is legible—"

"Hey." His voice was low and even, a hand on a spooked animal. "Breathe."

"I am breathing."

"Breathe slower."

She breathed slower. The rifle sagged an inch. Somewhere above them, water dripped from the ruined roof into the flooded nave, each drop landing with a small silver sound, patient as a metronome, counting out a time signature neither of them was keeping.

"Lower the rifle," Hazel said, "and I'll lower my hands, and we'll sit down in that boat, and you'll tell me from the beginning. Not tonight — I know you're past tonight. Tomorrow. We'll eat something first. I've got coffee."

"Coffee," she repeated, as if it were a word in a dead language.

"Real coffee. Well. Coffee-adjacent. It's been on the boat a while."

A sound came out of her that she realized, distantly, was a laugh — dry, cracked, barely functional, but a laugh. She lowered the rifle the rest of the way and immediately felt naked without it, and hated that too, and did it anyway.

"Ranger Sandoval," she said. "If you're lying to me, I want you to know that I've had sixteen days to think about how I'd kill a man, and I've gotten very good at the theory."

"Noted," he said. "For what it's worth, I believe you about the shooting. All of it. And I'm sorry."

"You haven't heard any of it yet."

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry anyway. It seemed like the kind of thing you apologize for in advance."

They sat in the skiff, moored to a pillar that had once held up a bell tower, and ate hardtack and coffee-adjacent coffee while the lantern burned between them. Wren told him the shape of it — not the details, not yet, the details were still sharp-edged and she wasn't ready to bleed on a stranger — but the shape. The anomalous readings. The hidden gorge at the river's headwaters where the water simply vanished into the earth. The men who arrived on the fourth day with consortium papers and consortium rifles and consortium patience.

Hazel listened the way she imagined good surveyors listened: completely, without interrupting, letting the data accumulate.

When she finished, he was quiet for a long moment, turning his cup in his hands.

"There's a man coming up this river after you," he said finally. "I don't know his name yet. But the radio call I got today used the call sign of a ranger who's been dead five years. Whoever's hunting you has friends inside the service, or a key to our frequencies, or both." He looked up. "Which means I can't take you downstream. Downstream is where they'll be watching."

"Then where?"

"Upstream." He said it simply, the way you'd read a bearing off a map. "Toward the source. Toward the thing they killed for. Because right now they think you're a loose end running blind. But if we show up at that gorge with proof — charts, testimony, that compass — we stop being a loose end and become a problem. Problems get handled differently."

"That's insane," Wren said.

"So I've heard."

She looked at him across the lantern light — this broke-jawed, broke-pocketed lawman in a confiscated boat, proposing they sail directly into the teeth of the thing that had murdered everyone she loved — and felt something shift in her chest that she refused, for the moment, to name. Relief, maybe. Or the beginning of a heading.

The compass lay on the thwart between them, needle trembling, faithful as a heartbeat, pointing upstream into the dark.

"Fine," Wren Okafor said. "But I'm navigating."

Hazel almost smiled. "Wouldn't dream otherwise."

Above them, through the broken roof, the first stars were coming out over the drowned mission, and somewhere far downstream, a radio crackled to life in a darkened camp, calling a dead man's name.
