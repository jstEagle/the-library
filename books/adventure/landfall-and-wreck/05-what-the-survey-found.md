## 5. What the Survey Found

*Wren*

The rifle was still in her hands when the ranger sat down across the fire like he had every right to it.

She hadn't decided to let him live. That was the ugly truth of it. Her hands had simply run out of the will to pull the trigger, and then he'd said *ranger* and shown the badge, and she'd watched six days of terror drain out of her arms all at once. Now she sat with her back against a fallen roof beam, the flooded nave of the mission lapping quietly at its own pillars behind her, and the man — Sandoval, he'd said, Hazel Sandoval — was heating water for coffee over a fire no bigger than two cupped hands.

"You don't have to tell me anything," he said. "But I came upriver looking for seven people, and so far I've found one tent, one burned cache, and you."

"Six people," Wren said. "You were looking for six."

He looked up. The firelight moved on his face and didn't change it much. A face built for waiting, she thought. Desert faces were like that.

"There were six of us," she said. "Dr. Amara Okonkwo, team lead. Yusuf on hydrology. Priya and Deng on instruments. Tomás drove the boat." She stopped. The next name took a moment. "And Elias Voss. He was my — we mapped together. Four years."

"Sandoval," he said again, as if reminding himself. "The manifest says seven."

"The manifest is wrong. Or someone wanted it wrong." She turned the compass over in her lap. Brass, dented at the bezel, its needle swinging in small restless arcs that had nothing to do with north. "This was Elias's. He built it. And whoever wrote that manifest knew about him and not about me, which means they counted us before the last resupply at Kilometer Forty. Which means—"

"Which means somebody upstream knew your route before you ever took it," Sandoval finished.

The river spoke through the broken wall behind them, patient and dark. Somewhere out in the flooded transept a fish rose and fell. Wren realized she was gripping the compass hard enough to leave its knurled edge printed into her palm, and made herself stop.

"Eat first," Sandoval said. "Then tell me. Stories go down easier with hot food. My brother taught me that."

"Your brother sounds wise."

"He sounded wise. Mostly he owed money to everyone from here to the coast." Something crossed his face — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had been demolished and never rebuilt. "Eat."

* * *

*Hazel*

He let her eat in silence because he'd learned long ago that people told you everything eventually if you gave them room enough to fall into it. His superintendent would have called this soft. His superintendent had also sent him upriver alone with a confiscated skiff and forty dollars of supplies, so Hazel figured soft was what the job could afford.

The woman ate like someone relearning it — mechanical at first, then faster, then catching herself and slowing down again out of some old courtesy. Wren Okafor, she'd said. Cartographer. Her hands were ruined: split nails, rope burns, a gash along one forearm bound with strips of survey flagging tape, orange and faded pink, the kind they strung along transect lines. She'd torn down her own markers to bind her wounds. There was something in that he understood without wanting to.

Outside, the canyon walls held the last grey light. He kept his voice low anyway.

"Start wherever's easiest."

"There isn't an easy part." She set down the tin cup. "But there's a beginning. We put in at Corriente Landing eleven weeks ago. Contract work — regional water authority, or so the paperwork said. Mapping seasonal flows above the gorge country. Nobody maps up there. Everyone says the river just dies at the head of the canyon system, sinks into the rock, gone. Officially it's a losing stream. Unofficially—" she shrugged, "—nobody cares. Water that isn't there doesn't get fought over."

"But your readings disagreed."

"Our readings said the river wasn't losing anything. It was being taken." She pulled a folded sheet from inside her jacket — oilcloth-wrapped, soft at the creases from handling. She opened it across her knees. Even by firelight Hazel could read a chart when he saw one, and this one was beautiful: contour lines fine as hair, the river drawn in blue ink running up the page until, near the top, it simply stopped, and where it stopped someone had written in pencil, in a cramped urgent hand: *anomalous gradient — flow continues subsurface — see Voss modification.*

"Elias caught it first," Wren said. "Flow rates that made no sense. We were losing two hundred cubic feet per second between Station Nine and Station Eleven, but the seepage measurements showed dry rock. The water wasn't soaking away. It was going somewhere." Her finger traced the blank space past the end of the blue line. "So we went to find out where."

"And did you?"

"We found the gorge." For the first time since he'd met her, something other than fear moved through her face. Wonder, briefly, before grief closed over it like water over a dropped stone. "Hazel, I've mapped rivers on three continents, and I have never — the canyon narrows to nothing, maybe four meters wide, walls two hundred meters high, and the river runs *into* the mountain. Not underground through some crack. Into a cathedral. A cavern big enough that our headlamps couldn't find the ceiling. And rising out of the middle of it, a spring — a column of water coming up from bedrock, clear as glass, cold enough to burn. Flowing upward. Out of the deep aquifer."

"How much water?"

"At low estimate? Enough to irrigate the whole basin. Enough to end the drought tomorrow." She said it flatly, the way you state a distance or a depth, and somehow that made it worse. "Amara cried when she ran the numbers. Then she got careful. She said — she said numbers like this get people killed, so we document everything twice, we tell no one by radio, we run home fast and hand it to the authority ourselves."

Hazel fed a stick to the fire. "How did that go?"

"Somebody was already there."

* * *

*Wren*

She had rehearsed this in the mission for six days, telling it to the water, telling it to Elias's compass, telling it to God if the mission's God was still taking visitors. It came out differently every time. This time it came out in order, which was almost worse, because order made it real.

"They came on the fourth morning at the source. Two boats, grey, no markings, engines muffled. Eight men, armed, moving like they'd done it before. They didn't ask questions. They took our samples — Amara had sealed the vials in a case, and the leader went straight to it, straight to it, like he had a list. Like he knew exactly what he was collecting." Her hands had started moving without her permission, sketching the scene in the air. "They zip-tied us at the camp. All six. And then their officer — tall, grey jacket, calm, that's the thing I can't make myself forget, how *calm* — he asked Amara who else had the data."

"And she said?"

"She said the data belonged to the water authority and the water authority would be expecting us." Wren heard her own voice go distant. "He nodded like that was a reasonable answer. Then he shot Deng. Just — like closing a ledger. And he said, *the river keeps its secrets; you'll keep yours,* which makes no sense, none of it makes sense, except that it does, doesn't it? If nobody knows the spring exists, nobody fights for it. You let the drought do the negotiating. You buy land from desperate people at desperate prices, and then one day the water comes back — *your* water, on *your* land — and everyone downstream calls it a miracle."

Sandoval had gone very still. In the firelight his eyes had the flat shine she'd seen in men reading casualty lists.

"Who?" he said. "Who owns grey boats with no markings?"

"I didn't know then. I know now. One of the men wore a patch under his jacket when he reached for the sample case — I only saw it a second. A pick and a drill bit, crossed. Over the letters C.M.C."

"Cordillera Mining Consortium," Hazel said. The name came out of him like a stone out of a sling. "They've been buying up failed ranches along the upper basin for two years. Everyone thought they were after lithium."

"Maybe they were. Maybe lithium was the excuse to hold the land." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "After Deng, Elias broke his ties and went for the sample case. They shot him too. I don't remember deciding to run. I remember the water, and the cavern, and swimming the siphon channel in the dark with the compass in my teeth because it was the only thing I could carry and I chose it over—" Her breath snagged. "I chose a piece of brass over Tomás. He was calling my name. I heard him calling my name and I swam."

The fire popped. Down in the flooded nave, the river shifted against the old stones, patient as arithmetic.

"He modified the compass," Hazel said gently. "Your partner. Before any of this."

"Years ago. He was obsessed with the losing-stream problem even back then — said a normal compass was useless in basalt country, iron in the rock throws the needle everywhere. So he built one that didn't look for magnetic north. It looks for the aquifer. The deep water has its own signature — dissolved minerals, a faint charge where the flow moves through limestone. His needle finds that." She held it up. In the firelight the brass was scarred and lovely, and the needle swung toward the canyon wall, toward the high dark country upstream, steady as a heartbeat. "It points at the source. It's pointing right now. And it's the only instrument on earth that can find it again, which is why I'm alive, and why I'll stay alive only as long as they don't catch me."

"Then they'll be hunting the compass, not the woman."

"They're hunting both. But yes." She lowered it. "There's a copy of the survey data. Amara hid it — I watched her do it and I didn't understand until later. She sewed the microfilm into the lining of Tomás's spare jacket, the one we left at the Kilometer Forty cache. The cache they burned." She looked up, and her eyes were dry in the way of eyes that have spent all their water somewhere private. "Burned, or searched and burned. I went back two days ago. It was ash. Either they found it or they wanted me to think they did."

"So the evidence is gone."

"The evidence I can touch is gone." The compass turned in her fingers. "But the spring is still down there, running day and night whether anyone believes in it or not. And this—" she tapped the brass, once, like knocking on a door, "—still knows the way. Elias built it so the truth could always be found again. He used to say a map was a promise: *this exists, come see.*" Her voice finally cracked, just once, and she let it. "I intend to keep his promise. Or die keeping it."

* * *

*Hazel*

For a while neither of them spoke, and the fire did the talking, and the river underneath its murmur carried on whatever business rivers carry on in the dark.

Hazel thought about his badge, lying in the bottom of his pack. Thought about the word *unofficial*, which his superintendent had used twice in a five-minute conversation, which meant *deniable*, which meant *if you die up here we will describe you as a recreational boater*. He thought about debts — his brother's, the ones with numbers attached, and this woman's, the kind with no number, the kind you pay in witness. He was a ranger. Witnessing was the whole job. Everything else was paperwork.

"If I take you downstream," he said slowly, "to the tribunal at Corriente, you testify, the consortium buries it in motions for a decade, and meanwhile they cap the spring and the drought finishes the job. If I take you upstream, to the source, we're two people with a rifle and a compass walking into the property of men who shoot cartographers." He rubbed his jaw. "Neither option is good."

"No," Wren agreed. "But one of them is mine."

"That's what I was afraid of." He should say no. Every trained instinct said no: secure the witness, withdraw, report. Every trained instinct had been assembled by the same institutions the consortium owned, and he was tired of pretending otherwise. "There's a smuggler works this stretch. Marisol Vega. Fast boat, faster mouth, hates the consortium for reasons of her own. If anyone can move us upstream past their patrols, it's her."

Wren stared at him. "You know a smuggler."

"I know everyone on this river. It's the one benefit of being poor and official." He kicked dirt over the fire, and the dark came in close and friendly. "We move at first light. And Okafor — the compass stays where I can see it. No offense."

"None taken." A ghost of a smile, the first. "Elias would have liked you. He trusted pessimists. He said optimists get you killed and pessimists get you home."

"Your Elias sounds like he'd have gotten along with my brother."

Upstream, far off, so faint it might have been wind in the canyon — a sound neither of them named. An engine, running quiet, running patient, somewhere on the night water between them and the source.

The compass needle didn't waver. It pointed at the spring, and past the spring, at everything coming to meet them.
