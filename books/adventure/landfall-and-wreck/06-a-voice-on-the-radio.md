## 6. A Voice on the Radio

The radio woke him an hour before dawn.

Hazel had slept badly anyway — the kind of sleep the mission ruins allowed, shallow and salted with dreams of water where there shouldn't be any. He'd bedded down in what had once been a chapel, he supposed, judging by the arched window frames still standing against the sky like ribs of something long dead. Wren had taken the corner nearest the door, her rifle across her knees even in sleep, and the brass compass sat between them on a flat stone like a third person nobody wanted to talk to.

The radio was his own — a battered ranger-issue set bolted to his pack frame, its antenna a whip of scarred aluminum he'd straightened more times than he could count. It crackled once. Twice. Then a voice came through, thin and static-shredded but unmistakably human.

*"Any station, any station. This is Ranger Vasquez, call sign Lantana, requesting status on the Sandoval party. Do you copy, Hazel?"*

He was upright before he knew he'd moved, hand on the dial, heart doing something stupid in his chest.

Vasquez.

The name arrived whole, the way old names do — not as a word but as a weather system. Tomás Vasquez, who'd taught him to read water signs in his second year on the flats. Who'd pulled him out of the Arroyo Hondo flash flood with a rope and a stream of curses so inventive Hazel had written half of them down. Who'd told him, the night Hazel pinned on the badge, *You'll be broke your whole life, muchacho, but you'll never be bought, and that's the better trade.* Who'd carried him through the worst year after Mateo died, showing up at his door with food Hazel hadn't asked for and couldn't afford, refusing every attempt at repayment with a wave of one scarred hand.

Dead six years now. Heart, they said. Hazel had carried the flag at the funeral and stood afterward in the heat while the superintendent said words about service.

He knew all this. He knew it the way he knew his own hands. And still his thumb found the transmit key, because grief is not a rational animal and hope is worse.

"This is Sandoval," he said. "Identify."

Static. Then: *"Good to hear your voice, son. We've been sweeping the reach since word came down about the survey team. Where are you holed up?"*

Hazel's mouth went dry. He hadn't transmitted his position to anyone. Hadn't reported in since leaving the district office — unofficial assignment, unfunded, unrecorded except in the superintendent's private ledger. Nobody downstream should have known he was on the river at all, let alone been "sweeping" for him.

And the voice —

He pressed the key again, slowly, buying time. "Who's this? Say again your name."

*"Vasquez. Tomás Vasquez, District Nine. You know me, Hazel."*

The static ate the edges of the words, but underneath it the voice was wrong. Not the timbre — time and distance and cheap speakers could explain timbre. The rhythm. Vasquez had spoken Spanish first and English second, and English had always come out of him slightly sideways, articles dropped, vowels bent toward the interior valleys where he'd grown up. This voice spoke English the way water runs through a channel cut by other water — smooth, worn, correct. Correct in a way Vasquez had never been.

And Vasquez had called him *muchacho* for eleven years and *Sandoval* when he was angry. Never *son*. Never *Hazel*, soft and familiar, like a man reading from a file.

"I knew Tomás Vasquez," Hazel said carefully. "Knew him twenty years. He's dead."

A pause. Long enough that the static seemed to lean in.

*"That's a hard thing to hear,"* the voice said, and there was something almost gentle in it, which made it worse. *"I understand you're under strain. You've been upriver a long time alone. Come downstream, son. We've got a boat two days below your position, running relief. Medical, supplies, extraction. Whatever you've found up there, you don't have to carry it alone."*

Behind him, in the dark, Wren's breathing had changed. She was awake. Of course she was awake; she'd probably been awake before the radio crackled, sleeping the way she did — he was learning her rhythms now, three days into knowing her, the way she surfaced instantly and completely, no groggy borderland, a woman who had taught herself never to be caught mid-dream.

She didn't speak. She watched him across the ruined chapel with eyes that caught the last starlight, and she waited, because she understood before he said it aloud what he was only beginning to understand himself.

"Two days downstream," Hazel repeated. "Running relief."

*"That's right."*

"For a survey expedition that went silent three weeks ago. That nobody's sent anyone after until me. Unofficially. On a skiff I confiscated off a chicken thief." He kept his voice level, conversational, the tone he used on men he was deciding whether to arrest. "District Nine doesn't run relief boats. District Nine doesn't have relief boats. We've got two airboats and a pontoon held together with prayer, and both are assigned to the southern flats until monsoon season."

Another pause. When the voice came back, the gentleness had drained out of it, and what remained was flat and patient and very cold.

*"You sound tired, Ranger. Why don't you give me your position, and we'll come to you."*

"There it is," Hazel said quietly.

*"Excuse me?"*

"The first ask was vague. Come downstream, find us. Now it's specific. Give me your position." He turned the gain down a notch, watching the needle. "You want coordinates. You want to know exactly where I am, and who's with me, and how many rifles we're holding. Because whoever you are, you didn't get my name off a duty roster. My assignment isn't on any roster. Which means somebody told you I was coming, and the only somebodies who knew are people who shouldn't be talking to rangers at all."

The static breathed. Somewhere in it, faintly, he thought he heard another voice — a second speaker, low, a word or two, cut off fast. A relay station. Someone else listening.

*"Hazel,"* the voice said, and now it didn't bother with warmth at all, and somehow that was almost a relief, like a mask coming off. *"Think about your situation. You're a long way from anywhere, on a river nobody patrols, with a woman who is either a witness or a suspect, carrying equipment that doesn't belong to her. There are people who would prefer that situation resolved quietly. I'm offering you the chance to resolve it loudly, officially, with backup. Or you can stay up there and find out what quiet means."*

"Is that a threat?"

*"It's arithmetic."*

Hazel looked down at the compass on the stone between them. In the dark he couldn't see the needle, but he could feel it anyway, the way you feel a tooth that's been bothering you — pointing, always pointing, away from north, away from everything sensible, toward the source.

"You know what Tomás Vasquez would say to you?" he said. "If he were alive, and some stranger picked up a radio wearing his call sign?"

*"I imagine he'd say whatever kept him alive."*

"He'd say you talk too much." Hazel keyed off the transmission without waiting for an answer, then — after a moment — keyed it back on one last time. "For the record. Lantana is retired. It was retired with honors six years ago, and if I ever find out whose throat it came out of, I'm going to enjoy the conversation."

He killed the set. Pulled the battery. Wrapped the whole unit in his spare shirt and stowed it deep in the pack, because a radio that could receive was a radio that could be tracked, and he was suddenly certain — with the flat certainty of a man who has just heard his dead friend's ghost try to negotiate — that somewhere downstream, someone was triangulating.

* * *

Wren had a fire going by the time he finished checking the skiff. Small, screened behind the chapel's fallen wall, burning dry mesquite that gave off almost no light. She'd heated water and was holding two tin cups, and she handed him one without asking, which was becoming a habit between them he neither encouraged nor ended.

"You knew him," she said. "The real one."

"Twenty years." Hazel sat on the stone beside the compass. Above them the sky was going gray at the edges, the canyon walls emerging from black into a deep bruised purple. "He trained me. Half of what I know about water I learned watching him. The other half I learned the day he wasn't there to catch my mistakes."

"And someone's using his call sign."

"Yeah."

"That's not standard procedure."

"No." He drank. The water was bitter with mesquite smoke and he was grateful for it. "Call signs get reassigned sometimes, after a death. Sloppy districts do it. But you don't reassign a man's sign and then have the new holder claim to *be* him. That's not paperwork. That's a lure."

Wren crouched by the fire, rifle across her thighs, and fed it a stick. In the small light her face was all planes and exhaustion, three weeks of terror carved into her, and yet her voice, when it came, was steady. Steadier than his felt.

"They knew your name. They knew you were coming upstream. They knew enough to dress it up as rescue." She looked at him. "Hazel. Who knew?"

He turned the cup in his hands. The tin was dented, and his thumbnail fit into one of the dents like it had been made for it.

"The superintendent. Two clerks. The thief I took the skiff from heard the orders, maybe — he was in the holding cell, walls are thin." He paused. "And whoever the superintendent reports to, which is a list I've never seen and probably couldn't read if I did."

"You think your own district sold you."

"I think," Hazel said, "that a survey crew of nine people went up this river with district sanction, and eight of them died, and the district's response was to send one broke ranger on a stolen boat. I've been telling myself that's budget. That's the way things are. Underfunded, understaffed, everybody doing three jobs." He set the cup down. "But a relief boat takes a signature. Fuel takes a signature. Somebody signed for a boat that doesn't exist, staffed it with a dead man's name, and pointed it at me. That's not budget. That's intent."

Wren was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Downriver, somewhere beyond the bend, the water talked to itself over the shallows, the endless patient argument of a river that had been having the same conversation for ten thousand years.

"My partner built that compass," she said finally. "Emeka. He built it because he didn't trust paper maps anymore — said maps were just stories people agreed to believe, and he wanted an instrument that believed in nothing but the water table." Her thumb moved over the compass's brass rim, an unconscious gesture, the way you touch a wound to check it's still there. "The night they came to camp, he shoved it into my jacket and told me to run inland, away from the river, because everyone would look along the water. And then he stood up and walked toward them talking, loud, giving me time." Her jaw worked. "I've been trying to figure out what he said to them. What you say to armed men to buy thirty seconds. I keep landing on: anything. Anything at all. And it still costs everything."

"Wren—"

"Don't." Not sharp. Just final, the way a door closes. "I'm not telling you so you'll comfort me. I'm telling you because you need to hear it. They didn't kill Emeka because he fought. They killed him because he *knew*, and knowing was enough. And now you know. And now they know you know, because you just told them so on an open channel." She looked up at him, firelight moving in her eyes. "You gave them confirmation, Hazel. Your voice, your suspicion, your position within ten miles. That transmission cost us."

He wanted to argue. The honest part of him — the part that had spent twenty years writing incident reports in which his own errors were recorded in the same ink as everyone else's — wouldn't let him.

"Yeah," he said. "It did."

"So what do we do?"

Hazel stood. The gray was turning to gold along the eastern rim, and the flooded mission emerged around them — arches and standing walls and water-stained stone, beautiful the way ruins are beautiful, which is to say honestly. He thought about the voice on the radio. *There are people who would prefer that situation resolved quietly.* He thought about the arithmetic of it. One ranger, one witness, one compass, one skiff. Against a consortium that could put a dead man's voice on the air and a gunboat on the water and a signature on a fuel requisition.

Against all that, what did he have? A badge that paid nothing. A debt that paid itself endlessly. A dead friend's memory. A woman with a rifle and a map in her head that no one else alive possessed.

And, somewhere downstream, two days' hard run, a boat full of men pretending to be rescue.

"We don't go downstream," he said. "We go up."

Wren's head came around. "Up? The gorge is four days above us through the narrows, and the narrows are—" She stopped herself. Recalculated. He watched her do it, watch the cartographer take over from the survivor, fear giving way to terrain. "The narrows are suicide in a loaded skiff."

"In a loaded skiff, yeah." He was already moving, pulling the tarp off the boat, checking the lines. "But Rook's people — and it's Rook, or someone working his playbook — will expect us to run downstream. Downstream is escape. Downstream is the district, the tribunal, the safety of paperwork. Everything about us says downstream." He glanced back at her. "So we go the direction nobody sane goes. We go toward the thing they're killing to protect. We get to the source before they cap it, and we document it so thoroughly that no amount of quiet can bury it."

"That's not escape. That's walking into the mine."

"It is." He cinched the last line and straightened. "But here's the thing I learned from Vasquez, the real one, the one whose voice you didn't hear this morning. He used to say: when you're being hunted on open ground, stop running for the fence. Run *at* the hunter. Because the fence has a gate and the gate has a toll, and the hunter has only ever planned for prey."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then she began breaking down the camp with quick, economical movements, and he understood that this was her answer, delivered in the only language she fully trusted — logistics.

"One condition," she said, stuffing the bedroll into its sack. "When we get there. When we find what Emeka found. I'm the one who charts it. His instrument leads, my hand records. That's the debt I'm paying, and I don't share it."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

They pushed the skiff into the current as the sun cleared the rim and lit the whole canyon the color of old brass — the same color, Hazel thought, as the compass in Wren's jacket, pointing its stubborn impossible point toward the source. Behind them, far downstream, the river ran on toward the district, toward the voice, toward the trap dressed as rescue.

Ahead, the water narrowed. The walls drew in. And somewhere in the high silence, Hazel had the distinct, skin-crawling certainty that eyes were already tracking their wake — that the hunt hadn't started this morning with a radio call.

It had started the day he left the district office. Maybe earlier. Maybe the day Mateo died, and a debt was born that someone, somewhere, had always intended to collect.

He took the tiller and turned the bow upstream.
