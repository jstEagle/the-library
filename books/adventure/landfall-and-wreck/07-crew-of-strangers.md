## 7. Crew of Strangers

**Hazel**

The ambush came at the narrows where the canyon pinched to a boat's width and the water ran green and slow, like something holding its breath.

Hazel saw the glint first — sun off a rifle barrel on the east rim — and had time for exactly one word.

"Down."

Wren dropped flat in the skiff before the first shot cracked across the water. It hit the transom where her head had been and splintered wood into his collar. He steered with one hand low on the tiller, keeping his body below the gunwale, running blind through a stretch of river he'd only seen from above. The second shot went wide. The third found the fuel can.

Not the tank. The can. Lashed to the stern rail. Whoever was shooting knew boats, and wanted them alive long enough to be caught.

"They're herding us," Wren shouted over the engine's ragged coughing. "Downstream — there'll be more of them downstream!"

"There's no upstream!" Hazel said. "Upstream is where they are!"

The engine died a hundred yards later, and the current took over, carrying them sideways toward a gravel bar where three men stood waiting with a net and a look of professional patience. Hazel got his hand on his sidearm. Wren got hers on the rifle. Neither of them got to use it, because that was when Marisol Vega came around the bend at forty knots in a boat that had no business being in a canyon that narrow, and the men on the bar had to scatter or die.

She didn't slow down. She came alongside the dead skiff close enough to slap hulls, and she yelled one word over the roar of her engines:

"Jump!"

They jumped.

* * *

**Marisol**

Marisol had rules. Rule one: never stop for anyone on this stretch of river. Rule two: especially not for rangers. Rule three: if you break rules one and two, don't make it a habit.

She'd broken all three inside of ten seconds, and she still couldn't fully explain why, except that she'd seen the man in the ranger's shirt take a bullet-spent second to push the woman flatter before he ducked himself, and there was something in that order of operations she couldn't leave alone. Men who put other people's heads below the gunwale first were rare enough on this river to be worth a detour.

Also, they were being shot at by consortium contractors, and Marisol had a grudge against the consortium the way other people had a pulse.

"So," she said, once they were six miles downriver and the canyon had opened into brown afternoon light. "Ranger. Cartographer. You're the ones Rook's paying for."

"You know about the bounty?" Hazel asked.

"I know everything about the bounty." She kept her eyes on the channel. "It's how I know you're worth more to me floating than delivered. Sit down before you fall down. There's water under the bench."

The woman — Okafor, she'd said, though she flinched when Marisol used it, so Marisol filed that away too — sat down. The ranger didn't. He stood braced in the stern, watching the way they'd come, and Marisol decided she could tolerate that.

"Why help us?" he asked.

"I'm not helping you." She cut the throttle as the canyon mouth widened onto the long lake reach, letting the boat settle. "I'm inconveniencing them. There's a difference. You want to live, fine, I'm going that direction anyway. But understand what you're riding in. This is a smuggling boat. Everything on it is stolen, most of it from people who stole it first. If your badge has a problem with that, you can swim."

Hazel looked at her for a long moment. Then he sat down next to Wren, which was answer enough.

"Good," Marisol said, and opened the throttle again.

* * *

**Teo**

Teo had been living in the mission village since the flood took his family's farm four years ago, and in that time he had learned three things: stay small, stay useful, and never let anyone count you twice.

He'd stowed away on the smuggler's boat at dawn because the soldiers had come to the village asking questions about strangers on the river, and the way Padre Anselmo's face went gray while answering them told Teo everything he needed to know about what happened to villages that answered wrong. If trouble was coming upriver, Teo wanted to be downriver of it. That wasn't cowardice. That was arithmetic.

He'd been folded into the bilge space behind the fuel tanks for nine hours, listening to smugglers argue about tide tables, when the boat stopped at a ruined dock to take on a passenger who turned out to be an old man with a geologist's hammer and a face like a collapsed mine shaft.

And then the old man had said, climbing aboard: "You've got a stowaway back there. Been breathing on my neck since the dock."

Teo froze. Considered denying it. Considered running.

Then the hatch opened, and a ranger looked down at him, and Teo thought: *of course*. Of course the one boat he chose carried the law.

"He's a kid," the old man said. "Sixteen, maybe. Mission village accent. Somebody's missing him already or nobody is."

"I'm fourteen," Teo lied, and climbed out on his own, chin up, hands visible, the way you did with armed men. "I work for my passage. I can run lines, patch hulls, read water. Ask her." He nodded at the smuggler. "You didn't even know I was aboard until he said so. That's useful."

There was a silence. The cartographer woman laughed — a short, cracked sound, like something breaking open — and covered her mouth.

"He's right," she said. "That's exactly how I survived the last month."

The ranger crouched to Teo's eye level. Up close he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the day. "Nobody's throwing you off," he said. "But you should know these people are hunting us, and being near us is the most dangerous thing you've ever done."

Teo shrugged. "Second most dangerous. First was staying home."

Something moved across the ranger's face — recognition, maybe, of a kid doing arithmetic with his own life — and he stood up and stepped aside, which on any boat means *welcome aboard*.

* * *

**Bram**

Ambrose Hollis Bram — Old Bram now, though the name had rotted through him years before his hair went white — watched the boy climb out of the bilge and felt the familiar turn of his own stomach. He'd spent thirty years reading rock strata and twenty of those lying about what he read, and he'd learned that the truth always surfaced eventually, usually in the form of a child or a flood.

He hadn't meant to board this boat. He'd meant to die quietly in the mission ruin with his maps and his shame, and then the cartographer had walked in with a compass that pointed at the worst thing he'd ever helped bury, and dying quietly had stopped being available.

Now there was a ranger aboard. A consortium bounty on all their heads. And a boy who breathed like someone trying to take up less space than a person occupies.

Bram knew that breathing. He'd done it for two decades.

"The compass," he said to the woman, that evening, at anchor in the reeds while the ranger stood watch. "May I?"

She hesitated — he understood; you didn't hand a thing like that to just anyone — then passed it over. He turned it in his thick fingers. Fine brasswork. A modified declination ring. And beneath the needle housing, soldered with a jeweler's care, a coil of wire he recognized the way you recognize handwriting.

"Dashiell Okonkwo built this," he said. "The damping coil. He published the design in '41 and then withdrew it. Said it was too easy to weaponize."

Wren went very still. "You knew Dash."

"I funded his last expedition." Bram handed the compass back. "Through the consortium. Before I understood what the consortium was *for*." He looked out at the darkening water. "Ask me what's at the source, girl. You've been carrying that needle for weeks. Ask me why it points where it points."

"I know what's at the source. Water. Enough to end the drought."

"That's the half of it." Bram heard his own voice go flat, the voice he used for numbers, because numbers were the only way he could say the rest. "The aquifer under that gorge doesn't feed the river. The river feeds *it* — has for ten thousand years. The consortium's engineers worked that out in '39. Cap the recharge point, and the spring dies. The valley above dries. Farms fail. Land sells cheap. They buy the watershed for pennies and hold the only water left." He turned to face her. "I wrote the falsified survey reports. All of them. My name is on the geology that made the drought legal."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the bank, the boy was showing the ranger how to rig a trotline, both of them pretending not to listen.

"And now?" Wren asked quietly.

"Now their cap is ninety percent complete, and the land titles clear at the end of the season, and the only proof of any of it is a brass compass and a woman who was supposed to die with the rest of her crew." He spread his scarred hands. "So yes. I'm coming with you. Not for redemption. Redemption's for people whose accounts can still be balanced. I'm coming because I'm the only one aboard who knows where the cap's weak."

* * *

**Hazel**

Hazel stood the middle watch because he didn't trust anyone else to stand it, and because standing watch gave him somewhere to put the restlessness that had lived in his chest since the radio call in the flats. Except the restlessness had changed shape. Back home it had been a holding pattern. Now it was a crew.

He counted them the way you count anything you might lose. Marisol at the wheel she refused to surrender even at anchor, one hand on the throttle like a woman holding a door shut against weather. Wren asleep sitting up, rifle across her knees, the compass in her fist even in sleep. Bram by the fire, awake, always awake, a man serving a sentence he'd sentenced himself to. And the boy, Teo, who was supposed to be asleep and instead sat down beside Hazel without asking, and handed him half a fish without comment.

"Eat," Teo said. "You gave me your blanket earlier. I do arithmetic."

Hazel ate. The river ran dark and quiet past the reeds. Downstream, somewhere, Rook was spending consortium money to buy every pair of eyes on this water, and upstream the spring was being strangled in the dark, and here in the middle was a smuggler, a ghost, a liar, and a kid, and him.

"We're not a rescue party anymore," Hazel said. "You understand that. What we're doing now — there's no version where I file a report and it's over."

"I figured," Teo said. "Padre Anselmo says a crew isn't people who share a boat. It's people who share what happens after."

Hazel looked at him. "How old are you really?"

"Fourteen," Teo said, and this time it sounded true. "Fifteen in the wet season."

Hazel almost smiled. Then the radio on his belt crackled — the band they'd agreed to monitor, the one Rook's people shouldn't have known existed — and a voice came through, calm and pleasant and wrong:

*"Skiff at Reed Anchor, this is River Control. Be advised: checkpoint established at Serpent's Neck, effective dawn. All vessels subject to inspection. Have your papers ready."*

Papers. On a smuggling boat. In a canyon with no government within two hundred miles.

Marisol was already at the throttle. Wren was already awake, rifle up. Bram was already pulling charts from his pack with shaking hands.

"Serpent's Neck," the old man said. "Class five water. Nobody runs it clean. Nobody's ever run it *chased*."

Marisol grinned like a woman being offered exactly what she'd always wanted.

"Then we'll be the first," she said, and opened the throttle, and the river took them.
