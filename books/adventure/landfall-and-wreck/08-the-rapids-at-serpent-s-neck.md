## 8. The Rapids at Serpent's Neck

The first thing you learn running cargo on the Río Bravo del Norte — the real river, not the pretty one on the maps they sell tourists — is that the water always knows something you don't.

Marisol Vega had been reading that knowledge for eleven years, since she was fourteen and her father put her at the tiller of a loaded skiff and said, *Feel it, mija. Don't fight it. Argue with it.* She'd argued with a lot of things since then. The river was the only argument she'd never once won, and the only one she kept coming back to.

She stood now at the stern of her boat, the *Cuervo*, one hand on the wheel, watching the canyon walls close in ahead like a book deciding to end. Behind them, maybe four kilometers back, the gunboat came on. She could hear it before she could see it — a flat diesel growl bouncing off stone, arriving out of order, echo before engine.

"They're gaining," Hazel said beside her. He'd been saying versions of that for an hour, and each time she liked him less for it.

"I have eyes, ranger."

"Didn't mean anything by it."

"Everybody means something by it." She adjusted the throttle a quarter inch. "You want to help? Get your weight forward. All of you. Stern rides light when we hit the drop, and if the prop comes out of the water I lose steering, and if I lose steering in Serpent's Neck, we're a story people tell."

Wren Okafor moved up without being asked, clutching the brass compass against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Teo crouched low between the supply crates, all elbows and watchfulness, the way he'd been since they pulled him off the mission dock two days ago. Old Bram sat where Marisol had told him to sit and touch nothing, which so far was the only useful thing the old man had done.

"How bad is it?" Wren asked. Her voice was steady. Marisol gave her credit for that. Most people asking about Serpent's Neck let a little wobble into it.

"You see how the walls pinch up there?" Marisol nodded at the gap ahead, where the canyon narrowed to less than half its width and the water went from brown-green to a churning bone-white. "That's the Neck. The river drops nine meters through a channel about as wide as my boat is long. There's a rock in the middle of it shaped like a serpent's head when the light's right, which is why the name, and there are two ways past it. One is correct. One is what happened to the Herrera brothers in '09."

"What happened to the Herrera brothers?"

"Nobody knows. That's the point." She cut the throttle back as they entered the shadow of the walls. "I've run it six times. Loaded, twice. Never with a gunboat behind me and never with this many strangers on my deck trusting me with their lives, which, no offense, is a lot of weight I didn't ask for."

"None taken," Bram said.

"Everything's taken, old man. That's the whole economy."

The diesel growl swelled. Around the bend behind them, maybe three kilometers now, a gray shape resolved against the water — low, armored at the bow, a mounted gun platform where a cargo crane should be. Consortium colors. Not even pretending to be anything else anymore.

Hazel turned. "Marisol. How much time?"

"If they hold their line, twenty minutes. If their pilot's good, fifteen." She looked at him, and let him see her make the decision she'd already made. "We go now. Full throttle. Everyone down and hold on to something welded."

* * *

The *Cuervo* leapt like something alive.

Marisol ran the approach the way her father taught her — read the V of the current, find the tongue of smooth water that marked the safe channel, commit before fear could get a vote. The canyon screamed around them, walls blurring, spray coming over the bow in cold sheets. Ahead, the serpent rock rose out of the white water, black and streaming, and the river split around it into two channels: left, wide and inviting and wrong, funneling straight into a submerged shelf that had eaten better boats than hers; right, narrow, ugly, a slot barely wider than the *Cuervo*'s beam, choked with standing waves.

"Right side," she said, mostly to herself, partly to the river. An argument, not a fight.

The gunboat's engine note changed behind them — accelerating. They'd been seen committing.

"Marisol—" Hazel started.

"Sit *down*, ranger."

She brought the wheel hard over. The *Cuervo* heeled, dug its shoulder into the current, and shot for the slot. For three seconds the world was noise and green water and the serpent rock sliding past close enough on her left that she could have touched it, could have read the old carved initials tourists had scratched into it decades ago, and then the bow dropped.

Nine meters. The river fell out from under them.

The *Cuervo* went airborne for a moment that lasted a year — Marisol felt the hull leave the water entirely, felt the prop scream free-spinning, felt every crate on deck try to become a separate projectile — and then slammed down into the boil below with a crack she felt in her teeth. Water came aboard in tons. Something heavy broke loose aft. The boat wallowed, shipped half the channel, and for one endless second sat dead in the vortex below the drop, steering gone, the current chewing at them.

Then the engine caught. The prop bit. The *Cuervo* answered her hands.

"Report!" she shouted over the roar.

"Teo's bleeding," Wren called back. "Crates broke loose — we lost the port rail crates. Food, most of the medical kit—"

"The fuel drums?"

"Holding!"

"Then we're rich." She hauled the boat out of the boil and into the calmer water below the Neck, heart slamming, hands steady, the way they always were during and never after. Behind and above, the gunboat reached the lip of the rapids and stopped. She watched it sit there, turning slowly in the eddy, too heavy, too deep-drafted, its pilot unwilling or unable to follow.

A voice carried down the canyon, thin with distance. A loudhailer. Words she couldn't make out and didn't need to.

"That's Rook's boat," Hazel said quietly. He'd come up beside her again, soaked to the skin, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before. Not fear. Recognition. "He'll know another way down. Portage road on the north bank. We've bought hours, not days."

"Hours is what I sell, ranger. Days cost extra."

* * *

They grounded the *Cuervo* on a gravel bar five kilometers downstream to take stock, and the stock was ugly.

Half the food gone with the broken crates. The medical kit reduced to a roll of bandage and a bottle of antiseptic. Two of the four fuel drums intact. Bram sat on a rock looking older than he had that morning, and Wren was wrapping Teo's forearm where a crate edge had opened him up — a long shallow gash, ugly but clean.

Teo himself wasn't crying, which Marisol noted, and wasn't showing off about not crying, which she noted harder. He just held his arm out and watched the canyon with the rest of them, like the wound was somebody else's problem happening nearby.

"Hold still," Wren told him.

"It doesn't hurt."

"Liar."

"It doesn't hurt *much*."

Wren tied off the bandage. Teo flexed his hand, tested it, and then — instead of going back to his corner of the boat — walked down the gravel bar toward Marisol and stood there with his jaw set, working up to something.

"You steered us through that," he said.

"I steer boats. It's the job."

"My uncle ran cargo out of San Esteban. He said nobody runs the Neck loaded more than twice and lives to be old about it."

"Your uncle sounds like a careful man."

"He drowned. In '09." Teo looked at her. "With the Herreras."

The gravel bar got very quiet. Marisol kept her eyes on the fuel drums and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't either open something or lie, and she'd done enough of both for one lifetime.

Teo didn't push it. He just crouched down next to the starboard drum, studied the fitting for a moment, and said, "This one's seeping. Where the bung sits. You'd lose it all by tomorrow night if you run hard."

Marisol crossed to him, checked, and found he was right — a hairline weep around the bung, invisible until you knew to look, which apparently this kid did. "Where'd you learn that?"

"Mission village. Somebody has to know which drums keep water and which ones kill you." He shrugged, defensive already, braced for the dismissal kids like him expected from adults like her. "It's not a big thing."

"It's the whole thing," Marisol said. "Fuel's the only cargo that matters now." She dug in the tool locker, found the spare gasket and wrench, and handed them to him. "Fix it. Both drums. Then you're crew, and crew eats first when we eat, and crew gets told things. Starting with: we're not stopping tonight, and tomorrow we run the Narrows at dawn, and after that there's a man named Dane Rook who wants all our heads, and I intend to disappoint him."

Teo took the wrench. His face did something complicated — surprise trying to become pride and not quite managing it — and he turned to the drums without another word, but his shoulders had come down from around his ears for the first time since she'd met him.

Wren appeared at Marisol's elbow. "You just adopted him."

"I hired him. There's a difference."

"There really isn't."

"Get away from me, cartographer." But she said it almost warm, and Wren smiled and went to check on Hazel, who was standing at the downstream end of the bar staring at the water like it owed him money.

Marisol looked back upstream. The canyon had swallowed the gunboat, but she could feel it anyway — the way you feel weather coming in your knees. Somewhere behind that bend, Rook was putting men ashore, finding his road, arguing with geography the way she argued with rivers. He'd win some of that argument. Good pilots always do.

She turned back to her boat — her patched, overloaded, half-starved boat full of strangers who were starting, against all her professional judgment, to feel like passengers instead of cargo.

That was the dangerous part. Cargo you can jettison.

"Dawn," she called out. "Everyone aboard. We run the Narrows before the wind comes up, and we don't stop until the junction."

Bram raised a hand from his rock. "What's at the junction?"

Marisol looked at Wren, and Wren looked at the compass in her hand, and the compass — needle trembling, pointing not north but upriver, up-canyon, toward whatever waited at the source — seemed to answer for all of them.

"Trouble," Marisol said, and swung aboard, and took the wheel.
