## 9. Bram's Confession

The fire was small on purpose. Old Bram built it that way — a fistful of greasewood in a ring of stones, tucked into a fold of the canyon where the light wouldn't carry. Thirty years ago he'd have laughed at such caution. Thirty years ago he'd have built a bonfire and roasted a goat over it and sung until the coyotes complained.

Thirty years ago he'd been a different man, or the same man wearing a better face.

He sat with his back against a boulder and watched the others settle around the flames. Marisol had beached her boat below the bend, hull scraped but whole, and she was going through her salvaged supplies with the grim arithmetic of a woman counting what the river had taken from her. Teo had already eaten his share and was pretending he hadn't, eyes half-closed, chin dropping toward his chest. The boy had run the Serpent's Neck rapids like he'd been born to it, and now he slept like a stone, because that was the bargain youth made with terror: spend it fast, sleep it off.

Hazel Sandoval sat apart, as he always did, cleaning a rifle he kept cleaner than his own hands. And Wren Okafor sat closest to the fire, the brass compass open in her lap, turning it over and over like a rosary.

Bram had been watching her do that for three days. He knew what it meant when a person handled an instrument that way. It wasn't devotion. It was penance.

*You too,* he thought. *I know you too.*

The geologist's kit he'd carried for thirty years weighed less than the thing he'd left out of it. That was the joke he'd never told anyone. All those core samples, all those assay reports, all those careful measurements in their careful cases — and the heaviest cargo he'd ever hauled was invisible. It didn't rattle. It didn't shift. It just rode in him, mile after mile, year after year, getting no lighter at all.

Marisol looked up from her inventory. "We're short," she said. "Rapids ate the fuel drums and half the food. Two days to the source gorge if we push hard. Three if we eat."

"Then we eat," Hazel said, not looking up from the rifle.

"Two days is fine," Wren said. "If we're right about where the river goes underground—"

"We're right," Bram said.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't meant to speak. The words had come up on their own, the way water comes up through cracked ground, finding the fault line whether you want it or not.

"You've seen it?" Wren leaned forward.

"I've drawn it." Bram turned his hands over, palms up, as if the maps might still be printed there. "Every contour. Every stratum. I drew the gorge before any of us ever saw it, girl. Twenty-two years ago." He paused. "And then I drew it wrong."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, the river muttered over its rocks, patient as everything else out here.

"What do you mean, wrong?" Hazel asked. His voice was flat, ranger-flat, the voice of a man who'd learned that questions were safer than accusations. But his hands had stopped moving on the rifle.

Bram took a long breath. It tasted like dust and smoke and the inside of his own mouth, which for twenty-two years had tasted like this moment approaching.

"I mean I falsified my reports," he said. "All of them. Every reading, every survey, every map I signed for the Caldera Consolidated Mining and Water Company. I mapped the largest aquifer in the territory and then I buried it on paper so thoroughly that even I sometimes believed it wasn't there."

Teo's eyes were open now. Nobody spoke. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Start at the beginning," Hazel said quietly. "Or don't start at all. But if you start, finish."

So Bram started.

* * *

"I was young," he said. "That's not an excuse. It's just where the story starts, and stories should be honest about their beginnings or they're worthless.

"I came out here in '61 with a degree from a university that had taught me everything about rock and nothing about men. The consortium hired me before I'd finished unpacking. They wanted the upper basin surveyed — water table, flow rates, recharge zones. Standard work. Good work. The kind of work I'd dreamed about since I was Teo's age, poking at stones behind my father's house, wondering what held the water under the world.

"And I found it. God help me, I found it. A spring-fed aquifer the size of a sea, sitting under the headwaters like a coin at the bottom of a well. Enough water to green every valley from here to the border. Enough to end the drought before the drought ever got its name. I stood on the rim of that hidden gorge with my instruments singing and I thought: *this is why I came here. This is what I'm for.*"

Wren's hands had gone still on the compass. "And then?"

"Then I flew home to present my findings, and a man met me at the airfield. Not a thug. Worse than a thug — a gentleman. Silver hair, soft voice, briefcase full of paper. He told me the company had reviewed my preliminary data and reached certain conclusions. He told me the aquifer's yield estimates were, regrettably, overstated. He told me that a report confirming a major water source would trigger land-use protections, federal review, homestead claims — years of litigation, millions in costs. But a report showing marginal yields, seasonal fluctuation, unreliability—" the old man's mouth twisted, "—that would let the company buy up the basin cheap while everyone above ground fought over scraps.

"He offered me a number. A very large number. And he offered me something else, which was worse: he offered me the work itself. Twenty more years of surveys, expeditions, canyons, instruments. My name on the science. My life exactly as I'd imagined it — except bent, from the first line, around a lie.

"I told him no." Bram smiled, and there was nothing in it. "I remember telling him no. I remember meaning it. And I remember flying back out two weeks later with revised figures, because between the no and the flight home there had been other conversations, other numbers, and a quiet word about how difficult it is for a disgraced geologist — one who'd fudged a site report early in his career, nothing serious, a small thing, a youthful error they happened to have documentation of — to find work anywhere in the territory again.

"They had me before I knew I was caught. That's how it works. They don't buy your soul outright. They find the debt you already owe and they buy *that*, and then you work it off forever, and the interest compounds, and every year the principal gets bigger because now there are new lies stacked on the old ones, and each one would ruin you alone, and together they'd bury you."

"The drought," Wren said. Her voice was very controlled, the way voices get when they're holding something heavy by a thread. "The villages upstream. The dead orchards. The wells going dry. You're saying—"

"I'm saying the drought is partly real and partly manufactured, and nobody downstream can tell the difference, which was the whole design." Bram stared into the fire. "The spring feeds the aquifer; the aquifer feeds the seeps and the side-canyon streams. The consortium has been pumping the upper basin dry for two decades through wells they registered under eleven different names. Slow enough that it reads as climate. Fast enough that the ranchers sell. Every family that walks away from dead ground, the company buys for pennies. Every title that clears brings them closer to owning the one thing they can't legally claim yet — the source itself, and the land over it.

"They don't want to sell the water, girl. Water's leverage. They want to *cap* it. Control the flow, ration the flow, price the flow. A drought you can end is worth nothing. A drought only they can end is worth a fortune."

The silence after that went on a long time. Teo had pulled his knees up to his chest. Marisol had stopped her inventory entirely; she sat with a dented tin cup in her hands, knuckles white.

"My village," she said finally. "On the lower river. We blamed the sky." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Twenty years we blamed the sky. My father sold our boatyard because the fishery died and he thought God had turned his face away. Sold it to a man whose paperwork traced back to Caldera Consolidated. I ran contraband on this river for ten years and I never once thought to ask who'd turned off the tap."

"Now you know," Bram said.

"Now I know." She set the cup down with exaggerated care, the way people do when the alternative is throwing it into the dark. "And knowing it doesn't fill a single well. That's the part they never put in the confession, old man. Knowing changes nothing except what you carry."

"No," Bram agreed. "It doesn't fill anything. It just tells you who broke it."

* * *

Hazel hadn't spoken in a long time. When he did, his voice came low, from somewhere deeper than the ranger's flat tone.

"How many reports? Total."

Bram considered the question honestly, because the man deserved honesty and because Bram had spent twenty-two years practicing the arithmetic anyway. "Fourteen major surveys. Forty-some supplementary filings. Three expert testimonies — one of them in a land dispute where a widow named Ferro lost her ranch to the company. She hanged herself the following spring. I read about it in a newspaper I still keep, though I couldn't tell you why."

"And the crew." Hazel's eyes came up. "Okafor's crew. The survey team that went upriver last month. Were they looking for your aquifer?"

"They were looking for anomalies," Wren answered for him, quietly. "Readings that didn't match the published record. My partner — Tomás — he'd found Bram's old field notes in a university archive. The original notes. Before the revisions." She looked at Bram, and there was no accusation in her face, which was somehow harder to bear than accusation would have been. Only exhaustion, and a question. "Your raw data survived. That's how we found the gorge. Your honest numbers, the ones you wrote down before you were made to unwrite them."

Bram nodded slowly. Something in his chest cracked along an old seam. "I kept the originals," he said. "Carbon copies, sealed, mailed to myself, postmarked, never opened. A coward's insurance. I told myself if I ever found the courage I'd publish them, and I told myself that for so long that keeping them became its own kind of sin — proof I *could* have done right, filed away next to the proof I didn't."

"That's why they killed them." Teo's voice, thin in the dark. Everyone turned. The boy's eyes were huge in the firelight. "The armed men. They didn't kill the survey crew because of the compass. They killed them because the crew found the spring, and the spring proves the reports are lies, and the lies are worth more than the water."

Out of the mouths of stowaways. Bram felt the truth of it land on the whole circle like a dropped weight. The boy had said in two sentences what the old man had needed twenty-two years and four hundred words to say.

"Yes," Bram said simply. "That's the shape of it. Which means every one of us sitting at this fire is now carrying the same cargo I've been hauling alone since before that boy was born. The consortium can't let us reach the source. Can't let us document it. Because documentation is the one thing their money can't unwrite — once it's in the public record, the titles fail, the sales reverse, the whole beautiful machine comes apart."

"So they'll send Rook," Hazel said. "Again. With more boats."

"With everything," Bram said. "I know how they think. I helped build how they think. They'll stop treating this as a retrieval and start treating it as a containment. And containment—" he spread his hands, "—has simpler arithmetic than retrieval. Fewer variables."

Marisol snorted. "Meaning they'll shoot us on sight instead of trying to arrest us. Wonderful. Anyone else have confessions tonight, or shall we all just write our wills and turn in?"

But she said it without heat, and when she looked at Bram across the fire, what was in her face wasn't contempt. It was recognition — the look of one person who'd spent years on the wrong side of the law seeing another one admit it out loud.

"Why tell us now?" Hazel asked. It wasn't hostile. It was the question of a man who counted things. "Three days on the river together. Why talk tonight?"

Bram looked into the fire for a while before answering.

"Because tomorrow we go into the gorge," he said. "And whatever's down there, I won't come out of it the man I've been. Either the truth gets out and my name goes with it — disgrace, testimony, maybe a cell, I've earned all three — or we die down there and the truth dies with us and none of it matters. Either way, tonight is the last night I get to be the version of me that kept it secret. I figured I ought to spend it clean."

He reached into his coat — slowly, deliberately, so the rifle across Hazel's knees wouldn't mistake the motion — and drew out an oilcloth packet, soft with age, tied with waxed string. He set it on the stone between them.

"Original field notes. 'Sixty-one through 'sixty-three. Unrevised. Signed." He pushed it an inch toward the firelight. "It's not much. Paper never is. But paper is what titles are made of, and paper is what breaks them."

Wren picked it up like it might crumble. Maybe to her it was holy. To Bram it was just twenty-two years of cowardice, finally set down, and his shoulders felt strange without the weight — lighter and wrong at once, like a man who'd carried a pack so long his body had grown around it.

Hazel looked at the packet, then at Bram, for a long moment. Then he nodded once, and went back to his rifle, and that was all the verdict there was. No absolution. No condemnation. Just a man deciding the cargo was worth carrying forward.

"Sleep," Hazel said. "First watch is mine."

They banked the fire and rolled into their blankets. Teo went down instantly. Marisol lay facing the river, one hand on her boat line. Wren sat up a while longer, the oilcloth packet in her lap beside the compass, two relics of other people's sins.

Bram lay on his back and looked at the stars through the canyon notch, and for the first time in twenty-two years the sky looked enormous instead of watchful.

He was nearly asleep when Hazel's hand closed on his shoulder — gentle, but awake, urgent.

"Bram." The ranger's voice was barely a breath. "Fire's reflected in the water downstream. Somebody's running a lamp without a shade."

Bram came up on his elbow. Far down the black ribbon of the river, small and steady, a point of light swung gently at anchor — too far to identify, too close to ignore.

Nobody anchored on this stretch of the river. Nobody good, anyway.

"Get Marisol," Hazel whispered. "Quietly. We may need to be on the water before that lamp decides what it's looking at."
