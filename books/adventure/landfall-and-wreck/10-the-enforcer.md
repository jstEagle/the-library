## 10. The Enforcer

The gunboat's cabin smelled of diesel and old coffee, and Dane Rook had learned to find a kind of home in both.

He sat with the radio handset in one hand and a photograph in the other — not the photograph he was supposed to be looking at, the survey crew manifest with its red X's scrawled across five of seven names, but an older one, creased soft as cloth from years in a breast pocket. Two men in ranger greens squinting into a sun that had not yet burned everything it touched. Rook on the left, younger by fifteen years and forty pounds of bitterness. Hazel Sandoval on the right, grinning like the desert owed him something and had just paid up.

"Rook." The voice through the handset was dry as ledger paper. Mr. Vane did not waste syllables; syllables cost money, and money was the only language the consortium spoke fluently. "Status."

"The survivor and the ranger are moving upstream," Rook said. "Slow. They picked up a smuggler's boat at the bend below Serpent's Neck — Marisol Vega, if the river gossip is worth what I paid for it. Lost them at the rapids. Found them again this morning."

"How?"

Rook set the photograph down, face-up, where the cabin lamp could reach it. "I know how Sandoval thinks. He'll run the high water channels because they're fastest, but he'll camp on the low banks because his fuel is short and his conscience is long. He'll stop for people. He always stops for people." A pause, and then, because Vane would ask anyway: "It's a liability. His. Ours too, eventually, but his first."

"And the compass?"

"Not recovered. The woman has it hidden somewhere on her body or her boat, and she's not the type to surrender it while she's breathing. Which is why we're going to arrange for her to stop breathing."

Vane was quiet for three seconds. In Rook's experience, three seconds of Vane's silence meant the man was recalculating costs. "The board prefers discretion. Bodies downstream are headlines. Headlines are—"

"I know what headlines are." Rook rubbed the bridge of his nose. Fifteen years of fixing problems for men who never dirtied their hands, and they still explained messes to him like he was the new hire. "No bodies. I heard you the first time, and the second time, and every quarterly review since. Accidents only. The river does our work for us, and the river doesn't leave notes."

"The spring stays buried until the titles clear. That is the objective. Everything else is noise management."

Noise management. Rook almost laughed. Somewhere behind him, in the dark water off the stern, there were six surveyors who had been noise, once. He'd signed the order that made them disappear, and he'd done it without losing sleep, because sleep was another thing he'd learned to live without. But he didn't laugh. Laughing was for men whose hands were clean.

"Understood," he said. "One more thing. I'm opening a bounty."

"We pay your crew adequately—"

"Not my crew. The river people." He could picture Vane's face doing whatever Vane's face did instead of expressing emotion — some small muscular adjustment around the eyes, the expression of a man finding a hair in his soup. "Every fishing skiff between the gorge and the delta. Every fuel dock, every ferry crossing, every kid selling ice out of a cooler. The ranger can't move without buying gas or food or goodwill, and goodwill on this river is cheaper than gas. Five hundred a head for information leading to recovery. A thousand for the compass itself, no questions about condition."

"That will generate false reports. Chaos."

"It will generate coverage," Rook said. "You want two fugitives found in four hundred miles of canyon? You don't send men. You send money. Money recruits better than any payroll I've ever managed, and it never sleeps, and it never needs feeding." He let that sit. "Besides, the river people already hate us. Might as well rent them."

Another silence. Longer. Then Vane said, "Approved. Within budget. And Rook—" the voice dropped half a register, which for Vane was practically a confession, "—the Sandoval matter. Handle it personally. If he reaches the source, if he documents anything, the entire acquisition timeline collapses. The board has asked me directly whether you're the right instrument for this."

*Whether you're the right instrument.* Not *whether you're the right man*. Instruments got replaced when they went flat.

"I'm the right instrument," Rook said, and killed the handset before anything else could be taken from him.

* * *

He slept badly, which was standard, and dreamed of the raid at Cañon Verde, which was also standard, though he'd have cut out his own tongue before admitting either to anyone.

Fifteen years ago. A joint operation — rangers and consortium security, back when the consortium still bothered with the fiction of partnership, back before it simply bought the sheriff's office outright. A smuggling ring running water out of protected headwaters, selling it to villages that couldn't afford the legal price. Rook had planned the breach. Hazel Sandoval had been his point man, his friend, the one guy on the force who'd eat cold beans with him and talk about nothing until the fear went out of their voices.

The intel had been wrong. Or the intel had been right and someone upstairs had wanted it wrong — Rook had spent years deciding which story he could live inside, and had finally settled on neither, because both of them ended with the same image: the flash-bang going through the wrong window, the fuel cache igniting, the fire climbing the canyon wall toward the village above, and Hazel Sandoval standing in the smoke making a choice.

Pull back. Save the crew. Let the village burn.

Or push up, alone, into a burning building to get the families out, and damn the operational integrity of the whole raid.

Sandoval had pushed up. Had come out coughing black with a child under each arm, a hero, a legend, a name the river towns still said like a prayer. And the raid — blown, exposed, every smuggler scattered, every piece of evidence burned — had died in the fire with the village. Someone had to be accountable. Someone junior enough, expendable enough, close enough to the failure.

They'd ended Rook's career for it. Quietly. No charges, just a reassignment to a desk, then a resignation "offered," then a phone call from a consortium recruiter who knew exactly which wounded animals made the best working dogs. Fifteen years of fixing things since. Fires started instead of put out. Names crossed off manifests.

And Sandoval had never once called. Never visited. Never said *I'm sorry they used you as the price of my halo*. Just kept being the hero, wearing the badge Rook used to wear, patrolling the same flats, probably telling the same stories.

That was the part Rook came back to, in the bad hours. Not the career. Not even the fire. The silence after. You can forgive a man for ruining you. It's harder to forgive him for not noticing.

He got up, poured coffee that tasted like the pot it came from, and stood at the wheelhouse glass watching the canyon walls slide past in the pre-dawn gray. Somewhere ahead, maybe thirty miles, maybe fifty, Hazel Sandoval was sleeping on a low bank next to a woman who'd watched her friends die, and he was probably — Rook knew him, god help him, he still knew him — taking the first watch so she could rest. Doing the decent thing. Being the decent thing. It was the most infuriating thing about him, that the decency wasn't performance. Rook had checked, over the years. He'd looked for the seam, the cynicism underneath, the moment the hero mask slipped. There was nothing. Just a broke, debt-ridden ranger who kept choosing the hard right over the easy wrong, and hated himself for wanting otherwise, and chose it anyway.

"You should've stayed a smuggler," Rook murmured to the glass. "Would've been easier on both of us."

Behind him, the radio crackled. One of his scouts, posted at the fuel dock at Hondo Bend. "Base, this is Pelican. Skiff matching the description bought diesel at oh-four-hundred. Four aboard, count 'em, four — two men, a woman, and a kid. Kid was asking about the gorge road."

Four. So the smuggler and the geologist were confirmed — Bram Kessler, the old falsifier, come home to roost. Rook filed it. A kid asking about roads was more interesting. Kids talked. Kids could be bought for less than five hundred.

"Pelican, base. Do not approach. Shadow only. Report heading changes hourly." He keyed off and pulled the river chart toward him, tracing the route with a blunt finger. Upstream past Hondo Bend the canyon narrowed. Two ways through: the main channel, deep and slow, or the old mission cutoff, shallow, rocky, and — this was the point — overlooked by the ridge where Rook had already positioned two men with rifles and a satellite phone.

Sandoval would take the main channel. Sandoval played it safe with other people's lives; it was the one kind of gambling he allowed himself. Which meant Rook would hold the ridge position through tomorrow, and if the skiff appeared in the cutoff anyway, it would mean something had changed in the man — something worth knowing.

Either way, the net was closing. He could feel it the way old fishermen felt weather, in the scar tissue.

* * *

At noon he stopped at the ferry crossing at Las Uvas, a settlement of tin roofs and rope bridges that existed because the river permitted it and would unmake it someday without malice. He came ashore alone, unarmed visibly, wearing plain clothes, because he'd learned long ago that menace traveled best in modest packaging.

The ferryman was a heavy man named Cordero who'd sold information twice before and pretended each time was the last. Rook sat on the planks beside him, shared his tobacco, and laid a fold of bills on the wood between them — real money, consortium-printed, spendable anywhere the law reached.

"Two travelers," Rook said. "A tall ranger, gray at the temples. A woman, dark-skinned, carries herself like a soldier. Possibly others. Boat's a green-hulled skiff, patched portside."

Cordero looked at the money the way hungry men look at bread. "And if I see them?"

"You send word downriver. There's a man at the Hondo Bend dock, calls himself Pelican. Anything you pass him gets you five hundred. More if it leads to recovery." Rook stood, brushed off his trousers, and added, lightly, the way you'd mention the weather: "There's also a standing arrangement for anyone who helps them instead. The consortium remembers names, Cordero. We remember them the way a flood remembers a house."

He walked back to the boat without waiting for an answer. Behind him he could feel the settlement's attention crawling over his back — fear doing its quiet arithmetic, every family weighing five hundred dollars against their neighbors' lives and finding, mostly, that the math worked. That was the thing about the river people that outsiders never understood. They weren't brave. They were brave *when they could afford it*, and the drought had spent the last of everyone's account.

By evening the bounty was seeded at nine landings. By midnight, Pelican reported the skiff had passed Hondo Bend without stopping — running dark, conserving fuel, headed for the narrows.

Rook stood in the wheelhouse with the chart light on and the canyon black beyond the glass, and took out the old photograph one more time. Two young men grinning at a future that had only room for one of them.

"Hazel," he said to the picture, conversationally, the way you'd speak across a campfire to a friend. "You're going to make me do this the hard way. I know you are. You're going to stop for somebody, or shelter somebody, or go back for something you left behind, because that's who you are, and it's going to put you exactly where I need you."

He turned the photograph face-down.

"And when it's done," he told the empty cabin, "maybe one of us finally gets to sleep."

Ahead, the narrows waited — and in the narrows, unknown to him, a boy with quick hands was already rerouting a river.
