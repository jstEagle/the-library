## 11. Village of Ash

We smelled it before we saw it.

Marisol cut the engine a half mile downstream and the smell came across the water anyway — wet char and something sweeter underneath that I didn't want to name. Smoke from a burned building holds its stink for days, especially when the burning was done slow, with diesel poured on doorframes instead of a clean torch through the roof.

"San Rafael," Bram said quietly. He was crouched at the bow with his hands wrapped around a tin cup he wasn't drinking from. "Fishing village. Maybe two hundred people when I came through here, back when I still had a company badge in my pocket."

"You've been here?" Teo asked.

"I've been everywhere the consortium wanted water rights." Bram's voice had gone flat, the way it did when he talked about the years he didn't like to count. "I never stopped here. We flew over."

"Flew over," Marisol repeated, and spat into the river.

I stood at the tiller and looked at the bend ahead, where the current slid around a gravel bar and the tops of trees showed black against the morning haze. Burned trees don't fall right away. They stand there like witnesses nobody will swear in.

"We could go around," Wren said. She was beside me at the stern, her rifle slung across her back, the brass compass hanging open in her hand out of habit more than need. She'd been checking it every hour since Serpent's Neck, as if the source might move if she blinked. "Push through to the gorge. Every hour we sit is an hour Rook gains."

"And every hour we run is another village that burns because somebody saw our boat go past and didn't say so." I heard my own voice come out harder than I meant it to. Wren looked at me, and I made myself look back. "That's how this works, isn't it? That's what Rook's bounty bought. He doesn't have to find us. He just has to make sure everyone between here and the source knows what happens to people who help us."

Nobody answered. The river made its small sounds against the hull. Teo sat on the supply crates with his knees pulled up, fourteen years old and trying to make his face unreadable, which is a skill kids from river villages learn early or not at all.

"We stop," I said. "An hour. We see if anyone needs anything, and then we go."

Marisol sighed through her nose, but she turned the skiff toward the bend.

* * *

The village had been built on stilts along the bank, half of it over the water, the way river people build when they trust the river more than the land. Now half of it was charcoal and collapsed tin, and the other half stood empty, doors open, cooking pots still on cold hearths. The fire had come in the night, three days ago by the softness of the ash, and it had started at the north end — the end facing upstream. Facing us.

"They gave them a choice," Wren said. She'd gone ahead of me along the walkway, moving like a soldier now, checking corners. She'd learned that in nine days of being hunted, and I hated that she'd had to learn it. "Inform or burn."

"That's the arithmetic." I stepped down onto the mud and felt it give under my boots. "Rook doesn't spend bullets when fear does the work for free."

There were bodies. Two, laid out under woven mats at the edge of the square, with candles burned down beside them. Someone had stayed long enough to bury their dead properly and then gone. I knelt and lifted the corner of one mat, because it was my job to look at things other people couldn't, and I made myself memorize the face underneath. Middle-aged man. Hands calloused in the pattern of a net-mender. There was a rope burn around his wrists that told me he hadn't died in the fire. He'd died first, and slowly, and the fire was the message sent after.

I put the mat back down the way I'd found it and stood there for a moment with my fists closed.

My whole career I'd thought about debt in terms of money. Numbers on a ledger, my brother's ledger, four thousand two hundred and sixty pesos that had followed me from his grave into every decision I'd made for six years. I knew exactly what it cost a person to owe something they couldn't pay. I'd built my whole life inside that knowledge.

But standing in that square, I understood that I'd only ever seen debt from above. The collectors came to my door; I paid; the numbers went down. What I was looking at was the other side of the machine. This was what debt looked like when you were too poor to pay cash — when the consortium lent you your life and the interest was your silence, and when you finally refused to pay, they collected in fire.

"Hazel." Wren's voice, low, from the far walkway. "Over here. You should see this."

* * *

They were under the floor.

The houses on stilts had crawl spaces beneath them, storage mostly, and someone had pried up a section of planking behind the third house from the water. Wren crouched at the opening with her rifle ready, and I dropped flat and looked into the dark, and a pair of eyes looked back.

A girl. Eight, maybe nine, filthy, hollow-eyed, pressed against the far post with a machete held in both hands in front of her like a shield. Behind her, in the black, I could hear breathing — more than one set of lungs.

"It's all right," I said, and kept my voice level, the voice I used on spooked livestock and spooked people, which are nearly the same thing. "We're not with them. We're running from them, same as you."

The machete didn't waver. The eyes didn't blink. Then a small voice from deeper in the dark said, in accented border Spanish, "Ask him his name."

I almost laughed, except nothing about it was funny. They'd survived three days under a floor by trusting no one. So I told them. Name, rank, the number on my badge, the names of everyone on my boat. And then the girl with the machete said, "Say the ranger oath. All of it. My tía says the bad ones can't say all of it."

So I knelt in the mud outside a burned village and recited the ranger's oath, word for word, including the part about protecting the people of the watershed from harm, *from all harm*, the part I'd been skating around for six years without noticing. Somewhere around the third line, the machete lowered. Around the fifth, a hand reached out of the dark and dragged the planking wider, and they came up into the light blinking: eleven of them. Three old women, a man with a bandaged arm, five children, and a boy of maybe sixteen holding the machete now, though he held it like he'd never swung it at anything bigger than kindling.

The oldest woman looked at my badge for a long time. Then she looked at Wren, at Marisol coming up the bank, at Teo standing frozen on the walkway with an expression I couldn't read yet.

"You're the ones," she said. "The boat they described. The ones who wouldn't stop."

"Yes," I said.

"Then get off your knees and come help us, Ranger. We have wounded, and the man who dressed that arm didn't know what he was doing."

* * *

Her name was Abuela Inés, though nobody called her anything else, and she ran what was left of San Rafael the way a keel runs a boat — invisible, essential, unarguable. She marched me to the church, which hadn't burned because the fire had been aimed, not random, and inside, on pews pushed together, lay the wounded. Seven of them. The man with the arm — Felix, the boy's father — had taken a bullet through the forearm the night of the fire, and the dressing on it had been cloth boiled in river water, which is better than nothing and not much better.

I'd treated gunshot wounds before. Desert rangers do everything: search and rescue, livestock disputes, the occasional shooting, and you learn field medicine the way you learn everything else out there — badly, fast, and from a manual you keep in the truck. I cleaned the wound, dug for the fragment with forceps sterilized over an alcohol flame while Felix bit down on a leather strap and his son held his shoulders, and packed it, and splinted it, and told him he'd keep the arm if infection stayed away, which was true, and that it would hurt for months, which was also true.

He watched me work with his jaw tight and finally said, "You're paying for supplies out of your own pocket, aren't you? I know what a ranger's kit carries. That's not it."

"I am."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and deserved a fair answer, so I gave him the honest one. "Because I'm tired of being broke in ways that don't matter. Money's just the shape my debt takes. Yours took a different shape." I tied off the last knot. "Same creditor, though."

Abuela Inés fed us all — rice and dried fish and a jar of pickled things that Marisol declared the best thing she'd eaten in a year, which from Marisol was practically a marriage proposal. We ate in the church because it was the only roof left with no holes in it, and while we ate, the story came out in pieces, the way these stories always do.

Three nights ago, a boat had come upstream flying no colors, and a man had stood in the bow with a loudspeaker. He hadn't threatened anyone, exactly. He'd explained, very reasonably, that strangers would be passing soon, fugitives, criminals, and that anyone who gave them food or fuel or directions would be considered an accomplice. And anyone who reported them would receive five hundred pesos per head. He'd said it twice, politely, and left a printed sheet nailed to the church door with the numbers on it, and then he'd waited until dark to see who came to take the sheet down.

Felix had taken it down. That was all. That was the whole crime. He'd walked to the church door in front of forty witnesses and pulled a piece of paper off a nail, and for that they shot him in the arm as a warning and his neighbor Tomás — the man under the mat — they'd worked over with rope and questions until dawn, and when Tomás still wouldn't say he'd seen any boat, they burned the north end of the village and left him for the fire.

"He never saw us," Wren said quietly. "We came through the night before, dark, no lights. He couldn't have told them anything even if he'd wanted to."

"He knew that," Abuela Inés said. "They knew that too. It wasn't ever about information, Ranger. It's about the next village hearing what happened here. Fear travels faster than boats." She refilled my cup without asking. "Five hundred pesos. Do you know what five hundred pesos is here? It's a season of nets. It's medicine for a grandchild. They priced us cheap on purpose, so that everyone would know they could afford to buy anybody. That's the real message. Not the fire. The price tag."

I looked down at my rice and thought about a ledger in a drawer in my barracks room, four thousand two hundred and sixty pesos, and the collection agency letters, and the way I'd spent six years believing my debt was the heaviest thing on this river.

It wasn't even close. My debt had a number. Theirs had a price list.

* * *

It was Teo who found them.

I noticed it later, reconstructing the afternoon — how he'd drifted off while the rest of us worked, how he'd come back with ash on his sleeves and his eyes red-rimmed and saying nothing. He was a mission kid, a stowaway we'd picked up two hundred miles downstream, and he'd never told us much about where he came from, only that there was nothing to go back to. I'd believed him. Kids lie about home the way soldiers lie about being scared.

But near sunset he came and found me where I was restocking my kit from Marisol's smuggled medical cache, and he stood there working up to it, and finally said, "The girl. Under the floor. The one with the machete."

"The one who made me say the oath."

"Her name's Luz." His voice cracked on it. "She's my cousin, Hazel. My aunt is one of the old women. This is — San Rafael is ten miles from where I grew up. I ran away two years ago and I never came back because there was nothing here for me, and now—" He stopped, jaw working. "Now there's less than nothing, and it's because of us. Because of the boat we're in."

I set down the forceps. "Teo—"

"Don't say it's not our fault. I know the math. I know Rook would've burned them anyway eventually, I've heard Bram say it, I'm not stupid." He wiped his face with his wrist, angry at himself for the tears. "But knowing the math doesn't make it weigh less. Does it? Nobody ever tells you that. Everybody acts like if you understand why a thing happened, it stops hurting, and it doesn't. It just hurts with footnotes."

I didn't have anything wise to say to that, so I said the true thing instead. "No. It doesn't weigh less. It shouldn't. The day it weighs nothing is the day you're the one nailing up the price list."

He looked at me for a long moment. Fourteen years old, and he nodded like a man agreeing to carry something heavy for a long distance.

"My aunt says Luz won't sleep unless someone she trusts is sitting up," he said. "Can I stay tonight? On shore, with them? I know it's safer on the boat, I know what you'll say—"

"I'll say yes." I said it before he finished, because some asks you answer fast or they turn into wounds. "And I'll say this: you sitting up with that girl tonight matters more than anything the rest of us did today. Including the surgery."

He ducked his head, embarrassed, and went, and I watched him cross the mud toward the crawl space where his family lived under a burned house, and I thought: there it is. That's the thing Rook's arithmetic can't price. Five hundred pesos buys informers. It doesn't buy a boy who walks toward the ashes instead of away from them.

* * *

We left at first light.

Abuela Inés wouldn't take money — "What would I buy?" she said, gesturing at the ruins — so we left what mattered: half the medical kit, all of Marisol's spare fuel except what we needed, and Bram's satellite phone, the one he'd sworn we might need for the gorge. He handed it over without being asked, and I caught his eye, and neither of us said anything about what that sacrifice meant, because men like Bram and villages like this understand each other without narration.

At the landing, Inés gripped my forearm with a hand like a root.

"You're going to the source," she said. Not a question.

"We are."

"Then hear an old woman. When I was young, before the drought, my grandmother told me the river didn't start in the mountains. It started underneath, in a place the old people called the throat of the world, and it sang there, and you could put your ear to the stone and hear it." Her eyes were dry and fierce. "If men have put their hands on that place, Ranger, they haven't stolen water. They've stolen the song. And a people without their song will sell anything, because nothing belongs to them anymore. That's what the fire was. That's what the price list was. Understand what you're really going to get back."

I promised her nothing, because promises are debts and I've learned to be careful about signing those. But I held her grip a moment longer than I needed to.

Wren was waiting at the skiff with the compass open in her palm. As I stepped aboard, she tilted it toward me. The needle had shifted overnight — not much, a degree, maybe two — swinging off the bearing it had held steady since Serpent's Neck.

"It moved," she said. "Last time it did that, we were within a day of the gorge."

Marisol already had the engine turning over. Downstream of the village, the morning mist was lifting off the water, and somewhere upriver, past the next bend and the next, a hidden gorge waited with a spring under it and a wall around it and men with guns on top of the wall.

Six days ago this had been a missing-persons report. Yesterday it had been survival. Standing at the tiller with the smell of smoke still in my clothes and a boy asleep against a crate below decks, I felt the last of the holding pattern burn off me like mist.

This wasn't duty anymore, and it wasn't debt, and it wasn't even survival.

It was purpose, and it pointed upstream.
