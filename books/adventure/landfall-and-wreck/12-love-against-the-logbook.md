## 12. Love Against the Logbook

**HAZEL**

The anchor went down in a side channel the charts didn't name, a slough of black water behind a screen of cottonwoods where Marisol said the gunboat's sonar would read them as driftwood. She said it like a woman who had bet her life on worse odds and won most of them.

"Two hours' watch rotation," she said, killing the engine. "Nobody smokes. Nobody sings. Teo — that means you."

"I don't sing."

"You hummed for six miles back there."

"That was breathing."

Hazel let them argue. It was good, the arguing. It meant they were still alive enough to be annoyed with each other.

He took first watch because nobody argued about that. He climbed onto the skiff's cabin roof with his rifle across his knees and the incident log on his lap, and he watched the last light drain out of the canyon walls until the river ran the color of old tin.

The log was the problem.

He'd known it for three days now, carrying it against his ribs like a second badge. Every entry in it was true. That was what made it dangerous. *Recovered stolen property, one brass compass, modified. Detained subject Okafor, Wren, cartographer, sole survivor of the Calloway survey expedition.* True, all of it, and if he ever transmitted it — if he ever did the thing the badge on his chest told him to do — Wren would spend the rest of her short life in a consortium-built courtroom while the men who murdered her crew testified as expert witnesses.

He could end this tonight. That was the thought that wouldn't leave him alone. One radio call, coded, official: *fugitive located, evidence secured, requesting extraction.* Rook's hunt collapses into a manhunt for a thief instead of a war over a spring. Hazel goes home. The debt collectors get their schedule back. Everything returns to the holding pattern he'd been flying for nine years.

All he had to do was hand her over.

"Hazel."

Wren stood at the base of the ladder, a tin cup in each hand. Steam rose off both of them. Behind her, the campfire Bram had insisted on — small, screened, reckless — threw orange light up through the cottonwood branches.

"Marisol says you take your coffee like an apology," Wren said. "Bitter and too late."

"Marisol talks too much."

"She does." Wren climbed halfway up the ladder and handed him a cup. "She also hasn't slept in two days because she's been running watch so you could sleep. So maybe we let her talk."

He took the coffee. Their fingers touched around the tin, briefly, and neither of them made anything of it, which was its own kind of making something of it.

She didn't climb down. She stayed on the lower rungs, shoulder level with his boots, looking out at the water the way she'd looked at it every night since the mission — like she was reading it. Like it was a chart with something wrong in the margins.

"My partner used to say the river keeps its own records," she said. "Every flood, every drought, every body. It just doesn't write them down where anyone can read."

"Your partner built the compass."

"He did." A pause long enough to have weight. "They shot him third. I've counted the order so many times I've worn grooves in it. Reyes first, because he argued. Then Okonkwo, because he ran. Then Mateo, because he was standing next to me, and I think — I've decided to think — that he stepped between us on purpose."

Hazel set the cup down. "You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to. That's why I'm doing it." She turned her face up toward him. In the firelight her eyes were dry and very steady, the steadiness of someone who had cried everything out days ago in a flooded building with a rifle across her knees, waiting for footsteps. "I'm telling you because tomorrow you might make a decision about me. And I want you to make it knowing exactly what you'd be deciding about."

* * *

**WREN**

He didn't answer right away. That was the first honest thing he'd done since she'd met him — most men would have filled the silence with reassurance, cheap and fast, the way you'd throw a rope to someone drowning. Hazel just sat there with the steam coming off his cup and let the truth sit between them like a third person at the fire.

She'd watched him for days now. Watched him ration his own food into Teo's pack when he thought no one saw. Watched him listen to Bram's confession about the falsified aquifer reports without saying *you're under arrest*, which by every law Wren had ever read was exactly what the man deserved, and had not done it anyway, because some part of him understood that the law and the right thing had diverged somewhere upstream and hadn't rejoined yet.

Watched him check the logbook. That was the thing. Every night, same ritual: rifle down, coffee gone, logbook out, pencil hovering. Like a man praying to a god he wasn't sure was listening anymore.

Tonight he opened it.

"Read me your statement," he said quietly. "The one you'd give a tribunal. All of it."

So she gave it. The anomalous readings. The hidden gorge. The armed men with consortium markings who arrived on the fourth day, took samples, and executed eight people in the space of eleven minutes. The compass in her hands, warm from Mateo's pocket, pointing south-southwest at nothing any map agreed with. Eleven minutes. She'd counted those too.

When she finished, Hazel wrote for a long time. The pencil scratched in the dark, and she sat below him on the ladder and listened to the river move and thought: *this is the moment. This is where he writes me into custody.*

He closed the book.

Then he did something she didn't understand until he'd already done it. He tore the pages out. Carefully, along the binding, one by one — the whole record of her, of the expedition, of the compass, of every decision he'd made since the radio crackled in chapter one of all this — and held them out over the black water.

"Wren Okafor," he said, "is not in my jurisdiction. Never has been. The Calloway expedition was contracted through the territorial land office, which means any crime against it is federal, which means my report would be hearsay anyway." His voice was flat, reciting, and she realized he was giving himself legal cover the way she gave herself map cover — drawing a border around the truth so he could live inside it. "There's nothing to arrest anyone for. There never was."

"Hazel—"

"There's just a ranger escorting a witness upstream." He let the pages go.

They caught the wind for half a second, white rectangles tumbling over the current, and then the river took them the way it takes everything — without malice, without ceremony, keeping its own records in its own way.

* * *

**HAZEL**

He felt lighter and heavier at once, which he hadn't known was possible. Nine years of entries, of procedure, of *the log is the law and the law is the only thing between you and your brother's creditors* — gone in ten seconds of paper on water.

His brother would have laughed at him. Eli had never trusted institutions; he'd trusted people, badly, generously, one bad loan at a time, and it had killed him at thirty-four with his name on debts Hazel was still paying. Hazel had spent nine years being everything Eli wasn't. Careful. Documented. Honest in the way that costs nothing because it's enforced.

And it had bought him exactly nothing. The badge kept him broke. The honesty kept him alone. And the debt — the debt just grew, patient as the river, indifferent as the consortium.

But sitting on the cabin roof with the ashes of his career drifting downstream, he found he wasn't afraid. He was furious, actually, and underneath the fury something else, something that felt dangerously like relief.

Wren climbed the rest of the ladder and sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched. Neither of them moved away.

"You could still do it," she said. "Radio call. You remember the codes better than I do."

"I could."

"They'd erase your debt. Probably promote you. Rook would shake your hand."

"Rook doesn't shake hands. He counts fingers afterward."

She almost smiled. "So why aren't you calling it in?"

Because of the way she'd looked at him in the mission, rifle shaking, choosing not to shoot a man whose insignia she recognized. Because of Teo humming off-key for six miles. Because of Marisol betting her boat, Bram betting whatever was left of his soul, all of them betting on a stranger with a badge because he hadn't yet given them a reason not to.

"Because I've been paying other people's debts for nine years," he said. "Time I owed somebody something worth owing."

The words came out before he could weigh them, and once they were out he couldn't take them back, and he found he didn't want to.

Wren turned to look at him. Up close, in the dark, the exhaustion showed — the hollows under her eyes, the crack in her lip that hadn't healed right — but underneath it was the same steadiness he'd seen on the ladder, the steadiness of a woman who had walked out of an execution and kept walking, upstream, alone, for eleven days, because the truth needed a courier and she was the only one left to carry it.

"Hazel Sandoval," she said, "are you flirting or confessing?"

"Yes."

She laughed — quiet, careful of the sleeping crew below, but real, the first real laugh he'd heard from her, and it undid something in his chest that had been knotted since the flats.

Then she kissed him.

Or he kissed her. Later neither of them would be able to say, and neither of them would care. What he remembered was the taste of river water and tin-cup coffee, and her hand finding the front of his shirt, and the compass — the broken, lying, true-north-when-it-mattered compass — digging into his ribs between them where she'd tucked it for safekeeping, pressing its brass edge into his sternum like a small cold heart beating against his own.

Somewhere below, Teo shifted in his sleep and muttered something about singing. Somewhere downstream, a man wearing a dead friend's call sign was counting money and naming prices. Tomorrow there would be rapids, and walls, and a gorge where the river vanished into the earth with all its secrets.

But not tonight. Tonight there was an unnamed slough, a torn logbook, and the astonishing discovery that the future could be chosen rather than owed.

* * *

**WREN**

She woke before dawn to the sound of the radio.

Not the voice — just the carrier wave, a low hiss from the set below decks, the kind of static that meant someone, somewhere, was keying a transmitter and deciding whether to speak.

Hazel was already awake beside her. She felt him go still, the way a man goes still when he recognizes a sound from a bad dream.

Below them, in the grey light, Marisol stood over the radio with her arms crossed. Old Bram hovered behind her, glasses in hand. And Teo — Teo was holding up a scrap of paper, fishing it out of the radio's housing, his young face pale in the blue glow of the dial.

"It was wedged behind the panel," the boy said. "Someone put it there."

Hazel came down the ladder fast. Wren followed.

On the paper, in handwriting none of them knew, five words:

*WE KNOW WHERE YOU ANCHOR.*

Marisol looked up at Hazel, and her voice, when it came, was flat as the river at dawn.

"Somebody's been aboard."
