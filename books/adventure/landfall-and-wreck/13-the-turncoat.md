## 13. The Turncoat

The fire was small and wrong, and Hazel knew it before he knew why.

They'd made camp in a side canyon off the main channel, a slot of red rock deep enough to hide their light. But Marisol had built the fire the way she always did — a ring of stones, a reflector wall, the flames tucked low. And someone had moved the stones. Widened the ring. Let the light climb higher up the canyon wall than it needed to.

He didn't say anything. Not yet. He stood at the edge of the glow with a tin cup of coffee he wasn't drinking and watched the five of them the way he'd once watched a suspect across an interrogation table, and hated himself for it.

Wren sat cross-legged with the chart spread over her knees, redrawing the channel by firelight. Teo was asleep — or performing sleep — curled against the skiff's hull with his arm through the strap of the supply bag, the way he always slept, the way a boy slept who had learned early that things left unguarded stopped being yours. Old Bram sat apart, as he always sat apart, nursing his flask and his guilt in equal measure. Marisol stood at the canyon mouth with a cigarette, watching the dark water.

Five people. One of them had moved those stones. Or so Hazel had decided somewhere in the last hour, without evidence, without admitting it even to himself.

Because the shot had come at dusk. A single rifle round from the far rim, cracking off the rock thirty feet above Wren's head — not aimed to kill. Aimed to announce. *We see you. We're close.* And Rook's men did not find people in slot canyons off the main channel by luck. Not in one day. Not in this maze.

Somebody had told them.

* * *

Marisol saw it first, because Marisol saw everything first.

She came back from the canyon mouth at moonrise, dropped the cigarette and ground it under her heel with more force than the act required, and said, to the fire, to all of them, to no one: "Second boat. Quarter mile down. No lights. They're drifting."

The fire popped. Wren's pencil stopped.

"Drifting," Hazel said.

"Engine off. Riding the current like they've got all night." She turned, and the firelight caught the hard line of her jaw. "Which means they know where we are, and they're in no hurry."

Teo sat up slowly, his hand still through the bag strap. Bram didn't move at all. He was looking into the flames with the fixed attention of a man reading something written there.

"Nobody followed us in," Wren said. Her voice was level, but her knuckles had gone white around the pencil. "I watched the channel for an hour before we turned in. There was nothing on the water."

"There was nothing on the water," Marisol agreed. "So either Rook's got a divining rod, or—" She let it hang. She was good at letting things hang. She'd been letting it hang since Serpent's Neck, since the gunboat had found them at the rapids with a timing that still didn't sit right, and Hazel had watched her not say it, night after night, the way you watch a sore tooth.

"Or somebody's talking," Teo said.

The boy said it simply, the way children say the true thing the adults are circling, and the canyon went very quiet except for the river somewhere out in the dark, talking to itself.

* * *

It came apart over the next hour, the way things do — not all at once but in pieces, each one small, each one reasonable, each one a wedge.

Hazel tried to stop it. "We're tired and we're scared and that's exactly what Rook wants. A crew that turns on itself doesn't need chasing. It just needs collecting." He said it standing, cup in hand, badge-weight in his voice, and he watched it fail.

"Easy for you," Marisol said. "You've got a rulebook to fall back on. I've got a boat and a sister in Vado who Rook's people already visited once. You think I came up this river for the scenery?" She kicked a stone into the fire, hard, and sparks went up. "Somebody moved those fire stones. Don't look at each other like you didn't all see it. Somebody widened that ring so the light would show on the rim. So who?"

"I did."

They all turned. Bram.

The old man hadn't raised his voice. He was still looking at the fire, his flask forgotten in his hand, and when he spoke again his voice had the flat, tired quality of a man stepping off a ledge he'd been standing on for days.

"I moved the stones. Two nights ago. I told myself it was for the warmth." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "I'm an old man. I get cold. It was even true, the way most lies are true."

"You son of a—" Marisol started forward and Hazel's arm came up, not touching her, just there.

"Let him finish."

"I didn't signal anyone." Bram finally looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious, mostly at himself. "I want that understood. I moved the stones because I'm a coward and cowards like to leave doors open. But I never made contact. I never — " He stopped. His hand went to his pack, there by the rock, the old canvas rucksack he'd carried since the mission village, and Hazel saw the exact moment the thought arrived on the old man's face.

The moment he remembered what was in the pack. Or what had been.

Bram got up slowly, knees cracking, and dragged the rucksack into the light. He went through it with hands that were trying to be steady and failing. Bedroll. Tin plate. The geologist's hammer he'd carried for thirty years. The oilcloth packet of old survey notes he'd shown them outside the gorge, the falsified reports, his confession in his own handwriting.

He went through it twice. Then a third time, slower, turning every pocket inside out, shaking each one over the fire like a man shaking out a dead man's clothes.

"It's gone," he said.

"What's gone?" Wren asked, though her voice said she already knew.

"My credentials." Bram sat back on his heels. "My consortium field pass. The laminate, the photo, the authorization codes. The thing that says *Bramuel Ochoa, Senior Hydrologist, Caldera Consolidated Mining.* The thing that would open their checkpoints, their comms frequencies, their — " He closed his eyes. "I kept it to remind myself. That's what I told myself. A reminder of what I was."

"And what was it really?" Hazel asked quietly.

"A key," Bram said. "I kept it because part of me never left. And somebody in this canyon found it, and somebody in this canyon used it, and I will never be able to prove it wasn't me."

* * *

They turned on each other then, and it was ugly, and Hazel would remember the shape of it for years: how fast five people who had pulled each other out of rapids could become five suspects in a ring of firelight.

Marisol accused Bram first, of course — the stones, the key, the whole architecture of it, and Bram took it without defending himself because he'd stopped defending himself years ago and it looked, horribly, like guilt. Then Marisol turned on Teo — "the kid shows up out of nowhere, stows away, knows every back channel on this river better than I do" — and Teo came off the ground with his fists up and his voice cracking, and Wren got between them with her palms out.

Then, because paranoia is democratic, someone's eyes found Hazel.

It was Marisol again. She was crying with rage by then and didn't know it. "You. Ranger. You could end this tomorrow. One radio call — *I've got the witness, bring the boat.* You think I haven't thought about it? You think I haven't watched you thinking about it?"

"I tore up my log," Hazel said. "Chapter and verse. You watched me burn it."

"Paper burns. People don't change that fast."

"And you?" Wren said, turning, and her voice had gone quiet and cold, the voice she used on maps when the terrain didn't match the chart. "Your boat is the only one fast enough to have run a message downstream and back while we were at the gorge. You 'went fishing' for two hours. Alone."

"I was fishing."

"Nobody fishes during a manhunt."

"I fish when I'm scared, which is what normal people do, which is more than I can say for the rest of you—" and Marisol's hand came up and pointed, shaking, around the circle, and finished on Hazel, and the word she said was: "*Vasquez.*"

The name landed like a dropped engine part.

"You knew him," she said. "Your friend. Your mentor. The call sign. You knew his voice better than anyone on this river, and you're telling me you couldn't tell that transmission was wrong until the details slipped? You're telling me a man who loved you like a son couldn't hear his own ghost and know it was a stranger wearing him?" Her voice dropped. "Or did you know right away, and say nothing, because you wanted to see how we'd all run?"

Hazel stood very still. Because the terrible thing — the thing he'd never told any of them — was that she was half right. He *had* known sooner than he'd said. He'd known in the first four seconds of that transmission, in the pause before *Hazel*, the way Rook had pronounced it like a man reading it off a file. And he'd waited, and let the trap half-spring, because some cold ranger arithmetic in his chest had said *a known trap tells you who built it.*

"I knew," he said. "Not everything. But I knew early. And I didn't tell you, because I wanted to know who Rook was talking to. I thought — " He stopped. There was no version of it that sounded like anything but what it was. "I thought I could use you as bait."

Teo made a small sound. Wren looked at Hazel like he'd hit her, and he deserved the look, and the fire crackled into the silence, and out on the black water, a quarter mile down, the second boat drifted with its engine off, patient as a spider.

* * *

It was Wren who broke it, and she broke it by leaving.

Not far. She walked to the skiff and came back with her survey case, the battered aluminum one with the expedition's stencil still on the side, and set it down in the middle of the firelight with a click that silenced them all.

"You want to search someone? Search me. All of me. Then we search everyone, everything, tonight, in front of each other, and we do it now before Rook's boat finishes drifting." She knelt and opened the case. "Because I'll tell you what I know about paranoia. I watched eleven people catch it on that survey. Day by day. The water readings were wrong, then the food counts were wrong, then the man on watch was wrong. And every single day we trusted each other a little less and told each other a little less, and that is *exactly* what killed them. Rook didn't beat us with guns. He beat us by making us quiet." She looked around the circle. "I will not be quiet again. Search me."

Nobody moved.

"Search me, Marisol. You first. You've got the most to lose."

Marisol stared at her for a long moment, jaw working. Then she swore, low and filthy, and knelt, and went through the survey case with a smuggler's thoroughness — every compartment, every false bottom Wren didn't know she had. Nothing. Then Wren's pockets, her bedroll, her boots. Nothing but charts and a pencil worn to a stub and a photograph of the survey team, eleven faces, that she let Marisol hold for a moment longer than necessary.

"Teo," Wren said.

The boy flinched. But he came forward, and he emptied the supply bag himself, and he stood there shaking while Marisol went through it, and when she found nothing her face did something complicated — shame and relief and the loss of a certainty she'd been leaning on.

Then Hazel. Then Marisol herself, furiously, throwing her own things around like they'd insulted her.

Then Bram.

The old man knelt by his rucksack and didn't touch it. "I told you," he said. "It's already gone. Whoever took it took it days ago. Search it and you'll find nothing, and finding nothing proves nothing, and that's the design, don't you see? That's the *design.*" His voice cracked. "Rook doesn't need my pass anymore. He's had it for days. He doesn't need a traitor anymore either. He just needed us to believe in one."

Hazel crouched beside him. "Bram. The stones."

"I moved the stones." Bram looked at him, and his eyes were clear and wrecked and honest, and Hazel had spent twelve years reading men across a table, and every year of it said: *this one is telling the truth, and it doesn't matter.* "I moved the stones because I'm a coward. That's my whole crime. That and the reports. That and eleven graves I helped dig with a pencil." He looked down at his hands. "Search me anyway. I want you to. I want to be innocent the way other men are innocent — checked."

So Hazel searched him, gently, and found nothing, and stood up into a silence worse than the shouting.

* * *

"Then it's one of us," Teo said, "and it isn't any of us."

Everyone looked at him. The boy was standing by the supply bag with his arms wrapped around himself, and he was fourteen and exhausted and he had just been searched like a criminal by people he'd bled for at Serpent's Neck, and he was the only one in the canyon thinking clearly.

"Think about it," he said. "Rook's boat is a quarter mile down. It's been there since dusk. If the traitor was *here*, they wouldn't need to signal — they'd just wait for us to sleep. But nobody's coming up the canyon. They're just *drifting.*" He swallowed. "It's not a trap. It's a *show.* They want us to do exactly this. They want us awake and at each other's throats so we're no good tomorrow, or so we scatter, or so somebody bolts and leads them right to us. The pass wasn't stolen to be used. It was stolen to be *missed.*"

The fire popped. Marisol had gone very still.

"He's right," Wren said slowly. "It's what they did to the survey team. I told you — the paranoia came *before* the shooters. Weeks before. We did Rook's work for him." She looked at Hazel. "You said it yourself the first night. A crew that turns on itself doesn't need chasing."

Hazel looked around the fire — Marisol with her arms crossed and her trust in pieces, Bram kneeling by his empty pack like a man at a graveside, Teo standing his ground, Wren with the photograph of her dead eleven still in her hand. And he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that this was the actual battle. Not the rapids. Not the gunboat. This. Rook had figured out that the fastest way to kill a crew was to make them audit each other, and he'd spent one rifle round and one stolen laminate to do it.

"Here's what we know," Hazel said. "One: nobody in this canyon talked, because nobody in this canyon had to. Two: Rook has something of Bram's and he's using it to farm suspicion, which means he's worried about something — worried enough to spend effort breaking us instead of just shooting us. Three: that boat is drifting, not attacking, because it's waiting for us to do its job for it." He picked up a stick and drew a line in the dirt. "So we don't give it to them. We break camp in the dark, we run the channel quiet, and we go to the source tonight, ahead of them, and we finish this before we finish each other."

"And if there *is* a turncoat?" Marisol said. "If I'm wrong about all of you and right about one of you? What then, ranger? You got a procedure for that?"

"Yeah," Hazel said. "We stay together. That's the whole procedure. A traitor can't betray a crew that won't split."

Marisol looked at him for a long time. Then at the river. Then at her boat — her boat, the one thing in the world that was hers, the thing she'd said she'd leave in, more than once, when it got to be too much.

"I should go," she said quietly. "Any sane person would go. Take the boat, take my odds, let you four drown in each other's secrets." She crouched and picked up her keys. Held them. The firelight moved on her face and Hazel watched her decide, watched it cost her, watched her pay.

She threw the keys to Teo.

"Prep the boat," she said. "Quiet lines. And if I ever catch any of you moving my fire stones again, I'll shoot you myself and save Rook the ammunition."

It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't trust. It was something better — a decision, made with eyes open, in the dark, by people who had every reason not to.

They broke camp without lights. And as they slid the skiff into the black current, Teo at the lines, Marisol at the tiller, Hazel at the bow, Wren's hand found his in the dark and held on.

Behind them, a quarter mile down, the second boat's engine finally turned over — a sound like something waking up hungry.

They had perhaps ten minutes.

The source lay upstream, through the gorge, in the dark under the world.

They ran for it.
