## 14. Gorge of the Silent Spring

The compass stopped lying to us on the third morning.

I want to say it turned, but that's not right. It had never pointed north — not once since Teo found it in my pack and asked why the needle trembled like a scared animal. What it did was worse than turning. It *settled*. The needle swung through a slow arc and then held, dead steady, quivering at some bearing no map in the territory acknowledged. Bram checked it against his pocket transit twice, said nothing, and checked it again.

"That's not possible," he finally announced, to the river generally.

"Good," Marisol said from the tiller. "I was getting bored."

We'd been running the channel since dawn, past terraces of red rock so tall they swallowed the sky by mid-morning, leaving us in a corridor of shadow and brown water. The canyon had been narrowing for two days — the walls leaning in like conspirators, the current quickening under us as if the river itself knew where it was going and resented our company. Hazel sat at the bow with the compass open on his knee, calling bearings to Marisol in a low voice, and I watched him watch the water and thought: this is what he looks like when he's not paying off anyone's debt. When he's just a man doing the one thing he's good at.

The gorge opened without warning.

One moment we were in the canyon; the next the walls simply stepped back, sheared vertical, and rose a thousand feet into air so clean and blue it looked painted. The river spread wide and slow across a floor of pale stone, and the silence came down on us like a lid. No birds. No insects. Just the water moving over rock with a sound like someone breathing in another room.

"There," Bram said quietly. "Do you see it?"

I saw it. We all saw it. The river ran broad and confident to the head of the gorge — and then it didn't. It poured over a lip of black basalt and *stopped*. Not pooled, not slowed. Vanished. A hole in the world the width of a church door, swallowing the entire flow of the Río Blanco without splash or sound, as if the earth had opened its mouth and drunk a river whole.

Marisol brought the skiff alongside a shelf of stone and killed the engine, and the silence got louder.

"Underground," Teo said. He'd gone very still. "The whole river goes underground."

"The aquifer," Bram murmured. He was staring at the basalt with an expression I recognized from the nights around the fire, when he talked about numbers instead of names. "It's not feeding the river. The river is feeding *it*. Everything above us — every dry village, every failed farm from here to the border — and all of it pouring down into—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "They knew. God help me, they knew twenty years ago."

Hazel stood up in the bow. "Wren. Your chart."

I unrolled it on the thwart — my chart, the one I'd been redrawing in pencil since the mission, the honest one, the one that existed nowhere else. The compass bearing crossed it like a scar and terminated at a point I had labeled, in my partner Amara's careful hand before she died, with a single word: *SOURCE?*

We were standing on it.

* * *

We beached below the falls and climbed.

The way up was a goat track along the gorge wall, switchbacking through talus and creosote, and I climbed it with the compass in my hand like a rosary. Halfway up, Teo, who moved over broken ground the way water moves downhill, pointed ahead and made a small sound.

There were stakes in the ground.

Surveyor's stakes, steel-capped, driven into the rock in neat rows. And strung between them, following the contour of the gorge: wire fence. New wire, galvanized, unweathered. It ran along the rim as far as I could see in both directions, and at intervals hung signs enameled white and bolted facing outward, toward the empty air, toward nothing:

**RÍO BLANCO WATERSHED AUTHORITY — RESTRICTED AREA — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**

Bram read the sign twice. "There is no such authority," he said. "I would know. I invented half the paper ones."

"It's a claim," Hazel said. His voice had gone flat, ranger-flat, the voice he used on the radio when things were bad. "A land claim dressed up as a government office. They've claimed the source."

"They've claimed more than the source." I was looking down. From the rim, the gorge floor lay open beneath us, and I could see what the slow water had hidden: concrete. Piers of it, rising out of the shallows, connected by catwalks. A staging yard. Crates stacked under tarps. A crane rail running along the base of the far wall toward the black mouth where the river went under. And built against the cliff beside the vanishing point, half-hidden in the gorge's permanent shadow, a structure with loading doors and ventilation stacks and floodlights burning even now, in daylight, behind wire-reinforced glass.

A mine. Or something wearing a mine's clothes.

"How long?" Marisol asked. She wasn't asking me.

Bram answered anyway, because he couldn't stop himself, because guilt is a river too and it runs downhill. "The engineering takes years. The permits take longer — unless you write them yourself. Two decades, at least. They've been building this since before the drought got bad enough to matter." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "Before it got bad enough to *create*."

Nobody said anything. Below us the river poured into the dark, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

We found the survey camp an hour later, on a bench of flat ground near the rim, and I knew it before I saw the tents.

I knew it because I had drawn it. Amara's last transmission had included a sketch — a bench site, wind from the northeast, good sight lines, standard protocol. I had mapped her camp from her description while she slept in it, three hundred miles downstream, and told her the sight lines looked excellent, tell everyone Wren says good job.

The tents were still there. Three of them, collapsed and weathered gray, arranged exactly as I'd have drawn them. A fire ring. A instrument table with a theodolite still mounted on its tripod, the lens cap on, the way you leave it overnight when you expect to work in the morning.

That was the first wrong thing. You don't cap the lens if you're leaving in a hurry. You don't leave the tripod standing. And you don't—

Hazel crouched at the edge of the bench and lifted something with a stick. A boot. Then another. Five sets of remains, laid out with terrible tidiness among the tents, positioned as if they'd fallen where they stood. As if five people had been struck down simultaneously in the middle of ordinary evening tasks — one at the fire ring, one at the instrument table, two in the nearest tent, one halfway to the latrine trench, hand outstretched toward nothing.

"No," I said. Out loud. To no one. "No, that's not—"

Amara hadn't fallen at the fire ring. Amara had run. Her last radio call — thirty seconds of static and her voice saying *men coming up the wash, they're armed, get the compass out, Wren, get it out and go* — Amara had run northwest, away from the water, away from everything, and the boot prints I'd found days ago heading inland had been hers, and she had died running with my name in her mouth.

And here she lay, arranged at the fire ring like a prop in a play about a peaceful expedition that met a natural end.

"Gas," Bram said hoarsely. He'd gone gray. "Carbon dioxide seep. It happens in volcanic country. A pocket of heavy gas fills a low campsite at night and everyone just — goes to sleep." He looked at me and I watched him understand, watched the knowledge arrive in his face like weather. "That's the report. That's what the report says. That's what the tribunal will be shown. A tragic seep. Condolences. Case closed."

"It's a lie," Teo said. Simply, the way children say true things.

"It's a *staged* lie." Hazel was moving along the bench now, reading the ground the way I read maps. "See the drag marks under the gravel? See how the scree's been raked? Someone carried them here. Someone spent hours arranging the dead." He straightened up, and his face in the flat light of the gorge was the hardest thing I'd ever seen on it. "This isn't a cover-up, Wren. This is a *presentation*. They built a museum of their own crime and left the doors open, because nobody official will ever come look. Nobody official comes four hundred miles up a dead river to check a gas seep."

Except one broke desert ranger with a confiscated skiff and a debt collector's patience.

I knelt by the instrument table because my legs had decided for me. Under the theodolite, weighted by a stone, there was a field notebook. Amara's. Her handwriting, her abbreviations, her little stars in the margins for confirmed readings. My hands shook so badly Hazel had to hold the notebook steady while I turned the pages.

Readings. Dates. The anomalous flow data, week after week, converging on the gorge. And on the final page, dated the day before the last transmission, a note in her tight, furious script:

*Men at the fence today. Said they were watershed authority. Had no paperwork. Took our samples "for verification." Told us to pack out. Told us the spring is "subterranean and unstable" and any public record would cause panic. J. says sell the story or else. We are not selling anything. Full survey complete. Data copies hidden. If anything happens to us, the compass finds the rest.*

The compass. Her compass — ours, now, the brass thing modified with Amara's own hands to hold a bearing no magnet should know. Hidden data. Somewhere in these caverns, in the dark under the river, Amara had cached copies of everything, and the compass didn't point north, it pointed *home*, to the thing she'd trusted me to finish.

"Wren." Hazel's hand on my shoulder. Gentle. "Wren, breathe."

"She was here." My voice came out in pieces. "She died fifty yards from where I'm kneeling and they *arranged her*, Hazel. They posed her by the fire like she belonged in a painting. Like she was ever the kind of person who sat still."

"I know."

"They made her part of the scenery."

His grip tightened. "Then we make her part of the evidence."

* * *

We buried what we could bury. There wasn't much of anyone left to bury, and Hazel wouldn't let us disturb the scene more than we had to — "the less we touch, the more it convicts them" — but we raised cairns, five of them, on the rim where the wind came through from the northeast, and Teo cut a sixth stone for Amara and set it apart, facing the water. Marisol poured rum on the ground, which surprised everyone including Marisol. Bram recited something in Latin that he refused to translate. I mapped the graves. Of course I mapped the graves. Grid references, elevations, bearings to the fence posts. If the consortium burned this place to glass tomorrow, the cairns would exist forever at latitude such-and-such, longitude such-and-such, in ink, in triplicate, in my hand.

Then I stood at the rim and looked down at the structure by the vanishing point, at the floodlights burning behind their reinforced glass, and the awe I'd felt at the mouth of the gorge curdled all the way through, sour and cold, into something with edges.

They hadn't just stolen the water. They hadn't just murdered six people. They had built a *world* down there — fences, signs, concrete, light — a whole counterfeit reality laid over the truth like a chart drawn over a chart, and underneath it the real river ran on in the dark, unseen, unowned, patient. Twenty years of engineered drought. Five posed corpses. A tribunal that would grieve politely over a gas seep. And somewhere in the flooded dark beneath my feet, Amara's data waiting in the black like a loaded gun.

"Hazel," I said.

He came to stand beside me. Below, the river fell into the earth and did not come back.

"The entrance," I said. "The loading doors. That's how they get in — and it's how we get in."

He was quiet a long moment. Down in the yard, a figure in a hard hat crossed between crates, small as an insect, and disappeared inside.

"You understand what's down there," he said. "Not just the spring. Them. Armed men, sealed doors, and whatever they've built to keep the water theirs."

"I understand that Amara hid proof in the dark," I said, "and the compass knows the way, and I am done letting other people draw the map."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he'd started looking at me somewhere back on the river, when the rules began to bend — and something in his jaw set that I was beginning to recognize as the shape of a decision.

"Bram," he called, without turning. "How do we get into a walled mine without being seen?"

Bram limped over, squinted down at the floodlights, and smiled the smile of a man about to commit a crime he'd been rehearsing for twenty years.

"Through the water," he said. "There's always a second way in. The engineers who build these places always leave themselves one — and I trained half of them."

Below us, the river poured into the dark, and for the first time since the mission, the compass needle in my hand didn't tremble at all. It pointed straight down.
