## 15. Into the Under-River

The gorge swallowed the river at a place Wren called the Throat.

We stood on the lip of it in the gray half-light before dawn and watched the water pour itself into a crack in the earth no wider than a wagon road. It didn't fall so much as lean — the whole river tilting, gathering speed, then folding down into darkness with a sound like a held breath. Below us, where the water went, there was nothing. No spray. No echo. Just the Throat taking its drink and asking for more.

"That's not natural," Marisol said. She'd said it three times since we'd made camp above the gorge, and each time she said it like she was arguing with someone who wasn't there.

"It's natural," Bram said. "It's just not accidental." He crouched at the edge, running his thumb along a seam of cut stone that most eyes would've taken for rock. I'd learned to watch his hands instead of his face. His face lied out of habit. His hands always told the truth. "Somebody widened this. Somebody wanted the river coming down here."

"Then why build a wall around it?" Teo asked. He was pointing downstream, to where the hidden gorge opened up — the one the compass had dragged us two hundred miles to find. From the rim we could see it now: a slot canyon walled off with poured concrete and chain-link, survey stakes marching along its spine, a steel gate across the only approach by water. The consortium hadn't just found the source. They'd fenced it.

"Because they don't want the river," Bram said quietly. "They want what's under it."

* * *

We went in through the dry way.

Wren found it — of course she did, she'd spent three days mapping the gorge's rim while the rest of us argued about the gate. An old mining adit cut into the north wall, timbered at the mouth, its boards stamped with a consortium mark that made Bram go still when he saw it. Not the current mark. The old one. The one from before he'd left, or been pushed, depending on which version of the story you got.

"This is my adit," he said. His voice had gone strange and flat. "I signed the survey order for this tunnel. Nineteen years ago."

"You said you never came back here," Hazel-and-Wren — no. I said it. I said, "You told us you'd never worked this river."

"I told you I'd never worked *the spring*." Bram wouldn't look at me. "There's a difference, and I spent nineteen years pretending there wasn't."

Marisol stayed topside with the skiff and the rifle, watching the gate. Teo wanted to come and we told him no and he sulked about it in the way that meant he'd do exactly what we asked, because Teo had learned somewhere hard and young that being useful was how you kept people. I gave him the radio and told him if anything moved on the water, he was to blow the signal whistle and run, and not be brave. He nodded like a boy agreeing to a chore. I didn't believe him for a second. But there wasn't time to argue him into sense.

So it was the three of us: me, Wren, and Bram, with lamps, rope, a camera Wren had salvaged from the expedition's wrecked supplies, and her notebook wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart.

The adit ran level for forty yards and then began to descend, and the air changed. You feel it before you hear it — the pressure of buried water, a fullness in the ears, the way your lamp flame leans as though listening. The timber shoring got older as we went down, then newer again, and that was the thing that started crawling up my spine: the newest beams were *this year's*. Fresh-cut pine, still bleeding sap. Someone was working down here right now.

"Stations," Wren whispered, marking the wall with chalk. She'd been doing it every fifty feet since the entrance. "If we get separated, follow the marks back up. Don't follow the water. The water goes where they want it to go."

"And we don't," I said.

"We don't," she agreed.

Bram stopped at a junction where the tunnel split, one branch angling toward the sound of moving water and the other toward silence. He put his palm flat against the rock the way a man checks a horse. "Pump gallery," he murmured. "Feel it? They're dewatering the workings. Running pumps day and night." He looked back at us, and in the lamplight his face was older than it had any right to look. "You don't pump a mine dry unless you mean to work it. And you don't work a mine under a living river unless—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. We'd all read the land titles Marisol had lifted from the burned village office, the ones with the consortium's name crawling across every parcel from the gorge to the headwaters. Once the paper cleared, they wouldn't need to share the spring. They wouldn't need the spring at all.

"They're going to cap it," Wren said. Not a question. Her voice came out very small in all that stone. "The whole thing. Seal it underground."

"Grout curtain, bulkheads, diversion galleries." Bram ticked them off like a man reciting his own sins, which, in a way, he was. "I helped design systems like it. In the north. They call it aquifer management." He spat. "It's a coffin. You bury the water alive and sell the dirt on top."

* * *

We came down into the flooded workings an hour later, and I will carry the sight of them as long as I carry anything.

The consortium's engineers had driven their tunnels straight into the throat of the under-river, and the river had answered the way rivers answer — by filling everything they dug. Our lamplight lay across black water stretching away into the dark, dead calm, deep past knowing. Timber walkways floated where the crews had abandoned them. A pump barge sat half-sunk against the far wall, its machinery furred with mineral bloom, white as bone. And everywhere, everywhere, the walls glittered. The rock down here was shot through with seams of something bright, and the water magnified it, and the whole drowned cathedral shimmered like the inside of a geode.

"The anomalous readings," Wren breathed. She had her notebook out, sketching fast, barely looking at the page. "The survey traced them to this horizon. The spring doesn't feed *in* — it feeds *through*. This whole formation is saturated. It's not a pool, it's a vein. The river is just the part of it that found daylight."

She turned and looked at me, and even exhausted, even scared, there was a light in her face I recognized from the mission, from the rapids, from every bad night she'd bent over her charts. Cartographer's joy. The world showing her its true shape.

"Hazel. This could water everything from here to the flats. Every village. Every acre. For a hundred years."

"And they'll trade it for gravel rights," I said, because someone had to say it.

I waded out onto the nearest walkway to test it. The boards held. Beyond the barge, a side gallery opened to our left, and from it came a smell I knew from too many bad scenes: fuel, mud, and something else underneath. Something sweetish and wrong.

I knew what that smell was before I let myself know it.

"Bram. Wren. Stay here."

"No," Wren said immediately, and Bram said nothing at all, which was worse, because Bram going quiet meant Bram already understood.

The side gallery climbed slightly, and the water in it shallowed, and then the water ended and the mud began, and the mud was what I'd feared. Boot prints. Dozens, overlapping, days old at least. Drag marks. And at the gallery's end, where the tunnel had been bulkheaded with fresh concrete, the consortium's people had left their housekeeping.

Three bodies. What was left of them. They'd been arranged — that was the word my mind kept insisting on, *arranged* — beneath a shelf of collapsed-looking rock that any fool could see had been pried loose and levered down on purpose. Hard hats. Survey vests. One of them wore a brass compass on a lanyard, and I had to turn away from that, because I knew whose design it was, and so did Wren, standing behind me with her hand over her mouth.

"Accident report writes itself," I heard myself say, in the flat voice I use for the worst parts of the job. "Crew ignored warnings. Tunnel flooded. Rockfall. Tragic."

"Elias," Wren whispered. She knelt beside the man with the compass and touched his shoulder, once, gently, the way you'd wake someone who'd earned another hour of sleep. "Elias built the compass. Elias found the readings. Elias—" Her voice cracked clean through. Then she straightened, and wiped her face with the back of her wrist, and took out the camera, and I understood what she was doing before she said it.

"Photograph everything," she said. "Names, badges, the rockfall bolts — look, Hazel, the drill holes are cut but never charged, they staged it with hand bars. Photograph the bolt holes. Photograph the concrete date stamps. Everything."

So we did. It was the hardest work I've done in twenty years of hard work, kneeling in the dark among murdered men while the woman I — while Wren documented their deaths with steady hands and tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face. Bram stood at the gallery mouth the whole time with his lamp turned away, and when we finished he still hadn't moved, and when I put my hand on his shoulder he flinched like I'd struck him.

"I falsified reports for these people," he said to the wall. "Nineteen years ago I sat in a office and drew the maps that told them where to dig. And I told myself it was just geology. Just rock. Rock doesn't bleed." He finally turned, and his eyes were wet and furious. "Rock doesn't bleed. But men do. And I drew them the map."

"Then draw them one more," I said. "Show us the cap works. Show us where they'd seal it. Show us everything you know, Bram, because in three days or thirty, some clerk signs the last title, and this all goes dark forever."

He looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere below us, through a hundred feet of stone, the under-river moved in its sleep, patient as it had been for ten thousand years, unaware that men had measured it for a coffin.

"All right," Bram said. "All right. Follow me. And stay close, because the place I'm taking you is the reason I never slept a full night after I left their payroll."

* * *

He led us down a maintenance ladder bolted into the rock — new bolts, new rungs, the consortium's money gleaming everywhere — into a gallery lower than any of the others, where the air was warm and wet and the sound of the spring came up through the floor like a heartbeat. Here the engineers had begun their great work. Half-built bulkheads of rebar and formwork stood in ranks down the tunnel, waiting for their concrete. Grout injection ports studded the walls at intervals, labeled in neat stencil. At the gallery's end, behind temporary fencing, yawned the mouth of the spring itself — a cavern of flowing water rising out of the deep earth, wide as a chapel, lit faintly from below by some phosphorescence in the minerals, glowing like a coal banked under ash.

Wren stood at the fence and didn't say anything for a long time. Neither did I. There are places that make language feel like litter.

"They'd put a plug there," Bram said at last, pointing into the glow. "Right at the neck. And grout the fractures for half a mile in every direction. The spring would find nowhere to go. Pressure builds. Either it dies quiet, or it finds a new path nobody can predict — and either way, the land above stays dry, stays cheap, stays theirs." He pulled out his own notebook, an old one, soft with years, and began to sketch the works with hands that shook only a little. "Cap locations. Pump schedule. Concrete delivery records — those will be in the site office, topside, and they'll match the purchase orders, and between those and your photographs and my testimony—"

"Your testimony," I said carefully.

"My testimony." He met my eyes. "I know what it costs. I know what I am in a courtroom. But a confession is the one map I can still draw straight."

That was when the lights came on.

Not our lamps — the gallery floods, strung along the ceiling, blazing up section by section toward us like a fuse burning backward. Someone, somewhere above, had thrown a switch. And through the stone, muffled but unmistakable, came a sound I'd been listening for since the Throat: engines. Not pumps. Engines with intent, out on the open water, closing on the gate.

The radio at my belt crackled. Teo's voice, pitched low and fast, trying so hard to sound calm that it broke my heart:

"Hazel. Boat at the gate. Big one. Gun mounts. And — Hazel, there's a man on deck with a loudhailer, and he's asking for you. By name. He says—" Teo paused, and I heard him swallow. "He says he has something of yours. He says come up and talk, or he comes down and takes."

Wren looked at me. Bram looked at me. Down in its glowing chapel, the spring went on pouring itself into the dark, generous as ever, patient as ever, waiting to learn whether it would be set free or buried alive.

I clicked the radio. "Tell him," I said, "we're on our way up."
