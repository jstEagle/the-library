## 16. Betrayal Paid in Full

Teo had learned to count by listening.

In the mission village, before the fire took it, his grandmother had made him sit with his eyes closed and name every sound in the dark: the goats shifting in their pen, the wind working at the tin roof, his uncle's cough two rooms away. *A person who sees is fooled easy,* she said. *A person who hears knows what's true.*

So Teo heard it first.

They were camped inside the consortium's upper gallery — a rough-cut chamber above the flooded workings where the river vanished into the earth. Bram called it a "manifold room," all rusted valves and riveted pipe, and had spent an hour explaining how the consortium meant to choke the spring with concrete and paperwork. Hazel was on watch. Wren was asleep against her pack with the compass cradled like a sleeping bird. Marisol had gone down to check the boat lines in the lower passage. Old Bram snored like a landslide.

And beneath all of it — under the drip of seepage, under the groan of the mountain settling — there was a sound that did not belong.

Footsteps. Soft ones. Moving away from the group, toward the gallery's far mouth, where the exit tunnel ran back up toward daylight.

Teo opened his eyes and counted the sleepers. Hazel on watch. Wren. Bram. Marisol below.

Five of them had come down into the gorge.

He counted again. Hazel. Wren. Bram. Marisol below.

Four.

* * *

The thief's name was Silas Pell, though he'd told them to call him Finch, and he'd been with them since the flats outside Perdido — since the day Hazel caught him stealing from a supply wagon and then, three days later, found him half-dead of thirst and dragged him aboard anyway because that was the kind of man Hazel Sandoval was, the kind who paid other people's debts until the paying killed him.

Teo had liked Finch. That was the worst part. Finch taught him card tricks. Finch gave him the first honest laugh Teo'd had since the mission burned. Finch said, *Kid, you got good ears; you should be a scout,* and Teo had glowed for a week.

But Teo had also watched Finch's hands.

Finch's hands never stopped moving. They checked knots they hadn't tied. They drifted over packs when everyone slept. And twice now — once at Serpent's Neck, once at the burned village — Teo had seen him slip away at dusk and come back with dust on his knees and a look on his face like a man swallowing something that fought going down.

At the village, Teo had found a scrap of mirror-glass wedged in the rocks above the landing, angled to catch light from the water. A signal. He hadn't known what it meant then. He'd almost told Hazel. But Finch had been kind to him, and kindness was rare enough in Teo's life that he'd hoarded it, even when it started to smell wrong.

Now he lay still in the dark and listened to Finch's footsteps fade up the exit tunnel, and he understood everything at once, the way you understand a trap only after your foot is in it.

* * *

He should wake Hazel. That was the rule. Hazel was the ranger; Hazel decided things.

But Hazel was forty feet away with his back to the tunnel mouth, watching the lower passage where the gunboat might come from, and if Teo shouted, Finch would hear it too, and whatever Finch was doing up there — sealing a door, lighting a signal, opening a way for men with guns — would be finished before anyone reached him.

Teo thought about the burned village. About the woman named Adaeze who'd held him like she was afraid he'd blow away, who smelled like woodsmoke and whose arms were the closest thing to home he'd felt in weeks. Rook's men had burned that village because someone wouldn't give up travelers. Because of people like Finch, telling people like Rook where people like Teo slept.

His grandmother's voice: *A person who hears knows what's true.*

He got up without a sound. He'd gotten good at that too — a boy learns to move quiet in a house full of grief — and he ghosted along the gallery wall, past the sleeping shapes, into the throat of the exit tunnel.

* * *

The tunnel climbed in switchbacks, lamplit at the bottom and black above. Teo followed the sound instead of the light: the scrape of a boot, the clink of metal, Finch breathing hard through his nose the way he did when he was scared and pretending not to be.

Forty yards up, the tunnel narrowed between walls of raw stone, and here Finch had stopped to work. Teo pressed himself flat behind a fallen slab and looked through the gap beneath it.

Finch had a kit open on the ground. Not a thief's kit — a sapper's. Coiled detonation cord. A block of grey explosive with a paper wrapper still on it, consortium-stamped. He was tamping charges into drilled sockets in the ceiling of the tunnel with the focused tenderness of a man planting seeds.

Above the sockets, Teo saw why. The tunnel roof here was a shelf of cracked rock sitting on a seam of softer stone. Blow the seam and the whole section came down. Blow the whole section and the exit sealed — with four people on the wrong side of it, in a cavern complex that had exactly one other way out, and that one ran down through the flooded workings, through water and dark and whatever else the consortium had left down there.

Finch wasn't just signaling Rook. He was building the box they'd all die in.

Teo's heart went strange and slow. His hands, though — his hands were steady. Grandmother's training. Fear was a sound like any other; you could listen to it without obeying it.

He needed to do three things, and the order mattered.

One: see the rest. Two: stop Finch from finishing, or make sure that even if he finished, it wouldn't matter. Three: get back down and tell Hazel before the shooting started.

He was small. Everyone said it like an apology — *the kid*, *the stowaway*, *dead weight*. Marisol had said it to Hazel's face at the burned village: *We're hauling a child through a war.* Teo had cried about it that night, quietly, into his sleeve, hating her and hating more that she was right.

But small meant he fit where Finch didn't look. Small meant the gap under the slab was a door for him and a wall for everyone else. Small meant that when the world got built for big people with big plans, there were cracks left over, and cracks were where Teo lived.

* * *

While Finch worked on the near sockets, Teo wormed around the slab's edge and up onto its top, belly-crawling through a space no grown man could have used, and came out above and behind him.

From there he saw the rest of it.

Past the charge sockets, the tunnel opened into a wider chamber, and in that chamber stood a man with a shaded lantern, and the man was talking low into a field radio, and even turned half away, even in the bad light, Teo knew the set of those shoulders. He'd seen them across the water at Serpent's Neck. He'd seen them standing in the ash of Adaeze's village, directing the burning like a man overseeing a harvest.

Rook.

And beside Rook, stacked against the wall with their straps cut loose: Bram's old credentials case. The consortium papers that had gone missing from Bram's pack two nights ago, the discovery that had nearly torn the crew apart, that had put a knife's edge into every conversation since. Planted. All of it planted, and Finch had done the planting while Teo slept ten feet away.

*I'm sorry,* Teo thought, not sure who he was apologizing to. Bram, maybe, whom they'd all looked at sideways for three days. Or maybe to himself, for the week he'd spent glowing over card tricks.

Rook's voice carried in the stone. "...charges are set at six points. Once I have confirmation the girl's chart is secured, we bring the gallery down and the problem solves itself. The spring stays buried, the titles clear, and nobody upstream ever knows there was water under their dust." A pause. A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Sandoval? Sandoval gets the same hole as the rest of them. He had his chances."

Teo lay on cold rock and listened to a man schedule their deaths, and something in him went very quiet and very clear.

Six points. Finch had tamped four. Two remained.

* * *

He couldn't defuse what was already set — he didn't know how, and one wrong tug would bring the roof down early, on all of them. But the last two sockets were Finch's, and Finch was hurrying now, because Rook had said *confirmation*, which meant the signal had to go out soon, which meant Finch was rushing the one part of the job that mattered most.

Teo did the arithmetic fast, the way he did everything, in sounds.

The gallery below them sat above the flooded workings. Bram had spent a whole evening drawing it in the dirt: the spring under pressure, miles of capped water pushing against the consortium's walls, the manifold room full of valves that hadn't been turned since the surveyors died. *Water's patient,* Bram had said. *Water always wins eventually. The consortium's whole plan is a bet that it won't win before the paperwork does.*

There was a valve stem in the manifold room, Teo remembered — a big red-rusted wheel Bram had touched like it was holy, feeding a bypass line that ran up along the workings toward the old survey adit. A pressure relief, Bram called it. Sealed. If it ever opened, the under-river would come up that line looking for somewhere to go.

Somewhere like a tunnel with freshly drilled sockets in its ceiling. Somewhere like soft stone already cracked and waiting.

Teo couldn't fight. Couldn't out-think Rook, couldn't out-shoot Finch. But he could reroute a river. Well — he could turn a wheel and let the river do the fighting, which amounted to the same thing.

The valve was fifty feet below, through the sleeping camp, past Hazel's watch position. And Finch was reaching for his fifth charge.

Teo made a choice that aged him years in a single breath: he let Finch finish.

Because if he stopped Finch now, Rook's men simply came down the tunnel and shot them all in the dark. But if the charges blew and the exit sealed — and if the water came up through the bypass at the same moment — then the cave-in wouldn't seal them in with Rook's men waiting outside. It would seal Rook's men *out*, on the wrong side of a hundred tons of collapsed rock, while the flood tore through the workings below and gave the crew a new way up and out through the old survey adit, exactly the secondary passage Bram had sworn existed if anyone could find it.

It was a terrible plan. It depended on a wheel turning, on Bram being right, on timing that no one had agreed to. Teo was thirteen years old and he knew all of this and he did it anyway, because the alternative was lying on a rock listening to a man schedule their deaths and doing nothing, and his grandmother hadn't raised him for nothing.

Finch tamped the sixth charge. Stood. Wiped his hands on his thighs like a man finishing a chore.

"Signal," Rook said from the chamber, and Finch pulled a shuttered lamp from his kit and began angling it toward a vent shaft that faced the river — the same trick as the mirror glass, light talking to light, telling the gunboat *come now, it's done.*

Teo slid back off the slab and ran.

* * *

He went down through the dark on memory and hearing, taking the switchbacks at a speed that should have broken his neck, and burst into the gallery gasping.

Hazel spun, rifle up, and lowered it in the same motion. "Teo? What—"

"Finch," Teo wheezed. "Finch is Rook's. Always was. He's blowing the exit — charges in the tunnel — Rook's up there *now* — and Bram, the valve, the red wheel, the bypass line, can it reach the survey adit?"

Bram came awake like a man surfacing from deep water, and Teo watched the geologist's face do the whole calculation in two seconds. "The relief line. Yes. Yes, it runs within twenty feet of the adit — the old engineers tapped the same aquifer—" His eyes went wide. "Boy, if you're saying what I think you're saying—"

"The charges crack the rock," Teo said. His voice didn't shake. Later, maybe, it would shake for a week. "The water finds the crack. We don't need the tunnel."

Hazel looked at him — really looked, the way he rarely did, like Teo was a map worth reading — and then he was moving, waking Wren, grabbing the evidence satchel, shouting Marisol's name down the stairwell to the boat landing. "Everyone up. We have minutes. Maybe less."

Wren stared at Teo. "How did you know?"

"I listened," Teo said. "Somebody had to."

Marisol came up the stairs three at a time, took one look at their faces, and asked no questions, which was its own kind of trust.

Hazel gripped Teo's shoulder, hard. "The valve. Can you turn it?"

"It's heavy."

"Then we'll both turn it."

* * *

They reached the manifold room as the first muffled shout came down from the tunnel — Finch, discovering the camp empty, or Rook, deciding confirmation had waited long enough. There was no time to be quiet anymore. There was only time to be fast.

The red wheel was fused with rust. Hazel threw his whole body against it and it gave a quarter-turn with a shriek like a dying animal. Below their feet, through the soles of their boots, they felt the answer: a deep, rising hum, the under-river noticing the door they'd opened, pressing up through miles of pipe with the patience of centuries suddenly spent all at once.

"Again," Hazel gritted.

Together — the ranger and the smallest member of the crew, the man who paid everyone's debts and the boy nobody counted — they heaved the wheel around, and somewhere below them a seal let go, and the water began to climb.

From the tunnel above came Finch's voice, high and cracking: "It's ready! Light it, light it *now*—"

Teo looked at Hazel. Hazel looked at Teo. Neither of them ran for cover, because there wasn't any, because there was only forward, down toward the flooded workings where Bram was already shouting that he'd found the adit marker, where Wren held the compass aloft like a torch pointing at nothing and everything, where Marisol stood at the boat lines with her hand on the throttle of the only way out.

The charges blew. The mountain came down across the exit tunnel with a sound like the world ending — and through the dust and the dark, from deep below, came another sound, older and vaster:

Water, finding a crack.

"*Adit!*" Bram roared. "*Go, go, GO!*"

Teo went.
