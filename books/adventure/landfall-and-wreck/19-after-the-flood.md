## 19. After the Flood

The water came back at dawn.

Wren heard it before she saw it — a low, rolling thunder from the gorge, like the river clearing its throat after years of being strangled. She climbed out of her bedroll in the grey half-light and walked to the rim of the canyon alone, because some things you had to witness by yourself, and this was one of them.

The spring was running.

Not trickling, not seeping — *running*. It came up out of the breached cap in a white column that caught the first light and threw it back in pieces, and where it hit the old dry channel it spread and gathered and became, within minutes, a river again. The under-river, the one the consortium had walled and claimed and tried to own, was pouring itself back into the world.

She stood there a long time. Her hands were shaking, and she let them.

Three weeks ago she had crouched in a flooded mission with a rifle across her knees and a compass in her pocket that pointed at nothing any sane person would want to find. She had counted her bullets twice a day and rehearsed what she'd say if anyone came through the door — *I'm the cartographer, I'm the one who can prove it, don't shoot, please don't shoot* — until the words wore smooth as river stone. She had not expected to be rescued. She had not expected anyone to come at all.

And then Hazel Sandoval had walked into her sights with mud on his boots and a ranger's star on his chest, and she had almost killed him, and everything since had been rapids and fire and a betrayal she still couldn't think about without her chest tightening — the thief with his easy grin, the sealed cavern, Teo's small hands rerouting a river's worth of pressure while grown men panicked around him. Rook going down with the gunboat and the mine and the whole rotten architecture of the claim, refusing the rope Marisol threw him, his face turned up toward a sky he'd spent his career poisoning.

The water was rising fast now. Downstream, she knew, it would reach the first village by tomorrow, the second by the day after, and by the end of the week it would touch the mission where she'd hidden, and the wells that had been going dry for six years would fill, and children who had never seen the river above their ankles would stand in it and scream with joy. She thought of Teo's face when he'd said that, standing in the wreckage of the cap, soaked to the bone and laughing. *They'll drink this season, Wren. They'll actually drink.*

She unslung her satchel, took out the field book, and began to draw.

* * *

By midmorning the others had gathered at the gorge rim, and Wren had claimed a flat shelf of rock as her drafting table — the first drafting table she'd had since the survey camp, since before the men with the rifles, since before she'd run inland away from the water like a fool, like the only survivor of eight.

"You're doing it now?" Marisol stood over her, arms crossed, hair still stiff with mineral spray from the flood. "The river's still deciding where it wants to go. You'll draw it and it'll change its mind by lunch."

"Then I'll draw it again." Wren didn't look up. Her pencil moved in quick, sure strokes — the old channel, the new channel, the breach in the cap, the seep lines where water was already finding its way through rock the engineers had sworn was impermeable. "That's the whole point. That's what a chart *is*. Not a claim. A record of what the water actually does."

Marisol was quiet a moment. "You know they'll come for that," she said. "The consortium's not dead. Men like that don't drown."

"Then they can read it in the tribunal record." Wren said it flatly, and heard, underneath the flatness, the thing she'd been carrying since the gorge: *let them try.* She had spent her whole professional life making maps for people who paid her, and every one of them had been a kind of lie — boundaries smoothed, flows exaggerated, the truth bent just enough to serve the client. Her partner Ilya had died holding the one honest instrument either of them ever built. She would not draw a dishonest line again. Not one.

Bram came and sat beside her without asking, the way old men do. He looked terrible — he'd looked terrible for days — but his eyes were clear in a way she'd never seen them, unburdened, or at least beginning to be.

"May I?" He nodded at the field book.

She turned it toward him. He studied the new course of the river, the way it split around the collapsed workings and re-formed downstream, and his weathered finger traced the main channel and stopped.

"You've got the seep line wrong here," he said. "It won't hold the east bank past the second bend. The saturation'll undermine it inside a month. The river will cut a new channel through the old survey camp."

Wren stared at him. "The old camp."

"Yes." Bram's voice was gentle, and she understood what he was telling her, and what he was asking permission to tell her. "The water's going to take it back. All of it."

She looked down at her map. The old survey camp — the tents, the instrument cases, the shallow graves they'd never had time to dig properly because they'd been running — sat right in the path of the new channel. The river was going to reclaim the place where her people had died, and carry it downstream, and scatter it through every village that would drink this season.

She found, to her surprise, that this didn't hurt. It was almost right. *Let the water have them,* she thought. *Let the water carry them somewhere people are grateful.*

"Then I'd better chart the second bend accurately," she said, and her voice held, and Bram squeezed her shoulder once and went to help Teo rig a signal line across the gorge.

She worked through the heat of the day. It was the best work of her life and she knew it even as she did it — not because it was beautiful, though some of it was, but because it was true. Every line measured against the land itself, not against what anyone wanted the land to be. Her first honest chart. She thought Ilya would have laughed at her for the sentiment and then quietly kept a copy.

Hazel came by in the late afternoon and sat down on the rock beside her without a word, and for a while that was enough. That was one of the things she'd learned about him in the canyon: he could be silent with you, actually silent, not the loaded silence of a man waiting his turn to talk. He'd been a debt collector in a ranger's uniform for so long that he'd forgotten how to want things, and she was watching him learn again, slowly, the way a river learns a new channel — hesitantly, with a lot of backing and eddying, but learning.

"How's the chart coming?" he asked finally.

"It's coming honest." She showed him the breach, the new channel, the villages marked downstream with the dates the water would reach them. He studied it the way he studied everything — carefully, like it might cost him.

"They'll want this in the tribunal," he said. "Your testimony. Mine. Bram's, God help us all." He said it lightly, but she heard the weight under it, and she looked up at him.

"You're worried about what it costs you."

He was quiet for a moment. "I spent six years paying for something that wasn't mine," he said. "I got good at calculating costs. It's a hard habit to break." He turned the field book toward himself and looked at the small marks she'd made for the villages. "But I keep thinking about what you said in the gorge. Before we went under. You said the truth was the only thing down there worth more than our lives."

"I said that?"

"You said it better. I'm paraphrasing."

She laughed — a short, cracked sound, her first in days — and bumped her shoulder against his. Below them the river ran white and loud and free, and the sound of it filled up all the silences they couldn't.

* * *

They came down to the water at dusk, all of them, because Marisol had found a stretch of bank where the new channel ran slow and wide and the skiff could be beached safely, and because none of them quite knew what else to do. The mission was over. The running was over. The fight was over. And five people who had been held together by pursuit and necessity stood on a riverbank in the fading light and looked at each other like strangers who'd woken up in the same bed.

"So," Marisol said.

"So," said Bram.

Teo, sixteen and soaked and still radiating the fierce pride of a boy who had saved everyone's life with a valve wheel, looked around at them all. "Is somebody going to say what happens now, or are we all just going to stand here?"

"Standing here is underrated," Marisol said. "I've spent three years running. I could stand here a while."

Wren looked at Hazel. Hazel looked at the water. She could see him doing it — the calculating, the cost-counting, the old arithmetic of a man whose whole life had been a ledger. He had torn up his incident log for her. He had refused Rook's offer with his brother's debt on the table, and she would never forget the set of his jaw when he did it, the moment the debt stopped owning him. But refusing a debt wasn't the same as knowing what to do with the freedom on the other side of it. She recognized the look. She'd worn it herself, in the mission, every morning: *I survived. Now what was it for?*

"The water reaches the first village tomorrow," she said, into the silence. "The wells fill by the weekend. Someone has to tell them where it came from, and why, and who to thank, and who to never, ever trust again."

"That'll be the tribunal's job," Hazel said.

"The tribunal's job is the consortium." She turned to face him fully. "Ours is the truth. There's a difference, and you know there's a difference, because you're the one who taught me it."

He looked at her then, and something in his face let go — not all at once, but like a knot slipping. "I don't know what I am," he said quietly. "Without the debt. Without the badge telling me what I'm for. I've been a holding pattern for six years, Wren. I don't know how to land."

"You will." She took his hand. His fingers were scarred and cold from the river and they closed around hers like they'd been waiting to. "You're not a holding pattern. You're a flood plain. You just spent six years waiting for the water to come back, and now it has, and you don't know yet what grows in you. That's not nothing. That's *topsoil*."

Behind them, Teo made a small gagging noise, and Marisol laughed, and the moment broke the way good moments do — not shattered, just distributed, shared out among all of them.

They built a fire on the bank, the first fire that wasn't for warmth or signal or concealment, just a fire, and Bram told a long terrible joke about a stratigrapher and a barmaid, and Marisol taught Teo a card game that was probably illegal in two territories, and Wren sat with her back against Hazel's shoulder and watched the sparks go up and thought: *this. This is what I ran inland to find without knowing it. Not the source. This.*

She had lost eight people to the river's secret. She would carry them — the graves, the guilt, the sound the rifles made. That debt didn't wash out. But tonight, for the first time, it wasn't the only thing she carried.

* * *

She woke before dawn again, out of habit, and found Hazel already awake, standing at the water's edge with his radio in his hand.

He wasn't transmitting. He was just holding it, the way a man holds a thing he's not sure he wants to work.

"What is it?" she asked, coming up beside him.

"Static." He turned it over. "I've had it tuned to the district band all night. Superintendent Reyes will have heard about the flood by now — you can't hide a river coming back to life. He'll want a report. He'll want *me*." He was quiet a moment. "And the tribunal convenes in the district seat in four days. They'll want testimony. All of it. The consortium, the murders, the falsified reports — and Bram's part in them. And mine."

"Your part?"

"I knew things, Wren. Small things. Signs I explained away because explaining them away was cheaper than following them." He looked out at the water, grey in the pre-dawn. "I told you I got good at calculating costs. The tribunal's going to make me say that out loud, on the record, and then they're going to take my badge, and I'm going to let them."

She studied his profile. "And you're standing out here at four in the morning holding a radio because—"

"Because the district band isn't only static." He raised the radio slowly. "There's something under it. A carrier wave. Somebody's been transmitting on repeat since midnight — too weak to resolve, too regular to be interference." He looked at her, and the old dread was back in his eyes, but it was different now, shared, carried between them instead of by him alone. "Wren, upriver — past the source, into the high country nobody surveys — there's a station that went silent eleven days ago. A weather post. Two rangers."

The static hissed. And underneath it, faint as a heartbeat, the carrier wave pulsed on.

"Some debts you pay forever," Hazel said quietly. He looked at the radio, and then at her, and then at the crew's sleeping shapes around the dead fire. "I'm going to need a boat."

Wren looked upriver, where the new water was running, and reached for her field book.
