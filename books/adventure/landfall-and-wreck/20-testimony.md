## 20. Testimony

The tribunal met in the old assayer's hall, the one with the high windows and the dust that never quite settled, and Hazel Sandoval walked into it the way a man walks to his own hanging — on time, sober, and with no illusions about the rope.

They'd given him three days' notice. Three days was generous, by the standards of the Territorial Water Authority, which had spent those same three days doing everything short of begging him to reconsider. Superintendent Alvarez had called him in twice. The first time she'd offered him a transfer. The second time she'd offered him nothing at all, just sat behind her desk with her hands folded and said, *You understand what you're doing.*

He'd understood. He understood now, standing in the witness box with the consortium's lawyers arranged along the defense table like crows on a fence line, their black suits pressed sharp enough to draw blood.

"State your name and posting for the record."

"Hazel Sandoval. Senior ranger, out of the Cobre Flats district. Fourteen years."

"And you're here voluntarily?"

"I'm here because I subpoenaed myself," Hazel said. "Nobody else was going to do it."

A murmur went through the gallery. Wren was up there somewhere — he could feel her the way you feel weather coming, a pressure change at the back of your neck. Teo too, and Marisol, and Old Bram sitting very straight in a clean shirt that didn't fit him right, like a man wearing his own ghost.

The tribunal chair, a dry woman named Corliss with a voice like gravel poured slow, leaned forward. "Ranger Sandoval. You've submitted testimony alleging that the Meridian Consolidated Mining Company engineered an artificial drought across four districts, falsified hydrological surveys for a period of eleven years, murdered six members of a territorial survey expedition, and attempted to cap a natural spring holding rights senior to every claim they've filed. That's what I'm reading here."

"That's what happened."

"That's an extraordinary set of allegations."

"It's an ordinary crime," Hazel said. "It's just a big one. Somebody found water where other people were thirsty, and instead of sharing it, they decided to sell the thirst."

Corliss let that sit. One of the lawyers — Voss, the lead one, a man with a face like a closed ledger — made a note he didn't need to make.

* * *

They broke him down the way rivers break stone. Not all at once. Drip by drip.

Voss started easy, establishing the timeline, and then turned on him like a switch had been thrown.

"You testified that you destroyed your official incident log covering the period of the expedition's disappearance."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because if I'd filed it as written, Wren Okafor would have been arrested for theft and the survey deaths would have been logged as drowning accidents. I knew that within a week of finding her. I chose not to file it."

"So you obstructed an investigation."

"I *was* the investigation. There wasn't anyone else for two hundred miles."

"You obstructed an investigation you were conducting," Voss repeated, letting the words hang. "You harbored a material witness. You conspired with a known smuggler—"

"Marisol Vega has two smuggling convictions, both for moving medicine during the quarantine years when the Authority wouldn't open the river," Hazel said. "I'd have done the same. I did do worse."

"You'll have your turn, Ranger. You conspired with a disgraced geologist who, by his own admission, took consortium money to falsify the very reports this tribunal is now reviewing."

Hazel looked at Bram. Bram looked back, steady, waiting for whatever was coming, because he'd told them all from the first night: *Don't soften it for me. I sold the water before anyone else drank.*

"No," Hazel said. "I won't let you use him that way. Bram Okonkwo falsified eleven years of aquifer reports under pressure and payment from Meridian Consolidated, and he has confessed to it in full, in writing, and he will answer for it. But he also came back upriver with us knowing the men who killed the survey crew wanted him dead too, and he mapped the cap structure in those caverns from memory so we could prove what they built. Both things are true. This region doesn't get clean again by pretending only one of them is."

Voss smiled thinly. "How noble. And how convenient. Tell me, Ranger — when Dane Rook offered you the settlement of your personal debts in exchange for surrendering Miss Okafor and the evidence, what did you say?"

The gallery went quiet. This was the part Alvarez had warned him about. The part the consortium's investigators had dug out of Rook's recovered radio logs and put in front of the tribunal themselves, because it was the only dirt they had on him and they meant to make it count.

"He offered to erase my brother's debts. About nine thousand credits, plus interest, owed to Meridian's lending arm since my brother died. In exchange for handing over Wren Okafor and destroying the evidence of the spring."

"And your answer?"

"I told him the debt wasn't mine to trade. It was mine to carry. Then Marisol rammed his gunboat."

Laughter in the gallery, quickly smothered. Even Corliss's mouth twitched before she killed it.

"Ranger. You've spent fourteen years as a lawman while functionally insolvent. Your wages have been garnished for a decade. Isn't it fair to say that a man in your position might be *motivated*—"

"To take the deal? Sure." Hazel gripped the rail of the box. "Every day for ten years I've been motivated to take some deal or another. That's what debt is. It's a voice in your ear that sounds like reason. Rook just said it louder than most. But I want the record to show something, since we're doing honesty today." He turned to face the tribunal directly. "I'm not here because I'm clean. I'm here because I'm dirty in all the ways they can prove and some they can't, and I burned my own log rather than trust the system I serve to handle the truth, and I was right to burn it, because the system sent a dead man's call sign up that river to hunt the last living witness. If this tribunal wants to know why people out past the flats don't report crimes anymore — why they run, and hide, and trust smugglers over rangers — I'm the exhibit. I did everything right for fourteen years and it bought me nothing but the chance to watch the wrong thing happen correctly. So charge me with what I did. All of it. But charge Meridian Consolidated for what *they* did, or this whole proceeding is just a longer way of drowning."

* * *

It took four days. They brought in hydrologists, survey records, the salvaged charts Wren had redrawn from memory with her hands still scarred from the rapids. They played Rook's transmissions. They heard from the survivors of the village at Ash Bend, a woman named Ferro who described the riders who came asking after travelers, and the fire that followed her refusal.

On the fourth day, Corliss read the findings. Meridian Consolidated's charters suspended. Criminal referrals for seven named officers. The spring placed under territorial trust pending a water-rights settlement that would take years and would be fought inch by inch, but which would begin, for the first time in a generation, with the truth on the table.

And then: "Ranger Sandoval. Regarding your own conduct." She paused, and Hazel felt the whole hall lean in. "This tribunal finds that your destruction of your incident log constituted gross dereliction of duty. Effective immediately, your commission is revoked."

There it was. Fourteen years, gone in a sentence. He'd known it was coming and it still landed like a blow to the chest — not the badge itself, but the sound of it, the finality. The thing that had kept him honest and broke, gone.

"However," Corliss continued, "in light of your voluntary testimony, and its centrality to these findings, the tribunal declines to refer you for prosecution. You are dismissed. This session is concluded."

* * *

They came for him that evening.

Of course they did. The badge was gone but the debt remained — nine thousand credits plus a decade of compounding interest, and Meridian's lending arm might be under suspension but the paper had been sold twice over before the hammer fell, and the new holders were a collection house out of the capital with no charter to lose and no shame to spend.

Two of them found him at the boarding house where he was packing a bag he no longer had a destination for. Big men, polite the way weather is polite. The older one laid a document on the bed.

"Full balance due, plus penalties. We can take the garnishment route, but your wages ended today, so." He shrugged. "Asset seizure. There's the skiff. There's the brother's old claim marker, such as it is. Or there's the work program up at the salt camps. Three years clears it."

Hazel looked at the paper. Ten years of his life on one page, and the arithmetic hadn't changed by a single credit. He'd stood in front of a tribunal and burned everything he had, and the number was still the number.

"The skiff isn't mine alone," he said. "There's a lien dispute. Marisol Vega holds half-title."

"Then Ms. Vega can surrender her interest."

"Her interest," said a voice from the doorway, "is that you're standing in my partner's room uninvited."

Marisol filled the doorframe the way she filled a river channel — completely, and with intent. Behind her: Teo, sixteen and trying to look taller. And Bram, in the shirt that didn't fit, holding a leather folder against his chest like a breastplate.

The older collector sighed. "This isn't a negotiation."

"No," Marisol agreed. "It's a payment." She jerked her chin at Bram.

Bram stepped forward and opened the folder. "Consolidated severance. Eleven years of retained fees I never touched because I couldn't stand to look at them. Held in escrow under my name since the day I walked out." His voice was level, but his hand wasn't. "It comes to slightly more than the principal. I'm applying it to Ranger Sandoval's account. Consider it restitution — the kind I actually owe."

The collector blinked. For the first time, he looked uncertain. "That's... irregular."

"So am I," said Teo, and slapped a second document on top of Bram's — the salvage award, the one the tribunal had granted the crew for recovery of the survey evidence. His share. Every credit of it. "I did the math on the boat coming in. Between the three of us it covers the balance, the penalties, and about forty credits over. Buy him dinner."

Hazel stood there with his bag half-packed and his throat working and nothing in it. Ten years he'd carried the number alone. Ten years of garnished wages and saltless meals and the voice in his ear sounding like reason. And these three — a smuggler, a stowaway, and a man paying off his own sins — had killed it in ninety seconds without a word of discussion, because there had never been anything to discuss.

"You can't—" he started.

"We can," Marisol said. "We already did. Sign the release, gentlemen, before I remember I'm a smuggler."

The collectors signed. The older one paused at the door. "For what it's worth, Ranger — the salt camps are full of men who took better deals than yours."

"Then it's worth exactly nothing," Hazel said, "which is more than I expected."

When they were gone, Wren appeared in the inner doorway, leaning on the frame, watching him with that look she had — the cartographer's look, measuring, precise, fond.

"They paid your debt," she said.

"They paid *a* debt." He crossed the room to her. "I've got a new one. Four of them, actually. Plus interest."

"Good," Wren said. "Those are the kind worth carrying." She nodded toward the window, where the evening light was going gold over the rooftops, and beyond them, faint but unmistakable, the sound of the river running full and free toward the delta. "Besides. You'll want to hear this before you decide what you owe and to whom."

She held up the portable radio. On it, faint through static, the dispatcher's voice was repeating a call: distress signal, two days upstream, an entire district gone silent.

Hazel looked at the radio. At Wren. At the three faces crowded in the doorway behind her, already arguing about fuel and supplies, already loading the boat in their heads.

Some debts you pay forever. He was beginning to learn which ones gladly.

"Get the skiff ready," he said. "We leave at dawn."
