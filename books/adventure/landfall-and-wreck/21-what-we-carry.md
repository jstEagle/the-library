## 21. What We Carry

*Marisol*

The skiff had a hole in her hull the size of a dinner plate and a name nobody remembered, so Marisol gave her both a patch and a new one: *Segunda Vez*. Second time. Because that was what the river did — it took things under and gave them back changed.

She worked on the refit through the whole of the dry season, when the water ran low and clear and the trade routes opened like veins after a tourniquet came off. The spring at the source had rewritten the map downstream. Villages that had been selling their land for drinking rights now sold melons, goats, rope, river salt. There was traffic on the water for the first time in Marisol's adult life, and most of it needed someone willing to run cargo fast and ask no questions about tariffs.

She asked questions anyway. She just charged extra for the answers.

The hull went from patched to plated to something approaching armored, because the consortium was dead but its creditors were not, and there were men upriver who still thought a fast boat was a threat rather than an opportunity. She reinforced the transom for a second engine she couldn't afford yet. She built a cabin where the old fuel tanks had been — two bunks, a stove, a shelf for charts. Wren drew the layout for her on the back of a supply manifest, all clean lines and no wasted space, and refused payment twice before Marisol left a crate of oranges on her doorstep and drove off before she could refuse a third time.

Some debts you paid in currency. Some you paid in fruit.

On the morning the *Segunda Vez* first floated with her new plating, Marisol sat on the dock with her boots dangling over the water and drank coffee that Teo had made badly and felt, for the first time since she was nineteen years old, that the boat beneath her belonged to a future instead of a grudge.

Her brother would have hated it. Her brother had believed the river only ever took.

She looked down the channel, where the morning light lay flat and gold on the current, and thought: it gives back too. You just have to be stubborn enough to wait.

*Bram*

The mission school had forty-one students, three chalkboards, one roof that leaked in exactly the wrong places, and Bram Okafor-adjacent — no, just Bram, he'd given up his surname along with his reputation and hadn't decided yet whether he wanted it back — standing at the front of the oldest classroom with a lump of sandstone in each hand.

"Water," he said, "always wins."

He held up the stones. "These are the same rock. This one" — he raised the left hand — "sat on a hillside for ten thousand years and is exactly as hard as the day it formed. This one" — the right — "spent those same ten thousand years in a riverbed." He knocked them together. The riverbed stone rang dull and hollow; the hillside stone cracked clean. "Guess which one I'd rather build a house on."

Forty-one faces looked back at him. In the third row, a girl named Adaeze — sharp-eyed, gap-toothed, the daughter of a woman who had once run supplies past consortium checkpoints — raised her hand.

"Is this about the flood?"

Bram smiled. Everything was about the flood, now. The whole valley taught its children in the grammar of that eruption: the day the buried spring blew its cap and the under-river came home.

"It's about lying," he said. "It's always about lying. Somebody told the valley there wasn't enough water. Somebody falsified my reports — well. My reports. I wrote them. They paid me to write them wrong." He set the stones down. He had promised himself he would say this part plainly, every year, to every class, until the shame wore smooth as river stone or he died, whichever came first. "I was the best geologist in the territory, and I sold the truth because they offered me money and I was tired of being poor. Forty-one of you exist partly because other people fixed what I broke. That's not a reason to feel good. It's a reason to do the work right."

Silence. Then Adaeze again, hand already up, insufferable and wonderful:

"So which stone would you build on?"

"The broken one," Bram said. "You build on what's been through the water. It knows something the dry ones don't."

After class he stayed behind and graded maps. Wren sent him her old teaching charts, hand-corrected, the new course of the river inked in her precise cartographer's hand, and he copied them onto butcher paper for the walls. Children learned fastest from maps of places they knew. And every child in this valley now knew a place that hadn't existed on any honest chart a year ago: the gorge where the river vanished, and the caverns beneath, and the spring that ran free.

He pinned the newest chart above the chalkboard. Underneath it, in small letters, he wrote the only motto he trusted anymore:

*Check the source.*

*Teo*

Ranger Captain Ilsa Ferro had a reputation that preceded her by about half a canyon: strict, humorless, allergic to stowaways, and — according to everything Teo had heard — Hazel Sandoval's rival since before either of them made rank.

Which was why it was so strange that she'd said yes.

"You'll sweep the post every morning before drills," Ferro said, walking him through the station at a pace designed to lose him. "You'll log every vessel that passes. You will not touch the radio without supervision. And if you ever, *ever* climb onto my roof again—"

"That was one time."

"It was twice. Once before I knew your name and once after, which means you didn't learn." She stopped at the radio room door and turned, and her face did something complicated — the look of a woman deciding whether to say a thing she'd already decided to say. "Sandoval vouched for you. Personally. Do you understand what that costs a man like Hazel? He doesn't spend trust. He hoards it against bad years."

Teo looked at his boots. "I know what I cost people."

"Do you?" She opened the door. Inside, the radio room hummed — the same make of set that had crackled lies into Hazel's ear upriver, though this one had been swept for tampering twice and would be swept again tomorrow. "Then you know the difference between costing and owing. Costing is what happens to people who take you in. Owing is what you carry afterward. I don't want your guilt, boy. Guilt sits still. I want the thing that moves."

She put a headset in his hands. It was heavier than he expected.

"First lesson," Ferro said. "Listen before you speak. On the river, on the radio, everywhere. Most trouble comes from people transmitting when they should have been receiving."

Teo settled into the chair, put the headset on, and listened. Static, mostly. The river talking to itself. But underneath it, faint and steady, the whole basin breathing: traders checking in, villages reporting water levels, a world rewiring itself around a spring that wouldn't stay buried.

He picked up a pencil and started his first log entry. His handwriting was terrible. It would get better.

*Hazel & Wren*

The house sat on the bluff above the mission village, close enough to hear the school bell and far enough not to hear Bram's fourth-period geology lecture, which everyone agreed was one lecture too many per day. It had two rooms, a porch, a roof that Wren had personally re-shingled while arguing with Hazel about load-bearing walls, and a wall — the good wall, the one facing the river — where the compass hung.

Brass casing split down the middle. Needle snapped at the pivot, frozen at an angle that pointed at nothing: not north, not the source, not anywhere. Wren could have repaired it. She was probably the only person in the territory who could have. She had taken it apart once, laid the pieces out on a cloth, looked at them for a long time, and put them back together broken.

"Some instruments," she said, when Hazel asked, "are more honest ruined."

They kept it mounted between the window and the door, so that anyone leaving the house passed under it, and anyone arriving saw it first. Visitors asked about it. Marisol pretended not to. Teo, on his monthly runs down from Ferro's post, touched it lightly with one finger on his way in, the way other people touched a saint's medal.

Hazel woke early, as he always did — years of debt-collecting hours ground into him deeper than the debt itself — and stood at the wall with his coffee while the light came up over the water. Below, the *Segunda Vez* sat at her mooring, loaded for the week's run: flour, lamp oil, school supplies, a crate of oranges with no recipient listed. Downstream, the villages were building. Not rebuilding — building. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole point.

Wren came up behind him and put her chin on his shoulder. She smelled of ink and river air.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"Doing what?"

"Standing guard over a compass that doesn't work."

"I'm having coffee."

"You're standing at attention in front of a broken instrument at dawn, which is either guarding or praying, and you're Protestant enough that we can rule out one of them."

He laughed — really laughed, the kind that still surprised him coming out of his own chest — and turned, and kissed her, and for a while neither of them guarded nor prayed nor did anything useful at all.

Later, on the porch, she spread her working charts across the table: the valley, the new channels, the villages strung along the free-running water like beads on a wire. Her first honest chart hung framed in the tribunal hall in town. These were the working copies, annotated, alive, corrected in pencil because the river was still deciding what it wanted to be.

"There's a stretch here," she said, tapping the upper reaches, "past the old gorge. Nobody's surveyed it since the flood. The course shifted — maybe a lot, maybe a little. Ferro wants a chart before the trading season peaks."

"Upriver," Hazel said.

"Upriver." She looked at him over the table, and her eyes had the same expression they'd had in the flooded mission, rifle leveled, except that now the fear in them had burned down to something steadier. "It's a two-week run. Marisol's boat, Bram's instruments, Teo begging Ferro for leave. The whole ridiculous crew."

Hazel looked at the compass on the wall. Broken. Pointing nowhere. Needing to.

His whole life, he had understood the world as a ledger: what you owed, what you were owed, what could be seized to balance the difference. His brother's debts. His badge. His hours. Even love, when it finally came, had arrived looking like a liability on a balance sheet — the woman he should arrest, the truth he should file, the choice between the law that made him and the people who saved him.

But ledgers closed. Debts got paid. What remained after the arithmetic was done — that was the part nobody taught you to carry.

"Yeah," he said. "All right. Upriver."

Wren grinned and pulled a fresh sheet toward her, and began, in her precise cartographer's hand, to draw the edge of a map that didn't exist yet.

Above them, on the wall, the broken needle hung motionless in the morning light — pointing nowhere, needing to, waiting the way rivers wait: patiently, and always, and toward the sea.
