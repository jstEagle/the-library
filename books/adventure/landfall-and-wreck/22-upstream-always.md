## 22. Upstream, Always

The radio woke me before the sun did.

I'd learned to sleep through most things — gunshots, thunder, Marisol's singing — but the radio had a particular crackle that cut straight through whatever dream I was having and dropped me into the old habit: hand out, volume up, listen before you speak.

"—repeat, any station, any station. Survey party four days overdue at—" Static chewed the rest of it. Then, faint as a voice through a wall: "—upriver. We are upriver and we cannot—"

Silence.

I lay in the dark of our room above the mission school and listened to the hiss fill the space where a person used to be. Somewhere out there, past the last bend anyone bothered charting, someone had stopped talking mid-sentence.

Wren was already awake. I could tell by her breathing — she never slept deep anymore, not since the gorge, maybe not since before that. In the gray light coming through the shutters I watched her eyes open and find mine without her moving anything else.

"Upriver," she said.

"Upriver."

She closed her eyes for a moment, and I knew she was doing what I was doing: running the map in her head, the one she'd redrawn with her own hands after the flood, every reach and riffle of it honest now. Four days overdue. The dry season starting early. The stretch above Serpent's Neck where the canyon walls came close enough to touch both sides with an oar.

"Somebody's surveying up there again," she said. "After everything."

"Somebody always is."

"That's not comforting, Hazel."

"It wasn't meant to be." I sat up. The floorboards were cool under my feet. "It's just true."

* * *

By full light the whole crew knew, because Teo had heard the repeat broadcast on the schoolhouse set and done what Teo always did — told everyone, loudly, before anyone had decided anything. That was the boy's gift and his curse. He'd been a stowaway once, half-starved in the hold of Marisol's boat, and he'd never gotten over the discovery that being useful was the fastest way to be kept. Now he was sixteen and wore a ranger's trainee badge that Superintendent Ochoa had grudgingly issued after the tribunal, and he treated every emergency like a personal invitation.

"We're going," he announced in the yard, where Bram was chalking geology lessons on slate for a ring of mission kids who mostly wanted to hear about the flood again. "Obviously we're going."

"Nobody said we're going," I said.

"You're packing your kit right now."

I looked down. I was, in fact, holding my kit. There was no good answer to that, so I didn't try to make one.

Bram set down his chalk and dusted his hands. He was slower these days, grayer, but his eyes still went sharp when somebody mentioned the upper river. "Four days overdue," he said, more to himself than to us. "If they took the east fork, there's nothing out there but bad water and worse rock. If they took the west—" He stopped. He'd spent twenty years falsifying reports for men who owned the west fork, and even now, even after testifying, even after the consortium's directors went to trial and the land titles burned like the paper they were, he couldn't say certain sentences without flinching.

"If they took the west," Wren finished for him, quietly, "then they might have found something worth killing for. Again."

The kids had gone quiet, sensing the weather change. One of them, a girl named Pilar who'd lost her village to the burnings, raised her hand like this was still a lesson.

"Yes, Pilar?"

"Are you going to come back?"

It was such a simple question and it landed harder than any of the others. I crouched to her level. "We always come back. That's the deal."

"The last crew didn't."

"No," I said. "They didn't. And that's exactly why we go." I tapped the slate where Bram had drawn the water table, the buried spring, the new course of the river. "Somebody has to be the ones who come back."

Marisol arrived mid-sentence, as she always did, boots first. She'd heard the broadcast too, or Teo had run to the docks and told her, which amounted to the same thing. She looked at the four of us standing in the yard looking at each other, and she sighed the sigh of a woman who had already loaded the skiff.

"I gassed her yesterday," she said. "Don't ask me why. I just had a feeling."

"You always have feelings," Teo said.

"And they're always right, which is why you should fear me." She jerked her chin toward the dock. "Tide turns at noon. If we're going, we're going."

* * *

We were going.

That was the thing I understood now, standing on the deck while Wren spread her charts across the crate we used as a table — the thing the debt had taught me and the flood had confirmed. For years after Mateo died I'd thought of myself as trapped. The debts, the badge, the long patrols across flats that never changed. A holding pattern. I'd believed that if I could just pay it all off, some other life would open up on the far side, a life I'd choose instead of endure.

But the debt had never really been money. Money was just how it got measured. The debt was this: people upstream, in trouble, and me downstream with a boat and a reason. Mateo had owed it. I'd inherited it. And somewhere between the flooded mission and the gorge of the silent spring, between tearing up my incident log and watching the river take back what was stolen, I'd stopped counting it as something owed to me and started counting it as something owed by me. Paid forward, never paid off. The collectors had come for the money once, after the tribunal, and the crew had covered it without a word of discussion — Marisol slapping down a pouch, Bram emptying his teaching stipend, Teo contributing eleven crumpled bills and a gold tooth of dubious provenance. But nobody had treated it as settling anything. It was just what the family did.

"East fork or west?" Marisol asked, hand on the tiller.

Wren ran her finger along the chart. Her charts. The first honest maps this river had ever had, drawn by a woman who'd once hidden in a flooded ruin with a rifle and a compass that pointed at the truth instead of north. That compass hung on our wall now, its needle dead, mounted in a frame Teo had built from driftwood. It pointed nowhere. It didn't need to. Everything it had ever needed to tell us, we carried.

"West," Wren said. "The distress call gave coordinates. Sloppy ones, but they put them above the old mission trail." She glanced at me. "Close to where you found me."

"Then we know the country," I said.

"We know it better than anyone alive," Bram said, and there was something in his voice — not quite pride, not quite grief. The voice of a man who had helped bury the truth and then helped dig it up, and would spend the rest of his life making sure it stayed dug up.

Teo appeared at my elbow with the radio log. "I transcribed the call. Three voices, maybe four. They sounded young." He hesitated. "One of them said 'ranger' at the end. Like they were calling for us specifically."

Of course they were. Word traveled downriver faster than water. The villages that had drunk free this season, the families that had come home to land the consortium couldn't steal anymore — they talked. A ranger who chose the crew over the badge. A cartographer whose maps couldn't be bought. A smuggler, a disgraced geologist, a kid with a trainee's tin star. Upstream, when things went wrong, somebody always said the same sentence: *send for Sandoval's people.*

Not the department. Not the law. Sandoval's people.

Ochoa had offered me my commission back after the tribunal. Full reinstatement, back pay, a desk if I wanted it. I'd taken the badge and left the desk, worked out an arrangement that made the superintendent grind his teeth: I answered distress calls on the upper river, filed honest reports, and reported to no one he could name. He called it a rogue operation. I called it a ranger's job, finally done right.

"Cast off," I said.

Marisol grinned and threw the line.

* * *

The river had changed since the spring ran free. That was the strangest part, the part visitors never believed. Where the water had been a thin brown rope through cracked mud, it ran fuller now, greener, and the banks that had been salt-scalded wasteland were patchy with the stubborn green of things trying again. Not paradise. Just a chance. Downstream villages had planted in the wet season for the first time in a decade, and the wells that had been brackish ran sweet.

But upriver, past the last of the settled reaches, the country went back to being what it had always been: narrow canyons, blind bends, water that could kill you in six different ways before lunch. The flood had rearranged half of it. Wren stood at the bow with her notebook, correcting her own charts in real time, calling depths and hazards back to Marisol, and I watched her work and felt the thing I felt every time — that quiet astonishment that she was here, that we were here, that the woman I'd nearly shot in a ruined mission had become the fixed point my whole life swung around.

She caught me looking. "You're staring."

"Making sure you're real."

"I'm real. And you're going to make us hit that snag if you don't watch the channel." But she smiled when she said it, and came back to stand beside me at the rail, shoulder against shoulder, the way we'd learned to stand through rapids.

"Four days overdue," she said. "Could be mechanical. Could be fever. Could be nothing."

"Could be."

"Or it could be men with guns again."

"It could be that too." I turned the radio dial through the static bands, listening. Nothing but hiss and a distant weather report in a language neither of us spoke. "Whatever it is, we'll handle it."

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true."

She leaned into me. Below us, Teo was coiling line with the quick competence that had saved all our lives in the under-river, and Bram was explaining sediment layers to Marisol, who was pretending not to listen and absorbing every word. The skiff rode light and fast, gassed and provisioned, carrying everything we owned that mattered, which had never been much.

"Hazel," Wren said, after a while. "Do you ever think about stopping? Just — living somewhere flat and safe, where nobody goes missing?"

I thought about it honestly, the way she deserved. About a house with thick walls. About sleeping through the night. About a radio that stayed silent.

"Every bad day," I said. "And then somebody calls for help, and I remember that I don't actually want the silence. I just want the silence to mean everyone's okay." I nodded at the canyon walls sliding past, at the unknown waiting above the next bend. "This is the only shape my life fits. Took me forty years and one dead compass to figure it out."

"Some debts you pay forever," she said. She'd heard me say it before. She said it back anyway, the way you repeat a vow.

"Gladly," I said.

Ahead, the river bent west, toward the high country, toward whatever had gone quiet four days ago. The current picked up. Marisol opened the throttle. Teo scrambled to the bow to watch for rocks, and Bram checked the medical kit for the third time, and Wren uncapped her pen over a fresh page.

I keyed the radio one more time, sent our call sign rolling upriver into the static — *this is Sandoval's party, we are coming, hold on* — and smiled.

The water was high. The crew was aboard. We were upstream, always.

And somewhere ahead, faint through the hiss, almost too broken to believe — a voice answered.
