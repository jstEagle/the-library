## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The river told you its mood before it told you anything else.

Mara Lockridge had learned that at nine years old, standing barefoot on the Lena's foredeck while her father held the wheel and made her read the water aloud like scripture. *Sandbar forming off the left bank—see how the current wrinkles there, like a frown. Snag under that brown foam—the river doesn't foam for nothing. Deep channel hugging the far side where the herons stand.* Her father had said a river was a face, and if you looked long enough, it stopped being water and became expression. She had thought it was just something fathers said to keep their daughters quiet.

Twenty years on, she knew better. The Vasseur had moods the way old men had moods: slow to change, quick to punish, full of grievances nobody remembered causing anymore.

This morning it was sulking.

She stood at the wheel with the brass rail warm under her palms and watched the light come up gray-gold over the eastern bluffs, and she read the river the way other people read weather or letters from home. The surface lay flat as poured tin between the sandbars, but underneath, in the places where the current bent around submerged spits of silt, there were little seams of darker water, threads pulled tight. A sulking river. Holding its breath. Somewhere upstream in the high country the monsoon was still weeks out, but the Vasseur already knew about it. Rivers always knew first. They felt the rain coming the way old scars felt weather, and they grew heavy with anticipation, thick and slow and secretive.

"Bar's shifting again," Okonjo said beside her. He said it without looking up from the lead line he was coiling, because he'd been saying some version of it since before dawn, and because on the Lena you didn't need to finish sentences. "Third time this season."

"I see it."

"You always see it." He hung the coil on its peg. "That's not the same as liking it."

Mara didn't answer. There wasn't anything to say that the river hadn't already said. The great bar below Kessel Bend had been building all season, a pale shoulder of sand rising out of the channel like something surfacing to look at them, and every week it crept another boat-length toward the deep water, narrowing the passage, squeezing the current faster through the gap. By late season it would close the bend entirely and the steamers out of Port Anselm would have to find another way or stop coming. But that was late-season trouble, and Mara did not trouble herself with things more than a few days downstream. That was another thing her father had taught her, though he'd taught it by dying of it rather than saying it aloud.

Below, on the main deck, the morning cargo was waking up. Crates of salt pork and gunpowder lashed under canvas. Bales of indigo going downriver to the dye houses. Six goats who had opinions about everything and expressed them in a language of complaint that never varied. And old Madame Cissé's coffins, three of them, empty for now, bound for the delta towns where people paid good money to be buried above the flood line. Death, like everything else on the Vasseur, traveled by boat.

The Lena was forty feet of black-hulled stubbornness, a stern-wheeler built before the war by shipwrights who had expected her to last ten years and been dead too long to be embarrassed by their arithmetic. Her paddle wheel turned with a sound like slow applause. Her boiler ate wood with an appetite Mara found honest, at least; the river took its toll silently, but the firebox at least complained where you could hear it.

She rang the engine bell twice—half ahead—and the deck shivered beneath her boots, and the Lena leaned into the current like a woman leaning into wind, and the day began.

* * *

They ran the bend at mid-morning, threading the gap between the new bar and the drowned willows on the far side, and it went the way such things went when Mara was at the wheel, which was to say that nothing happened, which was the whole art of it. Nothing happening was the most expensive commodity on the Vasseur. Passengers paid for it without knowing that was what they were paying for. Cargo owners trusted her with their goods because nothing happened around the Lena, and none of them ever asked why, and she never told them.

Why would she? Telling them would mean telling them about the ledger she kept in her head instead of in a book—a book could be seized, a head could only be shot, and even then it kept its secrets better than most men kept theirs. It would mean telling them that she knew the depth of the channel at Redcliff Bend within a hand's width in any season, that she knew which snags the flood of six years ago had left and which ones the beavers had added since, that she knew the Vasseur the way a widow knows a house she can't afford to leave: room by room, creak by creak, including the rooms that no longer existed except in memory.

It would mean telling them her surname. And that she did not do.

On the manifests she was M. Lennard, master, of the steamer Lena, home port listed as whatever town she'd last bribed a clerk in. Lennard had been the maiden name of a woman who'd sold her eggs in the Kessel market twenty years ago and died without anyone to remember her, which meant the name was free, which meant it was safe. Downriver, in the customs houses and the survey office windows with their clean glass and their clerks with clean hands, there was another name attached to charts and expedition reports and a file that had never been closed, and Mara had spent half her life making sure the two names never touched.

Lockridge. Even thinking it felt like stepping on a board she knew was rotten.

She put it away and gave her attention back to the water, because the water deserved it and because the water didn't ask questions.

* * *

Noon brought them to the landing at Petit-Merle, a shelf of packed clay and rotted pilings where the forest came down to drink, and they took on wood and let off two bales of indigo and one passenger, a cloth merchant named Aubert who had spent the entire trip upstream of her trying to make conversation and downstream of her trying to pretend he hadn't. He shook her hand at the gangplank with the careful enthusiasm of a man handling something he suspected might bite.

"A fine vessel, Captain. Remarkable, really, how you read this river. I've ridden boats on the Vasseur for thirty years and I've never seen a master know the water so—" he searched for the word, "—personally."

"She tells me things," Mara said. "I listen."

Aubert laughed, pleased, and went up the landing path toward the village, and Mara watched him go and felt the small familiar tightening in her chest that was not quite regret and not quite relief. He would tell the story tonight over palm wine: the woman captain who talked to rivers. It was a harmless story. Harmless stories were armor, if you wore enough of them. Let them think she was a curiosity, a character, a woman with a boat and a quirk. Curiosities got remembered wrong, and remembering wrong was almost as good as forgetting.

What she couldn't afford was to be remembered right.

Okonjo appeared at her elbow with the wood tally, and behind him young Ferren, who was seventeen and all elbows and appetite and asked questions the way other boys threw stones, constantly and without aiming.

"Captain," Ferren said, "the factor at Petit-Merle says there's a survey launch come up from Port Anselm. Passed him yesterday, flying the blue pennant. Asking after boats."

Mara took the tally and read it without reading it. "Survey launches ask after boats. That's what survey launches do."

"This one's asking after boats that ain't registered." Ferren had the particular courage of the very young, which is to say he hadn't yet learned what it cost. "Asking after boats running cargo without papers. Asking after—" he dropped his voice, though the nearest listener was a goat, "—after the Lockridge girl's old boat."

The name landed in Mara's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall a long way before it hit bottom.

"That girl's dead," she said, and her voice came out level, which cost her something. "Drowned with her father eleven years ago. Everyone on this river knows that story too."

Ferren blinked. "I only meant—"

"I know what you meant." She handed back the tally. "Count the wood. We cast off in twenty minutes."

He went. Okonjo stayed, which was his privilege and his punishment both, twenty-two years on the river and the patience of a man who had learned that silence was a tool you could build almost anything with.

"You could register," he said quietly. "Pay the fees. Take the number. Be done with hiding."

"And give the survey office my route, my cargo, my home port, and my name, all in the same ink?" Mara watched a kingfisher work the shallows below the landing, electric blue against the brown water, patient as a debt. "Vale's people don't want me registered, Okonjo. They want me documented. There's a difference. A registered boat is a boat they can find."

"They found us anyway, looks like."

"Then we'll be somewhere else when they arrive. That's the whole trick of it." She turned back toward the wheelhouse. "Wood counted?"

"Counted."

"Then we're somewhere else."

* * *

They ran the afternoon stretch with the sun full on them and the heat rising off the banks in visible waves, and Mara fell into the trance that steering put her in, the good trance, the one that was half attention and half prayer. The Vasseur unspooled ahead of her, wide and brown and patient, carrying its red silt down from the highlands the way it had carried it since before there were names for any of this, since before there were chart offices in Port Anselm with clean glass windows, since before anyone decided that a river should belong to somebody.

Her father had believed a river belonged to itself. He'd written that once, in the margin of one of his charts, in that slanted, impatient hand of his, and the survey office had spent eleven years and considerable money trying to prove him wrong—or rather, trying to prove that whatever he'd found out there past the last surveyed reach, past the gorge, past the fork where the maps went blank, belonged to the Crown instead. They wanted his charts. They had wanted them for eleven years, politely at first and then less politely, and Mara had sometimes wondered, in the hollow hours of the middle watch, whether they wanted the charts at all, or whether what they wanted was simply to be sure that no one else had them.

Either way, the charts were gone. Burned with the rest of it, the night the news came upriver. All except the parts she carried in her head, and those she carried the way you carry a wound: invisibly, and always.

The light began to fail around five, going amber, then copper, then the bruised violet that meant the river would soon be unreadable until dawn. Mara brought the Lena into the night anchorage below the old mission bluff, a cutbank pool deep enough to swing in, sheltered from the current, and the crew tied her off with the ease of long habit while the fireflies came up out of the grass in their thousands, stitching the dark together.

Supper was rice and dried fish and the goats complaining about it. Afterward the crew settled onto the cargo hatch in the last light, and Okonjo tuned his thumb-piano, and for a while there was music, and Mara sat apart on the foredeck with her back against the flue casing, feeling the boiler's warmth through her shirt, and let the evening happen to her.

This was the part she was best at, she thought. Not the rapids, not the bends, not the reading of sandbars. This: being alone in company. Being the still point. Twenty years of learning how to be necessary and unknowable at once, and she had gotten so good at it that some nights she could almost convince herself it was the life she'd chosen rather than the one the river had left her.

Sable Thorne would have laughed at that.

The thought arrived uninvited, the way those thoughts did, maybe four times a year, usually at dusk, usually when the fireflies were out. Sable Thorne, who had spent one furious luminous summer on this boat when they were girls, before her father dragged her off to the diggings, before the years and the silence swallowed everything. Sable, who used to sit exactly where Mara was sitting now and say that Mara piloted like she was apologizing to the river, and that someday she ought to try commanding it instead.

Mara hadn't thought of her in months. She thought of her now, thoroughly, watching the fireflies, and the loneliness of it was so precise and so old that it barely hurt anymore. It had gone past hurting into architecture. It was load-bearing.

Downstream, faint on the evening air, came a sound she catalogued without deciding to: a steam whistle, thin and high, two notes, from the direction of Petit-Merle. Too far to identify. Too far to matter.

Probably.

She listened anyway, the way you listen for a name you've trained yourself never to speak, and the river carried on around the anchored hull, dark and murmuring, keeping its own counsel, holding whatever it knew about tomorrow the way it held its silt—in suspension, and without mercy.

The whistle sounded again. Closer now. And it was not the two-note call of any commercial packet she knew.
