## 2. A Stranger at the Landing

The rain had been threatening all week without the decency to fall, and the air at Tamarind Landing sat thick as tallow. Mara Lockridge felt it in her shoulders first, the old barometer of her body that no instrument aboard the *Lena* could match, and by the time she'd finished greasing the paddle shaft she knew the weather would hold another two days—maybe three. Long enough to load. Long enough to be gone before the sky finally broke its promise.

She wiped her hands on a rag that had long ago surrendered any claim to cleanliness and stood at the rail, looking down at her dock.

It wasn't much of a dock. Fourteen pilings of black ironwood, half of them softened by rot and river worms, a deck of rough-sawn planks that she'd replaced board by board over six years until not a single original timber remained. It was hers, though. That was the point. In a country where everything worth owning had a name stenciled on it in colonial blue—*Property of the Vasseur Survey, by right of charter*—the dock belonged to no ledger. She'd bought it through a Chinaman in Port Meridian who never asked her surname, and she'd paid in coin that had passed through so many hands it had forgotten its own history, same as she intended to.

The *Lena* sat low in the brown water, riding light. She'd run cargo down from the head of navigation that morning—kerosene tins, salt pork, a crate of survey stakes that she'd delivered to the government depot with a smile she kept in a jar for exactly such occasions—and now she'd take on rubber and dried fish for the return. Routine. The river rewarded routine the way a crocodile rewarded stillness: not with love, but with a temporary decision not to eat you.

Below, the landing dozed in the heat. Madame Ossang's trading house with its corrugated roof ticking as the metal expanded. The fish racks. The leaning sign that read TAMARIND LANDING in letters some clerk had painted twenty years ago, the R's gone to rust. A dog of no particular breed lay in the one stripe of shade the whole settlement offered, panting like a bellows with a hole in it.

Mara was about to go below and argue with her engineer about the starboard bearing—he'd been making a sound like a man clearing his throat all afternoon and insisting it was nothing, which was precisely what her father used to say about engines, right up until they seized—when she saw the man on the far bank.

She saw him before she understood what she was seeing. A shape at the treeline, moving with the particular broken rhythm of someone whose body had stopped negotiating with him. Not walking. Spending. Each step cost something he couldn't afford, and he was spending anyway.

He came down to the water where the mud went soft, and he stood there a long moment, swaying, looking across at the landing the way a drowning man looks at a light on shore. The river was maybe sixty meters wide there, brown and slow and full of everything the wet season upstream wanted rid of. There was no ferry. There was no bridge. There was the *Lena*, and the *Lena* was on her own side.

The man waded in.

Mara's breath caught. The channel ran deep in the middle—she'd sounded it herself, a hundred times, four and a half fathoms at low water—and this was not low water. The man went in to his knees, his waist, and then he went under and did not come up, and the river folded over the place where he'd been as if he'd never existed at all.

"Ah, no," she said aloud, to no one, to herself, to the stubborn animal part of her that had already started moving. "No, you don't."

She took the stairs of the pilothouse three at a time, took the gangplank at a run, and by the time she reached the dock's edge the man had surfaced twenty meters downstream, coughing, one arm windmilling, the other clamped against his chest like a bandage. He wasn't swimming. He was being carried by the current and arguing with it, and the current was patient, and the current always won.

Mara looked at the water. She looked at the boat hook leaning against the last piling. She did the arithmetic she always did—the weight of a drowning man, the slip of wet timber, the four and a half fathoms that had taken her father's deckhand Baptiste when she was fourteen and had never given him back—and then she stepped off the dock into the shallows, hook extended, and waited for the river to bring him to her.

It did. The river brought her everything, eventually. That was its nature and its cruelty.

She got the hook under his arm where the elbow bent and hauled, and he weighed nothing, that was the terrible thing, nothing, a frame of bone and wet canvas with a skin of leather stretched over it. She dragged him up the mud and onto the planks and he collapsed there, streaming, and for a moment she thought she'd hooked a corpse. Then he coughed out half the Vasseur and began to breathe in long, tearing, grateful gulps, and she crouched over him, dripping, her heart going like the *Lena*'s paddle wheel at full steam.

"Don't," he rasped, before she could speak. His eyes were open—gray, fever-bright, focused on her face with an effort that seemed to cost him everything he had left. "Don't say it. Don't say *fool*. I know."

"I wasn't going to say fool," Mara said.

"What, then?"

"Dead man. I was going to say dead man. You nearly were one."

He managed something that had once been a laugh. It came out as a series of clicks, like a dying engine. "Nearly," he agreed. "The river and I have an understanding. It keeps missing."

Up close he was worse than he'd looked from the rail. Sixty, maybe, or fifty worn down to sixty. A beard gone white and matted. Hands like roots, split at every knuckle, the nails black. His shirt was canvas, expedition-weight, the kind the outfitters in Port Meridian sold to men going where outfitters' goods were the last thing to matter, and it had been mended and remended with wire and sinew until the original cloth was more memory than material. And clutched against his chest, under the ruined shirt, wrapped in his arms the way a mother wraps a child, was a brass tube the length of her forearm, sealed at both ends with wax the color of dried blood.

Mara looked at the tube for a long moment. Something turned over in her chest, slow and heavy, like a stone shifting on a riverbed.

"You'll want the trading house," she said, standing. "Madame Ossang will—"

"No." The word came out flat and final, and then he ruined it by shivering so hard his teeth chattered. "No trading house. No depot. No—no officials." His eyes tracked past her, up the landing, quick and hunted. "You're the boat. The riverboat. The one that runs without papers."

Mara went very still.

"I don't know what you mean," she said. "I run cargo. I have a registry."

"You have a registry the way I have a fortune." He tried to push himself up on one elbow and failed, and lay back against the planks, breathing. "I've asked after you at four landings. The survey office asks too. They ask at every landing on the river, a man in a white uniform, polite as a priest. *The boat that runs light and runs late and never files a manifest.*" His gray eyes found hers and held them, and there was something in them that she recognized, something she saw every morning in her own reflection and did not love. "They want your charts, girl. The Lockridge charts."

The name went through her like cold water through a hull.

She had not heard it spoken aloud in six years. She had paid good money to make sure she never would again. And here was a half-drowned stranger saying it on her own dock, in the open air, where Madame Ossang's boy could hear it from the fish racks if the wind turned, and the wind was turning, the wind had been turning all week.

"You're mistaken," she said, and her voice was steady, and she was proud of how steady it was. "I don't know any Lockridge. I'm called—"

"Mara." He said it softly, and the fever in his voice made it sound like something out of an old story. "Mara of the *Lena*. I know your father's hand. I've slept beside it in the rain. I've eaten from the same tin as the man who drew those charts, and I watched—" He stopped. His jaw worked. The tube in his arms seemed to weigh more than he did. "I watched what happened to him. At the end."

The dock tilted. Or she did. She put a hand on the piling and the world steadied itself, grudgingly, and the dog on the far side of the landing barked once at nothing and went back to its panting.

"You're a liar," she said. "Or a madman. Or you're working for Vale, and if you are—"

"Vale." He spat, and it was mostly river water. "Adrien Vale. Yes. He's on the river. Two launches, thirty men, a warrant with more seals than a beach." He coughed again, longer this time, and when it passed he was grayer than before, and Mara understood with a cold, professional clarity that she was watching a man run down like an eight-day clock. "I've been ahead of him for eleven days. Sleeping in the water. Eating—" he gestured vaguely at the river— "whatever this offers. I'm done running. I'm done, do you hear me? There's no more ahead. There's only you."

"Then you've come to the wrong place."

"No," he said. "I've come to the only place."

* * *

She got him aboard against her own better judgment, which was a familiar feeling, like an old coat she'd outgrown and kept wearing anyway. She told herself it was because a corpse on her dock would draw the survey office faster than a beacon, which was true. She told herself it was because Madame Ossang would talk, and the fish-rack boy would talk, and the whole river would know by morning that a dying man had named the *Lena*'s captain out loud, which was also true. She told herself a third thing, about simple decency, and did not examine it too closely, because the third thing was the truest and the most dangerous.

She laid him in the forward hold, on the canvas cot she kept for deckhands who drank too much, and Okonjo looked at her over the man's gray head with his eyebrows raised and said nothing, because Okonjo could say more with his eyebrows than most men could with a pulpit.

"Fever," Mara said. "He came out of the river half-dead. I'm putting him off at Port Meridian."

"Port Meridian's two days downstream," Okonjo said mildly. "We're loading for upstream."

"I know what we're loading for."

"Yes, Captain." He handed her the basin and the cloth and went back to his cargo manifests, and if his eyebrows had opinions, he kept them to himself, and Mara loved him for it and would have shot him if he'd said one word more.

The man—Thorne, he'd said, when she demanded a name, Elias Thorne, as if the name should mean something to her, and it did, it did, and she hated that it did—sank into shivering sleep and then out of it again, surfacing in delirium, talking to people who weren't there. She heard names in the mumbling. *Amélie. The boy with the lantern. Don't drink the green water, Amélie, for God's—* And once, clearly, in a voice gone suddenly young: *Lockridge, get them out, I'll hold the line.*

She sat with the cloth in her hand and did not move for a long time.

Her father had been dead six years, officially. Lost with his expedition, upstream, in country no charter map admitted existed. The survey office had sent a letter of condolence with a blue stamp on it and, eight months later, a second letter requesting the return of all expedition property, charts included, for the purpose of *completing the historical record*. She had burned the first letter in the *Lena*'s boiler and used the second to wrap fish. The charts themselves she had never surrendered, because they were not hers to surrender and not theirs to take, and because her father had taught her that a map was not paper. A map was a promise. You did not give your promises to men with blue stamps.

Now a dying man lay on her cot, clutching a sealed tube like a rosary, and called her father's name in his fever, and said *I'll hold the line* in the voice of a man who had not, in the end, held anything.

* * *

Toward evening he surfaced clean, the way the fevered sometimes do, a window opening in the storm of him. She was sitting beside the cot because there was nowhere else to sit in the hold, and she told herself that too. His eyes found her and this time they were clear, and terribly, lucidly tired.

"How long?" he asked.

"Since the river? Two hours. Since you last ate? I'd guess longer than that."

"I meant," he said, and his hand moved, weakly, to the tube, "how long until the monsoon."

Mara looked at the porthole, where the light had gone the color of brass. "Nineteen days. Maybe twenty. The barometer's been sulking all week."

"Nineteen days." He closed his eyes. "Then there's time. Barely. There's barely time, but there's time."

"Time for what, Mr. Thorne?"

He opened his eyes again and studied her, and she had the uncomfortable sense of being sounded, depth by depth, the way she sounded the river. Whatever he found seemed to satisfy him, or else he was simply too far gone to keep testing.

"I need passage," he said. "Upstream. All the way up. Past Kessel, past the Redcliff, past the last charted water, to the head of the Vasseur. To the source."

"The source is a story," Mara said. "Prospectors' talk. There's no source. The river comes out of the gorge and the gorge comes out of the mountains and the mountains don't give up their water to men with boats."

"Your father found it."

The hold went very quiet. Somewhere above, a deckhand's boots crossed the deck. The river muttered against the hull, patient, patient.

"My father," Mara said carefully, "found nothing. My father is dead."

"Yes," Thorne said. "He is. I know better than anyone living how dead he is." His hand closed on the tube, and the knuckles went white, or whiter. "I was there. That's the whole of it, girl—that's the confession and the bargain and the sin of it, all in one. I was there, and I came down the mountain, and he didn't. And I have spent eleven years telling myself I'd go back when I was ready, and I am out of ready." He pushed himself up against the bulkhead, and it cost him, and she almost moved to help him and did not, because some part of her understood he needed to do this sitting up, needed to say what he had to say from something like level ground. "I'm dying. Not fever—fever's just the courier. It's in my lungs. The doctor at Kessel told me what the cough meant and I told him what he could do with his tonics. I have weeks. Perhaps as many as the monsoon allows, and no more."

"Then rest," Mara said. "Port Meridian has a hospital."

"Port Meridian has a hospital and a survey office, and the survey office will take this—" he lifted the tube an inch— "off my corpse before the fever's cold, and it will go into a drawer in a blue-stamped building, and everything your father bled for will become a filing error." His voice cracked and steadied. "I won't allow it. I've allowed too much. I won't allow that."

Mara stood. She walked to the porthole and looked out at the water going dark, at the landing lamps coming on one by one across the channel, small and yellow and indifferent. Her reflection looked back at her, ghosted over the river, a woman she maintained carefully: no surname, no history, no debts. A boat that ran light and ran late and filed nothing.

"Say the bargain," she said at last, without turning around. "You didn't cross a crocodile river to make conversation. Say it."

She heard him breathe. She heard the tube shift in his arms.

"The map to the source," he said. "Your father's map—the last one, the true one, the one the survey never saw. It's in this tube, and it's yours by every right that matters. I'll give it to you. All of it. In exchange, you carry me up that river before the rain comes. You put me at the headwater pool with my own two feet on the ground, or as close to it as these legs will take me. That's all. That's the whole of what I have left to want in this world." A pause, and then, quieter, with something in it that was almost gentle: "I know what I'm asking. I know what it costs to run the upper water uncharted. I watched it cost your father everything. But I'm asking anyway, because you're the only one left who can say yes, and I am the only one left who can tell you what really happened up there. One truth for another, Mara. One river for another."

Mara stood at the porthole a long moment, watching her ghost-self hang over the darkening water.

"Let me see it," she said.

The wax cracked under his thumbnail with a sound like a small bone breaking. He tipped the tube, and a roll of oilcloth slid out into her hands, tied with waxed cord, and she untied it with fingers that were not quite steady, and unrolled it on her knee by the light of the porthole.

The paper was good paper, expedition stock, and the ink had faded to the brown of old blood, but the hand was crisp, confident, beautiful—the hand she had learned her letters from, the hand that had written *Mara* on the flyleaf of her first primer, the hand she had last seen on a letter that said *back by the dry season, little fish, mind the boiler.*

She read only enough. The river's spine, drawn bold. The gorge. And beyond the gorge, where every charter map she'd ever stolen a glance at simply stopped, a tributary drawn in fine, sure strokes, with soundings, with notes in the margins in that same beloved hand—*green water, don't drink; narrows at the standing stone; the pool lies under the twin peaks*—and at the head of it all, boxed and underlined twice, a single word in her father's writing:

*SOURCE.*

The hold tilted again. Or she did.

"Well?" Thorne said softly, from the cot. "Do we have terms, Captain?"

Mara rolled the map closed with great care, the way you'd close a coffin, and held it against her chest, and found that her eyes were wet and was glad the porthole light was behind her.

"Nineteen days," she said. "You'd better hope the river's kind."

Above her, on the deck, she heard Okonjo's boots stop directly over the hold and stay there, listening, and she knew that whatever he heard in the silence below, he was already counting the cargo they'd have to leave behind to make the head of the Vasseur before the rain.
