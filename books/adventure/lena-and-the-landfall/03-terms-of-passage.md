## 3. Terms of Passage

He slept like the dead for eleven hours.

Mara knew because she checked on him at every bell, ostensibly to see whether he'd stopped breathing and could be moved off her deck without ceremony, but really because there was something in his sleep that held her—the way his hands stayed curled even in unconsciousness, fingers hooked as if still gripping a pick or a rope or some other instrument of a hard life, and the way his lips moved sometimes, shaping words too soft to catch. Once she leaned close enough to smell the fever on him, sweet and sour like fruit rotting in a closed room, and once he said a name she didn't catch, and once he said *no* very clearly, with an old terror in it that made her step back from the bunk as though she'd walked into a wall of heat.

By morning the rain had passed off downriver and the light came slantwise through the Lena's cabin windows, gilding the dust motes, gilding the grey stubble on Thorne's cheeks, gilding the wax-sealed tube that lay on the shelf above his head where Mara had put it within his sight so he would know it was safe—and know, also, that she had not touched it.

She had thought about touching it. She wanted that recorded honestly, if only to herself. She had stood in the lamplight the night before with the tube in her hands for the length of three breaths, feeling its weight, reading the plainness of it—waxed canvas over brass, the seal stamped with no crest, only a letter T pressed deep by a thumb—and she had put it back unopened because opening a dying man's property while he slept felt like a theft she couldn't take back. Not because of law. Because of the look in his eyes when he'd pressed it toward her on the dock, that terrible naked hope, as if the tube were the last organ of his body still functioning.

Now he woke.

It happened between one bell and the next: his breathing changed, the shallow flutter of fever giving way to something slower, deeper, deliberate, and then his eyes opened—pale eyes, river-grey, bloodshot at the rims—and found her where she sat mending a net by the window, because she had decided that if he woke he would find her working, not watching. A captain did not watch her cargo. She mended the net and let him find his bearings.

"You're on my boat," she said before he could speak. "The Lena. You collapsed on my dock at Sarn's Landing two nights ago. I paid the landing-master for your berth and I've been feeding you my crew's rations. When you can stand, we'll discuss what you owe."

His mouth worked. He tried to sit up, failed, tried again, and she set down the net and crossed to push him back by the shoulder—gently, but firmly, the way she handled anything on her boat that needed handling. Under her palm his shoulder felt like a bundle of kindling wrapped in skin.

"Water," he rasped.

She gave him water, holding the cup because his hands shook too badly, and he drank like a man who had learned never to trust the next drink to arrive. When it was gone he lay back and looked at the ceiling beams and breathed, and she returned to her net, and for a long while neither of them spoke. The river talked instead, slapping the hull, worrying the mooring lines. Somewhere upstream a boatman sang a hauling song with more grief than tune in it.

"Lockridge," Thorne said at last.

Her needle stopped.

"I asked at the landing who'd taken me in. The landing-master said the riverwoman. Said nobody knows your name, but the old men call you Lockridge's girl." His voice was gravel dragged through water, but underneath the ruin of it there was something steady, something that had once been used to command or to argue or both. "I knew a Lockridge."

"Everyone on this river knew a Lockridge," Mara said. "He hauled cargo for twenty years. Half the landings have a story about him."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know that's not what you meant." She tied off a knot harder than it needed tying. "Drink your water and don't spend your strength on talk. Whatever you came upriver to say, it will keep."

"It won't," he said. "That's rather the point of me."

* * *

He ate at midday—rice and dried fish and the last of the ship's biscuits, which he ate slowly and completely, leaving nothing, a man who had starved enough to have opinions about crumbs. Then he asked her to open the cabin shutters so he could see the river, and she did, and he looked at the brown water sliding past for so long that she began to wonder if he'd fallen asleep sitting up.

"The monsoon comes early this year," he said finally. "Birds are already moving down the valley. I saw them from the ridge above Kessel—a whole sky of them going south, weeks ahead of schedule. The barometers at the trading posts are falling and half the factors are too greedy to read them." He turned his head. His pale eyes were clear now, fever-bright but focused, and she understood suddenly that everything since waking had been him spending strength deliberately, hoarding it for this. "I need to be at the headwaters before it breaks."

Mara set down the net. "The headwaters."

"The source of the Vasseur. The true one—not the survey office's little pencil line that stops at the gorge and says *unexplored*." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; it turned into coughing, and she waited it out with her arms crossed, telling herself the tightness under her ribs was impatience. "Everyone knows the river dies in the gorge. Everyone official, anyway. It doesn't die. It turns. There's a tributary the maps don't carry, and it runs up into country where the survey boats have never been, and at the end of it there's a pool of blue water under a cliff face, cold as a dead man's kiss, and that pool is where my father spent thirty years of his life trying to get, and where I intend to die."

"You intend to die at the headwaters," Mara repeated flatly.

"I intend to *arrive* there," Thorne said. "The dying is already arranged. It's just waiting on geography."

She should have refused him then. She knew it even as the silence stretched—knew it the way she knew the pull of a sandbar under clean-looking water, some instinct older than reasoning saying *this is where you turn the wheel*. A dying man wanted passage into unmapped country during monsoon season. Every sane answer was no. Her crew was seven people whose wages she owed, on a boat whose hull she'd patched twice since the spring floods, running a route that only stayed profitable because she kept it small and quiet and beneath anyone's attention.

But he had said *the survey office's pencil line*, and something in her had leaned forward like a dog hearing its name.

"What's in it for me?" she asked. "Passage to the headwaters is six hundred miles against current through the worst water on the river, into a season that kills boats, on a tributary that isn't charted—which means no pilots, no soundings, no rescue if we come to grief. What does a dying prospector offer that makes that arithmetic work?"

Thorne reached up with a shaking hand—not for food, not for the cup, but for the shelf above his bunk. His fingers found the tube and drew it down and laid it across his knees, and for a moment he simply looked at it, the way a man looks at a thing he has carried so long it has grown into him.

"This," he said.

"A map."

"The map." He worked at the wax seal with his thumbnail, and she watched the red wax crumble away in flakes, and felt her heartbeat change tempo without her permission. "I was going to sell it, years ago. To the survey office, or the trading companies, whoever paid. Could have bought a farm with it. A fleet." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, all teeth and no warmth. "Couldn't do it. Some things you keep because selling them would mean they were never true. So instead I'm going to give it to you, and you're going to give me a promise, and we'll call it trade."

"Give it to me," Mara said carefully, "and what exactly do you want in return?"

"Carry me up. Before the rains. Get me to that pool alive—or near enough to alive that I can touch the water." He held the tube out across the space between them. "And when it's done, the map is yours. Not a loan. Yours. Sell it, burn it, trade it to Vale himself if you want—I won't care, I'll be past caring. But until then it's yours anyway, because I can't pilot a boat and I can't fight a river, and a map in the hands of a man who can't use it is just paper waiting to rot."

She didn't take it.

That was the strange part, the part she would turn over later in the dark hours at the wheel: that her hand, which had never hesitated over money or cargo or any honest transaction on that river, hung at her side and would not rise. Something about the tube, about the weight of it being offered freely, made taking it feel like a door swinging open onto a room she had nailed shut long ago.

"Why me?" she asked. "There are other captains. Bigger boats. Boats with engines and insurance and owners who could be bribed."

"Because bigger boats can't make the upper water. Because owners can be bought means they can be sold, and I need someone who can't be." His voice dropped, roughened. "And because you're a Lockridge, and a Lockridge reads water like scripture. I saw your boat come into the landing two nights ago, in the dark, in the tail of a storm, and you brought her in on the flood tide through a channel I remember having four feet less water than you were drawing. I watched you do it and I thought: *there*. That's the hand I want on the wheel when the river tries to kill us."

"You watched me bring her in?"

"I've been watching you for three days. Since Kessel." He had the grace to look slightly abashed. "I heard there was a riverwoman who ran cargo quiet and asked no favors, and I followed her landings down from Kessel hoping to buy a berth, and then the fever took me faster than I planned, and the rest you know."

Mara stood very still. Three days. He had been on the landings, in the taverns, among the boatmen, for three days, asking after her—and she had known nothing. That fact frightened her more than the fever had, more than the dying man on her deck. She had built her life on being unseen, and here was proof that invisibility was a garment with holes in it.

"Show me the map," she said.

* * *

He unrolled it on the bunk between them, weighting the corners with the cup, the biscuit tin, his own trembling hands.

It was drawn on linen-backed paper gone soft and yellow with age, and it was beautiful in the way that only working documents are beautiful—every line functional, every mark earned. The Vasseur ran down the left margin in confident ink, and the survey office's territory ended abruptly at the gorge, exactly as Thorne had said, the official line dissolving into blank space marked with the survey stamp and a date twenty-two years old.

Beyond the blank space, in different ink—older ink, browner, laid down with a finer nib—the river continued.

Tributaries branched like veins. Soundings crowded the channels in tiny patient numerals. Rapids were marked with the cartographer's shorthand for danger, and campsites with small crosses, and one stretch of bank bore a notation in a tight, slanted hand that Mara could not read from where she knelt but recognized instantly, completely, the way you recognize a voice through a wall after twenty years.

She knew that handwriting.

She had grown up watching it. She had seen it on cargo manifests and chart margins and letters home that began *My dear Isolde* and were signed with a flourish she used to practice copying in the dust of the dock. She had watched that hand grow drunker and more desperate in the last year of his life, the letters leaning like a man walking into wind, and she had burned most of those letters when he died because keeping them hurt more than losing them, and she had kept only one, and she knew the exact shape of every word on it.

The map was in her father's hand.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. *His.* The particular way he crossed his sevens. The way his numerals climbed slightly uphill. The little hook he put on the terminal of every sounding, a habit he'd picked up from his own master and complained about and never lost. And the ink was old, older than the survey stamp, which meant—

"When," Mara said. Her voice came out wrong, flattened, as if heard through the hull. "When did he draw this?"

Thorne was watching her. She saw him register the change in her, whatever it was that showed in her face, and she saw him decide not to pretend surprise.

"Twenty-three years ago," he said quietly. "The summer of the last expedition."

The words went through her like cold water through a plank seam.

The last expedition. Two words that had hung over her entire childhood like weather. Eleven men had gone upriver beyond the gorge with her father in command, and eight had come back, and her father had not been among them—had come back, in fact, three months later, alone, half-mad with fever, refusing to say what had happened, refusing to speak of it ever, drinking himself from respected master to cautionary tale in the space of a single wet season. The survey office had questioned him twice. He had told them nothing. They had confiscated his charts—all of them, officially, every sheet—and he had signed the inventory with that same slanted hand, and Mara had always believed, because everyone believed, that the river beyond the gorge had died with him.

And here it was. Alive. In his hand. Dated, sounded, annotated, *finished*—this was no sketch, no fragment. This was a completed chart of waters the empire's own maps called empty.

"He told them he surrendered everything," she whispered.

"He surrendered everything they knew to ask for," Thorne said. "I was there when he hid this one. I helped him hide it." He paused. "Mara. Look at the date in the corner."

She looked. Beneath the scale bar, in her father's neat capitals: a date, and beneath the date, a single word she hadn't noticed, small, almost hidden in the border decoration:

*RETURNED.*

"They think he never finished," Thorne said. "They think the expedition broke apart in the gorge and the survey was lost, and that's why they've spent twenty years buying up every scrap of paper connected to it. They're not hunting a map, girl. They're hunting proof that their maps are lies. And your father carried the proof out of the gorge alone and hid it and let them believe him broken." His hand closed over hers on the edge of the chart, and his grip, for all the fever, was strong. "He died protecting this. I ran from that expedition when it turned bad—I've owed that debt twenty-three years, and I'm too late to pay it to him. So I'm paying it to you."

Mara looked down at the map and the river rose up in her—her father's voice reading soundings aloud in the dark of the wheelhouse, the smell of his pipe, the one letter she'd kept, the shame she'd carried at being the daughter of the man who broke, the careful smallness of the life she'd built in the shadow of that story. All of it cracked along a fault line she hadn't known was there, and through the crack came not grief exactly, nor rage, but something with the weight of both.

"Say the terms again," she said. "All of them. Precisely."

"You carry me to the headwater pool before the monsoon breaks. You keep the map safe until then. After—" he shrugged, and winced, and went on, "—after, it's yours outright. My promise on it. It's the only thing I have left worth promising with."

"And if the river takes us? If the season turns early, as you say it will?"

"Then the map goes to the bottom with the boat, and neither of us will be in a position to complain."

She almost laughed. It startled her, the laugh, rising out of the cracked place like a bird out of cover. She pressed it down.

Outside, the light had gone long and amber; evening was coming up the river the way it always did, without hurry, certain of its welcome. She could hear Okonjo on deck coiling lines, singing low to himself, and the ordinary sounds of the Lena settling into the night, and she understood that whatever she said in the next minute would reach further than any words she'd spoken in ten years of quiet running.

"If I agree," Mara said, "we leave tomorrow on the morning tide. No delays for your recovery—you ride or you're left. My crew takes orders from me and only me, including you. And if at any point I judge the boat or the lives aboard her to be done, we turn around, and the deal is void, and you'll thank me for it or you'll curse me, but you'll live to do one or the other." She held out her hand. "Those are my terms. Take them or find another fool."

Thorne looked at her hand, and then at her face, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been recognition—as if he were seeing, in the set of her jaw, a man twenty-three years drowned.

"Agreed," he said, and took her hand, and his grip was dry and hot and did not let go at once. "Captain."

She took up the map, rolled it, slid it back into the tube, and sealed the cap. It was lighter than she expected. It was heavier than anything she had ever carried.

At the cabin door she paused, the tube in her hand, the evening river gold beyond the glass.

"Who else knows it exists?" she asked. "Besides you. Besides the dead."

Thorne's smile came back, the terrible one.

"No one," he said. "Which is why, Captain, we sail before anyone learns to ask."
