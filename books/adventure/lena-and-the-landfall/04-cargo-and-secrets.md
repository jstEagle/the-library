## 4. Cargo and Secrets

The letter had taken eleven weeks to find her, and when it did it was not a letter at all but a scrap of oilcloth with three lines scratched into the waxed side in a hand that shook like a leaf in a gale. *Sable. Coming up the Vasseur. Find me before the rains.* No signature. He didn't need one. She would have known that handwriting anywhere — she'd known it on the backs of her school lessons, on the margins of her mother's recipe book, on the single postcard a year that arrived from wherever Elias Thorne had dug himself to that season. Eleven words, and every one of them an apology he was too proud or too broken to write out.

She read it forty times on the coach north. She read it again in the ferry shed at Vasseur-mouth while the rain came down like a curtain being pulled across the world. And then she folded it small enough to hide inside her palm, because there was no version of this story in which her father wanted to be found.

* * *

Sable Thorne had spent nine years learning how to be someone's absence.

It began the winter her mother died, though of course she hadn't understood it that way then. Grief makes cartographers of children; she had mapped the house by its silences. The chair where her mother sat was not to be moved. The wardrobe smelled of camphor and lavender for four years afterward, and her father never once opened it, and neither did she, and between them they built a country out of things unopened, unsaid, ungrieved aloud. Then the spring came when Elias packed his kit and stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands and said, *There's work upriver, love. Good money. I'll send for you when I'm settled,* and Sable — sixteen, furious, fluent in the language of doors closing — said, *Send for me whenever you like. I won't come.*

He had looked at her for a long moment. Not hurt. Worse than hurt: patient. The way a man looks at weather he knows is coming and has decided not to argue with.

Then he went, and the letters thinned from monthly to seasonal to the annual postcard, and Sable told herself a story about it that let her sleep. The story was: he chose the river over me. It was a clean story. It had a villain, and the villain wasn't her. She kept it polished all through the years of the boarding house in Marroway, through the mill work and the mill strike and the walking out, through two winters of thin soup and one of none, and the story held its shape right up until the oilcloth scrap arrived and she spread it flat under a lamp and saw, beneath the shaking words, pressed faintly into the wax like a watermark, the ghost of something written and scraped away.

She held it to the light at an angle. Turned it. Held it closer.

Someone had written more and thought better of it. Under the lamp's hard eye she could make out the shadowed troughs where a stylus had pressed and been rubbed smooth: *—should have—* and then nothing, and then lower down, half-legible, *—your mother would—* and that too erased. Whatever he had meant to say, he had decided dying men shouldn't say it. But the pressure of the pen remained, the way pressure always remains, and Sable sat in the ferry shed until the lamp burned low, reading not the letter but the letter under the letter, and understanding at last that the story she'd kept for nine years was wrong in the way that matters most: it wasn't that he'd chosen the river. It was that he'd never forgiven himself for something, and he had decided she was better off far away from whatever it was.

That was worse. That was so much worse. Because you could hate a man who chose against you. You could put him down and walk away and be done. But a man who exiled himself out of shame — a man who looked at his daughter and saw, every time, the thing he couldn't undo — that man you could only fail, forever, at loving properly.

So she came north. Of course she came north. Stubbornness ran in the family like brown eyes.

* * *

Vasseur-town stank wonderfully of tar, wet rope, frying fish, and the particular green rot of a river in late summer, and Sable walked its length twice before she let herself ask anyone anything. Asking questions in a river town is like dropping bait; what comes up is rarely what you were fishing for. She learned within an hour that everyone knew the *Lena* — knew her lines, her whistle, her captain — and that no one, not the chandler, not the fuel seller, not the woman who ran the pie stand at the head of the dock, would tell her a single thing about Mara Lockridge except that she paid fair and asked no questions and expected the same courtesy returned.

*A private woman,* the pie woman said, handing over a pasty hot enough to blister. *Keeps herself to herself. You wanting work?*

*Wanting passage,* Sable said, and watched the woman's face close like a purse.

Passage upstream on the *Lena*, it turned out, was not a thing money bought. Money bought deck space on the mail packets, the fat slow government boats with their registry numbers painted on the paddleboxes and their manifests read aloud at every landing. Money bought those easily. What money didn't buy was a berth on a boat that answered to no office, flew no house flag, appeared on no schedule anyone would admit to. Sable stood at the rail of the public pier that evening watching the *Lena* lie at her own dock fifty yards downstream — a long low riverboat, black-hulled, white superstructure gone grey with sun, her name on the bow in flaking gold — and understood that the boat was exactly what her father's letter implied: a way of moving upriver that left no wake in any ledger.

Which meant, if her father had bargained his way aboard, that he'd found the one vessel in the territory as invisible as he'd made himself. Which meant, further, that whoever captained her was either a fool or a person with reasons of their own.

Sable had grown up around prospectors. She knew fools. This was something else.

* * *

She watched the *Lena* for two days.

This was easier than it sounds, because watching was the one skill her childhood had perfected. A girl raised in a house of silences learns to read rooms the way sailors read water — the set of a jaw, the pause before an answer, which chair a man avoids. She took a room above the cooperage whose back window faced the river, and she learned the *Lena*'s rhythms the way you learn a heartbeat. Cargo loaded at dawn, mostly baled cotton going up and tin coming down. The crew was small — a big West African deckhand with forearms like mooring posts whom the town called Okonjo, a wiry engineer who smoked in the engine hatch and swore at the boiler in two languages, a boy of maybe fifteen who did everything else. And the captain: tall, dark-haired, moving along her own deck with the unhurried certainty of someone walking the floor of the only house she'd ever owned. Sable watched her take a line from the dock one morning in a swell that had other boats yawing, and watched her lay it over a bollard without looking at it, and thought: *there. That's who carries him.*

She tried, once, honestly, to do it the open way. She walked down to the *Lena*'s dock on the second afternoon and found the captain coiling rope on the afterdeck and said, *Ma'am. I'm looking for passage to the headwaters.*

Mara Lockridge looked up. Up close she was younger than the dock gossip suggested and harder, with a face that seemed built for weather rather than for company, and eyes the exact flat grey-green of the river in October. She finished her coil first. Sable noted that, filed it away: this was a woman who finished things before she attended to people.

*Headwaters' a long way,* Lockridge said. *What's up there for you?*

*My father.*

Something crossed the captain's face — fast, gone before it finished arriving, but Sable had been reading faces since she was six and caught it anyway. Not surprise. Recognition, almost. As if the word *father* had knocked on a door in her too.

*Name?* Lockridge said.

And here was the hinge of the whole thing, the moment Sable would turn over a hundred times in the nights afterward. All she had to do was say it. *Thorne. Elias Thorne. He's sick, he came to you, I know he did — the prospector, grey beard, half-dead on your dock two nights ago.* Say it plainly and either the captain helped her or she didn't, but everything after would be honest, and honest things, however hard, could be survived. Her mother had taught her that much before the silences took over the house.

Instead Sable heard herself say: *Delacroix. Marie Delacroix. I've family at the mission past Redcliff Bend.*

The lie came out smooth as oiled rope, and she hated herself for it even as it left her mouth, and she could not have stopped it, because underneath the lie was the true thing, the thing she'd carried since the oilcloth scrap: *if I say his name out loud, I'll have to share him.* Nine years of postcards, eleven words of dying apology, and some starved animal part of her could not bear the idea that this stranger — this capable, guarded stranger — might already know more about Elias Thorne's last days than his own daughter did. Might already have his confidence. Might already have heard whatever it was he'd written and scraped away.

Lockridge studied her for a moment longer. *No passengers,* she said. *Cargo only. Sorry.*

But her eyes went somewhere Sable's face for just a beat — to the tube-shaped bulge, maybe, or nothing at all — and Sable walked back up the dock knowing two things with absolute certainty. One: her father was aboard, or had been. Two: the captain knew who Sable was, or would the moment anyone said *Thorne* within earshot of the boat, and had chosen, politely, to give her nothing.

Fine. Sable Thorne had been given nothing her whole life. She'd gotten good at taking.

* * *

The third night was moonless and the rain held off, which the cooperage's old man called a bad sign — *the river resting up for something* — and Sable used it for what it was worth.

She'd planned it the way her father must once have planned his routes: obsessively, on scraps, alone. She knew the watch changed at ten and that Okonjo took midnight; she'd counted the crew ashore at dusk and seen only the boy return. She knew the *Lena*'s gangplank came off at dark and that the gap between the boat's gunwale and the dock pilings was perhaps four feet of black water, crossable by the fender line that hung at the stern where the launch was tied. She had one bag, packed like a confession: her mother's shawl, a change of clothes, a tin of willow bark tea, the oilcloth letter in its oilcloth wrapping, and — wrapped separately, at the bottom, because some things you carry whether or not you mean to use them — a folding knife her father had given her at fourteen, saying, *A Thorne travels armed,* the last gift he ever handed her in person.

She waited until the pie stand's lamp went out. She waited until the packet boat's generator coughed silent. She went down the cooperage stair in stockinged feet with her boots slung round her neck, and the town behind her was so quiet she could hear the river working at its pilings, that deep patient chewing sound water makes against wood, as if the Vasseur were slowly eating the whole idea of the town.

The *Lena* lay black on black water, one lantern burning dim in the wheelhouse, another low aft near the engine hatch. Sable crouched on the dock edge in the smell of tar and bilge and listened to the boat breathe — hull timbers ticking as they cooled, water lapping in the well deck, somewhere below, faint as memory, a man's voice muttering in fever. Her chest seized so hard she had to put a hand against a piling.

He was aboard. He was alive. Muttering, feverish, alive.

*Coming, Papa,* she whispered, absurdly, to the dark hull, as if the boat were him, as if the boat would carry the message down to wherever he lay. *Hold on. I'm coming, and I'm sorry, and I'm not sorry, both, all of it, everything.*

Guilt is a strange cargo. It weighs nothing and sinks everything. All the way across that four-foot gap — fender line under her palms, boots knocking once against the hull, heart slamming — Sable rehearsed the speech she'd been building for nine years, the one that began *you should have sent for me* and ended *I should have come anyway*, and she understood, hanging over the black water with her arms shaking, that the speech was really only one sentence wearing a long coat: *I loved you the whole time and I never once told you, and now the clock is running.*

She came over the gunwale soft as fog and crouched in the shadow of the deckhouse among coiled hose and a stack of crates stencilled FLOUR, and the boat rocked her gently, the way you'd rock a child, and she began, very quietly, to look for a place to exist.

Under the afterdeck stairs: too exposed, the crew passed hourly. The flour hold: dry but locked. She was working aft toward the launch cover, measuring her breaths, when the wheelhouse lantern brightened — someone trimming the wick — and she froze, and in the freeze she heard, drifting down from the wheelhouse, a woman's voice, low, talking to no one, or to the river, or to the boat itself. Reading soundings off to herself. *Twelve feet at the bar. Falling.* And then, softer, almost tender, in a tone Sable recognized because it was the tone her own voice used alone in her room: *Not yet. Hold on. We go when we go.*

Captain Lockridge, keeping her own vigil. Keeping watch over something below decks that muttered in fever.

Sable closed her eyes in the dark and felt the strangest sensation move through her, halfway between grief and kinship: the knowledge that the woman above her was also awake at midnight, also carrying something heavy, also talking softly to a man who might not last the season. They were two people keeping the same bedside from opposite sides of a wall, strangers, adversaries-in-advance, and neither of them knew it yet.

She found her place at last: a shallow lazarette right aft, under the steering gear, accessed by a hatch the size of a coffin lid, lined with spare canvas. It smelled of hemp and mouse. It was exactly the size of a person who had spent nine years making herself small. She lowered herself in, drew the hatch to within a finger's width of closed, and lay in the dark listening to the engines tick as they cooled, and the river chew the pilings, and above her, faint through the deck planks, the captain's footsteps crossing and recrossing the wheelhouse like a pulse.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, meaning the captain, meaning the lie, meaning the trespass, meaning all of it. *I'll work. I'll pull rope till my hands bleed. I'll be cargo that carries itself. Just get him upriver. Just let me reach him before—*

Before. The word hung in the dark of the lazarette like a lamp with the wick turned too low.

She slept in fragments, waking at every shift of the hull, every footstep overhead, and once — deep in the smallest hour — to a sound that undid her completely: her father's voice, clear through the planks, not muttering now but calling, urgent and lost, a name repeated three times. Not her name. A man's name. *Josiah. Josiah. Josiah—*

Her father had never once spoken of a Josiah. Her father had never once spoken of anything, that was the whole wound, and here was a name rising out of his fever like a body surfacing from deep water, a name that mattered enough to call for while dying.

Sable lay in the canvas-smelling dark with her hand pressed flat against the hull planks, as if she could pass her palm's warmth through the wood to him, and made the promise she'd actually come to make, the one under all the others: *Whoever Josiah was, Papa — whatever you did, whoever you lost, whatever you wrote and scraped away — I'm here now. Tell me or don't. But don't die with it all still inside you. Don't do to me what the silence did to us.*

Above her, the wheelhouse footsteps stopped.

Then started again, faster, heading for the companionway ladder — heading down, toward the fever, toward the voice calling a dead man's or a living man's name in the dark — and Sable held her breath in her coffin-sized hiding place and understood that the clock she'd been racing had just begun to run without her.

Morning would find her. Morning, or the captain, or the fever, or all three at once.
