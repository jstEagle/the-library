## 5. The Survey Man

Kessel Wharf smelled of tar and rotting fruit and the particular sweetness of river water gone stale in the sun. The Lena lay against the pilings with her holds half-empty, riding high as a woman who has set down a heavy load and not yet decided what to carry next. I stood on her deck in the hour before noon, watching the town assemble itself out of the heat haze—tin roofs flashing, the customs house flag hanging limp as a dead man's tongue—and I felt the mood of the day before I had any reason for it.

The river had told me nothing. That was the trouble. A river always tells you something: where the sandbars have shifted in the night, where the current runs sullen or quick, where the crocodiles have dragged their kills into the shallows to soften. But that morning the Vasseur had come down flat and brown and silent as a shut door, carrying no message at all, and I had learned long ago that when the river keeps its mouth closed, it is because someone upstream has paid it to.

I was coiling line when the launch came.

It came the way survey launches always do—too fast for the channel, throwing a bow wave that slapped against the moored barges and set them grumbling against their fenders. White hull, brass fittings polished to a shine that hurt the eye, the colonial crest painted on the stern in colors that would peel by season's end while the crest itself never faded, because there was always another clerk with another brush. It cut across the wharf approach with the confidence of a vessel that has never once been made to wait, and it swung alongside the Lena's berth with a precision that was either excellent seamanship or excellent arrogance, and from what I saw of the man in the bow, I judged it was both.

"Captain Lockridge."

He said the name before his boot touched my deck. That was the first thing wrong with him. Not *captain*—that much courtesy any inspector extends—but the surname, spoken whole, spoken plainly, in a port where I had spent six years being nobody's daughter of anybody, just the woman who ran the Lena, no name needed, no name wanted. He said *Lockridge* the way a man lays a card face-up on a table.

I set down the coil. "You have me at a disadvantage."

"Captain Adrien Vale." He stepped aboard without asking, which was the second thing wrong with him, though he did it lightly, almost gracefully, one hand touching the rail as if the Lena were an old acquaintance he was pleased to see again. "Colonial Survey Office, Hydrographic Division." He produced a leather wallet and opened it toward me, and inside was a badge and a photograph of himself looking younger and somehow less honest. "I'm conducting routine inspections of vessels operating above the Middle Reach."

"Routine," I said.

"Entirely." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was the kind of smile that had been measured and fitted like a uniform, and I thought: this man has practiced that smile in a mirror until it stopped being a thing his face did and became a thing he carried. "May I?"

He gestured at the deck around him, and I understood he was asking permission for something larger than a walk. I weighed refusing him. The customs men could be refused, sometimes, for a price. The survey office could not be refused at all; they simply wrote your name in a ledger, and then you discovered, months later, that every lock on the river had been told to open slowly for you, every fuel depot to count its drums twice before selling you anything, every insurance clerk to lose your papers. The survey office did not punish. It dried things up. It was a drought wearing a uniform.

"Mind the hatch coaming," I said. "She bites ankles."

* * *

He walked the Lena the way a buyer walks a horse he intends to purchase cheap. His fingers found the seams in the planking. His eyes went up the mast and down into the hold, along the cargo booms, over the engine housing where Okonjo had left the cover off, and I watched him catalog everything—the age of her, the strain in her, the places where she'd been patched and the places where she hadn't—and I understood that he wasn't inspecting her seaworthiness at all. He was reading her history. A boat tells you where she's been, if you know how to listen, and this man listened the way a magistrate listens to testimony: waiting for the lie.

"She's older than she looks," he said, straightening from the engine housing. "Or younger than she should be. Someone has loved this vessel past all economic sense."

"My father built her."

"I know." He said it mildly, wiping his fingers on a handkerchief, folding the handkerchief away. "That is rather the point, Captain."

We stood in the shade of the deckhouse, and the heat pressed down on the wharf like a thumb on a bruise. Somewhere behind the fish market a woman was singing, badly, to a child. I kept my hands loose at my sides and my face arranged into the expression I used for customs men and drunk passengers: attentive, empty, faintly bored.

"The Lockridge charts," Vale said. "You'll know of them."

"There are charts in the wheelhouse. Delta approaches, the Middle Reach, the Redcliff bends. Drawn by my father, corrected by me. They're available for inspection whenever the office likes."

"They are indeed. We've inspected them. Twice." He turned a slow circle, taking in the wheelhouse windows, the river beyond them, the far bank where the jungle stood in its green wall, breathing. "What we have never inspected is the rest of them."

"There is no rest of them."

"No?" He looked back at me, and for a moment the polished surface of him shifted, and underneath it I saw something that was not polite at all—a hunger, quick and bright, like a fish turning under brown water. "Jonas Lockridge surveyed the upper Vasseur for eleven years. Eleven years, Captain. He mapped tributaries that appear in no other record. He filed reports through the Kessel station until—" Vale paused, delicately, a man stepping around a puddle, "—until his final expedition, after which he filed nothing at all. The office holds his early work. It does not hold his late work. Every surveyor who followed him into that country has come back with blanks on their sheets where his knowledge should have filled them. Blanks, Captain, are expensive."

"He died upriver. Whatever he had died with him."

"That is one theory." Vale moved to the rail and rested both hands on it, looking downstream toward the delta as though the whole river were laid out for his consideration, which perhaps, in his mind, it was. "Another theory holds that a careful man, growing old in a country that takes everything, might keep his best work close. Might copy it. Might entrust it to—" he turned his head, just slightly, so that I caught the edge of his gaze, "—family."

The word sat between us. I let it sit. Behind my ribs my heart was working harder than it needed to, and I hated it, hated that this man in his white suit could reach into my chest with a single word and find the old grief still warm, still tender, six years buried and not one day healed.

"My father drowned," I said. "The river took his boat, his crew, and his papers. If you want to recover his charts, Captain Vale, I suggest you dredge the Vasseur."

"We have dredged the Vasseur." He said it without embarrassment, and that frightened me more than anything else he'd said. Somewhere in some office a man had signed an order, and men with chains and grappling hooks had raked the bottom of a hundred miles of river looking for paper. "We recovered an anchor, a stove door, and a great deal of silt. Rivers keep what they're given, Captain. People are less reliable custodians."

* * *

He asked to see the wheelhouse, and I led him there because leading him myself was better than letting him wander. Inside, it was cool and dim and smelled of varnish and old wood and the particular ghost-smell of my father's pipe tobacco that no amount of scrubbing had ever fully lifted, though whether the smell lived in the wood or only in my memory I had stopped trying to determine years ago.

Vale stood very still in the middle of it. His eyes went to the chart table first—I had expected that—and I watched him read the charts lying open there, the ones I'd offered him, the honest ones, and I watched him understand that they were exactly what they appeared to be, and I watched that understanding disappoint him.

"You correct these yourself," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"When the river changes, the chart changes or it lies."

"And the river changes often."

"Every flood. Every dry season. The Vasseur is not a document, Captain. It's an animal."

Something moved through his face at that—approval, maybe, or its cousin—and he leaned over the chart table and studied the inked corrections in my hand, my small precise letters beside my father's broader strokes, two handwritings layered on the same paper like two voices in conversation across a grave. When he spoke again his voice had changed register, dropped its official weight, become almost gentle, and I understood that this too was technique, that he had a whole wardrobe of voices and was trying them against me one after another like keys in a lock.

"You know what I admire about Jonas Lockridge's work? Its honesty. Other surveyors flattered the river—drew channels wider than they were, marked shoals where the governor's steamers could pass without embarrassment. Your father drew what was there. Shoals, snags, drowned villages, the whole truth of it." He straightened. "The office hated him for it. And relied on him completely. There is a kind of man, Captain, whom institutions cannot forgive precisely because they cannot function without him."

"You've come a long way to deliver a eulogy."

"I've come to offer you one." He turned to face me fully then, and the light through the wheelhouse windows fell across him in bars, and I remember thinking how easily he wore the shadows, how at home he seemed in the dimness of my father's cabin, as though he had already imagined himself standing here many times. "Legitimacy. Registry under your own name—your full name, restored to the rolls with your father's commendations attached. Priority berths. Fuel contracts. A commission as a licensed pilot of the upper reaches, which would make you the only one, since everyone else who knew that water is dead or retired or unwilling to say so." He paused. "And protection. Which you will need, Captain, because there are people above the Middle Reach who remember Jonas Lockridge, and not all of them remember him kindly, and a woman running alone without registry is a woman no one will ever come looking for."

The threat arrived dressed as kindness, the way it always does. I felt it settle over me like the wet heat off the river, and beneath it the cold thing, the fear, moving in my stomach with its slow eel patience. Not fear of him. Fear of how reasonable he sounded. Six years of invisibility, and here was a man offering to make me visible, legitimate, named—everything I had trained myself not to want—and he had priced it at exactly the one thing I could not pay.

"I run cargo," I said. "Timber, rice, salt, whatever pays. I don't need a commission."

"Everyone needs something." He took out a card—not a government card, I noticed, but a plain one with a handwritten number—and laid it on the chart table, weighting it with my father's brass dividers, which I am certain he did deliberately. "When you find whatever it is you're carrying that you haven't told me about—and you will find it, Captain, because men like your father don't die clean, they leave threads, and threads get pulled—you'll want to talk to me before someone else talks to you. The office can be generous. It can also be thorough. You'd prefer generous."

* * *

He left the way he'd come, without asking, pausing at the gangway to look back at the Lena with that appraising buyer's eye, and I stood in the wheelhouse doorway and watched his launch churn away downstream until the sound of its engine dissolved into the ordinary noises of the wharf. Then I picked up his card, read the number without needing to, and held it over the little spirit stove I kept for coffee, and watched it curl and blacken and go to ash among the coffee grounds.

Ash was not forgetting. I knew that. Ash was only making sure the evidence of the conversation stayed mine.

I stood a long time at the wheelhouse window afterward, looking upriver, past the last roofs of Kessel, past the green wall, toward the country where the river forked and forked again into channels no living chart admitted to. My father's handwriting swam in my mind—not on any paper I owned, but in memory, the big confident letters of a man who believed the world could be drawn and therefore known. *Blanks are expensive,* Vale had said. He was right. I had been living inside a blank for six years, and I had called it freedom.

Okonjo came up the ladder with a manifest and stopped when he saw my face. He was a man who read weather in people the way I read it in water.

"Trouble?" he asked.

"A survey man."

Okonjo grunted, the particular grunt that meant the word had landed somewhere near old bruises of his own. "They don't send survey men for timber and rice."

"No," I said. "They don't."

Below us, at the foot of the gangway, a figure had detached itself from the shadow of the customs house and was walking slowly along the wharf, looking at the Lena, looking at the mooring lines, looking at the wheelhouse windows. Not approaching. Measuring. And behind him, further off, a second figure, and the sight of them put the cold thing in my stomach back on its eel-slow patrol, because inspections end, but watchers don't.

I was still standing there, deciding whether to cast off that afternoon instead of morning, when the harbor boy came up the ladder with a message that had no sender's name on it—only a time, tomorrow's dawn tide, and a single line in a shaky hand that stopped my breath:

*You promised passage once to a dying man. He says you keep your promises. Prove it.*

I read it three times. I had promised no such thing—to anyone. Which meant either the river had finally sent me a message after all, or someone out there knew things about my father, and about me, that no one living should know.

The tide turned at dawn. I intended to be on it.
