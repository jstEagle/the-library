## 6. Stowaway Revealed

The river above Kessel Wharf narrowed the way a fist closes—slowly at first, then all at once. By the second morning upstream, the Vasseur had shed its delta manners entirely. The banks stood up sheer and rust-colored, ribbed with roots that hung into the current like the fingers of drowned men, and the water itself had gone from tea-brown to something darker, colder, tasting of stone when you cupped it in your palm. The Lena pushed against it anyway, her paddle wheel turning its patient half-circle, half-circle, throwing up spray that caught the early light and scattered it like struck flint.

Mara stood at the wheel with one hand on the brass and the other on nothing, which was how she preferred to stand. Okonjo was forward on the cargo deck, coiling line with the economy of a man who had done ten thousand things ten thousand times and saw no reason to do any of them differently. Devereux—the boy they'd taken on at Kessel for coal and because he asked so badly to come—was chipping rust off the rail with more enthusiasm than skill. And Elias Thorne lay below, in the small cabin aft of the engine room that Mara had given up without quite admitting to herself why she'd given it up.

Two days of fever now. Two days of the doctor's words from Kessel Wharf still ringing in her ears: *He's past what I can do. If he's going home, take him there quick.*

Mara had been down four times since dawn. Each time she'd found him the same: sunk deep in the mattress she'd aired herself, sweat-dark hair stuck to his forehead, hands moving restlessly against the blanket as though gripping tools that weren't there—a pick, a rope, a man's wrist. His lips moved constantly, shaping syllables she couldn't catch over the thump of the engine. Once, passing the door, she'd heard him say *not yet* in a voice so clear and reasonable that she'd stopped in the passage with her heart hammering, as if he'd spoken directly to her.

She went down again mid-morning, carrying broth she'd made herself on the galley stove, because the cook they no longer had would have made it better but nobody else would make it at all.

That was when she smelled him. Not Thorne—Thorne smelled of camphor and sickness and old sweat. This was different. Woodsmoke and river mud and underneath it something faintly sweet, like bruised fruit, like a woman's soap gone stale in a closed space. It came from the forepeak. From the chain locker where they kept spare anchor line and the tarpaulins nobody had inventoried since before the war.

Mara set the broth down on the engine-room step and stood very still, listening past the engine's rhythm the way she listened past the river's rhythm when she was reading sandbars by sound alone. There. A breath, too shallow and too quick to be Thorne's. The creak of a body shifting against planking.

She took the lantern hook off its bracket—not lit, just held, a weight in her hand—and crossed the deck. Her boots were quiet; thirty years of walking decks taught you where the loose boards lived. She hooked back the canvas flap over the chain locker hatch and looked down into the dark.

A girl looked back up at her.

Not a girl, exactly. Young—twenty, maybe twenty-two—but with the kind of face that had already finished deciding what it was. Sharp-boned, sunburned across the nose and cheekbones in a way that spoke of weeks outdoors rather than an afternoon, dark hair hacked off short with what had clearly been a knife and no mirror. She was wedged between a coil of anchor chain and the hull plating, knees drawn up, and she looked at Mara with an expression that was not fear, exactly, and not defiance either, but some alloy of the two that Mara recognized because she'd worn it herself once, at seventeen, on another boat, under another name.

"You," Mara said.

"I can explain."

"Can you." Mara lowered the lantern hook slowly, deliberately, letting the girl watch her weigh it. "You've been aboard since Kessel Wharf."

It wasn't a question, and the girl didn't treat it as one. She unfolded herself from the locker with the stiff care of someone who'd been folded too long, climbed the ladder rungs, and stood on the deck in front of Mara—tall, taller than Mara by two inches, wearing men's trousers cut off at the calf and a shirt that had belonged to somebody dead. Mud to the knee. She swayed once, caught herself.

"My name is Sable," she said. "Sable Thorne."

The name went through Mara like cold water through a crack in a hull. She kept her face still. She'd had practice keeping her face still for years, and this was what the practice was for.

"Elias Thorne has one daughter," she said. "He talks about her when the fever takes him. He doesn't talk about her kindly."

Something flickered across Sable Thorne's face—there and gone, fast as a fish turning—and Mara watched it go and understood that whatever it was, the girl had decided not to show her the whole of it. Good instincts, then. Bad circumstances.

"He has a right not to talk about me kindly," Sable said. "I'm here to take him home."

"He's already going home. That's arranged. That was arranged before you stowed away on my boat and ate my stores and—" Mara stopped, recalculated. "How long have you been in that locker?"

"Since Kessel Wharf. Before we cast off."

"Two days."

"Yes."

"In a chain locker. In this heat."

"It wasn't the worst place I've slept." Sable said it flatly, without bravado, and somehow that was worse than bravado. She looked past Mara toward the river, toward the gorge walls sliding by, and her voice changed—dropped its armor a half-inch. "Is he alive? Nobody would tell me anything. I heard the crew talking through the bulkhead. They said fever. They said the doctor gave him days."

"He's alive." Mara heard herself say it and hated how gently it came out. "Barely. He asks for water and he fights the blanket and he says names."

"What names?"

Mara looked at her. Sable Thorne looked back, and for a moment neither of them breathed, and the paddle wheel turned its half-circle behind them, and the gorge walls held their rust-colored silence.

"Come below," Mara said. "And if you make a sound that wakes the crew before I've decided what to do with you, I'll put you ashore at the next landing, wherever it is, whatever it is. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, Captain."

"Don't call me that."

* * *

The cabin was dim, shuttered against the heat, lit in slats. Thorne lay as he'd lain all morning, but the fever had turned in the last hour—Mara could see it in the color of him, the flush climbing from gray toward red, the way his breathing had picked up its pace like a man hurrying somewhere he didn't want to go.

Sable stood in the doorway and did not move.

Mara watched her look at her father. Watched the girl's throat work once, twice. Watched her hands open and close at her sides, empty, useless, the hands of someone who had traveled two days hidden in a hole full of chain to reach this exact doorway and now couldn't cross its threshold.

"He looks smaller," Sable said finally. Her voice had gone rough. "In the photograph he's—I have a photograph. He looks bigger than the world."

"People always do in photographs. It's the only place they hold still."

Sable crossed the cabin then, three steps, and knelt by the bunk, and took one of her father's restless hands in both of hers, and held it the way you hold a bird—firm enough to keep, gentle enough not to crush. Thorne's hand quieted under hers. Stilled. His fingers curled around hers with the slow certainty of a sleeping man finding a familiar rope in the dark.

For a long moment the only sounds were the engine, the water, and the two of them breathing.

Then Thorne opened his eyes.

They were unfocused, swimming somewhere above the cabin ceiling, and his voice came out cracked and distant, a voice speaking from the bottom of a well. But the name was clear. The name came out whole, the way fever-words sometimes did, stripped of everything except their weight:

"Sable."

The girl went rigid. Every muscle in her locked at once, and Mara saw her decide—saw the decision move through her like weather—whether to answer or stay silent, whether this was a trap her own longing had built or whether her father, dying, had finally said the one true thing left in him.

"Sable," he said again. "Sable-girl. I never—" His hand tightened around hers. "I never sent for you. I should have sent for you. Every year I should have—"

"I'm here," Sable said. Barely a whisper. "Papa. I'm here."

His eyes drifted down, found her face, and for three or four seconds—Mara counted them, because counting was the only thing holding her own chest together—they were both present, father and daughter, looking at each other across the wreckage of eleven years. Then the fever pulled him back under. His eyes slid away. His grip loosened but did not let go, and his mouth kept moving, softer now, words dissolving into the rhythm of the engine.

Sable bowed her head over his hand and wept without sound, which was its own kind of discipline, and Mara stood in the slatted light and felt fury and pity grind against each other inside her like millstones, and could not tell which one was winning.

* * *

She got Sable out of the cabin eventually—with effort, with the promise of food, with the flat statement that her father needed rest and not a vigil—and walked her up to the pilothouse, because the pilothouse was Mara's territory and Mara wanted to conduct what came next on ground she owned.

She fed her first. Hard bread, dried fish, the last of the oranges from Kessel. Sable ate like someone who had learned not to trust the next meal, quickly and completely, and thanked her in a voice that had been sanded back down to neutral.

"Now," Mara said. She leaned against the wheel housing, arms crossed. "Talk. All of it. Why you ran from your father, why you're running back to him, and how you knew he was on my boat—because that part interests me most, and don't insult me by pretending it doesn't."

Sable set down the orange peel. She looked out the pilothouse windows at the gorge for a while, gathering herself, and Mara let her gather, because a person assembling the truth deserved the time to do it properly.

"I didn't run from him," Sable said at last. "He ran from me. Or from himself. Same thing." She turned. "My mother died when I was nine. After that he couldn't look at me—she was in my face, everyone said so, even people who barely knew her. He took work that kept him away. Longer expeditions. Longer absences. Then one year he didn't come back at all. Just money, sometimes, through a shipping agent in Port Meridian. Sometimes not even money. Letters I wrote him came back unopened. Eleven years, Captain. I stopped writing four years ago, but I never stopped knowing where he might be. You learn the trade routes of a man the way you learn the habits of a fish."

"And this time?"

"A letter came. Not from him—from a man named Corvus, who said he'd been on an expedition with him. Said my father was sick and heading downriver, said he was trying to reach the coast, said—" Sable's jaw tightened. "Said if I wanted to see him alive I shouldn't wait for him to ask. Because he wouldn't ask. He'd die first."

"Corvus. Tall man, gray beard, gold tooth?"

Surprise flickered across Sable's face. "You know him?"

"I know of him. He crewed for my father once." Mara said it carefully, watching what it cost Sable to hear the words *my father*, and noting, too, the way the girl filed that fact away without asking—another good instinct, banked for later. "Go on."

"So I went to Kessel Wharf, because Corvus said that's where the letter would find him or find his boat. I got there and he was already gone—already aboard some riverboat, already upstream. The wharf-master told me which boat." Sable met her eyes squarely. "Yours. He told me the captain's name was Lockridge."

The name sat in the air between them. Mara let it sit. Downriver, behind them, Captain Adrien Vale of the colonial survey office also knew that name, had spoken it with a silk-gloved courtesy that had frightened her more than any threat, and every hour the Lena climbed deeper into country Vale had no charts for was an hour bought with fuel and luck.

"So you hid in my chain locker," Mara said, "instead of knocking on my door like a Christian."

"If I'd knocked, you'd have said no."

"Of course I'd have said no. I don't carry passengers. I especially don't carry passengers who are the daughters of dying men, because dying men die, and then I'm responsible for a grieving stranger in the middle of the gorge during monsoon season." Mara heard her own voice rise and brought it down. "Instead you made me unknowingly responsible anyway. Which is worse. Which is exactly the same, except now I'm angry about it."

"I know." Sable didn't flinch. "I'd do it again."

"I know you would. That's what makes you dangerous."

They looked at each other. The pilothouse was hot, the glass sweating, the light outside going hard and white as the morning burned off. Somewhere below them Thorne murmured in his fever, and the sound came up through the deck plates like something rising from under the floor of the world.

"Why does he call for me if he hates me?" Sable asked suddenly, and her armor was gone, all of it, and what was underneath was so young and so fierce that Mara had to look away for a second. "Eleven years. He never answered a letter. Why is my name the one in his mouth?"

"Because fever strips a man down to what he actually carries," Mara said. "Everything else is ballast. He jettisoned a lot of things, your father. Apparently he never managed to jettison you."

Sable's breath caught. She turned to the window fast, but not fast enough, and Mara pretended not to see her wipe her face with the heel of her hand, and gave her the time to do it.

When Sable turned back, her voice was steady again, and something new had come into it—something that sounded, uncomfortably, like Mara's own voice on her own best days.

"I'll work," Sable said. "I'll pull lines, I'll haul coal, I'll stand watches. I grew up on expedition camps; I'm not soft and I'm not slow, whatever you've decided about me. I won't be cargo. I won't be a secret you have to manage. Put me to work and I'll earn my board before we reach the headwaters."

"The crew will want to know who you are."

"Tell them whatever you want."

"They'll figure it out. Crews always do. They'll see you sit with him and do the arithmetic." Mara uncrossed her arms. "One condition. Mine."

"Name it."

"When he's lucid—if he gets lucid—you let him talk. Whatever he says, however bad it is, you listen. No scenes, no accusations, no making his last days about eleven years of postage owed. He's carrying something heavier than guilt, Sable. I've seen the shape of it on his face when the fever lets go. Something happened to him out here, something connected to my father, and I intend to hear it before the river takes him. If your grief gets in the way of that, you and I are going to have a problem."

Sable studied her for a long moment. "Your father," she said slowly. "The one Vale wants charts from. The one who disappeared."

"Who died," Mara said. "On an expedition your father was on. Which is why he's on my boat, and why I haven't put you ashore, and why I'm standing here having this conversation instead of turning us around." She straightened. "Welcome aboard the Lena. You start with the coal."

Below them, faint through the deck, Elias Thorne called his daughter's name again—once, softly, like a man testing whether a rope still held—and Sable Thorne closed her eyes as if the word had struck her physically, and Mara turned to the wheel and put both hands on the brass and steered them all, together, deeper into the gorge.

It was past noon when Okonjo came up the pilothouse ladder, unhurried as ever, and stood beside her without speaking for a full minute, which from Okonjo was a speech.

"Smoke," he said finally, nodding downstream, back the way they'd come. "Far off. White. Steady."

Mara raised the glasses. Found it: a thread of smoke hanging above the tree line astern, too disciplined for a campfire, too steady for driftwood burning.

A launch, running hot, coming upriver after them.

Okonjo watched her lower the glasses. "Vale?"

"Who else follows a woman into the wilderness twice?" Mara passed him the binoculars and turned back to the wheel, and the smoke hung in the glasses' ghost behind her eyes all afternoon, patient as the monsoon clouds stacking themselves beyond the western rim of the gorge.
