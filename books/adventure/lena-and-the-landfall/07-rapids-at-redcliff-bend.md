## 7. Rapids at Redcliff Bend

I heard the rapids before I saw them—a sound like the whole world tearing cloth, rising up through the soles of my boots and into my teeth before it ever reached my ears. Two days aboard the *Lena* and I had learned her language in fragments: the knock of cargo shifting in the hold, Okonjo's low humming that meant all was well, the particular creak of the wheel when Mara Lockridge leaned into a turn. But this sound was new. This sound was a warning written in water.

I climbed out of the shadow of the deckhouse where I'd been mending a frayed line—badly, because no one had taught me and I was too proud to ask—and went forward along the rail.

The river narrowed ahead. That was the first thing. The Vasseur, which had sprawled lazy and brown as a sleeping animal for two days, suddenly gathered itself between walls of rust-red stone, and the water went from the color of tea to the color of hammered tin, and then to white. Redcliff Bend, Okonjo had called it last night, pointing at a smudge on one of the few charts Mara allowed on board. He'd said it the way a man names a grave.

Mara stood at the wheel already. She must have felt it coming through the hull before any of us heard it, the way she seemed to feel everything about this boat, this river, as though they were extensions of her own nervous system. Her hands rested light on the spokes—not gripping, just touching, the way you'd touch a horse's neck—and her eyes were fixed downstream with an expression I could only call listening.

"You," she said, without turning her head. "Sable."

My name in her mouth still startled me. She said it flat, unadorned, but she said it, which was more than she'd granted me for the first day after I'd been dragged out of the cargo hold, dripping and defiant, while my father slept his fever sleep below.

"Captain."

"Can you tie a bowline?"

"Yes."

"Liar." She glanced at me then, one quick sidelong look, and there was something at the corner of her mouth that might have been amusement or might have been contempt. "Doesn't matter. Bowline won't save anyone today. Go to Okonjo. Do what he tells you, exactly, the moment he tells you. Nothing else. Nothing clever."

"And if he's dead?" The words came out of me before I could weigh them—my father's tongue in my mouth, always too quick, too sharp for my own good.

Mara looked at me fully then, and the river roared beneath us, and she said, "Then you'll have worse problems than knots," and turned back to the water.

* * *

Okonjo met me amidships with two coils of rope over his shoulder and an expression of profound, patient sorrow, as though I were a grief he'd been assigned personally by God.

"Redcliff Bend," he said. "You know what happens here?"

"The river narrows."

"The river narrows." He nodded slowly, handing me a coil. "And what does water do when you make it narrow?"

"It goes faster."

"It goes faster, and it gets angry, and it remembers every rock it's ever hated." He looped a line through a cleat with a motion so economical it barely looked like motion at all. "The Lena has run this bend forty-one times. Captain Lockridge has run it thirty-eight. Three times we portaged, when the water was high or the captain was tired. Today the water is high." He looked at me over the coil of rope between us. "So today we work. You hold this line. When I say give, you give. When I say take, you take. When I say nothing, you do nothing, even if your hands are screaming. Yes?"

"Yes."

"Good girl." He said it without condescension, the way you'd bless someone before a battle, and turned to check the forward lines.

The cliffs rose on either side now, close enough that their shadows lay across the deck like cold hands. The rock was the color of dried blood, banded with strata of ochre and ash, and the river ran between them fast and green-black, its surface stitched with white seams that grew longer and longer as we went. Spray came off the water in fine veils. My father would have loved this, I thought, and then hated myself for thinking it, because my father was dying in the cabin below and had traded away whatever love he had left for a map and a promise.

For a promise from her. Not from me.

I put that thought down like something hot and took my position at the line.

* * *

The first rapid hit us like a fist through the floor.

Not gradually—the *Lena* went from steady to chaos in the space of a breath, the bow lifting, the whole hull shuddering as the current seized us and threw us sideways toward the cliff wall. I heard Mara's voice come clear over the roar—"Hard to starboard"—and felt the boat answer her, felt the wheel speak through the timbers, and then we were through the first chute and slamming down into the trough beyond with water coming over the rails in cold sheets.

"GIVE!" Okonjo bellowed, and I gave, paying out line until it burned through my palms, and then "TAKE!" and I hauled until my shoulders screamed, and the line in my hands wasn't rope anymore, it was a living thing, thrashing, trying to kill me or save me, I couldn't tell which, and there was no time to tell which, only the next command and the next.

We came around the bend itself at speed I didn't know a boat could survive. The channel dog-legged hard to port around a spur of rock that stood in midstream like a broken tooth, and beyond it the water dropped—I saw it drop, saw the river simply fall away into a churning pit of foam—and every instinct in my body said *we die here.*

But Mara didn't slow down.

That was the thing I understood later, lying awake in the dark of the deckhouse, replaying it: she didn't slow. She *sped up*, feeding the wheel spoke by spoke, putting the *Lena*'s nose straight at the falling water as if the only way through terror was to outrun it, and the boat rose, and hung—for one impossible suspended moment we were flying, spray streaming off the bow, the red cliffs blurring past on either side—and then we landed, and the world became noise.

Water over the decks. Cargo groaning. Someone shouting a name that wasn't mine. My hands locked on the line, my arms burning, my feet skidding on flooded planking, and through all of it Okonjo's voice: "TAKE! Take, take, TAKE—" and I took, throwing my whole weight backward, boots braced against a cleat, hauling the stern line taut as the *Lena* swung her tail through the boil below the drop and came out—

—came out—

—into quiet.

It happened the way a fever breaks. One moment the roar had us entire; the next, the river widened, slowed, went silver-green and deep, and the *Lena* settled onto it like a bird landing on still air. The cliffs fell back. The sun came down warm through the spray-mist, and somewhere behind us the rapids went on raging at the next poor vessel that dared them, but not us. Not today.

I realized I was on my knees. I realized my hands wouldn't open. Okonjo knelt beside me and peeled my fingers off the line one at a time, gentle as a man handling a bird, and looked at my palms—rope-burned, bleeding in thin stripes—and then looked at my face.

"Huh," he said.

From him, I would learn, that was a speech.

* * *

Mara came aft along the rail, wringing water from her hair. She was soaked to the skin, her shirt plastered to her, mud streaked across one cheekbone where she'd been thrown against the wheel housing, and she moved with the loose-limbed ease of someone whose body had done exactly what it was built to do and knew it.

She stopped in front of me. Looked at my hands. Looked at my face.

"Your bowline's still a lie," she said.

"I'll learn."

"You'll learn." She crouched, took my wrist without asking, turned my palm up to the light. Her fingers were cool and rough and shockingly careful. "This needs dressing. Salt water in rope burn is how hands rot."

"It's fine."

"It's not fine." She looked up then, and her eyes were very near mine, gray-green, flecked with gold like the sediment of some other river, and for a moment neither of us moved. The mist drifted between us. Behind her, Okonjo had found somewhere else entirely to be, with a tactfulness I would think about later and smile about later than that.

"Why did you speed up?" I asked. It came out quieter than I meant it to. Everything about her made me quieter than I meant to be.

Her thumb moved once across my palm, checking the damage or not checking it, I couldn't say. "Because slowing down gives the river time to decide for you," she said. "Speed is the only argument it respects." She released my wrist and stood. "Get below. Kit's in the locker under the chart shelf. If you can't find it, ask Okonjo."

"If he's dead?"

That flicker again at the corner of her mouth—that almost-smile, gone almost as soon as it arrived, like a fish rising and turning under dark water. "Then you'll have worse problems than bandages," she said, and went forward to look at her river.

I stood there a moment with my burned hands open at my sides, watching her go, feeling the ghost-pressure of her fingers on my wrist like a brand that hadn't finished cooling.

* * *

Below, my father was awake.

He was propped against the bulkhead in the narrow cabin, gray as ash, but his eyes had that terrible lucidity the fever sometimes granted him, the way a storm will briefly show you the stars before closing over them again. The wax-sealed tube lay across his knees. His hands rested on it the way a miser's rest on gold.

"There was water," he said, when I came in. "I heard water. Big water."

"Redcliff Bend. We're through it."

"We." His eyes found my bandages—he'd watched me wrap them badly, and he noticed everything, he'd always noticed everything except the things that mattered, like daughters. "Whose we?"

"Mine. The crew's. Captain Lockridge's."

Something moved through his face then, too quick and too complicated to name—shame, relief, fear, all braided together. "Elias Thorne," he said softly, to himself, to the tube, to no one, "you old coward. You sent your daughter to do your dying for you."

"You're not dying yet."

"Aren't I." He smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen in years, that smile, because it was peaceful. "Sable. Come here. Sit. Let me look at you while I still know your face."

I sat. I let him look. Outside, the river ran wide and calm, carrying us upstream toward whatever waited at the head of it, and above us, on the deck, I could hear Mara's footsteps crossing and recrossing the planks, unhurried, certain, the sound of a woman who had never once asked the river's permission for anything.

"She runs this boat well," my father said, following my eyes upward, because he noticed everything. "Better than her father ever ran anything. Did she tell you about him? No." He answered himself. "Of course she didn't. There are things on this river older than charts, girl, and darker than rapids, and I am going to have to tell you about all of them before the monsoon comes."

"What things?" I asked. "Father. What things?"

But the fever was already closing back over him like water over a dropped stone, and all he said, sinking, was a name—not mine, not hers—a man's name, spoken with such raw regret that I sat very still in the dim cabin and did not breathe until it faded.

Above me, Mara's footsteps crossed the deck again, steady as a pulse.

I decided, sitting there in the dark with my father's secret pressing against the underside of my ribs, that tomorrow I would ask her about her father.

And I decided that tonight, when the lamp was lit and the river murmured against the hull, I would sit near her while she kept her watch, and I would not explain why.
