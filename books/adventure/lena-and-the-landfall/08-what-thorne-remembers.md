## 8. What Thorne Remembers

The fever had a geography of its own. Elias Thorne learned to navigate it the way he'd once navigated the Vasseur's upper reaches—by feel, by the color of the water, by the sound of things moving beneath a surface that looked calm and was not.

Some hours he was twenty-six again, boots in red mud, machete opening a corridor through green so dense it seemed less like forest than like a held breath. Some hours he was on the ice shelf of a mountain that had no business existing at that latitude, watching his own hands turn the color of candle wax. And some hours—the worst ones—he was standing at the edge of a clearing where nothing moved, not birds, not insects, not even the light, while a man with river-gray eyes asked him a question Thorne could not answer then and had been answering wrong ever since.

*Are you coming, Elias?*

He woke from that one gasping, salt sweat stinging his eyes, and for a moment did not know what vessel held him. Then the smell came: bilgewater and tar, engine oil and dried fish, the particular sweet rot of rope that has been wet too many seasons. The *Lena*. He was aboard the *Lena*, laid out in the shadow of her cargo hold among crates of kerosene tins and bolts of dyed cotton, and above him the deck planks crossed and recrossed like the ribs of some enormous patient animal.

A woman sat nearby with a rifle across her knees, cleaning it with the unhurried economy of someone who expected to need it soon.

Not Mara. Younger. Darker-haired, with a jaw that might have been cut from the same stone. She glanced over when he stirred, said nothing, went back to the rifle.

Sable. The name arrived in him like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it strike water a long time later.

*She boarded at Kessel,* someone had told him—or he had dreamed it. Fever made liars of everyone, including memory. But the way she cleaned that rifle—with her left hand bracing the stock, thumb riding the safety, exactly the way he had taught no one—was not a dream. That was real. His daughter was three meters away and would not look at him, and he deserved that, and it was somehow worse than all the fever countries combined.

* * *

The lucid hours came at dawn, usually. Something about the gray light, the river running quiet before the day's heat built, the crew moving soft around the sleeping hold. In those hours the fog thinned and he could see the shape of his own life clearly, which was a cruelty dressed as mercy.

On the fourth morning after Redcliff Bend—he was counting them now, hoarding days the way a drowning man hoards air—he woke clear-headed enough to sit up without the world tilting. Mara was at the rail a few feet away, a tin cup of coffee going cold in her hands, watching the water with an attention that was almost devotional. The river gave back the early light in long copper ribbons.

"You knew my father," she said, without turning.

It was not a question. It had not been a question since the night she'd unrolled the map by lamplight and gone white as bone meal. He'd watched her recognize the handwriting across the width of a chart room, watched something close behind her eyes open and then deliberately shut. She had not spoken of it since. Four days of silence, and now this, delivered to the river rather than to him.

"Yes," Thorne said. His voice came out sanded down, barely his own. "I did."

"How."

It was not *when* or *why* or *what happened to him*. Just *how*. As if the mechanism mattered more than the mystery. He understood that about her already—she wanted the working parts exposed, wanted to see how the thing was built, because things that were built could be understood, and things understood could be survived.

"He hired me," Thorne said. "Eleven years ago. I was running survey chains for the colonial office—young, stupid, flattered that a man like Josiah Lockridge would want me. Everyone knew his reputation. The man who mapped the middle Vasseur when the office said it couldn't be done. The man who refused to sell." He paused. "They told stories about him at the wharfside. Half of them were true. I found that out later. More than half."

Mara's shoulders did not move. The cup did not rise to her lips. She was listening the way pilots listen to water—for what ran underneath.

"There were nine of us," Thorne went on. "Your father, two guides out of the hill villages whose names the records never bothered to keep, a botanist named Ferro who talked more than he walked, and five hired men including me. We went up past the last surveyed bend, into the gorge country. Looking for the headwaters of the western fork. Your father believed there was a tributary there that fed the Vasseur from the high plateau—a river nobody official had ever seen, because nobody official had ever asked the right people."

"The drowned village," Mara said quietly.

Thorne's breath caught. "You've seen it?"

"I've seen what your map says about it." Now she turned, and her eyes in the gray morning light were the exact gray of the river, and he understood suddenly why men followed this woman into floodwater. "My father drew it twice. Once in ink. Once in pencil, smaller, like he wasn't sure he should have drawn it at all. There's a word next to it in his shorthand I've never been able to read."

"*Erased*," Thorne said. "The word is *erased*."

The silence between them filled with the sound of the river, patient and enormous.

"Tell me," Mara said. "All of it. Don't arrange it to spare me. I've been arranging things to spare myself for eleven years and it hasn't worked."

So he told her.

* * *

"We found the village in the third week," he said. "Or what was left of it. A settlement on a bluff above the confluence—stone foundations, orchard trees gone feral, a well with a carved lip. Old. Older than the colony, older than anything the maps acknowledged. People had lived there once, a long time, and then they hadn't, and the jungle had folded over the place like a hand closing."

"Ferro thought it was plague," Thorne said. "The guides wouldn't land. They stood in the shallows and spoke to us very quietly, the way you speak near a grave, and they said the village had been *put under the water*, and we should ask no more questions, and we didn't—not then. Your father charted it. That was his whole nature; he couldn't pass a thing without naming it, fixing it, giving it coordinates so it couldn't be lost. I think he understood even then that some things are lost on purpose. I think he charted it anyway because a truth that isn't written down can be denied forever."

"And the office—" Mara began.

"The office never saw those pages." Thorne's hands had begun to shake, and he let them. "That came later. First there was the mountain."

He closed his eyes and the fever-geography opened under him, but this time he walked through it awake, choosing each step, because she had asked for all of it and he owed her a debt that interest alone would never settle.

"The western fork narrowed into a gorge you could span with a thrown rope. Walls of black stone, water the color of tea, and at the end of it—the source pool. Cold enough to hurt. Fed from snowmelt off a massif that doesn't appear on any colonial chart because the surveys stopped a hundred kilometers short. Your father stood at that pool and laughed. Actually laughed, like a boy. Eleven years I've tried to remember anything else about him and that laugh is what comes. A man laughing at the top of the world because the world was bigger than the men who claimed to own it."

"But it turned," Mara said. Not cruelly. Simply.

"It turned." Thorne looked at his hands. They had been strong hands once, chain-hands, machete-hands. Now they trembled like an old man's, and he supposed he was an old man now, or near enough. "On the descent. The rains came early—we'd misjudged the season by two weeks, and the gorge became a trap. Water rose four meters in a night. We lost the boats. We lost half our food to the flood. And one of the hired men—Rask, his name was Rask—went into the water with a line around his waist trying to save the supply raft, and the line snapped, and we never found him."

He heard Mara set down the cup. Very carefully, the way you put down something you might otherwise throw.

"After that, everything got simple and terrible. Hunger does that. Fear does that. We were nine days walking out through country that wanted us dead, eating things I won't describe, and somewhere in those nine days the expedition stopped being an expedition and became a bargain. Your father had the charts. The office wanted the charts—wanted them badly enough, we learned later, that they'd offered him a commission, a house, a name restored. His family name had been struck from the registry years before, over some dispute I never fully understood. All he had to do was hand over the western fork and the village and the source, and he'd be a legitimate man again."

"What did he do?" Mara asked, though her voice said she already knew, had always known, had built her whole careful invisible life on the foundation of knowing it.

"He burned the field notes." Thorne smiled, and it was not a pleasant smile; it was the smile of a man handling a hot coal. "Right there on the trail. Tore them out of the waterproof case and burned them in a fire we needed for warmth, page by page, while Ferro screamed at him and the guides watched with their faces like carved wood. He kept only one copy—the master, the one you're carrying now—and he sealed it in wax and told us: *If I die, this goes to my daughter, and if the office takes it from her, then they win, and I will know about it from wherever dead men go.*"

"And you," Mara said.

And there it was. The question he had carried up rivers and down rivers, through gold camps and gambling dens and a marriage that failed and a daughter he failed worse, the question that had finally killed him by inches until dying became the only honest work left.

"I left," Thorne said. "At the last village before the delta. The guides took us in, fed us, and when the office men came asking—because they came, they were already looking for your father—I told them where he'd landed. Not the charts. I didn't know where he'd hidden the charts. But I told them where he was, and I took the passage money he offered me for the walk out, and I got on a boat, and I did not look back."

The words hung in the morning air. Downriver a kingfisher broke the surface and rose with a flash of blue, and the river went on doing what rivers do, which is carry everything away except the things you deserve to keep.

"They caught up with him at Port Anselm," Thorne said. "But he'd already sent the map ahead. To a woman he trusted. And the office men questioned him for three days, and he gave them nothing, and on the fourth day he was dead—they called it fever, and maybe it was, and maybe it wasn't. I read about it in a newspaper in a town two hundred miles inland, sitting in a chair with money in my pocket that had his fingerprints on it, and I told myself I'd had no choice. I told myself that for eleven years." He lifted his eyes to hers. "I'm done telling myself that. I served on your father's last expedition, Mara Lockridge, and when it turned desperate, I chose myself. That's the whole of it. That's the truth, offered plain, and you can put me off this boat at the next landing and I'll call it justice."

* * *

For a long time Mara said nothing. She picked up the cold coffee, considered it, poured it over the side into the river—an offering, or a libation, or simply a woman who no longer wanted the taste of anything lukewarm.

"Sable doesn't know," she said at last.

"No."

"She came aboard to reach you before you died. Whatever else is between you—that's what she wants. Days. Hours, maybe." Mara turned to face him fully, and he saw that she was not unmoved; he saw the grief in her moving underneath like current under ice. "So here is what happens. You'll tell her yourself. Today, while you still have the mind for it. All of it, the same as you told me. She's spent her life piecing you together from other people's silences, and she deserves the whole cloth from your own mouth, however ugly it is. Can you do that?"

Thorne looked toward the shadowed corner of the hold where his daughter sat with the rifle across her knees, pretending not to have heard every word, failing utterly, her eyes bright and terrible.

"Yes," he said.

"Then do it now." Mara rose, and paused beside him, and for one moment her hand rested on his shoulder—light as a bird landing, gone almost before it arrived. "My father died protecting strangers," she said. "Strangers who included you. I spent eleven years hating the office for taking him. I find I don't have anything left over for hating you. Maybe that's a flaw in me. Maybe it's the inheritance." Her mouth bent, not quite a smile. "Either way—you're still under my keel, old man. What's left of this run belongs to getting you home."

She walked forward along the deck, calling the engine room to answer, and the *Lena* leaned into the current like a dog leaning into a collar it has chosen.

Thorne sat in the growing heat and gathered himself the way a man gathers himself before a dive into dark water. Then he pushed himself upright on shaking arms and turned toward his daughter, and opened his mouth to begin—

—and that was when the lookout called from the bow, voice cracking across the morning: "*Smoke astern! White hull, steam launch, gaining fast!*"

Thorne knew that hull. He had seen it tied at Kessel Wharf, gleaming and official, and he had seen the man who commanded it smiling his patient smile.

The survey office had found them.
