## 9. Vale's Offer

The river gave us three days of grace after Kessel Wharf, and I spent them like a miser counting coins.

The Lena ran heavy and slow with Thorne aboard—his fever had broken into something quieter but no less hungry, a wasting that ate him from the inside while he slept in the aft cabin under Sable's watch. I'd moved my own gear forward to make room for the sickbed, and now I slept in the wheelhouse on a pallet that smelled of engine oil and old canvas, waking every hour to check the sounder, the sky, the water's color against the bank.

The Vasseur was behaving itself. That should have comforted me. It didn't. A polite river is a river thinking about something else.

Okonjo had the noon watch when we saw the launch. He didn't shout. He did something worse—he went quiet, and his silence came up through the deck plates into my boots before I ever heard the engine myself.

I climbed to the wheelhouse roof and looked back downriver.

It came around Redcliff Bend's far shoulder riding high and white-waked, a survey launch with the colonial pennant snapping off its stern staff and a bow gun mount that was supposed to be for survey equipment and never once had been. Two men stood in the cockpit. One held binoculars. The other held nothing, which meant he didn't need to hold anything, because Captain Adrien Vale had a way of standing that made other men into his hands.

"How long?" Okonjo asked, coming up beside me.

"Twenty minutes if he pushes her. Fifteen if he doesn't care what the wash does to our lines."

"He'll push her."

"He'll push her."

I rang down for half ahead and began doing arithmetic I didn't like. We were twenty miles above the fork where the Vasseur split, and past the fork lay the tributary—the one no chart named, the one drawn in my father's hand on a tube of paper sleeping in the false bottom beneath my berth. If Vale caught us below the fork, there would be no turning into unmapped water without an audience. If he caught us above it, there would be no turning at all.

Sable came up the ladder without being called. She'd developed a habit of appearing whenever trouble did, as though she could smell it through the deck. Her hair was tied back with a strip of red cloth and there was fever-sweat still damp at her temples from leaning over her father.

"That's him," she said. Not a question. She'd heard me talk about Vale at Kessel Wharf the way you talk about weather coming.

"That's him."

"What do you want me to do?"

The question sat strangely in my chest. Nobody on this river asked me what I wanted them to do; they waited for orders and took them. But Sable asked, and something in the asking made the answer easier to find.

"Go below. Sit with your father. If he wakes, don't tell him who's coming."

"And if he asks anyway?"

"Then lie to him gently. It's a skill. You'll need it out here."

She almost smiled. Then she squeezed my arm once—brief, warm, gone—and went down the ladder, and I stood there a moment longer than the situation allowed, watching the launch grow.

* * *

Vale came alongside at slack water above the Narrows, where the current eased and a captain could hold station without fighting. He handled his launch well—I'd give him that. He brought her in slow, fenders out, and hailed us with a courtesy that cost him nothing and meant less.

"Captain Lockridge." His voice carried across the gap between hulls, pleasant, unhurried. "Might I come aboard?"

"You might not."

A pause. Somewhere behind him, his crewman shifted, and the pennant cracked in the wind off the water.

"I have a writ of inspection signed at the district office," Vale said. "You can refuse me at the rail and I'll read it to you from here, word by word, until your crew tires of hearing it. Or you can invite me up and we can conduct this like two professionals. Your choice, Captain. I'm told you value choice."

He'd learned things about me at Kessel Wharf. That was the part that put frost along my spine—not the gun mount, not the writ, but the small evidence that he'd been listening.

I looked at Okonjo. Okonjo looked at the sky, which is deckhand for *your funeral, Captain*. I looked at the hatch where Sable had disappeared. Then I threw the line.

* * *

He came up the ladder like a man visiting a museum exhibit of a boat he intended to buy. Grey uniform pressed despite four days on the river, boots polished, a leather document case under one arm. He was younger than his reputation—thirty perhaps, with the kind of face that would stay smooth its whole life because it never did any work—and his eyes were the exception: pale, patient, the eyes of a man who reads slowly so as to miss nothing.

"May I?" He gestured at the wheelhouse.

"You may stand on my deck. My wheelhouse is for my crew."

"A reasonable boundary." He set the document case on the rail, opened it, and did not take anything out. "Do you know why I'm here, Captain?"

"Because the survey office has nothing better to spend fuel on than harassing a cargo boat."

"Because you're carrying something that belongs to the Crown." He said it mildly, the way a man says *it looks like rain*. "Charts. Surveys. Hydrographic material relating to the upper Vasseur and its tributaries. Material compiled, I might add, by a surveyor who was himself operating under Crown commission when he compiled it."

"My father worked for no one."

"Your father—" and here Vale smiled, thin and correct, "—worked for everyone. That's what a survey is, Captain. It's knowledge taken from a place and held in trust for the people who govern it. Josiah Lockridge understood that. He signed the articles. He drew charts under commission number 114-C. And then he went upriver one last time and none of it came back. No reports. No charts. No Lockridge." He tilted his head. "No body, either, I'm told. The river keeps what it likes."

The name spoken aloud on my own deck did something to the air. Okonjo had stopped coiling rope. Down the length of the boat, everything had gone very still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"Say what you came to say, Captain Vale."

"Gladly." He finally lifted a paper from the case—a single sheet, folded once, heavy stock, official watermark catching the light. "Here is what I know. Three days ago a dying prospector named Elias Thorne collapsed at your landing clutching a sealed map tube. Here is what I believe. That tube contains the final chart of Josiah Lockridge's last expedition—including, I suspect, the location of certain settlements the office has reason to believe existed in the upper gorge." His voice stayed level, but underneath it something had begun to run fast, like current under ice. "And here is what I am authorized to offer."

He held the paper out.

I didn't take it. So he read it aloud instead, and I will say this for Adrien Vale: he read beautifully. Every clause landed like a coin on a counter.

"*In consideration of the surrender of all hydrographic materials pertaining to the upper Vasseur basin, the Colonial Survey Office hereby offers:*" — his eyes flicked up to check I was listening; I was; he knew I was — "*one: full restoration of the name and standing of Josiah Ambrose Lockridge, late surveyor, including formal recognition of service, posthumous commendation, and expungement of all records relating to the matter of the lost expedition.*"

The river made its small sounds against the hull. A kingfisher went downstream low over the water.

"*Two: a captain's commission in the colonial river service, vessel and route of the holder's choosing, salary commensurate with seniority.*" He paused. "That means the Lena, Captain. Registered. Legitimate. Papers in your own name. No more running dark past every wharf between here and the delta. No more paying harbor men to look away. A berth. A pension. A flag that protects you instead of hunting you."

"*Three: immunity, retroactive, for all parties currently aboard this vessel.*"

He folded the paper along its crease, precisely, and held it out again.

"All of that," he said, "for one tube of paper. Paper, Captain. Old paper. Ink on linen. What is it worth against a life?"

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in the next thirty seconds, because honesty is the only thing I've kept clean all these years, and I nearly spent it right there on my own deck.

He said my father's name and the river gave it back to me. Twelve years I had carried that name like contraband—never speaking it at landings, registering the Lena under initials, letting dockmasters call me *Cap* or *the Lockridge woman* or nothing at all. Twelve years of invisibility bought with silence, all of it to keep the survey office from finishing what it started: erasing him. Because that was what they'd done, whatever the polite word for it was. A man dies on their expedition under their commission, and instead of a report there is a hole in the record, and instead of a hole there is a family that learns to flinch at its own surname.

And here was a man in a grey coat offering to un-erase him. To carve the name back into the stone. To give me papers, protection, a flag. All I had to do was hand over the one thing Elias Thorne trusted to me with his dying breath—the one thing my father died rather than let them file away in some drawer in the district office.

Temptation dressed as justice. That's what it was. It fit so well I almost couldn't see the seams.

"Who drowned the village?" I asked.

Vale's smile didn't move, but something behind it did. "I beg your pardon?"

"The settlements in the upper gorge. The ones the office has *reason to believe existed*. Somebody erased them from the record before my father ever drew his chart. Somebody decided a few hundred people weren't worth putting on a map. Was that a decision or an accident, Captain?"

"You're straying into matters above your—"

"Rank? Registry? Existence?" I stepped closer, close enough to see the sweat at his hairline that the polish of him couldn't quite hide. Four days pushing a launch upriver in this heat had gotten to him too. "You want to offer me legitimacy, Captain Vale. Let me tell you what I've learned about legitimate men on this river. They sign things. They always sign things. And the ink dries, and somewhere upstream a village isn't on the map anymore, and everybody agrees it was never there."

"The office doesn't erase villages."

"No. It just declines to remember them."

For a moment neither of us spoke, and the Vasseur ran brown and patient between its banks, carrying silt from places neither of us would ever chart.

"You misunderstand your position," Vale said at last, and the pleasantness had thinned enough now to show the wire underneath. "This is not a negotiation. It is an offer, and offers expire. I will follow you to the fork, Captain. Past the fork, if I must. The tributary you're contemplating is a hundred and forty miles of dead water and worse, and monsoon season begins in—" he consulted a pocket watch, a small theatrical cruelty, "—eleven days. A steamboat cannot outrun the rain. But a launch can follow it anywhere, and I have fuel enough for anywhere."

"Then you'll get wet."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "I expect we both will."

He tucked the offer back into its case, closed the clasps, and paused with his hand resting on the leather.

"For what it's worth, Captain—and I mean this sincerely—your father was the finest surveyor this office ever employed. That's why the loss of his final work has been felt at the highest levels. There are men in the capital who still speak his name with respect." He straightened his cap. "Give them a reason to keep speaking it. Give yourself a reason to say it out loud. You've spent twelve years pretending you don't have one. I watched you at Kessel Wharf. I know what that costs."

He turned to go, then stopped, one hand on the rail, and looked back with those pale reading-slow eyes.

"One more thing. The prospector. Thorne. When he dies—and he will die, Captain, I've seen that look before, I grew up in mining country—you'll be carrying stolen Crown property with no living claimant. At that point the offer changes shape. Do you understand me? Offers are made to captains. Something else is done to thieves."

"I understand you perfectly."

"Good." He descended the ladder, unhurried, and dropped into his launch, and the crewman cast off, and the wake came against our hull in long slow slaps as the survey pennant shrank back downriver toward Redcliff Bend.

Except he didn't turn downriver.

He turned up.

* * *

We stood at the rail and watched him go, Okonjo and I, until the launch was a smudge of smoke against the heat shimmer above the water.

"He'll anchor at the fork," Okonjo said. "Wait for us there. Why chase when he can sit?"

"Because sitting lets us choose the hour. He wants us to see him waiting. He wants the choice to feel like walking into a trap."

"It is a trap."

"It's a trap either way. The question is whose." I rubbed my eyes. The sun was dropping toward the western ridge and the light had gone the color of old brass. "Eleven days, he says. He knows the monsoon tables better than I do. Which means he also knows what happens to a steel launch in the gorge when the rain comes down."

"Same thing that happens to a wooden steamboat."

"Not the same thing." I thought of the Lena's shallow draft, her flat bottom, the way she could slide over water that would tear a keel out. "She's built for floodwater, Okonjo. He isn't. That's the one card we hold that he can't take."

Below us, faint through the deck, I heard Sable's voice in the aft cabin, low and steady, reading something aloud to her father—I couldn't make out the words, only the cadence, the way you'd read to someone you loved who was leaving. Thorne's cough answered her, twice, then quieted.

Okonjo followed my gaze down. "The crew's asking questions," he said quietly. "They saw the uniform. They heard the name Lockridge spoken on deck. Men are wondering what we're really carrying, and what it costs to carry it."

"I know."

"They'll want words from you. Soon. Before the fork."

"I know, Okonjo."

He nodded and went forward to relieve the watch, and I stood alone at the rail as the first stars came out over the ridge, holding the wheelhouse frame with both hands, feeling the Lena breathe under me—engine vibration, current, the old patient heartbeat of her.

Restored name. Commission. Papers. My father's name carved back into the world's stone, and all it would cost was the truth of what he found, filed away in a drawer next to the erasure of a village.

Or eleven days of dead water, a dying man, a stowaway I hadn't yet found the courage to name what she was to me, and a gorge that would fill with rain.

Behind me, the cabin lamp glowed gold through the aft windows. Ahead, the river ran black toward the fork, and somewhere in that darkness, a survey launch was already anchoring to wait.

I went below to open the false bottom under my berth, and take out the map, and look at my father's handwriting one more time before I decided what it was worth.
