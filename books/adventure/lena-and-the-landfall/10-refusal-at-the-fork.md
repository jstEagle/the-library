## 10. Refusal at the Fork

The river split ahead of us like a sentence with two endings.

I saw it before Okonjo called it, the way I always did—before the sound reached the wheelhouse, before the water itself seemed to know. The Vasseur came down out of the high country in a long brown shoulder of current, and where it met the tributary it divided without ceremony: the main channel swinging wide and easy to the west, all its old confidence intact, and the other mouth—a narrow throat of green water, half-hidden by fig trees that leaned out over it like conspirators—slipping away north into country no chart in Kessel Wharf had ever bothered to name.

*Unsurveyed. Unregistered. Unclaimed.* My father's words, not mine, though I'd read them so many times the ink had worn soft as breath on the page he never surrendered.

I held the Lena steady and let the fork come to me.

Behind us, maybe four miles back, Vale's launch was coming. I could feel it the way you feel a storm cell building past the ridge—not seen yet, but present, a pressure against the ear. He'd found a pilot willing to run the night stretches, and he'd found coal enough, and he'd found the particular patience of a man who believes the world owes him an answer. Four miles was nothing. Two hours, perhaps less if the current favored him, and the current always favors men like Adrien Vale. It has never once favored me.

"Captain." Okonjo stood at my shoulder, arms folded, watching the fork the way he watched everything—with the stillness of a man who had learned young that motion attracts trouble. "Main channel's got the depth this time of year."

"I know."

"Delta run's three days. We'd make Montrousse inside the week, cargo intact, everyone paid."

"I know that too."

He didn't say anything else. That was the thing about Okonjo—he understood that some silences are questions and some are answers, and he'd learned to tell them apart on my boat better than anyone alive. This one was a question. He was waiting for me to answer it with more than silence.

Thorne lay below, in the cabin I'd given up for him, breathing in that shallow, rationed way the dying have, as though each breath were coin he was spending from a purse nearly empty. Sable hadn't left his side in two days except to stand her watches on the lines, and when she came up she had the look of someone carrying water in her hands across a long distance—careful, exhausted, unwilling to spill a drop of what she knew.

And in the wheelhouse drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in wax that Thorne himself had pressed his thumb into three weeks ago on my dock, lay the map. Her father's hand and mine. The same handwriting. I'd known it the moment I broke the seal's edge—known it the way you know a voice through a wall—and I had spent every hour since pretending I didn't know what that meant.

It meant my father had been here. Or meant to be. It meant the last expedition—the one the survey office pretended had never been mounted, the one they'd erased from their own records with the thoroughness of men burying a body—had aimed for this green throat of water. And it meant Elias Thorne had walked off it alive while my father had not walked off it at all.

Vale wanted the map. Vale wanted it with the whole weight of the colonial survey behind him, and he'd offered me what he thought I couldn't refuse: my father's name restored to the registry, a commission signed in proper ink, the Lena made legitimate at last after six years of running gray water with no papers worth the paper they weren't printed on. Legitimacy. A berth in the world. All I had to do was hand over the one thing my father had died keeping, and let the office fold it into their archives where it would be catalogued, classified, and forgotten by everyone except the men who needed it forgotten.

Comfortable living. That was the shape of it. I turned the phrase over in my mind like a stone from the riverbed, smooth and cold and exactly the size of the hole in me.

"Mara." Sable's voice from the deck below. She'd come up without my hearing her, which should have frightened me and instead felt like something else entirely. She stood at the rail with the morning light behind her, and there was river-mist caught in her hair, and she looked at the fork the way Thorne looked at it in his lucid hours—as though it were a door he'd once walked through and never stopped hearing close behind him. "He's asking for you."

"What does he want?"

"To know which way you're going." She climbed the steps to the wheelhouse, and her voice dropped low, meant only for me. "He says you already know. He says he watched your father stand at this exact bend twenty-two years ago and choose wrong."

The words went through me like cold water through a seam.

*Choose wrong.* I gripped the wheel. Below us the two channels ran side by side for a hundred yards, the main Vasseur broad and brown and forgiving, the tributary dark under its fig trees, its current sliding fast and silent, the kind of water that doesn't show you its rocks until you're on top of them. Somewhere up that channel was the headwater pool Thorne had traded his last strength to reach. Somewhere up that channel was whatever remained of my father's last expedition—the drowned village, the erased settlement, the truth the survey office wanted buried deeper than any man.

And somewhere behind us, closing at eight knots with government coal in her furnace, was Captain Adrien Vale, who had promised me legitimacy with one hand and kept the other resting, very casually, very politely, on the hilt of everything I loved.

"Okonjo," I said. "How's our draft?"

"Four foot six, lightened since Kessel. We've burned most of the cargo coal."

"The tributary at this season?"

He was quiet a moment. I watched him read the water the way I'd taught him—or the way he'd taught himself, which amounted to the same thing now. "Fast," he said finally. "Green means deep but it means blind. No charts, no soundings, no pilot marks. If we go up that, we're navigating on memory and luck."

"On memory," I said. "Luck can stay ashore."

"And behind us?"

That was the real question, and we both knew it. Behind us was the main channel, and the main channel led to the delta, and the delta led to Montrousse, and Montrousse led to a life in which I handed over the map or lost the boat or both, and either way I lived on afterward, comfortable, legitimate, and hollowed out like one of those river snails the gulls crack open on the sandbars.

Behind us was also Vale's launch, and Vale's launch was not going to let me simply decline. He'd find another way, another season, another pressure point—he'd found Sable once already; I'd seen him file that knowledge away behind his eyes like a man pocketing a key.

"Captain," Okonjo said quietly, "the crew's earned their pay. Some of them have families downstream."

"I know."

"You're going to ask anyway."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, and then he did something he almost never did: he smiled, small and crooked, gone again in a second. "Good. Hate following a captain who asks permission first. Means she's not sure."

* * *

I called them together on the foredeck, all seven of them, with the fork opening ahead and Vale's smoke still hidden beyond the bend. I told them plainly: the map, the expedition, the offer Vale had made me at Kessel Wharf, the launch behind us. I told them the tributary had no charts, that the monsoon was counting itself down in falling barometers and fleeing birds, that going up that channel meant going in blind with a dying man aboard and a government boat on our wake.

Then I told them what the alternative was.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The river talked instead—that endless low conversation water has with itself, against the hull, over the shoals, under everything.

It was Tam, the youngest, who broke it. "They say the survey office drowned a village up there," he said. "My uncle used to talk about it. Said the maps just—stopped. One year there were people, next year there was river." He looked at the green water with an expression I couldn't fully read. Fear, maybe. Or hunger.

"They say a lot of things," said Ferro, the engineer, wiping his hands on a rag that had been black longer than he'd owned it. "They say the survey office is going to pension us all for honesty, too. I'll believe the village first."

"So we run?" Tam asked. "Delta, Montrousse, done?"

"And give him the map?" Sable's voice cut clean across the deck. She stood beside her father's cabin door, and there was nothing soft in her now, nothing of the stowaway caught red-handed two days upstream. "Give him the only thing my father has left to give, the only thing—" She stopped. Breathed. When she continued, her voice was lower and worse for being calmer. "You don't know what that office does with things. They don't keep them. They *end* them."

"And if we go up there," Ferro said, not unkindly, "and the monsoon catches us in the gorge, she ends us too. Whole boat. Everybody."

"Yes," Sable said simply. "Probably."

I loved her a little, right then, for not lying to them. It was a dangerous amount.

Okonjo hadn't moved. He stood at the rail with his arms folded, looking at the fork, and I realized he was waiting—not for me to argue, but for the crew to finish arguing with each other. When the silence finally settled, he unfolded his arms.

"I've followed this woman six years," he said. "She's never once taken the easy water when the hard water was honest. I don't know what's up that channel. Neither does she. But I know what's down the other one, and it's a man who buys things that aren't his." He looked at me. "Your word, Captain. Whatever happens up there—you get us home?"

"I'll get you home," I said. "Or I'll die trying, and you'll eat me if you have to."

A rough laugh went around the deck, the kind that lives next door to fear, and that settled it the way votes settle nothing and laughter settles everything. Ferro spat over the rail and went below to his engines. Tam started coiling lines. The verdict, unrecorded anywhere, unanimous anyway.

* * *

Vale's launch showed her smoke at half past ten.

A gray smudge above the western bend, then the smudge grew a hull, and then the hull grew details I didn't want: the pennant at her masthead, the brass glint of her forward rail, the figure at her bow who even at that distance had the unmistakable posture of a man who has never once doubted his own authority. Eight knots. Maybe nine. The main channel was his road, wide and deep and lawful, and he came up it the way the law comes up everything—certain of its welcome.

I put the Lena's nose toward the fig trees.

"Ferro!" I shouted down the engine tube. "Full steam. Everything she has."

"She hasn't much!"

"She has more than he thinks!"

The tributary took us the way a gate takes a traveler—narrowing, quickening, the banks closing in with walls of fern and hanging vine, the light going suddenly green-gold and underwater-bright. The current seized the hull and hurried us, and I steered by the oldest instruments there are: the color of the water, the lean of the foam, the way the driftwood rode the surface. Dark green meant depth. Brown streaks meant sandbar. Standing waves meant rock underneath, and standing waves that broke white meant rock that wanted to meet us personally.

Behind us, the launch's whistle sounded—one long blast, official, commanding. Then a second. Then, when we gave no answer, a third, shorter, angrier, and I could picture Vale at his rail doing the arithmetic: the tributary was unsurveyed, uncharted, technically unnavigable, and every instinct of his office-trained soul must have been screaming at him to take the main channel and cut us off at some junction downstream—if one existed. But the map was aboard the Lena, and Vale was not a man who trusted rivers to deliver what he was owed.

"He's turning in," Okonjo reported from the stern. "He's actually—yes. He's following."

"How far back?"

"Half a mile. Closing."

The tributary bent hard to starboard around a bluff, and I took the bend wide and then tight, riding the outside current in and cutting across the slack, and the Lena answered me sweetly, six years of knowing between hull and hand. But a loaded riverboat is not a dancer, and Vale's launch drew two feet less than we did and burned fuel like jealousy. Half a mile became a quarter. His whistle came again, closer now, and beneath it I could hear the flat slap-slap of his screw beating the water faster than ours.

At the rail, Sable had the map tube slung across her back like a rifle. When had she taken it? I hadn't asked and she hadn't offered, and there was a whole conversation in that which we were postponing until we had the leisure to survive it.

"There," Okonjo said. "The rope."

I'd rigged it myself an hour earlier, running back to the stern with a coil of the best line aboard, and I'd told no one why. Now I went aft, taking the wheel-long seconds I could spare, and paid the line out over the stern quarter into the water—a long pale serpent floating in our wake, invisible unless you knew to look, trailing back toward the pursuing launch like an invitation.

It wasn't a towline. Nothing about it would hold. It was hemp and theater, and its entire purpose was to be seen.

Vale saw it. Of course he saw it—his bow lookout sang out, and I watched through the glass as the launch slowed, as figures gathered at her stern, as a boathook rose. He would think it panic. He would think the Lena had tried to foul his screw and failed, or that we were offering surrender terms, or simply that debris was debris and caution was cheap. He closed the distance to investigate, because men like Vale cannot leave a loose end untied—it offends them physically.

Which is exactly what I was counting on, because a launch slowing to examine a rope in the water loses way, and a launch that loses way in a rising tributary falls behind, and a quarter mile became half again.

But ropes float, and ropes drift, and the current carried my pale serpent directly toward his screw.

I heard it before anyone could have predicted it—the sudden change in the launch's note, the screw faltering, the thin thread of hemp finding the gap between blade and shaft the way water finds any gap at all. Not fouled. Not stopped. Just enough to make him back full and shake it free, just enough to cost him five minutes of swearing and reversing while his pilot fought the current, and five minutes in a narrowing gorge is a country.

By then we were through the narrows.

The banks pinched in to less than a boat's length on either side, fern brushing the rails, the water running green and swift and utterly committed, and when I looked back—all of one glance, both hands on the wheel—I saw Vale's launch stopped dead in the channel below, her screw churning foam, her whistle blowing that short angry blast over and over like a man shouting into a well. And I saw Vale himself at the rail, small with distance, perfectly still, watching us go.

Not shouting. Not gesturing. Watching. Memorizing. Filing us away the way his office filed everything—against a future use.

"Will he follow?" Sable stood beside me at the wheel now, her shoulder almost touching mine, her eyes on the receding launch.

"Not through the narrows today. He'll lose his nerve or his propeller, and today he'll tell himself it's the propeller." I eased the Lena around a leaning fig trunk, water sliding past the hull with a sound like a long exhale. "But he'll go back down, and he'll telegraph Kessel Wharf, and he'll come again with something bigger and slower and patient. Men like Vale don't chase, Sable. They besiege."

"Then we'd better find what we're looking for before the siege starts."

"We'd better find it before the monsoon starts," I said. "The siege is the least of what's coming."

Ahead the gorge opened just enough to show the river climbing—actual climb, visible in the white seams of the current, the water working uphill against its own nature toward some high source we couldn't see. The air had changed too, cooler, greener, tasting of stone and old rain. Somewhere up there was a pool Thorne had carried in his mind for twenty-two years. Somewhere up there was a village the maps refused to remember. Somewhere up there, if the river was honest, was the answer to what my father had died for—and whether it had been worth the price, and whether the price was finished being collected.

Sable's hand found mine on the wheel. She didn't look at me. Neither of us said anything. Her fingers were cold from the spray and they stayed anyway.

Behind us, faint now, swallowed by the gorge's turning, Vale's whistle blew one last time—long, level, unhurried. Not rage. A promise.

I turned the Lena's face north, into the green throat of the unknown water, and left the comfortable living to drown in our wake.
