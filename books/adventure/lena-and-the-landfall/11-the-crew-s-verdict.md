## 11. The Crew's Verdict

Okonjo had been on the river long enough to know that a boat argued the way weather did—not all at once, but in pressure. You felt it in the way men coiled lines. In how long the silence lasted between one order and the next. In whether the cook salted the stew or left it thin, because a cook who had stopped caring about salt was a cook who had stopped believing in tomorrow.

By the second hour in the uncharted tributary, the *Lena* was arguing.

He stood at the rail near the stern winch with his arms folded, watching the water slide past green and unfamiliar. Behind them, far back now, the fork of the Vasseur had closed like a mouth. Ahead, the channel narrowed between walls of wet stone hung with vines that trailed in the current like drowned hair. No survey buoys. No cut banks with the survey office's orange paint. No names on anything. Just water going where it wanted, and Captain Lockridge taking them into it.

The rope she'd cut still lay coiled on the aft deck where it had fallen. Fifty feet of good hemp, slashed clean through with her knife, its severed end trailing over the transom for an hour before someone hauled it in. Okonjo kept looking at it. A cut rope was a message any deckhand could read: *we are done being towed by other people's intentions.*

But messages were cheap. Rope cost money. And what came next would cost more.

* * *

She called no meeting. That was the first thing he noted, and he noted it carefully, because it told him she already knew. A captain who called a meeting to defend a decision had already lost the room. Lockridge simply worked—worked harder than any of them, as she always did—took the wheel through the first blind bend herself, then handed it to Petit and went forward to sound the channel with the pole, wading to her knees in the shallows off the bow, reading depth by the feel of the silt under her boots.

It was Ashen who finally said it out loud.

Ashen was the oldest of them, sixty if he was a day, a rigger with hands like tree roots and a face that had stopped expressing anything around the time Okonjo was born. He said it quietly, to no one, the way you'd note a change in the wind.

"She turned us off the map."

Nobody answered him. Nobody had to. The words sat down among them in the fo'c'sle that evening like a fourth man, taking up space, breathing.

They were gathered in the mess because the rain had started—a soft preliminary rain, monsoon's scout, tapping at the deck above—and because the stew was hot and nobody wanted to eat alone. Petit, the pilot, sat nearest the lamp, cleaning a compass he did not need cleaning. Ferro, the engineer, had grease to his elbows and a plate balanced on his knee. The boy, Callum, sixteen and all elbows, sat by the door where he could pretend he wasn't listening while listening to everything. And Ashen sat against the bulkhead with his pipe unlit, which was itself a kind of announcement. Ashen only left his pipe cold when he wanted his mouth free for something else.

"Off the map," Petit repeated. He set the compass down. "You understand what that means? Not off the *charts*. Off the map. There is no map. There is nothing ahead of us but what she remembers from a father who's been dead nine years."

"Thorne has a chart," Ferro said.

"Thorne is dying," Petit said. "Thorne can't tell me the draft over a bar from his deathbed, and even if he could—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "I've piloted this river eleven years. I don't turn up unknown water in the dry season. She's done it two weeks before the rains."

"Two weeks before the rains," Ashen said, "is exactly when Vale expects us to run for the delta."

"And maybe running for the delta is the smart thing!" Petit's voice cracked upward, and he seemed embarrassed by it, dropping to a lower register. "Maybe smart is allowed. We have cargo contracts. We have wages. I have a daughter in Kessel who thinks I work an honest boat on an honest river. Now we're smugglers in a gorge that doesn't exist, with a survey launch somewhere behind us full of men with rifles, and our captain cutting ropes like a woman settling a private quarrel."

"It isn't private," Callum said, before he could stop himself. Everyone looked at him. The boy flushed dark but held his ground, which Okonjo noted too—the boy was growing something in him this trip, some spine-wood. "Vale offered to buy the map. She wouldn't sell. That's not a quarrel, that's—that's just not selling."

"Not selling," Petit said softly. "That's a fine phrase when it costs you nothing. It's costing us everything."

The rain thickened overhead. Somewhere forward, faint through the hull, they could hear the muffled rhythm of Sable Thorne moving about the sick berth where her father lay, the small domestic sounds of a dying man's care—water poured, cloth wrung. Okonjo found those sounds worse than shouting. They made the whole gamble human-sized. Made it about a girl and her father and a captain and hers, all of them dead or dying or refusing to be either.

Ferro spoke into the quiet. He was a slow man, deliberate, the kind who thought with his hands. "I've been below since the fork. Checking us over." He wiped his palms on his thighs, leaving fresh streaks. "Shaft bearing's singing a little. Nothing fatal. But if we go back down the Vasseur hard, through Redcliff again at flood stage, I'd want three days in a yard I don't think we'll be given." He shrugged, an enormous shrug, shoulders like a door swinging. "So. Forward or back, the boat pays either way. I only say: don't pretend turning back is safe. Turning back is just a different way of gambling, and the house behind us is named Vale."

Ashen grunted. It might have been agreement. It might have been indigestion. With Ashen you learned to wait.

Okonjo said nothing. He ate his stew. He watched the lamp flame lean when the hull rolled, straightened when it didn't, and understood that every eye in the room had touched him at least once and slid away, the way eyes touch the deep water of a channel without wanting to admit they're checking its depth.

He was the one they were waiting for. He knew it and hated knowing it, because authority was a thing he had never asked to carry and could not put down now that others had decided he held it. Eleven years on the *Lena*. He'd come aboard a runaway with river mud on his feet and nothing in his pockets but a grandmother's proverb, and Lockridge—she'd been mate then, her father alive and roaring at the wheel—had looked at him once, looked at his hands, and said, *Can you coil?* And that had been the whole interview. Can you coil. As if a life could be measured in the neatness of its loops.

Her father had taught him sounding. Had taught him that the river keeps its promises only to those who keep their attention. Had died, according to the official record, in a boating accident on an expedition no record described. Okonjo had stood at the funeral with the rest of the crew and watched the survey office send a single wreath with a printed card, and he had watched young Mara—twenty-two, iron already in her—read that card, fold it once, and drop it into the grave.

He had never discussed with her what the survey office had done or hadn't done. You didn't discuss a wound with the wounded; you just steered clear of pressing on it. But he had watched her refuse Vale's legitimacy twice now, and he had watched her cut that rope, and he knew—as surely as he knew the weight of a wet line—that whatever she was carrying up this dead tributary, it was not greed and it was not madness. It was a debt. And debts to the dead, in his grandmother's country, were the only debts that outranked the living.

Still. The living were sitting in this room, eating stew, and the living got a voice.

* * *

It came to a head past midnight, the way these things do—not in the mess with the lamp lit and everyone reasonable, but in the dark, in the rain, when Petit found Okonjo on watch at the bow and said, low, "We're turning her tonight. Me and Ferro and Ashen. At first light we put the wheel over and run for the Vasseur. I'm telling you because you've earned the telling, and because if you stand against us I don't know what happens, and I'd rather know."

The rain came down in the gray dark like a curtain drawn across the world. The gorge walls were invisible; the river was a sound more than a sight, a long muscular whisper against the hull. Okonjo stood with the rain running off his chin and considered the shape of what Petit was asking, which was mutiny wearing the polite coat of a vote.

"You'd take the wheel from her," Okonjo said.

"I'd *relieve* her," Petit said. "There's a difference."

"There is," Okonjo agreed. "One of those words is true."

Petit's breath came out ragged. In the dark Okonjo could barely see him, just the pale blur of his face and the shine of rain on his oilskin. "Okonjo. I'm frightened. I'll say it plain since no one else will. I am frightened, and fear makes bad pilots, and I know it, and I still want to turn the boat. What does that make my judgment worth?"

"Worth hearing," Okonjo said. "Not worth obeying."

"And yours?"

Ah, there it was. The question underneath the question, rising slow as a sounding lead coming up out of black water. *And yours.*

Okonjo turned his face up into the rain and let it hit him, cold needles on his eyelids, and thought about his grandmother's proverb, the one he'd carried aboard with the mud on his feet: *the rope that holds is not the rope that is strongest, but the rope that is tended.* He thought about the cut hemp lying on the aft deck. He thought about a twenty-two-year-old folding a printed card into a grave.

"Come with me," he said.

* * *

The sick berth was warm and smelled of camphor and river damp. Thorne lay in the narrow bunk like a man already half-carved from wood, his chest lifting in shallow, uneven pulls. Sable sat beside him, asleep sitting up, her hand resting on her father's wrist, her head tipped against the bulkhead. She woke the moment the door moved—woke the way river people wake, all at once, no surface between sleep and readiness—and her eyes went from Okonjo to Petit and back, and Okonjo saw her understand instantly that something was being decided, and see, too, that she had no vote in it, and square herself anyway.

"How is he?" Okonjo asked.

"Quiet tonight. He had hours of talking earlier. Some of it to me. Some of it to a man named—" she hesitated, glanced at Okonjo, chose care, "—to people he served with. Long ago."

"The expedition," Petit said. Not a question. The word had gotten around, the way words do on a boat, traveling through bulkheads faster than sound.

"He talks about the water," Sable said. Her voice was hoarse with keeping vigil. "About a pool at the top of everything. He says my grandfather looked for it his whole life and died one valley short. He says—" she stopped, and her jaw tightened, and Okonjo saw her decide to spend the truth rather than hoard it, spending being the family disease apparently, "—he says he ran away from the last one. From your captain's father's last one. He's been walking back toward it ever since, and he's too late, and he knows it, and he asked us to bring him anyway. That's the whole of it. That's why we're here. Not gold. Not charts. A man trying to finish a thing he abandoned, in the last weeks he has."

The rain drummed. Down in the bunk, Thorne stirred and murmured something—a name, half-formed—and settled.

Petit stared at the old man for a long moment. Then at the girl. Then he took off his cap, which Petit never did indoors, and put it back on, and Okonjo knew the argument had gone out of him even if the fear hadn't. Fear stayed. Fear was ballast; you just learned to sail with it.

"That's not my business," Petit said at last, roughly. "A man's soul is not my business. My business is the channel, and the channel ahead of us is unnamed, and I am supposed to be the one who knows things." He laughed, short and bitter, at himself. "I don't know things. I know the Vasseur. I don't know this."

"No," Okonjo said. "None of us does. That's the whole voyage."

* * *

They gathered on the aft deck at first gray light, all six of them plus the captain, because word travels on boats and because Petit, to his credit, refused to conspire in whispers once he'd stopped conspiring at all. The rain had thinned to a drifting mist. The gorge rose on both sides, dripping, green-black, ancient. The river ran smooth here, deep, deceptively gentle, like a held breath.

Lockridge stood at the wheel, hands light on the spokes, and said nothing. She let them arrange themselves. Okonjo watched her do it and understood it was not passivity—it was the discipline of a woman who had decided that authority taken by force was authority forever borrowed, and authority given freely was the only kind that held in floodwater.

Petit spoke first, and spoke well, better than he had in the mess. He laid it out plainly: the risks astern, the risks ahead, the bearing that sang, the daughter in Kessel, the launch with rifles somewhere behind. He did not ask to turn the boat. He asked, instead, the question he should have asked from the beginning: "Captain. Tell us why. Not the map. Not the dead man's promise. Why *you*."

And Lockridge was silent so long that the mist beaded on her hair and ran down her face like the river itself considering.

Then she said, "Because the survey office erased a village from the maps. A whole village, Petit. People. My father charted it, and it's gone from every record, and Vale's office wants the one document that proves it existed. Because they didn't kill my father for a boating accident—they killed him for what he wrote down. And because if I give them that tube, I make myself the kind of person who survives by handing the dead over to the men who buried them." She looked at each of them in turn, and her voice never rose, which somehow made it carry further. "I won't order you forward. I'll say it once: I'm going to the source. Any man who wants off at the first landing, I'll put him ashore with his wages and no grudge. That's my whole speech."

Silence. The river whispered against the hull.

Ashen stepped forward. The old rigger looked at the captain, then at the water, then spat over the rail with great deliberation, and said, "My father was erased too. Different office. Same ink." And he went back to his coils.

Ferro shrugged his door-wide shrug and went below to listen to his bearing sing.

Callum said, "I'm staying," too loudly, and blushed, and stayed.

Which left Petit, and Petit looked at Okonjo—looked at him the way a man looks at the last card in his hand—and Okonjo understood that whatever happened next would be remembered as having been decided by him, fairly or not, and that this was what earned authority was: not the choosing, but the being seen to choose.

He thought of the rope on the deck. Tended rope holds. Cut rope drifts. And a crew, like a line, was only as strong as the figure at its end who refused to let go.

"We stay," Okonjo said. His voice came out low and even, and it carried down the gorge the way a bell carries. "We stay because turning back is not safety, it's just drowning in water we know. We stay because the captain has never once spent one of us cheap, and I'll not start pricing ourselves for her now. And we stay because—" he paused, and found the truth waiting, simple as a coiled line, "—because I have watched her read water for eleven years, and I have never once seen her wrong about what a river intends. This river intends something. I want to be aboard when we find out what."

Petit closed his eyes. Opened them. Nodded, once, like a man setting a bone.

"Ahead, then," he said hoarsely. "God help us. Ahead."

Lockridge exhaled—Okonjo saw it, the small surrender of her shoulders, the only thanks she allowed herself—and turned the wheel, and the *Lena* leaned into the current and went on.

It was Sable who came to him an hour later, at the rail, as the gorge began to narrow and the water began, very softly, to speak in a new voice—faster, shallower, urgent. She stood beside him without speaking for a while. Then:

"Thank you. Whatever that cost you."

"It cost nothing yet," Okonjo said. "That's what worries me."

She almost smiled. Then her gaze went past him, upstream, and fixed, and the smile died, and Okonjo followed her eyes to the sky above the gorge's far shoulder—where the clouds, all morning white and idle, had begun to stack themselves into towers, blue-black and boiling, building toward the passes like an army that had found the gate open.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable over the water, came the sound he had been dreading all night: an engine. High-pitched. Persistent. Gaining.

The barometer in the wheelhouse, Okonjo would learn within the hour, had begun to fall like a stone let go in deep water.
