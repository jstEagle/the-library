## 12. Fever Dreams

The fever came in tides, and Sable learned to read them the way her father had once taught her to read weather—by the color of things, by the weight of the air, by the small betrayals of the body that no one else thought to watch.

In the low hours, Elias Thorne lay quiet as driftwood, his breathing shallow and even, his hands folded on the blanket she'd pulled from her own bunk because it smelled less like engine oil than the ones Okonjo kept in the galley. In the high hours, he was somewhere else entirely. He spoke to men who had been dead for eleven years. He argued with a river that wasn't there. And twice now—twice, and Sable had counted, because counting was the only thing that made it bearable—he had said a name that stopped her heart in her chest.

*Lockridge.*

Not Mara. Not *the girl*. Not *her father's daughter*, which was how the crew said it when they thought Sable couldn't hear. *Lockridge.* Said the way a man says the name of a debt.

She sat beside him in the narrow cabin that smelled of camphor and river damp, wringing a cloth in a basin of water Okonjo had fetched from the coldest depth of the hold, and she watched her father's face work through whatever country the fever had carried him to. The lantern swung gently with the boat's motion, throwing shadows that climbed the bulkhead like slow animals. Somewhere beyond the hull, the Vasseur murmured its endless monologue against the planking, and above that, fainter, the creak of rope and the tread of feet on deck—the sound of the *Lena* alive around them, carrying them all upstream toward something none of them could name aloud.

"Here." She pressed the cloth to his forehead. "Papa. Here, I've got you."

His eyes opened—not seeing her, not yet—and fixed on some middle distance where the air itself must have been burning. "The water's wrong," he rasped. "Tell him the water's wrong. Tell Lockridge we shouldn't—"

"You're on the boat, Papa. You're on the *Lena*. We're going home."

"Home." The word seemed to puzzle him. His gaze drifted, found her face, held it for one lucid, terrible second. "Sable?"

"I'm here."

"You came." Something moved through his expression that she would spend the rest of her life trying to name—joy and shame tangled so tightly together that neither could be separated out. "You shouldn't have come. It's not—it isn't safe, the things I know aren't—"

"Hush." She wrung the cloth again. "Drink this."

He drank. Obedient as a child, which frightened her more than the fever did, because her father had never been obedient to anything in his life—not to the survey office, not to the companies that wanted his maps, not to her mother's dying wish that he stay in one piece long enough to raise his daughter. He drank because she told him to, and then he lay back, and his eyes went glassy again, and he began to speak.

Sable had learned by the third day not to interrupt. Interrupting scattered him like a startled flock. But listening—listening was its own kind of drowning. She dipped the cloth, pressed it to his brow, his throat, the hollows under his arms where the heat pooled worst, and she listened.

"—told him the tributary was a lie," he murmured. "Told him no river runs backward into its own source. But Lockridge only laughed. He said rivers don't lie, Thorne, they just wait. He said—" A cough took him, wet and deep, and Sable lifted his shoulders and held the tin cup to his lips until the spasm passed. When he subsided, his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "He said the map was drawn by someone who loved the water more than he feared it. He said that's the only kind of map worth following."

"Whose map?" she asked softly. "Papa. Whose map were you following?"

But the fever had him again. His hand rose from the blanket, grasping at nothing, and Sable caught it and held it, and his fingers closed around hers with a strength that surprised her—a prospector's grip, rope-hardened, the grip of a man who had spent forty years pulling himself up out of gorges.

"We left him," he whispered. "God forgive me, Sable, we left him on the ridge."

Her breath went still in her chest.

"The water rose and he wouldn't leave the village. Said the people wouldn't go without their dead. Said—" His voice cracked, splintered, became the voice of a much younger man, a man thirty years younger standing in rain on a ridge above a drowned valley. "Said a captain doesn't abandon his crew, Thorne, and I said neither does a partner, and then I ran anyway. I ran. I took the mule and I ran downriver and I let the flood take the rest of them."

"*Who* left him?" Her own voice sounded far away. "Papa—who did you leave? Who was the captain?"

His eyes rolled toward her, and for a moment—just a moment—the fever thinned, and she saw herself reflected in his pupils, small and distorted, and behind her reflection something else: recognition, horror, the dawning of a man waking on the gallows steps.

"Sable," he said. "Oh, Sable. What have I done."

"Papa—"

"He has a daughter." Tears cut through the grime on his cheeks, two clean lines in the dirt. "Did you know that? Did you know he has a girl? She'll be grown now. She'll be—she runs a boat, the boy at Kessel Wharf said, a riverboat, she works the Vasseur like her father did—" His hand tightened on hers, painful now. "And I took his map. I took his map and I traded it for passage, and she's the one carrying me."

The lantern swung. The river spoke against the hull. Sable sat very still, the cloth dripping forgotten through her fingers onto the deck boards, cold drops landing like small clocks.

"Mara," she said. Her mouth was dry. "Mara Lockridge."

Her father wept. There was no other word for it—no gentler word, no feverish delirium to hide behind. He turned his face into the pillow and wept with the whole wrecked machinery of his body, shoulders shaking, breath hitching, an old man crying for a young man dead eleven years in a flooded valley, and Sable held his hand and felt the grief move between them like current, like the river itself, finding every channel, filling every low place.

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She sat in the corridor outside the cabin with her knees drawn up, watching lamplight waver along the deck seam, turning it over and over: *we left him on the ridge. We left him.*

Her father had spoken of the expedition exactly four times in her childhood, and always the same way—briefly, bitterly, as a story about money lost and promises broken by other people. *They took everything,* he'd say. *Every chart, every sample, every year of my life.* Sable had built her whole understanding of those missing years out of that bitterness. She had believed her father the wronged party. She had believed it the way you believe the ground under your feet.

Now the ground was moving.

Near dawn, footsteps came soft along the corridor, and she looked up expecting Okonjo with the morning tea, but it was Mara. She stood in the lamplight with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back, and up close Sable could see what the daylight usually hid—that she hadn't slept either, that there were shadows under her eyes deep enough to hold water.

"How is he?" Mara asked.

"Worse at night. Better at dawn. The fever breaks a little every morning and comes back by evening." Sable stood, stiff-jointed. "He talked. All night. About the expedition."

Something crossed Mara's face—quick, controlled, gone. Eleven years of control, Sable thought. Eleven years of being nobody's daughter, just a woman with a boat, no surname spoken aloud, no history declared. She knew that much from the crew's careful silences.

"I don't want anything from him," Mara said quietly. "Whatever he tells you. Whatever he thinks he owes me. I didn't take this passage to collect a debt."

"He wants to pay one."

"Then he can pay it by living." Mara's jaw tightened. "Is he asking for me?"

"No. But he talks about you. Or—" Sable hesitated. "About your father. He calls him Lockridge. He says—" She stopped. How did you hand a woman a thing like this? There was no gentle angle. There was only the truth, blunt as a cleaver. "He says they left him. On the ridge, when the flood came. He says your father stayed with the villagers and sent the others up to high ground, and my father ran."

For a long moment Mara said nothing. She looked past Sable, down the corridor, at nothing—or at something eleven years away. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if the whole boat were holding its breath.

"I used to think he abandoned us," Mara said at last. Her voice was level, but Sable heard the cost of the levelness, the way you hear the strain in a rope before it parts. "My father. Three months overdue, then six, then a year, and the survey office sent one boat and called it a search. My mother waited two years and died waiting. I grew up hating him a little, if I'm honest. For choosing a river over us." She exhaled slowly. "And the whole time, he was dying on a ridge keeping strangers alive."

"Sable—" her father's voice, cracked and thin, from inside the cabin. "Sable, is someone there?"

Mara moved first. She brushed past Sable—close enough that their shoulders touched, close enough that Sable caught the smell of woodsmoke and river water that clung to her—and pushed open the cabin door, and knelt by the bunk as naturally as if she had been doing it all her life.

"Elias Thorne," she said. "Look at me."

Her father's eyes opened, found her, and filled with such naked terror that Sable's chest seized. He tried to push himself upright. "Lockridge—I can't—you have to understand, the water came up faster than—"

"I know what happened." Mara's voice was low and steady, the voice she used on the river, the voice that had talked the *Lena* through Redcliff Bend while the rocks reached for her hull. "I know all of it. He stayed. He sent the others up. That's what he did, and that's who he was, and I need you to stop drowning in it long enough to get well. Do you hear me? I didn't bring you up this river to watch you die of guilt. Guilt is cheap, Mr. Thorne. Living costs more. Pay that instead."

Her father stared at her. Then his face crumpled, and he reached out with both hands—one catching her wrist, one catching Sable's—and drew them both close, and for a moment the three of them were bound together over the bunk like a knot, like a splice, three frayed ropes joined into something that might hold.

"I searched for years," he whispered into the space between them. "After. I went back twice. The valley was gone—whole villages gone, the maps erased, the office pretending none of it ever existed. I couldn't find so much as a grave marker. So I kept his charts. The ones he gave me to carry out. I kept them because selling them felt like killing him twice, and losing them felt the same." His grip tightened. "I'm sorry, girl. I'm sorry it took dying to bring me back to his river."

"Don't talk about dying," Sable said, and was surprised by the fierceness in her own voice.

But her father smiled—a small, exhausted, terrible smile—and looked from her to Mara and back again, as if seeing some pattern in the two of them that they could not yet see themselves.

"Two daughters," he murmured. "He'd have liked that. He always said family wasn't blood, it was ballast. Whoever keeps you steady in the rough water." His eyes were closing. "You keep each other steady. Promise me. The river's going to ask more of you than it asked of us—"

He slept. Truly slept, the fever finally ebbing, his breathing settling into something like peace. Sable stayed kneeling, her father's hand still wrapped around hers, and across the bunk Mara knelt too, and neither of them moved to break the circle.

Outside, the light was coming up gray-gold through the mist, and the *Lena* walked her slow way upstream, and somewhere ahead, days distant, the gorge waited with its drowned villages and its rising water and its secrets that the survey office would burn whole towns to keep buried.

Mara rose first. At the door she paused, her back to Sable, her hand resting on the frame.

"My father's charts are aboard this boat," she said quietly. "Thorne's tube. I read enough of it to know the handwriting." She turned her head, just slightly, and the morning light caught the edge of her profile. "I haven't opened it past the seal since the fork. I think—I think I was afraid of what I'd find in his hand." A pause. "Tonight, maybe. When the heat lightning comes. Will you sit with me while I do it?"

It was not a question about a map, and they both knew it.

"Yes," Sable said.

Mara nodded once and was gone, her footsteps fading up the companionway toward the wheelhouse, toward the river, toward the day's work. Sable turned back to her father's sleeping face—at peace, truly at peace, for the first time since the dock at the landing—and touched his brow one last time. Cool. Almost cool.

On the horizon ahead, past the mist, thunder muttered like something clearing its throat.
