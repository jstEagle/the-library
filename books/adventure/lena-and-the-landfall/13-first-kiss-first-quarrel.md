## 13. First Kiss, First Quarrel

The heat lightning came up from the south that night, silent and endless, strobing the underside of the clouds like a lamp behind silk. No thunder followed it. The air was too heavy to carry sound; it lay on the river like wet wool, and the Lena sat at anchor in a backwater below the drowned village, her deck boards swollen with damp, her lanterns burning low against the mosquitoes.

Mara couldn't sleep. She told herself it was the watch schedule, the barometer, the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet. She told herself a great many things, standing at the rail with a cup of cold coffee going bitter in her hand, and none of them were the truth.

The truth was asleep in the forward cabin, three doors down from Thorne's sickroom. The truth had spent the evening hauling lines beside Okonjo until her palms bled and she laughed about it, and Mara had watched her laugh and felt something come loose in her chest that had been rusted shut for years.

The lightning flashed again. In the white instant, the whole river showed itself—every sandbar, every drowned tree, every dark current threading through the black water—and then went out, and Mara was alone with the dark again, which was where she had always been most comfortable.

"You're doing it wrong."

Sable's voice came from the shadow of the deckhouse. Mara didn't startle—she'd heard the bare feet on the boards, had known for a full minute—but she let the silence stretch anyway before answering.

"Doing what wrong?"

"Standing at a rail at midnight pretending you're thinking about the river." Sable came forward into the dim glow of the binnacle lamp. Her hair was down, unbound, and the humidity had curled it into something wild. She leaned on the rail an arm's length away, close enough that Mara could smell woodsmoke and river mud and something underneath that was only her. "Okonjo says you do this when you're worried. Stands his watch so he can see you do it."

"Okonjo talks too much."

"Okonjo is right." Sable turned her face up toward the clouds. Another flash lit her profile—the strong jaw, the mouth set in that stubborn line Mara had been cataloguing for weeks without admitting it. "You're thinking about tomorrow. Whether we push past the village or turn back."

Mara looked out at the water rather than at her. "I'm thinking about a lot of things."

"Name one that isn't the river."

The lightning came again, long this time, a slow pulse of white that held the world still as a photograph: Sable's eyes on hers, steady and unafraid, waiting. When the dark returned, Mara found she had already spoken.

"You," she said. "I'm thinking about you. I've been thinking about you since Redcliff Bend, when you took that line off Okonjo's shoulder without being asked, and I've been lying to myself about it ever since, because lying to myself is the one cargo I've always carried well."

The words hung there between them, impossible to take back. Mara gripped the rail and waited for the sky to fall.

It didn't. Instead Sable laughed—a small, cracked sound, half relief—and reached over and took the cold coffee out of her hand and set it on the caprail, and then her fingers found Mara's and stayed there.

"I've been lying longer," Sable said. "Since the night I stowed aboard. I told myself I came for my father. That was true. But I stayed for—" She stopped, jaw working. "I watched you read that map by lamplight and recognize your father's handwriting, and your face did something I've never seen a face do, and I thought: *there*. Whatever that is, I want to be near it. I want to be near *you*, Mara Lockridge, even if you never say your own name out loud."

"My name's not a secret anymore. You know it."

"I know more than that." Sable stepped closer, until the rail pressed into Mara's back and there was nowhere left to retreat, not that she wanted any. "I know how you hold a wheel. I know you hum when you're reading charts and don't know you're humming. I know you checked on my father four times tonight while pretending to check the bilge."

"He snores. I thought he was drowning."

"Liar." Sable smiled, and the lightning chose that moment to flood the sky again, and in that white light they looked at each other with nothing left hidden, and Mara understood that some things, once illuminated, cannot go back to the dark.

She kissed her.

It was not gentle. It was salt and river water and months of restraint breaking all at once, Sable's hands fisting in her shirt, Mara's palm cradling the back of her head like something rescued from a wreck. Somewhere below, thunder finally spoke—a low grumble, far off, the storm finding its voice at last—and neither of them moved. The Lena rocked gently at her moorings. The mosquitoes sang. The heat lightning pulsed on, silent and enormous above them, and Mara thought, distantly, that she had spent her whole life learning rivers and had never once learned this, and that she wanted to spend the rest of her life failing at it.

When they broke apart, breathless, Sable rested her forehead against Mara's collarbone.

"Well," she said. "That was inevitable."

"Catastrophic, more likely."

"Both." Sable tipped her head back to look at her, and her eyes were bright and fierce and afraid. "Mara. Tell me we're still going on. Tell me this doesn't change anything."

And there it was. The crack beneath the ice.

* * *

They stood apart now, at either end of the rail, the warmth of the kiss already curdling into argument, though neither would have called it that yet.

"We should turn back," Sable said. "Look at the sky. Look at the water. My father is dying, Mara—he doesn't need the source. He needs to die somewhere soft, with his daughter holding his hand, not in some freezing pool at the top of the world while a monsoon eats the valley."

"He made me promise."

"He made you promise a dying man's promise! You think he knows what he asked? He's feverish half the time. He thinks he's twenty-five years old. He thinks my mother is still alive." Sable's voice rose, and she pressed it flat again, glancing toward the deckhouse where Okonjo slept. "He traded you a map for a lie he needed to believe. And you've carried us all into the gorge to honor it."

"The map is mine now. The promise is mine. That's what a promise *is*—it doesn't stop being yours just because it gets heavy."

"And what about the living?" Sable's hands were shaking; Mara could see it even in the dark. "Okonjo. Petit. The boy who fires your boiler—you'd drown all of them for a dead man's geography? For *Vale* to catch us in a flooded gorge with no way out?"

"Vale won't catch us. Not above the fork."

"You cut one rope, Mara. He has two boats' worth of stubbornness and a colonial office behind him, and you think a severed towline stops a man like that?" Sable stepped toward her, and the lightning lit her face, and Mara saw that she was crying without seeming to know it. "I love you. Do you understand what that means? It means I'm asking you to choose us over the dead. My father among them. He'd forgive you. He'd *thank* you. He's spent twenty years trying to apologize to your father's ghost—do you really think dragging him to the source settles anything except the river's appetite?"

Mara was quiet for a long moment. The storm breathed overhead, building itself in silence.

"When I was nine," she said at last, "my father took me up on the pilot house roof during a storm like this one. Just heat lightning, no rain. He pointed at it and said, *that's the river deciding*. All that light with no sound—all that violence with nowhere to go yet. He said a captain's whole job was knowing which way the decision would break." She turned to face Sable fully. "I've spent my life invisible so the survey office couldn't find me, so my father's name couldn't be used, so his charts couldn't be burned by men in clean coats. Turning back now means all of it was for nothing. Every year of hiding. Every run without a registry. The map goes back up that tributary or everything my father died protecting dies with him, twice."

"Your father is dead, Mara."

"Yes," Mara said, very quietly. "He is. I know exactly what that costs. Better than you do, maybe—because I've paid it every day for twenty years, alone, and you got to grieve yours in the open, angry, alive. So don't stand on my deck and tell me what the dead are worth."

The words landed like a blade. Sable flinched as if struck, and for a moment Mara wished them unsaid, but they were true, and true things, once loosed, swim like fish.

"That's not fair," Sable whispered.

"No," Mara admitted. "It isn't. But it's honest, and I've had so little of that lately that I'm keeping it."

Above them the sky tore open—not rain, not yet, but a sheet of lightning so continuous it seemed the clouds themselves had caught fire, and in that terrible daylight Mara saw the future split the way the river forked: one channel narrow and drowning and true, one broad and safe and empty. She had already chosen. She had chosen the night she cut Vale's rope. Everything since was only the river catching up to her decision.

"I can't turn back," she said. "I'm sorry. I love you, and I can't."

Sable wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, like a woman furious at her own tears. "Then we go on. But hear me, Mara Lockridge—if the monsoon catches us in that gorge, if anyone drowns because of that pool of water at the top of the map, I will never forgive you. Never. I'll say it now so you can't pretend you weren't warned."

"I'd think less of you if you didn't."

"And if we make it—if my father touches that water and dies smiling—then what? What are we, after? Two women with a burned boat and a story nobody will buy?"

Mara almost smiled. Almost. "We'll be two women with a story nobody will buy," she said. "Together. I've survived worse odds."

Sable stared at her, and then, despite everything—despite the grief and the fury and the storm stacking itself against the southern horizon—she laughed, wet and broken and real, and crossed the deck and put her arms around Mara and held on like the river was already trying to take her.

They stood like that until the first fat drops began to fall, spotting the deck planks like dark coins.

* * *

The rain arrived an hour later, proper rain at last, drumming on the deckhouse roof, and Mara went below to find the watch changed and the boat breathing easy. She paused at Thorne's door, as she always did.

He was awake. Sitting propped against his rolled bedroll, eyes open in the lamplight, clear for the first time in days. Sable knelt beside him with a cup of willow bark tea, and when she saw Mara in the doorway, something passed across her face—grief and love and warning all braided together.

Thorne looked at Mara for a long time. Then he raised one trembling hand and pointed past her, through the bulkhead, upstream, toward the source.

"She's decided," he said, his voice thin but steady. "I can hear it in the engines. Good girl." His eyes were wet. "Your father would have said the same thing. He'd have said it differently—louder, with more swearing—but he'd have said it."

Mara's throat closed. She managed a nod.

"Captain," Thorne said, and the word landed on her like a christening. "One thing. Before the fever takes me back. The gorge above the drowned village—it floods fast. Faster than any water you've ever seen. When the birds go quiet, you don't wait for the vote. You don't wait for me." His grip found her wrist, surprisingly strong. "Promise me the second part. Promise me you'll leave me if you have to."

Behind him, Sable went very still.

Mara looked at the old man's face—at the shame and the hope tangled in it—and felt the promise she'd already made pulling against the one he was demanding now, two ropes hauling in opposite directions, and somewhere far downstream, faint under the rain, she thought she heard the sound of an engine that wasn't hers.

"I promise," she said.

Thorne smiled, satisfied, and slid back into sleep. Sable rose slowly, staring at Mara with an expression caught between fury and fear.

"An engine," she whispered. "Tell me you heard it too."

Out in the dark, under the rain, the sound came again—low and persistent, fighting upstream, fighting the current, getting closer.

Vale.
