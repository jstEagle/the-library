## 14. The Drowned Village

The tributary narrowed by degrees, the way a sentence narrows to its point. What had been a broad brown avenue on the second morning became a corridor of green by the third, the banks closing in until the jungle leaned over the Lena's rails and dripped onto her deck. The air changed too—thicker, sweeter, rotten with fruit and flowering rot—and the light came down in coins through a canopy so high it seemed more memory than leaves.

Mara stood at the wheel with one hand on the spokes and the other on the chart that was not a chart: Thorne's map, unrolled and weighted at the corners with two brass engine-parts and a coffee tin of rivets. She had not let anyone else touch it since the fork. Not because she feared theft—Okonjo would break the fingers of any man who tried—but because touching it felt like holding her father's wrist again, feeling for a pulse that had stopped years ago.

The handwriting was his. She had known it before Thorne said a word, before she'd read a single label. The way he crossed his sevens. The little hooks he put on his *r*s, like the letters were trying to fly. *Vasseur headwater survey — private log — J. Lockridge*, written along the top margin in ink gone the color of old tea.

Her father had drawn this river. Her father had stood where she was standing, or near enough, and had put down every bend and bar and snarl of deadfall in that quick sure hand, and then he had died somewhere ahead of her, upstream, in country the colonial survey office insisted did not exist.

"Bar's moving," Sable called from the bow, her voice carrying back along the deck the way it always did now, easy and certain, as if she'd been born to shout across water. "Left side's clean. Right's got teeth."

"I see it." Mara eased the wheel over. The Lena answered with the heavy grace of an old woman turning in her sleep, and they slid past the sandbar with a fathom to spare.

Six days up the tributary now. Six days since she'd cut the rope and left Captain Adrien Vale cursing on the water behind them. No sign of him since—a launch that size couldn't force the shallows above the fork without breaking something vital, and every hour she'd spent listening for engines had returned only the sound of birds and the river talking to itself. But she didn't believe in being rid of men like Vale. Men like Vale didn't give up; they found other doors.

She hadn't told the crew what was bothering her. She wasn't sure she could say it aloud yet herself. It was this: the map showed things that should not be there to show.

* * *

They made camp that evening in a cutbank where the map promised firm ground, and the map did not lie. Okonjo got a fire going under a lean-to of oilcloth while the rain held off, and Perrine—the cook, a Basse-Terre woman with forearms like dock rope—produced a stew that smelled like forgiveness. The crew ate in their usual circle, boots nearly touching, passing the pot hand to hand.

Thorne sat apart, as he always did now, propped against a coil of spare line with a blanket over his knees. The fever had burned him down to a scaffolding. Some days he could walk to the rail and back; other days he slept like the dead and woke unsure which decade it was. Tonight was a middling night. He ate half a bowl, watched the fire, and said nothing, which from Thorne was almost conversation.

Sable sat beside him. She always sat beside him now, close enough that their shoulders touched when he swayed. Mara watched them from the wheelhouse steps and felt something complicated move in her chest—not jealousy exactly, but its cousin: the ache of watching someone love openly what you had only ever loved in silence.

"You're staring at that paper again," Sable said, without looking up. She had a talent for knowing where Mara's attention went.

"It stares back."

"Come sit, then. Staring's better done in company."

Mara took the map and folded into the circle, cross-legged, the paper across her knees. The firelight made the ink swim. She traced the river's course with one finger, downstream to upstream, past all the marks her father had set down—*snag, bad water, good wood, village*—

She stopped.

*Village.*

There it was, on the east bank, three days' run above the fork, marked with a small square and her father's tidy annotation: *Ketimbo landing. Trade canoes. Friendly. Salt for fish.* And below it, in smaller script, pressed hard enough to tear the paper slightly: *Ask for Mama Yole. Tell her Joachim still owes her a song.*

Mara looked up at the dark bank across the water. The jungle stood there black and total, giving back nothing.

"There was supposed to be a village here," she said.

Okonjo looked over the fire at her. He was a big man, slow-moving until he wasn't, with a face that kept its own counsel. "Here?"

"Three days up. Ketimbo landing." She turned the map so the firelight fell on it. "My father traded here. Salt for fish. He knew people by name."

Perrine squinted at the page. "That's survey ink? Official?"

"My father worked for the survey office for eleven years. Everything he charted went into the colonial atlas." Mara heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for numbers. "Every trading post, every mission, every village on the Vasseur is in the atlas. I've read it. There's no Ketimbo in it. There's no Ketimbo anywhere."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the river, a monkey screamed once and thought better of it.

"So somebody erased it," Sable said quietly.

"Somebody erased it," Mara agreed.

Thorne stirred against his coil of line. His eyes had come open, and in the firelight they were very clear, the fever burned back for the moment, leaving only the man underneath—old, ashamed, and lucid. "Not erased," he said. "Drowned."

Everyone looked at him.

"The year after your father's last expedition," Thorne said. His voice was gravel dragged uphill. "I wasn't there—I'd already run home to the coast with my tail between my legs, God forgive me—but word travels. A company man wanted the land. Timber concessions, rubber, whatever the season's greed was. The village sat on the best landing for forty miles. So the company bought the survey report, and the report was amended, and the amendment said the bank was unstable, flood-prone, unfit for habitation. And then one dry season the company sent men with dynamite to prove the report right."

He paused. His hands had closed on the blanket.

"They blew the levee upstream," he said. "At night. The whole valley filled in a day. Canoes, houses, the church bell, the graves. Everything." He looked at Mara, and there was something terrible in his face—not guilt this time but recognition, the look of a man seeing his own crime repeated in someone else's handwriting. "Your father charted that village two years before it died. He wrote *friendly* on his map. And the office that employed him needed it never to have existed."

No one spoke. The river ran past in the dark, making its endless small remarks.

It was Okonjo who finally moved. He reached out, took the stew pot off the fire, and set it down in front of Thorne with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about him. "Eat," he said. "Dead men make poor witnesses on an empty stomach."

But Mara barely heard him. She was looking at the map—at the small square, at *Joachim still owes her a song*—and understanding, with a cold that started in her spine and spread outward, what the survey office actually wanted.

Not charts. Not her father's surveys for their accuracy. They had better instruments now, better men, steam launches and echo soundings. What they wanted was the *other* thing her father's maps contained. The names. The debts. The songs owed. The proof that the river had been full of people before the company decided it was empty.

A blank map is a license. A peopled map is a witness.

And her father, dead six years in unmarked water, was still testifying.

* * *

They passed the site itself the next afternoon, and Mara steered the Lena close to the east bank on purpose.

You couldn't tell, from the water, that anything had ever been there. That was the horror of it. The jungle had taken the ground back with the thoroughness of a creditor—vines over everything, trees grown thirty feet in six years, the whole valley a single seamless wall of green. If you didn't know, you'd never know.

But Mara knew. And knowing, she began to see.

A straightness in the tree line that wasn't natural—that was the old levee road. A gap in the canopy where nothing grew quite as tall—the flooded fields, silted shallow, choking on reeds. And there, half-drowned in the green, a shape that made her breath catch: stone. A corner of dressed stone, mortared, standing alone among the trees like a tooth in a gum. The church, or what the flood had left of it. The bell tower's base, maybe. The rest was under six feet of river mud.

"Hard to starboard," she called softly, though there was nothing to avoid. She brought the Lena in until her hull nearly brushed the drowned ground, until the paddle wheel slowed and churned mud-smell up out of the depths, until Sable at the bow could have thrown a stone onto the church foundation.

The crew lined the rail. Nobody said anything. Even the engine seemed to lower its voice.

Sable came back along the deck and stood beside Mara at the wheel, close, her shoulder just touching Mara's arm. She didn't speak either. She just looked, the way Mara was looking, and after a while her hand found Mara's on the spokes and stayed there.

"He knew them," Mara said at last. Her voice came out rough. "By name. He ate at their tables. He—" She stopped, swallowed. "*Joachim still owes her a song.* He wrote jokes on his charts, Sable. Little human things. And they erased all of it because a village full of witnesses was worth less than a timber concession."

"And your father found out," Sable said. "Didn't he. That's why he came back up here. That last time."

Mara had wondered it for years, in the sleepless arithmetic of grief. Now the answer assembled itself out of Thorne's confession, out of the map, out of the drowned stone standing in the green, and it fit with the horrible click of a lock turning.

"He charted the truth," she said. "All of it. Every village, every name, every crime the amendments buried. And then he ran the river one last time to carry it out—to carry *them* out, maybe, warn them, I don't know—and the river kept him." She stared at the foundation stone sliding slowly past. "Vale doesn't want my father's charts restored to the archive. He wants them destroyed. Because as long as they exist, somewhere, in some hand, the company's clean blank atlas is a lie with a witness."

The Lena drifted on. Behind them the stone sank back into the green, and the river closed over the place where a thousand people had lived, as if it had never happened, as if the water had always been empty and always would be.

Except it wasn't empty. Mara could feel it—the way you feel a room where someone has just been crying. The drowned village rode beneath her keel the whole length of its reach, silent, patient, keeping its dead the way the river kept everything.

"We'll stop," she said suddenly. She hadn't planned to say it. "Above the site, where the ground rises. We'll bury something. A marker. Names don't need stone, but stone helps."

Sable squeezed her hand and let go, and went forward to take the lead line, and the moment passed into the ordinary work of the boat, which was how the truest moments always passed.

* * *

They camped that night on high ground above the village, and Mara climbed the bank alone after the watch was set.

The map said there was a trail. There was—barely—a seam in the jungle, widened by nothing but animals and six years of forgetting. She followed it twenty minutes with a lantern hooded to a slit, sweat running down her spine, moths orbiting her like small pale moons. The trail crested a ridge, and the trees opened, and she stopped.

Below her, in a fold of the hill the flood had never touched, stood a graveyard.

Small. Humble. A few dozen stones, most of them wood, rotted to gray lace. But the ridge had spared them, and someone—no one, everyone, the jungle itself—had kept the ground swept clear. Ferns grew to the edge of it and stopped, as if respecting a boundary no living hand had drawn.

Mara walked the rows with her lantern low. The wooden markers had lost their letters. But near the center stood one stone—river granite, carried up from the landing by many hands, surely—and on it, cut shallow and already softening with moss, words in French and in trade-script beneath:

*We remember what the water was told to forget.*

She stood there a long time. The insects sang. Far off, thunder muttered in the direction of the mountains—not monsoon thunder yet, not the real voice of the season, but a rehearsal, a throat clearing.

When she came back down to the boat, the crew was turning in and Thorne was asleep, breathing in that thin whistling way that frightened her more than any fever spike. Sable was waiting at the gangway, arms crossed against the night chill.

"Well?" she asked. She'd seen it in Mara's face before Mara said anything. She always did.

"There are graves on the hill," Mara said. "Someone kept them. Someone comes back." She looked upstream, into the dark where the river gathered itself out of the mountains. "People survived, Sable. The ones who were upriver, or fast, or lucky. And they remember. Which means Vale isn't chasing a map anymore. He's chasing the last copy of a truth that's still walking around alive."

Sable was quiet a moment. Then, softly: "Then we'd better get your father's map to the source before he does."

Mara nodded. But as she took the wheelhouse steps she heard it—far downstream, faint under the frogs and the river-noise, so faint she might have invented it—a sound like an engine laboring against current, there and then gone, there and then gone.

She stood in the dark and listened until the wind changed, and told herself it was nothing.

She did not believe herself.
