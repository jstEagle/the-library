## 15. Vale Follows

The launch was called the *Perseverance*, which Adrien Vale had always considered a fitting name for a vessel of the colonial survey office, and which he now understood to be a joke the river was telling at his expense.

She sat low in the water with her engine flooded and her starboard rail stove in where the rocks had taken their first payment. Two of his men were gone. Not dead—Vale refused the word—*lost*, the way instruments were lost, the way charts were lost, the way whole villages could be struck from a ledger by a man with a steady hand and an official stamp. Corporal Reyes and the boy Fenn had gone into the white water at Redcliff Bend when the towline parted, and the river had folded over them like a page turning, and that was all.

"Sir." Sergeant Okoye stood at his shoulder, dripping, her voice pitched low enough that the remaining crew wouldn't catch the fraying in it. "We should put in. Patch the hull, send word downstream. We've lost the line, sir. We've lost two men."

"We've lost nothing," Vale said.

He heard how it sounded even as he said it. He heard it hang in the wet air between them, absurd and shining, like something a madman would say on a wharf. He said it again anyway, softer, as if repetition could make it true. "Nothing that matters."

* * *

Three days earlier he had still been a reasonable man.

He could mark the hour of it. They had been tied up at Kessel Wharf, and the Lockridge woman's riverboat had come down the current with her paddle wheel turning lazy as a fan in a drawing room, and Vale had stood on the boards with his papers squared and his questions prepared, and he had felt—briefly, foolishly—the pleasure of a task nearly complete. The father's charts. The daughter's silence. Twenty years of a file thickening in the survey office drawers like sediment, and here at last was the last Lockridge, carrying whatever remained of it straight down the Vasseur into his jurisdiction.

She had been polite. That was what he remembered most afterward, in the long nights when memory turned traitor: she had been *polite*. She had offered him tea he didn't drink and answers that were smooth as river stone and just as empty. *My father's papers? Captain, my father has been dead since I was a child.* She had smiled when she said it, and there had been no fear in her face at all, and that absence of fear should have told him everything.

A guilty woman fears. An innocent woman is merely inconvenient. But a woman who knows exactly what she carries and intends to keep it—that woman smiles like she's doing you a kindness.

He had let her go. He had actually stood on the wharf and watched the *Lena* dwindle upstream, telling himself the survey office moved by procedure, that launches were requisitioned through channels, that obsession was a disease of novelists and widows. And then, somewhere past midnight, lying in the customs house bunk listening to the river talk to itself, he had understood that he had been given one look at the thing and let it drift out of reach, and that understanding had entered him like a splinter working toward the heart.

By dawn he had a launch, six men, and a warrant so broadly worded it was less a legal instrument than a blessing.

* * *

Now the *Perseverance* groaned against her mooring line above Redcliff Bend, and the gorge narrowed ahead of them into a throat of granite, and Vale stood at the bow with his binoculars raised and did not lower them.

Through the lenses the rapids resolved into a staircase of white ruin, drops and counter-drops, holes you could drown a horse in, and beyond them—when the spray thinned—the wake of a paddle steamer, faint but unmistakable, climbing.

"She can't be taking the *Lena* through that," Okoye said behind him. "No pilot alive would—"

"He would have," Vale said. "Her father would have."

The words came out of him before he could weigh them, and once they were out he found he believed them completely, the way a man believes in gravity. Lockridge had run this river blind and unregistered for years; Lockridge had died somewhere up this same water with secrets in his saddlebags; and daughters inherit more than names. The girl was not fleeing upriver. She was following a map in her blood.

And somewhere ahead of her, at the end of it, was the source. The headwater pool. The place the old expedition files circled around in that maddening bureaucratic way, redactions stacked like sandbags, the coordinates burned out of every surviving copy. Vale had spent eleven years in the survey office reading those files. He knew what was up there the way a starving man knows there is bread in a locked room: not its shape, not its weight, only that it existed, and that men had been paid to forget it.

The drowned village. Even the name survived only in a marginal note, in a clerk's hand, half-erased: *settlement extirpated prior to annexation — no further record required.*

No further record required. As if records were a courtesy the past could decline.

"Sir." Okoye again. Closer now. "Corporal Reyes has a wife in Kessel. The boy Fenn was nineteen. I'm required to log their loss, and I'm required to recommend we abandon the ascent, and if you order me otherwise I'll obey, but I want it heard that I said it."

Vale lowered the binoculars at last and looked at her. She was a good soldier, Okoye—square, patient, the kind of woman who kept a pencil stub behind her ear and a private ledger of debts the army never collected. He had liked her once. He noticed, distantly, that he no longer felt anything about her at all except impatience, and that frightened him more than the rapids did, because a man who stops feeling his own people is a man the river has already begun to take apart.

"It's heard," he said. "Log it. Recommend it. Put it in your report under my name and spell my name correctly, because in five days' time no one will remember the two men, and everyone will remember whether the Lockridge charts came home."

"That isn't duty, sir."

"No," Vale agreed, and heard the strangeness in his own voice, calm as deep water. "Duty is what I tell myself at night. This is what I am in the morning."

* * *

They ran the rapids at first light, because Vale wanted the sun behind them and the shadows in the gorge still standing, and because waiting had become unbearable in a way he recognized from his father's deathbed—a need to have things finished, settled, filed.

It went wrong almost immediately, and it went wrong in the way he should have predicted, because the river did not fight them. It simply declined to help.

The *Perseverance* was a government launch: heavy, honest, built for channels with names and depths. In the first drop she took water over the bow and her engine coughed, and in the second the current took her stern and swung her broadside, and Vale stood at the wheel with his knuckles white and his collar soaked and shouted course corrections that the river received with perfect indifference. Okoye worked the throttle like a woman wrestling a door shut in a storm. The other three men bailed with buckets and cursed in three languages.

They cleared it. By some arithmetic of luck and stubbornness they cleared the worst of it, and then, in the long churning tail of the rapid where a sane man would have exhaled, the hull met a submerged shelf of rock with a sound like a cathedral door closing, and the starboard seam opened, and the river began, unhurried, to come aboard.

They grounded her on a gravel bar below the bend, listing, taking water faster than the pumps loved. One oar gone. Two hundred rounds soaked but serviceable. Three men left besides himself and Okoye—and one of those, young Duret, had stopped speaking somewhere in the second drop and had not started again.

Vale stood on the gravel bar in the gray light and took inventory the way he'd been trained to, item by item, and found that the inventory of the boat mattered to him not at all. What mattered was upstream. What mattered was a paddle wheel he could no longer see, beating its way toward a pool of water that belonged in no ledger.

He caught himself smiling at the thought, and made himself stop.

* * *

They walked when they couldn't float. The gorge permitted no bank worth the name—granite walls rising sheer on either side, hung with vines that dripped constantly, so that walking the river's edge was like walking through a room where it was always raining indoors. The air was green and warm and thick enough to chew. At night they wedged themselves into crevices above the flood line and listened to the water roar in the dark, and Vale lay awake doing sums.

Not supply sums. He had stopped caring about supplies somewhere past the second day, and Okoye had begun rationing without being asked, silently, the way good sergeants absorb their officers' failures. No—the sums Vale did were older ones. Twenty years since Lockridge vanished up here. Eleven years since Vale first opened the file and read the phrase *charts not surrendered* and felt, in the dull administrative part of his mind, a small cold hook set itself beneath his skin. He had told himself for eleven years it was professional interest. Order, completeness, the empire's great hunger to have everything written down and therefore owned.

But lying on stone with the river shouting, he admitted at last what the hook had been.

His father had been a clerk too. A customs man in a port town that smelled of tar and rot, a small precise man who died owing money to men who did not forgive debts, and who had spent his last week begging officials—in vain—to amend a single entry, a misfiled manifest, that would have saved the family's name. Nothing in the world had ever been amended. The entry stood. The name rotted. And Adrien Vale, nine years old, had learned at the funeral that the world keeps its records the way the river keeps its drowned: it takes what it wants and writes down whatever suits it.

So yes. He wanted the charts. He wanted the truth of the drowned village dragged up into daylight and stamped and signed and *made real*, because a thing made real could be argued with, could be amended, could be—God, the childishness of it, and he knew it was childish and wanted it anyway—*corrected*. He wanted the Lockridge woman to hand him her father's maps so that he could write across them, in his own careful hand, the words no official had ever written for his father: *this record is amended. It stands corrected.*

The river roared. Somewhere above them, faint on the wind, he imagined he could hear a paddle wheel.

"Sir." Okoye crouched beside him in the dark. Her voice had changed over the four days—not insubordinate, worse than that: gentle. Pitying. "Duret hasn't spoken in thirty hours. Menard's hands are cut to the bone. We are five days' march from anywhere, chasing a boat up a gorge ahead of a monsoon that the birds have already started flying away from. I'm asking you, officer to officer. What is it we're going to find up there that's worth a fifth name in your report?"

Vale was quiet a long time. Above the gorge the stars were out, hard and indifferent, and the heat lightning flickered along the rim like a signal no one was sending.

"A correction," he said.

Okoye waited for more. There wasn't any more. There never had been; he saw that now, saw the whole architecture of his career laid bare in the lightning's stutter—a man who had mistaken a wound for a vocation, who had joined the survey office because it promised that everything forgotten could be found, everything erased restored, everything crooked made straight, and who had climbed its ladder for eleven years only to learn that the office forgot things on purpose, that erasure was not the system's failure but its function.

Well. Then he would be the failure the system hadn't planned for.

"Sleep, Sergeant," he said. "Tomorrow we pass them. Tomorrow we're done."

* * *

They passed signs of the *Lena* all the next day, and each one tightened the splinter deeper.

A mooring ring driven into granite, fresh-chipped stone around it. A coil of hemp left behind in the haste of departure—or abandoned deliberately, Vale thought, a message: *we know you follow; we do not care.* Ash from a cookfire still warm when Menard put his hand near it. And once, pressed into the mud of a gravel shoal, the clear print of a woman's boot beside a smaller print, lighter, quicker—two women walking the bank together in the dusk, talking, or arguing, or doing whatever it was people did instead of surrendering.

Vale stood looking at the prints for longer than was useful. The smaller ones bothered him. Thorne's file listed a daughter, estranged, unlocated. So the prospector's girl had come aboard. Families gathering at the deathbed. It was almost touching.

Almost. Everything about them was almost touching, and that was the trap. He made himself see it plainly: a smuggler, a dying prospector, a stowaway, a crew of hired rivermen. Criminals and ghosts, running from the law with stolen history in a wax-sealed tube. If he dressed them in sympathy they would defeat him. He had learned that much from his father's creditors: sentiment was a currency the ruthless did not accept.

That night the rain came—not the monsoon, not yet, but its herald, a brief violent squall that turned the gorge into a drum and washed the trail to nothing. They sheltered under an overhang, five people pressed together in the dark, and Duret finally spoke, one sentence, to no one: "I hear her wheel." And then he was silent again, and no one corrected him, because in the pauses between thunder they all heard it too—real or imagined, a slow patient heartbeat somewhere above them in the dark, climbing.

Vale did not sleep. He sat with his back to the stone and cleaned his pistol by feel, though it was clean, and thought about the wax seal on the tube, and about how a document becomes sacred only when someone is willing to burn for it, and about how he had never once in his life been willing to burn for anything, and how that was about to change, or how it had already changed without his noticing, sometime back at Redcliff Bend, when two men went into the water and he had felt—not grief, not horror—but a kind of terrible lightness, as if the river had taken them and thereby released him from something, as if each thing the journey stripped away made him faster, purer, more nearly what the task required.

Okoye watched him across the fireless dark. He could feel her watching the way you feel weather coming.

"You've stopped logging, sir," she said quietly.

"I'll log it at the end," Vale said. "All of it. One entry."

"What entry?"

He looked up at the black walls of the gorge, where the lightning wrote and rewrote its bright illegible script.

"*Amended,*" he said. "*Corrected. Closed.*"

* * *

On the sixth day the gorge opened its throat and gave them the fork.

Vale saw it first from a shoulder of rock: the main channel bending away eastward, wide and brown and sane, and cutting north from the junction a narrower stream, darker, swifter, running out of country no map in the survey office acknowledged—country shown, on the official sheets, as blank paper. Blank paper. He had stared at that blankness in the archives for eleven years, and here it was wearing water.

And moored at the junction, small in the distance, black smoke standing up from her stack into the windless morning: the *Lena*.

Beside her, absurdly, a second craft—a survey launch, government grey, flying the pennant Vale knew better than his own face. Another launch? His own office? Or the *Perseverance*'s twin, sent by some superior who had decided Vale himself was the anomaly to be corrected?

It didn't matter. Nothing mattered but the geometry of the moment: the *Lena* at the fork, the blank tributary waiting, and the current of the world narrowing, narrowing, toward a single point.

"Signal them," Okoye said at his elbow. "We can hail from here. End it properly, sir. Warrant, witnesses, paperwork. The way it's supposed to go."

Vale raised his binoculars. Through the lenses he saw figures moving on the *Lena*'s deck—small, indistinct, alive—and at the rail a woman with her hair tied back, standing very still, looking not downstream at the sane water but north, into the blankness, like a woman reading a letter only she could see.

"They're going to take the fork," he said.

"Then we've got them cornered. A dead-end tributary, monsoon coming—they'll have to come back down, and we'll be waiting."

"Yes," Vale said softly. He lowered the glasses. His hands were steady. His heart was doing something he had no entry for. "Yes. Let her take the fork. Let her carry it up into the dark for us."

Behind him, Menard began unstowing the rope. Ahead, the *Lena*'s wheel began to turn, and the paddle bit the water, and the little boat swung her bow north into the unmapped current, dragging twenty years of buried paper behind her like a keel.

Vale watched her go, and smiled, and did not notice that he was smiling.

"Come along, Sergeant," he said. "Let's go collect what the empire forgot."
