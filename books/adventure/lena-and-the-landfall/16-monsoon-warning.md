## 16. Monsoon Warning

Okonjo had learned to read weather the way other men read scripture, and he did not like what the sky was saying.

He stood at the Lena's bow rail in the grey hour before dawn, before the deckhands were due to rouse, before the cook's fire sent its first smoke up through the galley vent, and he watched the birds. That was the thing most of them missed, the green hands Okonjo had trained over nine years on this river — everyone watched the water, but the water was a liar. The water told you what the river wanted you to believe. The birds told you the truth. And for three days now, the truth had been flying south past them in long ragged skeins: ibis in their hundreds, whistling ducks packed tight as shot, a heron so exhausted it had landed on the cargo hatch and let Sable walk within an arm's length before it flapped off, heavy with dread, downstream. Birds did not flee a river. Birds fled what was coming *down* a river.

The barometer confirmed it. Mara kept the instrument in the wheelhouse, brass-cased, mounted by the door where her father had mounted it, and Okonjo checked it each morning out of habit older than his service aboard. Yesterday it had stood at twenty-nine point two. This morning the needle sat at twenty-eight point six and had begun, he could see it if he watched long enough, to tremble. Not swing. Tremble. Like a man deciding whether to run.

Behind him the gorge walls rose sheer and black-green, dripping even in dry season, and the river ran narrow and fast between them, brown-green and muscular, carrying broken reeds and whole trees past the boat at a speed that made the mooring lines hum. The Lena lay tied up at a gravel shelf below the last named bend on Thorne's map — *Kwende Shelf*, written in a hand Okonjo now knew belonged to a dead man — and above them, maybe two days' steaming through water no chart admitted existed, waited the headwater pool where Elias Thorne meant to die.

Okonjo turned his face up. The clouds had come down overnight. Not rain clouds yet — something lower and more patient, a ceiling of slate pressing on the gorge rims, swallowing the peaks. The air tasted of iron and hot stone and something green and rotting underneath, the smell of a swamp breathing out. His grandmother would have called it *the sky lowering its shoulder*. She'd have started moving the goats.

He heard footsteps on the deck plates behind him and didn't turn. He knew the weight of everyone aboard. Light and quick, always half-running even when there was nowhere to be: Sable Thorne.

"You're up early," she said, coming to stand beside him at the rail. In the grey light she looked younger than she was and harder than she looked, which Okonjo had decided was the definition of her. "I couldn't sleep. The boat feels wrong."

"The boat feels right," Okonjo said. "That's what's wrong. Feel the deck."

She frowned at him, then crouched and laid her palm flat against the planking. He watched her feel it — the fine tremor running through the timbers, too fast for the engine, too steady for the current.

"What is that?"

"That's the river talking in its sleep," Okonjo said. "High water coming. Days away, maybe less. You can hear it in the hull before you ever see it. Old boats know first." He tapped the rail. "This one was built on this river. Her bones remember monsoon."

Sable straightened slowly. She looked upstream, into the dark throat of the gorge, and Okonjo saw her jaw set in the way it set when she was about to argue for her father.

"He's worse today," she said quietly. "The fever. Mama—Mara says we're close. Two days, maybe three, if the channel holds."

"And if the channel doesn't hold?"

"Then we carry him. I'll carry him myself."

Okonjo nodded, because there was nothing else to do with words like that. They were not argument words. They were rope words, and you didn't test rope while someone's life hung from it.

But he thought about what he did not say, which was this: three days was exactly how long they might have, and the delta was four days downstream, and a boat caught in the gorge when the flood came down was not a boat anymore. It was a coffin with a wheelhouse.

* * *

By midmorning the whole crew knew. There was no hiding it; the river itself announced it. The water had risen a hand's width since dawn and gone from brown-green to the color of strong tea, thick with silt, carrying debris in earnest now — branches, fence rails, a dead antelope turning slowly, its legs stiff as oars. The current against the mooring lines had begun to sing, a low keening note that never stopped, and twice during the morning the Lena shuddered against the shelf as though something enormous had brushed her keel in passing.

Mara called them together on the foredeck at noon. All seven of them: herself, Sable, Okonjo, the twins Bem and Yusuf on the lines, old Ferro from the engine room still wiping grease from his hands, and young Adisa who had signed on at Kessel Wharf and regretted it daily and stayed anyway. Elias Thorne lay below in the aft cabin, sleeping or unconscious — no one used the word *dying* aloud, but everyone thought it in capital letters.

Mara stood by the cargo winch with the map tube under her arm, and Okonjo watched her the way he always watched her in these moments — not her face but her hands. Her hands were calm. That was how you knew how bad it was. When things were merely difficult, Mara Lockridge's hands moved fast, sharp, alive. When things were truly bad, her hands went still, and her voice came down to almost nothing, and every man aboard found himself leaning in to hear.

"I won't dress it up," she said. "The river's coming down early and hard. Barometer's falling like a drunk off a roof. Every bird on this water has gone south. We have a choice, and I want it made by all of us, not just me."

She held up one finger. "Upstream. Two days, maybe less, to the source pool. We put Thorne ashore where he wants to be, and then we run back down ahead of the flood — if the flood lets us. If it catches us in the gorge, we lose the boat and possibly everything else. The gorge narrows to forty meters at the Narrows. A flood surge comes down that throat, nothing floats."

A second finger. "Downstream. Four days to open delta water, where a flood spreads out instead of stacking up. We lose the chance Thorne traded everything for. He dies in a bunk instead of at the water he walked ten years to find. And we go home safe, and some of us will be able to live with that, and some of us won't."

Silence, except the singing of the mooring lines and the far-off mutter of the gorge.

"I'm not voting first," Mara said. "And whatever you decide, I'll steer it. But I want to hear it said out loud. Ferro?"

The old engineer spat over the rail, a ceremony he performed before every serious statement. "Engine's sound. I can give you full steam either direction. But I'll tell you what I told your father, Captain, may he rest, the year we ran the Kessel flood: a boat can outrun water going down. Nothing outruns water coming at you in a gorge. My vote's downstream." He crossed his arms, daring anyone to call him a coward. No one did. Ferro had scars from that Kessel flood that ran from hip to shoulder.

"Bem?"

The taller twin shrugged. "Yusuf and me, we go where the boat goes. But our mother's in Kessel, and she's got one son already buried in this river. Downstream."

Adisa, youngest, stared at his boots. "I signed on for cargo runs," he said miserably. "Nobody said anything about floods and gunboats and dying prospectors." Then he looked up, and to his credit his voice steadied. "But I've seen the captain's face since we took the tributary. I don't think she sleeps. I vote upstream, because I think not finishing it would eat her alive, and she's the only reason any of us eats at all."

Okonjo felt the crew's eyes begin to swing toward him and made them wait. He looked at Mara. She met his gaze without flinching, and he saw what she was carrying behind the calm: her father's handwriting on that map, her father's death in this same water, and now perhaps her own. She was asking him not to save her. She was asking him to help her choose honestly.

Nine years ago, Okonjo had come aboard the Lena owing a debt he could never repay — Mara had pulled him half-drowned out of the Vasseur after the ferry went down, and had never once mentioned it, never once held it over him, had simply handed him a mop and said *you live here now*. Everything he had, he owed to her instinct to stop for drowning strangers. Elias Thorne was a drowning stranger. So was Sable, in her way. So, if he was honest, was Mara herself.

"Captain," he said, and his voice came out low and even, the voice he used for line work in bad water. "You asked us to say it out loud, so I'll say mine out loud. I voted in my head three days ago, when the ibis went past. But I want to say why."

He stepped forward to the winch, where the whole crew could see him.

"Ferro's right. The math is downstream. Any fool can see the math is downstream. But I've been on this river nine years, and I'll tell you what I know about the math. The math says a man should never cast off from a safe dock. The math says stay registered, stay small, stay invisible, pay the survey office its fees and keep your name out of ledgers. The math has kept this boat alive, and it has kept all of us small, and I am tired of small." He turned to look at the aft cabin hatch, behind which an old man was spending his last days. "That man gave the captain a map that belongs to her own blood. He gave it for a promise. Now, I don't know what promises are worth on paper. But I know what they're worth on this boat, because I've watched the captain keep hers for nine years without ever writing one down. If we turn back now, we keep the boat. We lose the crew. Because a crew is not men on a deck. A crew is a promise being kept."

He looked back at Mara. "My vote's upstream. And my word with it, whatever the count says."

Nobody spoke. The river sang against the lines. Then Sable, who had been standing apart, silent as a figurehead, said: "You all know my vote. He's my father. I'll swim it if the boat won't go."

Mara let the silence sit a moment longer, letting each man feel the weight of his own word. Then she nodded, once.

"Three for downstream, four for upstream, and the captain's hand goes with the majority," she said. "We go to the source. Ferro, full steam by the top of the hour. Bem, Yusuf, double-check the lines and lash everything that isn't nailed. Adisa, get extra canvas over the hatches. Okonjo—" her eyes held his, and something passed between them that needed no words, the old acknowledgment, the debt acknowledged both ways at last, "—you're on the bow with the sounding pole. I want eyes on the water every second."

* * *

They cast off into a river that had changed its mind about them.

The current hit the hull the moment the lines came free, shoving the stern downstream, and Mara stood at the wheel wrestling the Lena's nose into it, engine hammering, paddles biting, forcing the boat upstream through water that now fought like a living thing. Okonjo took his post at the bow with the sounding pole, and the work was immediate and brutal — the channel markers of Thorne's map were mostly drowned already, and he called depths by feel, reading the brown water for the darker seams that meant deep channel, the sudden smoothness that meant a snag lying just under the surface waiting to gut them.

By late afternoon the banks had vanished. Water stood in the trees on both sides, silver trunks rising out of flood, and the debris came down heavier — whole rafts of tangled brush, a dugout canoe spinning empty, a roof. Once Okonjo saw, far off, a village's cattle standing belly-deep on a shrinking rise, and he made himself look away because there was nothing the Lena could do for them and he could not afford the grief.

The barometer, checked at dusk, read twenty-seven point nine.

The sky had come down to touch the gorge rims. The air was so thick with pressure that sounds carried strangely — the engine seemed muffled, the shouts of the line crews flattened, and overhead the first heat lightning flickered inside the cloud, silent, like a lamp behind a curtain. Sable came to the bow as the light failed, taking the second watch beside him without being asked, and for a while neither of them spoke. The river roared around the hull. Somewhere upstream, deeper in the gorge, thunder muttered continuously, not cracking but rolling, one long unbroken syllable.

"Do you hear that?" Sable said finally.

Okonjo listened. Under the engine, under the water, under the thunder — a new sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Like a drum beaten very far away, or a heartbeat in the river's chest.

"What is it?" she asked, and he heard the fear in her voice, honest fear, and respected her for not dressing it.

Okonjo gripped the sounding pole and looked upstream into the dark where the sound lived.

"That's the flood," he said. "Coming down the gorge. It's still hours above us. Maybe a day. But it's coming, and it's coming faster than we're climbing." He turned to her. "Tell the captain. Whatever she means to do at that pool, she'd best do it quick. The river's done waiting."

Above them, inside the cloud, the lightning flickered again — once, twice — like something counting.
