## 17. The Source

The river had grown small enough to hold in two cupped hands.

Elias Thorne felt this before he understood it. He had spent the better part of a lifetime thinking of rivers as vast things—brown-shouldered, patient, indifferent to the men who crawled along their margins—and here at the top of the world the Vasseur had been reduced to something a child could dam with stones. It chuckled over white pebbles. It was cold in a way that had never known a delta, never known silt or barge or the greasy shine of a harbor at dusk. It was cold the way the beginning of anything is cold: clean, uncommitted, full of what it had not yet become.

He could not stand without help. That was the arithmetic of his body now, and he had stopped arguing with it. Okonjo walked on his left with the man's big hand fisted gently in his collar, the way one steadies a drunk, and Sable walked on his right with her shoulder wedged under his arm, and between them they moved him up the last stretch of the gorge like a parcel everyone was afraid of dropping.

The rain began as they climbed. Not monsoon rain—not yet, not the wall of it he could hear gathering somewhere beyond the ridgeline like a held breath—but a fine, needling drizzle that beaded in his beard and made the black stones of the gorge gleam like oiled iron. He tasted it. He had been tasting things for weeks now, cataloguing them with the greed of a man eating a last meal: river water, quinine, the smoke of the Lena's stove, the salt of his daughter's skin when she pressed her forehead to his. This rain tasted of nothing at all. It tasted of height.

"Nearly there," Sable said. She said it the way she had said everything for three days—brightly, too brightly, her voice stretched over the truth the way a tarpaulin is stretched over cargo, lashed down at the corners, bulging with what it hides.

He knew what she saw when she looked at him. He had seen it in the polished back of a spoon in the galley, the night before. A face like something left out in weather. Cheeks gone to paper. Eyes sunk back so far the lamplight couldn't reach the bottom of them. And still, absurdly, the old hunger in them, the one thing the fever had not been able to burn out of him.

"Put me down a moment," he said.

"We're close, Papa. Ten minutes, maybe—"

"Put me down." He made his voice into the voice he had used on her when she was small and the world was negotiable. It still worked. It had always worked, and he had spent thirty years ashamed of how easily it worked, and he used it now anyway, because he was a selfish man and had never once, in all his long and wandering life, learned to be anything else.

They sat him on a slab of granite worn smooth by ten thousand years of snowmelt. The gorge walls rose on either side of them, close and dripping, furred with moss the green of bottle glass. Above, a seam of sky the color of pewter. The drizzle ticked softly on the stones. Somewhere upslope, the water made its small, insistent argument with gravity.

Okonjo looked at Sable, and Sable looked at her father, and Elias Thorne understood that they were deciding how much of this he could hear. He almost laughed. Dying men heard everything. Dying men were the best-informed creatures on the river; the whole crew had begun talking over him as though he were furniture, and he had learned more about Mara Lockridge's childhood from the cook's gossip in one week than he had learned about his own daughter in fifteen years of letters never sent.

"Go on," he said. "I'll sit. I'm very good at it now. Practiced."

Sable hesitated, then squeezed his hand and went a few paces up the trail with Okonjo, their heads bent together, their voices dropping into the rain. He watched them go. He watched his daughter's back—straight, stubborn, her hair dark with drizzle and tied back with a strip of red cloth she'd torn from a flour sack, because his daughter had never in her life owned a ribbon she hadn't lost—and he let himself feel the whole weight of what he had done to her, the way a man lets a heavy crate down onto his shoulders to test whether he can still carry it. He couldn't. He had never been able to. He had simply kept walking as though the weight were someone else's problem, and now it was too late to set it down anywhere but here.

The map was in his breast pocket. Or what remained of it.

He took it out. The wax-sealed tube had been opened weeks ago, back at the landing, when he'd traded its contents to Mara Lockridge for a promise. He had kept the paper itself—his one indulgence, his one dishonesty, because he had told her he surrendered it all and he had not. He'd kept it folded against his heart where the fever could not get at it, and he took it out now and unfolded it on his knee with hands that shook like a drunkard's and were, in every way that mattered, exactly that.

Her father's handwriting. Mara's father. Josiah Lockridge's hand, fine and slanted and certain, the letters of a man who had never once doubted that the world could be written down and made to hold still. *Tributary, unnamed. Source pool at bearing 311 from the twin cairns. Depth unmeasured. Water sweet.* And beneath it, in the same hand, smaller, almost shy, the line that had haunted Elias Thorne for twenty years the way a splinter haunts a palm: *E.T. says the old men call it the Eye of the River. I say it is only water. I hope he is wrong. I hope we are both wrong.*

He had been twenty-four years old. Josiah had been thirty-one, and already famous in the way that survey men are famous—known to a handful of clerks and a handful of rivals, a name on the spine of a folio in a government office, a man who could look at a stretch of unmarked green on a chart and see not emptiness but a promise. Elias had been his instrument man. His chainman, his sounder-of-depths, his left hand. He had loved him. He had never said so. Men of that time and that trade did not say such things, and so the love had gone underground and become something harder and less useful: loyalty, then habit, then memory, then guilt.

Then the expedition had turned. He had never been able to find a better word for it, though he had searched for one for two decades, turning it over in the dark of a hundred mining camps and river dives. The expedition had *turned*, the way weather turns, the way a patient turns, and it had turned on them in the drowned country below the gorge, in the village that was not on any chart, in the week when the food went and the guides went and Josiah Lockridge had stood in the rain and refused, absolutely refused, to turn back.

Elias had turned instead. That was the shape of it. That was the simple, ugly, irreducible shape of his whole life since: Josiah would not turn, and Elias did, and he had walked out alive with three others, and Josiah had stayed with the rest, and the survey office had spent twenty years wanting the charts of a dead man's last route, and Elias had spent twenty years wanting the one thing no chart could give him, which was to stand at the top of that river and say *I'm sorry* to a man who could not hear it.

He heard footsteps on the stone. Mara Lockridge came up the trail alone, rain-slicked, moving with that economy he had come to know on the passage—the smallest possible motion, nothing wasted, a woman built out of economy the way a boat is built out of necessity. She stopped a few feet away. She looked at the map on his knee, and he watched her face do something complicated and quick, a whole argument happening behind her eyes in the space of a second, and then settle.

"You kept it," she said. Not an accusation. Something worse. An observation.

"I kept it." He didn't try to excuse himself. Excuses were for men who expected to be believed, and he had forfeited that expectation a long time ago. "I told you I gave you everything. I lied. It's the last lie I had left, and I find I'd rather die without it."

She crouched, slow, the way you crouch near something wounded, and took the paper from his knee. She read it. He watched her read her father's handwriting for the first time in her adult life—he knew the story; the whole crew knew the story by now; the charts confiscated, the name blackened, the daughter who had grown up learning to spell her own surname quietly—and he saw her thumb move once across the small shy line at the bottom, *I hope he is wrong*, and he saw her eyes go bright and furious and wet all at once, the way water goes bright over a stone.

"Why did you leave him?" she asked. "That week. The drowned village. Why did you walk out?"

It was the question he had carried up half the rivers of the world. He had rehearsed answers to it in a thousand camps. None of them would come now. What came instead was the truth, which had no rehearsal and needed none.

"Because I was afraid," he said. "Because the food was gone and the guides said the river ahead ate men, and because Josiah looked at me and asked me to stay, and I could not make my legs obey love. That's all it was. That's all it ever was. He asked, and I said no, and I have spent every day since walking toward this pool to take the *no* back." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "And here's the joke, Miss Lockridge, the fine joke of it: I don't think it can be taken back. I think a *no* said to a man in the rain, twenty years ago, in a country that isn't on any chart—that's a permanent thing. I think it's carved into the water. I think the river remembers."

Mara folded the map with great care, along its old creases, and put it back in his breast pocket herself, pressing it flat over his heart with the heel of her hand, the way you'd press down a dressing on a wound.

"Then let the river hear something else," she said.

She stood and offered him her arm, and Elias Thorne, who had not wept since he was a boy, felt his eyes go hot and did not let them, because he was saving the last of his water, the way he'd learned to save everything on that first terrible expedition, and some habits outlive their reasons.

* * *

The last hundred yards, they carried him.

Okonjo took the weight across his back without ceremony, the way he did everything, and Sable walked with her hand flat against her father's spine as though she could hold his heart beating by pressure alone, and Mara went ahead cutting through the fern brake with a machete, opening a green corridor, and the drizzle thickened as they climbed and the sound of the water grew until it was not a chuckle anymore but a voice, low and constant, the voice of a thing that had been saying the same word since before there were ears to hear it.

And then the ferns ended, and the gorge opened like a hand, and there it was.

The headwater pool.

It was smaller than he had dreamed it, and larger. Smaller in its dimensions—a bowl of perhaps forty paces across, ringed in black stone, fed by a spring that rose in the center with a soft perpetual upwelling, like breath, like the pool was inhaling itself. Larger in every other way. The water was so clear it seemed not to exist, seemed only a lens laid over the stones, and the stones at the bottom were white and rose and one of them, near the spring, was veined with a thread of green that glowed faintly even under the gray sky, some mineral thing, some accident of the deep rock, and Elias Thorne, hanging on Okonjo's back, felt his breath go out of him and not come back for a long moment.

His father had looked for this water. That was the thing. That was the thing he had never told Sable, never told anyone, because it made the whole ruinous obsession hereditary and he could not bear the inheritance passing down. His father had been a well-sinker in the old country, a diviner, a man with a forked stick and a reputation, and on his deathbed—Elias was nine; he remembered the room; he remembered the smell of camphor and the rain on the shutters—his father had gripped his wrist and said, *There's a water at the top of everything, boy. Every river has an eye. Find one, before you die. Find one and look into it.* And Elias had spent fifty years finding it, and the finding had cost him his partner, his daughter's childhood, his name in any book worth being in, and here it was, the Eye of the River, breathing in the rain, and it did not care that he had come. It had been here before his father. It would be here after the survey office and the empire and the Lena and every name on every chart. It was only water. Josiah had been right. And it was the eye of everything. And his father had been right too, and the two truths sat inside Elias Thorne without contradiction, the way a river holds both its banks.

"Set me down," he said, and his voice came out strange and calm. "At the edge. Set me down at the water."

They did. Okonjo lowered him onto the black stone at the pool's rim, and Sable knelt on one side of him and Mara on the other, and the rain came down a little harder, ticking on the pool, ringing it with small silver circles, and the spring breathed in the center, up and up and up, patient as forever.

Elias took off his right boot. It took him a long time. His hands had gone stupid with cold and fever, and Sable moved to help him and he shook his head, and she let him, because she was learning, too late, the same lesson he had learned too late: that there are things a man must do with his own hands or they do not count.

He put his bare foot into the water.

It was so cold it burned. It was so cold it was sweet. It came up over his ankle and his calf and he gasped, and the gasp turned into something that was not quite laughter and not quite weeping, and he knelt there at the rim of the source of the Vasseur River, the river he had followed down as a young man and fled from and spent his whole life crawling back up, and he put his hand into it as well, and he looked into it, the way his father had told him to, fifty years ago, in a room that smelled of camphor.

He saw the stones. He saw the green-veined rock. He saw his own face, wavering, and beside it, in the distortion of the rain-rings, other faces that were not there: Josiah, thirty-one, laughing at something, rain in his black beard; the guides, whose names he had never let himself forget—Mbeki, Anselm, the boy Tomas—faces he had abandoned to the drowned country; and his father, old and dying, saying *find one, before you die.*

"I found it," he said aloud. His voice was very small against the water's voice. "Papa. I found it. It took me long enough."

Then he turned—not his body, which no longer turned easily, but his face, his whole attention—to Mara Lockridge, kneeling at his right with the rain running off her jaw, and he said the other thing. The thing he had carried up every river of his life.

"Josiah," he said, because it was to her father he spoke and he would use the man's name like a man. "Josiah Lockridge. I'm sorry. I turned back. I was afraid, and I turned back, and you stayed, and you died for the others—she told me, your daughter told me, you went back into the water for them, you carried them out one at a time until it took you—and I have spent twenty years letting the world believe I was a hero of that expedition, and I was not, I was the man who walked, and I am sorry, and I am sorry, and I am—"

His voice went. Not from weeping, though he was weeping now, freely, the rain and the river and his grief all one water. It simply went, the way a lamp goes, and Sable's arms were around him and Mara's hand was on his shoulder, and he knelt at the eye of the river with his foot in the beginning of everything and felt, at last, after fifty years of walking, the extraordinary lightness of having set the crate down.

The rain thickened.

It came off the ridgeline in a gray sheet, the first true arm of the monsoon reaching down into the gorge, and the pool's silver circles merged into one shivering skin, and the spring breathed, and Elias Thorne felt the cold of the water climb his arm and enter him and become, at last, indistinguishable from himself. He was not afraid. That was the miracle of it. He had expected fear—had planned his courage around it, had assumed dying would be the last and hardest turning-back—but there was no fear, there was only the water's voice saying its one word, and Sable's heartbeat against his side, and the smell of the rain on hot stone, and somewhere below them, small and iron-colored and brave, the boat that had carried him home.

He smiled. He felt himself smile and could not have stopped it if he had wanted to, and he did not want to.

"Sable," he said, and her name came out whole, unbroken, the first whole thing he had said in weeks. "Look. Look at the water, my girl. It's—"

The word did not finish. Or perhaps it did, and the rain finished it for him, and the river took it up and carried it down through the gorge and the rapids and the drowned village and the delta and out into the sea, the way it carries everything, the way it keeps everything, the way it forgets nothing.

Sable's cry went up against the rain. Okonjo took off his hat. Mara Lockridge knelt at the rim of the pool with a dead man's hand in hers and did not move for a long time, and the spring breathed, and the rain came harder.

And far below, faint under the water's voice, Elias Thorne—had he still had ears to hear it—would have caught another sound rising up the gorge: the flat, insistent, mechanical cough of a launch engine, pushing against the current, coming on.
