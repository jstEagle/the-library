## 18. Landfall

The rain did not begin so much as arrive.

One moment the gorge held its breath—the air thick as tallow, the river running green and low between walls of black stone—and the next the sky simply opened, all at once, like a wound. Mara had seen storms her whole life on the Vasseur: squalls that came up the delta in gray shoulders, rains that fell for a day and a night and moved on. This was none of those. This was the monsoon itself, the thing the old pilots spoke of in the third person, the way sailors speak of drowning.

It came down in sheets, in ropes, in columns you could have moored to. The deck of the *Lena* vanished under it. The gorge walls disappeared. Sound collapsed into a single vast roar—rain on water, water on stone, stone giving way somewhere upstream with a boom she felt through her boots before she heard it.

"Get the cargo lashed!" she shouted, and Okonjo heard her anyway, because Okonjo always heard her, and his great shape moved through the deluge hauling tarps over the crates of Thorne's gear, over the wood they'd split for the boiler, over everything that could float away or rot or drown.

They had made it out of the headwater pool by minutes. Only minutes. She could still see it in her mind's eye when she closed them: the small round pool where the river was born, ringed in fern, and Thorne kneeling at its edge with his ruined hands in the water, whispering an apology to a dead man. And then the first fat drops striking the surface, and Sable screaming that they had to go, *now*, and Mara already turning, already running for the wheel.

That had been two hours ago. It might have been two days. Time had gone strange in the rain.

* * *

The gorge was not built for this.

She stood at the wheel with the canvas hood dripping above her head and watched the river rise with an appetite that made her stomach clench. The gauge painted on the hull—her father's marks, the flood levels of forty years, each one a thin line of white paint with a year beside it—was disappearing line by line beneath the brown water. '09 went under. Then '11. Then the highest mark of all, the one her father had labeled in his careful hand: *the year the gorge ate the ferry.*

"We're above every recorded flood," Sable said beside her. Water ran off her in ropes; she'd stopped bothering with oilskins an hour ago. Her hair was plastered flat, her eyes huge and dark in her pale face. "Mara. We're above everything."

"I know."

"The tributary's never been charted. Nobody knows what this water does here. Nobody's ever *seen* what it does—"

"I know." Mara's hands were steady on the wheel. That surprised her. Inside she felt like the boiler—pressure building past every safe line, the needle trembling against the pin. "Get below. Check on the firebox. If we ship water down the stack we lose steam, and if we lose steam in this—"

Sable didn't move. "You can't pilot us out of this alone."

"I piloted the delta blind before I could write my own name."

"That's not what I said." Sable grabbed her sleeve, turned her, forced her to meet those eyes. Rain hammered the hood between them like applause from a hostile crowd. "I said *alone*. You don't have to do anything alone anymore. That's the whole—that's what I've been trying—" She stopped herself, jaw tight, and Mara saw her decide not to say it. Not now. Not with the sky falling. "*Together,* then. You read the channel. I read the water. Like Redcliff Bend."

Like Redcliff Bend, where Sable had stood on the bow rail with the spray going over her and called the rocks by their shadows, and Mara had understood, with a lurch like ground falling away, that she would trust this woman with the boat, with the crew, with the map, with everything.

"All right," Mara said. "Bow watch. Shout me everything."

Sable squeezed her arm once and was gone into the rain.

* * *

The gorge narrowed ahead—or the water rose to meet the walls, which amounted to the same thing. What had been a channel fifty yards wide that morning was now a brown torrent shouldering against the cliffs on both sides, carrying whole trees end-over-end, carrying the roof of something that might once have been a house. The current had changed its voice. In the morning it had muttered over the shallows; now it bellowed, deep and continuous, a sound Mara felt in her back teeth, in the bones of her skull.

And the *Lena* fought it. Heeled hard to starboard even at half-steam, her paddlewheel thrashing, taking water over the main deck in green surges that swept aft and drained slow through the scuppers. Okonjo appeared at her elbow, streaming, holding his cap crushed in one fist.

"Captain." He never used her name on deck. "Boiler's holding. But the wood's wet through—we're burning half again as much for the same head. And the stern cable's chafing on the bollard. I've doubled it, but if we take another wall of water abeam—"

"How long can we hold?"

He looked at her. Rain sheeted off his broad face. "Against this?" He shook his head slowly. "Captain, this isn't weather. This is the river deciding what it wants to be. We hold until we don't."

Below them, faintly, through the roar, she heard the crew working—Piet at the firebox feeding the wet wood, the boy Tomas bailing the lazarette, the low rhythm of men doing what must be done because the alternative was to stop. She loved them then, suddenly and fiercely, each one, the way you love people only when you understand you may be leading them into the water.

"Keep the stern cable doubled," she said. "And get everyone into life vests. No arguments. If she goes down, I want bodies floating, not drowned."

Okonjo put his cap back on, which he only did when he disagreed with her and had decided to obey anyway. "Yes, Captain."

* * *

It was Sable who saw the launch.

Mara heard her first—a scream torn thin by the rain, barely human, more gull than girl—and then Sable was scrambling back along the gunwale, pointing downstream, shouting words the storm kept stealing.

Then the rain shifted, parted for one breath, and Mara saw it too.

Vale's launch came up the gorge like a knife coming up a throat. It should not have been there. Nothing that size should have survived the rapids below—she knew what those rapids did, she'd threaded them at half-flood and prayed the whole way—but there it was, riding high and wrong, listing to port, its canopy half-torn away, two figures at the bow with lines in their hands and a third figure standing behind them who did not need to hold anything to be the most dangerous object on the river.

Adrien Vale. Come up through water meant to kill him. Losing men to do it—she could see the launch was short-handed, could see the blood-dark stain on the deck planking near the engine housing—and still coming.

"He'll sink," Sable shouted, hauling herself onto the wheel deck. "In this? He'll sink and take his men with him!"

"Yes," Mara said.

"And?"

Mara looked at her. The rain ran between them. "And that's his choice, Sable. He made it below the falls. He's been making it since Kessel Wharf."

But even as she said it, her hand was moving—not to the wheel but to the telegraph, ringing down *full ahead*, because whatever else Vale was, his men were boys from the delta towns pressed into a survey uniform, and she had buried enough people this season.

The launch closed the distance with terrible speed. The current here ran with them, and Vale rode it like he rode everything—without doubt, without mercy, without any sense that the river might have opinions. Fifty yards. Thirty. Close enough now that Mara could see him clearly through the rain: uniform soaked black, hat gone, gray hair plastered to his skull, and his eyes fixed on her with an expression she had last seen on a man watching his house burn.

He raised a speaking trumpet.

"*CAPTAIN LOCKRIDGE.*"

Her father's name. Not hers—she hadn't used it aloud in years, hadn't let anyone use it, had spent a decade being just *Mara, the Lena's captain, no surname worth saying*—and hearing it now, hurled across floodwater by that voice, hit her like a slap.

"*I KNOW YOU CAN HEAR ME. HEAVE TO AND PASS THE TUBE, AND I'LL SEE EVERY SOUL ABOARD PARDONED. UNREGISTERED VESSEL, SMUGGLED CARGO, FALSE PAPERS—I'LL BURN IT ALL. THE MAP FOR YOUR LIVES, CAPTAIN. THAT'S THE LAST OFFER THE EMPIRE MAKES.*"

The empire. Standing in a sinking boat in a flooding gorge, he still spoke as if the empire were present, as if it were a fourth passenger on both vessels, as if its will mattered more than the four hundred thousand tons of water per minute tearing past his hull.

Sable was gripping the rail, knuckles white. "Don't answer him. Don't even—"

"Heave to," Mara repeated softly. "Look at him. He thinks I can heave to."

She took the trumpet from its bracket. Considered. Then lifted it.

"You're taking water amidships, Captain Vale," she called back. "Your engine housing is bleeding oil. You have perhaps twenty minutes of river left, and the gorge has other plans for all of us. Go back. Run for the delta while your boat still floats. I won't think less of you."

Something crossed his face—something almost human, almost grief—and then it closed over, smooth as the water closing over a stone.

"*THE MAP,*" he said. "*OR I COME ABOARD AND TAKE IT.*"

"You'll drown trying."

"*I HAVE DROWNED FOR LESS, MADAM. MY WHOLE CAREER IS DROWNING.*" And there it was again, that crack in the voice, that glimpse of the man underneath the office—some younger Vale, maybe, some survey cadet who'd once believed the maps were for finding things instead of burying them. Gone as fast as it came. He lowered the trumpet and raised his hand, and the two men at the bow began readying grappling irons.

"They're going to board us," Sable said. "In *this*. They'll tear both boats apart."

"Then we make sure they miss."

Mara rang the telegraph. Put the wheel over hard to starboard, toward the cliff-shadowed side where the current ran slack in an eddy line no chart had ever drawn because no chart had ever existed. The *Lena* groaned, swung, and the gap between the boats began to widen—ten yards, fifteen—as Vale's helmsman, less attuned to the water than any soul aboard her vessel, failed to read the eddy and lost steerage in the crosscurrent.

But Vale did not follow. Vale stood at his rail with the rain hammering him flat and drew a pistol from inside his coat, and even at thirty yards, even through the deluge, Mara saw the motion for exactly what it was.

"Sable. Get down."

"No—"

"*Down.*"

The first shot went wide—of course it did, no man alive could shoot straight in this—but the second cracked off the wheel housing a hand's width from her head, splintering wood into her cheek, and the sound of it cut through the storm like a bone breaking.

* * *

Everything converged then, the way rivers converge, the way her whole life seemed to have been converging toward this single brown mile of water.

The gorge flooded. She could see it happening—see the water climbing the cliff walls in visible surges, see a shelf of rock upstream shear loose and slide into the torrent with a concussion that rolled the *Lena* hard enough to throw Tomas against the rail. The river was no longer a river. It was a moving lake with a temper, filling the gorge throat by throat, and every minute brought the water level closer to the top of her paddle boxes, closer to the moment when the *Lena* would no longer be a boat but a piece of driftwood with opinions.

Vale's launch came about, fighting for position, and fired again—twice, three times, wild in the rain, one shot sparking off the boiler casing close enough to singe paint. His men threw a grapnel that fell short, swirled away. He was screaming orders she couldn't hear, and his launch was lower in the water than it had been five minutes ago, visibly lower, and still he came.

"Mara!" Sable, at her shoulder again despite everything, blood or rain on her temple. "The wax tube—it's in your coat—"

"I know where it is."

"If he boards us—"

"He won't board us." She spun the wheel, feeling the rudder bite, feeling the *Lena* answer her the way she had answered the river her whole life—with obedience that was really a kind of argument. "Because in about ten minutes there won't be anything left to board *with*. Look at his freeboard, Sable. He's dying on his feet and he doesn't know it yet."

But she knew, looking at him across the boiling water, that he did know it. That was the horror of Adrien Vale—he knew exactly what he was spending, and he spent it anyway, because the alternative was to go home without the map, and going home without the map meant admitting that his career, his office, his whole gray architecture of legitimacy had been built over a grave. Her father's grave. The drowned village's grave. Everything the survey office wanted sleeping under official silence.

He would rather die in the gorge than allow that. Some part of her respected it. Most of her wanted to spit on it.

The launch surged closer on a wave—a freak wall of water, born of some collapse upstream, that lifted both vessels and slammed them down again. On the *Lena*, crockery shattered below. On the launch, a man went overboard—she saw him go, arms windmilling, mouth open in a cry the rain erased—and the brown water took him under before anyone could so much as throw a line.

Vale did not look after him.

That was the thing Mara would remember afterward, in the long years when she told this story and never sold it: Vale did not look. He stood at the rail with his pistol dripping and his eyes fixed on her coat, on the shape of the wax-sealed tube beneath the oilskin, and he did not glance once at the water that had just swallowed his man whole.

"Okonjo!" she roared.

"Aye, Captain!"

"If he gets a line aboard, cut it. Cut it fast and cut it clean."

"And if he gets *himself* aboard?"

Mara looked at the survey captain—soaked, half-mad, pistol rising again—and felt the tube against her ribs like a second heart. Her father's handwriting inside it. Thorne's confession. The drowned village. The source. Everything the empire wanted and everything she had promised a dying man to carry home, though home, now, was underwater.

"Then I'll deal with him myself," she said.

The third wave came out of the upstream dark without warning—black water, higher than the wheelhouse roof, carrying a whole tree in its teeth—and it struck the *Lena* abeam and drove her sideways toward the cliff, and through the chaos Mara heard, impossibly, above the roar of the monsoon and the groan of her straining boat, the sound of Vale's launch grinding against stone.

She hauled the wheel. The *Lena* answered—slowly, heavily, a wounded animal answering its name—and came off the rocks with paint peeling and timbers shrieking. Ahead, the gorge bent eastward into the worst of the flood, a chute of white destruction where no vessel had ever gone and come back.

Behind her, Vale's launch lay heeled against the cliff foot, engines screaming, pinned and taking water, and Vale himself stood at the rail aiming his pistol at her one last time—and did not fire. Couldn't, or wouldn't. Just stood there in the rain, watching her run, with the expression of a man who has finally understood that the river does not negotiate.

Mara met his eyes across the water. Then she reached into her coat and closed her hand around the wax-sealed tube, warm from her body, and turned her face toward the flooded chute ahead.

"Sable," she said. "Find me dry tinder. And keep it away from the water."

Sable stared at her. "What are you going to do?"

"What my father should have done," Mara said, and put the *Lena*'s nose into the flood.
