## 20. Blind Water

The map burned to nothing in less time than it took to say a prayer.

I stood at the rail and watched the last flakes of ash lift off the deck plates, spin once in the wind off the gorge wall, and vanish into rain so heavy it seemed less like weather than like the sky itself coming down to drown us. Somewhere behind us—upstream, above the roar—Vale's launch was still fighting the current, or being beaten by it. I couldn't tell which. The river had stopped distinguishing between things that wanted to live and things that wanted to die. It simply took everything now, indiscriminately, the way a flood takes fences and cattle and churches alike.

Mara turned from the ashes without ceremony. Her hands were burned along two fingers where the flame had licked too close, and she didn't look at them, and I knew she wouldn't let me look at them either. Not yet. There would be time for wounds later, if there was a later.

"Okonjo," she called, and her voice carried through the rain with that flat, carrying quality I had learned meant she was more afraid than she would ever admit. "Soundings. Every ten seconds. Call them loud."

"Aye." Okonjo's voice came back from the bow, steady as bedrock. He had the lead line already swinging, the wet rope hissing through his palms.

"Amara, keep pressure on the boiler but be ready to kill it on my word. If we take water in the firebox, I want steam dead before it scalds anyone."

"Understood."

"Sable."

My name. Just my name, but the way she said it made me turn fully, made the rain run cold down the back of my neck.

"You're with me," Mara said. "At the wheel. You read the water. I'll steer."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about trust: it does not feel like warmth. It feels like stepping off a ledge in the dark because someone's voice told you the ground was there.

The charts were gone. All of them—the survey office's stolen copies, Vale's precious archive, and now her father's map, the true one, the one Elias Thorne had carried half across the colony in a wax-sealed tube against his heart. Ash on the water. And the Lena was committed now to the gorge, committed past any point where turning back meant anything but death, riding a flood that had swallowed the banks whole, with no chart between us and the sea except whatever lived inside Mara Lockridge's skull.

She took the wheel with both hands, sleeves soaked to the elbow, hair plastered flat against her skull, and she looked—not calm. I want to be honest about this, because everyone who tells this story later will want to make it pretty. She did not look calm. She looked like a woman standing at the edge of her own grave and deciding, deliberately, not to fall in. Her jaw was set so tight I could see the muscle jump beneath the skin. Her eyes moved constantly, reading, reading, reading.

"Talk to me," she said.

So I did. That was the bargain, unspoken but absolute: she held the wheel, and I gave her the river.

"The left bank's gone," I said. "There's no bank anymore—it's all one current, but there's a seam out there, maybe forty yards off the starboard side, where the water changes color. Darker. Deeper."

"I see it. That's the old channel." Her voice was almost conversational, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "My father used to call it the spine. Everything else is just the river wearing its flood clothes."

The Lena drove on into the murk. Rain hammered the pilothouse roof like a thousand small fists. Through the glass—what little the glass showed—all the world was gray-green water and the black walls of the gorge rising on either side, streaming, dripping, crowned in cloud. We were small. We were absurdly, insultingly small, a wooden boat with a tired boiler crawling down the throat of a mountain while the mountain drowned.

"Ten feet!" Okonjo's voice. "And soft!"

"Starboard a point," Mara said, mostly to herself, and the wheel spun under her hands.

* * *

You learn a person fast when you stand beside them at the edge of dying. You learn the small things. I learned that Mara counted under her breath—not prayers, not numbers exactly, but distances, reciting them like a rosary: *bend, shoal, bend, the narrows, the narrows.* I learned that when she was afraid her accent thickened, the river-town vowels of her childhood surfacing through the careful colonial polish she wore everywhere else. I learned that she talked to the boat. Not the way sailors joke about talking to their ships—she talked to the Lena the way you talk to an old dog, low and constant, *easy girl, hold, hold, now*, as if the hull were listening, as if the keel remembered every mile they'd ever run together and only needed reminding.

Maybe it did. Maybe that was the whole secret of her, the thing Vale could never have understood even if he'd tortured it out of her: you cannot chart a river. A chart is a corpse of a river, a river embalmed in ink at one particular hour of one particular year. The living river is something else entirely, and the only way to know it is to give it years of your life until it lives in your blood alongside everything else your father gave you.

"There," I said. "Dead ahead—white water, but it's moving wrong. It's folding back on itself."

"Keeper hole." No hesitation. "Off the port bow, maybe where the old ferry landing used to wash out. My father nearly lost a barge there when I was nine. He said you go at it straight or not at all." Her hands tightened on the wheel spokes. "Straight, then."

We went straight.

I will not pretend I understood what happened next in any technical sense. The bow rose, and rose, and for one long suspended moment the Lena was less a boat than a thrown stone, and then the world dropped away beneath us and we were falling into green chaos, and the impact came up through the deck and into my teeth and knocked the breath out of me like a blow. Water came over the foredeck in a single solid sheet, waist-deep, tearing at anything loose. Somewhere below, Amara was screaming about the firebox doors. Okonjo's lead line sang out—*and-a-half fathoms!*—and then we were through, wallowing, shipping water, alive.

Mara exhaled. One long breath, controlled, the kind you practice.

"That," she said quietly, "was the easy part."

* * *

*

The gorge narrowed as the afternoon failed. I don't know how else to describe the light except that it failed—not dimmed, not faded, but *failed*, the storm swallowing the day whole so that by what should have been mid-afternoon we were running in a green twilight lit only by the stuttering lightning above the canyon rim. Each flash turned the gorge walls into a negative image, white cliffs hanging in black air, and between flashes we ran on sound and memory and faith.

Faith. I kept circling that word. I had never been a woman of faith; my father's God had died somewhere in the same country that killed him, or maybe he'd simply stopped believing in anything he couldn't dig out of the ground. But standing at that wheel, watching Mara's hands, I understood that faith is not belief. Faith is what happens when belief runs out and you keep going anyway.

"Tell me what you see," she said. Her voice had gone hoarse from shouting over the rain.

I leaned into the pilothouse window, wiping the glass with my sleeve, useless, the rain winning every time. "Water's higher. Still rising—I can see the debris line on the walls, it's climbed two feet since the keeper hole. Current's faster. There are trees in it now, whole trees, coming down from upstream."

"Strainers." She said the word the way other people say *plague*. "Where?"

"Port side, hugging the wall. Three of them. Maybe four."

"Then we can't hug the port wall." Her eyes flicked across the water, calculating. "But the starboard side has the eddy under the cliff—that undercut, the one shaped like a hook. If I cut too close we get sucked under the rock. Too far out, the strainers take us." A pause. Lightning strobed. In its light I saw her face clearly for one instant: exhausted, terrified, absolutely resolved. "There's a lane. There's always a lane. My father found it in '09 with half the crew sick and the river in spate. He wrote—" She stopped. The sentence ended there, because the thing he'd written was ash on the water now, and they both knew it.

"Mara." I put my hand over hers on the wheel. Her skin was cold and trembling very slightly, a tremor she'd never have allowed me to see in daylight. "You remember it. You don't need the paper. You've had it in you the whole time."

For a moment she didn't answer. The rain filled the silence, and the river roared underneath it, and somewhere far above, thunder rolled along the gorge like a door slamming in an empty house.

"He used to test me," she said finally, so quietly I had to lean close to hear. "When I was small. He'd cover the chart and make me draw the reach from memory. Bend by bend, snag by snag. I thought it was a game." Her laugh was short and bitter and wet. "He was building a chart that couldn't burn. He just didn't know he was building it in me."

"Then let's use it," I said. "All of it. Every bend he ever taught you."

She looked at me then—really looked, the first time since the burning—and something passed between us that had no name I trusted myself to give it. Not gratitude. Something older and stranger, the recognition of two people who had each inherited a dead man's unfinished business and were, at last, finishing it together.

"Hook rock," she said. "It'll come up on the starboard side looking like a hand. Past it, hard to port, thread between the second strainer and the wall—no wider than the Lena twice over. Then the current does the rest."

"And if you're wrong?"

Her mouth curved, just barely. "Then we'll be having this conversation underwater, and it won't matter."

* * *

*

She was not wrong.

The hook rock rose out of the flood exactly as she'd promised, black and grasping, and she took us past it so close I could have touched it, and then the wheel spun and the gorge tilted and we threaded the gap between the drowning tree and the cliff with inches to spare—I heard the branches rake the hull, a long terrible scraping whisper along the planking, like the river running its fingers over us deciding whether to keep us—and then the current seized the Lena and flung us around the bend and we were through, through, through, and Okonjo whooped from the bow, a raw animal sound of joy, and Amara's voice floated up from the engine well laughing, actually laughing, and I realized my own hands were shaking so badly I had to grip the rail to stay upright.

Mara killed the wheel and stood there, swaying, drenched, her chest heaving.

"That," I said, and had to stop, and start again. "That was impossible."

"No." She shook her head slowly. Water flew from her hair. "Impossible is what Vale thinks this is. This is just Tuesday." But she was smiling as she said it—a real smile, cracked open, exhausted—and she reached out and gripped my shoulder, and her grip was strong, and warm despite the cold, and it did not let go quickly.

* * *

Night, if you could call it night, came down into the gorge like a lid. We could not anchor—the flood would tear any ground tackle to shreds—so we ran on, slower now, Mara choosing the dark water, the deep lanes, feeling her way by the sound of the current against the cliffs. Okonjo rigged lanterns at the bow, and their light showed us nothing but rain and the first few feet of boiling water, but it showed us that, and that was enough. You survive on enough.

Somewhere in those hours I stopped being afraid.

I want to record that accurately, because it surprised me more than anything else that day. The fear didn't leave; it changed. It stopped being a wild animal trying to get out of my chest and became something else, something almost like calm, almost like the eye of the storm the old delta pilots talk about. Because I watched her hands, and her hands never hesitated. Because I listened to her voice, and her voice never broke. Because every time the river offered death in a new costume—strainer, whirlpool, submerged fence line, the drowned steeple of some village that had no right to exist and yet existed, drowned, right there in the lightning flash—she named it before I saw it, and steered past it, and the Lena lived.

Terror transmuted. That's the only phrase I've ever found for it. The alchemy of watching someone do the impossible thing competently, hour after hour, until the impossible becomes simply the work, and your own heartbeat settles into the rhythm of the engine, and you understand that you would follow these hands anywhere, into anything, and that this knowledge is no longer frightening at all. It is the most restful thing in the world.

"Sleep," she told me once, near what should have been midnight. "Even an hour. Okonjo can read the water."

"No," I said.

"Sable—"

"I'm where I'm supposed to be." I said it simply, and she heard what was underneath it, and she didn't argue again.

* * *

*

Dawn came up gray and enormous, and the gorge began, at last, to open.

It opened gradually, the way a fist opens—cliff giving way to slope, slope to wooded bluff, the walls leaning back, the sky widening overhead from a ribbon to a band to a country. The flood was still with us, still monstrous, the Vasseur grown fat and brown and insane with rain, but it spread itself thinner here, and the current gentled from rage to mere violence, and the Lena rode it with her decks awash and her boiler grumbling and her people standing at the rails staring at a world remade overnight: fields become lakes, roads become rivers, treetops standing in water like drowned men waving.

Mara steered us into the spine of the old channel and held us there, and her shoulders, which had been iron for eighteen hours, finally began to come down.

"We'll raise the delta by tomorrow's dawn," she said. "If the bar holds. If the bar hasn't shifted." She glanced sideways at me, and there was exhaustion in her face, and grief—for Thorne, for the map, for everything the fire had taken—and underneath it, unmistakable even through all of that, a fierce and quiet pride. "My father ran this reach blind once. Flood of '09. He said afterward it was the finest morning of his life, because he'd asked the river for everything it had, and it had given it, and he was still floating."

"And you?" I asked. "Is this yours? Your finest morning?"

She considered the question seriously, the way she considered everything, her eyes on the brown water, her burned fingers resting light on the wheel.

"It's not finished yet," she said.

That was when Okonjo's voice came down from the bow, and it was not the voice of a man reporting depth.

"Captain." A pause, the kind of pause that has weight. "Smoke. Astern. Low on the water, coming fast."

I turned. Far downstream of us—behind us, impossibly, *still* behind us—a smudge of coal smoke hung above the flooded treeline, and beneath it, cutting its own wake through the drowned fields, a shape was moving. Small. Persistent. Angry.

Vale's launch. Half-drowned, half-broken, missing men, missing reason—but coming.

Mara looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked at me, and the exhaustion fell away from her face like a mask dropping, and what was underneath was not fear.

"Well," she said, and took the wheel again. "She's not done with us after all."
