## 21. Delta Light

The river stopped being a river somewhere in the dark, and by the time the sky began to pale, we were moving through a country of water that had forgotten which way it meant to go.

I stood at the wheel with my hands still shaking, though the flood was three hours behind us. The gorge had tried to keep us. I could feel it yet in my shoulders, in the ache along my jaw where I'd been clenching since the last bend, in the raw places on my palms where the spokes had burned through skin. But the Lena was under me, alive under me—the old diesel muttering its one stubborn note, the hull groaning but holding—and the water around us had gone wide and slow and braided, splitting into a hundred channels that wandered through drowned grass like veins through a leaf.

Dawn came up out of the east the way it does only in the delta: not gradually, but all at once, as if someone had tipped the light out of a jug. It came gold and then rose and then a white so clean it hurt, and it lay across the flooded savanna in long flat sheets, and every channel we passed caught it and threw it back doubled.

Sable was beside me. She hadn't left the wheelhouse since Blind Water—since she'd stood at my shoulder reading the current while I drove us down through a gorge that no longer existed on any chart, calling the water for me in a voice that never once cracked. She was asleep now, or nearly; she sat on the pilot's bench with her knees drawn up, her chin on her arms, her eyes half-open and fixed on the light with the particular blankness of a body that has used up everything it has.

"You should sleep," I said.

"So should you."

"I'm flying the boat."

"You're steering a river," she said, "and the river's asleep too." But she didn't move, and neither did I, and the Lena carried us both down through the gold.

Okonjo came up from below with coffee in an enamel pot and four chipped mugs, because he was the kind of man who counted heads even when he knew there were only three left awake. He poured without asking. The coffee was terrible—scorched, over-sweetened, made with water that still tasted faintly of the gorge—and it was the best thing I had ever drunk.

"How is she?" I asked, nodding at the deck below, where they'd laid Thorne out under the awning aft, wrapped in canvas, weighted according to his own instructions given weeks ago in a lucid hour none of us had known would be his last lucid hour.

"Quiet," Okonjo said. "The dead are always quiet. It's the living I'm watching." He looked at me over the rim of his mug with those patient, unreadable eyes. "You look like a woman who burned something."

"I did."

"Good," he said, and drank his coffee, and said nothing else about it, which was its own kind of absolution.

* * *

We came into the first of the delta villages as the mist was burning off—a scatter of stilt houses above brown water, fishing nets hung out like banners, children already paddling dugouts between the channels. They saw us coming and stood up in their boats to watch, because a boat like the Lena doesn't come down the Vasseur's delta often anymore, and never, I'd have wagered, looking like this: paddle-wheel housing stove in on the port side, rail torn away in a six-foot gap, superstructure scorched where Vale's launch had come alongside and burned, mud to the gunwales and a dead man lying under canvas on her stern deck.

I brought her in slow. There was no dock worth the name, just pilings and a plank walk, and I set us against them so gently that the fenders barely sighed. My hands knew how. That was the strange thing—all night I'd been certain I was flying apart, and my hands had known exactly what to do anyway, and they went on knowing.

The village headman came down the planks, an old man with a face like driftwood and a machete at his belt more out of habit than threat. He looked at the boat a long time. Then he looked at me.

"You came down the gorge," he said. Not a question.

"We came down the gorge."

"In monsoon."

"In monsoon."

He nodded slowly, the way men nod when a story confirms something they've suspected all their lives. "My grandfather said the old captain did it once. Going up. Nobody believed him either." He looked past me then, at the canvas shape on the stern, and took off his hat, and held it against his chest, and after a moment the whole village had gone quiet behind him—not silent, because the delta is never silent, water and birds and children see to that—but quiet in the way of people making room for grief they haven't been introduced to.

We buried Elias Thorne that afternoon on the levee above the village, in ground the floods don't reach, under a fig tree that had probably been there before the survey office had a name. Sable read nothing. She just knelt and put her hand flat on the turned earth for a long time, and then stood, and brushed off her knees, and asked the headman if the tree would be there in fifty years, and he said trees outlive everyone's reasons for planting them, and she said good, and that was the whole of the service. I kept my hand on her shoulder the entire time. She never leaned into it. She never moved away from it either.

* * *

That evening the crew gathered in the saloon, what was left of the saloon—one bench cracked, the table scarred where Vale's boarding hook had gouged it, the lamp swinging on its gimbals though the water was flat as poured tin. Six of us besides Sable and me: Okonjo, Petit, the Ferreira brothers, old Mabille who'd run the engine room thirty years and swore at it like a wife he couldn't live without, and young Anselm who'd signed on at Kessel Wharf eight months ago for the wages and stayed for reasons he probably couldn't have named.

I told them the truth. All of it. About my father, about the charts, about Thorne and the map and the tributary and the drowned village and Vale. I'd spent my whole life being careful with the truth, rationing it out like water in a dry season, and I found that after the gorge I simply didn't have the strength to be careful anymore.

When I finished, nobody spoke for a while. Mabille cleaned his pipe with a bit of wire, very thoroughly.

"The survey office will come asking," Petit said finally. He was the practical one. "They'll want statements. They'll want the map."

"There is no map," I said. "There's ash on the water forty miles upstream, and by tomorrow there won't even be that."

"They'll want the story, then."

"And that," I said, "is yours to give or not. Every one of you. I can't order silence. I can't pay for it. What happened on this boat belongs to whoever carried it, and that's all of us." I looked around the table. "If any man wants to sell it—to the papers, to the office, to whoever pays—I'll write him a fair reference tonight and shake his hand at the gangplank. No grudge. No debt. I mean that."

Mabille tamped his pipe, lit it, got it going on the third match. "Thirty years I've run engines on this river," he said. "You know what I've learned? A story's like steam. Worth something only while it's doing work. That story—" he jerked his chin toward the stern, toward the levee and the fig tree, "—that story isn't for sale because selling it wouldn't do anything. Wouldn't bring him back. Wouldn't un-burn the map. Wouldn't make the office anything but richer in paper." He drew on the pipe. "Some things you keep because keeping them is the only use they've got."

One by one, the others said their piece, and it amounted to the same thing said different ways. The Ferreira brothers talked about Thorne teaching them card games during the fever watches. Anselm said he'd signed on for wages and was getting off with something wages couldn't buy, and he wasn't fool enough to trade it back. Okonjo said nothing at all until the end, and then he said only: "Captain. When do we sail?"

And that was the verdict. Not sworn, not signed—just a question about sailing, asked as if the answer were obvious, as if of course we would go on, as if the Lena were not a wreck with a grave on her stern but a boat with a run to make.

I slept that night in the wheelhouse chair, and for the first time since Kessel Wharf I dreamed of nothing at all.

* * *

Repairs took nine days. The delta gave us what it had: a shipwright two channels over whose grandfather had built riverboats before the office existed; timber cut green and stacked to season by men who laughed at our hurry; pitch and canvas and a new section of rail welded from scrap by a smith who worked barefoot in the shallows. The villagers wouldn't take coin at first. We paid them in cargo runs instead—three trips ferrying dried fish and salt up-channel to the market towns—and by the second trip the Lena was carrying freight again, riding low and honest, doing the work she was built for.

Sable worked alongside the crew the whole nine days. She learned caulking. She learned the engine room well enough that Mabille started letting her mind the fuel line, which those who knew Mabille understood to be a decoration of the highest order. On the fourth day I found her up in the rigging at dawn, re-lashing the signal halyards that had frayed in the gorge, working with a focus so total she didn't hear me come aboard.

"You could rest," I called up.

"I could," she said, not stopping. "Resting feels like leaving."

I understood that better than I wanted to. So instead of arguing I climbed up and worked beside her, and we lashed line in the early light, and neither of us said anything important, and it was one of the best mornings of my life.

On the seventh evening she came to find me on the roof of the wheelhouse, where I'd taken to sitting after supper with the last of the light. She climbed up beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and for a while we watched the egrets come in to roost along the far channel, hundreds of them, settling into the mangroves like snow falling upward.

"My father loved you," she said. "Before he ever met you. He loved what you were going to be. He told me that once, years ago—that Lockridge's daughter would either hate the river or marry it, and either way she'd be magnificent." Her voice caught, steadied. "He was right about the river part."

"And the other part?"

She turned and looked at me, and in the last of the light her eyes were the exact color of the channel water, brown and gold and moving. "The other part," she said, "is standing right here deciding whether to kiss you again, and wondering why that keeps feeling like a decision instead of a fact."

"It feels like a decision," I said, "because I spent twenty years deciding everything alone. Give me time to learn otherwise."

"We have time," she said. "That's the whole point of surviving. You get the time."

So we sat, and the egrets settled, and the delta breathed around us, and I let the time begin.

* * *

On the tenth morning we cast off for the run down to Port Meridian, the delta's mouth and the world's front door. The crew voted on it formally, which made me laugh—Okonjo insisting on a show of hands as if we were a parliament—but I understood him too. After a voyage like ours, you re-form the crew deliberately, out loud, or the voyage keeps you.

The Lena went down through the delta channels with the tide, patched and painted unevenly, her port rail newer than her starboard, her wheelhouse roof still bearing the scorch shadow that no amount of scrubbing would lift. She looked, Mabille said, like a veteran of something—which was true, and which was also, I realized, exactly what I looked like, and what Sable looked like, and what every soul aboard looked like. We had come out of the gorge with the same face the boat wore: battered, whole, unmistakably marked.

Port Meridian rose out of the haze by midday—masts and cranes and the long gray sheds of the customs house, the survey office flag hanging limp over its annex like something ashamed of itself. There would be questions there. There would be Vale's superiors, or Vale's replacements, and clerks with forms, and someone would eventually ask me my full name for the record, the way they always did, the way I had dodged for fifteen years with a first name and a shrug.

Sable stood beside me at the rail as the harbor opened up. "They'll ask," she said.

"I know."

"What will you tell them?"

I watched the cranes swing, the ferries cross, the small busy traffic of a world that had gone on without me and would go on without my father's charts and without Elias Thorne and without everything the gorge had swallowed. I thought about a registry book, and a pen, and a blank line waiting.

"I'll tell them the truth," I said. "All of it. Starting with my name."

And the Lena carried us in through the harbor light, straight into whatever came next.
