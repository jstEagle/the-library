## 22. Lena and the Landfall

The registry office at Port Meridian smelled of ink and mildew and old paper gone soft at the edges, the way everything in the delta smelled when the monsoon had finished with it and moved on to ruin somewhere else. Sable stood by the window while Mara spoke to the clerk, and she watched the light come off the harbor in long copper sheets, broken up by masts and cranes and the slow brown turn of the river going out to meet the sea.

Three weeks since the gorge. Three weeks since they had come down out of the floodwater with the Lena's rail stove in on the port side and half her paint stripped to bare grey wood, and the crew standing on deck like survivors of a war nobody would ever write down. Three weeks of repairs, of haggling with shipwrights who asked too many questions and got answers that were true but incomplete, of Okonjo teaching Sable the knots she'd fumbled in the rapids until her fingers stopped bleeding and started remembering.

And three weeks of watching Mara sleep.

Not every night. Not in any way that either of them had named aloud yet, because naming things had a way of making them real, and real things could be taken. But Sable had learned to read water in the gorge—learned it from Mara herself, shouting over the roar, *watch where the current smooths, smooth water lies*—and she read Mara now the same way. The smoothed-over surface. The thing moving underneath.

Her father was buried above the headwater pool, under a cairn they'd built from river stones with Okonjo's strong hands doing most of the lifting. They'd said words over him. Sable had said the most, though she couldn't remember a single one of them now, only the sound of rain starting on the leaves as if the sky itself had come to listen. She carried his wax-sealed tube still—the empty one, the map burned to ash on the flooding deck—and she kept her father's brass compass inside it, the needle bent two degrees off true by some blow it had taken years before Sable was born. A compass that lied gently. It seemed right, somehow, that she should navigate by it.

"Name of the vessel?" the clerk asked. He was an old man with spectacles thick as portholes, and he said it the way clerks always said it, bored, already reaching for his pen.

Sable turned from the window.

Mara stood at the counter with her hands flat on the wood, and Sable saw—because she had learned to see it—that her hands were not quite steady. The same hands that had held the wheel through Blind Water, that had fought Vale on a drowning deck, that had burned a map worth more than the boat herself. Steady through all of that. Shaking now over a form and a pen.

"Lockridge," Mara said.

The name went into the room like a stone into still water.

The clerk's pen stopped. He looked up over his spectacles, and behind him, in the corner where the office's older registers were shelved—the ones bound in cracked leather, the ones nobody opened—a kind of silence gathered, as though the books themselves were listening.

"Lockridge," the clerk repeated. "That name was struck from the rolls. Eleven years ago. By order of the survey office."

"I know," Mara said. "I'm asking you to strike it back in."

* * *

Sable had heard the story in pieces, the way you hear anything important: never all at once, never in order. On the third night after the delta, sitting on the Lena's repaired foredeck with a bottle of rum Okonjo had produced from somewhere no one asked about, Mara had told her about her father. About the charts he'd drawn of the upper Vasseur and the tributaries beyond it—charts so good the survey office had wanted them, and then wanted them suppressed, and then wanted the man who drew them gone. About how he'd refused to surrender them, and how the official record had simply closed around him like water closing over a stone. No trial. No verdict. Just a name removed from the rolls, a boat impounded, a daughter left to grow up learning to be invisible because visible people got asked questions.

"He wasn't a criminal," Mara had said, turning the bottle slowly in her hands. "He was a better cartographer than they were. That was the whole crime. He mapped things they'd decided didn't exist." She'd laughed, short and without much humor in it. "The drowned village. You saw what they did there. Wiped a whole settlement off the record like wiping a smudge off glass. My father put it back on the page. That's all he did. He put things back on the page."

"And you spent your whole life being the opposite," Sable had said. "Erasing yourself."

"It worked," Mara said. "Nobody looked twice at me for eleven years."

"Did it work?" Sable asked. "Or did you just get very good at drowning quietly?"

Mara hadn't answered that. But she'd reached over, in the dark, and found Sable's hand, and held it, and that had been its own kind of answer—the first honest chart either of them had drawn.

Now, in the registry office, the clerk was saying something about procedure, about appeals, about the survey office's authority over the rolls, and Sable watched Mara listen to all of it with the patience of someone who had already decided how the story ended.

"The survey office," Mara said mildly, "no longer has an officer on this stretch of the river."

That was true, though the shape of how it became true was something they agreed never to sell, along with the rest of it. Vale's launch had been swept away below the second falls with Vale himself on board—or so the river had told them, in its indifferent way, and the river was the only witness whose account anyone trusted. Two of his men had washed up downstream in the days that followed. Vale had not. There had been a young lieutenant at Kessel Wharf last week, pale and polite, asking careful questions, and Mara had given him careful answers, and in the end he'd written something in his notebook that satisfied whatever needed satisfying and gone away. Perhaps the survey office preferred, now, that the whole affair stay unwritten. Empires are made of paper, and paper burns.

"There is a process," the clerk said weakly.

"I have time," Mara said. "I've got a boat that needs painting anyway."

* * *

It took nine days. Nine days of forms and fees and one memorable afternoon in which a survey office functionary arrived to object and departed looking like a man who had walked into a door he couldn't see, and on the tenth morning the clerk dipped his pen and wrote the name into the register in his small precise hand:

*Lena. River steamer, forty-two feet. Master and owner: M. Lockridge.*

"Mara Lockridge," the clerk read aloud, as if tasting it. "Daughter of Josiah Lockridge?"

"Yes," Mara said.

He wrote that too. Then he looked up. "And crew?"

"One mate," Mara said. And she turned, and looked at Sable across the dusty sunlit room, and there was something in her face that Sable had seen only once before—at the top of the gorge, in the first terrible hour of the flood, when Mara had shouted *stay with me* and Sable had stayed, and both of them had understood afterward that neither of them meant the boat.

"Sable Thorne," Sable said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "Mate."

The pen scratched. Two names, side by side, in a government book that would outlive them both. Sable thought of her father, who had spent his last months trying to get home to a daughter who wouldn't speak to him, and who had died at the source he sought with an apology on his lips and a smile on his face, and she thought: *he would have liked this part.* The paperwork. The ordinary miracle of two names written down where anyone could find them. Her father had spent his whole life making maps so that people wouldn't be lost, and here, at last, was a map that led to her.

The clerk blotted the page and slid the register toward them. "Sign."

Mara signed first. Then she passed the pen to Sable without a word, and their fingers touched over the barrel of it, and Sable signed beneath her, and the ink dried almost instantly in the delta heat, as if the page had been waiting for it.

* * *

They walked down to the wharf together through the morning market—fish and fruit and rope and the endless argument of gulls—and Sable kept glancing sideways at Mara, who wore her new-old name the way she wore everything, close and quiet, but with her chin up in a way Sable hadn't seen in the weeks since the gorge. Since before the gorge. Maybe since ever.

"You're staring," Mara said.

"I'm navigating," Sable said. "You taught me to keep my eyes on the water."

"I taught you to watch for smooth water. Smooth water lies."

"So does your face, sometimes." Sable bumped her shoulder against Mara's arm. "Right now it's lying about how much this costs you. Saying the name out loud. In daylight. To a clerk."

Mara was quiet for a few steps. A porter went past under a mountain of bananas; a child chased a chicken between the stalls; the river breathed its slow brown breath beside them all.

"My father used to say," Mara said finally, "that a river doesn't hide. It just moves. Everything in it is visible if you're patient enough to look. He thought that was the most honest thing in the world." She looked out at the harbor, at the Lena riding at her moorings with fresh white paint on her rails and the old scars still showing underneath if you knew where to look. "I spent eleven years trying to be invisible. Turns out I'm just a river. I move."

"That's the worst metaphor I've ever heard," Sable said, "and I grew up with a prospector."

Mara laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, loud enough that a fishwife glanced over—and Sable felt it land in her chest like an anchor finding bottom.

* * *

They spent the afternoon aboard, working. There was always work; a boat is a promise you make daily or not at all. Okonjo came by with caulking compound and opinions, and little Ferro, the youngest of the crew, swabbed the deck while pretending not to listen when Okonjo retold, for perhaps the fortieth time, the story of Redcliff Bend—"and this girl, this *girl*, takes the line and walks the gunwale like she was born on it"—and Sable let him tell it, because the crew had earned their version of events, and their version had more laughing in it than the truth did.

Toward evening the others went ashore, and the wharf emptied, and the heat lightning began far upriver—silent flickers over the hills where the gorge was, where the source was, where the cairn was. Sable sat on the foredeck with her father's bent compass open in her lap, watching the needle hold its gentle lie, pointing two degrees off true and perfectly content about it.

Mara came and sat beside her. Close. Closer than the temperature required.

"What will you do with it?" Sable asked. "The name. Now that it's yours again."

Mara considered. Down the wharf a lantern swung on its post, throwing their shadows long across the planks, two shadows leaning into each other like pilings against a current.

"Cargo runs," Mara said. "Honest ones. Upstream as far as Redcliff Bend, maybe farther, once I learn the water again—but different water, next time. Water I choose." She paused. "And maybe, someday, a proper survey. My own charts. Things put back on the page."

"They'll hate that."

"They'll survive it." Mara reached over and took the compass from Sable's hands—not taking it, really; holding it with her, both their fingers on the brass. "Your father's?"

"Bent," Sable said. "Points wrong. Always has."

"Good," Mara said. "We'll steer by it. Everyone else can follow the true needle straight into whatever the survey office wants them to find. We'll go two degrees off, where the real country is."

Sable turned her hand over, palm up, and Mara's fingers settled into it, and neither of them said anything for a while, because there are moments the river gives you that are wasted on words.

Then Mara said, quietly, "I should have said it before the gorge. Before any of it. Years ago, maybe, if I'd had the courage of a clerk with a pen."

"Said what?"

"You know what."

"I want to hear it anyway," Sable said. "Say it in daylight. To me."

Mara looked at her. The heat lightning flickered once, twice, far upriver, lighting the hills where her father and Sable's father lay under stones, and in that borrowed light Sable watched eleven years of invisibility end all at once, quietly, without thunder.

"I love you," Mara Lockridge said. "My name is Mara Lockridge, and I love you, and I'm done hiding either one."

The lantern swayed. The river breathed. Somewhere out in the dark the tide was turning, the whole delta exhaling toward the sea, and Sable leaned in and kissed her—there on the foredeck of the *Lena*, registered owner and listed mate, two names in a book, two degrees off true and exactly where they belonged.

When she pulled back, Mara was smiling, and Sable thought: *this is the landfall. Not the delta. Not the shore. This.*

Above the hills, the heat lightning pulsed again—steady now, rhythmic, less like a storm than like a signal. Sable frowned at it, the navigator in her waking despite everything.

"Mara," she said slowly. "Storms don't flash in rhythm."

Mara followed her gaze north, upriver, toward the gorge, toward the country they had sworn never to chart and never to forget. The light blinked again. Once. Twice. Once more.

Neither of them moved for a long moment.

"Well," Mara said at last, standing, pulling Sable up with her, her hand warm and certain around Sable's wrist. "Someone's signaling. And we're the only fools on this river who know the code."

She was already walking toward the wheelhouse, and Sable—two degrees off true, and glad of it—went with her.
