## 1. The Blacklist

There are three ways to learn you're finished in this business, and I'd managed all of them inside a year.

The first is the phone that stops ringing. That one's quiet. You check it the way you'd check a wound for infection — once a day at first, then hourly, then you just hold it in your palm like a stone you're deciding whether to skip. The second is the email from the guides' association with the word *suspended* in the subject line, which arrives formatted like a newsletter, cheerful banner and all, as if your career were a seasonal sale. The third is the moment you catch yourself pricing out work you swore you'd never do. Night security. Warehouse shifts. Anything with a lanyard.

I was on the third way when the lawyer found me.

I'd come down to the coast because the coast is where people go to be forgotten politely. My sister had a couch in a town whose name I kept forgetting on purpose, and I told myself it was temporary, the way you tell yourself a storm is temporary while you're digging out the tent. Eleven months since the accident. Ten months since the inquiry. Nine months since Soren Hale stood up in a room full of people wearing fleece vests worth more than my remaining savings and said, in that warm radio-voice of his, *"In my professional judgment, the guide's route decision was indefensible."*

Indefensible. I've thought about that word a lot. It has a nice legal ring to it. It also happens to be false, but falseness turns out not to matter much when the man saying it has three first ascents and a smile and you have a dead client and no witnesses above treeline.

So: broke. Unemployable in two countries, if you count the season I'd worked in Chamonix before the blacklist followed me across the water like a dog that had learned my name. I was drinking coffee I couldn't afford at a counter I didn't like, reading job listings for the word *outdoors* and finding only *outdoor seating*, when a man in a good coat sat down next to me without asking.

"You're Vasquez," he said.

"I'm nobody," I said. "You're thinking of someone employable."

He put a card on the counter. Heavy stock. Embossed. The kind of card that costs more per unit than I used to pay per day for food. "Merrick Aldous. I represent the estate of Adaeze Okonkwo."

The name landed somewhere under my ribs before my brain caught up. Okonkwo. Even down here, even among the job listings and the lanyards, that name carried altitude. Adaeze Okonkwo had climbed things in the northern range that other people wrote papers about. She'd run routes through glacier country that the association officially classified as *not recommended* and unofficially called *Okonkwo country*. She'd vanished from the scene twenty years ago, retired or exiled depending on who told it, and every guide of my generation had grown up on her stories the way kids grow up on saints.

"She's dead?" I asked.

"Three months ago. The estate requires certain services." He turned his coffee cup without drinking from it, a man who ordered things for appearances. "Discretion. Competence. A guide who can be hired cheaply and asks no questions."

"I'm expensive," I lied. "And I ask excellent questions."

"That's not what your file says."

"My file says a lot of things."

He almost smiled, which for a lawyer is roughly the equivalent of a standing ovation. "Here is what I need. Ms. Okonkwo's granddaughter is flying in Thursday. Lena Okonkwo. She will require an escort to Salt Hollow, a village on the coast north of here, for the reading of the will. The journey takes two days by road and boat. You will accompany her, see her delivered safely, and remain available for the duration of the proceedings. Payment upon completion. Generously."

"Why me?" I asked. "You could hire anyone with a current certification."

"Because," Merrick said, gathering his coat, "Ms. Okonkwo specified in her instructions that the escort should be someone the mountain community has given up on. Her exact words, and I quote, 'someone the valley threw away, because those are the only ones who still look up.'" He stood. "I confess I don't understand it. But the deceased paid her invoices promptly for forty years, so I've stopped requiring understanding."

He left the card. I turned it over maybe two hundred times that night on my sister's couch, listening to the radiator clank like a failing ice screw.

Here's what I told myself: it's money. Three weeks' pay for a chauffeur gig with a view. Take it, bank it, use it to appeal the suspension, get back to real work. It's not a job, it's a transaction. Guides don't escort heiresses. They climb. This is just what climbing looks like when you can't afford it anymore.

That story held together about as well as wet rope.

Because underneath the arithmetic there was the other thing, the thing with hooks in it. *Adaeze Okonkwo.* I'd read her journals in a library at nineteen, sitting cross-legged on the floor until my legs went numb, tracing her hand-drawn sigils — she marked her routes with a private shorthand, little glyphs like weather symbols crossed with prayers — and thinking, *this is what mastery looks like.* And now her lawyer was buying my silence by the week, and her granddaughter needed walking to some fishing village I'd never heard of, and the whole arrangement smelled faintly of something old and deliberate, like a route planned decades in advance by someone who knew exactly which storms would come.

People say pride is what keeps you from taking a bad job. In my experience pride is what keeps you from admitting you already took it.

I called him Wednesday morning.

* * *

Lena Okonkwo arrived the way weather arrives — announced, unwelcome, and impossible to ignore. I met her at the regional airport, holding a sign with her name on it, feeling like a fraud in both directions: too much a guide to be a driver, too ruined to be anything else.

She came through arrivals pulling one bag and carrying a box against her chest like something rescued from a fire. Late twenties, city clothes chosen for a funeral she hadn't decided how to feel about, eyes doing a fast inventory of me and clearly filing me under *problem.*

"You're the guide," she said. Not a question.

"Lior Vasquez."

"I looked you up." She let that sit between us. Whatever she'd found, she didn't share it, which meant it was bad. Everyone I'd ever been was bad; the internet had been very thorough. "My grandmother's lawyer said you'd know the way to Salt Hollow."

"There's one way. Two days. Road, then ferry."

"Fine." She shifted the box higher. Up close I could see it was old, wooden, corners worn soft, sealed with tape gone amber with age. "Do you know anything about her? About Adaeze?"

It was the first question she'd asked that wasn't logistics, and something in her voice made me answer honestly instead of professionally.

"Everyone in the range knows something about her. Nobody knows much. She left before my time and took her reasons with her."

Lena nodded slowly, looking past me toward the mountains stacked grey against the horizon, the ones she'd flown over without knowing they were hers by blood. "The box came to me instead of her lawyer. No note. Just my name on it." Her thumb moved over the lid. "I haven't opened it. I keep telling myself I'll open it in the village. Like it knows where it wants to be opened."

Superstition. Grief dressed up as instinct. I recognized both, having worn them myself for eleven months.

"We should get on the road," I said. "Weather window closes tomorrow night."

Something flickered across her face at that — the phrase *weather window* meaning nothing to her yet, though it would. She followed me to the truck, and we drove north out of the city with the box riding between us on the seat like a third passenger neither of us acknowledged.

We made the coast road by dark. At a fuel stop she bought tea she didn't drink and asked, casually, in the way people ask questions they've been rehearsing:

"The lawyer said you used to guide. What happened?"

I kept my eyes on the road. "I made a decision on a mountain. Someone died. A colleague disagreed with my account of it."

"And?"

"And his version had better posture than mine."

She was quiet for a long time. When she spoke again the rehearsal was gone from her voice. "I never met her. My grandmother. Twenty years of silence, then a letter saying there was a will, then a box with my name on it. I don't even know why she picked me. I have cousins. I have a father she never spoke to. For all I know I'm inheriting a debt."

"Could be," I said. "Estates are usually debts."

"You're comforting."

"I'm honest. It's cheaper."

She almost laughed — I heard the shape of it, aborted — and turned to watch the dark water flashing past beyond the guardrail. Somewhere out there was Salt Hollow, and whatever Adaeze Okonkwo had left behind in it, and the box on the seat seemed to lean toward it the way water leans downhill.

I told myself again, driving through the dark with a stranger and a legend's belongings: *it's just money.* Three weeks. Bank it, appeal it, go back to the life I was supposed to have.

But when we finally rolled into Salt Hollow near midnight — a scatter of roofs around a black harbour, gulls arguing in their sleep, one streetlight burning like a signal fire nobody claimed — I felt it again, that hook set deep and old. Because painted on the harbour wall, faded but stubborn, in a hand I recognized from a library floor twenty years ago, was a single climbing sigil.

Adaeze Okonkwo had marked this place the way she marked her routes.

And I had a sudden, cold certainty that I hadn't been hired to deliver an heiress.

I'd been hired to deliver her to a trailhead.
