## 2. Salt Hollow

The bus dropped me at the end of the road because there was no more road, which felt less like geography and more like a metaphor I hadn't consented to.

I stood with my suitcase on a gravel shoulder while the driver gave me a look that said *good luck* in the universal language of people who are glad it isn't them. Then he was gone, and Salt Hollow arranged itself around me: twenty-odd houses the color of storm clouds, a harbor full of boats that had clearly stopped trying, and a church steeple leaning at an angle that suggested even God had given up on plumb lines here.

My grandmother had lived here for forty years. I had learned this three weeks ago, from a lawyer's letter, along with everything else I knew about Adaeze Okonkwo — namely that she existed, that she was dead, and that she had left me something called "the source," which sounded less like an inheritance and more like a riddle written by someone who hated me.

Which, to be fair, she may have. We'd never met.

"Are you getting off or just admiring the view?" The voice came from a woman stacking crab traps by the seawall. She was sixty-ish, built like driftwood, and looking at me the way you'd look at weather you didn't trust.

"I'm Lena Okonkwo."

The crab traps went down. Not dramatically — she set them down carefully, one by one, like she was buying time to decide what to do with her face. What she decided on was nothing.

"So you're the granddaughter." She said *granddaughter* the way other people say *tax audit*. "Ruth Okonkwo's girl's child."

"You knew my mother?"

"Knew of her. Left when she was nineteen and never wrote back, same as her mother before her taught her." The woman picked up another trap. "I'm Marta Voss. Your grandmother bought diesel from my husband for thirty years. Never missed a payment. Never stayed for coffee either."

"That sounds... efficient."

"It sounds like Okonkwos." She nodded toward the village. "Lawyer's office is the blue house past the chandlery. He'll be waiting. They're always waiting, lawyers. It's the only thing they do quickly."

I thanked her. She did not acknowledge it, which I was beginning to understand was the local dialect.

* * *

The lawyer's name was Emil Brandt, and his office smelled of pipe smoke and damp paper, which is apparently what justice smells like in coastal Norway-adjacent nowhere. He was small, precise, and wore the expression of a man who had read this will aloud before and knew exactly how it landed.

"Miss Okonkwo. Thank you for coming. I know it's a long way."

"I got your letter. It wasn't clear why I needed to come *here* to be told I'd inherited nothing."

He didn't flinch. Points for professionalism. "You've read the summary, then."

"'The source, if she can find it.'" I quoted. "That's not an inheritance, Mr. Brandt. That's a scavenger hunt. My grandmother died owning a house, a boat, presumably some money — and instead of any of that, I get a treasure map with no X?"

Brandt folded his hands. "Your grandmother owned very little, actually. The house is leased back to the village cooperative. Her savings went to the mountain rescue fund years ago. What remained — what she specifically, deliberately reserved for you — is the source."

"The source of *what*?"

"That is rather the question, isn't it." He produced a document, old paper gone soft at the edges. "Her exact wording, witnessed twice: 'To my granddaughter Lena, whom I have never met through no fault of my own, I leave the source, if she can find it. If she cannot find it, it was never hers, and it will keep better than she would have.'"

I read it twice. It didn't improve. "'Through no fault of my own' — whose fault does she think it was? Mine? I was four when my mother cut contact. I didn't even know her name until your letter."

"She believed," Brandt said carefully, "that silence runs downhill. That children inherit their parents' silences whether they want to or not." He shrugged, a small apologetic motion. "She was not a warm woman. But she was a precise one. Everything in that will means exactly what it says."

"And what does 'the source' mean?"

"If I knew, I'd have told you. She never told me. In thirty years as her solicitor, she told me many things — where the roof leaked, who owed her money, which of the village men had been lying about the fish counts since 1987. Never once what the source was." He leaned forward. "But I'll tell you what I observed. Twice a year, sometimes three, she would disappear for days. Upriver. Alone. And when she came back, she'd sit in that chair—" he pointed at a leather armchair held together with duct tape and stubbornness, "—and she'd write letters she never sent. A whole drawer of them. Addressed to you."

The room tilted slightly. "Letters to me?"

"Unsent. Unopened. Legally they form part of the estate, but she sealed the drawer with instructions: they go to whoever finds the source. Not before." His eyes were kind and tired. "I'm sorry, Miss Okonkwo. I know how this sounds. But she wanted you to come find her, not receive her. There's a difference, and she thought it mattered."

"It matters to dead people," I said. "The living just get the bill."

* * *

I found the only place serving food that wasn't attached to a fuel pump around seven, and by eight I'd been thoroughly informed of my family's crimes.

It wasn't subtle. The pub — called The Anchor, though the anchor on the sign had rusted into an abstract shape best described as *despair* — went quiet in stages when I walked in, like dominoes falling in slow motion. Conversations didn't stop so much as hold their breath. I ordered fish stew from a barman who took my money without touching my hand, and I ate it at the corner table under the combined weight of forty years of grievance.

It arrived via delegation. A big man, fishing-boat shoulders, face like a cliff that had been arguing with the sea and losing slowly.

"You'll be the Okonkwo girl." He sat down uninvited. "Tor Halvorsen. My father fished beside your grandmother before your mother was born."

"Congratulations?"

His jaw worked. "You think this is funny."

"I think I've been in your village for six hours and I already owe emotional damages to a woman selling crabs. So no. I don't think anything is funny."

Something flickered behind his eyes — maybe surprise, maybe respect, hard to say. "Your grandmother kept this town alive. Bad winters, bad catches, the co-op nearly folding twice — Adaeze Okonkwo carried us. Money, routes up the glacier, knowledge nobody else had. And then she gets old, and we ask her, plain out, who takes over when she's gone. Who keeps the source." He tapped the table. "And she says: my granddaughter. Across the ocean. A girl none of us has ever seen, who's never seen this place, who doesn't know a cleat from a cleaver. That's who she chose. Over every person in this room who bled for her."

"I didn't ask to be chosen."

"No. You just showed up to collect." He stood. "That's the Okonkwo way, seems like. Take the inheritance, skip the living."

"Your father bled for her?" I asked. "Then you tell me — what did she actually leave this town? Because according to her lawyer, she left it everything except whatever the source is. House, money, all of it, signed away years ago. Whatever I'm inheriting, Tor, it isn't the part that kept you alive. So maybe aim your resentment somewhere with a pulse."

He stared at me for a long moment. Then he laughed — one short, unwilling bark — and shook his head. "Marta said you had the mouth."

"Marta also implied my whole family line is emotionally constipated, so I'm not sure her reviews are reliable."

He left. The stew was actually good. Small mercies.

* * *

The lawyer had arranged a room above the chandlery, which meant I slept in a bed that smelled of rope and listened to the tide go out beneath the floorboards like something enormous exhaling. Around two in the morning I gave up on sleep and stood at the window watching the bay.

That's when I noticed the water was wrong.

Not wrong, exactly. Just — leaving. The harbor shallows were retreating in long silver sheets, exposing mudflats I was fairly sure hadn't existed at dinner. Boats settled onto their keels with groans. And far out, where the headland met the sea, a dark shape was emerging from the waterline — a black seam in the cliff, growing wider as the tide fell.

A cave. The tide was going out far enough to show a cave.

I should have gone back to bed. Instead I pulled on my coat and walked out into the cold, because apparently I was the kind of person who inherited riddles, and riddles, I was learning, don't wait for business hours.

The flats were treacherous — ankle-deep silt over stone, smelling of iodine and old kelp — but the moon was generous, and I picked my way toward the headland until the cave mouth loomed above me, tall as a church door, breathing cold air.

I didn't go in. I want that on the record. I stood at the threshold like a sensible person, shivering, telling myself this was a tomorrow problem.

But the moonlight caught something inside, low on the rock wall. Marks. Rows of them, carved deliberate and deep, and even without knowing what they meant, I knew they meant something — they had the grammar of intention, of someone leaving a message for a reader they trusted to come.

And below them, half-buried in silt, something glinted. Metal. A box, maybe. Or a tin.

Behind me, gravel crunched.

I turned. A man stood at the edge of the flats — tall, weathered, carrying himself with the wary economy of someone who'd spent his life on mountains, not beaches. He looked at the cave, then at me, and his expression did something complicated.

"You're the granddaughter," he said. Not a question. Word travels fast in towns with one road.

"And you are?"

"Lior Vasquez." He was already moving toward me, eyes on the cave, and there was something in his voice I recognized from the lawyer's office — the sound of a person realizing a riddle had just gotten teeth. "We need to talk about what your grandmother left in there. Before the tide comes back."
