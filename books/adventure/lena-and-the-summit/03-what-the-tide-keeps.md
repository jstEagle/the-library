## 3. What the Tide Keeps

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

I'd seen the notice pinned to the harbormaster's door — *Spring tide, minus four point one meters, lowest in a decade* — and I'd thought, fine, mudflats, a few stranded crabs, tourists taking pictures of nothing. What I hadn't expected was for the bay itself to pull back its skirt and show us what it had been hiding under there all along.

Lena saw it first. She was standing at the rail of the seawall with her coffee going cold, the way she did every morning since the will reading, staring out at the water like it owed her an explanation. Then she went very still, and said, "Lior," in a voice that made me put down my mug before I dropped it.

"The rocks," she said. "There are marks on the rocks."

There were. Where the sea had been for as long as anyone in Salt Hollow could remember, black basalt now stood dripping and gleaming in the gray morning light, and cut into the stone — no, not cut. *Chiseled.* Deliberate. A line of symbols running from the tideline up toward a dark mouth in the cliff face that none of the village maps showed.

I knew those symbols.

I want to be clear about something: I don't believe in signs. I believe in weather, in rope, in load ratings, in the difference between a forecast and a hope. Ten years of guiding will beat the mysticism out of anyone. But when I looked at those sigils — three chevrons stacked over a broken circle, with a slash through the arc — my hands went cold inside my gloves, because I had spent two weeks of my life reading that exact mark in a dead woman's climbing journals, cross-referencing route descriptions by lamplight while Lena slept badly in the next room.

Maren Okonkwo had signed her routes the way other climbers sign summit registers. And she had signed this one into living rock, below the sea.

"That's not possible," Lena said beside me. Not a question. A flat statement of someone renegotiating their relationship with reality.

"Nothing about your grandmother has been possible so far," I said. "Why start now?"

We climbed down to the flats. The mud sucked at our boots, treacherous in the way only freshly exposed seabed can be — it looks solid and lies about it. I tested every step the way you test snow on a slope, weight back, listening. Old habits. The ones that kept me alive and got me called paranoid by people who'd never buried a client.

The cave mouth was maybe eight feet high, half-hidden behind a shoulder of rock that must have shielded it from all but the most violent storms. As we got closer I could see the sigils continued inside, chiseled in a hand that grew more confident with each mark, the way handwriting does when someone stops being afraid of the medium.

A lantern hung on a rusted iron bracket just inside. Empty, obviously. But the bracket was sound. Someone had meant to come back.

"Lior." Lena's voice had gone strange. "How long does the tide stay out?"

I checked my watch, then the almanac figures I'd memorized out of professional habit. "Slack's maybe ninety minutes. Then it turns. When it comes back through a gap this size—" I didn't finish. She got it. People always get it; they just don't always believe it fast enough.

"Then we go in," she said, "and we're quick about it."

* * *

Inside, the cave opened wider than the entrance promised, a throat of stone going back perhaps forty feet, dry above the high-water line, the air smelling of salt and old iron and something faintly organic — kelp, sealed-in time. My headlamp found the walls and I stopped walking.

Sigils everywhere. Dozens of them. Dates beside some. Route names in Maren Okonkwo's tight, angular script: *Gullwing Arete, 1974. The Drowned Stair, 1979. First Ascent — Solo — do not tell Tomas.* Whoever Tomas was, he'd apparently been told anyway, because beneath that one, in different ink, someone had written: *You told me anyway.*

Lena made a sound low in her throat. "That's her handwriting. That's really her."

"And that's not." I was looking at a coil of rope hanging from a wooden peg, and everything in me that had ever touched cordage was screaming. I lifted it down. Hemp, old-generation lay, stiff but not rotted — someone had maintained it, waxed it, coiled it properly. This wasn't abandoned gear. This was *stored* gear. Cared-for gear.

"There's a box," Lena said.

It sat on a natural shelf of stone, a tin biscuit box gone to rust-bloom at the corners, weighted under a flat rock against — what? Curiosity. Storm surge. Time itself. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that crackled when I unfolded it: a notebook, a folded paper map, and a small brass compass whose needle still swung true, which felt like a small miracle or a small joke.

The notebook was Maren's. I recognized the script from the journals the lawyer had given Lena — the ones nobody else in Salt Hollow seemed to know existed, or if they knew, they weren't saying. But these entries were older. Younger handwriting. And the last third of the book changed character entirely: less poetry, more arithmetic. Distances. Times. Water levels. A woman planning something with the precision of an engineer and the secrecy of a smuggler.

"She used the tides," I murmured, half to myself. "Look — she logged the spring tides for years. Every decade-level low. She was waiting for one."

"For what?"

"For this." I unfolded the map.

It was drawn on tracing paper over a survey base, and it followed a river — the Hollow River, the same gray-green water that ran past the village and emptied into this bay — upstream. Way upstream. Past the last marked trailhead. Past the gorge where the surveyor's ink simply gave up and wrote *UNSURVEYED — GLACIER COUNTRY — ACCESS PROHIBITED BY ORDER.*

And then into the cirque.

I knew the cirque. Not from maps. From the worst week of my life.

Here is the thing I hadn't told Lena yet, the thing I'd been carrying like a stone in my boot since Chapter One of this whole disaster: the route on that map, the river gorge below the glacier, the ice road along the moraine — I had been on parts of it. Three years ago. With Daniel Reyes, my client, a good man who wanted to see the source of the Hollow before the calving season closed access, and who died on the descent when a serac let go above the exit gully and dropped a house-sized piece of glacier onto the route.

Soren Hale testified that I'd taken them onto the route outside the safe window. That I'd ignored posted closures. That my judgment was compromised. The association believed him. The blacklist believed him. Daniel's family, God help them, believed him, because grief needs somewhere to go and a guide is easier to blame than a mountain.

What I never understood — what kept me up nights for three years — was why Soren had been on that route at all that day, close enough to see us, close enough to testify with such confidence. He told the inquiry he'd been scouting for a future expedition. Scouting alone. In prohibited country. During calving season.

Nobody asked the question hard enough. I was too wrecked to ask it. And then the door closed.

Now I stood in a sea cave holding a dead woman's map, and the route on it matched the route in my nightmares stroke for stroke — except hers was annotated. *Ice bridge stable until late June in cold years. Serac field above east gully — WATCH IT. S.H. has been cutting the warning ropes on the lower crossing. Ask him why.*

*S.H.*

Soren Hale.

The cave got very quiet around me, or maybe that was just my own blood. Lena was watching my face, and whatever she saw there made her set down the compass carefully, like it might go off.

"Lior. What is it?"

"Your grandmother," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved, "was documenting Soren Hale. Years ago. Safety records, route conditions, maybe worse. And she knew about the lower crossing. She knew things about that man that—" I stopped. Recalibrated. "The inheritance isn't land, Lena. I think the source is a place, yes. But I think what she left you is proof of something."

"Proof of what?"

"I don't know yet. But I know where it ends." I tapped the map, at the little penciled X in the forbidden cirque, at the notation beside it in Maren's hand: *Final camp. Everything here. Come prepared to read.*

The tide had begun to turn. I could hear it — a soft, insistent whisper far out on the flats, the sea remembering its claim. Ninety minutes, I'd said. It would be less than that now. Somewhere beyond the cave mouth, water was already sliding back over ground that hadn't been wet in ten years, patient as anything that knows it always wins.

"We need to move," I said. "Now."

Lena didn't argue. She'd learned that much about mountains, and about me. But at the cave mouth she stopped, one hand braced on the chiseled sigil of the broken circle, and looked back into the dark.

"She wanted someone to find this," she said. "On exactly this day. She planned for a tide ten years out. Lior — how do you plan like that?"

I didn't have an answer. I had something better and worse: a map to the place that ruined my life, a dead legend's rope in my pack, and the growing, sickening certainty that the route ahead and the route behind were the same road, and Soren Hale was already on it.

Behind us, the sea came home.
