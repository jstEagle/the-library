## 4. Rivals on the Ice Road

The trouble with small towns, I decided on my third morning in Salt Hollow, is that they have the memory of elephants and the discretion of sieves.

I'd been in the village four days total. In that time I had learned exactly three things: that the fish stew at the Anchorline was better than it had any right to be, that the woman who ran the chandlery would serve me but never smile at me, and that my grandmother's name was a door slammed in my face rather than an invitation through one.

And then, on the fourth morning, everyone knew about the cave.

I understood how these things worked — I'd grown up around money and its attendant leaks — but I hadn't expected the speed of it. By seven a.m., Mrs. Aldous at the bakery was giving me a look I couldn't parse. By nine, two men in matching technical jackets I'd never seen before were photographing the harbor wall as though it might testify later. By ten, a lawyer named Prewitt — not *my* lawyer, not the dead woman's lawyer, but a third kind entirely, the kind with good shoes and no local accent — was waiting outside my rented room with a document wallet under his arm like a communion Bible.

"Lena Okonkwo." He said it warmly, the way you'd greet someone you'd already won. "Soren Hale sends his regards."

"I don't know who that is."

"You will." He handed me a card. Heavy stock, matte black, a single mountain silhouette embossed in silver. Hale Expeditions. The kind of card designed to make you feel poor holding it. "Mr. Hale has filed a competing claim to the Okonkwo estate. He'll be arriving within the week. In the meantime, he wanted me to convey his sympathies for your loss, and his hope that we can resolve this matter amicably."

"Amicably," I repeated.

"There's also this." From the wallet came a second envelope, thinner, and he held it out with the delicacy of a man passing contraband. "A gesture of goodwill. Mr. Hale understands the village has been through hard years. He'd hate to see anyone here struggle unnecessarily."

Inside was a check. I won't tell you the number, partly because I've decided some things stay private, and mostly because the number made my hands go cold. It was made out not to me but to the Salt Hollow Fishermen's Cooperative — new roof for their drying shed, engine repairs, a season's fuel. It was, in other words, a check designed to be impossible for a dying town to refuse, and it was signed by a man whose name meant nothing to me and everything, apparently, to everyone else.

"Why?" I asked.

Prewitt smiled with all the warmth of a tide chart. "Because Mr. Hale believes in community."

He left. I stood in the lane smelling salt and diesel and old rope, holding a stranger's bribe, and realized I had absolutely no idea what game I'd been dropped into the middle of.

* * *

By evening, the village had chosen sides, and the side they chose wasn't mine.

I heard it in fragments at the Anchorline, where I'd gone mostly to avoid eating alone in my room. The cooperative had accepted the check — of course they had; the drying shed roof had been failing since before I was born. And acceptance, I learned, comes bundled. The men at the corner table weren't even whispering. They didn't need to. I was the outsider; whispers were wasted on me.

"—filed proper papers, hasn't he—"

"—her lot left sixty years ago and never looked back—"

"—Hale's a fair man. Guided my cousin up the Kestrel route, never lost so much as a mitten—"

That last one stung worse than the rest, though I couldn't have told you why yet. I just sat with my stew going cold and listened to a town I didn't belong to hand itself over to a man I'd never met, and felt the particular loneliness of being an heir to nothing — because that's what it amounted to, wasn't it? A riddle. *The source, if she can find it.* No deed, no coordinates, no key. Just a dead woman's scavenger hunt, and now a professional competitor with lawyers and local goodwill and, apparently, a reputation for never losing so much as a mitten.

I paid and walked out into the blue dusk. The bay lay flat as hammered tin. Somewhere under that water, behind the rock shelf the locals wouldn't look at, was a cave full of my grandmother's handwriting, and half the town had already drawn a map of it from hearsay.

Lior found me on the seawall.

They had a way of appearing without seeming to arrive — one moment absent, the next simply there, sitting on the granite with their forearms on their knees, watching the water like it owed them something. Four days of shared silence had taught me their moods in outline: the flat watchfulness, the dry commentary delivered mostly to the horizon, the way they went very still when the glacier country north of the bay was mentioned. I still didn't trust them. I want to be honest about that. But they were the only person in Salt Hollow who looked at me like a person instead of a ledger entry.

"So," Lior said. "The check."

"You heard."

"The whole village heard. The whole village heard everything, always. It's their one industry besides fish." A pause. "You should know what you bought with your grandmother's cave."

"Tell me."

They turned then, and in the last light their face was hard to read — weathered, guarded, older than their years in the way of people who've been punished for something. "Soren Hale. You said you didn't know the name. So let me save you the discovery." Their voice stayed level, but I heard the effort of it, like someone carrying weight up a steep pitch. "Six years ago I guided a client on the Serac Traverse. Icefall collapse. She died. Not because of anything I did — because our route plan was falsified upstream of us. We were told the ice road above the cirque had been assessed stable. It hadn't. The assessment was forged. And the man who signed that assessment, the man who stood in front of the inquiry afterward with his beautiful voice and his beautiful reputation and swore the paperwork was sound — the man whose testimony got me blacklisted in two countries while he got a commendation — was Soren Hale."

The sea made its small indifferent sounds against the pilings.

"He's coming here," Lior said, "for the same inheritance you are. Which means whatever your grandmother found, whatever she wrote down in that cave, it isn't just land. Land doesn't scare men like Hale. *Records* scare men like Hale. Testimony. Proof. If he's spending this kind of money on a fishing village, it's because he thinks there's something in that cirque that can end him."

I thought of the rusted tin box. The sigils cut into stone, decades of them, a life written in a code only climbers could read. My grandmother hadn't left me property. She'd left me a position, and I hadn't even known I was standing in one.

"And the village took his money," I said.

"The village took his money," Lior agreed, without judgment, which somehow made it worse. "Roofs are real. His version of events is real too, as far as they know. You can't blame people for believing the story that comes with a check attached."

"But you can blame them for selling me out before they even asked what I—" I stopped. Breathed. This was the part I'd been chewing since the seawall, the thing I'd sworn I wouldn't say because saying it made me vulnerable, and vulnerability in Salt Hollow was apparently currency you could get robbed with. But Lior was watching me with those patient, unsentimental eyes, and I found the words coming out anyway.

"When I got here, I hated this place. On principle. My mother hated it, my grandmother supposedly abandoned it, and I arrived ready to sign some papers, collect whatever this 'source' was, and leave. That was the plan. Sell it, probably. Whatever it is." I looked out at the darkening water. "But today a man offered this town thirty pieces of silver to bury my family's name, and the town said thank you, and I discovered that I'm furious about it. Not scared. Furious. Because it's *mine* they're burying. Mine and hers. And I never even met her."

Lior said nothing for a long moment. Out on the water, a boat light swung at anchor.

"So here's my problem," I went on, and my voice steadied as I said it, which surprised me. "The will says I inherit the source if I can find it. Find it. Not buy it, not litigate it — find it. Hale can file all the claims he wants, but he can't file a claim on a route nobody's walked in twenty years, through a river under a glacier, in a window that closes when the calving season starts. That's not a courtroom. That's a mountain. And mountains don't take briefs."

"They take blood," Lior said quietly. "Sometimes."

"Then I need the best guide I can get." I turned to face them fully. "And the best guide in this bay is sitting next to me, unemployable through no fault of their own, being paid — I checked — a fraction of what a man with a commendation commands. So I'm asking. Officially. Take me upriver. Up the gorge, past the ice road, to the source, before the season turns. I know what it costs you. I know what happened to your last client. I'm asking anyway."

The silence stretched. I watched Lior's jaw work, watched six years of ruin and grief and blacklisted pride pass behind their eyes like weather. Every rule they lived by — every rule the association had left them — said refuse. Say no to the grieving heiress with the death wish and the rival on her tail. Stay small. Stay safe. Stay ruined but alive.

When they finally spoke, their voice was rough.

"Hale will have guides on the river within two days. Maybe less. He'll move fast because he thinks we're slow, and he'll move quiet because he thinks we're stupid, and both of those assumptions are going to cost him." They stood, and something had changed in the set of their shoulders — not confidence exactly, but decision, which is sometimes the same thing wearing different clothes. "We leave tomorrow, first light, before the tide finishes turning. Pack for cold. Tell no one — and I mean no one, Lena. Half this village would sell us for less than a roof."

"And if we're too late? If the window closes?"

Lior looked north, where the glacier country stood invisible beyond the headlands, white and vast and keeping its secrets.

"Then we'll be the ones who tried when no one else dared," they said. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we go upriver."

Above us, the first stars were out over the water, and somewhere beneath the bay, in a cave carved with a dead woman's handwriting, the map waited — and so, I suspected, did the truth that could burn a legend down.
