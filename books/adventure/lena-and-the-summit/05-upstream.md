## 5. Upstream

**Lior**

The gorge didn't want us in it. That was the first thing I understood about the river route, and I've been paid my whole life to understand what mountains want.

We'd left Salt Hollow before dawn, moving like thieves through our own expedition — which, legally speaking, we were. The lawyer's letter said *the source, if she can find it*, and Soren Hale's lawyers said everything else, loudly, to everyone who'd listen. So we took the fisherman's track along the water instead of the ice road, because the ice road had eyes on it, and because the river was the grandmother's way in. Her sigils started appearing by midmorning: three chevrons cut into the schist above the high-water line, old enough that lichen had begun to forgive them.

"Is that a trail marker or a warning?" Lena asked, pointing at a chevron half-swallowed by orange crust.

"With her? Both." I checked the sky while I said it. Clouds were stacking over the divide like plates in a sink nobody intended to wash. "She marked the safe lines so people could follow them, and she marked them where you'd only see them if you were already committed."

"That sounds like her," Lena said, and there it was again — that little hitch of ownership in her voice, the claim she hadn't earned yet and knew it. She'd been doing that for two days. Quoting a woman she'd met four times before the age of ten, from letters she wouldn't show me.

I let it go. Hypocrites can't afford to audit each other.

The gorge narrowed through the afternoon until the walls pressed close enough that we walked in the river's cold breath, spray slicking the rock, the water below us running green with glacier flour. Beautiful country. Country that had killed better people than me. Every hour I did the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing: distance to the cirque, days left before calving season opened the glacier's mouth, hours of daylight, and now, subtracting steadily, the weather.

By four o'clock the light went the color of dishwater and the wind came down the gorge ahead of the rain like a landlord serving notice.

"We're stopping," I said.

"There's a chevron right there." Lena pointed upstream. "That means go."

"It means *go if you can*. It also means the woman who carved it got caught out here at least once, because look—" I scrambled up a ledge to a shallow overhang, dry-ish, floored with gravel, big enough for two people who weren't planning to enjoy each other's company. "Somebody built a wind wall here. See the fitted stones? That's not geology. That's patience."

Lena stood in the rising wind looking at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the arithmetic of pride versus survival, running long division. Then a fat drop of rain hit her square between the eyes, and nature cast the deciding vote.

We built the wall higher, rigged the tarp, ate cold food, and listened to the storm arrive like it owned the deed.

* * *

**Lena**

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it gets bored. Two days into the gorge, my grandmother had stopped being a wound and started being a riddle I was failing, and I hated her a little for it. Four meetings in my whole childhood. A woman who signed birthday cards with climbing sigils instead of love. And now I was freezing in a hole in a rock wall, following her carvings toward an inheritance that might be a view, while a man I'd known for a week watched me shiver with professional sympathy and personal suspicion.

He thought I was hiding something. He was right. Just not what he thought.

The storm hit full dark around eight. Lightning came down the gorge in silent white flashes, thunder arriving late like bad news always does, and the river rose audibly — a deepening roar, the sound of the mountain clearing its throat.

"You should sleep," Lior said. "I'll take first watch."

"I'm not tired."

"You're shaking."

"I'm *cold*. It's different." It wasn't different. My hands had gone past shaking into something finer, a vibration, like I'd become a tuning fork for fear. I pulled my knees up under the tarp and stared at the lightning and decided, in the way you decide things at altitude when your blood sugar is low and your defenses are lower, to spend some honesty.

"My mother told me Edith Okonkwo died on a mountain because she loved it more than her family," I said. "That was the whole inheritance, growing up. One sentence. I built a person out of it. Cold, selfish, brilliant, gone. And then I get here and there are sigils on every cliff and a village that worships her and a tin box with a map in it, and I realize I don't know anything. I never knew her at all. I invented a stranger and spent twenty years resenting her."

Lior was quiet long enough that the storm filled the silence twice over.

"And the letters?" they said finally. "In the tin box. You read them before you showed me the map."

Not a question. Damn this person and their observational competence.

"There were letters," I admitted. "To me. Dated across — I don't know, decades. She wrote to a child who never wrote back. Because my mother never gave them to me, or maybe never told her she'd been born, I don't even know that part." My voice cracked on the last word and I hated that too. "I've been reading one per night. Like rationing. I haven't shown you because they're the only thing I have of her, and everyone in this story wants something from her — the village, Hale, the lawyers, you—"

"Me?"

"You want redemption. Don't pretend the map is incidental to that."

Another silence. But a different kind — I'd felt it once before, when I'd said something true to a stranger on a night train and watched their face change. The silence of a lock turning.

"Fair," Lior said. "That's fair. Then here's mine."

And they told me about the client.

* * *

**Lior**

Her name was Marta Reyes. Thirty-four. Software architect, first big mountain, smile like she'd already summited. I tell the story in facts when I tell it at all, because facts are load-bearing and feelings are decoration, and the structure has to hold.

Soren Hale and I were running adjacent ropes on the same peak, his outfit and mine sharing a permit — his idea, his contract, his paperwork. On day three, above the seracs, his rope team crossed a slope he should have assessed and didn't, and the report he filed afterward said the hazard briefing had been delivered to all parties. It hadn't. I never saw it. When the slope released — small slide, nothing dramatic, just enough — Marta was on the wrong side of the debris because she'd been positioned according to Hale's route plan, which put clients in the runout zone to save forty minutes.

Forty minutes. I've done that math every day for six years. Forty minutes of schedule convenience bought with a thirty-four-year-old woman who liked to photograph marmots.

The investigation found ambiguity. Ambiguity is what investigations find when one guide has a lawyer and a reputation and the other has neither. Hale testified that I'd been briefed. I testified I hadn't. They believed the man with the clean record, and my record stopped being clean, and that was that. Blacklisted in two countries. You can appeal; appeals cost money; I had exactly none.

"So you've been telling people it wasn't your fault," Lena said slowly, "and it wasn't your fault, and nobody believed you."

"The worst part isn't that they didn't believe me." I watched the lightning stitch the far wall of the gorge. "The worst part is that for about a year, I almost didn't believe me. That's what a blacklist does. It doesn't just close doors, it makes you stand outside them wondering if you deserved it."

"And the grandmother's papers—"

"Prove the briefing never happened. Prove the route plan put clients in the runout. If those records are real, they're not just my name back. They're the reason Marta died, written in his own hand." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the way it goes when the alternative is breaking. "So yes. I want something from her too. You called it."

Lena was quiet. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a small, wrecked sound.

"Look at us. Trading confessions in a storm like it's currency."

"It is currency. It's the only thing I've ever trusted anybody to pay in."

* * *

**Lena**

We slept in shifts after that, and the shift thing was mostly a fiction — I think we both lay awake most of the night, newly unburdened and therefore heavier, the way truth works. Somewhere around two in the morning the storm broke apart and the clouds tore open, and I crawled out from under the tarp to look.

The gorge at night, washed clean, under a sky salted with stars. The river had dropped already, muttering instead of roaring. And high above us, catching starlight, the glacier came down off the divide like a frozen waterfall — the source country, the forbidden cirque, one day's hard travel away.

Edith had looked at this. Had climbed toward it, year after year, writing letters to a granddaughter she'd chosen over the family that cut her off. Not cold. *Patient.* There's a difference, and I'd spent my whole life confusing the two, probably because I was raised by people who confused them too.

Lior came and stood beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. Neither of us moved away.

"One more day," they said. "If the weather holds and the ice holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we find out what kind of guides we actually are."

I was still turning that over — the *we*, casual as a coin, the first time either of us had used it — when Lior went very still beside me and lifted a hand toward the glacier's base.

Light. Down in the black throat of the gorge below us, where we'd come from. Two lights, three, moving fast against the current of the river. Headlamps, traveling at night, traveling hard.

Nobody travels that gorge at night unless they're chasing something.

"Soren," Lior breathed, and the word came out with no air behind it, just shape.

Above us, faint as a held breath, the glacier groaned — a long, deep sound, like something enormous shifting its weight. Earlier than it should. Weeks earlier than it should.

The window was closing, and someone was coming through it after us.
