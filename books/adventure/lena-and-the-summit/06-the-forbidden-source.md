## 6. The Forbidden Source

The river told us we were close before anything else did.

For two days it had been a grey-green argument between canyon walls, loud and sullen, but that morning it went quiet — the way water does when it's about to do something dramatic. Lior's rule of rivers: silence is never peace. Silence is a held breath.

Lena noticed it too. She'd stopped talking somewhere below the ice bridge, which was unlike her; she'd been narrating her grandmother's journal entries aloud for hours, doing voices, giving old Adaeze Okonkwo a wry delivery that sounded suspiciously like Lena herself. Now she just walked with her head tipped, listening.

"It stops," she said. "The sound."

"It doesn't stop. It goes under." I pointed upstream to where the glacier's toe came down out of the cirque like a fist closing over the valley mouth. The river ran straight into blue shadow beneath it and didn't come out the other side. "Subglacial channel. Your grandmother's map calls it the Throat."

"Cheerful name."

"She climbed things named worse." I checked my watch, then the sky, then the glacier — in that order, because the first two were lies I could afford and the third wasn't. Calving season was supposed to be nine days out. The seracs above the cirque lip had other opinions; I'd been watching them shed crumbs all morning like a dog shaking off rain. "We go through the Throat now, while the water's low, or we don't go at all."

"And if it rises while we're inside?"

"Then your grandmother's route becomes a very exclusive tomb, and I become a cautionary tale twice over."

She looked at me. She'd gotten good at looking at me — the flat, measuring stare of someone who'd learned not to take comfort on credit. "You'd still go," she said. Not a question.

"I'd still go," I admitted. "That's not bravery. That's the disease."

* * *

The Throat was exactly as bad as advertised and less bad than feared, which on my personal scale of routes makes it a four-star classic. We went in single file through a corridor of ice the width of a bus, headlamps carving blue from black, the river chuckling along our ankles where it had braided shallow across the floor. Overhead the ceiling arched away into darkness shot through with daylight leaking down from crevasses far above, so that we walked through falling columns of light like people crossing the nave of some drowned cathedral.

Lena kept stopping to touch the walls. At first I thought she was steadying herself. Then I saw what she was touching: small carved marks, waist-high, spaced like fence posts. A chevron. A circle bisected by a line. Three dots in a triangle.

"The sigils," she said quietly. "From the cave. From the journals."

"She carved them going in," I said. "So anyone following would know they were on her line — and so she could count her way back out by feel, in the dark, if her lamp died." I ran my glove over one. Twenty years old at least, maybe thirty, the edges softened by meltwater. It's a strange thing, putting your hand where a dead woman put hers. You expect the stone to be warm. It never is.

Halfway through, the ceiling dipped and the light died entirely, and for forty minutes there was nothing in the world but the sound of water and our own breathing and the tick of Lena's ice axe against rock every few steps — her counting marks, I realized. She'd learned the system without me teaching it. Okonkwo blood, doing what Okonkwo blood did.

Then the corridor turned, rose, and opened, and we stepped out of the glacier's throat into the cirque.

I have stood on summits on three continents. I have watched dawn come up over the Karakoram from a height where the curvature of the earth is visible to the naked eye. None of it prepared me for the simple domestic shock of what lay in that hidden valley: a bowl of green, actual green, ringed by walls of ice a thousand meters high, with a stream running through it and — on a bench of dry ground above the stream — a tent.

Not a ruin. Not a tarp skeleton. A proper pyramid tent, canvas, weathered silver-grey, guyed out with lines that had been retensioned recently enough that my hands remembered how before my brain caught up. A cairn beside it. A stove. A stack of supply drums stenciled with faded letters: S.H. OUTFITTERS.

Salt Hollow Outfitters. Her outfit. The one the village blamed the Okonkwo family for abandoning.

"She lived here," Lena said. Her voice had gone strange. "At the end. She didn't abandon this place. She *stayed*."

The grief coming off her was so raw I did the only thing I know how to do with other people's grief, which is point it at work. "Tent first. Check the anchors. Then we see what she left."

* * *

Inside the tent, everything was ordered the way a certain generation of guide orders things: rope coiled and hung, tools laid on a groundsheet in silhouette order, a tin first-aid kit with the cross repainted so many times it had gone from red to something like rust. And on the camp table, squared to its edges as if awaiting inspection, three objects.

A ledger, bound in oilcloth.

A bundle of letters, tied with waxed cord, addressed in a strong slanted hand: *For Lena Okonkwo. For when she comes.*

And a photograph, weighted under a stone: a younger woman on a glacier, arm around a man who was laughing at whatever she'd just said. On the back, in the same hand: *Adaeze & Tomas, the Cirque, the year everything was still possible.*

"Tomas," I said, and my mouth had gone dry. "Your grandmother's partner. The one who died on the descent that made her legend."

Lena nodded slowly. "The story the village tells is that she pushed too hard. That she got him killed chasing a first ascent." She looked at the laughing man in the photo. "Nobody tells it like *this*."

I untied the letters and handed them to her — they were hers, that was clear as gravity — and took the ledger myself, because ledgers are my native language. Guides keep records the way priests keep confession: privately, exhaustively, and with an eye toward the day someone reads them.

Adaeze Okonkwo's ledger was not a route log. Or it was that, but underneath the route notes, page after page, year after year, was something else. Client lists. Trip rosters. Dates. And next to certain dates, in red pencil, small furious annotations:

*Hale party, 6 clients, 2 guides listed, 1 guide present. Where is the second?*

*S. Hale certifies rope team of 3 on the North Couloir. Photos show unroped. Who signed the safety filing?*

*He is selling certifications he doesn't run and insurance he doesn't carry. Someone will die. Told him to his face. He smiled.*

I turned pages with hands that had stopped being steady somewhere around the second annotation. Because I knew the date that was coming. Every disgraced guide knows the date their life ended; mine was written in my body the way scar tissue is written in skin.

There it was. Fourteen years ago. *Vasquez party.* And beside it, in red:

*Soren Hale filed the route report for this trip. I guided his clients on the lower mountain that week — he was not on the route at all. The anchor failure on the descent was reported as "improperly placed by lead guide." I placed those anchors with Tomas two seasons prior. They held my weight in March. Someone re-bolted them. Ask who inspected the route that spring. Ask why the inspection report is dated three days AFTER the accident.*

The cirque was very quiet. Somewhere above us, the glacier ticked and settled, the sound of a thing shifting its weight.

"Lior." Lena's voice came from far away. She was holding one of the letters, and her face had changed — not broken, *hardened*, the way ore changes in a fire. "Read this one."

*My Lena,* it began. *If you are reading this, you found the source, which means you found me, which means you are braver than the family that raised you and kinder than the world that tried you. I am dying slowly and writing quickly. Here is what I have: twenty years of watching Soren Hale lie, and paper to prove it. Here is what I lack: standing. A dead woman's accusations are gossip; a living heir's are testimony. I could not make them listen. Perhaps you can. The inheritance was never land, child. It was the truth, and the truth needs a witness with a name people will hear.*

I sat down on the camp stool, which was the only graceful option left, because my legs had resigned.

Fourteen years. Fourteen years of losing contracts, losing my certification, losing friends who found it easier to believe the association's finding than to ask me directly. Fourteen years of telling myself the same sentence until it wore smooth as river stone: *it wasn't your fault, but no one will ever believe that, so stop expecting them to.* And the whole time, in a tent above a glacier, a woman I'd never met had been writing down exactly what happened, waiting for someone to come collect it.

"You knew," Lena said. Not accusing. Wondering. "When you saw the sigils in the cave. Some part of you knew this was what she was leaving."

"I knew the route," I said. "I didn't know the reason." I closed the ledger. My hands wanted to shake and I declined to permit it. "Your grandmother spent twenty years building a case. Soren Hale has spent twenty years making sure no one reads it. When word gets out we've reached the cirque—"

"He'll come," Lena said. "He won't come for us. He'll come for *this*." She put her hand flat on the letters, and I watched her arrive at the understanding that had taken me fourteen years and a ruined career to reach: that some things are worth more than the way out.

Above the cirque wall, with a crack like a rifle shot, the glacier let go of a piece of itself — early, days early, a white plume rising off the icefall and the report of it rolling around the valley walls like thunder looking for a place to land.

Calving season hadn't waited for the calendar.

Neither, I suspected, would Soren Hale.
