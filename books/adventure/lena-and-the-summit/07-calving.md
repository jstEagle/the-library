## 7. Calving

I had been awake for an hour when I heard them coming, and in that hour I learned something about grief that no therapist ever managed to teach me: it makes excellent noise discipline.

Lior was still asleep beside the cold stove, breathing in that controlled way they did even unconscious, like sleep was a skill they'd had to relearn after the accident. Outside, the cirque held its blue dawn light, and across the ice — faint but unmistakable — came the flat clank of carabiners and the murmur of voices that were trying very hard not to be a murmur at all.

Soren Hale had found us. Of course he had. Men like Soren always find you; it's the finding-out part they can't abide.

I should have woken Lior immediately. Instead I knelt there with the oilcloth bundle in my lap — twenty years of my grandmother's letters, addressed to a girl who didn't exist yet when the first one was written — and I read the top page again by the grey light coming through the tent seam.

*Lena, aged nine, if she likes horses: tell her the mule at Salt Hollow is called Bishop and he bites only liars.*

I'd never had a horse. I'd never had a grandmother, really — just a name on Christmas cards from a woman who signed herself *Nana Okonkwo* in handwriting like weather. And here she'd been, year after year, writing to me across a glacier, keeping a vigil of paper in a valley no map admitted to.

The voices outside got closer. I woke Lior with a hand on their shoulder, and credit where it's due: they went from asleep to fully operational in about four seconds, which is either impressive or deeply sad depending on how you feel about a person's relationship with rest.

"How many?" they whispered.

"At least three. Maybe four."

"Side of the cirque?"

"The far moraine. They're being quiet about it, which means Soren thinks we don't know."

"Soren," Lior said, "has never once in his life considered the possibility that someone else might be smart." They were already lacing boots. "We go now. Down the exit gully before they're in position. It's four hours to treeline if the ice behaves."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then it's a shorter trip." They said it dryly, but their eyes went to the glacier face as they said it, and stayed there a beat too long.

That's how I learned what Lior had seen and hadn't told me: the seracs above the exit route were leaning. Leaning the way old men lean into wind, the way things lean when they've decided, privately, to fall.

* * *

We broke camp fast. Or rather, Lior broke camp fast and I stood in the middle of it clutching an oilcloth bundle like a life vest, because that's what I was doing. The letters weighed maybe three pounds. They felt like a cathedral.

"You'll have to rope up," Lior said, nodding at the gully. "It's straightforward, but the runout is—" They stopped. Recalibrated. "It's fine. I'll be right behind you."

"Lior."

"What?"

"Thank you for lying badly instead of well. It's oddly comforting."

Something moved through their face that wasn't quite a smile. "Family trait, probably. Get moving."

We'd made perhaps six hundred meters toward the gully when the mountain made its opinion known.

It started as a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it — a deep, structural groan, like a ship changing its mind — and then the whole left flank of the glacier face simply *let go*. Not dramatically, not all at once, the way films show it. First a crack like a rifle shot, then a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse, and then forty thousand tons of ice committed itself to gravity with a roar that erased every other sound in the world.

The calving season had come early. The window had come early. Everything had come early except us.

"RUN," Lior screamed, and I ran.

I want to be honest about what running from a glacier actually looks like, because the movies lie. You don't sprint heroically. You flounder across hummocked ice in mountaineering boots while the air fills with a white hiss of pulverized snow, and the ground bucks under you like a deck shifting, and somewhere behind you the world ends in installments. The shockwave hit us before the debris did — a wall of displaced air that picked me up and set me down three meters from where I'd been standing, minus one glove and most of my dignity.

When the white cloud thinned, the exit gully was gone.

Not damaged. *Gone.* Where there had been a ramp of old ice sloping down toward the river gorge there was now a cliff of fresh fracture, blue and raw as a wound, dropping away into a chaos of blocks the size of houses.

"There's another way," I said. It came out as a question, which was accurate.

Lior looked at the new cliff for a long moment. "There's the descent route. Your grandmother's original line. The one that—"

"That killed her partner. Yes. I've read the letters, Lior. I know what it cost her." I heard my own voice and didn't recognize it. It sounded like hers.

Before they could answer, a voice came across the ice, amplified by the cirque walls into something godlike and awful.

"Lena! Lior Vasquez!" Soren Hale, standing on the moraine with two of his people, arms spread like a man welcoming pilgrims. "Glad you're alive! Terrible business, the route collapsing — we should talk about getting everyone down safely!"

"He wants the letters," Lior said quietly. "He's known what was up here longer than we have. Why do you think your lawyer's contract was so vague? He needed someone else to find the camp first. Someone expendable."

"I'm standing right here, you know."

"Yes," Lior said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, the way disasters do — not in slow motion, whatever the survivors later claim, but in a scramble of seconds that afterward refuse to arrange themselves in order.

Soren crossed to us alone, hands visible, voice reasonable. That was his gift, I understood then. Not climbing. Reasonableness. A man who could make anything sound like a sensible arrangement. My grandmother's journals described him the same way: *S.H. again today, all charm and arithmetic. He counts lives the way he counts anchors — as costs to be minimized.*

"Look," he said, close enough now that I could see the frost in his eyebrows, "you're both in over your heads. The route's gone. My team has the technical gear to get us all down the west flank. All I ask is a look at what the old woman left. Due diligence. If there's documentation in that bundle that touches on professional matters, it belongs with the association, not with—" he gestured at me, at all of me, "—a girl who didn't know the woman existed until last month."

And there it was. The knife inside the handshake.

"She's right, though," he added, softer, to Lior. "You especially need this handled quietly. One more irregularity on your record, Vasquez, and even the village bleeding-hearts won't touch you."

I watched Lior's jaw work. I watched twenty months of blacklist and hunger and shame pass through their face like weather. And I understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what Soren was offering: survival, in exchange for the truth staying buried under another layer of ice.

"No," I said.

"Lena—"

"They're not yours." I pulled the bundle tighter against my chest. "She wrote these to me. Twenty years of them. She documented you, Soren. She documented everything — the falsified inspections, the routes you cut corners on, the client who died on the icefall in—" I saw his face change, just slightly, and knew I'd hit bone. "You testified against Lior to bury your own record. She watched you do it and she wrote it all down and she kept it here, in the one place you were too afraid to come back to."

The glacier groaned again. Somewhere above us, another serac shifted its weight.

Soren smiled. It was the worst thing I'd ever seen, that smile — patient, unhurried, a man who had decided the arithmetic still favored him. "Then I suppose I'll need to take them."

He lunged for the bundle and everything happened at once.

I twisted away; his hand caught the oilcloth and tore it; pages burst loose into the wind like a flock of white birds. I dove for them — I want to be clear that I *chose* this, in the half-second I was given, chose the letters over balance, over the rope, over the solid ground Lior was shouting me back toward — and the ice chose that exact moment to tilt.

The world rotated. Blue sky, white ice, blue sky. I slid.

Here is what I remember of falling on a glacier: it is very quiet inside yourself. The roaring is outside. Inside there is only a strange, crystalline thought, which in my case was: *the letter from when I was twelve is going into a crevasse, and Nana will mind, Nana has minded for twenty years, Nana has been waiting.*

A rope snapped taut around my harness and the fall became a swing, a long pendulum arc across the fractured ice, and I stopped with one arm stretched out over a crevasse whose darkness had no bottom I cared to imagine. Below my dangling boot, a single page lay on a shelf of ice — the oldest one, the first letter, dated the year I was born.

*I haven't met you yet,* my grandmother had written. *But I've decided to keep talking to you anyway. Someone should.*

I reached. The ice creaked. Lior's voice came down the rope, stripped of all its dryness, just a human voice holding another human being over a hole in the world: "Lena. Lena, look at me. Take my hand, not the paper."

"I can do both."

"You can't—"

"I can do *both*," I said, and I did. I took Lior's wrist with one hand and the letter with the other, and between the two of us — between their grip and my grandmother's twenty-year patience — I came back up onto the ice with the page crumpled in my fist and my heart trying to leave through my mouth.

Above us, Soren was already retreating toward his team, empty-handed, his reasonableness finally spent. But he paused at the moraine and turned, and shouted one last thing across the widening gap:

"The season's closed, you understand! Nothing gets down this mountain before thaw — not you, not the letters, not *anyone!*"

Lior pulled me upright, checked the rope, checked my eyes, checked the letters — in that order, which I noted, and filed away somewhere permanent.

"He's wrong," Lior said. "There's one route out. Your grandmother's gully. The legend line." They looked up at it — a thread of rock and ice stitching down the cirque's east wall, thin as a signature — and I watched them confront it the way you confront a debt you've been avoiding for years.

"It killed her partner," I said.

"It did." Lior began coiling the rope with hands that almost didn't shake. "And then she climbed it alone, carrying his body-weight of guilt, and she became a legend, and then she spent twenty years writing letters to you. So." They tied off the coil and met my eyes. "We do it her way. We climb it together. And God help us, we do it *right*."

Behind us, the glacier calved again — a long, rolling thunder, the mountain clearing its throat — and the light on the east wall turned gold, as if the whole cirque had decided, at last, to show us the door.
