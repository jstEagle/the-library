## 8. The Long Way Down

The glacier finished eating our exit at 4:17 in the morning.

I know the time because I was looking at my watch when it happened — a bad habit of guides, checking time instead of facing what the time is telling you. The serac above the ice road groaned once, like an old man shifting in bed, and then two hundred tonnes of blue ice peeled off the face and took the route with it. The calving season had come early, and it had come for us specifically.

Lena stood beside me in the grey light, holding the satchel of letters against her chest like a life vest that worked on grief instead of water. Her hands were shaking. Mine weren't, which surprised me, because inside I was doing arithmetic no guide should ever have to do: one way in, zero ways out, and a man with a rifle and a grudge somewhere behind us on the moraine.

"So," she said. "The main route is gone."

"The main route is gone."

"And we're still on the wrong side of it."

"Correct."

She looked at me the way clients look at you when they've just realized the brochure left some things out. "You said there was another way."

I hadn't said that, exactly. I'd said the grandmother's journals mentioned an escape gully, and I'd said it the way you say things you hope nobody asks you to follow up on. But she was asking now, with her whole face.

"There's a gully," I said. "West wall. It's how Edith Okonkwo got her team out in '79 when the ice road collapsed under them. It's also how she lost her partner, because they were tired, and rushed, and unroped, and the gully does not forgive any of those things." I paused. "It's rated five-point-seven. Loose rock. Two thousand feet of exposure. And we don't have rope."

"We don't have—"

"Rope went into the crevasse with Soren's second climber's pack, if you'll recall, along with most of my dignity and one perfectly good stove."

Lena sat down heavily on a boulder. Behind us, the cirque held its breath — the grandmother's final camp, the tin trunk of letters, twenty years of testimony addressed to a granddaughter who'd only learned her name last week. Ahead of us, nothing but a wall of shattered granite going up into cloud.

I should tell you what I felt, since this chapter is apparently about my feelings. What I felt was the accident. Not the memory of it — the accident itself, live and current, sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone. Three years since Marcus Reyes fell off a pitch I'd been told was safe. Three years since Soren Hale stood up at the inquiry with his clean little report and his clean little tie and said *the guide assessed the anchor as sound*, and everyone believed him because believing the dead man's partner is easier than auditing a rival's paperwork. Three years of not touching a rope longer than a clothesline.

And now the mountain was asking me to do the one thing I'd sworn off: put someone else's life in my hands with nothing between them and the void except my judgment.

* * *

"You're afraid," Lena said. Not accusingly. Observationally, the way she observed everything, like a woman taking inventory of a house she'd just inherited.

"I'm a professional," I said. "Professionals prefer the term *appropriately cautious*."

"You're afraid," she repeated, gentler, "and you're going to take me down it anyway."

I looked at her. Twenty-six years old, raised in cities, had never slept above sea level until ten days ago, and she was asking to go down a legend-killer because the alternative was letting Soren Hale burn her grandmother's letters. Somewhere in the last week she'd stopped being a client and become something else — a person I'd walk into fire for, which is a terrible thing to become, professionally speaking.

"Here's what I know," I said, crouching so we were eye level. "Edith climbed this gully twice. Down once, up once, in a storm, carrying an injured teammate on her back. She wasn't stronger than us. She wasn't braver. She was slower. She made every move three times before she committed to it, and she never moved two people at once, and she never let anyone hurry her. That's the whole secret. That's what made her a legend — not boldness. Patience with teeth."

"And her partner?"

"Her partner hurried." I let that sit. "So here are the rules. One: I go first, always. You move where I move, hold where I hold, and nowhere else. Two: we test every hold like it's lying to us, because loose rock is a liar. Three: if I say freeze, you freeze even if the glacier is calving, even if Soren himself appears over the rim playing a violin. Four—" I hesitated. "Four, if I fall, you don't try to catch me. There's nothing to catch me with. You stay anchored where you are and you keep going down. Promise me."

"That's a horrible promise."

"It's the only kind worth making up here. Promise me."

She promised. Her voice cracked on it, and mine almost did too, and then we packed the letters into waterproof bags, ate the last of the food standing up because sitting down felt like surrender, and walked to the base of the gully while the light came up the color of old pewter.

* * *

The first hour was the worst hour, which is standard, and also a lie, because the fourth hour was worse, and the seventh was worse than that. That's the thing about descent: it doesn't get easier, it gets steeper, and your arms get deader, and the rock gets looser exactly in proportion to how much you need it to hold.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me tell you about the fear, because I promised myself I would be honest in this account, the way Edith was honest in hers.

For three years I'd believed the fear was about the fall. About Marcus, about the sound — I won't describe the sound; some things you keep. But halfway down the gully, wedged in a chimney with my back against warm stone and two thousand feet of morning air under my boots, I understood the fear had never been about falling. It was about being trusted. Every step of this descent, Lena did exactly what I told her. She tested the holds I tested. She froze when I froze. Once, when a block the size of a refrigerator shifted under my palm and I shouted *freeze* with genuine panic in my voice, she stopped with one foot on a ledge the width of a paperback and didn't even breathe loud until I said move.

That's trust. That's the thing I'd been hiding from since the inquiry — not ropes, not mountains, but the weight of someone believing you. Because the last time someone believed me, they died believing me.

And here was the terrible, liberating discovery of the seventh hour: the trust was holding. Not because I was brave. Because I was careful. Because I checked every hold twice and made her check it a third time and treated the whole gully like a hostile witness under oath. Edith's patience with teeth. I could feel her in the moves — her sigils were chalked faintly on the stone at each rest point, the same looping mark from the tide cave, and I started reading them like notes from a dead instructor. *Slow down.* At the next stance: *Test it twice.* Further down, half worn away: *Your partner is more important than the schedule.*

I laughed out loud at that one, alone on a ledge, and Lena called up asking what was funny, and I said, "Your grandmother is coaching me," and she said, "Good. Someone should."

* * *

We came off the last pitch at dusk, onto the scree fan below the west wall, and I have never in my life been so happy to stand on ground that was merely unstable. My forearms were cooked. My palms looked like I'd lost a fight with a cheese grater. Lena sat down where she stood, which is against all wilderness protocol, and I let her, because some rules exist to be suspended by people who've earned it.

Soren's team never showed. Later we'd learn why — the calving had cut them off too, and Soren had turned back rather than risk the gully, which told me everything I needed to know about the difference between us. He'd spent twenty years building a reputation on other people's courage. I'd just spent fourteen hours spending mine.

We made camp without a fire, shared the emergency chocolate I'd forgotten I had, and watched the glacier calve in the dark — great silent white collapses, lit from within by moonlight, like the mountain exhaling years.

"You knew," Lena said quietly. "Down in the gully. When it got bad. You knew exactly what to do."

"I knew what Edith wrote."

"No." She shook her head. "Edith wrote instructions. You made decisions. There's a difference, and I was on the wrong end of it for fourteen hours, so believe me, I know." She pulled her sleeping bag up to her chin. "Whatever happened with Marcus — whatever Soren said happened — the person who brought me down that wall today is a real guide."

I didn't answer. I lay on my back looking at the stars through the gap in the cirque walls and let the words do their work, slow as meltwater, finding the crack in the stone I'd built around that part of myself and widening it.

In the morning we'd descend to the valley, and after that to Salt Hollow, and after that to whatever happens when twenty years of letters meet daylight. Soren Hale didn't know yet that his own forged safety records were folded in that satchel next to Edith's handwriting — page after page where she'd documented, in her neat legend's hand, the anchors he'd certified that were never tested, the inspections he'd signed that never occurred. Including the report for the pitch that killed Marcus Reyes. Including my name, exonerated in advance by a woman I never met, dated three weeks before I ever heard it spoken aloud.

But that night, none of that mattered. That night there was only the scree, and the chocolate, and Lena asleep with the satchel strapped to her wrist like something newborn, and the strange, unfamiliar quiet in my chest where the stone used to be.

At dawn, breaking camp, I found one more sigil chalked on the boulder at the edge of the fan — fresher than the others, the loops still sharp. Edith had come down this way too, near the end. And beneath it, small, almost shy, four words:

*Bring her home safe.*

I touched the chalk. It came away on my fingers.

"Lena," I said. "Time to go home."
