## 9. Testimony

The letters went public the way most true things do in Salt Hollow: not with a proclamation, but over fish stew.

I'd imagined something grander, if I'm honest. A reading at the association hall, microphones, Soren Hale's face going grey under fluorescent light while I held up page after page of my grandmother's handwriting like a prosecutor. I'd rehearsed it on the drive down from the glacier — Lior driving with both hands white-knuckled on the wheel even though the road was straight as a sermon, me muttering accusations into the window glass.

Instead, Marta Voss — who had spent two weeks refusing to look at me, and three years before that pretending I didn't exist — knocked on the door of my grandmother's hut at six in the morning and said, "The whole town knows you're back. Either you tell them what's in those letters or they'll invent it. Salt Hollow always invents."

So we did it her way. In the community hall that smelled of diesel stoves and decades of herring, with folding chairs and a crockpot and children banished to the back benches where they could fidget in peace. I stood at the front with a cardboard box on a table covered in a fish-cleaning cloth, because that was the only clean surface anyone could find, and I read my grandmother's words aloud to a room full of people she had loved and left and loved again.

*Dear Lena,* every letter began. *You are nine today. You are eleven. You are fourteen.* Twenty years of birthdays, written to a granddaughter across an ocean, never sent, kept in a tin box in a cirque no map admitted existed. *Today I watched the glacier calve and thought: she is learning long division right now, and I am watching a mountain the size of a cathedral give birth to ice, and neither of us can explain ourselves to the other.*

The room was very quiet. Old Tomas Halvorsen, who had called me "that woman's blood" to my face my first day in town, sat with his cap wrung in his fists like he was trying to strangle time itself.

Then came the other papers. The safety records. My grandmother hadn't just been documenting the route; she'd been documenting *Soren*. Incident reports he'd altered. Client waivers with dates changed. A 2019 expedition log where the original entry — in her hand, witnessed by two guides whose names half this room recognized — read *rope team anchored above serac line against protocol*, and beneath it, in different ink, someone's careful forgery moving the anchor point, redistributing blame, rewriting a death into an accident of weather and one guide's negligence.

One guide. Mine.

I didn't say any of that out loud. I just held the pages up, side by side, and let the room do what rooms do when they finally see a thing they'd chosen not to see for years.

Marta stood first. That mattered more than anything the association would ever decide. She crossed the hall in front of everyone and took the pages from my hands gently, like they were eggs, and read them herself, lips moving, and then she looked at me and said, loudly enough for the back benches, "Your grandmother wrote about you like you were the north star. And we let her think we'd hate you." A pause. "We did hate you. It was easier than missing her."

It wasn't an apology, exactly. It was better. It was the truth, which in Salt Hollow is the local currency and apparently I'd finally learned the exchange rate.

* * *

The association men arrived on Thursday, two of them, damp and official, carrying folders that probably contained my entire ruined life summarized in bullet points. They interviewed Lior in the back room of the harbormaster's office for four hours. I know because I sat outside on a crate of crab pots the whole time, listening to the gulls argue, unable to read, unable to sit still, doing the thing where you count your own heartbeats and pretend it's patience.

When Lior finally came out, their face told me nothing, which after everything we'd survived together felt almost like a betrayal. Four hours. Four hours of testimony, of the forged records, of my grandmother's notes cross-referenced against incident timelines, of the account Lior had given seven times before to seven audiences who'd already decided.

"Well?" I said.

"They asked me," Lior said, leaning against the wall next to me, "what I would have done differently." They were looking at the harbor, at the boats knocking their hulls together like impatient horses. "First time anyone's asked that question like they wanted the answer instead of a confession."

"And what did you say?"

"That I'd have checked the anchors myself. That trusting another guide's rigging isn't humility, it's negligence wearing a polite hat." A breath. Something in their shoulders unclenched by a degree. "And then Halvorsen's daughter — she's on the board now — asked me if I'd take clients again. This season."

I turned to look at them properly. "What did you say?"

Lior smiled, and it was the real one, the rare one, the one I'd seen exactly twice: once at the source, once on the descent when we were both still alive enough to be surprised by it. "I said yes. Then I threw up in the parking lot. Don't tell anyone."

"Your secret dies with me," I said, and meant: *nothing about you dies with me, not anymore, I keep things now.*

* * *

The reinstatement notice went up on the association board outside the general store, which is how official news travels here — pinned between a lost-dog flyer and an advertisement for a used skiff. REINSTATEMENT OF CERTIFICATION: L. VASQUEZ. EFFECTIVE IMMEDIATELY. It was typed, bureaucratic, bloodless. Half the town came to look at it anyway, standing in the drizzle like it was a shrine.

Lior pretended not to care. Lior stood across the street drinking terrible coffee and pretending not to care so hard that I finally walked over and said, "You've read it eleven times. I counted."

"It's a piece of paper," they said.

"It's a piece of paper that says you were right for five years while everyone decided you were wrong."

"Yeah." They crumpled the coffee cup and missed the bin, which is the closest I've ever seen them come to losing composure in public. "Five years. You know what nobody says about being cleared? Nobody says sorry. The paper does the apologizing so the people don't have to. It's efficient. It's also—" They stopped. Recalibrated. "It doesn't feel like I thought it would. I thought it would feel like getting my name back. It feels like getting handed a receipt for something that can't be returned."

"Welcome to inheritance," I said. "I got a riddle and a glacier. You got a laminated apology. We're both rich beyond measure."

That got the laugh — short, startled, real. And then old Tomas Halvorsen crossed the street, slow, his bad hip complaining audibly, and stood in front of Lior the way men stand in front of other men in fishing towns when they're about to say something that costs them.

"My brother climbed with your grandmother," he said. "Before the accident that took him. She wrote about it. About all of it. Including about a young guide she trained who reminded her of herself — too proud, too careful, too hard on themselves." He stuck out his hand. "She'd have cleared you herself, years ago, if anyone had thought to ask her. We didn't ask. That's our sin, not yours."

Lior shook the hand. I watched them hold on maybe a second longer than the handshake required, and I watched something leave their body — not the guilt, guilt doesn't leave, it just changes rooms — but the *exile*. The specific loneliness of being the person a story has been told about without you in the room.

Afterward, Lior said nothing for a long time. Then: "Your grandmother trained me. Did you know that?"

"I suspected. The sigils matched her journals."

"Three summers. She taught me that a guide's job isn't to get people up a mountain. It's to bring them home telling the truth about what happened up there." They looked at the reinstatement notice flapping in the wind. "Took me five years and a dead woman's letters to learn it."

* * *

That evening the whole village ended up at the hut — not invited, just arriving, the way small towns do, carrying food as apology and conversation as penance. Someone brought a fiddle. Someone brought aquavit that could strip paint. Marta sat beside me on the bench my grandmother had built and told me stories with names attached: who taught whom to gut fish, who fell through the ice in '08, who my grandmother had pulled off a ledge in a storm while pregnant with my mother, apparently, a detail no one had ever mentioned to my mother, a detail my mother would be hearing from me by phone tomorrow whether she liked it or not.

I listened until midnight. I laughed at things I had no context for and found I didn't need the context — that's the strange grace of a place deciding to claim you. It doesn't check your credentials. It checks whether you stay.

At some point Lior appeared in the doorway, backlit, watching the room with the expression of someone memorizing it. I went to stand beside them in the cold.

"They offered me the guide service," Lior said quietly. "The village contract. Year-round. Winter routes, the school program, the tide cave tours once they're approved." A pause. "I told them I'd think about it."

"You're going to say yes."

"I'm going to say yes." They glanced at me. "You're staying too. Don't pretend you're not. There's sawdust on your sleeves and you've been measuring the hut's windows for glass since Tuesday."

"The letters belong here," I said. "The camp at the cirque needs a keeper. And someone should answer twenty years of birthday letters, even late. Even just out loud, to the glacier."

Lior nodded slowly, looking out at the black water where, somewhere far below the tide line, a cave waited with its sigils and its secrets, patient as stone. "You know what the strangest part is? Five years ago I lost everything because of a lie. Now I've got it all back because of the truth, and it turns out the truth was sitting in a tin box the whole time, waiting for two strangers to go find it."

"Not strangers," I said. "Heirs."

The fiddle struck up something fast inside, and someone shouted my name — my actual name, *Lena*, in a tone that meant *come inside, you're one of us now, stop loitering with your guide* — and I laughed and turned toward the door.

Which is when we both saw it: headlights coming down the coast road, slow, wrong, stopping at the edge of the village where no car had any business stopping at midnight. A door opened. A figure stepped out, tall, unhurried, and even at this distance, even in the dark, I knew the walk.

Soren Hale had come to Salt Hollow himself.

And he wasn't alone.
