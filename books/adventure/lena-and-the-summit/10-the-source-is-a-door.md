## 10. The Source Is a Door

**Lior**

A year ago, if you'd told me I'd be running a guide service out of Salt Hollow — the same village whose harbormaster once looked through me like weathered glass — I'd have checked you for altitude sickness and sent you down the mountain with a stern word about hypoxia.

And yet.

The Salt Hollow Mountain School, est. last spring, population two guides, one dog, and a wall of borrowed gear that squeaks when the wind hits it right. We run day trips up the river gorge in summer, avalanche courses in winter, and a glacier-awareness seminar that Lena insists on teaching herself, because, as she puts it, "I died on that ice once already. I'm uniquely qualified."

She didn't die, obviously. She nearly did, choosing twenty years of her grandmother's letters over the escape line while the calving face came apart behind her like the sky deciding to relocate. I hauled her out of the gully by her pack straps, both of us screaming things we later agreed never happened. That's the thing about terror: it edits itself into something almost fond, given enough time and enough tea.

Today is the anniversary tide.

The almanac promised it months ago — the same once-in-a-deade spring low that drained the bay last year and showed us the cave, the sigils, the tin box, the whole inheritance neither of us asked for and both of us would now defend with our teeth. The village has turned it into something between a pilgrimage and a festival. There's a bake sale. The harbormaster — Marta, who once wouldn't sell me diesel, now saves me the good coffee — has strung lights along the quay like the bay itself is being dressed for company.

"You're brooding," Lena says, appearing at my elbow with two paper cups of Marta's coffee and the particular smile of someone who has learned exactly how to handle me.

"I'm assessing conditions."

"You're brooding *at* conditions."

"Same skill set," I say, taking the cup. "One of them pays."

We walk out onto the exposed seabed together, boots crunching on kelp and shell, the sea held back beyond the drop like a held breath. A year ago this walk was trespass. Now there's a rope line, a sign in three languages, and a laminated map — my map, redrawn properly, credited to "L. Vasquez & L. Okonkwo" in a font far too cheerful for what we found here. The conservation trust got the cave protected in under six months. Bureaucracy, it turns out, moves fast when you hand it a story with a dead legend, a disgraced guide, and an heiress who chose paper over her own life. Lena hates how they tell it. I've stopped minding. The truth survived the telling; that's more than most truths get.

At the cave mouth, the sigils catch the lantern light — her grandmother's marks, chiseled into rock by a woman who came here alone, year after year, to plan a route no one was supposed to follow. Except she'd left it all where someone would find it. That's the part I keep turning over. You don't document a rival's forged safety records and seal them in a tin box unless some part of you is waiting for the right pair of hands.

"After you," Lena says.

"Nope." I step past her into the dark. "Guides go first. House rule."

"It's your only house rule."

"It's a good one."

Inside, the cave smells the way it always does — cold stone and old salt and, faintly, the paraffin of the lamp we leave stocked for visitors. The rusted tin box sits in its case now, glass-fronted, museum-clean. But deeper in, past the mapped chamber, there's the alcove where we found the rope coil, and I've left that as it was. Some things shouldn't be tidied.

Lena crouches beside it and touches the rock where the sigils climb toward a crack in the ceiling that goes nowhere — or went nowhere, until you understood it wasn't a route at all. It was a signature. *I was here. I kept going.*

"She'd have liked the bake sale," Lena says. "She'd have complained about it the entire time, and then eaten four things."

"You knew her one letter at a time," I say. "How would you know?"

"Because I'm her," Lena says simply. "It took me thirty years and a glacier to figure that out, but I'm her. Stubborn, dramatic, terrible at goodbyes." She stands, brushes off her knees. "Come on. Tide turns in forty minutes and Marta will kill us if we miss the ceremony. She made a *plaque*, Lior. There will be speeches."

"God help us."

"Amen," says Lena Okonkwo, heiress to nothing but everything, and walks out of the cave ahead of me, which is also fine, because the second house rule is that keepers take turns.

* * *

**Lena**

Here is what nobody tells you about inheriting a riddle instead of a fortune: somewhere along the way, the riddle starts answering back.

A year ago I stood in the Salt Hollow church hall while a lawyer read my grandmother's will aloud — *to my granddaughter, the source, if she can find it* — and felt the whole village's resentment settle on my shoulders like wet snow. They thought the Okonkwo name meant abandonment. Money sent north, people never sent back. They weren't entirely wrong. My mother fled this coast at nineteen and never explained why, and I grew up thinking my grandmother had simply chosen the mountains over us.

Then I found the letters. Twenty years of them, addressed to a granddaughter who didn't exist yet when the first one was written, stacked in a final camp beneath a forbidden cirque. *When you're old enough. When you're angry enough. When you're ready.* She'd been writing to me my whole life from a place my mother couldn't bear to look at, and the reason my mother couldn't bear it was folded into those pages too — a family history of routes climbed and partners lost and a woman who refused to let a dangerous man's lies stand unchallenged, even if challenging them cost her everyone.

The source was never water. It was testimony. And apparently it was hereditary.

Now the hut at the cirque has a stove that works, a radio that mostly works, and a ledger where climbers sign in on their way to the high country. I've painted the door blue because every photograph of her shows blue paint, flaking, stubborn. Volunteers from the village carried the lumber up in stages last summer — Marta's son among them, and old Piet who once told me I had my grandmother's chin and not as a compliment. It took eleven trips. Nobody complained. Or rather, everybody complained, constantly, the entire time, which I understand now is how love sounds in coastal villages.

Today, back at sea level, the whole town has come down to the quay for the anniversary. There is a plaque. It is enormous. It reads:

**THE SOURCE IS A DOOR**
*In memory of Adaeze Okonkwo, who kept the way open.*

Marta gives the speech. It runs nine minutes and mentions the bake sale twice. Lior stands beside me with their hands jammed in their pockets, radiating the specific agony of a person who would rather solo a serac than be publicly appreciated, and when Marta says "*and to our guides, who found what the tide keeps*" the applause lands on Lior like falling rock, and I watch them endure it, jaw tight, eyes bright, and think: *a year ago these people wouldn't sell you diesel.*

Afterward, when the crowd thins and the tide begins its slow return, filling the bay back up like a lung, Lior finds me at the rail.

"So," they say. "Keeper."

"Don't start."

"One year. One season of clients nobody died on. One hut. One plaque." They nudge my shoulder with theirs. "You could at least pretend to be smug."

"I am profoundly smug," I say. "Internally. Where it counts."

Below us, the water slides up the seabed we walked an hour ago, erasing our footprints, closing over the cave mouth — but not really closing it. That's the thing I finally understand about doors. They're not walls with holes in them. They're promises that both sides can be reached. My grandmother stood in that cave with her chisel and her grief and her fury, and she carved a promise into the rock: *someone will come. Someone will finish this.* She just couldn't have known it would take a blacklisted guide with something to prove and a granddaughter with everything to learn, meeting in the middle of a route that should have killed them both.

The last of the light goes gold on the water. Somewhere behind us the festival is striking the tents, and Piet is arguing with Marta about next year's tide dates, and the dog is stealing a third pastry with the confidence of a creature who has learned exactly whom he can rob.

"Same time next decade?" Lior asks.

"Every year," I say. "Low tide or not. We walk in anyway. Waders if we have to."

Lior laughs — actually laughs, the rare full version, the one I've heard maybe six times and hoard like letters. "That's a terrible operational decision."

"It's the family style."

The sea closes over the cave, patient as handwriting, and above it the first stars come out over the range where a blue door waits at the top of the world, stove lit, ledger open, keeping the way for whoever comes next.

We stay at the rail a long time. Neither of us is going anywhere.
