## 1. The Weight of Optimists

The storm came off the flats at four in the afternoon, and Hazel Kavanagh was already waiting for it, parked on a gravel shoulder with her engine ticking and her coffee gone cold an hour ago.

She'd named the cell *Bessie* because naming storms made them feel like work instead of weather. Bessie was a fat-bellied supercell dragging a skirt of dust across the prairie, rotating slow and mean, the kind of storm that paid well if you could read it and killed you if you couldn't. Hazel could read it. That was supposed to be the whole point of her.

The radio crackled. "Kavanagh, you seeing this hook on radar?"

"Seeing it, Dorn. She's turning left." Hazel tipped her coffee back, found nothing, and set the cup in the footwell with the other six. "She's going to cut southeast toward Halloway. You want your footage, get ahead of her now or don't bother."

"You sure? Models say—"

"Models say what the models are paid to say. I'm telling you what the sky's saying." She watched the base of the cloud lower, tighten, begin to spin like something deciding. "And the sky doesn't lie. It just doesn't explain itself."

Dorn grumbled something about freelancers and hung up. Hazel pulled onto the road and drove into the green-gray light.

This was the part she loved, and she hated that she loved it, because loving it meant she was good at exactly one thing, and that one thing didn't pay. Chasing was a young man's game with a rich man's equipment budget, and Hazel had neither youth nor money, only a ten-year-old truck called *Vera*, a cracked windshield, a barometer app she didn't trust, and instincts that had never once been wrong about where a storm intended to go. Instincts were free. Everything else cost.

The debt did too. Forty-one thousand dollars to the Meridian Syndicate, accruing interest at a rate that felt less like arithmetic and more like erosion. She'd borrowed it three years ago to buy Vera and the sensor rig, back when a lender with a polite voice and a corner office had seemed like salvation instead of just another predator with better manners. The Syndicate lent to people banks wouldn't touch — chasers, prospectors, small operators — and they collected with a patience that was worse than violence. Violence ended. The Syndicate just waited.

Hazel drove toward Halloway with Bessie breathing down her neck and did the math she always did, the one that never changed. Footage fee from Dorn: eight hundred, maybe twelve if the tornado verified. Fuel, food, Vera's next repair: most of it. Remainder: not enough. It was never enough. She was a woman bailing a boat with a thimble, and the boat had opinions about sinking.

The hail started five miles out of town.

It came sideways first, then straight down, chunks the size of fists hammering the roof like the sky was trying to get in. Hazel hunched over the wheel and kept driving, because stopping meant hail damage and hail damage meant repairs and repairs meant Meridian, and Meridian meant the phone call she'd been dodging for two weeks. Ahead, through the white chaos, the road bent along a coulee, and the light went strange — that flat, bruised green that meant the atmosphere had stopped negotiating.

"Come on, Bessie," she muttered. "Don't do this to me today."

Bessie did it to her anyway.

The wind hit from the north, a gust front she should have seen coming and hadn't, because she'd been watching the mesocyclone instead of her own mirrors, and that was the kind of mistake experience was supposed to prevent. The truck lurched. The trailer with her sensor mast — her best asset, her collateral, her livelihood — swung wide and caught the crosswind like a sail. Hazel felt the rear end step out, corrected too hard, felt the wheels leave the pavement entirely as Vera slid for the shoulder, for the coulee, for the drop.

There was a long moment where nothing happened and everything was decided. She remembered thinking, absurdly, *not the mast, save the mast.*

Then the world turned over.

* * *

She woke to rain on her face and the smell of gasoline and dirt.

Vera lay on her side in the coulee, wheels up, one headlight still burning into the mud like a stubborn eye. The cab was folded around the steering column. The sensor mast was snapped in three places. Hazel crawled out through the shattered passenger window because the driver's door was a sculpture now, and she stood in the rain looking at eleven years of her life crumpled into scrap, and she laughed — actually laughed, once, a short ugly sound — because the alternative was sitting down in the mud and not getting up.

Her ribs hurt when she breathed. Her left wrist was swelling inside her jacket. Nothing seemed broken except everything.

Dorn's footage never materialized; Bessie dropped her funnel twenty miles east over empty farmland and nobody got paid. Hazel sat in the Halloway diner until closing, drinking bad coffee and icing her wrist against a glass of ice water she kept refilling so the waitress wouldn't notice, and at nine-fifteen her phone rang with a number she knew by heart.

"Ms. Kavanagh." The voice was smooth, unhurried, almost kind. Corven. Senior collections, Meridian Syndicate. He had a way of pronouncing her name like he was reading it off a deed. "I understand you've had some vehicle trouble."

"Some," Hazel said.

"And some difficulty meeting this month's obligation."

"That's right."

A pause, comfortable on his end, terrible on hers. "We've been patient, Ms. Kavanagh. Genuinely. But patience has a balance sheet too, and yours has run long. So here is what we're prepared to offer."

Here it comes, she thought. Here's the shape of the trap.

"The full amount stands at forty-six thousand, two hundred and change, with the late accruals. We're prepared to settle for the sensor rig and the vehicle — salvage value, assessed fairly — plus thirty thousand cash, due in ninety days. Or—" the pause again, savoring it, "—you come work for us. Weather services division. Storm positioning, route assessment, timing analysis. Your particular talents. Contracted, salaried, and every dollar against your principal."

"And what does the weather services division do, exactly?" Hazel asked, though she already knew. Everyone in the chasing community knew. Meridian bought land cheap ahead of disasters they saw coming and sold high after, and lately there were rumors of worse — rumors of claims staked on ground the weather would make valuable, permits filed with data that came from somewhere. From someone. "You want me to tell you where the storms are going so you can be standing there first."

"We want you to tell us where the opportunities are going," Corven said pleasantly. "Same thing, from where I sit."

From where I sit, it's the same thing as selling a map to wolves, Hazel thought, but she didn't say it, because her wrist hurt and her truck was dead and forty-six thousand dollars was a number with teeth.

"I'll need time," she said.

"Ninety days," Corven agreed. "For the cash option. The employment option expires Friday. After that, we pursue standard remedies." He let *standard remedies* sit there between them, doing its work. "You've always struck me as a woman who understands leverage, Ms. Kavanagh. I'd hate to see it used against you."

He hung up. Hazel sat in the emptying diner with her iced wrist and her cold coffee and watched the last of Bessie's rain crawl down the window, and she understood something with total clarity: her skills were worth a fortune to everyone but her. To Dorn they were worth eight hundred dollars. To Meridian they were worth a fortune. To herself, to the only person whose life they actually governed, they were worth a debt that grew while she slept.

Desperation, she decided, tasted like diner coffee. You drank it anyway, because it was hot and it was there.

She had ninety days, a dead truck, and no options she could live with. Which meant, she thought, draining the cup, that she needed an option she couldn't live with — something desperate, something off the maps, something worth more than her fear.

She got it eleven days later, in a ravine off the old survey road, in the shape of a dying man with a weathered roll of paper in his bleeding hands and a promise he needed somebody — anybody — stupid enough to make.

But that morning, all she had was the walking. Eighteen miles to the nearest garage, wrist swelling, ribs screaming, past fields still dripping from Bessie's tantrum. Halfway there, a battered pickup slowed beside her, and the driver — an old man with a prospector's squint and a canvas case riding shotgun — leaned across and asked if she needed a lift.

"Yeah," Hazel said, and got in, not knowing yet that she'd just accepted the second-worst ride of her life, or that the man beside her was carrying the thing that would either hang her or save her.

His name, he said, was Wren. Elias Wren.

And he was heading into the Carrow Range, and he looked like a man who knew he wasn't coming back.
