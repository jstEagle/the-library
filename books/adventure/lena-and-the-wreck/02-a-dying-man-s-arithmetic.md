## 2. A Dying Man's Arithmetic

The ravine was the color of an old bruise, and something in it was moaning.

Hazel heard it before she saw anything — a low, wet sound carried up on the wind off the scree, rhythmic, almost patient. Not wind in rock. Wind didn't say *help* between gusts.

She should have kept walking. That was the arithmetic she'd learned over three years of chasing storms for hire money: every detour cost fuel, fuel cost dollars, dollars were the thing she had least of and owed most of. Her truck was a total somewhere back on the switchbacks, her gear was a duffel and a barometer case, and her net worth was a number Meridian Syndicate accountants recited to her like a sentence. Stopping for a stranger's moaning was a luxury item.

But her feet were already going down the slope, because that was the other thing about her — the thing the collectors hadn't figured out how to invoice yet. She couldn't hear a sound like that and file it under *not my problem*. It was a character flaw with interest accruing.

She found him forty feet down, wedged against a slab of granite where he'd evidently slid a long way. Old man, sixty at least, in a prospector's canvas coat gone gray with dust. One leg bent wrong below the knee, and there was blood on the rock beneath him in a spread that told her everything she needed to know about timelines. He'd been here a while. He'd be here not much longer.

"Hey." She crouched, careful of the loose stone. "I'm Hazel. I heard you from the trail."

His eyes came up slow, milky at the edges but sharp underneath, the way old men's eyes got when they'd spent decades reading country nobody else could read. He looked at her face, then at the barometer case slung on her shoulder, and something in his expression shifted — recognition, or its cousin.

"Storm reader," he said. His voice was gravel poured through a sieve. "You've got the look."

"I've got the debt," she said. "The look came free."

That got a breath out of him that might have been a laugh if laughing hadn't cost too much. "Kavanagh," he said. "The one who totaled her rig on the Carrow switchbacks this morning. Word travels faster than weather out here."

"Great. I'm famous."

"You're marked." He said it without drama, the way you'd report a pressure drop. "Meridian's boys don't like losing collateral. They'll find you by tomorrow night."

"They always say that." She pulled her water bottle out and held it to his mouth. He drank in small, disciplined sips, like a man conserving even his dying. "You know my name, old-timer, but you haven't given me yours."

"Elias Wren." He said it like he was checking whether it still fit. "And before you ask — no, I don't want you hauling me out of here. My femoral's gone. I felt it let go when I hit. You carry me up that slope, I'm dead on top of it instead of dead at the bottom of it, and you'll have broken your back for nothing."

Hazel looked at the leg. She wasn't a medic, but she'd seen enough wrecks to read one. He was right, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third person.

"So what do you want?" she asked. "A witness? Last words? I charge for those now."

"Both." His hand crawled across the rock toward a battered aluminum case strapped to his pack frame, half-buried under scree. "Help me sit up first. If I'm doing business, I'll do it level."

She shouldn't have helped him sit up. Every rational cell in her body filed the objection. But she braced his shoulders and eased him against the granite anyway, and he grunted once, white-faced, and then settled like a man arranging himself for a long negotiation — which, she would understand later, was exactly what it was.

The case, when he dragged it into the light, was nothing much to look at. Dented aluminum, corner guards worn to bare metal, a latch that took him two tries with shaking fingers. Inside: a bundle of papers wrapped in oilcloth, a leather-bound notebook swollen with damp, and — folded flat, soft as cloth from handling — a map.

He unfolded the map on his knee. It was hand-drawn, ink faded to the brown of dried blood, contour lines laid down by someone who understood terrain the way priests understand scripture. The Carrow Range ran along its spine. And near the head of a drainage Hazel half-recognized, someone had marked a point with an X so old the ink had ghosted into the paper around it.

"What is it?"

"That," Elias Wren said, "is the only honest survey ever done on the north face of the Carrows."

* * *

She wanted to tell him she didn't care. That was the correct move — the move the collectors would have made, the move any sane debtor made when a dying man started talking about surveys. She had a deadline breathing down her neck, fourteen days to produce a sum that might as well have been lunar dust, and no capacity whatsoever for another man's unfinished business.

Instead she said, "Honest compared to what?"

"Compared to Meridian." He tapped the X with one cracked fingernail. "Twenty-two years ago I flew assay runs for them. Before the concessions, before the permits, before they were a syndicate instead of a company. There was a plane. There was a storm. There were samples in that plane worth more than everything Meridian has claimed since." His breath caught, held, released. "They wrote us off as lost with all hands. They falsified the loss report. Do you understand? They buried the wreck on paper so they could bury it on the mountain, because what's in that fuselage proves their concession assays are fiction. Every permit they hold expires at first snow. Every claim they've leveraged against people like you rests on numbers somebody invented in a office in Meridian City."

Hazel sat very still. Above them the wind moved through the ravine with the low whistle of a system building somewhere west, and some trained animal part of her brain noted it automatically — *pressure falling, maybe twelve hours* — while the rest of her stared at the old man and did new math.

"And you've known this for twenty-two years," she said, "and you're telling me now."

"I went back twice." His voice flattened. "First time, the pass beat me. Second time, I found the approach, got within a day of it, and broke my ankle in exactly the kind of stupid way that teaches you humility. By the time I could walk again, Meridian had watchers on the range roads. They knew I knew. I've spent two decades being a harmless old crackpot with stories, because harmless old crackpots live longer than witnesses." He looked down at his ruined leg, almost fondly. "Then this morning I got careless on a slope I've climbed a hundred times. So. Arithmetic."

"Whose?"

"Mine." He closed the case, both hands, like closing a coffin. "I can't climb. I can't testify — I'd be dead before any court convened, and dead men's stories are just stories. But a case delivered by a living woman with a reputation for finding things nobody wants found—" He stopped. Looked at her. "I have a daughter."

The word landed strangely. Hazel waited.

"Mara. Coldbrook. She hasn't spoken to me in nine years, and she has every reason — I was a bad husband before I was a bad father, and I was a bad father for a long time after that. She won't want anything from me. She'll tell you so. She'll probably be right." Something moved behind the milky eyes, quick and painful. "But she's also the only honest bookkeeper I ever met. She keeps ledgers for the co-op in Coldbrook, and she can read an assay table better than any assayer in the territory, and she hates liars more than she ever hated me. If the samples reach her hands, she'll know what they mean. And she'll do the right thing with them, because she always does the right thing, God help her, even when it costs."

"And what am I supposed to get out of it?" Hazel heard her own voice go flat and hard, the collector's voice, the voice she hated. "Because I'll be honest with you, old man, since you're being honest with me. I owe Meridian more than I'll earn in five years. You're asking me to carry your box into their backyard, past their enforcers, up a mountain, to a woman who doesn't want it. What's my cut?"

Elias Wren smiled. It transformed his whole ruined face, made him briefly a man she might have liked.

"There it is," he said. "I hoped it would be. A saint would refuse this job, and saints die on mountains too." He pushed the map across the rock toward her. "Your cut is the truth, Kavanagh, and I know how that sounds. But think it through. If those samples void Meridian's permits, then every lien they hold gets tangled in litigation for years. Years. Nobody collects on a contested debt. You wouldn't be rich. You'd be *unreachable*. There's a kind of freedom in that, and I'm told it's the only kind people like us can afford."

Hazel looked at the map. Contour lines like fingerprints. The X ghosting in the paper. Somewhere under that mark, a dead plane full of ore that could unmake a syndicate, and above it, somewhere on the roads, men whose job was making sure it stayed dead.

"This is a terrible deal," she said.

"It's the best one on the table," he said. "For both of us."

* * *

Dusk came down the ravine in stages, gold to copper to iron, and Elias Wren talked less and less as the light failed.

She'd taken the case by then. She'd taken the map, and the notebook, and his instructions — repeated three times, each repetition shorter, until the last version was four sentences she could have recited in her sleep. *Coldbrook. Mara Wren. The co-op. Tell her it isn't the letter that matters.* He'd tried to give her his compass too, pressing the dented brass thing into her hand with a stubbornness that outlasted hers, and she'd accepted it because refusing a dying man's compass seemed like bad luck of a very specific kind.

What she hadn't done — what she noticed, near the end, that she still hadn't done — was promise.

He caught her at it, of course. He was dying, not stupid.

"You haven't said yes," he observed. The sun was a red smear on the western ridgeline. His voice had gone thin as wire.

"I'm deciding."

"You decided an hour ago. You just don't like the terms of your own decision." He coughed, wet and long, and when it passed he lay looking up at the first stars with an expression of vast, tired patience. "Say it or don't. I'd rather die owing you nothing."

Hazel sat with her back against the cold granite and turned the compass over in her fingers. North needle trembling, holding true. Everything else in her life trembling, holding nothing.

Here was the honest ledger, and she made herself read it: she wasn't doing this for him. She barely knew him. She wasn't doing it for Mara Wren, a stranger with a grudge and good handwriting. She was doing it because a dying man had handed her the only asset in the territory that Meridian feared, and because a woman drowning will grab anything that floats, even if it's anchored to a corpse. That was the truth of it, ugly and clean, and she figured he deserved the ugly version if he was going to get anything.

"I'll deliver the case," she said. "To Mara Wren, in Coldbrook. That's the promise. Nothing more. I'm not your avenger and I'm not your priest."

"That's all I bought," Elias said. "Nothing more."

The sun went out behind the ridge. In the last of the light she watched his breathing space itself further and further apart, gaps opening between breaths like crevasses, until one gap simply didn't close. No drama. No last words. Just a man finishing an equation he'd been working for twenty-two years, and arriving, finally, at zero.

Hazel sat with him until the cold made it pointless, and then she stood, shouldered the aluminum case, and climbed out of the ravine under a sky already filling with snow-clouds moving in from the west — early, too early, weeks ahead of schedule.

The first snows were coming to the Carrow Range.

And twelve miles east, on the range road, headlights were sweeping the switchbacks, looking for a woman who'd totaled a truck and inherited a secret.
