## 4. Coldbrook

Mara Wren knew the woman was trouble before the woman knew she was trouble herself. That was the gift of working a diner counter at six in the morning: you learned to read people by how they came in out of the cold.

This one came in like weather. Shoulders first, jaw set, eyes doing a slow sweep of the room — exits, faces, corners — before they settled on Mara behind the register. She wore a canvas jacket stiff with old rain and new dust, and she moved with the careful economy of someone counting her last dollars. Her left hand stayed closed around something in her pocket.

"Coffee," she said. "Black. And whatever's hot that isn't soup."

"Chili's not soup."

"Chili, then." She sat at the counter instead of a booth, which told Mara she didn't plan to stay long or didn't trust booths, and Mara had stopped believing in coincidences around the same time she'd stopped believing in her father.

She poured the coffee. The woman wrapped both hands around the cup like it was the only warm thing in the county.

"You're Mara Wren," the woman said. Not a question.

Mara set down the pot. "I don't serve people who know my name before I know theirs."

"Hazel Kavanagh." A pause, the kind people leave when they're deciding whether the truth will cost them. "Your father sent me."

The diner noise went on around them — the grill hissing, old Tompkins complaining about his eggs, the radio murmuring road conditions for the pass — but for Mara it all dropped away into a kind of ringing silence. Six years since Elias Wren had walked out of Coldbrook with a surveyor's case and a promise to be back by spring. Four since the letters stopped. Two since she'd stopped flinching every time the phone rang.

"He sent you," she repeated.

"He didn't exactly *send* me. It's more—" Hazel Kavanagh rubbed her face with a chapped hand. "Look. Can we talk somewhere? Ten minutes. Then I'm gone, and you never have to see me again."

"We can talk right here." Mara crossed her arms. "I've got a shift. And I've got nothing to say to Elias Wren or anyone carrying his messages."

Something flickered across the woman's face — not surprise. Recognition, maybe. Like Mara had confirmed something she'd already suspected and been hoping against.

"He's dead," Hazel said quietly.

The words landed the way a dropped glass lands: a sharp bright instant of chaos, then the long mess after. Mara gripped the edge of the counter. Old Tompkins looked over; she shook her head at him and he went back to his eggs.

"When?" Her voice came out flat. She'd practiced flat. Flat was what you built when grief kept showing up uninvited.

"Three days ago. A ravine off the north fork. His leg was broken. He'd been hurt a while — he was starving when I found him." Hazel spoke carefully, each word placed like a foot on ice. "He gave me a map and a case and asked me to bring them to you. That's the whole story. That's everything."

"That's not everything." Mara heard her own voice rise and hated it. "That's the version where he's a tragic figure. Where's the part where he sold our house? Where's the part where my mother worked double shifts for two years paying off debts he never explained? Where's the part where he looked me in the eye and swore this time was different, and then—" She stopped. Breathed. The grill hissed. "You want ten minutes. You've used four."

Hazel took it without flinching. Mara gave her credit for that. Most people, hit with that much anger from a stranger, would have retreated into apology or pity, and she'd have thrown them out for either. This one just drank her coffee and waited, like a person who understood storms.

"I'm not here to defend him," Hazel said finally. "I met him for about an hour and he was mostly dying. But he talked about you. Said you were the smart one. Said you'd know what to do with it even if you hated him for it."

"Know what to do with what?"

Hazel glanced toward the kitchen, then leaned in, and her voice dropped to something rough and low. "Ore samples. Assay records. From a plane crash up in the Carrow Range — a survey flight, years back. Your father was on it. He walked away from the wreck and spent six years sitting on what he found because he was scared of who owned the land." She paused. "The Meridian Syndicate owns the land. And if those samples say what I think they say, Meridian's permits are built on lies."

The name went through the diner like a draft under a door. Everyone in Coldbrook knew Meridian. Half the town worked the concession roads in summer; the other half had sold out to them or been ruined trying not to. Mara's mother had died owing a clinic bill they'd bought and doubled.

"And he thought I'd want that," Mara said. "Six years of nothing. Not a letter, not a call, not so much as a postcard while Mom got sick and died — and now he's dead, and suddenly there's a treasure map, and I'm supposed to be grateful?"

"No," Hazel said. "He thought you'd want the truth. Those are different things. I tried telling him that." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the counter. "He wouldn't hear it."

For one treacherous second — half a second, less — Mara felt something crack open. Her father, starving in a ravine, holding a stranger's hands around a case full of rocks and paper, saying *my daughter, my daughter, tell my daughter*. She killed the feeling fast. She'd gotten good at killing it. Grief was a con artist too; it showed up wearing his voice and made promises about closure, and then you were crying in a walk-in freezer over a man who'd chosen rocks over you twice.

"No," she said aloud.

Hazel blinked. "No?"

"No, I don't want it. Whatever's in the case — the samples, the map, the confession, whatever heroic speech he dictated with his last breath — I don't want any of it." She pulled her order pad out of reflex, needing something in her hands. "You drove all this way, Ms. Kavanagh, and I'm sorry for your trouble, genuinely. But I sell eggs and chili and pie. I don't deal in stolen mining data. I don't testify. I don't get involved. My father got involved his whole life and look where it got everyone who loved him."

"It's not stolen—"

"It doesn't matter what it is!" Heads turned. Mara lowered her voice, and it came out worse than shouting — quiet and final, like a door closing. "It doesn't matter. That's the lesson he taught me. Whatever he was chasing, it never mattered enough to come home for. So why should it matter enough to ruin my life now that he's gone?"

Hazel looked at her for a long moment. There was something in her face Mara couldn't read — not judgment, which she'd have bristled at, and not pity, which she'd have hated. Something more tired than either. Understanding, maybe, from a woman who clearly carried debts of her own.

"Okay," Hazel said.

"*Okay?*"

"Okay. You don't want it. I'm not going to shove your father's ghost down your throat." She finished the coffee and stood, peeling bills from a fold that had nothing left to hide. "Chili was good. Thanks for the coffee."

"That's it?" Mara hadn't expected relief. What she felt instead was closer to vertigo — she'd braced for a fight and the fight had simply declined to happen. "You drove through the pass in October to hand me a dead man's case, and *that's it*?"

"You said no." Hazel shrugged, and the shrug cost her something; Mara saw it land in her shoulders. "It's yours to refuse. That was kind of the point."

She put money on the counter, more than the bill, and walked toward the door. And Mara stood there with her order pad and her father's name ringing in her ears, watching the woman go, and discovered that winning could feel exactly like being buried.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said, the thing that scraped at her as the door swung shut: she *had* wondered. All those years, some small shameful corner of her had imagined him finding something real — not a scam, not another sure thing, but actual ore in actual stone, proof that the obsession had been pointing at something. And now a stranger with storm-grey eyes had driven out of the mountains carrying exactly that, and Mara had refused it without looking inside the case, because looking would mean admitting she still cared, and caring about Elias Wren was how you ended up like her mother: waiting, spending, forgiving, gone.

"Ma'am?" Tompkins, at the register, nodding at the window. "Friend of yours forgot something."

Hazel was already crossing the street, the case strapped tight to her pack, head down against the wind coming off the ridge. And Mara watched her go — a lone figure walking toward the range road with a dead man's burden and no reason left to carry it — and felt the shame arrive on schedule, right on time, right on cue.

* * *

Her shift lasted four more hours and lasted approximately forever. She broke two plates. She shorted Tompkins's change and gave him the wrong pie and he ate it anyway, kindly, saying nothing, which was somehow unbearable. At noon she untied her apron, told the cook she had a migraine, and walked home through a wind that smelled like snow.

Her apartment above the hardware store still held traces of her mother: the good teapot, the crocheted blanket, the photograph on the shelf where Elias had been cropped out with scissors, leaving only his shoulder and a sliver of grin. Mara sat on the bed and stared at that cropped edge for a long time.

*He talked about you,* the stranger had said. *Said you were the smart one.*

Smart. Smart would have asked what was in the case. Smart would have asked what the enforcers' trucks idling at the edge of town since yesterday were doing here — because Mara had noticed them, black SUVs with government-clean plates in a town where nobody's truck was clean, and she'd filed it under *Meridian being Meridian* and thought no more about it. Smart would have connected a dying prospector, a crashed survey plane, and two men in dark jackets asking Tompkins at the gas station whether anyone new had come through the pass.

She stood up so fast the bed frame knocked the wall.

They weren't here for her. They were here for *her*. For Hazel Kavanagh and the case. Which meant the case wasn't just rocks and papers. Which meant her father hadn't died of bad luck in a ravine — or if he had, the thing in that case was worth enough that men were already driving mountain roads in October looking for it.

Mara grabbed her coat, took the stairs two at a time, and pushed out onto the street, scanning south toward the range road.

The wind had picked up. Clouds were stacking over the Carrows like plates, grey going to white at the peaks — early snow, weeks ahead of schedule, the kind the old-timers called a liar's front because it looked like weather and behaved like a wall. And far down the road, nearly swallowed by it, a small figure with a heavy pack was walking straight into the mouth of the range, alone, carrying everything.

Mara ran three steps before she even decided to.
