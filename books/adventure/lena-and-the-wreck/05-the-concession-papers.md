## 5. The Concession Papers

The town was called Ferrow's Bend, which meant nothing to anyone except the people who lived there and the river that bent around it. Hazel rolled in on the last of her fuel with a cracked windshield, a dead man's map folded into her jacket, and a case of ore samples riding shotgun like a hostage she couldn't decide whether to protect or ransom.

She'd been driving since before dawn, putting distance between herself and Coldbrook, where Mara Wren had looked at her father's legacy like it was something scraped off a boot. *Take it away. I don't want any part of him.* Hazel had left with the case because leaving without it hadn't been an option — not anymore. The map had gotten under her skin the way weather did. You could ignore a forecast all you wanted, but once you'd read the sky, you couldn't unsee what was coming.

What was coming, she suspected, was trouble with a Syndicate logo on it.

Ferrow's Bend had one street, two bars, a feed store, and — wedged between a shuttered assay office and a place that sold snowmobiles nine months out of the year — a regional records annex with a green awning and a hand-lettered sign: **LAND & MINERAL ARCHIVES — PUBLIC ACCESS — NO FOOD OR DRINK**.

Hazel almost drove past it.

Then she thought about Elias Wren dying in that ravine with his blood freezing in the gravel, pressing a map into her hands like it was the last coin he owned. She thought about the coordinates she'd traced three times on the road, cross-referenced against the topographic sheets in her truck, and how each time they landed in the same impossible place: square inside the Meridian Syndicate's Carrow Range concession. Deep inside it. Forty miles from the nearest legal access point.

A prospector doesn't hike forty miles into land he can't file a claim on unless he's looking for something worth more than a claim.

She parked behind the annex, fed the meter with coins she couldn't spare, and went in out of the cold.

* * *

The archive smelled like toner and old paper and the particular staleness of buildings heated by baseboard units that had given up sometime last decade. A woman in her sixties sat behind the counter reading a paperback with a shirtless man on the cover. Her name tag said **DORIS**.

"Research," Hazel said. "Mining concessions. Carrow Range."

Doris looked at her over the paperback. "Commercial or personal?"

"Personal."

"Then it's free, but you do your own pulling. Terminal's in the back. Concession files are by parcel number or permit year. If you don't have either, start with the index maps." She went back to her book. "No food or drink."

Hazel found the terminal in a back room lined with gray filing cabinets and the flat map drawers that surveyors' dreams were stored in. The system was old but functional, and she knew how to work old systems — half her storm-chasing income came from data nobody else bothered to dig for.

She started with the index maps. The Carrow Range concession block glowed on the screen in Syndicate blue: a vast rectangle swallowing most of the high country, its boundaries drawn with the confidence of an entity that had never been told no. Parcel MC-4471. Permit issued eleven years ago. Renewal pending.

*Renewal pending.* Hazel stared at those two words until they stopped being bureaucratic filler and started being a door.

She pulled the renewal file.

It came up as a scanned packet, forty pages of dense legalese, and she printed the key sections at ten cents a page because she needed to hold them in her hands. Then she carried the pages to a table under a buzzing fluorescent tube and started to read like her life depended on it, because there was a decent chance it did.

The Meridian concession wasn't a claim. That was the first thing she understood. It was a *survey exclusion* — a permit granted on the strength of completed geological assessment, giving the Syndicate exclusive exploration rights across the entire range while it decided what to develop. The permits ran in five-year terms. They expired at first snowfall of the final year.

They expired in six weeks.

And here was the hinge, buried in section 14(b) in language so dry it crackled: *renewal contingent upon verification of original assessment data against independent field samples; material discrepancy shall constitute grounds for voidance.*

Material discrepancy. Independent field samples.

Hazel set the pages down. Her heart was doing something strange and slow, like the barometer drop before a twister.

Elias Wren had been a surveyor. She'd assumed he was a prospector gone rogue, some old-timer chasing one last strike into land he had no right to enter. But prospectors didn't carry sample cases with chain-of-custody tags. Prospectors didn't label cores by grid coordinate and depth interval in a hand so steady it looked typeset. She'd seen the labels when she'd checked the case at a gas station outside Coldbrook, turning the latches with her thumbs, telling herself she was just making sure nothing had shifted on the road.

He hadn't been stealing from the Syndicate. He'd been *auditing* them.

She went back to the terminal and pulled the original assessment — the one the Syndicate had filed eleven years ago to win the exclusion in the first place. The assay tables scrolled past, column after column of numbers, copper percentages and core depths, certified by a laboratory called Halvorsen Analytical, signed off by a field geologist whose name made her sit very still.

**E. WREN, FIELD SURVEYOR OF RECORD.**

"He worked for them," Hazel said aloud, to the empty room.

Doris's voice drifted from the front. "No talking on calls."

"I'm not on a call."

"Then talk quieter."

Hazel leaned back in the chair and let it assemble itself, piece by piece, the way a storm cell builds — invisible at first, then suddenly filling half the sky.

Eleven years ago, Elias Wren had been the field surveyor on the Syndicate's Carrow assessment. His name was on the assays. Which meant either he'd falsified the data himself and spent a decade drowning in it, or the numbers had been cooked above his head and he'd spent a decade trying to prove it. And now she remembered the ravine, his broken body, the way he'd gripped her wrist with the last functional strength he had and said: *They'll tell you I was a thief. I was the only honest pair of hands on that mountain.*

She'd taken it for delirium. Dying men said things.

Except dying men didn't spend their last months hiking into forbidden country collecting verification cores from thirty separate sites, tagging and logging them to standards that would survive a courtroom.

Hazel pulled the sample case onto the table and opened it under the light. Thirty-one cores in foam slots, each wrapped and labeled. She picked one at random — grid 77-C, depth 4.2 meters — and found the corresponding entry in the original assessment. The Syndicate's filed assay for 77-C listed copper content at 0.9 percent. Marginal. Barely worth the cost of extraction. One of dozens of marginal results that together justified sitting on the range indefinitely, excluding everyone else while developing nothing.

If the core in her hands said otherwise — if even a handful of these thirty-one said otherwise — then the entire assessment was fraudulent, the exclusion was void under 14(b), and every fraudulent claim the Syndicate had built on top of it came down like a cut slope in a rainstorm.

There was no assay lab in Ferrow's Bend. But there had been a shuttered one next door, and Doris, when asked, allowed that the old Halvorsen franchise equipment was still in the building, "if somebody bought the lease, which nobody has, so technically it's all still sitting there."

"Who holds the lease?"

"Meridian Syndicate, far as I know." Doris turned a page. "They own everything up here. Always have."

*Of course they do.*

Hazel stood at the copy machine for a long moment, the renewal pages warm in her hand, and felt something shift in her chest that she didn't have a clean name for.

For two weeks she had been a fugitive. Running from Rusk and his crew, running from the debt, running from the simple arithmetic that said a woman who owed the Meridian Syndicate two hundred and forty thousand dollars was worth more to them broken than whole. Every decision since the ravine had been shaped by that math. Deliver the case, sell the samples, clear the debt, disappear. It had been the only plan that added up.

But plans built on fear always looked airtight until you held them up to better information.

Because here was the thing about the samples: they weren't just ore. They weren't just leverage. They were *evidence*, and evidence of this particular kind didn't need to be sold. It needed to be *filed*. The permits expired at first snow. If the discrepancy was documented before expiry, the concession died with it — and every claim, every lien, every debt instrument the Syndicate had written against Carrow Range assets got dragged into the collapse with it.

Including hers.

Not forgiven. Not erased. But a debt held by an indicted creditor was a debt in chaos, tied up in receivership for years, negotiable down to pennies by any lawyer willing to wade in. She'd seen it happen to smaller fish. She'd never dreamed she'd be holding the hook.

Hazel looked down at the case. Thirty-one cores. A dead man's decade of quiet, methodical defiance, packed in foam.

"You son of a bitch," she said softly. "You knew exactly what this was worth."

Which raised the question she'd been avoiding since the ravine: if it was worth this much, why hand it to a stranger? Why not sell it? Why not bargain with it?

Because he'd tried, maybe. Because the Syndicate had buried him once already — professionally, reputationally — and would bury him again, permanently, the moment he surfaced. Because a dying man with no allies couldn't negotiate. He could only entrust. He'd needed someone outside the ledger entirely, someone with nothing to lose and a working vehicle and, God help him, a face that looked like it kept its promises.

Or maybe he'd simply seen her stop for a stranger in a ravine, and decided that was enough.

Hazel copied the renewal file twice, hid one set in the truck's spare-tire well, and paid Doris for the printing with coins counted out one at a time.

"Find what you were looking for?" Doris asked.

"Yeah." Hazel tucked the pages inside her jacket, against the map. "More than I wanted."

"That's usually how it goes." Doris marked her place with a receipt. "You driving north?"

"Why?"

"Roads close early this year. Early snow, they're saying. First real system comes down the range end of the week." She shrugged. "Nobody listens to me either."

Hazel stepped out into the street. The light had gone thin and hard, the kind of light that meant the atmosphere was scrubbing itself clean for something. She could read it the way other people read headlines: pressure falling, jet stream dipping, cold air mass stacking up over the Carrows like a debt coming due.

Six weeks until the permits expired. Maybe five until the snow sealed everything.

She got in the truck. In the mirror, on the far side of the street, a gray SUV idled outside the feed store with its wipers off and its driver watching her — not obviously, not staring, just present, the way a man is present when he's been present for twenty minutes already.

Hazel started the engine and didn't look again. She pulled onto the north road with the case between her boots and the renewal pages against her ribs, and for the first time since the ravine, she wasn't running from anything.

She was running toward the range, and she knew exactly why.
