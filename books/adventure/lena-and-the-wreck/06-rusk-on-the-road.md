## 6. Rusk on the Road

The river came out of the mountains the way bad news did — quiet for a long time, then all at once.

Hazel heard it before she saw it, a low grinding under the wind, and when she topped the last rise with the case strapped to her back and her boots crusted in two days of trail dust, there it was: the Sorel, wide and gray-green and moving fast, carrying the first meltwater of a season that hadn't decided to start yet. The ford sat where the map said it would, a shallow braided stretch marked by two leaning posts and a rusted sign that read KETTLE FORD — CAUTION IN SPRING.

It was October. The caution was aimed at her anyway.

She crouched at the top of the bank and let herself look at the water for a full minute, because looking was free and mistakes weren't. The channel split around a gravel bar in the middle, maybe forty meters of crossing total, the deepest cut running to mid-thigh by the color of it. Cold enough to pull your breath out through your ribs. Fast enough to take a careless person's feet.

Behind her, thirty kilometers back down the valley road, she'd left a gas station attendant who had looked at her too long. A man filling a black SUV who hadn't looked at her at all, which was worse. She'd been running toward escape since Coldbrook — south, then east, then this stupid looping north again because the archive in Harrow Junction was the only place with concession records open past five — and every kilometer of it she could feel the net drawing tighter. They knew her truck. They knew her face. Dade Rusk knew her habits better than anyone alive, because he'd spent three years learning them from the other side of a collections desk.

She should have ditched the case. She'd thought about it in Coldbrook, after Mara Wren slammed the door on her father's name, after Hazel stood on the porch holding a dead man's belongings like something contagious. Leave it in a locker. Mail it. Bury it.

But then Harrow Junction, and the permit files, and the assay numbers that didn't match Elias Wren's field notes, and now the case wasn't a burden anymore. It was a weapon. And you didn't throw away a weapon when the people hunting you were the ones it was built for.

She stood, rolled her shoulders under the straps, and started down toward the water.

* * *

Dade Rusk kept his hands loose on the wheel and his mind on arithmetic.

Three vehicles. Six men, counting himself. One woman in a region the size of a small country, except the region wasn't the size of a small country, it was exactly the size of the roads, and roads funneled. That was the whole secret of pursuit that the movies got wrong — you didn't chase, you waited. A person running always believed they were choosing their route. They were choosing from a menu you'd already priced.

Kettle Ford was the only crossing on the upper Sorel that a vehicle could manage, and the only one a woman on foot with forty pounds of gear would gamble on, because the bridge at Corben was forty klicks downstream and she'd want to be over the water before dark. Before snow, if she was smart. The forecast had turned overnight; the Syndicate's weather service — they bought everything, why not weather — called for the first serious system of the season to come off the Carrow Range inside seventy-two hours.

After that, the high country closed. After that, whatever she was carrying went into hibernation until spring, and Meridian's permits expired the same week the passes did. The old man's samples were a bomb with a fuse made of calendar pages.

Rusk had read the file twice. Elias Wren, senior surveyor, 1998, dismissed for "inconsistent methodology" eleven days before submitting final assays. Dead twenty-six years later in a ravine with a map in his kit. And now Hazel Kavanagh — storm chaser, debt holder, chronic optimist — sprinting across half the province with a metal case she wouldn't sell even when selling it would have cleared her ledger.

That was the part Rusk couldn't make fit. He understood debt. Debt was clean; it told you exactly what a person would do. But Kavanagh had walked out of Coldbrook still carrying the thing, and people who carried things they could drop were running on fuel he didn't have a gauge for.

"Vehicle's stopped," Voss said from the passenger seat, binoculars up. "Top of the north bank. She's looking at the water."

Rusk eased off the accelerator. "How's the ford?"

"High. Melt off the east faces came down yesterday."

"Then we don't need to be heroes." He picked up the radio. "Brann, take the track along the west side, get below her, block the far approach. Everyone else holds here. We let her get wet, we let her get tired, and we take her on the gravel bar where there's nowhere to go but sideways."

He watched the distant figure start down the bank, small against the gray water, and felt the familiar settling in his chest. Not cruelty. Just the satisfaction of a problem resolving along its natural lines.

Then the figure stopped. Turned. Looked back up the valley — directly at them, or at the space where they were — and stood very still for a long moment, the way a person does when they're not panicking but calculating.

"Huh," Rusk said.

* * *

Hazel saw the sun flash off glass two kilometers up the valley road and her stomach dropped through the ground and kept going.

Binoculars. Or a scope. Either way, eyes, and eyes meant time was over.

Her mind went cold and fast, the way it did inside a wall cloud, when the tornado warning was issued and the only thing left was reading the sky and trusting what it told you. Okay. They were behind her, upstream side, probably two vehicles. If they were smart — and Rusk was smart, Rusk had taught her the hard way that he was smart — they'd send someone around to the far bank to close the exit. Which meant the ford wasn't an escape. The ford was a trap with water in it.

So don't use the ford as a door. Use it as a tool.

She looked at the river again, really looked, the way she'd looked at a hundred supercells. Where the water came from. What fed it. Upstream, maybe six hundred meters, the valley pinched between two rock shoulders, and the east fork tumbled into the main channel through a narrow granite throat — she could hear it, a deeper note under the river's grind, a constriction. Constrictions backed water up. And above the constriction, on the east side, the bank rose in a shelf of fractured shale, the kind of slope that held together right up until it didn't.

A debris jam. A slide. Anything that choked that throat for ten minutes would put another meter of water through the ford and turn a crossing into a torrent.

Or she could do it herself.

There was an old logging road on the east bench — she'd crossed its scar on the hike in, rutted, abandoned, cut into the shale slope maybe fifty meters above the river. Cut deep enough that the upslope face was a wall of loose rock, stacked by machines decades ago and waiting ever since for permission to fall.

Permission. That was all a slope like that needed.

Hazel unslung the case and wedged it under a juniper, covered it with her jacket. Then she took the coil of rope off her pack, the pry bar, and started climbing the bench with her fear burning off behind her like fog in sun. Not gone. Converted. Fear was just energy with nowhere assigned yet, and she was very good at assigning energy.

At the cut face she found what she wanted: a keystone slab, house-sized, perched on a wedge of smaller rubble with the logging road's old drainage culvert hollowed out beneath it. Water had been quietly undermining it for thirty years. She jammed the pry bar under the wedge, leaned her whole weight, and felt the mountain consider her offer.

Come on, she thought. You've been waiting. I'm just the excuse.

* * *

"She's off the ford," Voss reported. "Moving upstream on the east side. Climbing."

Rusk frowned. Running away from the crossing made no sense unless — he ran the map in his head, the bench, the old logging cut, the constriction at the east fork, and the answer arrived with a cold, professional admiration he immediately resented.

"She's going to move rock into the channel," he said. "Flood the ford. Kill our crossing." He keyed the radio. "All units, east bench above the fork, NOW. Brann, if you're in position on the far side, do not wait for her at the ford — she's about to remove the ford. Intercept high."

He put the SUV in gear and drove too fast down a road that didn't deserve it.

* * *

The slab moved with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Not a crash — a groan, then a sigh, then the whole face of the cut letting go at once, tons of shale pouring down the bench in a slow-motion brown avalanche that hit the constriction and plugged it like a cork in a bottle. The river's note changed instantly, the grind rising to a roar, water stacking against the rockfall, backing up the channel with shocking speed.

Hazel scrambled back from the edge, heart slamming, dust in her teeth, and watched the river begin to drown its own ford. Two minutes. Maybe three, and the crossing would be chest-deep and lethal, and anything on the west bank would be stuck there until the jam blew out.

Which meant whoever was on the far side was stuck with her.

She was halfway down to retrieve the case when the man came out of the trees.

Big, gray jacket, moving fast and low — not Rusk, one of the hired ones, and he'd gotten across earlier or come down the far track, and he'd figured out the same math she had: the flood trapped them together, so the fight was happening now, on this side, before the water finished rising. He had a baton, not a gun. Syndicate policy — bodies created paperwork.

Hazel didn't have a baton. She had a slope, a river, and four seconds.

She threw herself sideways off the trail instead of backward, let her boots skid on the scree, grabbed a root, and swung down and around below him as he lunged past where she'd been. His momentum carried him onto the fresh slide scar — the loose stuff, the stuff still deciding where it lived — and the mountain, having just been given permission once, took it personally again.

The ground went out from under him like a trapdoor. He rode a sheet of shale thirty feet down toward the boiling water, screaming, and caught a stunted pine at the lip of the torrent, hanging above the flooded channel with both hands.

For one long second Hazel looked at him. He looked at her.

She could walk away. The river would do the bookkeeping.

"Rope!" he shouted. "Kavanagh — the rope, come on—"

And God help her, she did it. She dropped the coil, anchored it on the juniper, and hauled him up hand over hand while the flood thundered past close enough to spray her face, cursing him, cursing herself, cursing Elias Wren for making promises contagious. When he was on solid ground he knelt there coughing, and she stepped back with the pry bar raised and said, "Tell Rusk the range is closed."

Then she took the case off her shoulder, checked the straps, and turned north — not toward any road, not toward any escape, but straight at the white shoulders of the Carrows, where the snow was already starting to fall and the only way out was through.
