## 7. The Ascent

The treeline gave up on her at noon.

Hazel had been watching it come for two days — the spruce thinning, the trunks twisting shorter and more stubborn, until they were nothing but wind-bent shrubs clutching at scree. Then even those quit, and there was just the mountain, gray and white and utterly indifferent to whether she lived or died on it.

She stopped where the last tree stood, a blackened snag no taller than she was, and ate half a ration bar standing up. The other half went back in her pocket for an emergency she refused to name. Behind her, the valley she'd climbed out of lay green and soft as a lie. Ahead, the Carrow Range stepped upward into cloud, and somewhere up in that cloud, according to a dead man's map, sat a plane full of truth.

The sky was doing something she didn't like.

Most people looked at weather and saw weather. Hazel looked at it and saw machinery — pressure gradients meshing like gears, cold air sliding down off the high peaks like oil into water, moisture riding up from the lowlands with nowhere to go but up. She'd spent eleven years reading that machinery from the driver's seat of a chase truck, telling farmers when to get their hay in and film crews when to roll. The sky had never lied to her. It had only ever told her things she wished she hadn't known.

What it was telling her now: forty-eight hours, maybe less. A front was stacking itself against the western face of the range, fat and patient, and when it came over the top it would come down this side as snow. Early snow. The kind that didn't melt. The kind Elias Wren's permits were expiring under.

"Okay," she said to the snag, because talking out loud kept the fear at a manageable volume. "Okay."

She climbed.

* * *

By mid-afternoon she was above the snowline proper, boots crunching on a crust that had formed overnight and would soften by evening and refreeze by morning — a whole day's worth of information written in ice, if you knew how to read it. She did. It was the one thing she'd ever been good at that nobody could repossess.

That was the thought that kept surfacing as she climbed, unwelcome as a debt collector. Everything else she owned was gone or nearly gone. The truck, totaled back in chapter one of this disaster. Her gear, most of it sacrificed at the ford to buy herself thirty seconds ahead of Rusk's men. Her tent, her rope, her satellite phone with its cracked screen and dead battery. What remained fit in a daypack: the map, wrapped in plastic; Elias's brass compass; the case, lashed tight under the flap; two ration bars, a quarter tank of water, a space blanket, a knife, and a headlamp with batteries she was rationing like blood.

And the promise. That too. Heavier than everything else combined.

She paused on a shelf of rock to check the compass, and the needle swung lazily toward a col between two peaks — northwest, the way the map's faded pencil line crawled. Elias had pressed the compass into her hand along with the map, back in the ravine, his blood making the brass slippery. *You'll need this more than me,* he'd said, which turned out to be literally true, since he'd needed nothing at all by dusk.

Hazel had taken it the way she took everything those days — because it might be worth something. That was the arithmetic she'd run in the ravine, cold and honest: a dying man's map might clear a dying woman's debt. She hadn't promised Mara's father anything out of kindness. She'd promised him out of greed wearing kindness's coat, and she'd told herself the distinction didn't matter because the outcome was the same either way.

Up here, with the wind starting to carry grit and the light going flat and pewter, the distinction was starting to matter very much.

* * *

The fall happened at four o'clock, on a slope she should have read better.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing about mountains — they didn't do drama, they did physics. A band of rock that looked solid was iced underneath, and her boot found the ice instead of the rock, and the world simply rotated ninety degrees and started moving very fast.

She slid maybe sixty feet before a granite rib caught her, hard, across the ribs and one outflung arm. She stopped. The mountain continued being exactly what it was.

For a long moment she lay there breathing in careful sips, taking inventory. Ribs: bruised, probably not broken — she could still breathe deep enough to swear. Arm: scraped raw through her jacket sleeve. Head: intact, helmet-less, lucky. Pack: still on her shoulders.

The compass: gone.

She saw it happen in memory even though she'd felt nothing — the brass case torn loose from her chest pocket during the slide, spinning away downslope, a bright coin flickering across the snowfield and then over the edge of a drop she couldn't see the bottom of.

"No," she said. "No, no, no—"

She got up anyway. She climbed back up the slide path on hands and knees, then traversed along the top of the drop, scanning the snow below for any glint of brass. The light was failing. The snow was fresh enough to swallow small bright objects whole. She searched for twenty minutes, calling herself every kind of fool, and then she made herself stop, because the sky's machinery was shifting gears overhead and she could feel the temperature dropping through her jacket like a hand closing.

She stood at the edge of the drop with her breath smoking and understood, with total clarity, that she was going to die on this mountain if she spent another minute looking for a dead man's compass.

She turned uphill and climbed.

* * *

She found a wind scoop behind a fin of rock as the first flakes came down — big, slow, deliberate flakes, the kind that meant the front's leading edge, not its body. She stamped out a platform, wedged herself into the hollow with the space blanket around her and the pack hugged to her chest like a child, and watched the snow come.

This was the part nobody paid storm chasers for. The waiting. The part where the sky did its work and you sat inside your own skull with nothing but your reasons.

*Why are you here?*

The question arrived with the snowfall, quiet and total. Not why climb a mountain — that answer was easy: samples, Syndicate, debt, survival. But why keep the promise? Why not sell the map's location to the highest bidder months ago? Why not dump the case at the ford when she lost her tent, lighten the load, save her skin? Nobody was enforcing this. Elias Wren was dead. Mara Wren wanted nothing to do with him. The promise existed only in Hazel's head, and Hazel's head had a documented history of bad decisions.

She turned the case over in her lap. Dented aluminum, Elias's initials scratched near the latch. Inside it, ore samples and a confession that could burn the Meridian Syndicate's fraudulent claims to ash — or could be sold, quietly, to the Syndicate itself, who would pay very well to make it disappear. Rusk had offered, back at the river crossing, shouted it across the water while the flood roared: *Whatever he's paying you, we'll double it.*

Nobody would ever know. That was the thing. There was no witness up here but the weather, and the weather kept everyone's secrets.

Her hands were shaking, and not entirely from the cold.

Because — and here was the truth she'd been climbing away from for six chapters of her life — she was tired of being someone skills were extracted from. The Syndicate collected her debt. Film crews collected her forecasts. Even Elias, dying in that ravine, had collected her promise. Everyone she met treated her like a tool with legs. And the worst part, the part that made her want to scream into the snow, was that she'd let them, because a tool gets fed.

But a tool doesn't get to choose. And somewhere between the ravine and the treeline and the ford and this frozen hollow, without noticing the exact moment it happened, Hazel Kavanagh had chosen. Not for money. Not for safety. For the stupid, unenforceable, unprofitable reason that a man had asked her, and she had said yes, and she was the last person left alive who could make that yes mean anything.

"I'm not doing it for you, Elias," she said out loud, and the wind took the words. "I'm doing it for me."

It was the truest thing she'd said in years. The snow thickened. She pulled the blanket tighter, tucked her chin, and let the mountain have the night.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, which was worse than wind — wind meant the front was passing, stillness meant she was inside it. She dug out of a foot of fresh snow, checked the map by headlamp, and found the col she needed: a saddle two ridges east, maybe three hours' climbing, and beyond it, if Elias's pencil lines could be trusted, a hanging valley where a survey plane had gone down nine years ago carrying proof.

She climbed. The snow held her on slopes that would have killed her in summer, filled the cracks, smoothed the ledges. Twice she postholed to her hip and fought free. Once she heard — or thought she heard — the deep whump of a slab settling somewhere above her, and she changed her line without letting herself think about it, the way you'd flinch from a rattlesnake before your brain finished the word.

At ten o'clock she topped the ridge, and the wind hit her like a wall, and beyond the wind—

There. In the hanging valley below, half-drifted, one wing raised like a hand hailing a rescue that never came: the Wreck.

Hazel stood on the ridge with snow streaming off her hood and felt something crack open in her chest that wasn't pain, exactly. Relief, maybe. Or grief arriving nine years late for a man she'd known for one afternoon.

Then she saw the second set of tracks in the snow below — fresh, boot-printed, climbing toward the fuselage ahead of her — and relief curdled instantly into something colder.

Someone had reached the plane first.
