## 8. The Wreck

The plane was exactly where the map said it would be, which was the first honest thing Hazel had encountered in weeks.

She came over the last shoulder of scree at midmorning, wind at her back like a hand shoving her forward, and there it was — a silver scar on the white flank of the mountain, half-buried where the glacier had been slowly swallowing it for eleven years. The tail stood up out of the ice at an angle, the company markings still legible under a skin of frost: MERIDIAN RESOURCE SURVEY. The fuselage ran down-slope into the snowfield like a spear thrown by an angry god.

Hazel stood and looked at it for a long time.

Her hands were ruined. Three days above the treeline had taken the compass, most of her food, one glove, and whatever softness her face had left. Her lips were split. Her left boot leaked. She had spent the night in a rock hollow with her pack pulled over her legs, listening to the wind try to peel the mountain open, and she had not slept so much as she had stopped resisting consciousness in short, bitter intervals.

And still, when she saw the Wreck, something in her chest lifted.

"Okay, Elias," she said. Her voice came out cracked and small in all that emptiness. "Okay."

* * *

Getting into the plane was its own kind of arithmetic.

The tail section was accessible but useless — cargo netting, rotted insulation, a flight bag gone to mush. The cockpit was buried nose-down under four feet of consolidated snow. That left the fuselage door on the upslope side, jammed half-open by the impact and frozen since, probably, before Hazel was old enough to drive.

She worked at it with her ice axe for twenty minutes, chipping a channel around the frame, breathing through her teeth. Twice she stopped to check the ridgeline behind her. Nothing moved. Nothing ever moved up here except weather, and weather was moving plenty — a gray wall building in the northwest, fat and slow, the kind of sky that meant business by dark.

The door gave with a groan that echoed off the cirque walls. Cold air rolled out of the interior, air that hadn't been breathed in a decade, smelling of metal and old fuel and something faintly organic that Hazel chose not to think about.

Inside, the fuselage was tilted fifteen degrees and lit by shafts of light through frost-cracked windows. Seats torn loose. A clipboard floating in a puddle of refrozen meltwater. And lashed into the cargo rack aft, wrapped in canvas and strapped down with a care that said everything about the man who'd done it — a hard case, aluminum, stenciled in fading block letters: E.W. — ASSAY SET 3.

Hazel's breath caught. Not from the cold.

She cut the straps with her knife. The case was heavier than it looked, dense with glass and steel. She set it between her knees on the slanted deck and worked the latches with numb fingers.

Inside: sample vials in foam cradles, forty of them, each labeled in a precise draftsman's hand — grid coordinates, depth, date. Beneath them, a leather folio. She opened it.

Elias Wren's handwriting filled page after page. Assay results, cross-referenced against the official Meridian filings — and they didn't match. Not slightly. Not marginally. The ore grades in his samples were a fraction of what the Syndicate had certified to the permitting board. He'd documented it methodically: which claims were fraudulent, which permits rested on fabricated data, dated and signed and witnessed by a second name she didn't recognize. And then, loose in the back, a letter. Unsent. Addressed simply: *Mara.*

Hazel read the first line and had to stop.

*I was never good at being your father, but I was good at my work, and my work is the only honest thing I can leave you—*

She closed the folio. Sat there in the tilted hull of a dead man's airplane, holding the thing he'd died trying to protect, and felt the full weight of it settle onto her shoulders like snow.

He hadn't been running from the Syndicate. He'd been carrying this. Eleven years, maybe longer, waiting for a way down the mountain and a reason to hand it over. And the Syndicate had buried him — not with bullets, just with paperwork, with silence, with the slow erasure of a man who wouldn't sign his name to a lie.

"They'll come for you," she said quietly, to the case, to him. "You know that, right? They're already coming."

The wind answered through the broken windows.

She repacked the case, cinched it to the outside of her pack where she could reach it fast, and took one last look around the fuselage. On the bulkhead near the cockpit, someone had scratched a tally into the metal — days, she realized. Elias had survived the crash. Days counted in neat little strokes, three groups of five, then nothing.

Hazel touched the marks once with her bare fingers, then climbed out into the light.

* * *

Rusk was waiting at the edge of the crevasse field.

She saw him from two hundred meters out — a dark figure standing on the snow bridge where the glacier's surface fractured into blue chaos below, arms folded, patient as a fence post. Behind him, smaller, two more shapes working down from the ridge she'd crossed at dawn. They'd followed her tracks. Of course they had. There was nowhere else to go.

Her heart did something complicated — dropped, then steadied. Fear sharpening into something colder and more useful.

She could run. North along the moraine, maybe lose them in the icefall. Maybe. With a storm coming and her body running on fumes and forty pounds of truth on her back.

Or she could stop running toward escape and start running toward the range, and the pass, and Coldbrook. Which meant east. Which meant through him.

Hazel walked down onto the glacier.

"Kavanagh." Rusk's voice carried flat across the ice. Up close he looked worse than she remembered — the altitude sitting badly on him, lips gray, eyes watering in the glare. His men flanked wide, one on either side, crampons crunching. All three of them armed. All three of them hating every step of this climb as much as she had. "You've made this very expensive for everyone involved."

"I've heard that before," Hazel said. "Usually right before somebody offers me a deadline."

"This isn't a deadline." He nodded at her pack. "Hand me the case and walk south. You're done. Debt's cleared, no hard feelings, you get to be nobody again. It's the best deal you're going to get on this mountain."

"The case is assay data," she said. "It proves your bosses faked their way into half the Carrow concessions. Permits voided at first snow. Officers indicted. I read the filing requirements in a border-town archive while your friends were burning diesel looking for me." She let that land. "You're not collecting a debt anymore, Rusk. You're destroying evidence. That's a different animal, and you know it."

Something flickered across his face — not doubt exactly. Recalculation. Men like Dade Rusk didn't doubt; they priced things.

"You don't know what's in that case," he said.

"I know what's in the letter addressed to his daughter."

His jaw tightened. One of the flanking men shifted, boots scraping on the snow bridge, and beneath them — audible in the sudden stillness — the glacier groaned, a long tectonic complaint rolling through the ice.

"Last chance," Rusk said, and drew his pistol.

Hazel had spent ten years reading storms. She knew how systems built, where the pressure fell, when the whole sky was balanced on a single point of failure. She looked past Rusk's shoulder at the snow bridge he was standing on — the same bridge she'd have used, the only route east across the crevasse field — and she saw what the cold had done to it overnight. Meltwater had refrozen thin. The sag in its middle. The blue shadow showing through where the ice should have been white and thick.

She also saw that Rusk wasn't looking at the ground. He was looking at her. Men who chased people for a living watched faces, not footing.

"Okay," Hazel said, and raised her empty hands. "Take it."

She unclipped the case from her pack and held it out — and stepped sideways, two paces, angling away from the bridge, drawing his eyes with the movement, with the prize, with everything he'd climbed a mountain to take.

Rusk stepped forward onto the bridge to close the distance.

The ice spoke once — a crack like a rifle shot — and opened.

It happened without drama. No plunge, no scream. The bridge simply folded under him, a panel of white tilting down into blue, and Dade Rusk dropped out of the world between one heartbeat and the next. His pistol went off wildly, the report slapping off the cirque walls, and then there was just the hole, steaming faintly, and the two enforcers frozen at its rim with their weapons half-raised and their faces emptied of everything but arithmetic.

Nobody moved. Nobody shouted down into the dark. Down there was a hundred feet of blue funnel narrowing to a slot no wider than a coffin, and everyone on that glacier understood the math at the same moment.

"He's alive," one of them said finally. Barely a whisper.

"For now," Hazel said. Her voice sounded strange to her, very far away. "Rope him out or don't. But the storm's here in six hours, and if you spend them pulling him up, you're not spending them chasing me."

They looked at her. She looked at them. Then she turned and walked east across the glacier, crampons biting, the case riding heavy against her spine, and neither man followed.

* * *

She made the far side of the crevasse field before she let herself stop, braced against a boulder, shaking — not from cold, or not only from cold.

Triumph was supposed to feel like something. She had the evidence. She had the letter. She had a clear line east to the pass and a head start on two men who would now have to choose between their employer's secrets and their partner's life. By any accounting, she had won.

Instead she kept seeing the fold of white ice, the tilt, the absence where a human being had been standing. She hadn't pushed him. She hadn't even lied to him — the bridge had been failing whether she walked that way or not. But she had known. She had read the ice the way she read storms, seen the point of failure, and stepped aside and let the mountain do what mountains do.

Elias Wren had carried the truth for eleven years and never made anyone pay for it.

Hazel pressed her forehead against the frozen stone and breathed until the shaking slowed.

Then she shouldered the pack, checked the sky — six hours, maybe less, the gray wall already eating the western peaks — and started down toward Coldbrook, carrying the truth and hating what it had cost, praying to a God she didn't believe in that it would be enough.
