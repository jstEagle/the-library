## 10. After the Thaw

The snow went out of the Carrow Range the way it had come in — all at once, like a door swinging open. By the third week of April the passes were mud and running water, and Coldbrook smelled of thawed earth and diesel and the particular sweetness of birch buds splitting. Hazel stood on the courthouse steps in a borrowed coat two sizes too big and watched them walk the Syndicate men out in cuffs.

There were four of them. Not the men who'd chased her across half the range — those had scattered or lawyered up or, in Rusk's case, were still being dug out of the crevasse field by a recovery team that expected to find very little. These were the ones with offices. The ones whose signatures sat at the bottom of falsified assay reports, whose names were on the concession permits that had just been voided by a judge who read ore samples the way Hazel read cloud decks.

The tall one in the gray suit looked up as they passed her. He didn't know her face. That was the strange part. She'd carried his fraud on her back through a blizzard, bled for it, nearly died for it twice, and to him she was nobody — a woman on the steps, squinting in the light.

She decided she could live with that.

* * *

The reward came up in a conference room that smelled of burnt coffee, offered by a regional land board that wanted very much for this story to end cleanly.

"Compensation for services rendered," said the chairwoman, a broad woman named Okafor with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair. "You delivered evidence material to the court. You endured significant hardship. The figure we've discussed would clear your outstanding obligations."

Hazel turned her coffee cup a slow quarter-turn on the table. Outside the window, the parking lot was full of meltwater puddles, each one holding a piece of sky.

"I have an idea," she said. "What if I don't."

Okafor looked at her over the glasses. "Most people in your position—"

"Most people in my position took the money and got out." Hazel set the cup down. "I've been doing arithmetic my whole life. What things cost, what they're worth, what I can afford. And I've learned the hard way that some debts you pay and some debts you carry, and the carrying is the point." She thought of Elias Wren dying at dusk in a ravine, trading the only thing he had left for a stranger's promise. "I'm not saying I'm noble. I'm saying I finally know which kind I'm carrying."

"The debt doesn't disappear because you decline a check."

"No," Hazel agreed. "But it stops being theirs."

They settled, in the end, on something smaller than a reward and larger than nothing: the board covered her medical bills from the frostbite — three fingertips on her left hand that would never feel much again — and signed off on a salvage claim for the truck.

* * *

The truck was where she'd left it, more or less. It had been towed after the storm chase back in the fall, hauled to a impound yard outside Meridian Falls, and it had sat there through the whole winter gathering snow and citations. The yard owner wanted eight hundred dollars for storage alone. Hazel paid it in cash counted out from an envelope, and when she climbed into the cab the seat was cold as a headstone and smelled exactly the way it always had — old vinyl, chain grease, the ghost of cheap coffee.

She'd totaled it chasing a storm for hire money. Bent frame, cracked radiator, a windshield spiderwebbed from a hailstone the size of a fist. But trucks out here weren't vehicles so much as arguments with the landscape, and hers had won enough arguments to be worth rebuilding. She spent eleven days in a rented bay doing it herself, hands remembering faster than her pride did, and when she finally turned the key and the engine caught on the third try with a sound like a grumpy animal waking up, she sat there in the noise and let herself grin like an idiot.

Not free, exactly. Still owing the Syndicate's bankruptcy estate whatever fraction survived the indictments — debts outlive the debtor's crimes, apparently; the lawyers had been very clear. But the collectors wouldn't come anymore. The men who'd given her a deadline measured in days were giving depositions now, measured in years.

She drove north toward Coldbrook with the window cracked and the thaw running in the ditches.

* * *

Mara Wren met her at the edge of town, at the gas station with the leaning sign, the same place they'd first stood facing each other in November when Mara had shoved the case back into Hazel's arms and told her she wanted nothing of her father's lies.

That seemed like a different geological era now.

"You're actually leaving," Mara said. It wasn't a question. She had her arms crossed against the last of the cold, and she looked — Hazel searched for the word — lighter. Not happy, exactly. Unburdened isn't happiness. But it's adjacent.

"Truck runs," Hazel said. "Storm season starts in six weeks down on the plains. There's money in it, if you're stupid in the right ways."

"You'd know."

"I'm an expert."

Mara almost smiled. Then she looked past Hazel at the truck — the patched windshield, the new paint on one fender that didn't quite match, the whole battered honest wreck of it — and something moved through her face like weather.

"They asked me, you know. After the hearings. Whether I hated him." Her father. Elias. Said plainly, no flinch. "I told them the truth. I didn't know him well enough to hate him. He was a voice on bad phone lines and a name on envelopes that stopped coming." She paused. "Then a stranger nearly freezes to death carrying his box through a mountain range, and I find out he was the only honest man in a hundred miles of crooked ones. You don't get to hate someone for that. You just have to live with not getting the chance to know them."

"He knew you," Hazel said. "For what it's worth. He talked about you while he was dying. Not details. Just — that you existed, and that it mattered."

Mara looked away, out at the hills going green-brown at the edges. When she spoke again her voice was level. "It mattered. That's the hell of it."

She reached into her jacket and held out an envelope. "From the court clerk. Your deposition copies, and a letter confirming your testimony's sealed — no Syndicate holdouts can come after you for what you said on the stand. And—" a beat, a small shrug "—my address. In case you're ever passing through and want coffee that isn't gas station coffee."

Hazel took it. Her frostbitten fingers made the motion clumsy, and Mara noticed, and neither of them said anything about it, which was its own kind of courtesy.

"For the record," Mara said, "when you showed up at my door with that case, I thought you were just another person coming to collect on my father. Another bill with a face on it." She met Hazel's eyes. "I was wrong. You were the first person who ever came to give something back."

"Elias gave it. I just carried it."

"You carried it through the Carrow Range in early winter with professionals hunting you." Mara shook her head slowly. "Carrying is the whole thing. I understand that now better than I ever understood anything he wrote me."

* * *

Hazel topped off the tank, bought a thermos of terrible coffee, and stood by the truck while Mara walked back toward town. At the corner Mara stopped, turned, and raised one hand — not a wave exactly, more a salute between people who'd survived the same storm from different sides of it. Hazel raised hers back.

Then she got in, started the engine, and pointed the truck south.

The plains opened up ahead of her, flat and brown and waiting on the weather. Somewhere down there, supercells were already stacking themselves against the dry line, patient as creditors. Somewhere down there was work, and money, and the same old arithmetic — skills worth more to predators than to herself, unless she kept finding ways to be stupid in the right directions.

Her phone buzzed against the console. Unknown number. She let it ring out, then listened to the voicemail on speaker: a woman's voice, clipped and professional, identifying herself as a researcher with a university atmospheric lab. They'd read the hearing transcripts. They'd heard about a chaser who could read a storm cell the way other people read street signs. They had funding problems and instrument problems and a field season starting in five weeks, and would Ms. Kavanagh be interested in a conversation?

Hazel played it twice. Then she pulled over on the shoulder, because some things you shouldn't hear at seventy miles an hour, and sat with the engine idling and the wind rocking the truck gently side to side.

A university. Legitimate. Underfunded, probably, which meant underpaid, which meant — she laughed out loud, alone on the empty road — it would solve absolutely none of her problems.

Which was how she knew it was real.

She called the number back before she could talk herself out of it, and when the woman answered, Hazel said, "This is Kavanagh. Tell me about your instruments," and put the truck in gear, and drove south into the wide bright morning with the thaw water running silver in every ditch, carrying, as always, exactly as much as she could hold.
