## 1. Miles, Not Years

The storm announced itself the way storms always did out here—not with thunder, but with a smell. Green and metallic, like a coin held too long in the mouth. August Sandoval caught it through the truck's open window at 2:47 in the afternoon, forty miles east of nowhere in particular, and felt the old familiar lift in his chest. Relief, almost. Something in the world was moving faster than he was.

He pulled onto the shoulder of a farm road that hadn't seen grading since the last administration and killed the engine. The plains stretched flat to every horizon, wheat gone gold and tired, waiting for a rain that would either save it or flatten it. Above the western edge, the sky had turned the color of a bruise coming up—yellow-green underneath, slate above, and punching through the middle of it all, the tower. Rotating. Organized. Beautiful.

"Okay," he said to nobody, because there was nobody to say it to. "Let's see what you've got."

He worked fast, hands knowing the sequence without consulting him. Laptop on the passenger seat, anemometer mounted on the roof mast he'd welded from scrap pipe three years ago in a shop outside Amarillo. Radar feed stuttering on the cellular connection—he had two hotspots from two different carriers, both paid late, both somehow still alive. He logged coordinates, timestamped, noted the storm's motion: northeast at twenty-two knots, mesocyclone tightening.

*Supercell, discrete. LP structure but she's got a hook on her.*

He typed it into his log with the small satisfaction of a man writing down something true. That was the whole job, if you could call it a job. He sold data to three different outfits—a university lab that paid in installments and apologies, an insurance consortium that paid well and asked ugly questions, and a weather app startup that had already missed two payments. Between them, he cleared maybe eleven hundred dollars in a good month. A good month meant four or five chases, which meant four or five thousand miles, which meant fuel and food and the slow grinding attrition of a truck with 214,000 miles on its odometer.

It wasn't much of a living. But it was a living made entirely of leaving, and August had come to believe those were the only kind worth having.

His phone buzzed against the dashboard. He looked at it the way you'd look at a snake crossing the road—carefully, without sudden movement.

**MERIDIAN SYNDICATE — CUSTOMER CARE**

He let it ring out. Then, because the universe had a sense of humor, it started again immediately.

"Persistent," he murmured, and picked it up on the third ring of the second call, because ignoring them never actually made anything smaller. "This is Sandoval."

"Mr. Sandoval! Wonderful to reach you." The voice was bright, warm, rehearsed—the voice of a man who smiled for a living and had forgotten there was any other way to use a mouth. "This is Tobias, calling from Meridian Syndicate. We're *so glad* we caught you. I know you're a busy man."

"I'm chasing a tornado," August said. "Literally. Right now."

"That's fantastic. That's exactly the kind of initiative we love to see in our members." Tobias said *members* the way other people said *brothers*. "I'm calling about your account. You're currently ninety-one days past due, and I wanted to personally let you know that Meridian isn't like other lenders. We don't punish our members for hard times. We believe in second chances. We believe in *optimism*."

August watched the wall cloud lower itself toward the earth with the patience of something that had done this ten thousand times before. "What do you want, Tobias?"

"Just a good-faith payment. Even a partial one. Two hundred dollars would show us you're engaged, and I can flag your account for hardship review. There are options, Mr. Sandoval. There are always options when you stay in communication. That's what makes us a community."

A community of optimists. That's what they called themselves. He'd signed the loan papers eighteen months ago in a strip-mall office that smelled like vanilla air freshener, after the transmission blew in Wyoming and the bank said no and Meridian said yes before he'd even finished asking. Fourteen percent, compounding, wrapped in language so friendly it took him a week to find the teeth in it. By then the money was spent and the truck was running and the road was open, and he'd told himself what he always told himself: keep moving, outrun it.

You couldn't outrun compound interest. He knew that now. It moved at the same speed everywhere on earth.

"I'll pay you when I have it," August said. "Not before."

"And when might that be? Just so I can note it on the account." A pause, warm as syrup. "We really do want to work *with* you, not against you. But I'll be honest—and honesty is one of our core values—if we can't establish engagement soon, the account moves to field services. And nobody wants that. Field services is where optimism goes to die!" He laughed at his own joke, delighted.

August hung up. It was rude, and it accomplished nothing, and the call would happen again tomorrow or the day after, but there was a moment—just a moment—where hanging up felt like freedom, and he'd learned to take those where he found them.

Outside, the storm had decided. The funnel dropped in a single smooth gesture, rope-thin at first, then thickening as it chewed across a fallow field, throwing dirt a thousand feet into the air until the dust cloud swallowed the column entirely. No structures in its path for six miles. Nobody's home, nobody's life. Just wind rearranging dirt.

He filmed it. Logged it. Sent the clip to the university lab with the subject line *clean rural touch-down, no impact, high-quality footage.* Then he sat with his hands on the wheel and watched it go, and felt the thing he always felt, which was not quite happiness and not quite peace but some cousin of both that only showed up when everything around him was in violent motion.

His father had been a man of one address. Forty-one years in the same house outside Las Cruces, same chair, same coffee mug with the chip in the handle, same view of the same fence line. He'd died in that house, in that chair, and at the funeral people kept saying *he was a fixture, he was a rock,* and sixteen-year-old August had stood there thinking: *a rock is just something that can't leave.* He'd sworn then, standing in the church parking lot with his mother crying beside him, that he would never be geology. He would be weather. Passing through, touching everything, staying nowhere long enough to be blamed for anything.

Twenty years later, the philosophy still held. Mostly. There were nights—parked in a Walmart lot outside some town whose name he'd forget by morning, eating cold noodles while the truck ticked as it cooled—when the philosophy felt less like freedom and more like a very elaborate way of never being anywhere. When the miles stopped feeling like distance covered and started feeling like distance accumulated, a debt of another kind, owed to every place he'd driven away from.

But those were night thoughts. Day thoughts were better. Day thoughts were radar loops and gas station coffee and the pure animal pleasure of a horizon changing.

The storm died over empty country an hour before sunset, raining itself out over rangeland that didn't need it and wouldn't remember it. August drove north on two-lane blacktop, chasing the last light, looking for a place to sleep that wasn't technically trespassing.

He found a county park outside a town called Harrow Bend—population sign shot full of holes, the number illegible—with a boat ramp on a brown river and three picnic tables and not another soul in sight. He backed the truck up under a cottonwood, cracked the windows, and ate a can of chili cold because the camp stove was out of fuel and buying more meant spending money he'd earmarked for fuel itself. The math of his life had gotten that granular lately. Chili versus gasoline. Both essential. Only one got him further from things.

The river ran past in the dark, making its patient sound. He listened to it the way other people listened to music.

His phone lit up. Not a call this time. An email, from an address he didn't recognize, with a subject line that read:

**RE: Your Account — A Proposal (Confidential)**

He should have deleted it. He knew that even as he opened it, thumb moving before judgment arrived, the way he'd once reached for a rattlesnake's tail because it looked like a stick.

*Mr. Sandoval —*

*Meridian Syndicate values its members, and we value creative solutions. We are prepared to discuss full forgiveness of your outstanding balance ($18,441.07 and accruing) in exchange for a short-term engagement requiring your particular skills.*

*A vehicle. Experience in remote terrain. Discretion.*

*If interested, reply to arrange a meeting. Our regional representative will be in your area within 48 hours.*

*Remember: every problem is an opportunity wearing work clothes.*

*— The Optimists at Meridian*

August read it twice. Then a third time, slower, because the third read was where the wrongness lived. Lenders didn't forgive debt. He'd learned that lesson in a dozen small installments over eighteen months. Forgiveness wasn't in their vocabulary except as bait, and bait was only ever attached to something with hooks.

*Your particular skills.* Which skills? Driving? Storm-reading? The ability to be somewhere nobody expected him and leave no trace of having been there?

He thought about the phrase *field services*, and Tobias's laugh, and the way the email said *discretion* like it was a virtue instead of a price tag.

Then he thought about $18,441.07. About the truck that needed tires before winter. About the fact that his three data clients were, between them, currently $600 behind, and winter was the dead season anyway, and the math he did every night in the cab had recently started producing answers he didn't want to look at directly.

The river went on talking to itself in the dark. Somewhere downstream, water was heading for places he'd never see, carrying everything it touched along whether it wanted to go or not.

"One meeting," he said aloud, to the river, to himself, to the version of him that still believed he was free. "I'll hear the pitch. Doesn't mean I take it."

Even as he said it, he heard how it sounded. Every gambler says that. Every borrower says that. *Just one more hand, just one more loan, just one look at the terms.* The road had taught him a hundred ways to keep moving without deciding anything, and this was the newest one: forward, into whatever came next, dressed up as caution.

He set an alarm for dawn, lay back against the seat, and watched the stars through the windshield until they blurred. Sleep came the way it always did out here—fast, total, and temporary, a rest stop rather than a home.

In the morning there would be a message to answer. In two days, a meeting with someone named by an institution that called its debtors members and its collectors a community. He told himself he'd listen and walk away.

He was already lying to himself, and the worst part—the part he'd examine later, on a river far north of here, with consequences at his back—was how easy it was. How practiced. How much the lying sounded like the road: *keep going, keep going, nothing to see here, nothing to stop for.*

The cottonwood dropped a leaf on the hood of the truck. The river carried it off without asking.

August slept, and dreamed of driving, and woke to a signal bar and a decision he hadn't made yet, already half-made, waiting for him like weather on the horizon.
