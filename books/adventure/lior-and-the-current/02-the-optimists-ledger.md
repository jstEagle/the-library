## 2. The Optimists' Ledger

The storm died at 4:17 in the morning, and August Sandoval sat in his truck on a gravel county road watching the last of it walk away across the plains like something that had lost interest in him.

That was the trouble with supercells. They never stayed. You chased them for six hours, you bled adrenaline the whole way, you got your instruments out and your cameras rolling and your heart hammering against your ribs like it was trying to escape ahead of the hail—and then the mesocyclone would decay, or the cell would outrun its own moisture, and the sky would go flat and gray and ordinary again, and you'd be sitting in a truck that smelled like burnt coffee and old vinyl, alone, with nothing but data.

He logged the readings anyway. Peak wind gust 94 miles per hour at 3:02. Hail core to two inches. Rotation tight enough to make his teeth ache. He spoke the numbers into his recorder in the flat voice he used for storms, the one that had no fear in it because fear had been wrung out of him years ago, somewhere between a tornado in Kansas and a foreclosure notice in Ohio.

"Cell dissipating," he said. "Structure collapsing from the northwest. Nothing left to chase."

The recorder clicked off. The silence after was enormous.

His phone lit up on the passenger seat. Not a call this time. A message, the fourth this week, all from the same number. The text was always identical, word for word, like scripture:

*Meridian Syndicate values its community of optimists. Please contact us regarding your account.*

August looked at it for a long time. Then he turned the phone face-down on the seat, started the engine, and drove west into the gray morning, because driving was what he did instead of answering.

---

He'd done the math so many times he could do it in his sleep, and sometimes he suspected he did. Forty-one thousand three hundred dollars. That was the number. It had started as eleven—equipment financing, a new radar unit, a used truck with low miles and high hopes—and then the interest had compounded the way Meridian's interest always compounded, quarterly and merciless, and there had been the season the tornadoes didn't come, and the season they came but nobody wanted footage, and now here he was: forty-one thousand three hundred dollars owed to an organization that called itself a community of optimists and sent collectors who smiled too much.

He knew how they worked. Everybody in the chasing circuit knew. Meridian lent money to people whose livelihoods depended on weather—storm chasers, crop dusters, fishing outfits, river guides—and they did it fast and friendly, no collateral worth mentioning, just a signature and a handshake and a rate that seemed reasonable right up until it wasn't. And when you fell behind, they didn't send thugs. They sent people like Delia Marsh.

Delia Marsh found him two days later at a gas station outside a town called Ferris, population unknown, importance nil. He was wiping bug guts off the windshield when her sedan pulled in beside his truck, clean and silver and wrong for the landscape, like a fish tank set down in the desert.

"Sandoval." She stepped out with a paper cup of coffee she clearly wasn't going to drink. She was maybe forty, compact, wearing a jacket that cost more than his camera rig. Her hair was cut short and practical, and her eyes did something most collectors' eyes didn't do—they actually looked at you. "You're hard to find."

"That's the point of moving."

"I know. I've been following your movement patterns for three weeks." She said it without apology, without menace either, just as fact. "You log an average of four hundred miles a day during storm season. You've slept in thirty-one different counties since April. You have no lease, no utility bill in your name, no forwarding address anywhere in the system." She took a sip of coffee anyway, grimaced at it. "You know what that looks like to us?"

"A man who likes his freedom."

"It looks like a man running from something. Usually himself." She leaned against her sedan. "Can we talk? Ten minutes. Then I'm gone, and you can drive another four hundred miles if you want."

August should have gotten in his truck. Every instinct honed by years of dodging creditors said *get in the truck*. But there was something in her tone—not kindness exactly, but a kind of tiredness, the tiredness of someone doing a job they'd stopped believing in—and besides, he was curious, and curiosity had always been the crack in his armor that everything got through.

"Ten minutes," he said.

* * *

They sat in his truck because she suggested it, which surprised him. "Your territory," she said. "Feels fairer."

She opened a leather folder on her knees. Inside were documents, and August's stomach dropped before he even read them, because documents were how the Syndicate loved you.

"Forty-one thousand three hundred," Delia said. "As of Friday. Accruing daily." She let that sit. "I've read your file, August. I want you to understand that I actually read files. Most of my colleagues skim. You financed your first radar unit at twenty-three. You've never missed a payment until eighteen months ago. You've never defaulted on anything. You don't gamble, you don't drink much, you don't have a family draining you dry. You're not a deadbeat. You're a man whose industry had two bad seasons." She closed the folder partway. "In my professional opinion, you're the kind of client the Syndicate was supposedly built to help."

"Supposedly."

"Supposedly." Something flickered across her face, quick as lightning inside a cloud, gone before it touched ground. "Which is why I'm here with an offer instead of a lawsuit."

August's hands went still on the wheel. "I'm listening."

"The Kestrel River survey team. Six people, contracted through a subsidiary of ours, working the upper watershed under concession permits. Hydrology, cartography, boundary verification—the boring paperwork that makes big land deals legal." She pulled out a photograph and handed it to him. Six faces, sunburned and squinting, standing in front of a canvas tent with survey poles stacked behind them. "Twelve days ago their scheduled radio check-in didn't happen. There hasn't been one since."

"So send someone."

"We are sending someone." She looked at him. "We're sending you."

The words took a second to land, the way a distant thunderclap takes a second after the flash. "I'm a storm chaser. I drive toward weather and take pictures of it. I don't do search and rescue."

"You grew up on rivers. Your father ran freight on the Kestrel before the dams changed the current. You know that watershed better than any guide we could hire, and unlike a guide, you have no local entanglements—no family, no friends, no one asking questions about why you went upriver and what you saw." She said it all evenly, like reading coordinates. "The job is simple. Resupply run. Take a boat up to the main camp, deliver provisions, confirm the team's status, retrieve anyone who wants out, and bring back whatever field materials are ready for transport. Two weeks, maybe less. When you get back—" she tapped the folder "—the debt is void. Zeroed. Forgiven. In writing, before you leave."

August stared through the windshield at the gas station, at the flat horizon, at nothing. Forty-one thousand dollars. Gone. Like a storm cell dissolving.

"And if they're hurt?" he asked. "If something happened to them?"

"Then you render aid within your ability and radio it in. We have medical contractors on standby."

"And if they're dead?"

Her jaw tightened, just slightly. "Then you document the scene and come home. That's all."

It sounded clean. It sounded almost generous. And that was exactly why the alarm bells started, faint and far off, the way he'd learned to hear the first low rumble behind a blue-green sky.

"Twelve days," he said slowly. "Radio silence for twelve days. And nobody mounted a search?"

"The region is remote. Weather's been bad. Comms fail up there all the time."

"No." He shook his head. "I've worked remote concessions. Twelve days of silence on a contracted survey isn't 'comms fail.' Somebody would have flown a pass over the camp by day three. Somebody would have called the regional authority. Who's the concession holder?"

"That's not relevant to your job."

"Who holds the water rights?"

Delia was quiet for a moment. Outside, wind moved dust across the lot in long slow ropes, the way it did before weather changed. "August."

"Because if the answer is 'a Meridian subsidiary,' then this isn't a rescue mission. This is the fox checking its own henhouse, and you're hiring the fox's cousin to do the walking."

Something shifted in her expression—not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone hearing their own private thoughts spoken aloud by a stranger.

"The job is what I told you," she said finally. "Resupply, confirm status, retrieve. That's the whole of it. But I'll tell you something else, off the record, collector to debtor, human being to human being." She turned in the seat to face him fully. "I've been doing this nine years. I've written off debts, repossessed boats, taken a man's dredge away from him the week his daughter had surgery. I believed the pitch once—I genuinely believed we were helping people who banks wouldn't touch, building a community of optimists, weathering storms together. And in twelve days, a contracted survey team goes silent, and the Syndicate's response is to hire a single freelancer with no attachments and no questions, instead of mounting an actual search." She looked down at her coffee. "I can't tell you what that means. I can tell you it doesn't smell like optimism."

"Why tell me that? If I say no, you lose your commission."

"If you say no, I lose my commission." She shrugged. "But if you say yes and something's wrong up there, I'd rather you went in with your eyes open. You strike me as a man who survives by reading the sky. So read this one."

August looked at the photograph again. Six faces. One of them, a young man with dark curly hair and ink-stained fingers, held a rolled map the way other people held infants. Somewhere upstream, those six people hadn't checked in for twelve days, and the only person coming for them was a drowning man being offered a rope tied to a hook.

Forty-one thousand three hundred dollars. His whole future, weighed against strangers he'd never met.

He thought about the numbers the way he thought about storms—coldly, structurally, looking for the rotation. The deal was too good. The job was too vague. The silence was too long. Every instrument he owned was screaming.

And underneath all of it, quieter than any of it, was the simple animal fact: he was tired. Tired of the road, tired of the running, tired of sleeping in thirty-one counties and belonging to none of them. The offer felt like rescue. That was the trap of offers like this—they arrived precisely when you were too worn down to notice they were made of hooks.

"When do I leave?" he heard himself say.

Delia exhaled, and for just a moment she looked like a woman setting down something heavy. "Tomorrow. There's a boat staged at the Kestrel landing, sixty miles north. Supplies are already loaded." She passed him a contract, three pages, and a pen. "Read every line. Especially the parts about what happens to materials recovered from the site."

"'Recovered,'" August repeated. "Not returned. Recovered."

"I told you. Eyes open."

He read the contract twice while she waited, patient as stone. The language was soft where it mattered and sharp where it counted. His debt, forgiven upon completion. Completion defined as: delivery of supplies, confirmation of team status, and recovery of all field documentation deemed relevant by the Syndicate. He signed it because the alternative was another year of running, and because hope, even the cheap counterfeit kind, is still hope.

Delia collected the pages, stood, and paused with one hand on the door frame. She didn't look at him when she spoke.

"One more thing, off the record. The lead cartographer on that team is named Vassek. Lior Vassek. Young, brilliant, stubborn as a mule." Now she looked at him, and her eyes were doing that thing again, really seeing him. "If anyone up there is still alive, my money says it's him. Whatever you find—remember that I gave you the truth as far as I have it. That's more than my employer gave either of us."

She walked to her sedan, got in, and drove south, back toward the world of ledgers and smiling men. August sat in his truck for a long time afterward, watching the dust of her settle.

Then he checked his fuel, topped off his water, and pointed the nose of his truck north, toward the Kestrel, toward the silent camp, toward whatever was waiting upstream of nowhere.

The sky over the range was clear. That bothered him most of all. Clear skies meant nothing was hiding the weather—and nothing ever moved that quietly unless it didn't want to be seen.
