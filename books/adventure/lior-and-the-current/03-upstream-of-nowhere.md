## 3. Upstream of Nowhere

The Kestrel ran the color of old bottle glass in the early light, and August Sandoval hated how much it looked like home.

Not that he had one. He'd spent thirty-one years making sure of that. But there was a reach of river outside Harlow Flats where his father used to moor a leaky aluminum skiff, and water like this—slow, green-shadowed, holding the sky upside down—pulled at something behind his sternum that he'd long ago learned to treat like a bad tooth. You didn't chew on it. You kept moving.

He pushed the boat off the gravel bar at Mile 0 and let the current take him.

The skiff was Syndicate property, like everything else in his life lately. Twelve feet of dented aluminum with a nine-horse Evinrude that coughed twice before it caught, and a laminated card zip-tied to the tiller: *MERIDIAN SYNDICATE — A COMMUNITY OF OPTIMISTS — PROPERTY MUST BE RETURNED IN GOOD ORDER.* He'd read it four times and still couldn't decide if it was funny or sinister. Probably both. Most things the Syndicate touched were both.

The survey waypoints started at the mouth of the Kestrel and climbed upstream like stitches on a wound. Lior Vassek's team had marked them with orange survey tape and stenciled aluminum tags—*KR-01*, *KR-02*, *KR-03*—nailed to trees at eye height, each one logged with coordinates and a date. August had copied the whole sequence onto his own map before leaving Harrow Bend, because that was what you did when you were being paid to find people: you followed their breadcrumbs backward through the world.

Except these breadcrumbs were eleven days stale, and nobody had followed them forward.

That was the part that kept snagging on him, the way a loose thread snags on a barbed-wire fence. Twelve days of radio silence from a six-person government-contracted survey crew, and the concession company hadn't mounted a search. The Syndicate—which, according to Delia Marsh's glossy smile, was merely *facilitating logistics* for the retrieval—hadn't mounted a search either. They'd hired a debt-ridden storm chaser with a boat and a bad knee instead of, say, calling the regional ranger station forty minutes downriver by road.

When he'd asked Delia about it, she'd said, "Companies handle their own internal matters first. Standard practice."

Standard practice. He'd heard that phrase from adjusters after his father's shop burned, from the bank that took the shop, from every institution that had ever held anything of his. It was the sound a door makes when it closes politely.

The river bent north and narrowed. He killed the motor where the shallows went white over gravel and poled, saving fuel, listening to the country come awake around him. Kingfishers. The slap of a beaver tail somewhere upstream. Water talking over stones in its endless indifferent voice.

*You're being paid,* he told himself. *Twelve thousand against the ledger. That's all this is.*

His debt to the Meridian Syndicate stood at forty-three thousand, two hundred and ten dollars as of last Tuesday, accruing interest at a rate Delia had once explained to him with the cheerful fluency of someone reciting a nursery rhyme. Fourteen years ago he'd borrowed eight thousand to replace a chase vehicle's transmission. That was how the optimists worked: they found you at your lowest moment, when the alternative was losing everything, and they made the numbers feel like a lifeline. Then the numbers grew teeth.

Twelve thousand forgiven for one boat trip up a pretty river. Even he didn't believe it was charity. But belief was a luxury item, and August had been living without luxuries for a long time.

* * *

KR-04 sat on a bluff above a meander cut, and it was wrong.

Not the tag itself—the aluminum square was bright, unweathered, screwed flush to a lodgepole pine. Wrong was everything around it. A coil of orange surveyor's flagging lay on the ground beneath the tree, dropped, not hung. Beside it, half sunk in needle litter, a lens cap. August crouched and picked it up, turned it over. No name scratched inside. Survey crews marked their gear like sailors marked their kit; an unlabeled lens cap meant somebody had left in a hurry, or been made to.

He scanned the bluff. The meander below held water so still it looked solid. On the far bank, a game trail threaded into alder, undisturbed.

Eleven days. If the crew had walked out of here under their own power, calm and orderly, there'd be sign—cut branches, boot scuffs at the waterline where they'd crossed, maybe a cache. He worked the near bank slowly, the way he'd work a damage path after a tornado, reading the ground the way other men read print.

He found the crossing point fifty yards downstream. Boot prints in the mud shelf, at least four sets, headed east. Toward the range.

And they were fresh.

August knelt over them longer than he needed to. Rain-softened edges would have meant days old. These edges were still crisp, the mud squeezed up between tread lugs not yet dried to powder. Two days, maybe three. In eleven days of silence, somebody had crossed here recently, moving fast, heading for high country.

Could be the crew. Could be whoever the crew was running from.

He stood, wiped his hands on his jeans, and told himself the same thing he told himself in a truck with a wall of hail coming down: *fear is just information.* Fine. Information received. He got back in the boat.

* * *

By midday the valley walls closed in and the river turned from green to white, pitching over shoals in long brawling tongues. He ran the easy ones, lined the boat through the ugly ones, portaged around a falls that the map called nothing at all because maps are written by people who never came here. His shoulders burned. His knee sang its one note. He ate half a bag of jerky standing in the shallows and watched the water for the thing he wasn't ready to name yet.

Because here was the arithmetic he kept doing and kept disliking: a six-person crew does not go silent all at once by accident. Radios fail singly. People get sick singly. Boats sink singly. For twelve days of nothing, either everyone had left together—and left their camp, which he'd see soon enough—or something had happened to them *together*. And if something had happened to them together, then the men who did it might still be around, and August Sandoval in his Syndicate boat was motoring straight up the throat of it carrying a laminated card that said *community of optimists*.

He thought about turning back. He actually thought about it, honestly, for the length of a cigarette he didn't smoke anymore—he just stood there wanting one, which was nearly the same ritual. Forty-three thousand dollars. Delia's voice: *complete the retrieval, and we consider the account settled.* The word *settled* had sounded so clean in her mouth. Settled like sediment. Settled like a grave.

He got back in the boat.

* * *

KR-07 was where the trail left the river, and KR-07 was where the hurry became undeniable.

The waypoint tag was nailed to a granite boulder at the base of the first real climb, and scattered around it, like the leavings of a camp that had been picked up by its corners and shaken, lay the things a survey crew should never leave: a tent pole section. A sleeping bag, rolled, dry, valuable. A cast-iron skillet too heavy for any sensible person to abandon. A crate of the orange mylar markers they used to flag instrument stations—unopened.

August walked the scatter twice. Nobody abandons a sleeping bag in mountains where nights drop below freezing unless they're trading weight for speed, or running, or both. And nobody leaves a *crate*—not unless the leaving is happening in the dark, in minutes, with no time to choose.

Then he found the map.

It was wedged under the skillet as if someone had weighted it deliberately—a topographic sheet of the upper Kestrel drainage, the standard concession issue, covered in pencil annotations in a small precise hand. Waypoint times. Instrument readings. And along the eastern edge, where the contour lines stacked up into the range like a fist closing, a line drawn in red grease pencil: a route, climbing away from the river toward a saddle between two peaks the map named only with elevations.

The map was torn. Not worn—torn, raggedly, a quarter of the lower-right corner ripped away, and the rip was fresh enough that the paper showed white fiber at the edge. Someone had torn it on purpose. Someone had wanted the route known but not the destination—or had wanted to keep the destination themselves and leave the rest as a message.

Or two someones had disagreed about who got to keep the way out.

August stood in the green cold shade with the map in his hands and felt the unease in his chest change register. It had been a hum all morning, background noise, the static of a job gone strange. Now it was finding a frequency. Becoming a signal.

*Fresh prints heading east. Gear abandoned. Map torn on purpose. Eleven days of silence and nobody looking.*

He was not retrieving anyone. He was walking into the space where people stopped existing, and the Syndicate had sent him because a storm chaser going missing upriver wouldn't make waves. He was disposable, and whatever had happened here, the people who caused it knew exactly how disposable he was.

"Okay," he said aloud, to the trees, because saying it out loud made it smaller. "Okay."

He folded the map into his jacket, took the skillet too—out of spite, mostly, and because a heavy iron pan is a tool—and started up the trail.

* * *

The trail climbed through timber that got older and darker as the day steepened, spruce giving way to fir, fir giving way to wind-bent krummholz at the treeline's ragged edge. August moved the way he moved everywhere: steadily, without ceremony, logging landmarks out of habit. Creek crossing at 1,900 meters, bridge log rotted through. Slide chute at 2,200, old growth scar, decades healed. He'd spent his adult life measuring distance from things—towns, people, the past—and distance was the one skill he owned outright. Every mile up this trail was a mile further from Delia Marsh's smile and the laminated card and the number that owned him.

Except it wasn't, and he knew it. Debt traveled lighter than any gear. It weighed nothing until you tried to put it down.

Near dusk he found the boot prints again, and this time they stopped him cold.

They came out of a side drainage to the east—not following his trail but joining it briefly, a knot of overlapping prints at a spring seep, five or six sets, some large, one small and narrow. Around the seep: a wrapper, a broken pencil, a scrap of flagging tied low on a branch at knee height, the way you'd mark a turn for someone following in the dark.

The small narrow prints headed west. Downstream. Back the way August had come.

All the others headed east. Up. Toward the range.

He crouched there while the light went copper and then gray, working it. Part of the group had split off and gone back down—recently, within his window, possibly passing within hours of his own ascent. One person, by the size of the prints. Running from the others? Sent by them? Carrying something?

Lior Vassek, the file had said. Junior cartographer, twenty-four. The file also said *last confirmed contact day zero, 0640*, in a tone that suggested the Syndicate already knew more than it was telling, files always did.

If the kid had run west, the kid was behind him now. Or hiding somewhere he'd already walked past. Or gone into the river, and the river didn't leave prints.

August looked west, down the blackening corridor of the valley, and felt the strange double pull of it—relief and loss braided together, because a survivor behind him meant answers, and answers meant the job was real, and the job being real meant everything he was starting to suspect about it was real too.

Above him, the first stars came out over the saddle, and under the stars the peaks wore a thin new line of white. Snow. Early, weeks early, dusting the high country like flour sifted over a finished thing.

He made cold camp in the lee of a granite slab, no fire, and ate the rest of the jerky chewing slowly, listening. Wind in the firs. Water somewhere below. And once—distant, upslope, in the hour after full dark—a sound he couldn't classify and couldn't forget: a short metallic clank, like a carabiner tapping rock, followed by silence that had a quality to it. Silence with attention inside it.

August lay with the skillet by his hand and the torn map against his chest and did not sleep much.

In the morning he'd reach the main camp. He could feel it ahead of him the way he could feel a supercell before it showed on radar—that pressure drop, that green light, the sense of a large thing organizing itself quietly out of sight.

He broke camp before dawn and climbed toward it.
