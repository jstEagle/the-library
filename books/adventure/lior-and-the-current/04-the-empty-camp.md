## 4. The Empty Camp

The river told him before the camp did.

August had been reading water for twenty years—the way a current flattened over a submerged bar, the way foam braided against a snag—and the Kestrel was telling him now that nobody upstream was drawing from it. No pump hose trailing into the shallows. No gray ribbon of wash water seeping off the bank. A six-person survey camp drank a lot of river in twelve days, and this stretch of it ran clean and unbothered as if no human being had ever thought to stop here.

He beached the boat below the bluff where the waypoint flag—K-7, Lior Vassek's neat cartographer's hand on the coordinates Delia had given him—was supposed to mark the main station. The flag was still there, or what remained of it: a stub of orange surveyor's tape knotted to an alder whip, shredded by weather or by hands. August stood in the shallows a long moment, letting the cold climb through his waders, and listened.

Wind in the spruce. Water on gravel. Nothing else.

A working camp was never quiet. There were generators and voices and the clank of someone doing dishes badly. This was the quiet of a place that had been *stopped*, the way you'd stop a watch before putting it in a drawer.

He climbed the bank with the pistol loose in his jacket pocket—not drawn, just near—and came up through the trees into the clearing.

The main tent was down. Not collapsed—down, cut from its guy lines in long deliberate strokes, the fabric dragged half into the tree line as if someone had started to build something with it and lost interest or nerve. The mess fly still stood, canted at an angle like a hat knocked askew, and beneath it the evidence of a meal interrupted: three tin plates, food gone to mold and jays, a pot of something fossilized to its bottom. A card game lay where it had died—a hand of five faceup on the crate that served as a table, a losing hand, abandoned mid-round.

August moved through it the way he moved through storm damage, cataloguing, keeping his breathing slow. Radios first. He found the base unit on the comms table and stopped.

It hadn't failed. It hadn't run out of battery. Someone had taken a rock to it—more than once, methodically, the casing stove in, the circuit board cracked through, the handset separated from its cord and thrown ten feet into the brush. He found the second radio in the supply tent in the same condition, and the third in the fire pit, melted past recognition but not past identification. Three radios. Nobody smashed three radios by accident, and nobody smashed them in a panic—one clean blow each, unhurried, thorough. That was the work of someone making sure.

*Twelve days of silence,* he thought. *And Meridian didn't mount a search.*

He'd been turning that over since the boat, the way your tongue returns to a cracked tooth. Delia Marsh had sat across from him in a diner booth that smelled of fryer grease and pine cleaner and offered him forgiveness like it was a coupon, and when he'd asked why the company wasn't already upriver looking for its own contracted crew, she'd smiled her small professional smile and said, *We prefer to let the situation clarify itself.* At the time he'd filed it under corporate cowardice. Standing in the wreck of the Kestrel camp, the file re-labeled itself under something colder.

He worked outward from the tents. Six bunks; four bedrolls gone, taken or carried. One bunk stripped to the frame, mattress slashed open, foam guts scattered—searched, not slept in. And one bunk at the end, nearest the treeline, made up tight with military corners, untouched, a folded wool shirt laid on the pillow like a man expecting to come back for it. No name tape. Small-framed bedding. Whoever that belonged to had left carefully—or been left behind carefully.

The survey equipment bothered him most. Total stations and tripods are worth more per pound than most contraband, and they were all still here, tripod legs splayed in the grass, one instrument case open and empty, another sealed and stacked neat as Sunday. Thieves would have taken them. Vandals would have broken them. Whatever had happened here, it hadn't been about money.

On the doorpost of the supply tent, someone had nailed a strip of birch bark with a single line burned into it, the letters uneven, done in haste with a knife tip heated in coals:

**UP THE CACHE TRAIL. TELL NO ONE ON THE RADIO.**

No signature. The bark was curled at the edges, rain-swollen—old, but not twelve days old. Maybe eight. Maybe less.

August stood looking at it for a long time, wind moving the spruce overhead in slow dark rows, and felt the particular loneliness of a man standing in the middle of an answer he couldn't read yet. Four people had walked out of this camp heading somewhere. One person's bunk said they might not have wanted to go. And somebody had known, eight days ago, that radios could not be trusted—which meant somebody knew who was listening.

His satellite phone buzzed against his hip and he flinched like a man shot at.

Delia. Of course. He watched the screen pulse—MERIDIAN SYNDICATE, the word *community* nowhere in sight—and let it ring out. Then, because silence was itself a report, he thumbed out a text: *At K-7. Camp deserted. Signs of rapid departure upstream. Continuing.* He stared at it, deleted *continuing*, typed *investigating*, deleted that too, and settled on nothing, sending the message bare. Let her chew on the shape of what he hadn't promised.

He went back to the boat for supplies, and that was when he found the boot prints.

They came down the cache trail—the same trail the birch bark pointed up—and they were fresh enough that the edges still held their shape in the mud, hours old at most, small prints, a light walker, someone conserving steps. They came down as far as the woodpile, circled it once, and went back up. Somebody was watching the camp. Somebody had been watching it while he walked through it with his heart going like a two-stroke engine.

August looked up the trail, into the green dark where the ground began its long climb toward the range, and made the calculation every chaser makes eventually: the thing you're afraid of is almost never as dangerous as the thing you're curious about.

He shouldered his pack and went up.

* * *

The cache cave was exactly what the survey waypoints implied it would be—a limestone hollow forty minutes up the switchbacks, screened by deadfall, used by the crew to store resupply drums above flood line. August smelled it before he saw it: woodsmoke, faint and days-old, and under that the sour animal smell of a human body that has stopped caring what it smells like.

He stopped ten meters out and did what he always did before walking into a supercell's inflow—checked his exits, checked his timing, committed.

"Hello the cave," he called, voice pitched to carry without shouting. "I'm not Syndicate. I'm not police. My name's August Sandoval. I came upriver because nobody else would."

Silence. Long enough that he counted his own heartbeats. Then, from the black seam between two fallen logs, a voice—young, cracked from disuse, furious in the way that only terror can make a voice furious:

"Prove it."

"Can't," August said honestly. "That's the problem with proving things. You want me to take my boots off? People who wade rivers don't wear collector's shoes."

Another silence. Then, slowly, a shape resolved in the cave mouth, and August got his first look at Lior Vassek.

The photographs in the personnel file had shown a round-faced kid with ink-stained fingers and a smile that suggested the world was mostly an interesting problem. What crouched at the edge of the dark now wore that face starved down to its architecture—cheekbones like tent poles, lips split from altitude and dehydration, eyes sunk back and burning. Twenty-three, maybe. Filthy. A survey vest hung off him like laundry on a line, pockets sagging. In his right hand he held a machete with a blade rusted halfway to the handle, and he held it the way people hold things they've practiced with but never used—too tight, too high, elbows locked.

"You're alone?" Lior said.

"I'm alone."

"There were four of us." The machete didn't lower. "If there are more of you, I'll hear them. I've heard everything on this mountain for nine days. Every bird. Every rockfall. So if there are more of you, I'll know you lied, and—" He stopped. Swallowed. The threat had nowhere to go and both of them knew it.

"Nine days," August repeated. "You've been up here nine days."

"Ten." Something flickered across the ruined face—shame, maybe, arriving late like everything else in this story. "I ate the last of the cache yesterday. The rice. It had weevils. I ate it anyway."

"How much water?"

"There's a seep. It's fine. It's *fine*." As if August had accused him of something worse than thirst.

August crouched slowly, well outside machete range, and set his pack down between them. He unpacked it deliberately, narrating like a man defusing a bomb: "Oatmeal. Salami, real salami, not the kind that fights back. Coffee. A stove. Chocolate, which I was saving, but I find I'm suddenly generous." He let the items sit there in the gray light between them. "Here's how this goes. You eat. I ask questions. You answer some of them and refuse others, and I pretend that's normal, because I've met people before and they're all like this eventually."

Lior stared at the food the way starving men stare—with hatred, because wanting it that badly is its own humiliation. "Why did you come?"

"Paid to. Resupply and retrieve the Kestrel River survey team, radio's been silent twelve days." He paused. "That was the job description."

"*Was.*"

"The job smells wrong. Twelve days silent and the company sends one man in a jet boat instead of calling the regional office, which is ninety minutes away by air. Either they think everyone here is already dead, or they know something about why, and either way they sent the cheapest possible witness." August kept his voice flat, conversational, the tone he used talking himself through hail cores. "So yes. Was. Past tense is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

For the first time, the machete dipped—an inch, no more—but the eyes stayed hard, and August recognized the expression because he'd worn it for fifteen years in truck-stop mirrors: a person deciding whether the stranger in front of them was a door or a wall.

"You work for Meridian," Lior said. Not a question.

"Everyone works for Meridian. Half the county owes them money. I owe them more than most." He shrugged. "Debt's a leash. Doesn't mean I like the hand that holds it."

"They financed the concession." The words came out fast now, tumbling, a dam going somewhere. "Did you know that? The Kestrel Bend water rights—it's Meridian's paper underneath. All of it. They—"

"Stop." August raised a hand, and something in his own voice surprised him—how much he meant it. "Not yet. Eat first. Because whatever you're about to tell me, I'm going to be carrying it for a long time, and I want to be fed when I pick it up."

Lior looked at him for a long moment. Then the machete lowered all the way, and the boy—for he was a boy, August saw now, a boy who had aged nine days' worth of years in nine days—crawled forward into the light and took the salami with both hands like it was something holy, and ate with the terrible graceless efficiency of the truly hungry, tears running silently down his face the whole time, which he clearly didn't know was happening and wouldn't have been able to explain.

August built a micro-fire at the cave mouth, screened with deadfall, smoke thin as thread. He let the kid eat for ten full minutes before he asked anything, and then he asked only the logistics:

"Four left the camp. You weren't one of them."

"No." Rice grains stuck to Lior's chin. "Renn Okafor—he's the senior surveyor—he went up to the high station two weeks ago to close out the upper transects. The rest of us were supposed to follow. Then Dr. Halloran got a call on the sat phone, and after the call everything changed. She wouldn't say who it was. Two nights later men came up the river trail. Four of them, no markings, but they had—" His jaw worked. "They had the camp layout. They knew where everything was. They went straight to the map chest."

"And the others?"

"Halloran took Priya and Tomas and went up toward the high station to reach Renn. They made me stay behind with the duplicates." Lior's voice dropped to almost nothing. "I was supposed to bring the duplicate ledger downriver to the county seat if they didn't send word in three days. But they watched the river. I tried twice. I got as far as the narrows and there was a boat sitting there, just sitting there, engines idling, like they were fishing except nobody was fishing. So I came back." He laughed, a horrible small sound. "Ten days. I've been hiding in a hole with a machete for ten days, guarding a box of paper."

"Where's the box?"

Lior's hand went to his vest pocket, reflexive, and August watched him catch himself doing it and hate himself for it. "Safe," Lior said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you get." The hardness came back up like a drawbridge. "You said it yourself. You work for Meridian. Everyone does. So you'll understand why I'm not handing the only true map in this valley to the first stranger who walks up the hill with oatmeal."

August nodded slowly. Fair. More than fair. He fed a twig to the fire and watched the flame take it, and thought about Delia Marsh's small professional smile, and the three smashed radios, and the boat idling at the narrows with nobody fishing, and the sentence forming in him that he didn't want finished: *they didn't lose contact with this team—they severed it.*

"One more question," he said. "Then I'll let you sleep."

Lior waited, chewing.

"The note on the doorpost. *Tell no one on the radio.* Who wrote that?"

Lior went very still. When he answered, his voice had changed—flatter, older, the voice of someone reciting a fact that had stopped being frightening only because it had become permanent.

"Dr. Halloran. The night before they left. She said the phones were compromised, that someone was answering on frequencies that shouldn't have anyone answering." He looked at August across the tiny fire, and his sunken eyes were terribly clear. "She said if anyone came upriver claiming to be help, assume they were sent by the people who smashed the radios."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, far off, a branch cracked under weight—maybe a deer, maybe snow sliding off a limb, maybe nothing.

August kept his face still and his hand away from the satellite phone buzzing again against his hip, and understood that he had just walked into the middle of the storm he'd been hired to drive around—and that from here on, every direction was inside it.
