## 5. What Lior Saw

The fire was small enough to hide behind a folded tarp, and Lior kept his hands over it anyway, as if warmth were something that could be stolen back.

August crouched across the flames, chewing a strip of dried fish he'd found in the cache without asking. He'd been asking questions for an hour—gentle ones at first, then less gentle—and Lior had answered none of them.

"Twelve days," August said finally. "Your radio's been dead twelve days. Somebody sent me upriver to check on you, which means somebody downriver noticed you stopped talking. So talk."

Lior watched the fire eat the edge of a pine knot. The light moved on August's face and made him look older than his voice suggested—a weathered man, mid-forties maybe, with the flat patience of someone who had waited out storms bigger than this conversation.

"You're not with the concession," Lior said. It wasn't a question either, but he wanted it confirmed out loud.

"I'm with nobody. I'm paid by the day." August shrugged. "Which apparently makes me trustworthy, in your book."

"It makes you for sale," Lior said. "That's different."

Something flickered across the man's face—not anger. Recognition, maybe. He looked at the fire instead of Lior, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the cave breathed around them: cold stone, old cedar, the faint mineral smell of snowmelt seeping through cracks in the rock above.

"My name is Lior Vassek," Lior said at last. "Junior cartographer, Kestrel River watershed survey, Meridian-contracted. And eleven days ago I ran away while my team was—" His throat closed. He started again. "I need to tell it in order. If I don't tell it in order, you won't believe me."

"Try me," August said. "I've believed worse."

* * *

It started, as most things in the Kestrel valley did, with water.

They'd been in the field six weeks when the second phase of the survey began. The first phase had been ordinary work—channel depths, floodplain elevations, sediment loads along the lower river where the Meridian Syndicate's new concession was slated for irrigation draw. But phase two took them upstream, into the high tributaries, and there the survey changed character. Their supervisor, a soft-spoken hydrologist named Anselm Croft who wore the same gray fleece every day of his life, handed them a packet of historical claims and told them to verify boundaries.

"We're confirming what's already been surveyed?" Lior had asked.

"We're *re*-surveying," Croft said, and would not meet his eyes.

Lior understood why three days later, when he pulled the old permit files from the regional archive copies Croft carried and laid them against their own fresh measurements. The concession's water rights rested on surveys dated fourteen years earlier—surveys establishing that the upper tributaries lay inside land already alienated from indigenous title, land the Riverbend people had supposedly ceded in a settlement agreement signed before the permits were ever filed.

Except the settlement agreement postdated the permits. By two years. Lior checked the dates four times, then a fifth, then sat in the survey tent until dark with the papers spread out like a patient he couldn't diagnose.

The signatures on the old surveys were wrong too. Not forged in the clumsy way—forged in the careful way, the expensive way. The same hand had drawn contour lines on documents supposedly produced by two different surveyors nine years apart. The same idiosyncratic hatching pattern, the same habit of breaking elevation lines at stream crossings. Cartographers have handwriting like anyone else, and Lior had spent his whole adult life reading other cartographers' hands the way priests read scripture.

One person had faked both surveys. One person, or one office, working backward from the answer they needed.

"The permits are backdated," he told the team that night, huddled around the stove while sleet ticked against the canvas. "The whole concession is built on paper that didn't exist when it says it did. The Riverbend claims were never extinguished. They just wrote it so."

Renn Okafor, the lead cartographer, took the files from him and read them twice under the lantern, his lips moving slightly, the way they did when he was being very careful. Renn was fifty-one, precise as a plumb line, a man who had once delayed a project release for six weeks because a benchmark was off by four centimeters. When he finished, he set the papers down and aligned their edges with one another, square to the table, before he spoke.

"This goes nowhere until we document everything," he said. "Originals, chain of custody, our own certified measurements. If we're right, this ends up in front of a commission, and a commission will eat us alive if we arrive with anything less than perfect."

"And if we're wrong?" asked Priya, the field tech.

Renn looked at her for a long moment. "Then I'll apologize to some very powerful people," he said. "But we're not wrong."

Croft wasn't in the tent that night. Lior remembered noticing that—the supervisor's absence, the empty spot where his gray fleece should have been. Later he would understand that Croft had been walking the riverbank alone in the dark, deciding something. Deciding what he was worth. What all of them were worth, weighed against whatever the Syndicate had put in front of him.

Two mornings later, Croft signed the interim certification. Lior watched him do it through the tent flap—watched the pen hover for perhaps three seconds over the signature line, watched the hand steady itself the way a hand does when it has already grieved what it's about to do. Then the pen came down, and the fraud acquired its first legitimate witness, and the paper trail became a paper wall.

"Why?" Lior confronted him afterward, out past the gear cache where the others couldn't hear. "You know what those documents are. You taught half of us how to read a datum. How can you sign that?"

Croft wouldn't look at him. He stood facing the river, hands jammed in the pockets of the gray fleece, and when he finally spoke his voice was so quiet Lior had to step closer to catch it.

"They have my daughter's medical debt," he said. "All of it. Fourteen years of it. They bought it last spring, Lior. Every bill, every collection notice—they own the paper now, and they explained to me, very politely, what happens to paper when it stops being useful." He turned then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly calm. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But you should take your measurements and go home. There are things in this valley older than permits, and some of them have trucks."

Lior hadn't gone home. That was the sin he carried now, the one that woke him at 3 a.m. in a cave smelling of cold stone: he hadn't gone home, and he hadn't stopped Renn either. He'd helped. For five more days he'd helped build the case—photographing originals, logging hash values, sealing duplicates in waterproof tubes—because Renn believed documentation was armor, and Lior had wanted to believe it too.

On the sixth night, men came.

* * *

"—and that's when the radio died," Lior said, and realized his hands had stopped moving over the fire. August had let him talk for twenty minutes without interrupting once, which unsettled Lior more than questions would have. Now the storm chaser leaned forward, elbows on knees.

"How many men?"

"Four that I saw. Maybe more. They came up the river trail after midnight, no lights until the last hundred meters, and then they lit the camp like noon. Flood lamps. They knew exactly where the tents were." Lior's voice came out level, which surprised him; somewhere in the telling it had gone distant, a report about other people. "They weren't robbers. Robbers grab and go. These men went through everything *slowly*. Papers first. Then the hard drives. Then the radios—they didn't smash them right away, they smashed them after checking whether anyone had transmitted. Like they were erasing a record rather than stealing equipment."

"Where were you?"

"In the map tent." The words tasted like ash. "Twenty meters away. I heard Priya shout, and I heard Renn say—very calmly, you could hear how calm he was forcing himself—'Gentlemen, if you have a warrant, I'm happy to discuss the survey.' And one of them laughed. Just once. And then it got—" Lior's jaw worked. "Fast. Ugly. I heard something heavy hit the ground. I heard Priya screaming Renn's name, and then I heard nothing from Renn at all, and I did the thing I will do for the rest of my life, which is that I ran."

He said it plainly because dressing it up would have been worse. He had taken the sealed duplicate tube from the map chest—the one holding the photographed permits, the date analysis, the handwriting comparison—and he had gone out the back of the tent into the alder brush, and he had run uphill like an animal while the camp burned bright behind him and his team's voices scattered into the dark.

"I got to the cache cave by dawn. I told myself I'd go back at first light. I told myself I was protecting the evidence, that going back would just get me taken too, that the smart move—the *responsible* move—was to keep the documents safe." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked sound. "That was eleven days ago. Eleven days I've sat in this hole eating cached oats and telling myself the same story. I don't even know if Priya's alive. I don't know if they took Renn or left him. I ran, and I've been running in place ever since."

The fire popped. August fed it another stick, unhurried, and Lior hated him a little for the calm, and needed the calm more than he could say.

"They'll say you abandoned them," August said. Not cruelly. Testing.

"They'd be right."

"They'll also say you're the only reason any evidence exists." August looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were the color of wet slate. "Both things can be true. Usually are." He nodded at the waterproof tube strapped to Lior's pack. "That's the duplicate. Where's the original? The real ledger, the actual field book with the certified measurements in Renn's hand?"

"High Camp Two." Lior swallowed. "Renn never trusted the main camp with the primary record. He kept the original field ledger at the upper station—said paper should live higher than the flood line, higher than thieves bother to climb. If it's still there—"

"If?"

"If whoever hit us knew about it." Lior's stomach tightened around the oat paste he'd eaten hours ago. "They knew where the tents were. They knew which papers mattered. Someone told them, August. Someone who read our route plans." He didn't say Croft's name. He didn't have to; the silence between them held it.

August was quiet for a moment, turning a stick in the fire. "So the original might be sitting in a station full of men waiting for the next courier to walk in."

"Or it might be sitting in an empty station, waiting for anyone." Lior met his eyes. "That's the gamble. And here's the part you're going to hate: the permits finalize in nineteen days. When the concession term expires and renews, the falsified record becomes settled law—unchallengeable, permanent. The Riverbend lose the water forever, and every page we carry becomes a curiosity instead of a weapon. Nineteen days to cross the range, retrieve that ledger, and get it in front of the land commission at Harrow Bend."

"Nineteen days." August said it the way a man reads a gauge—measuring, not doubting. "Across the Saddleback, in October, with early snow already falling above treeline, carrying a kid who looks like he hasn't eaten properly in a week and a half."

"I'm not a kid."

"You're twenty-three and you flinched when I said *snow*." August rose, brushing off his knees, and began sorting the cache supplies into loads with quick economical movements, already solving the problem, already halfway committed. "Eat the rest of the fish. We move at first light."

Lior stared at him. "Why? Why are you helping me? Who sent you up that river?"

For a heartbeat—one long, strange heartbeat—something moved behind August Blackwood's weathered face, some calculation he performed and declined to share. He glanced at the radio clipped to his belt, silent these past hours, and then out at the darkness beyond the cave mouth where the peaks were building clouds like a debt coming due.

"Nobody sent me," he said. "Get some sleep."

* * *

Lior didn't sleep. He lay in the cave's mouth watching the stars wheel over the black serration of the range and replayed the night raid against the inside of his eyelids until the details wore grooves: the laugh, the floodlamps, the weight of the tube in his arms as he ran. Somewhere below them, down the dark river, were men who erased records. Somewhere above them, in the high cold, was a ledger that could unmake them—if it still existed, and if the two of them lived long enough to reach it.

Near dawn, August's radio crackled.

Lior saw the storm chaser stiffen before the sound fully resolved—a woman's voice, thin with distance, saying something about position, about status, about *the package*. August lifted the radio, and for a moment he held it the way Croft had held the pen, hovering, grieving something in advance.

"Yeah, Delia," August said. "I found him."

Lior sat up slowly in the gray light, and watched the man who claimed nobody had sent him lie—or begin to.
