## 6. Two Sets of Hunters

The radio crackled to life an hour after dawn, while August was still picking frost out of his bootlaces.

He'd slept badly in the lee of a granite shelf above the river, one arm through the straps of his pack the way he'd learned years ago on the plains, where a man who slept loose kept his gear and a man who slept deep kept nothing. The fire was a memory of smoke now, ash raked over, cold. Lior was already awake, sitting at the mouth of the shelf with his knees drawn up, watching the water the way some people watched a door.

"Kestrel Base, Kestrel Base." Delia Marsh's voice came through thin and metallic, shredded by distance and ridgeline. "August, you out there? Come back."

He looked at the radio for a long moment. Then he picked it up, thumbed the transmit key, and made his voice flat and professional. "This is Sandoval. Go ahead."

"Status report. Have you located the survey team?"

"Located the camp yesterday." He kept his eyes on Lior as he said it, and Lior's head turned slowly, like a weathervane finding wind. "Camp's been hit. Radios smashed, records gone or burned. No sign of personnel. I'm following tracks toward the range."

A pause. Static filled it like water filling a boot print.

"Personnel," Delia repeated. "Plural?"

"Can't confirm. Tracks are confused. Could be two, could be more."

Another pause, longer this time. When she spoke again her voice had changed—not softer exactly, but careful, the way a person walks carefully across a floor they've suddenly realized is rotten. "August, listen to me. There's been an update from Meridian. A revision to your tasking."

"I'm listening."

"The Syndicate has interest in any surviving members of the survey team. And in any documents, maps, ledgers, data—anything recovered from the site. They want those items *recovered*, August. Their word. Not rescued. Recovered." She let that sit. "You're to secure both and hold position for pickup. A collection team is moving upriver behind you. Two days out, maybe less if the weather holds."

Lior had come closer without seeming to move, the way animals close distance when they're deciding whether to run. His face in the gray morning light was all sharp angles and hunger, and his eyes were fixed on the radio like it might bite him.

August keyed the mic. "Define recovered."

"You know what it means."

He did. He'd known since the camp—the radios smashed from behind, the cookfire scattered by hands in a hurry, the way the whole place read not like abandonment but like erasure. He'd told himself it was the concession company, some rival outfit, bandits, anything. He'd carried the lie up the trail with him like a warm stone in his pocket, and now Delia had reached into that pocket and taken it out and shown it to him in the daylight.

"Copy," he said. "I'll secure anything I find and mark my position."

"Don't do anything stupid, August." And there it was under the words, something almost human, almost like the woman who'd sat across from him in the Meridian office pouring bad coffee and talking about community and optimism like they were line items. "Just bring it in. Bring yourself in. You do this right and you walk away clean. That's the deal. That's always been the deal."

"That's the deal," he agreed, and signed off, and set the radio down on the stone between them like it was something dead.

* * *

For a while neither of them spoke. The river talked instead, low and constant, the way it had talked all night—a sound he'd hated at first, this water that never shut up, and which he was beginning, against his will, to find less like noise and more like company.

"They knew," Lior said finally. "Before you ever launched. They knew the team was gone, and they sent you anyway."

"Twelve days of silence and no search mounted. Yeah. I noticed that part too. Late."

"Late how late?"

"Late enough that I took the job." He stood, brushed grit off his knees, started repacking with more force than the task required. "I owed them eleven thousand dollars, Vassek. Eleven thousand, plus the interest they compound weekly because calling it interest twice makes it legal. They offered me a clean slate for a boat trip up a pretty river. What would you have done?"

"I'd have asked why a lending company cares whether a survey team comes home."

"And I'd have told you nobody lends money to a man like me out of concern for his health, and then I'd have gotten in the boat anyway." He cinched the pack strap tight enough to bite. "Because that's what I do. I take the job in front of me and I don't ask what's behind it. Asking is how you starve in this business."

"So what are you going to ask me now?"

He stopped. Turned. Lior was standing with his arms crossed, half-starved and stubborn as a fence post, and August realized the kid wasn't asking to be clever. He genuinely wanted to know. He wanted to know what August Sandoval was, now that the shape of him had changed.

"What happened at that camp," August said. "All of it. Because I think whatever it was, it's the reason a debt collector just used the word *recovered* about living people. And I think the documents they want are the reason the people who were here got erased."

Lior held his gaze for a long moment. Somewhere upslope a bird called twice and went quiet, as if it had said its piece.

"Not here," Lior said. "Walking. We make distance first. If a collection team is two days behind us, we don't spend our hours standing still having conversations."

That, at least, August could agree with. He killed the last trace of their stop, checked the wind out of habit—an old habit, a plains habit, checking weather that wasn't there—and shouldered the pack. "Upstream trail follows the east bank to the gorge. After that it climbs. How good are you on elevation?"

"I grew up mapping it. Walking it is different." A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "But I'll keep up."

They walked.

* * *

The trail hugged the river through stands of birch gone gold at the edges, early color, earlier than it should be, and August filed that away with the other small wrongnesses: the frost three weeks ahead of schedule, the light that came slantwise like October pretending to be September. Weather turning early. He'd built a life on reading skies and he didn't like what this one was saying, though he couldn't have put it into words yet. It was a feeling like the pressure drop before a cell goes tornadic—that hush, that greenish cast, the sense of something enormous leaning on the day.

Lior talked as they climbed. Not all at once—he gave it out in pieces, rationed like hardtack, and August learned to hear the pauses as part of the story.

"We were six weeks into the survey when Renn found it. Renn Okafor—he ran hydrology, the senior field man. The concession permits, the ones that let Meridian's client pull water from the whole upper basin—they're dated 2019. Before that, the basin was under indigenous water claims. Unresolved, but on the books. You can't just date them out of existence."

"But somebody did."

"Somebody dated the permits *earlier* than the claims. Backdated them so the concession would grandfather in ahead of every objection. And to make the backdating stick, they needed surveys showing the watershed boundaries as they existed before the claims were filed. Original documentation. Field-verified." Lior's voice went flat in a way August recognized, the flatness of a man carrying something heavy and refusing to set it down. "So they forged them. Forged surveys, forged signatures, forged field notes. And the signature they needed most was the lead cartographer's. Ours."

"Your supervisor."

"Veyra Solano. Yes." A long breath. "She signed one page. One. Under pressure—I don't know what kind, but I saw her face afterward and I can guess. She thought it was contained. A single page in a filing cabinet somewhere. Then Renn pulled the permit file through a contact at the land commission and laid it next to our own original field ledger, the master copy, the one with the real readings, real dates, real boundaries—and the fraud just... unfolded. Page after page. It wasn't one lie. It was a whole architecture of lies, and we were standing inside it."

The trail bent away from the river and began to climb in earnest, switchbacking through scree. August set the pace slow enough to talk and fast enough to matter, and Lior matched it without complaint, which told August the kid had more endurance than his frame advertised.

"The men came at night," Lior said. "Four days ago. No badges, no names. They took the ledger—we thought they took the ledger—and they took Tomas and Priya and Dr. Solano. Marched them downriver. They were looking for Renn. Renn had gone up to the high station two days before with the master ledger, because he didn't trust the main camp anymore, and God, he was right, he was so right—" Lior's voice cracked and he mastered it. "I ran. I heard them coming and I ran and I hid in the cache cave, and I told myself I'd circle back, I'd follow them, I'd do something. And then I didn't. Three days. I hid in a hole in the ground eating survey rations while they—" He stopped walking. Stood very still, facing uphill, not looking at August. "I don't know what they did. That's the worst of it. I don't know."

August stood beside him and said nothing, because there was nothing true to say. *It's not your fault* was a kindness and kindnesses were currency and he was suddenly, sickeningly unsure what he had left to spend.

"They'll say you abandoned them," he said instead. "When this comes out. Whoever tells the story gets to shape it, and the Syndicate will shape it ugly."

"I know."

"And you're still going to testify."

Lior turned and looked at him, and there was something in the kid's face that August hadn't seen in a long time anywhere, least of all in mirrors—certainty that had been tested and hadn't broken. "There's a clock on it," Lior said. "The concession expires in nineteen days. When it expires, the renewal goes through automatically unless it's challenged, and once it renews with the falsified record attached, that record becomes settled fact. Legal bedrock. You can't unbuild a foundation after the tower's up. Nineteen days, August. And the only thing in the world that tears the foundation out is the original field ledger Renn carried up the mountain."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the treeline, and August sat up after Lior slept, turning the radio over in his hands.

He should call it in. That was the arithmetic, the clean simple arithmetic he'd lived by for fifteen years: he owed eleven thousand dollars; the job paid the debt; the job was to recover assets; the assets were a frightened kid and a box of paper. Hand them over, walk away clean, start over. Start over *where?* whispered something in the back of his skull, and he told it to shut up.

Except.

Except Delia had said *recovered* and meant *disappeared*. Except a collection team was moving upriver behind him, and collection teams didn't collect witnesses, they collected silence. Except the camp had been erased and the men who erased it worked for the same outfit that owned his debt, which meant his debt wasn't a debt at all—it was a leash, and the forgiveness wasn't forgiveness, it was payment. He hadn't been hired to save anyone. He'd been hired to bury it. To carry the last witness and the last evidence down out of the mountains in his own boat and hand them to the grave personally, with a smile, with a receipt.

Fifteen years of motion, and he'd never once looked at where the current was taking him.

He thought about the plains, about the supercells he chased, how he'd tell himself he loved the freedom of it, the open range, no fixed address, no one waiting. But freedom and avoidance wore the same boots, and he'd never stopped walking long enough to check which pair he had on. Every job was the next mile. Every mile was away from something. And now here was a kid who'd run from a massacre hiding in a hole, and a hydrologist somewhere up this mountain holding the truth in a box, and nineteen days on a clock, and August Sandoval sitting in the dark holding a radio that connected him to the people who'd built the whole rotten machine.

Delia's voice came back to him. *Don't do anything stupid.* And underneath it, fainter, the sound of a woman who'd once believed in something and was trying very hard not to remember what.

He keyed the mic at dawn, when the light was still gray and Lior was breaking camp with quick economical movements, watching him sidelong.

"Sandoval to Meridian. Sitrep."

"Go ahead, August." Delia. Awake already, or never asleep.

"Camp confirmed empty. No survivors located. Documents destroyed on-site—burned, along with the field records. Nothing to recover. I'm continuing upslope to check the high station per the survey waypoints, then I'll descend and exfil at Harrow Bend."

Static. Long static. He pictured her in some Syndicate relay office, a map on the wall, pins in it, his pin moving upriver toward theirs.

"Nothing to recover," she repeated.

"Fire does thorough work."

Another pause. When she answered, her voice was smooth as river ice, and just as full of things underneath. "Understood. Continue your sweep. Report at the next scheduled contact." A beat. "August. Whatever you find up there—remember what you're carrying. Remember what it weighs."

"Always do," he said, and signed off, and put the radio away, and understood that he had just lied to the Meridian Syndicate with his debt hanging over him like weather, and that the strange lightness in his chest wasn't relief.

It was the first crack in a wall he'd been building his whole life, and through the crack he could hear the river, still talking, still going somewhere, patient as truth.

Lior was looking at him. "What did you tell them?"

"I told them the truth," August said, shouldering the pack and pointing uphill, toward the high country, toward the snowline, toward a man named Renn Okafor and a ledger worth killing for. "Just not all of it. Move. We've got nineteen days and a mountain."
