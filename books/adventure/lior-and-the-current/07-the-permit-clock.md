## 7. The Permit Clock

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Lior finished drawing it out.

He'd taken a flat piece of birch bark from the cache cave's stores and a stub of carpenter's pencil that had lived in his coat pocket since Kestrel Camp, and now he laid the bark on his knee and drew a line across it. A long line, running left to right, like a river on one of his own maps.

"What's that?" August asked. He was sitting with his back against the cave wall, boots off, working at the blisters underneath with a strip of cloth and the grim patience of a man who had done this a hundred times in a hundred places and never once stayed long enough to heal properly.

"Time," Lior said. "Ours."

August looked up. In the firelight his face was all planes and weather, a face that seemed assembled for motion—wind-scoured, restless even when still. He'd been that way since he'd pulled Lior out of the cache cave two days ago: moving, checking, doing something with his hands. Lior understood it. It was easier than thinking.

"Start talking," August said.

Lior touched the pencil to the left end of the line. "Here is today. The fourteenth of October." He'd counted the days carefully during his weeks alone in the cave, scratching tallies into the stone with a survey pin, because keeping time was the last piece of the profession he had left and he'd clung to it like a rope over a drop. "Here—" he moved right, maybe a third of the way along the bark, "—is the second of November. Nineteen days."

"Nineteen days until what?"

"The concession expires."

August stopped working on his blister. "Expires how? Concessions don't just die. They renew."

"They renew if nobody contests them." Lior drew a small circle at the far end of the line. "That's the whole architecture of it. That's why they did it this way. The Meridian water permits were filed against the Kestrel headwaters five years ago under a provisional claim—provisional means provisional, it can be challenged by anyone with standing and any evidence of fraud. But on the second of November the provisional term runs out, and the commission converts the claim to permanent title. After conversion, the record is closed. Sealed. The permits stop being an argument and become a fact." He looked up. "You can't appeal a fact."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, up the dark valley, the river kept talking to itself—the same river that was the whole reason any of them were here, the river whose water had been sold twice before anyone thought to check the receipts.

"And the evidence," August said slowly. "The thing that would make it fraud instead of fact."

"The original field ledger." Lior's throat tightened around the words; he made himself say them plainly. "Every permit application has to be supported by a hydrographic survey. Ours. We ran the lines ourselves, August—we measured every tributary, every seasonal flow, every indigenous water use the old-timers along the valley still maintain. And what got filed with the commission isn't what we measured. The filed surveys are backdated copies. Altered. They erase three generations of downstream use so the concession looks like it's drawing from dead water nobody owns." He turned the bark over, drew a second line beneath the first, shorter, jagged. "This is what we actually recorded. This is what's true. And it exists in exactly one place."

"The high camps."

"You knew?"

"I didn't know. I guessed." August pulled his boots back on, and there was something new in his voice—not warmth exactly, but a kind of grim respect, the sound of a man recalibrating. "Ransacked camp, smashed radios, and you alive anyway. I figured whatever was worth killing for wasn't in the valley anymore. Nobody burns a camp and leaves the prize behind unless the prize is somewhere harder to reach."

"It's at Station Nine." Lior closed his eyes and let himself see it: the little timber hut bolted to the granite shoulder below the glacier line, the iron stove, the map drawers, the smell of kerosene and cold stone. He'd spent eleven days there in July, alone, running the upper gauges. Safest place he knew and loneliest. "We always kept the master records at Nine through the field season. Duplicate sets went downriver monthly; the originals stayed above treeline until season close. Season close never came. The raid happened first."

* * *

He hadn't told anyone this part yet. Not August, not anyone. But the fire was low and the night was long and the arithmetic of nineteen days had a way of prying things open, so Lior sat with his knees drawn up and let the memory come, the way you let cold water into a boot because fighting it only makes it worse.

It was the twelfth of September when Tomas Okonkwo, their lead cartographer, spread both versions of the survey on the mess table and went quiet in a way Lior had never seen him go quiet. Tomas was a man who filled silence—jokes, songs, complaints about the coffee—and when he finally spoke, his voice was almost gentle, which was worse than shouting.

"This isn't our data," he said. "Look at the flow figures for the Alder Fork. We logged peak discharge at forty-one cubic meters in June. This says twenty-six. Somebody took our numbers and shrank the river."

They'd checked it three times that night, cross-referencing against the gauge tapes, against Renn Okafor's meticulous daily logs, against Lior's own hand-drawn charts. There was no mistake. The filed surveys were forgeries built on the skeleton of their real work—good enough to pass a desk review, wrong enough to erase every downstream claim the falsification needed gone. And the signature block carried the certification of their own supervisor, Merrick Hale, dated a full month before the field season ended.

"Hale signed this in August," Renn said. "We were still on the water in August. You can't certify a completed survey from a completed season that hasn't happened."

"He signed it under pressure," Lior had said, and hated himself even then for saying it, for reaching instinctively for the charitable reading, the loyal one. "The Syndicate financed the whole expedition. Maybe he didn't have a choice."

Tomas had looked at him across the table with something like pity. "There's always a choice, Lior. That's what makes it a signature."

They voted to take the originals to the regional commission themselves. Tomas would carry them. And then the ninth of September happened—the night Lior still couldn't approach straight-on, only sideways, the way you look at an eclipse. Engines without headlights coming up the valley track after midnight. Men who moved like they'd rehearsed it, who went for the radios first and Tomas's tent second, and who were professional enough that there was almost no noise at all. Lior had been sleeping in the instrument cache two hundred meters off camp—a habit from a childhood spent around people whose tempers arrived like weather—and so he heard it rather than saw it, and by the time he worked up the courage to creep close enough to see, Tomas was being walked between two men toward a vehicle, upright, unresisting, and the camp was already burning its own paper in a steel drum, and Lior had done the thing he would spend the rest of his life either explaining or atoning for.

He had run. Upstream. Into the dark. With nothing but the clothes he wore and the location of Station Nine burning in his mind like a coal.

He'd told himself it was strategy. Get to the ledger, protect the evidence, come back with help. He'd told himself that every day for thirty-four days in a hole in the rock, eating half-rations to stretch them, listening for engines that never came back, and somewhere around day twenty the telling stopped working and he understood that he had run because running was what his body chose and his body had chosen faster than his courage could vote.

"You could've gone downriver," August said quietly, when Lior finally surfaced from the memory and found the man watching him with those flat, patient eyes. "After the raid. Flagged a boat, found a ranger station, told someone."

"To tell them what? My word against a certified filing?" Lior shook his head. "And I didn't know who to trust. The Syndicate financed our expedition. Their name was on our funding letters. For all I knew, every official between here and Harrow Bend was on their ledger too." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "I trusted a locked box more than I trusted people. That's what you learn growing up the way I grew up. Maps don't lie to you. People do."

"And now?"

"Now I'm trusting a debt collector I met two days ago, so apparently I'm learning nothing."

Something moved at the corner of August's mouth—not quite a smile, but the ground where one might grow. "Delia Marsh isn't a debt collector."

"She works for the Meridian Syndicate. She called your debt 'a relationship.' She used the word *community* without irony." Lior fed a stick to the fire. "Where I'm from, we had a word for organizations that talk like that."

"Let me guess. You're going to say cult."

"I was going to say *creditors*. But cult fits too."

* * *

August was quiet for a while after that, and Lior used the quiet to work the problem, because the problem was the only thing holding him together. Fear was a flood—you couldn't stand against it, but you could channel it, and channeling fear into arithmetic was the oldest trick he knew.

"Nineteen days," he said aloud, pencil tapping the birch bark. "Station Nine is six days' hard travel from here if the weather holds and the passes are open. Call it seven, because the weather won't hold—it's already turning early, you said so yourself, and you read storms better than my barometer does. So seven days up. Then back down to Harrow Bend, where the commission sits—that's nine days downriver on foot along the east bank, or four if we can get a boat, which we can't, because every boat on this river belongs to someone the Syndicate pays." He drew the numbers as he talked, small and precise, a habit he couldn't turn off: 7 + 9 = 16. Two days of margin. "Sixteen days of travel inside a nineteen-day clock. If everything goes perfectly, we make the hearing with forty-eight hours to spare."

"And nothing goes perfectly," August said. "Nothing ever goes perfectly. I once lost a whole chase season to a hailstorm in June. Hail. In June."

"Then we don't have margin. We have a knife edge." Lior looked at the numbers and felt the strange double weight of them—terror and hope in the same hand, indistinguishable. "But here's the thing, August. Here's why I've been living in a hole eating crackers for a month instead of giving up. Before the second of November, one honest document can tear the whole concession apart. The commission *has* to hear us—provisional claims carry a statutory challenge window, and the challenge doesn't need lawyers, it needs evidence and standing, and Renn and I have standing as the surveyors of record. One ledger, entered before expiry, and the permanent title dies in committee." He tapped the circle at the end of the line. "After that date, the same ledger is just a curiosity. Historical interest. The record's sealed, the water's titled, and the Syndicate's lawyers will spend thirty years making sure no court ever reopens it. We wouldn't be carrying evidence anymore. We'd be carrying a grievance."

"So it's not just a deadline," August said. "It's a door."

"A door that closes forever." Lior stared into the fire. "Do you understand what they built? They didn't steal the water. Stealing gets investigated. They built a machine where waiting does the stealing for them—where every day that passes makes the crime more legal. All they had to do was silence one survey team and be patient." His voice hardened. "Patience is the most violent thing rich people ever invented."

August huffed something that might have been agreement. Then: "The others. Your team. If we find them—"

"We won't." Lior said it flatly, because he'd earned the right to say it flatly, having rehearsed it in the dark for a month. "Tomas is gone. The others scattered or worse. Renn might be alive—he was running the upper gauges when it happened, he might have gone to ground the way I did—but I've had thirty-four days to hope about Renn and hoping about Renn is like betting on weather." He paused. "No offense."

"Some taken. Weather's the only thing I've ever bet on that paid."

"Then you'll like our odds," Lior said, and heard, to his surprise, that his voice had steadied. "One ledger. Nineteen days. Seven days of the worst country in the territory between us and it."

He looked down at the birch bark, at the little line with its circle at the end, and thought about how he'd spent his whole life drawing maps of places—every contour faithful, every label true—and how none of it had prepared him for drawing a map of time, where the terrain was hope and the elevation dropped toward a cliff with a date on it.

August stood, rolled his shoulders, and looked out the cave mouth at the valley, where the peaks upstream had put on fresh white caps since morning, early and wrong, winter arriving ahead of its schedule like everything else in this story.

"Seven days," August said. "Then we'd better walk fast."

Above the tree line, the first snow of the season had already begun to fall.
