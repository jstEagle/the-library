## 8. The Range Ahead

The range announced itself three days before we reached it.

We came down out of the burned timber at first light, and there it was across the valley floor: a wall of gray and white running north to south as far as the eye could follow, the high peaks still holding last winter's snow in their couloirs like old debts nobody had collected. The Kestrel headwaters spilled out of it in a braid of silver channels, and somewhere up in that stone maze was a survey station with a ledger that could unmake a fortune.

Lior stood beside me at the treeline with the pack straps creaking and didn't say anything for a long time. Then: "It's bigger than the maps make it look."

"Maps always lie about size," I said. "They tell the truth about shape. Learn which one you're looking at."

He glanced at me sideways, that quick assessing look he had, like he was triangulating me from two known points. "You've crossed it before."

"Twice. Once chasing a hail core that never materialized, once running from a woman in Cutbank whose husband owned a rifle and a grudge." I shouldered my pack. "The second crossing taught me more."

The valley floor took us most of the day—tussock grass gone gold and brittle, the river chattering alongside us like a nervous companion. By afternoon the light had changed. Anyone who'd spent enough years under open sky felt it before they saw it: the quality of blue deepening, the air taking on a glassy stillness that wasn't peace but pressure. I stopped at a creek crossing, drank, and watched the western horizon where a line of cloud was building over the far ridges—flat-based, anvil-topped, organized.

"We camp early tonight," I said.

"It's barely four."

"And by ten tonight that," I pointed with my chin, "will be sitting on top of us. See how the base is flat? See how it's got that hard edge, like someone drew it? That's not weather drifting around looking for something to do. That's weather with an agenda."

Lior looked at the cloud, then at me. "How do you know?"

"Twenty years of being wrong in interesting places." I found a stand of spruce backed against a gravel bar, wind-sheltered, above the flood line. "Come on. I'll show you what the sky does before it does it."

* * *

I built the fire small and taught him while we ate.

Not from books—I'd read the books, back when I still believed knowledge could be accumulated instead of survived—but from the sky itself, pointing with a stick like a lecturer at a university with no walls.

"First thing: clouds have families. You learn the family, you know the temperament." I traced the building cumulus in the west. "That one's born of heat. It'll grow all afternoon, go up like a fist, and either rain itself out or turn mean. You watch its top. If it starts spreading flat, going glassy on top—that's it punching through into the upper winds. That's when a storm stops being weather and becomes a creature."

"What kind of creature?"

"The kind that drops hail the size of your fist and lightning that finds you no matter where you hide." I fed a branch to the fire. "Second thing: wind lies at ground level. It bends around hills, funnels through gaps. The truth is overhead. Watch the clouds' direction against the surface wind. When they disagree, the weather's turning. When they agree hard—same direction, same speed—the front's already here."

Lior listened the way he did everything, completely, his whole attention a physical weight. He asked questions that weren't really questions—"Why does the anvil spread downwind?"—and then answered them himself half a beat later, working the physics aloud, and I realized I was talking to someone who'd spent his life doing with paper what I did with sky: reading systems, finding the pattern underneath the noise.

"Your turn," I said, when the fire had burned down to coals and the first cold drops were ticking through the spruce canopy right on schedule. "Show me a map."

He unrolled the survey sheets from their oilcloth, weighted the corners with stones, and turned his headlamp low so the light fell soft across the contour lines.

"Everyone reads maps like instructions," he said. "Go here, cross there, elevation this, distance that. But a map is a story somebody wrote with lines. You just have to hear the voice." His finger settled on the contours below our position. "See these lines, how they're packed tight together? That's not just 'steep.' That's a cliff face that sheds rock every spring melt. Nobody says that on the map. But the map knows. Look—here the lines widen, then pinch again in a long narrow finger. That's a ridge spine. Dry ground, good travel, exposed to wind. And this—" the finger moved to a broad amphitheater of nested curves, "—this is a basin. Snow collects here late into summer. Water pools. If you're ever caught out, a basin will kill you with cold faster than a summit will, because cold air drains downhill and fills it like a bowl filling with soup."

"You talk about it like it's alive."

"It is alive. Rivers move. Valleys deepen. A map is just a portrait taken on one bad hair day." He smiled, and in the firelight it transformed him—not the hollow-eyed kid I'd pulled out of a cache cave anymore, but someone lit from inside. "My grandmother taught me. She mapped the Kestrel country before any of the concession surveys existed. She said a mapmaker's job isn't to draw the land. It's to listen to it until it tells you where it's been."

I thought about that longer than I meant to. Twenty years of reading storms, and I'd never once thought of the sky as telling me where *it* had been. I'd only ever asked it where it was going.

"Your grandmother," I said. "She indigenous?"

"Lutsel K'e, on her side. The Syndicate's lawyers called her claims 'unsubstantiated traditional use.'" His voice went flat in a way I recognized, because I'd heard it in my own mouth talking about my father. "Then they backdated permits over the ground she walked her whole life. Substantiated that."

The rain thickened. We crawled into our shelters—his a scrap of silnylon, mine a tarp older than he was—and I lay listening to the storm work the ridge above us, cataloging its sounds the way other men counted sheep, and I understood something I hadn't let myself understand yet: I wasn't hauling this kid across a range for debt forgiveness. Maybe I'd told myself that. Maybe Delia had made it easy to believe. But lying in the dark listening to Lior breathe slow and even two yards away, I knew the ledger was only half the cargo now.

* * *

The storm passed by midnight and left the world scoured and glittering. We climbed.

The switchbacks began where the valley pinched shut, and they were the work of some forgotten crew decades dead—stone steps crumbling into the slope, grade steep enough that conversation became breathing and breathing became arithmetic. Count to sixty. Rest. Count again. Lior set a pace that surprised me, steady and metronomic, and when I adjusted to match him I realized he'd done it deliberately, letting me lead the first hour and then quietly taking the rhythm over.

"You've climbed before," I said at the first rest stop, not quite a question.

"Survey teams don't hire people who can't." He unwrapped a strip of dried fish and tore it in half, offering me the bigger piece without making a ceremony of it. "Renn used to say the mountain doesn't care about your schedule. So you make the mountain's schedule yours."

"Renn Okafor." I'd gotten fragments out of him over the past days, each one pried loose like a fossil from matrix. "He the one who stayed behind at the high station?"

Something closed in Lior's face. "He stayed. I ran. We've covered this."

"We've covered that you ran. We haven't covered why you think that makes you the worst person in the story."

"Because it does." He stood, shouldered his pack, and started climbing, and I let it go because the mountain doesn't care about your schedule and neither, apparently, does guilt.

By midday we'd gained the shoulder of the first ridge and the world opened up—range after range falling away westward in blue receding waves, the air thin and clean enough to taste. And then the trail dropped toward the river gorge, and I saw the bridge.

"Oh," Lior said.

"Yeah."

It hung maybe eighty feet above water that boiled white between granite walls, a span of cables and rotted planking strung anchor to anchor across the gap. Even from fifty yards I could see the state of it: cables furred with rust, several of the vertical hangers snapped and dangling, planks missing in runs of three and four like teeth knocked out of a jaw. The hand ropes sagged in a way that suggested they were holding opinions rather than tension.

"That's not on the map as impassable," Lior said, already pulling the sheets out.

"No, it wouldn't be. Maps are portraits, remember? This one's aged badly." I studied the gorge walls while he studied the paper. Downstream, the river slammed through a slot no wider than a wagon; upstream it bent out of sight. Climbing out of the gorge here would cost us a full day and put us on scree slopes I didn't like the look of with weather building again in the southwest. "Options. One: we go around and lose a day we may not have. Two: we cross that."

"Three: we cross it smart."

I looked at him. He was already walking toward the near anchor, and there was nothing hesitant in his stride.

We spent an hour on it, and it was the best hour of engineering I'd witnessed outside a storm chase rigging session. Lior tested every hanger by loading it with both our weights via a sling before we trusted it. He identified which cables carried load and which were decorative relics, traced the anchor stonework for movement cracks, and mapped the plank pattern like a route—left foot here, skip two, right foot there, hands on the upper cable the whole way, weight never fully committed to wood that might be powder inside its bark.

"Unbuckle your pack's hip belt," he said. "If you fall, you want to shed it, not drown wearing forty pounds of gear."

"When did you learn this?"

"River crossings. Survey work is mostly figuring out how not to die in beautiful places." He clipped a short sling between us—not connecting us, he was careful to say, just a guide rope he could pay out. "You go first. You're heavier. If a plank goes under you, I'll know it's rotten before I trust it."

"That's either very brave or very cold."

"My grandmother would say it's just mapping." He almost smiled. "Testing the ground before you ask someone else to stand on it."

I crossed. The gorge roared up at me through the missing planks, spray cold on my knuckles, the whole structure swaying with a rhythm I matched breath to breath—count to four, step, count to four. Halfway across, a plank I'd already weighted cracked along a hidden fault and dropped spinning into the white water, and my stomach dropped with it, and my hands on the cable were suddenly the only true things in the world. I finished. I didn't look down again.

Lior came across lighter and quicker, flowing over the gaps like he'd memorized the bridge from the near side, which I realized he had—he'd stood there for an hour drawing it into himself, contour lines and all.

On the far side we sat with our backs against warm granite and ate and didn't talk much, and the not-talking was different from before. Less like two strangers conserving words. More like two people who'd run out of need for them.

* * *

The weather turned early. That was the sentence the whole range kept repeating.

We'd planned on six days of autumn window. What we got, starting the third morning, was a sky that couldn't decide its age—one hour of hard blue, then a deck of cloud sliding in from the southwest with snow in its belly, then clearing, then cloud again, each cycle colder than the last. Winter wasn't coming to the range. Winter was already living here, descending daily from the summits to check on things, and every day it checked a little lower.

I taught Lior to read it as we climbed. He learned fast—faster than anyone I'd taught, though the list was short—and within two days he was calling changes before I did.

"Cirrus this morning moving against the surface wind," he said on the fourth day, squinting up from beneath his hood. "And the pressure feels wrong. My ears keep popping. Snow by evening?"

"Snow by midafternoon." I grinned despite myself. "Look at you."

"I had a good teacher who yells when I'm wrong."

"I don't yell."

"You communicate loudly."

In return he remade the world for me. Each night in camp he'd spread the maps and walk me through tomorrow's terrain as narrative: *here the glacier came down two hundred years ago and the ground is still confused about it, so expect hummocks and hidden meltwater; here the trail builders cut the grade along a fault line because the rock was broken and easy, which means rockfall, so we pass early before the sun thaws the ice holding it.* He read the land the way I read clouds—as a history of forces, everything currently visible just the present tense of an old argument.

"You know what I figured out?" he said the fourth night, firelight on the pages. "Storms and rivers are the same thing. Both of them are just terrain deciding where energy goes. You chase one and I draw the other. We're doing the same job."

"Yours has better hours."

"Mine has worse clients."

We laughed together, actually laughed, and the sound went up into the cold air and didn't come back, and I sat with the strange warmth of it and didn't examine it too closely. Trust, I'd learned, was like footing on scree—you didn't find it by looking for it. You found it by moving, testing, moving again, until one day you noticed you'd stopped checking every step.

* * *

The fifth day we topped out below the high country and saw the upper station's tin roof glinting in a saddle two ridges east—a speck of civilization nailed onto all that wilderness, absurd as a stamp on a glacier.

Also on the fifth day, at the last overlook before the descent, Lior went still beside me.

"What," I said.

He didn't answer. He was looking back down the trail we'd climbed, at a thread of switchbacks dropping through the burn scar toward the river gorge. At a point on that thread, tiny with distance, something moved. Not an animal's gait. Not weather.

I raised my binoculars. Glassed the switchbacks. Found them empty. Glassed the treeline beyond, the gravel bar, the far side of the gorge where the rotten bridge swayed in the wind.

Empty. All of it.

But the bridge was wrong. I'd memorized its sag, its list, its particular broken-toothed profile, the way I memorized every storm I ever chased. Someone had crossed it recently—recently enough that the fresh-cut saplings lashed to the worst gaps as temporary repairs were still pale and bright with bark.

Nobody repairs a bridge they don't plan to use twice.

"Pack up," I said quietly. "New pace. We reach the station before dark or not at all, but we reach it moving like the weather's after us."

Lior shouldered his pack without a word. Below us, far down the thread of the trail, the wind moved through the burn scar, and for half a second I could have sworn I saw the trees bend around something that wasn't wind at all.
