## 9. Delia on the Trail

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and Delia Marsh stood at the treeline watching the mud tell her everything she needed to know.

Two sets of boot prints, one long-strided and confident, one shorter, favoring the left foot. The kid was limping. Not badly—nothing that would slow them past tomorrow—but enough that August Sandoval would have noticed, and enough that he'd be rationing the pace without saying so. That was the kind of man he was. She'd known him for six years and he'd never once complained about a bill, a wound, or a forecast. He just adjusted and kept moving, like a river deciding around a stone.

"He went up," said Voss behind her, breathing hard from the climb. He was the bigger of her two collectors, a former dockworker with hands like mooring line and the imagination of a receipt. "Switchbacks. We could cut straight up through the pines."

"We could," Delia said, "and we could lose the trail in scree by noon and spend three days finding it again while they get to the high camps." She crouched, touched the print, pressed her own boot beside it. Half a size smaller than hers. The kid weighed nothing. "We follow their path. Exactly. Sandoval doesn't waste steps, so neither do we."

Brannick, the third of them, laughed softly from the rocks above. He was younger, quicker, meaner in the way the Syndicate liked its collectors mean—not cruel exactly, but hungry, the kind of man who read every contract as a menu. "You know his stride that well, Marsh?"

"I've repossessed four of his vehicles," she said, standing. "I've sat across a folding table from him eleven times. I know how he walks away from things." She didn't add: *and toward them.* "Move."

They climbed.

* * *

The Meridian Syndicate had recruited her twenty-two years ago, when she was nineteen and standing in a food bank line in Corvane with her mother's medical debt folded in her coat pocket like a letter she couldn't bear to reread.

The recruiter's name was Aldous Wren, and he hadn't looked like a lender. He'd looked like a teacher, gray-bearded, patient, with a voice that made waiting in line feel like a temporary condition rather than a life sentence. He'd bought her coffee. He'd asked about her mother. And then he'd said the sentence that rebuilt her whole world:

*"Debt is just the future arriving early, Miss Marsh. Most people run from it. Our community runs toward it—together. We call ourselves optimists because we believe every person is worth investing in. Even you. Especially you."*

She'd signed. Of course she'd signed. Nineteen years old, terrified, and someone had finally told her she was an investment instead of a burden.

It took her nine years to understand what *investment* meant when the investor controlled the ledger, the terms, and the definition of default. It took her another six to stop flinching at the word *community.* But she'd never stopped being good at the work, and the Syndicate had a genius for making competence feel like loyalty. Every year she stayed was another year of proof that she'd been right to sign. Leaving would have meant admitting she'd been wrong since the beginning, and Delia Marsh had never once in her life been able to afford that particular expense.

So she collected. Vehicles, mostly, at first. Then contracts. Then people.

And now this.

* * *

By midmorning they'd gained the ridge where the switchbacks began, and Delia called the first halt. Not for rest—for radio.

She climbed above the others, keyed the unit, and gave the scheduled check-in. The Syndicate's regional office acknowledged with coordinates, weather advisories, and then the addendum that had come down overnight, flagged priority:

**RE: SANDBAG CONTRACT 77-K. SUBJECT SANDOVAL TO CONFIRM RECOVERY OF ASSETS (DOCUMENTARY) AND PERSONNEL (VASSEK, L.). RETRIEVAL PRIORITY: DOCUMENTS FIRST. PERSONNEL SECONDARY. HANDLE ACCORDINGLY.**

*Handle accordingly.*

She'd been reading Syndicate language for two decades. She knew what *handle accordingly* meant the way a surgeon knows what the scalpel is for. Two weeks ago the contract had said *recover personnel intact.* Last week it had said *secure cooperation.* Now it said *handle.*

She thought about Lior Vassek. Twenty-three years old according to the file, a cartographer who'd drawn maps for the Kestrel survey, who had presumably seen whatever the survey team had seen, and who now weighed maybe forty kilos soaking wet and limped on the left foot. *Handle accordingly.*

"Problem?" Brannick had come up beside her without a sound. He did that. She hated that he did that.

"Weather window's closing," she said. "Early snows. If we don't have them before the saddle, nobody gets over."

"That's not what your face says."

"My face says my boots leak."

He grinned and dropped back down to Voss, and Delia stood a moment longer with the radio against her hip, looking north at the range where the clouds were already stacking up white and heavy, too early, too thick, the sky filing paperwork ahead of schedule.

Then she followed her own collectors back onto the trail, and hated how naturally her feet found it.

* * *

They caught sight of the pair at dusk, far below on the second switchback system—a speck of movement through a gap in the trees, firelight where there shouldn't have been firelight. August was burning wood. Sloppy, for him. Which meant the kid needed the warmth more than they needed the stealth, which meant the kid was worse off than the limp suggested.

Delia glassed the camp through the field scope and felt something in her chest pull tight and strange.

August had built the fire small, ringed with wet stones to kill the light. He'd strung the tarp low against the wind. And he was sitting with his back half-turned to the boy, angled so that his own body blocked the wind—a thing you did for someone you were protecting, not someone you were transporting. He was talking. She couldn't hear the words at that distance, but she could see the shape of it: hands moving, drawing lines in the air. Storm shapes. Cloud anatomy. He was teaching the kid to read weather.

Twenty-two years ago, Aldous Wren had drawn lines in the air for her too. Had explained the architecture of the future, the beautiful load-bearing logic of it all. *Every person is worth investing in.* And she had believed him with her whole starving heart, and the belief had been the most expensive thing she ever purchased.

"You're smiling," Brannick said beside her. She hadn't heard him move. Again.

"Am I."

"At Sandoval. You always get that look when it's him." He settled into the rocks, patient as a trap. "Six years of chasing that man's paper. There's easier money. You keep taking his file."

"His files are clean. Clean files are fast files."

"That's not it either." He watched her, and there was nothing stupid in his eyes; she'd never once made the mistake of thinking there was. "You know what I heard, back at the office? Before we deployed. The concession people came to Meridian. Not the other way around. Meridian financed the whole Kestrel water deal from the ground floor. The permits, the surveys, the lawyers—all of it, our capital." He picked up a pebble, turned it over. "And now there's a problem with the paperwork, and suddenly we've got collectors on a mountain instead of auditors in a room. You ever ask yourself why fraud gets handled by recovery teams?"

"Because recovery teams are discreet."

"Because recovery teams are deniable." He tossed the pebble away. "I'm not asking questions, Marsh. I'm telling you I can count."

She said nothing. Below, the small ringed fire burned, and two figures sat inside its circle of wind-shadow, and one of them was teaching the other to survive.

"Get some sleep," she said finally. "We take them at first light, on the trail, before the terrain gets serious. Nobody gets hurt if everybody does what they're good at. Sandoval's good at surviving. Let him do it somewhere with a roof."

"And the kid?"

"*Handle accordingly,*" she said, and heard her own voice go flat as a ledger line, and hated it.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat up under the pine cover with the scope across her knees and watched the fire burn down, and let herself remember the day she'd stopped believing.

Not the day she'd learned—the learning had taken years, a slow seep, like water finding every crack in a foundation. The day she'd *stopped believing* was specific. A Tuesday. A woman named Ondine Ferro, fifty-eight, a seamstress in Corvane, three payments behind on a loan she'd taken to bury her husband. Delia had been sent to restructure the debt, which in Syndicate language meant extend it, deepen it, roll the interest forward until the principal became a rumor. Ondine Ferro had listened to the whole recital, nodded politely, and then asked one question:

*"If your community believes in me so much, why does everything I pay make me owe more?"*

And Delia had opened her mouth to give the trained answer—the compounding answer, the risk-adjustment answer—and found that there was no sentence in her body honest enough to carry it. She'd stood in that sewing room surrounded by thirty years of finished dresses, every one of them evidence of a woman who worked, and worked, and worked, and she had felt the optimism pitch die in her chest like a bird hitting a window. Not dramatically. Just—stopped.

She'd restructured the loan anyway. She'd done her job anyway. That was the part she'd never forgiven herself for: not the believing, but all the years of continuing after the belief was gone.

Now she sat on a cold mountain with orders to *handle accordingly* a limping cartographer, and below her the man she'd spent six years collecting from was giving away his knowledge for free to a stranger, and Delia Marsh understood—with the terrible clarity that only comes at altitude, at night, in the cold—that she was on the wrong side of the scope.

Not the wrong side of the law. The Syndicate was very careful to stay on the right side of the law, or to write the side it needed. But the wrong side of the thing underneath the law, the thing the law was supposed to be a rough draft of. Ondine Ferro's question lived there. So did the truth about the Kestrel permits, whatever it was. So did a young cartographer with a bad left foot who had run from something instead of selling it.

"First light," Brannick murmured from the dark, already half-asleep. "Trail ambush. Clean and quiet."

"Clean and quiet," she agreed.

But she lay awake mapping the switchbacks in her head, and somewhere in the third hour she noticed what she was doing: not planning the ambush. Planning the counter to it. Where August would see them coming if he kept his usual watch. Which route the kid could manage on that foot. Where the terrain would let two people become three shadows instead of two targets.

She told herself she was simply being thorough. A good collector knew her subject's capabilities. That was all.

The snow line had crept visibly lower down the peaks since morning. By dawn it would touch the high camps. Somewhere up there was a ledger, or a grave, or both, and Delia Marsh had twenty-two years invested in an institution that wanted her to bring back paper and silence.

She closed her eyes and saw Aldous Wren drawing lines in the air, and for the first time she wondered what he'd been drawing *around*—what shape he'd been tracing so she wouldn't look inside it.

At first light, the mountain answered for her. Because when they moved down onto the switchbacks to set the ambush, the trail was empty—and August Sandoval had broken camp in the dark, gone cold and silent, and left them a message written in the only language Delia had ever truly trusted: tracks heading straight up into the storm country, unhurried, unhidden, and impossible to follow without becoming part of the weather.

He'd known they were there. He'd known all along.

And he'd chosen not to run sideways, not to hide—but to lead them higher, into the one place the Syndicate's reach thinned to nothing, carrying whatever truth the mountain still held.
