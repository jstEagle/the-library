## 10. The High Camp Ledger

The upper survey station sat in a bowl of granite like a cupped hand that had decided, long ago, to stop holding anything.

August saw it first as a smudge of rust against the gray scree—a corrugated roof half-collapsed under an early snow load, one wall peeled back to the weather like a page turned too far. Below it, the valley they'd climbed out of all morning lay blue and shadowless, the Kestrel reduced from up here to a bright thread someone had dropped and never bothered to pick up.

"Wind's shifting," Lior said beside him. She'd said little for the last two hours, conserving breath the way she conserved everything—food, words, trust. But her eyes were working, sweeping the station's approaches with a cartographer's habit of reading ground before trusting it. "Northeast now. It'll cover our sound if we come around the east side."

"You want to sneak up on a building."

"I want to not die next to one." She adjusted the strap of the pack she refused to let him carry anymore. "The men who came to camp at night didn't shout. They didn't knock. If anyone's up there, we don't announce ourselves."

August studied her a moment. Eleven days ago he'd pulled her half-frozen out of a cache cave, and she'd looked at him then with the same flat caution she wore now—the look of someone doing arithmetic on whether you were worth the risk of believing. He'd spent most of his adult life being liked quickly and trusted never, and there was something about Lior Vassek's slow, grudging arithmetic that made him want to pass.

"East side," he agreed. "You lead."

They came down through a field of broken rock, boots finding stone instead of snow where they could, because snow kept records. The wind carried the smell of cold iron and old fuel. Fifty meters out, August held up a fist and crouched behind a boulder furred with lichen, and Lior folded down beside him without being told, close enough that he could hear her breathing—shallow, controlled, the breathing of someone who had practiced being afraid.

The station resolved into detail as they watched. A door hanging by its lower hinge. A window dark with grime. A diesel stovepipe leaning at an angle that said nobody had cared about it in weeks. And there—August narrowed his eyes—a line of smoke, thin as spider silk, rising from a vent on the leeward side.

Somebody was home.

He touched Lior's arm and pointed. She went very still. Then she leaned close, her voice barely more than shaped air: "Could be squatters. Could be them."

"Them left," August said. "That's what we established."

"We established what I *told* you." Her jaw tightened. "I told you what I saw. I didn't see Renn leave."

Renn Okafor. The name had become a splinter over the past week, working deeper every time Lior mentioned it. The surveyor who hadn't run with the others. The one whose body they'd assumed was somewhere in the river or the ravines, another item on the list of things the Syndicate had erased. August had built his plans around that absence the way you build a bridge around a missing pier—and now there was smoke coming out of the hole.

"If it's him," August said slowly, "he's been alone up here since before the first snow. Wounded, maybe, if your guess about the night raid is right. Alone and wounded and hiding from exactly the kind of people we're going to look like—two strangers climbing toward his hideout in bad weather."

Lior was quiet. Then: "So we don't look like that."

"How?"

She reached into her jacket and drew out the thing they had crossed half a range to protect: the torn corner of the original survey map, the piece she'd ripped from the master sheet the night the men came, bearing the supervisor's countersignature and the coordinates of the upper station. She held it up between two fingers.

"We look like the survey team," she said. "Because we are."

* * *

They announced themselves from thirty meters, voices pitched to carry over the wind, hands visible, moving slow. August did the talking because his voice was built for open country—big, unhurried, the voice of a man who talked to storms. "Survey station! We're coming in from the Kestrel trail! Two of us! No weapons out!"

Nothing answered but the wind worrying the loose roofing.

At ten meters, the door exploded inward—not opening so much as being flung—and a figure filled it sideways, wrong-angled, one leg braced against the frame like a tripod missing a foot. He held a surveying rod reversed, gripped like a pike, both hands white-knuckled on the aluminum. He was gaunt in the way that only starvation and fever can make a man gaunt, cheekbones standing out like tent poles under skin gone the color of tallow, and his left thigh was wrapped in layers of shirt and duct tape stained a brown that had once been red.

But it was his eyes that stopped August on the scree. Not wild. Worse than wild. *Organized.* Eyes that had taken the measure of everything that could hurt him and arranged it into a system.

"Stop there." The voice was cracked but level. "Names."

"Lior Vassek," Lior said immediately, and something flickered across the ruined face—recognition, disbelief, a crack in the armor. "Kestrel River survey, junior cartographer. You signed my field certification in June. You said my contour work was—" she faltered, just barely, "—you said it was the cleanest you'd seen from a first-year."

The man's grip on the rod loosened a degree. His gaze swung to August.

"August Sandoval. Storm chaser. I'm—" and here was the moment, the one he'd rehearsed on the trail, the version of himself he'd packed like gear, "—I was hired to check on the team when the radio went quiet. Delia Marsh, Meridian Syndicate, contracted me for resupply and retrieval."

The effect was instant and total. The rod came back up. Renn Okafor took a step forward onto his ruined leg, and August watched him pay for it—watched the pain hit like a wave and get absorbed, filed, used. "Meridian." He said the word the way other men said the names of diseases. "You walked up to my door and said Meridian out loud."

"He's telling the truth about the contract," Lior cut in, fast. "Renn. Renn, listen. He lied to them. He's been lying to them for days. They think we're dead in a crevasse above Saddleback."

"Do they." Renn's eyes never left August. "And what do you think, storm chaser? What's your professional assessment? Because I've got a ledger in this building that says Meridian financed the concession itself. That says the permits are forged, that says my supervisor's signature was bought, and that says the people who hold your debt would burn this whole watershed to keep it buried. So tell me your honest assessment, since honesty seems to be going around."

The wind gusted. Somewhere below, a slope of snow let go with a long soft sigh and resettled.

August thought about lying. It was astonishing how available the option still was, how easily his mouth could shape the reassuring version—the version where he was a good man caught in bad circumstances, the version Delia Marsh had paid for. He'd been that version for so long it fit like broken-in boots.

"The way I read it," he said, "somebody hired me to walk into these mountains and come back alone. Or not come back. Either way, whatever happened to your team gets blamed on weather and bad judgment, and the paper trail dies with the season." He held Renn's stare. "I figured that out four days upstream. I've been lying to my handler since. That's my assessment. Take it or shoot me with your stick."

A long silence. Then Renn Okafor laughed—one dry, cracked syllable, more cough than humor—and stepped back from the doorway, lowering the rod.

"Get inside before the light goes," he said. "Both of you. And watch the third step. The floor's rotten and I'm in no condition to dig you out."

* * *

Inside, the station smelled of diesel, iodine, and unwashed human endurance. Renn had fortified it with the methodical desperation of a man who'd had nothing to do for weeks but think: crates stacked into barricades, tools sorted on a blanket by function, a peephole scratched through the grime of the window at sitting height. A camp stove burned low in the corner. On the table, weighted down with a carabiner, lay a green canvas-bound book.

August stopped three feet from it and couldn't make himself go closer.

It was unremarkable. That was the obscene part. A standard field ledger, the kind sold in any supply catalog, the kind August had seen a thousand times in a thousand truck beds. Its cover was scuffed, its corners soft with handling. Inside it—if Lior was right, if the fragments she'd pieced together were right—was the difference between a watershed belonging to the people who'd lived on it for six generations and belonging, permanently and legally, to a lending syndicate that called itself a community of optimists.

"That's it?" he asked.

"That's it." Renn lowered himself onto a crate with the careful choreography of the badly injured, extending the wrapped leg. Up close, the wound beneath the bandages had a sweetish smell August didn't like at all. "Original field surveys, spring and summer. Benchmarks, flow measurements, boundary markers—all of it contradicting the concession maps. The submitted permits claim our benchmarks were set in March. Here's the actual log: we set them in July, after the melt, because the passes were closed in March. Every date on the fraudulent filings is backdated to sit ahead of the indigenous claims. My supervisor initialed the falsified transmittal under pressure—I watched him do it, and I should have burned the building down that day, and instead I wrote everything down, because I was a coward with good handwriting."

"You weren't a coward," Lior said quietly. "You stayed."

"I hid." Renn's laugh again, that dry crack. "There's a difference, and the difference is everyone else is gone." He looked at the ledger a moment, then pushed it across the table toward August—an inch, no more, a gesture that cost him visibly. "Read the September entries. Last page. Read it out loud. I want to hear how it sounds in a stranger's voice."

August opened the book. The pages were dense with Renn's hand—small, exact, unhurried even under pressure, the writing of a man who believed the world could be measured honestly if you were careful enough. He found the final entry and read:

*"September 14. Confirmed: concession permit filings 44-C and 45-A contain benchmark dates fabricated prior to actual survey. Cross-checked against my original logs and Vassek's contour sheets. Supervisor T. Brandt acknowledges discrepancy; states he was instructed to certify by Meridian project office. Instructed by whom, he will not say. Photographed all documents. Hiding originals. If anything happens to this team, the record exists in duplicate, and whoever finds it should know we did not agree to any of this."*

The room was silent except for the tick of the cooling stove.

"Duplicate," August said.

"There's a second copy." Renn's eyes had gone distant, fixed on some middle distance of memory. "I split the evidence. Ledger stays with me. Photos and the signed transmittal page went into a cache—sealed ammo box, marked on no map I'd trust to a Syndicate employee. Lior." He turned to her. "Do you remember the cairn above the second lake? The one that wasn't on your sheets?"

Her breath caught. "The one you told me was a survey error. You said—"

"I said a lot of things in September." For the first time, something moved through the ruin of his face that might have been tenderness. "You were twenty-three and I wasn't sure yet which of us the Syndicate owned. The cache is real. It's under the false cairn. But the key to reading why those dates matter—that's this book. Book and cache together, the fraud falls apart. Either one alone, their lawyers eat us alive."

August set the ledger down with the care of a man handling something live. Proof. Actual proof, in his hands after nine days of frostbite and fear and lies told into a radio handset. He should have felt triumphant. Instead he felt the weight of it settle onto his shoulders alongside everything else he was carrying, and the weight had a pulse.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape: a ledger was paper. Paper needed a witness. And the only witness who could authenticate it was currently running a fever on a rotten floor, with a leg wound that smelled like the beginning of gangrene, unable to walk ten miles without being carried.

"Your leg," he said. "Show me."

"It's fine."

"It smells fine, does it? Show me anyway."

Renn bristled—but Lior was already kneeling, already unwinding the duct tape with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about her, and when the last layer came away August felt his stomach drop. The wound was a gash, deep and ugly, poorly closed and reopened at least twice. The skin around it was hot and tight and streaked with red lines climbing toward the hip like roads on one of Lior's maps, all pointing the same direction.

"How long ago?" August asked.

"Night of the raid. Twenty-six days."

"Twenty-six days." August stood and paced the length of the room, three steps each way, because stillness had never once solved anything for him. "You've been walking on this. Climbing on this. The infection's in the lymphatics. He needs antibiotics, real ones, and a proper debridement, and neither of those things exists within eighty miles of here."

"So we carry him," Lior said simply.

"Carry him." August gestured at the window, where the light was failing and the wind had begun to moan around the stovepipe with a note he recognized—the low, tuning-up hum of weather organizing itself into something serious. "Over Saddleback. In November. With the collectors behind us and a blizzard probably two days out. Three of us, one of whom can't walk, carrying enough food for—" he did the math and hated it, "—not enough."

"Yes," Lior said. "That's the plan."

"That's not a plan, that's a eulogy with extra steps."

"Then improve it." She rose, wiping her hands on her thighs, and looked at him with an expression he was beginning to know the way he knew cloud formations—the flat gray underside that meant pressure was building and something was going to give. "You wanted the job done. This is the job. The ledger isn't evidence unless Renn testifies to it. Renn doesn't testify unless he lives. He doesn't live unless we move tomorrow morning, ahead of the weather, and we move *fast*, which means tonight we rest, we eat, we wrap that leg properly, and you stop pacing like the walls owe you money."

August opened his mouth. Closed it. Out the scratched peephole, the last light was draining off the western ridgeline, and far below, impossibly small, the Kestrel caught it—one bright thread, still running.

Renn spoke from his crate, quiet, watching them both with those organized eyes. "For what it's worth," he said, "the last man who argued logistics with her lost. Ask Brandt."

August exhaled. Somewhere in the last minute, without his permission, the plan had stopped being his and started being theirs, and the strangest part—the part he'd examine later, on a rope bridge or in a whiteout—was that it didn't feel like losing control. It felt like joining a crew.

"Fine," he said. "Tomorrow, first light. But we take the south bench instead of the trail—it's longer but it stays under the cornice line, and if that sky does what I think it's going to do, the trail becomes a death trap by noon." He pointed at the ledger. "And that rides in my pack. Nobody's argument."

Renn almost smiled. "Deal. One condition." He nodded toward the peephole, toward the darkening north. "Before you sleep, take a look at the pass. Tell me what you see."

August bent to the scratch in the glass. Beyond the black shoulder of the ridge, Saddleback's saddle showed as a pale notch against the stars—and above it, faint but unmistakable, clouds were assembling out of clear darkness, stacking themselves in ranks like something mustered for war.

He'd spent fifteen years learning to read that particular handwriting.

"Snow," he said. "Early, heavy, and mean. Maybe two days. Maybe less."

Behind him, he heard Renn Okafor say softly, to no one in particular: "Then we'd better hope your handler's trackers read weather worse than you do."

August straightened. Outside, the first flakes had begun to come down—fat, slow, almost gentle, the way the worst ones always started.
