## 11. Renn's Bargain

Pain woke Renn Okafor before the argument did.

He heard their voices through the thin wall of the survey station's supply room—low, urgent, the way people talked when they thought the third man was still asleep. The girl. The storm chaser. Arguing about him, or near enough. He could hear his own name in it, passed between them like something hot.

He lay on the cot they'd dragged in for him and took inventory of himself the way he'd taught every junior cartographer to take inventory of a map: what's accurate, what's missing, what's been altered since the last edition. Left leg, broken above the ankle, splinted with a tent pole and a belt he'd cut off a dead man's pack. Ribs, cracked at least two, breathing like wet paper. Hands, shaking—not from cold anymore, not only from that. From eleven days alone at four thousand meters with a ledger and a dead radio and the certain knowledge that everyone who might have come for him had either died or been bought.

His name was on the concession paperwork. That was the part nobody understood yet, and the part he'd have to make them understand before they walked one more mile downhill carrying that book.

* * *

He'd signed page forty-one on a Tuesday.

That was how he remembered it—not by date but by weather, because a cartographer's memory is a weather memory first. Tuesday, clear, the kind of blue morning that made the Kestrel headwaters look like spilled glass. Supervisor Amara Diallo had put the page in front of him at the folding table outside the main tent, and she hadn't looked at him while she did it. She'd looked at the river.

"Renn. I need your certification on this boundary verification."

He'd read it. Of course he'd read it; that was the whole disease of being the careful one. The boundary line ran along the north bank of the second tributary, which was correct, except that the survey date stamped beneath it was fourteen months old, and Renn had personally run that stretch eight months ago, and the markers described in the document—the iron pins, the blazed cedars—did not exist. He'd checked twice. He'd spent two days checking, because he'd wanted to be wrong. Being wrong would have cost him an afternoon. Being right was going to cost him everything else.

The permits underneath that boundary were the foundation of the whole Meridian water concession. Every downstream allocation, every irrigation contract, every dollar the Syndicate's "community of optimists" had lent to ranchers and towns against future water shares—all of it stood on surveys that had never been taken. And beneath those falsified surveys, older than any of it, were the claims of the Ashkaun people, whose winter camps sat exactly where the concession drew its straight blue lines, and whose objections had been made legally impossible by documents that predated them on paper and postdated them in fact.

Backdated. Forged. Certified.

"I can't sign this," Renn had said.

Amara had finally turned to look at him, and her face had been the worst thing he'd ever seen on another human being—not cruelty, nothing so clean as cruelty. Fear wearing a supervisor's calm. "Renn," she'd said quietly, "there are men at the landing who flew up here on Meridian's charter. They're waiting for my signature. They've been very patient. I need you to think about what happens to this team if I don't give it to them."

And he had thought about it. That was his shame, precise and permanent: he had *thought about it*, weighing six lives against a line on a map, doing the arithmetic a careful man does, and the arithmetic had come out wrong in a way no amount of care could fix afterward. He'd signed. Page forty-one. His certification number, his hand, his name, making a lie into a legal fact.

Four days later, Tomas Reyes—the lead cartographer, the one who'd found the original Ashkaun claim records in the county archive and wouldn't stop saying so out loud—was gone. Not gone like left. Gone like his sleeping bag was still rolled, his boots were by the door, and there were boot prints at the treeline heading down toward the landing that belonged to no one on the team. Men had come at night. Renn knew because he'd heard the boat engine kill itself below the rapids and lain awake listening to voices he couldn't make out, telling himself it was fishermen, telling himself it until dawn.

Then the radios died—not failed, died, smashed, though they didn't learn that until later—and Amara came to him white-faced and said, "We're leaving. Now. All of us. We take the evidence down to Harrow Bend ourselves."

They never made the treeline together. The team scattered in the dark when the second boat came, and Renn had done the thing survivors do, the unforgivable practical thing: he'd grabbed the original field ledger—Tomas's ledger, the true one, the one with the real dates and the real boundaries and the photographs of the empty ground where the iron pins supposedly stood—and he'd climbed. Up. Away from the river, away from the road, up to the high station where nobody sane goes after the first frost, and he'd broken his leg on the last switchback doing it, and he'd told himself every day since that the leg was the price and the ledger was the reason and the math balanced.

Eleven days at altitude with a broken leg gives a man a lot of time to check his arithmetic.

It didn't balance. It had never balanced. Tomas was dead or worse because Renn's signature had told the Syndicate the forgery was safe—that the careful man had been bought, so there was no loose thread. His signature hadn't saved anyone. It had *certified* them.

So when he heard the storm chaser and the girl arguing softly about whether he could walk, Renn Okafor swung his ruined leg off the cot, bit down on the sound it cost him, and went to tell them the truth.

* * *

They were at the map table—of course they were, the girl couldn't stay away from maps, kept touching them the way other people touch railings—and they both turned when the door scraped. He saw them register the state of him: upright, gray, sweating, one leg held off the floorboards like cargo.

"You should be lying down," the storm chaser said. August Sandoval. Renn had pulled the man's file from the cache before they arrived, because paranoia is just diligence with bad press. Storm chaser. Debt with the Meridian Syndicate, substantial. Hired job, terms undisclosed.

Renn laughed, and it hurt, and he let them hear that it hurt. "You want to know what I've been doing for eleven days? Lying down. Lying down and reading this." He lifted the ledger from where it lived inside his coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and set it on the table between them with a care that made August's eyes narrow. "Tomas Reyes's field ledger. Original entries. Real dates. Photographs of ground that the certified permits describe as surveyed and marked, and isn't. This book is the only object in the world that proves the entire Kestrel concession is fraud from the foundation up."

Lior Vassek went very still. Renn had watched her do that before, back at the camp, back when she was the junior who asked too many good questions. She reached out and touched the oilcloth like it might be warm.

"And here's what you don't know yet," Renn said, "either of you, so listen, because I've had eleven days to get this straight and I'm only going to say it once." He pointed at August. "You. Your debt. What's the number?"

August's jaw worked. "That's not—"

"The number. Because I did the sums up here with nothing else to do. The Syndicate financed the concession itself. Not just the loans against it—the concession. The capital that paid for the falsified surveys came out of the same books that hold your marker. Which means forgiving your debt wasn't charity and it wasn't even business." He leaned on the table, and the table took his weight gratefully. "It was a purchase. They sent you up here to retrieve the evidence and the witnesses, and the price they named was the exact size of the hole you were in, because that's what silence costs a man like you—everything he owes. You weren't hired to save anybody. You were hired to bury us. The debt forgiveness was the shovel."

The silence in the room had texture. Renn watched August Sandoval take it, watched the man's face do something complicated—not denial; denial had already happened somewhere on the trail, Renn guessed, somewhere between the girl and the radio call he'd lied his way through. What was on his face now was the thing after denial, the bare cold air after a wall comes down.

"Yeah," August said finally. Roughly. "I figured that out around waypoint nine."

"Good. Then you'll understand why I'm going to say the next part." Renn put his hand flat on the ledger. "We burn it."

"No." Lior said it instantly, fiercely, stepping forward like the book was a child about to be snatched.

"Hear me—"

"We climb over a mountain range in early snow, we carry a man with a broken leg, we outrun collectors, and for *what*, if we burn the only proof?" Her voice cracked and she pushed through the crack. "Tomas is dead. Amara is dead or taken. The others ran into the dark with nothing. If we burn this, they died for paper, and the permits finalize in seventeen days and the fraud becomes law forever, and the Ashkaun lose the water under their own camps to a signature *you* gave them—"

"That's exactly why," Renn said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, almost gentle, which surprised him. "Because of what you just said. Look at the arithmetic, girl. You're three people and a cripple crossing a range ahead of a blizzard, pursued by an organization that has already killed once and has now upgraded its orders—I know how these people escalate, I watched them do it to a village dispute two concessions ago. If they catch us, they don't just take the ledger. They take *us*, and then they have the evidence *and* the witnesses, and the story ends with three more disappearances and a concession that stands unchallenged for ninety years. Burned truth protects people. Delivered truth gets people killed. I have done the second kind of math already once this season. I won't do it twice."

"Burned truth protects *no one*," Lior shot back. "Burned truth is just their lie with extra steps. You'd be finishing their work for them. You'd be signing page forty-one all over again, except this time with fire instead of ink."

The words landed where she'd aimed them. Renn felt them go in, felt the old wound open along its exact seam, and hated her a little for her accuracy, and respected her more than he could say.

August hadn't spoken. He stood at the edge of the lamplight with his arms crossed, watching them fight over the book like a man watching weather build on a horizon—reading it, not joining it. When the silence stretched, he said, quietly, "How heavy is it?"

Renn blinked. "What?"

"The ledger. Actual weight. Oilcloth and all."

"...Three pounds. Maybe less."

August nodded slowly, eyes on the middle distance, running some private calculation of miles and hours and snowlines. "Three pounds," he said. "Cheapest thing we're carrying." Then he looked up, and there was something in his face Renn recognized from the chase footage he'd studied in the cache—storm chasers get that look when the cell starts rotating, terror and fascination fused into focus. "Here's my problem with burning it. I've spent ten years running from things. Weather, creditors, every town I ever slept in twice. And I'll tell you what I learned about running: it only works if there's nowhere the thing can catch you. There's always somewhere. There's always a landing, a hearing, a record, a day when the past walks into the room and sits down across from you. Burning that book doesn't make the past smaller. It makes it *theirs*. Every copy of the lie survives, and ours is ash, and then there's nowhere in the world the truth can catch up to them." He tapped the table once, hard. "I'm done carrying things that only weigh on the way down."

"So we deliver it," Lior said. Not a question. A hand extended across the argument.

"To a land commission full of lawyers the Syndicate pays retainers to," Renn said, but the fire had gone out of it and they all heard it go.

"To daylight," Lior said. "Where lies dry up."

Renn looked down at the ledger. Under the oilcloth, Tomas's handwriting waited—patient, exact, dead. *Real dates. Real ground.* Eleven days he'd protected it like a heart outside his body, and in eleven days he'd never once managed to answer the simplest question: protected it *for whom?* For the dead, who couldn't read it? For himself, so his guilt would have a companion? Or for the people downstream—the Ashkaun camps, the irrigators, every soul whose water was being sold out from under them by his signature—who could only be helped by the one act he was most afraid of?

A careful man checks his work. He checked it now, one last time, and the sum came out the same place it had always been trying to go.

"Seventeen days," he said at last. "Permit expiry. After that it's law." He exhaled, long and shaking, and it cost him his ribs and he paid. "There's a route. Not the trail—the trail funnels at Saddleback and anyone hunting us knows it. There's an old survey line on the west flank, steep but blind, that comes down to the river above Harrow Bend. Four days if the weather holds. It won't hold. So we move tomorrow, first light, and we don't stop for anything that isn't bleeding." He looked at August. "You'll need to rig a litter. My leg is done as a walking proposition."

August was already pulling rope from his pack. "I figured."

"And Sandoval." Renn waited until the man looked up. "When we get down there, when the commission asks you what you were hired to do—you tell them all of it. Including the part where you said yes. Especially that part. A confession buys more credibility than a saint ever could, because saints are rare and liars are everywhere, and a liar testifying against his own bargain is the one witness no lawyer on earth can discredit." He allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "Consider it a debt you can actually pay."

August held his gaze for a moment, then nodded, once, and turned to the ropes.

Lior was already at the map, drawing the west flank route in pencil, quick sure strokes, and Renn watched her and thought: *Tomas would have liked you.* He didn't say it. Some truths keep.

Outside, the wind had changed. It came off the high peaks now with an edge in it, a mineral smell, the smell of snow deciding. Renn had spent his life reading terrain, and terrain was telling him what he'd known since the night the boats came: the window was closing, and everything they loved or owed or feared was on the other side of a pass that would not stay open for them.

First light, then. First light, and downhill, and daylight—if the mountain, and the men behind them, permitted it.
