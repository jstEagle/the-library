## 13. The Collector's Offer

Delia Marsh had tracked men through worse than this, but she couldn't remember when.

The blizzard's tail was still working itself out of the high country, spitting sideways snow in gusts that came like thrown fists, and the two collectors she'd been saddled with—Voss and the kid, Petey, who wasn't a kid at all but had the eyes of someone who'd never been told no—were falling further behind with every switchback. Good. She'd been managing that deliberately for two days, calling rest stops when they didn't need them, taking the wrong fork once and blaming the whiteout, letting their morale rot the way wet rope rots. Voss was competent and mean. Petey was eager and cruel. Neither was stupid, which was the problem. Stupid men could be led anywhere. Competent ones had to be miscounted.

She crouched at the treeline where the snow gave way to scree, pretending to read sign, and let the wind carry her thoughts where it wanted.

*Recovery*, the order had said, eleven days ago. *Recover the documents. Recover the witness. Deliver to Harrow Bend office.*

*Recovery* had a shape she knew. It meant a ride downriver, a room with a table, a signature extracted or a body weighed down in a reservoir—whatever the paperwork required. She'd done recoveries. She'd done them believing, once, that the paperwork meant something, that the Meridian Syndicate was what the brochures said: a community of optimists, lenders who believed in people the way farmers believed in spring. She'd been twenty-three and drowning in her mother's medical debt, and a man with soft hands and a softer voice had told her the debt could become a career, that the Syndicate didn't collect, it *redeemed*. She'd believed it the way you believe a warm coat in cold weather—because you need to, not because it's true.

Then the order had changed.

She'd been at the relay station at Miller's Fork when it came through, encrypted, priority-flagged, and she had read it three times standing in the cold with the radio humming against her hip. *Amendment: recovery suspended. Objective is elimination. All materials and persons to be removed from circulation. Confirmation of disposal required.*

*Persons.* Plural. Which meant the cartographer. Which meant the surveyor they'd all assumed was dead. And it meant Sandoval, too—the man whose debt she'd dangled like a carrot, the man she'd spent three years collecting from and two weeks, now, quietly rooting for.

She thought about August Sandoval more than she should have. Not in any way that would survive being spoken aloud. Just—the shape of him. The way he talked about weather like it was an old friend who'd wronged him. The way he'd signed her contract with a laugh that had no floor under it. She'd seen a hundred men sign that contract. Most of them signed like they were selling something they'd already lost. August signed like he was proving a point to himself, and she'd wanted, even then, to ask what the point was, and whether it was worth what the Syndicate always charged.

She'd told herself the job was clean. Retrieve the survey team, forgive the debt, everybody walks away. It was a lie she'd needed, and she'd needed it badly, because the alternative was admitting what the ledger in her chest had been whispering for years: that she wasn't an optimist. She was a tool, and tools get used on whatever the hand holding them decides needs breaking.

Voss crunched up beside her, breath steaming. "Sign?"

"Boot prints. Two sets, maybe three." She pointed down-slope, where the storm had half-erased a line of prints heading toward the saddle. "Heading for Saddleback Pass. They're trying to beat the snow."

"Trying to beat the snow with a cripple." Voss smiled. He had a way of smiling that used only the bottom half of his face. "Renn Okafor's alive, then. The amendment anticipated that."

Delia kept her eyes on the prints. "The amendment's a mistake. Board's jumpy. They always get jumpy before a hearing."

"The board doesn't make mistakes." Voss turned his smile on her. "You've been slowing us down, Delia. Don't think I haven't counted the rest stops."

She met his eyes. This was the moment, and she'd known it was coming the way you know a storm is coming—not from any single sign but from the whole weight of the sky. "I've been keeping us alive," she said. "You want to chase three exhausted men up a mountain in a whiteout, be my guest. I'll write your eulogy. It'll be short."

Voss studied her for a long moment, wind pulling at his hood. "The amendment says all materials and persons. You read it?"

"I read it."

"And you're still here."

"I'm still here," she said, "because I'm going to finish the job my way. Which means we do it smart, and we do it quiet, and we don't lose anyone else to a mountainside." She stood, brushing snow off her knee, and gave him the bottom half of a smile in return. "Now move. They've got half a day on us, and the pass closes tomorrow night."

It was a lie stacked on a lie. The pass closed tomorrow night, true. They had half a day, false—they had less. And she was not going to finish anything. She had decided that somewhere in the last two days, maybe in the moment she'd read *elimination* and heard her mother's voice saying *you always did believe the wrong people, Delia girl*, or maybe earlier, maybe the whole time, maybe she'd been walking toward this since the day she signed her own name on Meridian's line. You couldn't take back a warning. You couldn't take back a head start. But you could choose which irreversible thing to be.

She chose, and the choosing felt like the first deep breath in eleven years.

* * *

The chance came at dusk, in the bowl below the pass where the wind funneled and screamed.

Voss and Petey had made camp in the lee of a rock shelf, and Delia had volunteered for first watch—volunteered so smoothly that Voss, exhausted and self-satisfied, hadn't even questioned it. She waited until their breathing went long and even, until the fire burned down to a red eye, and then she moved.

She told herself she'd only scout. She told herself a lot of things on the way up the bowl, moving through the storm's ragged leftovers with the practiced silence of a woman who'd once been very good at her job. The truth was she'd packed a second radio that morning. The truth was she'd memorized their route from the waypoint marks. The truth was that a woman who only scouts doesn't leave her bedroll behind.

She found them in a crevasse shelter half a mile above the bowl—a fissure in the rock overhung with a shelf of ice, barely big enough for three. She saw the firelight first, a weak orange glow in the blue dark, and then the shapes: August sitting up, awake, a survey pole across his knees like a spear. Lior Vassek curled against the wall, young and hollow-cheeked, eyes open. And Renn Okafor, leg splinted with a ski pole and survey tape, asleep with the shallow rapid breathing of a man running a fever.

August heard her before she could announce herself—of course he did, the man was a tuning fork for trouble—and the pole came up. "Who's there."

"Easy." She raised her hands, hood back, letting the firelight find her face. "It's Delia."

She watched him process it, watched the arithmetic move behind his eyes: the collector, alone, at night, no backup visible, standing at the mouth of the only shelter they had. "Where are your friends?"

"Half a mile down. Asleep. I drugged the coffee." She said it flatly, and watched him decide whether to believe her. "Petey snores. You'd have heard him otherwise. He snores like a landslide."

"Why are you here?" Lior's voice, thin and hard. The kid had come up on one elbow, and there was nothing young in the look at all. "You came to collect us."

"I came to warn you." She knelt slowly, deliberately, keeping her hands visible, and let the cold truth out of her chest where it had been sitting like a stone for two days. "The order changed. Three days ago. It's not recovery anymore. It's elimination. You, the surveyor, the documents—all of it, gone, and confirmation of disposal required. Voss has photographs of your faces. He's supposed to bring back proof."

The silence that followed was the kind that has weight. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, ice creaked and settled.

"Proof," August said finally. His voice was very quiet. "You mean—"

"I mean what you think I mean." She looked at him straight on. "I'm not going to dress it up. I've spent eleven days telling myself the Syndicate was just a lender with bad manners, and I'm done. I read that amendment and I heard myself, August. I heard what I've been for nine years. A tool. And tools don't get to be surprised by what they're used for."

"Why should we believe you?" Lior again, and Delia respected it—the kid had earned that question, had probably earned it at gunpoint in the dark. "You hunted us. You put him—" a jerk of the chin at August— "on us like a dog."

"Yes." She didn't flinch from it. "I did. And I told myself it was clean. Retrieve the team, forgive the debt. I even believed it, mostly. But I've read the concession filings, Lior. I know what your team found. I know the permits are forged, and I know who forged them, because the same names are on my employment contract. Meridian financed the concession. Meridian wrote the permits. Meridian hired me to make sure nobody ever proved it, and when proving it got close, Meridian changed the word *recover* to the word *eliminate* and expected me to keep walking." She pulled the second radio from her pack and set it on the stone between them, along with a paper map on which she'd marked the pass, the descent route, and the timing. "So. Believe me or don't. But believe this: Voss and Petey wake in six hours. Voss is good. Petey is worse than good, he's enthusiastic. And they know you're in this bowl."

Renn Okafor had woken at some point—she'd seen his eyes open behind the fever-glaze, tracking—and now he pushed himself up on one elbow and looked at her with the flat assessment of a man who'd already survived one night visit from her employers. "What do you want, Marsh? Nobody burns their career for strangers."

"I'm not burning my career," Delia said. "I burned it three days ago when I decided not to radio in the amendment's confirmation requirement. This—" she tapped the map— "is just the ashes."

August hadn't spoken in a while. He was looking at her the way he looked at weather, she thought—that long, measuring look, reading what was coming. "You're giving us a head start," he said.

"A day. Maybe a little more, if I can sell Voss a false trail down the east chute." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than she let it show. "It's not much. Saddleback closes tomorrow night, and you've got a man who can't move fast. You'll have to rig a litter and burn hours you don't have. But it's what I've got."

"Why?" Lior again. The kid wouldn't let it go, and Delia was grateful, because she'd been asking herself the same question for two days and hadn't found an answer that fit in a sentence.

"Because when I was twenty-three, a man with soft hands told me the Syndicate believed in people," she said. "And I wanted so badly to be believed in that I let him hand me a leash and call it a lifeline. And I have spent nine years collecting on other people's worst weeks and calling it optimism." She looked at the fire, at the three of them—two men and a kid who had crossed a mountain range carrying the truth like a live coal. "You're the first people I've met out here who were carrying something heavier than debt. I'm not going to be the one who takes it from you."

The wind screamed over the crevasse mouth. Renn lowered himself back down, wincing, and said, "The radio. Frequency?"

"Channel six, low band. I'll be listening. If you make it to the hearing at Harrow Bend—" she met August's eyes— "I'll have evidence for the commission that Voss won't enjoy. My field logs. The amendment itself. Names."

"You understand what you're doing," August said. It wasn't a question. "They'll come for you too."

"They'll come for me either way, once I miss a check-in." She stood, knees cracking, and pulled her hood up. "Difference is, now I get to choose which direction I'm facing when they do."

She was three steps from the firelight when August spoke again, quiet, almost lost under the wind.

"Delia. Why'd you really pick me for the job? Back at the start. You could've sent anyone."

She paused. Snow settled on her shoulders. "Because you were the best tracker I knew," she said. "And because I thought—some stupid part of me thought—if I put the truth in front of a man who spent his whole life running from it, maybe he'd do the right thing with it." She turned her head, gave him the ghost of a smile. "Looks like I was an optimist after all."

Then she was gone, down into the dark, leaving boot prints she didn't bother to hide—prints that would fill with snow by morning, prints that would lead Voss and Petey toward the east chute and a false trail and a day's worth of wasted fury, while three exhausted people and a paper ledger climbed toward Saddleback Pass with the sky closing behind them like a door.

She didn't look back. There was nothing behind her she wanted to keep.

* * *

The false trail cost her four hours of the night and most of her remaining credibility, but Voss bought it—bought it because he wanted to be smarter than her, and men like Voss always paid for that want. By the time they'd thrashed down the east chute and back, cursing the storm, the bowl below Saddleback was empty except for wind.

Delia sat on a rock with her radio on her knee, channel six hissing static into her ear, and watched the pass. Around noon, a shape moved against the snowfield—three shapes, one limping, one small and quick, one broad-shouldered and steady, roped together with survey line. Tiny as insects against the white. Climbing.

Petey came and stood beside her, breath steaming. "They went up," he said. "Through the storm."

"They went up," Delia agreed.

Voss was already checking his weapon, already calculating, already smiling with the bottom half of his face. "Pass closes tonight. They'll never make the far side with the cripple."

"Probably not," Delia said, and keyed her radio once, twice—two clicks, the signal they'd agreed on, meaning *you're clear, move now*—and watched the three shapes on the snowfield pause, and turn, and climb faster.

She had maybe a day before Voss figured out what she'd done. Maybe less. Somewhere below, in the valley, the land commission was convening at Harrow Bend, and lawyers were arriving in black cars, and a lie eleven years deep was about to be tested against a ledger and a limping surveyor and a debt-ridden storm chaser who'd finally stopped running.

Delia Marsh clicked the radio off, shouldered her pack, and started up the pass after them.

Not to catch them.

To be there when it mattered.
