## 14. The Crossing at Saddleback

The storm gave us one clear morning and took the rest.

We broke camp in the blue half-light below the high station, Renn's leg strapped to a splint of survey poles and pack straps, his face gray with the effort of simply standing. He weighed less than he should have. Eleven days alone above the treeline had carved him down to wire and stubbornness, and now every step cost him something he couldn't afford to spend.

"Saddleback's six miles," Lior said, unrolling the field map against her knee. Her breath came out in white flags. "Four if the ridge holds. Eight if we have to carry him the whole way."

"We'll have to carry him the whole way," I said.

She didn't argue. That was the thing about Lior I'd learned on the switchbacks—she didn't waste breath disagreeing with the truth. She just started figuring out how to live inside it.

We built the litter from what the survey team had left behind: two tent poles lashed crosswise with rope from the rotten bridge, a ground tarp folded into a sling, Renn's own sleeping bag rolled beneath him for padding. It was ugly work, done fast, done cold. My fingers wouldn't close right around the knots until I'd beaten some feeling back into them against my thigh. When we finally lifted him—Lior at the head, me at the feet—Renn laughed once, a dry cracked sound.

"A cartographer," he said, "carried over his own contour lines like cargo."

"Shut up and hold still," Lior said, but her voice caught on it, and I saw her knuckles whiten on the pole. She'd spent eleven days believing this man was dead. Now she could feel his pulse through the canvas. Some burdens you don't mind carrying. They just happen to be heavy.

* * *

The first mile was mercy. The trail climbed gently through krummholz, wind-bent spruce that grew sideways like things fleeing, and we made good time. I counted our pace out loud without meaning to—a habit from chasing, where speed is the only currency that matters. A supercell doesn't wait. A closing pass doesn't either.

Above us, Saddleback showed its teeth. The saddle itself was a notch between two granite shoulders, maybe three hundred yards of exposed ridgeline, and beyond it the long descent toward the Harrow Bend country. Snow already streaked the notch like spilled flour. Every hour, the line crept lower.

"The forecast window closes tomorrow night," I said. "If we're not across by then, the drifts will be waist-deep on the descent. Deeper in the lee."

"And Delia?" Lior asked.

I checked the radio out of reflex, though I hadn't keyed it since the whiteout. It sat silent in its pouch, a dead bird. "Her day's head start ended yesterday afternoon. If she's coming, she's coming."

"If she's coming," Renn said from the litter, "she's bringing people who want us dead."

"She arrested her own partner," Lior said. "She burned everything."

"People do strange things at altitude." Renn's voice was flat, but I heard the fear under it, the way you hear water under ice. He'd been alone too long. Trust had atrophied in him like an unused muscle, and no amount of argument could flex it back. Only distance could. So we walked.

* * *

The second mile stopped being mercy.

The trail steepened into scree, loose shale that shifted underfoot with a sound like whispered arguments, and the litter became a problem neither of us wanted to name. On flat ground, two carriers could manage. On a slope, the downhill carrier bore most of the weight, and the shale offered nothing honest to stand on. Twice I went down to one knee with Renn's full weight swinging into the straps, and twice Lior braced me with her shoulder before we slid.

On the third fall, Renn said, "Leave me."

"No," Lior said.

"You're burning hours. Hours are permits. Permits are—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "I've done the math, Vassek. I know what I cost per minute."

"Then stop talking," she said, hauling him level again, "because your mouth costs more than your leg."

But he wasn't wrong, and we all knew it, and the knowing sat between us like a fourth passenger. By noon we'd covered barely two miles. The snowline had come down to meet us, first in patches, then as a continuous skin over the trail, erasing the cairns, erasing the path, erasing everything except the map in Lior's head and the compass in my hand. She navigated by memory of contours; I navigated by the sky. Between us we kept a straight line through country determined to make every direction the same color.

At the base of the final climb, we rested twenty minutes because we had to or we'd die stupid. Renn ate half a ration bar and kept it down, which felt like a victory worth celebrating. Lior wrapped his hands—frost-nipped, clumsy—in spare socks. I stood facing the saddle and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

Three hundred yards of ridgeline. Wind already combing spindrift off the crest in long white banners—the mountain waving us off. With the litter, the crossing would take forty minutes if the wind held at thirty knots. An hour if it didn't. And behind us, somewhere down the drainage, Delia Marsh was either an ally or a debt collector, and both moved faster than a man on a broken leg.

"We rig double ropes," I said. "One on each side of the litter. When the gusts hit, we drop and anchor. Nobody stands up until it passes."

"That could take all day," Lior said.

"Then it takes all day."

She looked at me—really looked, the way she did when she was redrawing someone on the map of her understanding—and nodded once.

* * *

The ridge tried to kill us politely, the way mountains do.

The first hundred yards were survivable. We moved in a rhythm I called out like a coxswain—*step, plant, breathe, move*—the litter swaying between us, Renn gripping the poles with white-knuckled hands and narrating the terrain under him out of pure nerves: "Scree band, ten meters, then bare rock, watch the left edge—" A cartographer to the end, mapping even his own humiliation.

Then the wind found the notch and came through it like something with intent.

It hit us broadside at maybe fifty knots, a fist of compressed air funneled between the granite shoulders, and it put Lior and me both on our knees with the litter slewing sideways toward the drop. I got an anchor piton—Renn's own survey stake, driven into a crack that morning—cabled to my harness, and I wrapped the rope twice around my forearm and held. The world went white. Spindrift screamed past horizontally. Somewhere in the roar, Lior was shouting Renn's name, and Renn was shouting back, and none of it meant anything except that they were both still there.

The gust lasted ninety seconds. It felt like a season.

When it passed, my ears rang, my forearm was striped with rope burn, and the litter had rotated fifteen degrees off-line. We re-rigged in silence, hands moving on muscle and desperation, and crawled the next two hundred yards in a crouch, dropping flat for every gust, rising for every lull, a slow brutal tide of effort. My shoulders burned. Lior's lip split in the dry wind and she licked the blood away without noticing. At the high point of the saddle—the actual notch, the place maps mark with a gentle X as if crossing were a formality—we stopped for sixty seconds, no more, and looked back the way we'd come.

The valley behind us was filling with weather. Not clouds exactly—clouds have edges, moods, intentions. This was a wall, a gray-white certainty sliding up the drainage we'd climbed out of, swallowing the high station, swallowing the switchbacks, swallowing our retreat. The storm we'd outrun for two days had arrived at the door behind us.

And far below, tiny as punctuation, three figures on the trail.

Lior saw them when I did. Neither of us spoke. Through the binoculars I kept in my chest pocket—they'd belonged to the survey team, everything we carried had belonged to somebody gone—I resolved them: three climbers, roped, moving fast and light. No litter. No burden. One of them carried a rifle slung across the back, muzzle-down, the way professionals carry.

"Delia?" Lior asked.

"Maybe." I watched the lead figure break trail, setting a pace the other two matched. Efficient. Angry, maybe. Or loyal. From here, at this distance, love and pursuit look identical. "Or maybe the Syndicate stopped trusting her after she arrested Kessler, and sent replacements."

"Can they catch us?"

I did the math honestly, because lying to myself at altitude gets people killed. Downhill, unburdened, they'd gain on us. But the descent on the far side was ours to choose—chutes, talus, timber—and darkness was four hours out, and the wall of weather behind them would reach their position within the hour. Anyone climbing into that front without shelter was gambling.

"They'll be pinned by the same storm we are," I said. "Question is whether they dig in smart or desperate."

"And if they dig in smart?"

"Then tomorrow they're an hour behind us instead of three." I packed the binoculars. "Which means tonight we descend like the debt is due. Because it is."

* * *

The far side of Saddleback was worse than the near side, the way the far side always is.

The trail fell away in steep pitches through snow-loaded timber, and the litter had to be lowered down sections by rope, passed hand to hand between trees, Renn riding it like a man in a boat he no longer trusted. At one pitch—a rock slab glazed with verglas, thirty feet of vertical nothing—I free-soloed down with the rope, anchored it, and belayed the litter while Lior climbed down beside it, one hand on the lines, boots skidding. Renn never made a sound. Later I understood why: he'd clenched his jaw so hard against screaming that he'd bitten through the inside of his cheek, and he spat blood into the snow at the bottom and said only, "Faster next time."

We lost the light at five o'clock and kept going by feel, Lior calling the grade changes from memory of the map, me reading the snow's texture underfoot the way I read cloud bases—firm crust, sugar collapse, wind slab. We fell often. We got up faster than we fell. That's the whole secret of mountains and debts alike.

At full dark, in a stand of spruce thick enough to cut the wind, we finally stopped, because Renn had stopped shivering, and that frightened Lior more than any hunter. We dug into the tree well of the largest spruce, piled him between us, fed him warm water from the bottle we'd kept against our bodies, and took turns holding him through the worst hour while his core fought its way back. Above us, through the branches, the sky cleared in ragged patches, stars appearing like witnesses.

"He'll live," Lior said finally, her ear against Renn's chest, listening to the engine of him.

"He'll live," I agreed.

She sat back against the trunk and looked at me across the dark. "August. Those climbers. If they're Syndicate—if they get orders like Kessler's order—"

"Then tomorrow we don't descend the trail at all. We take the creek chute. It's slower, it's miserable, and Renn will hate every yard of it."

"But?"

"But nobody can parallel us in it with a rifle." I pulled my collar up and watched my breath rise through the branches toward the indifferent stars. "We bought today with blood and rope burns. Tomorrow we buy the next one the same way. That's all a crossing ever is—paying for each mile before you get to walk it."

Lior was quiet a long moment. Then, softly, almost to herself: "Nineteen days when we started counting. Twelve left."

Twelve days. One ledger. Three lives moving at the speed of a broken man on a tarp sling, and somewhere below us in the dark, three more moving at the speed of money.

Sleep came in shallow installments. And sometime past midnight, half-dreaming, I heard it—faint, carried down the wind from the saddle we'd bled across: a sound I knew from a hundred plains horizons, the thin whistle of a storm line arriving early.

The pass had closed behind us.

Whatever was coming now would have to come the same way we did—the hard way, through the chute, through the cold, through everything the mountain had left to charge.

I lay awake after that, listening to Renn breathe and Lior murmur contour names in her sleep, and I understood that the head start was gone, the margin was gone, and all we had left was each other and twelve days.

For the first time in my life, that felt like enough to move with.
