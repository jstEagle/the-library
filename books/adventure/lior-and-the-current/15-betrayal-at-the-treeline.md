## 15. Betrayal at the Treeline

The treeline came down to meet them like a held breath finally released.

For six days above it, the world had been stone and sky and the small human sounds of their own labor—rope creaking, Renn's breath sawing through the litter straps, August's low voice calling weather that hadn't arrived yet. Lior had almost forgotten what trees looked like. Then the krummholz appeared, twisted and stunted, and after it the true firs, dark and dense, and standing among them Lior felt something loosen in his chest that he hadn't known was clenched.

Green meant water. Green meant soil. Green meant they'd crossed the worst of it.

Renn saw it too. He'd been silent for hours, conserving strength in the litter August and Lior rigged from survey poles and the last good rope, but when the first full-sized fir slid past his eyeline he said, "Trees," in a voice like a man greeting a relative.

"Trees," August confirmed. He was walking sweep, eyes moving constantly—uphill, downhill, the ridgeline behind them. He hadn't stopped doing that since Saddleback. "Timber's open down slope for maybe two miles, then it thickens toward the river drainage. We'll make better time under canopy. Snow load's lighter."

Lior adjusted his grip on the litter handles. His shoulders had stopped hurting somewhere on day four; now they simply existed as a dull fact, like altitude. Ahead, through gaps in the firs, he could see the valley floor beginning its long green descent toward the lower river, toward Harrow Bend, toward the hearing that was either nineteen days away or eleven—he'd lost count twice and made August recount the calendar each morning.

Delia's head start had evaporated somewhere above the icefall. They all knew it. Nobody said it.

They were perhaps forty minutes into the trees when the smoke found them.

Not wildfire smoke—cooking smoke. Woodsmoke with fat in it, meat rendered over flame, the smell of a camp that expected to stay. Lior froze mid-step and August froze a half-second later, and for a moment the three of them stood in the fir shadow listening to a sound none of them had heard in weeks: someone else's fire.

"Off the trail," August breathed. "Now."

They wrestled the litter behind a deadfall thick with moss. August went forward alone, low, moving the way he moved through storm country—reading, not hiding. Lior watched him vanish between the trunks and counted his own heartbeats the way he counted contour lines: steadily, because panic was just bad cartography of the self.

August was back in under ten minutes, and his face was wrong.

"Ranger station," he said quietly. "Small. One man. Forest Service green, proper uniform, proper patches." He crouched by the litter. "He's got a radio mast up. Medical supplies laid out on a table like a display. And a sled."

"A sled," Renn repeated.

"He says—" August paused, and Lior understood he was choosing words with unusual care, "—he says there's an evacuation protocol for injured survey personnel. Storm damage took out the upper stations. He's been waiting for stragglers."

Renn pushed himself up on one elbow. The color that came into his face wasn't hope exactly—it was hungrier than hope, and Lior recognized it with a sick lurch because he'd seen it in mirrors during the first days in the cache cave, before starvation burned it out of him. It was the look of a man who wanted so badly for something to be true that truth became negotiable.

"One man," Renn said. "With a sled and morphine and a radio. August. I can't do another pass. You know I can't. My leg's septic at the edges—I can smell it. If we go down to the river and the crossing is bad, I'm dead weight you bury in a snowbank to save the ledger."

"Nobody's burying anybody," August said.

"I'm telling you what the arithmetic is." Renn's voice cracked but held. "One ranger. One sled. I go with him, you two run the ledger light and fast. That's not surrender, that's—"

"That's splitting the evidence from the witness," Lior said.

They both looked at him. He hadn't meant to speak. But the sentence was out, hanging in the cold air between the firs, and it was true, and he watched it land on Renn like a weight.

"If they take you," Lior said, more gently, "the ledger is just paper. Paper can be disputed. A witness who signed those pages—who *confessed* to signing them—that can't be forged away. They don't want your body, Renn. They want your testimony never to exist."

"And if I die on this mountain," Renn said, "my testimony doesn't exist anyway."

The argument sat there, perfectly balanced, perfectly reasonable on both sides, and Lior hated it—hated how good the trap was, how it used Renn's pain and their exhaustion and dressed itself in forest-service green and woodsmoke. He looked at August, expecting the transactional calculus, the miles-and-odds voice.

But August was staring downslope, toward the smoke, and his jaw was working like he was chewing on something that wouldn't swallow.

"Let me talk to him," August said finally. "Alone first. Standard practice—you don't bring the injured party into an unvetted contact. Renn, you stay here. Lior, flank left through the deadfall, keep eyes on me. If I scratch my head, you pull Renn uphill no matter what he says."

* * *

The ranger station was a plank hut with a shake roof, half-swallowed by the firs. A radio mast leaned against it, guyed with wire. The man outside was broad, weathered, wearing the uniform well—boots broken in, jacket patched at the elbows, a Forest Service badge on his chest and a name tape that read HOLT. He was tending a spit of trout over the fire, and he looked up with the easy, tired smile of a man who'd been alone too long.

"Lost season," he called. "You people are the first faces I've seen since the storm. Heard somebody's been moving through the high country—figured it was you or bears."

"Survey team," August said. "We got caught above the icefall."

"Figured as much." Holt nodded toward the table under the hut's lean-to. "I've got a med kit, a litter sled, plasma if you need it. There's a man hurt?"

"There is."

"Well, get him down here." Holt turned back to his fish, casual, competent, kind. "Radio's spotty but I raised the district office two nights ago. They know injured personnel are coming off the range. Protocol's protocol—we get him stabilized and evacuated by helicopter from the meadow below the ridge. Two days' travel at most, even slow."

Two days. Helicopter. Plasma. Every word was a warm hand on a frozen wound, and Lior, watching from the deadfall thirty meters out, felt the warmth and distrusted it in equal measure, because he had learned on this mountain that the things most designed to comfort him were the things built to kill him.

Holt flipped a trout. Steam rose. And then, still not looking up, in the same easy tone, he said:

"You'll want to leave the documents with me, though. Chain of custody. Can't have original records bouncing around a helicopter zone—one spark, one mishap, and twenty years of survey work burns up with the patient. District office will log them properly. Safer all around."

The fire popped.

Lior saw August go very still—the particular stillness of a man who has just heard the second half of a sentence that reveals the first half was bait. August's hand drifted up toward his head—and stopped. Didn't complete the signal. Held there, ambiguous, while his mouth kept smiling.

"Chain of custody," August echoed. "Sure. Let me check with our injured man. He's attached to those records."

"Natural," Holt said warmly. "Take your time. Fish is nearly done—your friend eats free."

Lior began easing back through the deadfall to report, and that was when the wind shifted, carrying the woodsmoke across him in a gust—and beneath the smoke, faint but unmistakable, diesel exhaust. Fresh. A vehicle idling somewhere behind the hut, engine running cold-weather rich, hidden in the tree shadow where no ranger's truck needed to hide.

And the wind brought him something else. The man's voice, carrying as voices carry in cold still air, calling cheerfully to August about salt and trout—

Lior knew that voice.

He had heard it once, at night, through the canvas wall of a survey tent, giving orders in a register pitched to be calm and therefore worse than shouting. *Check the map case. Burn the drafts. The old man signed, he'll sign again.* He had lain in his sleeping bag with his heart hammering and memorized that voice the way he memorized everything, because memory was the only weapon a cartographer had, and he had sworn in the dark that if he ever heard it again he would know it anywhere.

He knew it anywhere.

It wasn't Holt. The badge was real—probably lifted off a real ranger, maybe a real ranger who would never need it again. The uniform was real. The kindness was real, rehearsed, performed by a man who had done violence in the dark and could do it again in daylight, and the whole station—the sled, the plasma, the helicopter, the chain of custody—was a stage set with one exit.

Lior didn't think. Thinking was for people with time.

He crashed out of the deadfall shouting, "AUGUST—IT'S HIM—THE NIGHT RAID—RUN—"

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, the way violence always does—faster than narration, slower than memory.

Holt moved. That was the terrible thing, how fast he moved, the ranger-slouch dropping off him like a coat, one hand sweeping under his jacket. August was already turning, already low, and the shot went past him and splintered the deadfall where Lior had been standing a heartbeat earlier.

"DOWN!" August hit Lior at the waist and drove them both behind the water barrel, and the world became noise—two more shots, the spit collapsing, trout hissing into coals, Renn screaming Lior's name from upslope.

"The ledger!" Holt's voice had shed its warmth entirely, and hearing it stripped bare, Lior's whole body shook with recognition confirmed. Same voice. Same cadence. The night the tents burned. "Give me the ledger and the old man walks! Last offer!"

"He was never going to walk," August snarled, dragging Lior backward through the mud. "You don't leave witnesses. That's the whole business model!"

Lior's pack was on his shoulder—he'd never taken it off, some animal instinct—and the ledger tube was inside it, strapped against his spine where it had ridden for eleven days. He felt August's hand find the strap and grip it, anchoring them together.

"Uphill?" Lior gasped.

"Downhill. He'll expect uphill—high ground instinct. Downhill's the river drainage, thick timber, no sightlines." August's eyes flicked to the hut, calculating. "Sled's there. Med kit's there. We're leaving both."

"But Renn—"

"RENN!" August bellowed, rising half out of cover. "DROP THE LITTER AND RUN DOWNHILL! TREELINE STRAIGHT BELOW YOU! GO!"

A pause. Then Renn's voice, ragged with fury and terror: "You're abandoning the sled?!"

"I'M ABANDONING THE BAIT!"

Another shot answered him, punching through the barrel above their heads, brackish water gushing over them both. Holt was advancing, using the hut's corner, professional, unhurried—he had a rifle and they had nothing and he knew exactly how much that was worth.

Then Renn did the thing Lior would remember for the rest of his life.

Instead of running, the old surveyor hauled himself upright on his ruined leg, grabbed the litter's iron frame, and hurled it down the slope toward the station—a clattering, crashing, fifteen-kilogram projectile of scavenged aluminum that bounced off a stump and slammed into the hut's wall directly beside Holt's head.

It bought them four seconds. Four seconds of Holt flinching, of the muzzle swinging wrong, of August hauling Lior over the deadfall and into the timber.

They ran.

Downhill through firs, branches whipping Lior's face, the ledger tube slamming against his spine with every stride, Renn crashing along behind them making sounds no injured man should be able to make—adrenaline spending the body like stolen money. Behind them, Holt's voice rang through the trees, no longer performing anything:

"THEY'RE IN THE DRAINAGE! MARK IT! GRID SEVEN-TWO!"

Grid coordinates. Radios. A net closing from below, because of course there were others—there had always been others; the lone friendly ranger was never alone, that was the entire architecture of the lie.

They ran until the light went gray-green and thick, until the slope gentled into fern and creek-moss, until August finally called a halt in a hollow walled with deadfall, and the three of them folded down among the roots, chests heaving, steam rising off their bodies into the cold.

* * *

Inventory, when August finally forced them to take it, was brutal.

"We had food for five days," he said flatly, laying it out on a ground cloth. "Most of it was in the camp duffel we cached at the saddle when we rigged the litter. We're not going back for it. So." He lined up what remained: two energy bars, half a bag of oats, a tin of sardines, a handful of trail mix crushed to powder, a nearly full stove bottle, one pot, one tarp, the first-aid kit Lior carried, and—August patted his chest pocket and produced it with grim ceremony—a single chocolate bar, slightly melted, that he had apparently been hoarding since the boat.

"Dinner," he said, dividing it into three unequal pieces, giving the largest to Renn without comment.

Renn took it. His hands were shaking—not fear anymore, Lior realized, but the crash, the body presenting its bill. "I threw away our litter," Renn said. "Our only way to move me."

"You threw away their target marker," August said. "That litter was visible from a ridge a mile out. From tonight on, we move you the way they can't track—no frame, no bright fabric. Two poles, a tarp, brush lashed on top. Ugly and slow and invisible."

"And hungry," Renn said.

"And hungry." August didn't dress it up. "Two days of food for three men covering four days of ground. After that we're eating what the river gives us."

Lior sat with his back against a root, the ledger tube across his knees, and let the shaking come now that stopping was allowed. "I knew his voice," he said, to no one, to everyone. "From the raid. I lay in my bag and I memorized it and I told myself if I ever heard it again—" His throat closed. "Four seconds later and he shoots you both and takes the ledger and I'm the reason—"

"You're the reason we're alive," August said. "I was buying it, Lior. The sled, the plasma, the helicopter. I stood there doing chain-of-custody math like it was a real thing. You're the only one of us who heard the trap under the kindness." He looked at Renn. "Both of you. Renn threw a litter at a gunman. Nobody in this hollow gets to carry that scene alone."

Renn chewed his chocolate slowly. When he spoke, his voice was quiet and stripped of paranoia for the first time since the high camp. "I wanted it to be real," he said. "The ranger. The rescue. I wanted it so badly I would have handed them everything." He looked at the ledger tube in Lior's lap. "That's what they build, you understand. Not traps—*wants*. They find what you want and they wear it like a uniform." He exhaled. "My supervisor wanted his pension. I wanted absolution. Delia Marsh, whoever she is, probably wanted out. And you two—"

"Wanted the debt gone," August said.

"Wanted to belong somewhere," Lior said, surprising himself.

Silence in the hollow. Somewhere above them, faint, a vehicle door closed. The net, drawing in.

"So we stop wanting," Renn said, "until Harrow Bend."

"No," Lior said. He pulled the ledger tube tighter against his chest and looked at these two battered, lying, confessing men he had known for less than two weeks and would apparently trust with his life tomorrow. "We want *harder*. We just stop letting them pick what."

August looked at him for a long moment, and something shifted in his storm-reader's face—the expression Lior had started to recognize as the one August wore when the pressure dropped and the sky changed and he committed to a heading.

"River drainage bottoms out at the confluence," August said. "Three days, the way we'll have to travel. Then the lower river, and the water's up with the thaw pulses." He shouldered his pack, light now, cruelly light. "Get some sleep in shifts. At first light we move ugly and invisible."

Above them, in the darkening firs, the Syndicate's radios murmured to each other in grid coordinates, closing on a hollow they would not find empty at dawn—only cold ash, brushed-over tracks, and three men already moving downhill toward the water, carrying the truth the way Lior now understood it had to be carried: not safely, not comfortably, but *through*.
