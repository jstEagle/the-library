## 16. River of No Permits

The trail ended where the water began, and the water had opinions.

August stood at the lip of the cutbank with the pack straps biting his shoulders and watched the Kestrel River move like something that had been insulted. It wasn't a river anymore so much as a brown argument—thaw-swollen, carrying whole spruces sideways, its surface braided with boils and standing waves where submerged boulders turned the current into sculpture. The trail they'd followed for two days simply stopped at the bank, swallowed under three feet of churning meltwater, and picked up again on the far side as if nothing had happened.

"Well," Lior said beside him. "That's fitting."

"Fitting how?"

"Water rights." She gestured at the flood with the flat hand of a cartographer presenting evidence. "The whole reason any of this started. And now it's the thing standing between us and the hearing."

August almost laughed. Almost. His ribs were still bruised from the scramble at the treeline, and laughing cost more than he wanted to pay. "The river doesn't know what it's doing to us," he said. "That's the difference between it and the Syndicate."

Behind them, Renn Okafor lowered himself onto a fallen log with the careful, grinding slowness of a man whose leg had stopped being an asset somewhere around the saddle. Three days since the betrayal at the treeline. Three days since the fake ranger's face had come apart in Lior's hands—literally, in the end, when the man grabbed for the ledger and Lior broke free and they'd run downhill through deadfall while collectors crashed through the timber behind them. They'd lost most of the food. They'd lost the tent. They'd kept the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth against August's spine, and that was the only inventory that mattered.

"How far to Harrow Bend?" Renn asked.

"By trail, two days," August said. "By river—" He studied the current, the way he'd once studied a supercell rotating up out of a Kansas afternoon, looking for the seam in it. "Maybe six hours. If we don't drown."

Renn looked at the water. Then at August. "Those are not comforting odds stated back to back like that."

"No," August agreed. "They're not."

* * *

He made them camp early, which felt wrong with the clock burning in his chest—the permits expiring in four days now, the commission convening at Harrow Bend, Delia's head start long since spent—but he'd learned on the range that rushing a read was worse than losing an hour. You didn't chase a storm by driving straight at it. You drove to where it was going to be, and you got there before it did, and you waited. Stillness wasn't the enemy. Blind stillness was.

So they sat above the bank on a gravel shelf, and August watched the river the way other men watched television.

It had a pulse. That was the thing nobody who hadn't lived in flood country understood. A thaw-swollen river wasn't one continuous violence; it was a series of pulses, each one a slug of meltwater released from some high basin when the sun found it, traveling down the drainage in waves. Between pulses the river dropped—not much, maybe eight inches, sometimes less—but it dropped, and the character of the rapids changed with it. At pulse-peak, the lower gorge ran like a washing machine. In the trough between pulses, it ran fast but coherent, the holes breathing instead of chewing.

"You're doing the storm thing," Lior said. She'd settled next to him, knees drawn up, sketchbook open on her lap out of pure habit even though there was nothing left to draw but the river itself. She drew anyway. She always drew. August had decided it was less a profession than a nervous system.

"What storm thing?"

"The squinting. The counting." She nodded at his lips, which he realized were moving slightly, timing intervals. "You did it on the saddle when you called that wind shift twenty minutes before it hit. You do it when you're deciding something."

"I'm timing the pulses." He pointed downstream to where the gorge narrowed between granite walls. "See that willow on the point? Watch the waterline on it."

They watched. The brown water climbed the willow's stems, held, hung there for a long ugly minute—and then began to fall, slow as a tide going out.

"There," August said. "That's a pulse passing. Now we count till the next one climbs."

"And how long is that?"

"First one took eleven minutes. If the second interval matches, we've got a rhythm. Melt runs on a solar schedule—sun hits the high snowfields midmorning, the basins drain through the afternoon, pulses stack up. By evening they space out." He checked the sky. The light was already going amber through the smoke-hazed west, the sun sliding toward the ridgeline. "We run at dusk. Last big pulse, longest gap after it. We'd have maybe forty minutes of the best water we're going to get, and we'd hit the landing in the dark, which is bad, but hitting it in daylight during peak flow is worse."

Lior wrote something in her sketchbook. When he glanced over, she'd drawn the gorge in section—a cartographer's cross-section, the river rendered as layers, pulses marked like contour lines. "My supervisor used to say maps are just storms drawn slowly," she said. "I thought it was a joke. I think he meant it."

"Your supervisor signed a false page," Renn said from the log, without heat. Just fact. The whiteout had burned all the politeness out of the three of them; they'd confessed their worst into each other's faces over two days of starvation and cold, and what remained was something rawer and more durable than courtesy.

"He did," Lior said. "And he taught me that. Both things are true about him. I've stopped trying to make people be only one thing." She looked at August when she said it, and he pretended not to notice, and failed.

* * *

The raft was Lior's idea, which surprised him until he thought about it and then it didn't surprise him at all.

"Survey poles," she said, kicking the bundle they'd salvaged from the ransacked cache at the treeline camp—the aluminum staves the team had used for mapping control points, nine feet apiece, featherlight and absurdly strong. "Twelve of them. Lash them into two ladders, deck them with the torn map tubes and the ground tarp, rig thwarts. It won't be pretty."

"It'll be a raft," August said.

"It'll be a raft," she agreed, "which is a category of object that includes both 'vessel' and 'coffin,' and the difference is steering."

They built it through the last hour of light, and the work was good in the way that shared labor is good—the kind of good August had spent thirty years avoiding because it required staying in one place with the same people long enough for the seams to show. But the seams had shown already, all of them, in a crevasse shelter during a blizzard, and there was nothing left to hide behind, so he just worked. Renn held poles for lashing despite his leg, directing the lashings with the fussy precision of a man who'd rigged field stations for a decade. Lior tied the wraps—she tied them beautifully, some old scout knot from wherever she'd grown up, cinched down with her boot for leverage. August drilled the holes and kept the count and, without announcing it, made every decision that mattered: where the thwarts went, where the rope would run, who would sit where.

Him at the stern with the sweep oar—he'd cut a paddle from a downed cottonwood, shaped it crude with his knife. Lior amidships with a shorter paddle. Renn forward, low, holding on, because his leg couldn't brace and everyone knew it and nobody said it.

The ledger went into the dry bag, and the dry bag went under the center thwart, lashed twice.

"If we flip," Renn said, testing the lashing, "the bag floats."

"If we flip," August said, "you swim for the bag before you swim for air. I'm serious. Air you can find anywhere. That bag exists exactly once in the world."

"That's a terrible survival philosophy."

"It's the only one that matters this week."

Dusk came down purple and cold. The river had gone quiet in the way of the trough between pulses—the boil lines smoothing, the roar dropping to a deep sustained note like a held organ chord. August stood at the edge and timed the willow one more time. Waterline steady. Eleven minutes since the last crest. Maybe twelve minutes of grace, and then the next slug of melt would come down the gorge and turn it hostile.

"We go now," he said.

* * *

The first mile was almost kind, and he distrusted it completely.

The raft ran true—better than true, actually; Lior's lashings had given it a flex that absorbed the chop instead of fighting it—and the current carried them at a speed that made the banks blur. August stood braced at the stern, sweep in both hands, reading the water ahead the way he read cloud structure: not the surface but the tells underneath. A V of smooth water pointing downstream meant a channel. A line of white across the flow meant a ledge rock, go around. Downstream-pointing Vs of white meant rocks buried shallow, avoid the apex. Upstream-pointing Vs meant holes—deep, recirculating, hungry. He called them to Lior in short words, and she ferried the middle of the raft left or right with her paddle, and the raft obeyed, and something in his chest that had been clenched since the treeline began, very slowly, to unclench.

This was the part he'd never told anyone. Not the chasing—everyone assumed the chasing was the addiction, the adrenaline, the spectacle of the tornado eating a barn a half-mile away. It wasn't. The addiction was this: the moment when the chaos resolved into pattern. When the thing that could kill you became legible, became a text you could read faster than it could act. Standing in front of a wall cloud with the wind shifting around his ears and knowing—knowing, not guessing—that it would drop its funnel two miles northeast, and being there when it did. The world rewarding attention. Mastery, reclaimed, every single time, because the world forgot you overnight and made you earn it again.

He'd told himself for years that he loved motion. Sitting in the truck outside a dying supercell at 2 a.m., engine ticking, no place to be tomorrow, he'd finally admitted the truth: he didn't love motion. He hated stillness, and those weren't the same thing. Motion was just the cheapest way to keep the world unread. Because a place you stayed became a place you knew, and a place you knew could disappoint you, and a person you stayed for could leave, or die, or ask you for something you couldn't give.

The river asked him for everything, right now, and it was the easiest request he'd had in weeks.

"Rock!" Lior shouted, and he saw it a half-second later—a fang of granite off the bow, the current piling against it in a glassy hump. He leaned the sweep, hauled, felt the raft pivot under him like a living thing, and they slid past close enough to touch it, the eddy line grabbing the stern and swinging them wide into clean water.

"Nice," Renn called from the bow, and then, immediately, "hole, hole, HOLE—"

"I see it." He saw it. A monster, right of center, the upstream V marking a recirculation the size of a truck bed. He threw the raft left, harder than he wanted to, hard enough that Lior went to one knee, and the current took the hull and flung them past the hole's shoulder so close he could feel the cooler air rising off it, that distinct breath a hydraulic exhales, wet and hollow.

And then the gorge closed.

* * *

He'd seen it from the bank and known it was coming, but knowing and entering were different animals. The walls pinched to maybe sixty feet of gray granite, the river compressed between them into a single chute of white, and the sound went from organ chord to freight train. There was no eddy. There was no shore. There was the line, and the line was the only thing in the world.

"Center!" he bellowed. "Paddle! Don't steer, paddle!"

They paddled. The raft hit the first wave square and climbed it—climbed absurdly, the bow lifting, Renn flattened against the deck with both fists in the lashings—and the world went white and loud and then they were down the backside, slamming into the trough, water sheeting over all three of them, and the next wave was already there. He stopped thinking in words. Words were too slow. He steered the way he breathed, weight and blade and angle, the raft a thing attached to his hands, and the gorge poured them through its throat in ninety seconds that contained no thoughts at all, only the read and the response, the read and the response.

Then the walls fell away, the river spread, and they were through.

The silence afterward was enormous. The raft bobbed in the widened channel, shedding water, all three of them soaked and gasping and alive. Renn started laughing first—a cracked, disbelieving sound—and then Lior, wiping her hair out of her eyes, and then August himself, a laugh that hurt his ribs and he didn't care.

"You—" Lior pointed at him, breathless. "You were smiling. The whole time. Through all of it. You were smiling."

"Was I?"

"You were. Like it was a storm chase. Like this is fun for you."

He looked downstream, where the last light lay copper on the water, and let himself answer honestly, because the whiteout had established the policy. "It's the only time I ever feel honest," he said. "When something's trying to kill me and I read it right anyway. Everything else feels like a forgery." He heard the word leave his mouth—forgery—and thought of permits, of ledgers, of a confession with his name on it waiting in some Syndicate lawyer's drawer. "Everything else."

Lior was quiet a moment, watching him with the look she gave maps. "Well," she said finally. "Tomorrow you testify. That's stillness that counts."

"That's the plan."

* * *

They came around the last bend in full darkness, and August killed their momentum against an eddy below the Harrow Bend landing, holding the raft in the slack water behind a gravel bar, and put a hand on Lior's arm before she could push off.

Lights.

Not many. Two lanterns at the landing dock, and the low shape of a vehicle parked beyond them, and a figure moving between the lights with a purposeful walk he recognized from a hundred miles of range and a hundred radio calls.

Delia Marsh.

His stomach dropped through the bottom of the raft. Of course she'd be here. The order had changed—he'd known it changed since the treeline, since the scout posing as a ranger, since elimination replaced recovery somewhere in a Syndicate office that smelled like carpet cleaner. Whatever Delia had done with her day's head start, she'd been ordered to this landing, and she was here, and the ledger was lashed six feet from her reach.

"She'll have backup," Renn whispered.

"Or not," Lior said. "Look at her. Look how she's walking."

August looked. Delia walked the dock alone, stopped at the end, and stood facing the river. Not searching the banks. Not sweeping a flashlight along the waterline. Just standing, a lantern at her feet, like a woman waiting for someone she'd arranged to meet.

She raised one hand—not a signal, not a weapon. Just raised it, palm out, toward the dark water, and held it there.

August sat in the eddy with the sweep across his knees, the current pulling patiently at the raft, and made the read. Storm or clear sky. Trust or run. Thirty years of motion had taught him every way there was to flee a moment like this one, and the strange thing, the thing that would have shocked the man who'd taken Delia's deal on that first day, was that fleeing didn't even tempt him.

"Stay in the eddy," he said quietly. "If I raise my voice, run the river without me."

He pushed the raft out of the slack water and let the current carry him toward the lanterns, toward the woman who'd bought him and might have freed him, and the river took him straight and true into whatever came next.
