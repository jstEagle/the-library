## 17. Delia's Choice

The landing at Harrow Bend was a gravel shelf and a rusted winch, and Delia Marsh had been standing on it for six hours watching the river decide what it wanted to be.

The thaw had come early and mean. The Kestrel ran brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways, slamming them against the pilings of the old footbridge downstream with a sound like artillery fired underwater. She'd seen floods like this before—in the basin country, the year Meridian bought out three farm loans in a single spring and called it *community renewal*. The water always looked the same when it was taking something.

Her partner stood twenty feet behind her, checking his sidearm for the fourth time.

"You want to tell me again why we're not just waiting at the truck?" Voss asked. He was a big man, careful with his hands, the kind of collector who filed his mileage reports to the decimal. Eleven years with the Syndicate. He still wore the lapel pin—the little brass circle, the open ledger embossed on it, their motto curved beneath: *A Community of Optimists.*

"Because Sandoval will read a vehicle before he reads us," Delia said. "He's spent his life reading things that are about to go wrong. We give him a truck, he gives us a river."

"And if he gives us a river anyway?"

"Then we get wet."

Voss grunted and went back to his weapon. Delia turned her face to the wind coming off the water and let herself feel, for one unguarded second, how far she had traveled from the woman who'd pinned that brass circle on her own collar at twenty-six, in a rented hall in Corvane, while a regional director with kind eyes told her she wasn't taking a job—she was joining a promise. *We believe people pay their debts,* he'd said. *We believe in second chances. We're optimists.*

She had believed him. That was the part she could never forgive—not them, but herself, the ease of it, how good it had felt to be handed a creed that made collection sound like care. *Every account is a person who believed in tomorrow.* She'd written that line herself, once, in an internal memo. It had gotten her promoted.

Now she stood on a gravel bar with orders folded in her breast pocket, and the orders said:

*Subject Sandoval to be intercepted at Harrow Bend landing. Assets (documents) to be secured. Secondary subject (Vassek) and tertiary subject (Okafor)—see amended directive 7-C.*

Amended directive 7-C did not use the word *eliminate*. The Syndicate never used words like that. It said *assets beyond recovery are to be written down.* It said *liability closure protocols apply.* It said, in the passive grammar of men who signed things in boardrooms with rivers running through them, *no residual exposure will be tolerated.*

Delia had read it four times on the drive in. Each time, the sentence had gotten heavier, until by the fourth reading it weighed what a body weighs.

"Sandoval's a good one," Voss said, conversationally, holstering the pistol. "You know he paid eleven months straight before the clinic bills took him under? I pulled the file. Never missed a payment until he couldn't stand up. There aren't many like that anymore."

"I know his file."

"He'll come down off that mountain half-dead and grateful, probably. Guy like that, you offer him the forgiveness again, he'll hand over whatever the kid's carrying and thank us for the ride." Voss smiled at the river. "That's the thing about debtors. They always think they're the ones being rescued."

Delia said nothing. The wind pushed at her jacket, at the paper inside it, as if it knew.

* * *

They came down off the ridge at 4:40 in the afternoon, and they were exactly as broken as she'd expected.

Sandoval first—a tall, weathered man moving with the deliberate economy of someone who has stopped trusting his own legs—and behind him a young cartographer, Lior Vassek, supporting a third figure on a litter made of survey poles and rope. The third man was gray-faced, one leg bound in splinted branches, conscious but barely. Two days of blizzard were written on all of them: frost-nipped cheeks, clothes stiff with ice-melt, the hollow-eyed look of people who had eaten their last ration somewhere above the treeline and kept walking anyway.

And Sandoval was carrying a canvas document case strapped across his chest like a heart.

Delia stepped out from behind the winch house with her credentials already in her hand, because that was the choreography—credentials first, warmth second, the paperwork always gentler than what it authorized. Voss flanked right, casual, hands loose. She saw Sandoval clock him instantly, saw the storm-reader in him read this too: two collectors at a landing, no search party ever mounted, twelve days of silence nobody investigated. She watched him do the arithmetic and arrive at the number she'd arrived at alone in her truck, four times, on the drive in.

"August Sandoval." She kept her voice level. "Meridian Syndicate, Recovery Division. You know why we're here."

"I know why you're here," Sandoval said. His voice was wrecked—two days of cold and smoke and whatever had happened up there—but steady. "Do you?"

Behind him, the young one—Vassek—shifted, putting himself slightly between the collectors and the injured man. Protecting Okafor. Delia noted it the way she noted everything now, with a collector's eye she couldn't switch off and didn't entirely want: these three had become a unit. Somewhere on that mountain, in a crevasse or a snow cave, they had become something the Syndicate's ledgers had no column for.

"The documents," she said. "Hand them over, and your account closes today. Full forgiveness. That was the deal."

"That was the deal I was offered," Sandoval said. "It's not the deal that exists."

Voss laughed, easy and warm. "August. Come on. You're freezing, your friend there needs a hospital, and you're standing in a river valley quoting conspiracy at us. Give the lady the case. We've got a truck with heat and a stretcher in it. Everybody walks away."

*Everybody walks away.* Delia heard the phrase land and knew—knew with the flat certainty of eleven years—that Voss believed it, or had decided to believe it, which amounted to the same thing in the end. That was the machine's genius. It didn't require monsters. It required people willing to hear *write-down* and translate it into *walks away* without asking what got written off.

"Directive 7-C," she said quietly.

Voss's smile didn't move, but something behind it did. "Marsh."

"Read it to me, Voss. Out loud. Tell me what happens to the cartographer and the surveyor after we secure the assets." She took a step forward, gravel crunching. "Because I've read it, and I can't find the word *rescue* anywhere in it. I can't find any word for what happens to them except the ones the lawyers invented so nobody has to say the real one."

The river filled the silence, brown and enormous.

"You're tired," Voss said gently. "You've been in the field nineteen days. This is fatigue talking."

"This is me talking. For the first time in eleven years." Her hand found the paper in her pocket. "I believed the pitch, Voss. I believed it so hard I wrote recruiting copy for it. Every account is a person who believed in tomorrow—I wrote that. And then I watched us repossess a dialysis machine from a woman in Corvane because her husband's co-signed loan crossed our desk on the wrong week. And I told myself the ledger doesn't lie. But the ledger doesn't tell the truth either. It just counts."

She pulled the directive out and held it up between them.

"So here's my count. Three witnesses. One set of documents proving the Syndicate financed a concession built on forged permits—our capital, our signature, our fraud. And an order that says when we find them, we close the liability." She looked at Sandoval, at Vassek, at the ruined man on the litter who was watching her with the wary, calculating eyes of someone deciding whether this was one more trap. "I'm done counting for them."

"Delia." Voss's voice dropped, lost its warmth. "Step back. That's a direct order structure you're interfering with. Board-level."

"Yes."

"You'll lose everything. Pension, license, clearance. They'll bury you in litigation until you're seventy."

"I know what it costs. I priced it on the drive in." She turned to Sandoval. "Give me the case."

Sandoval didn't move. "Why should I trust you?"

"You shouldn't. Not yet." She held his gaze. "But ask yourself why I'm telling you about 7-C with my partner standing right there, when the smart play—the safe play, the *paid* play—is to take the case and let the protocol run. Ask yourself what I have left to gain." She almost smiled, and it hurt. "I had a head start I gave you at the high camp. A day. I told myself it was procedure, a scheduling error, something I could take back if I needed to. It wasn't. Nothing about this is take-back-able anymore. So yes, Sandoval. Trust me the way you'd trust weather. Not because it's kind. Because it's true."

Something shifted in his face—the look of a man reading a sky that had finally told him the truth.

He unslung the case and held it out. She took it. It weighed less than she expected. Most damning things did.

* * *

Voss moved when she keyed her radio.

Later, she would understand it perfectly: he heard the channel click, heard her request for a Commission relay instead of the Syndicate's private band, and understood in the same instant she did that the moment the documents reached the regional land commission, directive 7-C became evidence. He was eleven years invested. He moved the way the invested always do—fast, certain, reaching for the pistol rather than the argument.

She was faster, because she'd been expecting it since Corvane, since the dialysis machine, since every small surrender she'd ever dressed up as pragmatism finally came due with interest. She had the weapon leveled at his chest before his cleared leather, both hands, stance wide, the way they trained everyone and then hoped nobody would need it.

"Don't," she said.

Voss froze, gun half-drawn, and looked at her across eight feet of gravel and eleven years, and his face did something terrible and human: it pleaded. "Delia. They'll do it anyway. Someone else will run 7-C if we don't. You know how the board works. You're not saving anyone, you're just going down with them."

"Then I go down with them." Her voice didn't shake, and she was grateful for that, at least. "But they go down *testifying*, Voss. In front of the commission. On the record. With the permits expiring in—" she checked nothing, because she'd memorized it, "—four days. Somebody else can try to stop that. Let them try doing it in daylight."

"They'll burn you."

"Probably."

"They'll take the pension. The license. Everything you built."

Delia looked at him—at the brass pin on his collar catching the last of the light, the little open ledger, the optimistic circle—and felt the strangest sensation, which was lightness. Nineteen days in the field, eleven years in the machine, and it came down to this: the weight she'd been carrying had never been the debt or the job or even the order in her pocket. It had been the distance between what she'd said she believed and what she'd done. And the distance was closing.

"It turns out," she said, "everything I built was the problem."

Voss holstered the gun. Or started to—she never learned how far the decision went, because she cuffed him to the winch housing with his own restraints, standard procedure, hands in front, dignity preserved, the way you treat a colleague. Because he was one. That was the worst of it. He'd shared her truck, her coffee, her belief, once. She read him his rights under the Syndicate's own conduct code—the internal one, Article Fourteen, *conduct bringing the community into disrepute*—and the irony of using their scripture against them sat in her chest like a coal.

Then she keyed the radio, Commission emergency band, open frequency, and spoke clearly enough that every word would survive transcription.

"Harrow Bend landing, this is Delia Marsh, Meridian Syndicate Recovery Division, badge 4471. I am placing myself and one detained colleague at the disposal of the regional land commission. I am in possession of original field documentation evidencing falsified water permits on the Kestrel concession, financed by the Meridian Syndicate. I also have three witnesses: August Sandoval, Lior Vassek, and Renn Okafor." She paused, and chose the next words the way you choose footing on rotten ground. "The documents and witnesses are in protective custody. My custody. Effective immediately."

Static. Then the dispatcher's voice, thin and astonished: *"Say again your status, Marsh?"*

"You heard me. And log the time. The permit clock is still running."

She lowered the radio. The river went on hauling its trees toward the sea, indifferent, enormous, honest in the only way water knows how to be.

Sandoval was looking at her with an expression she'd seen on chasers' faces in old footage—the moment after the tornado misses the house, when relief and grief arrive in the same breath and neither can be told apart. Vassek had both hands pressed flat against Okafor's shoulder, holding him to the world, and the injured man was murmuring something that might have been thanks or might have been a map coordinate; with cartographers it was hard to say.

"What happens to you now?" Sandoval asked.

Delia looked at the truck with its heat and its stretcher, at the man cuffed to the winch, at the mountains going violet behind them where two days ago she'd given three strangers a day's grace and burned her exit behind them without knowing it.

"Now?" She slung the document case across her own chest, the way he'd carried it, heart-position, and found it fit. "Now we drive to Harrow Bend. All of us. And on Monday morning, I walk into a hearing room full of the Syndicate's lawyers, and I testify."

She opened the truck door. The heater breathed out something almost like mercy.

"And after that," she said, mostly to herself, "I find out who I am when the ledger's empty."

The engine turned over. Behind them, the first flakes of the next storm were already falling on the range they'd come down from—falling, too, on the city where the board was meeting, on the files where 7-C waited to be entered into evidence, on everything the flood season would soon wash clean or carry away.

Four days to the hearing. Delia drove north into the snow, and for the first time in eleven years, she wasn't collecting anything.
