## 22. Lior and the Current

The ice went out of the Kestrel on a Tuesday, with a sound like a door being opened very slowly in a very large house.

Lior stood on the bank at Harrow Bend and listened to it—the groan and shift of the breakup, plates of white grinding downstream, the river underneath running brown and fast and alive. All winter the water had been a rumor under the ice. Now it was back, and it was telling the truth about itself: where it ran deep, where it had cut new channels through the flood year, where the old survey maps were wrong.

He had come out here every morning for two weeks with his sketchbook, watching the same hundred meters of river change its mind. The commission's hydrologists would arrive in April with their instruments and their official forms. But instruments measured what was there; they didn't ask what had been there before, or what wanted to be there next. That part was still his job. That part had always been his job—he just hadn't known, until last autumn, how easy it was to do it dishonestly, or how heavy the honest version weighed in your hands.

The original field ledger sat in a fireproof box at the regional archive now, catalogued as Exhibit A, its pages photographed and certified and argued over by men in good coats. Renn had gone down to testify at the evidentiary hearings twice already, walking on the repaired leg, refusing the offered chair each time. *I'll stand,* he told them. *I did enough sitting while you people made up your minds.* Lior had watched him say it and thought: that is what courage looks like when it has been broken once and set badly. It doesn't stand straight. It stands anyway.

But the ledger was history. The river was present tense, and the river was his.

He opened the sketchbook against his knee and began the morning's work: the bend below the confluence, where the spring melt had carved a side channel through what the falsified permit maps called stable ground. He drew the channel first, then the willows leaning into it, then the gravel bar that hadn't existed in October. His lines were quick and unhesitating. Somewhere over the winter he had stopped drawing tentatively, stopped second-guessing the pencil halfway through a stroke. You couldn't map a watershed honestly if you flinched at every contour. The land didn't flinch. It simply said what it was, and waited for you to catch up.

A truck door slammed up at the pullout behind him.

He didn't turn around. He knew the sound of that particular door—August never closed anything gently, not doors, not arguments, not chapters of his life—and he knew the rhythm of the boots that followed it, unhurried, slightly favoring the left foot where the frostbite had bitten deepest on Saddleback. August came down the bank and stopped beside him, hands in the pockets of a canvas coat that had been new in September and was now the color of the landscape it had absorbed.

"You're staring at the water again," August said.

"I'm working."

"You're staring at the water."

"Staring at the water *is* the work." Lior tapped the page. "You can't draw a current until you've watched it lie to you three times. It goes around the point like it's going to hold that course forever, and then the bar shifts and everything you drew is a beautiful fiction. Same as permits. Same as promises." He glanced up. "You're early."

"Couldn't sleep." August crouched, forearms on his knees, and looked at the river the way he looked at storm cells on the horizon—not admiring it, exactly. Reading it. "Ice went out loud?"

"Loud enough to wake the whole valley. Mrs. Aldous at the trading post says she thought it was the mountain coming down." Lior closed the book. "You could have slept another hour. We're not launching until the gauge drops."

"I know." August was quiet a moment. The water filled the silence, which was one of the things Lior had learned to love about it—it never left a conversation stranded. "I drove out past the county line this morning," August said finally. "Just habit. Got to the sign, pulled over, sat there with the engine running."

"And?"

"And I sat there for twenty minutes like an idiot, and then I turned around." He shrugged, but Lior saw the thing underneath the shrug, the small astonished man still living inside the restless one. "Twenty years, that drive ended somewhere else. Every time. Different somewhere, same ending. This morning it ended here."

Lior looked at him—at the gray coming into the beard, the scar on the knuckles from the rope bridge, the eyes that had spent so long measuring distance from everything and now, without ever announcing it, had stopped measuring. In the crevasse, starving, wrapped together under a tarp while the blizzard screamed, August had confessed the debt deal in a voice like a man reading his own autopsy. *I was paid to bury you.* And Lior had confessed running. And Renn had confessed the page he'd signed. Three liars in a hole in the ice, and somehow that was where the truth between them got built—not on virtue, which none of them had enough of, but on flaws laid open and found load-bearing.

"You could have slept another hour," Lior said again, softer.

"Yeah." August almost smiled. "Habit's a hard current to get out of."

* * *

They launched at midmorning, when the gauge read eleven inches below flood stage and falling.

The boat was one of the survey company's old freight canoes, repossessed along with everything else when the concession was revoked, and bought back cheap at the liquidation auction by the watershed cooperative that was forming itself out of the wreckage—Renn's idea, funded by the settlement, staffed so far by exactly three people and a rotating cast of skeptical ranchers. The cooperative's first mandate was written on a single index card taped inside the bow: *Map what is there.* Four words. Renn had fought for a week to keep it that short. Every longer version, he said, was a place to hide something.

August took the stern. He'd signed on as guide two weeks ago, in the same offhand way he did everything that mattered—shrugging, looking at the river instead of at Lior, saying *somebody who knows these waters should probably not drown alone* as if the whole arrangement were a joke he was letting them in on. But he'd shown up the next morning with the canoe patched and a tide-and-gauge log started in a waterproof notebook, and he'd been at the pullout every day since, early, unable to sleep, driving to the county line and turning around.

"Side channel's open," Lior called from the bow, paddle reading the water ahead of them. "Deeper than it looks. The bar moved east over the winter."

"The bar always moves east after a big melt." August adjusted his stroke without being asked, feathering the blade, and the canoe slid into the channel like it had been invited. "Same as the storms always build on the dry line. Everything in this country runs on patterns. People just don't stay in one place long enough to learn them."

"That used to be a compliment to yourself."

"It was never a compliment. I just dressed it up nice." August's voice carried no bitterness—only the flat honesty he'd been practicing all winter, the skill of saying true things without bracing for impact. "Man measures his life in miles, he gets real efficient at leaving. Turns out efficiency isn't the same as knowing where you're going. I could cross this range blindfolded and I couldn't have told you, any single night of those twenty years, why."

"And now?"

"Now I'm learning seasons." He nodded at the banks sliding past—the willows breaking bud, a pair of mergansers rocketing upstream low over the water, the last dirty snowbanks retreating into the coulee shadows. "Everything here happens on a calendar older than any permit. Ice-out. Green-up. Spawn. You don't outrun a season. You don't buy your way out of one. You just live inside it until it turns, and then you live inside the next one." A pause, and then, drier: "Turns out that's the whole job. Nobody told me the whole job was so simple. Probably because nobody could make a fortune off it."

Lior laughed and let the canoe run. Below them the channel rejoined the main stem, and the current took hold—strong, cold, certain—and for a while neither of them paddled much. There was no need. They were going with it.

* * *

They camped that night on the gravel bar below the old high-water mark, where the survey team's stake 14 still stood, leaning, its aluminum tag catching the last light.

Lior walked out to it after supper while August fed the stove. The tag read *KESTREL CONCESSION — SURVEY CONTROL*, and beneath that a number that had once anchored a lattice of lies: boundary lines drawn through indigenous grazing grounds that had been occupied for centuries and recorded as vacant; flow measurements invented at a desk in Meridian City; dates forged backward to leapfrog claims that predated the Syndicate's financing by a generation. Renn had signed one page of it. The lead cartographer, Amara Voss, had refused to sign any of it, and men had come at night, and Lior had run, and the ledger that undid it all had waited eight months in a steel cache at the high camp for someone brave enough and stubborn enough to carry it down.

He put his hand flat on the tag. Cold metal, warming toward evening.

The commission had voided the permits in December. The revocation order had come down in February, thirty-one pages of legal language that boiled down, Renn said, to four words of its own: *it was never theirs.* An investigation into the Syndicate's financing was ongoing. Delia Marsh had testified for three days, composed and merciless, her career burned down to its honest core, and when a reporter asked her afterward if she regretted it, she'd said, *I regret that it took me that long,* and walked out through the snow without waiting for the next question.

And the land itself—the actual land, the water, the gravel, the willows—had noticed none of it. That was the lesson Lior kept returning to, the one he was building the new maps around. The river had run true the entire time. Through the fraud and the hearing and the blizzard and the betrayal at the treeline, the water had gone on doing exactly what water does, keeping its own records in silt and cutbank and the bones of dead channels, patient beyond any human patience, waiting for someone to finally read it correctly.

He took out the sketchbook and drew stake 14 as it was: leaning, obsolete, half-swallowed by the spring grass that was already coming up around it. Then, because the new maps allowed such things, he wrote a caption beneath it in small careful letters: *Former control point. Superseded by the truth.*

"Supper," August called. "Before it freezes, which up here it might, in April, because this country has no respect for calendars either."

Lior pocketed the book and walked back across the bar. The fire was small and well-built, the way August built everything now—economically, to last the night. Two tin plates. A pot of beans with the dried morels Lior had found on the south-facing slope above camp, because August had taught him which slopes to search and Lior had taught him which names the old people gave those slopes, and the knowledge had braided together somewhere over the winter until neither of them could remember who'd contributed which strand.

They ate watching the river. The light went long and coppery, then rose-colored, then blue.

"Renn radioed from the archive," Lior said eventually. "The hydrologists confirmed the side channel. It's going on the official base map. Our line, my drawing, credited." He shook his head slowly. "First thing I've ever drawn that a government is going to print. Feels strange. Like being trusted with something breakable."

"You won't drop it." August said it plainly, without ceremony, the way he said the true things now. "You carried a ledger down a mountain through a blizzard with two half-dead men and a collector on your tail. You don't drop things."

"We carried it."

"We carried it," August agreed, and let it stand.

The stars came out in the cold snap after dark, hard and close. August banked the fire and rolled into his bag, and within minutes his breathing went slow and even—the sleep of a man who had stopped running, which Lior had learned was deeper than the sleep of a man who was merely tired. Lior stayed up a while longer with the sketchbook on his knees, finishing the day's sheets by firelight: the channel, the bar, the stake, the camp, the river running black and silver under the first stars. On the last page he drew the two of them as he'd seen them from the water that afternoon—a canoe, two figures, moving with the current instead of against it—and beneath it, after a long moment, he wrote the title he'd been carrying in his head since the ice went out.

Not a map title. Something better. Something that was also a promise.

*The current carries forward,* he wrote. *This time we go with it.*

He closed the book, banked himself into his bag beside the fire, and lay listening to the river work through the dark—cutting, carrying, depositing, keeping its honest accounts—and above it the sound of August breathing, steady as a gauge needle, and above that the enormous patient silence of the range they had crossed and would cross again, season after season, for as long as the maps needed making and there were two people stubborn enough to make them.

Spring had come to the Kestrel. The water was high, and clear about itself, and going somewhere.

So were they.
