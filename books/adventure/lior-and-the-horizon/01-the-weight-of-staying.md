## 1. The Weight of Staying

The town was called Puerto Salado, which was a joke, because there was nothing salty about it except the air and the way everything you owned eventually corroded. I'd been there eleven weeks, which by my standards was practically a marriage.

I was working a salvage dig behind the old cannery — a collapsed warehouse floor from the 1890s, silted in with a century of fish guts and broken crockery and the occasional coin that made the town council's eyes go wet with dreams of heritage tourism. My job was to catalogue what came out of the mud before the developers poured their foundation over it. It was honest work. It was temporary work. That second part was the part I liked.

"You're good at this," said Dr. Imelda Ferrán on a Tuesday morning in late October, standing at the edge of my trench with her hands jammed in her cardigan pockets like she was bracing for weather. "I mean it. You read soil like other people read street signs."

"Thank you," I said, because I had learned that when someone is about to ruin your week, it's polite to let them get through the preamble.

"The university wants to make the position permanent. Site director. Your name's already on the paperwork." She smiled the way people smile when they think they're handing you a gift. "Gideon. This is a life."

I looked down at the trench. There was a blue willow-pattern saucer half-emerged from the clay, its rim broken into a curve that looked almost like a smile if you were feeling generous, or like a wound if you weren't. Around it, the stratigraphy told the whole story of a family that had lived above this spot for forty years and then simply stopped: a layer of fine domestic ash, a layer of river flood, a layer where the pottery changed from hand-painted to factory-stamped and nobody bothered to write down why.

"This *is* a life," I said. "It's just not mine."

"Gideon—"

"I'll finish the season. Six more weeks. The report will be clean, the catalogue will be done, and whoever you hire after me will thank me for leaving the notes legible." I climbed out of the trench and dusted off my knees. "But permanent isn't something I do."

Imelda studied me with the particular patience of a woman who had spent thirty years coaxing secrets out of uncooperative ground. "You know what an archaeologist would say about you? If you were one of my sites?"

"I'm afraid to ask."

"They'd say the occupation layers are thin. Lots of them, all over the map, but thin. Nobody lives anywhere long enough to leave anything worth finding." She shrugged. "Just an observation. From a professional."

She walked off toward the cannery, and I stood there in the wind off the water feeling precisely as seen as she'd intended, which was to say: flayed, lightly salted, and hung out to dry.

* * *

That night I packed anyway. Not because of what Imelda said — or not only because of it — but because packing was the one ritual I still trusted. Other people had prayer; I had a duffel bag with a system. Books wrapped in shirts. Tools nested in their foam like surgical instruments. Documents in the waterproof pouch that went against my spine, always, everywhere, so that even if I lost everything else I'd still be able to prove who I was, which was a thing I'd needed to do more often than most people.

Eleven weeks. It was getting worse, I noticed. When I was twenty-two I could stay somewhere four days before the itch started under my skin. At twenty-five, two weeks. Now the clock began running almost as soon as I arrived, some internal hourglass turning over whether I wanted it to or not, sand hissing down in the dark while I slept. Eleven weeks in Puerto Salado was not restraint. It was a symptom.

Here is what I told myself, and I want to be clear that I believed it completely, the way you can believe anything if you rehearse it enough: I wasn't running from anything. I was a specialist. Salvage archaeology required mobility. Sites were ephemeral; contracts ended; it would be professionally irresponsible to put down roots over a trench that was scheduled for demolition in January anyway. Staying would have been sentimental. Sentimentality was how sites got looted and reports got padded and careers got buried. I was being rigorous. I was being *clean*.

The fact that rigor felt exactly like relief — that the moment I decided to leave, my shoulders dropped an inch I hadn't known they'd climbed — was a coincidence. Obviously.

I had the bag half-packed by midnight when the knock came. Not at my door — at the guesthouse office downstairs, followed by Señora Ibáñez's slippers slapping up the stairs, followed by her voice through the door, thick with sleep and grievance: "*Hay carta para ti.* A letter. At this hour the post boy brings letters. In my day—"

I took the envelope through a six-inch gap in the door, because Señora Ibáñez considered closed doors a personal insult and I had learned to manage her the way you manage weather.

The envelope was heavy stock, cream-colored, and it had crossed an ocean to get here. The return address meant nothing to me and everything: a field station designation, a mountain range I'd only ever seen on maps, a country I'd been carefully not thinking about for three years.

And the handwriting. That was the thing that stopped me with my thumb under the flap. Handwriting is a fingerprint you can't fake without effort, and this one I knew better than my own — the sharp ascenders, the way she crossed her t's like she was striking them through, the little hook on the V of *Voss* that made it look like a bird taking off.

Dr. Elena Voss. My mother. Three years of silence, delivered by a post boy at midnight to a rented room above a woman who hated doors.

I sat on the bed with the letter unopened for a long time. Long enough that the lamp buzzed and the wind changed direction and somewhere below a dog gave up on barking at the sea. Because here's the arithmetic they don't teach you about estrangement: every year of silence makes opening the next letter cost more. Silence compounds. Interest accrues. By year three, the envelope isn't paper anymore — it's a door, and you have no idea what's standing on the other side, and you know — you *know* — that whatever it is, it's going to be holding something you said, or didn't say, back when you were both stupider and louder.

I opened it eventually. Of course I did. I've never once in my life left a context unexcavated.

*Gideon.*

No endearment. We'd never been an endearment family. Even in the good years — and there had been good years, I had to keep reminding myself there had been good years, a whole childhood of them, camp kitchens and star charts and her voice reading site reports aloud like bedtime stories until I could recite stratigraphy before I could ride a bike — even then, we'd communicated mostly in data.

*I'm writing from the Sereno expedition. We're making good progress. The valley beyond the Cordillera del Viento is everything the survey promised and more — I'll say no more than that, because you of all people understand that a site isn't real until it's documented, and I refuse to jinx it in ink.*

*The team is well. Funding has been complicated, as funding is. I won't bore you.*

*I heard from Imelda Ferrán last month — we correspond about ceramic glazes, don't look so surprised — and she mentioned you were working the coast. Puerto Salado. She says you're very good. She also says, and I quote, "the occupation layers are thin," which I suspect means something different in her correspondence than mine, but then again perhaps it doesn't.*

*You'll be twenty-nine in March. I remember the dig at Talara when you turned nine. You found a fishhook in the midden and declared you were going to be an archaeologist, and I said nothing, because I already knew, and because some things shouldn't be said out loud before they're true.*

*I don't know why I'm telling you that. Letters are strange. They let you say things at people across distances, and the distance does the work of keeping it safe.*

*If you ever wanted to see the mountains — the real ones, the ones past the river — the expedition would have a place for you in the spring. No pressure. No obligation. Just an open door, which I am aware is not a thing I have much practice offering.*

*Be well. Eat vegetables. Document everything.*

*Mom*

I read it four times. Then I did what any reasonable person does with a letter that rearranges the furniture of their chest: I folded it along its original creases, put it in the waterproof pouch against my spine, and finished packing.

Because that was the move, wasn't it? That was always the move. She offers a door, I walk out a window. She says *spring*, I'm gone by dawn. Twenty-eight years of choreography, and we both knew the steps. She'd written knowing I wouldn't come — I could see it in the careful casualness of the invitation, the *no pressure, no obligation*, the armor of small talk around the one sentence that mattered. And I would leave knowing she knew, and she would receive my silence knowing what it meant, and the whole apparatus would grind on another year, another continent between us, another layer of thin occupation.

Except.

Except that at four in the morning, sitting on the edge of a stripped bed in a room I'd already mentally vacated, I caught myself doing something I hadn't done in three years: reading the letter a fifth time. Not for the invitation. For the *funding has been complicated*. Four words, buried in paragraph two, doing the work of a scream.

Elena Voss did not write *complicated* about money. Elena Voss wrote grant numbers and budget line items and, when funding fell through, she wrote furious eight-page analyses of institutional cowardice that she mailed to deans and, occasionally, to me. *Complicated* was what you wrote when the problem was a person. When naming it in ink felt dangerous.

And there was the other thing. The thing I kept circling like a potter circling a wheel. *I'll say no more than that.* Elena, who annotated her own grocery lists. Elena, who had taught me that undocumented enthusiasm was just superstition with a degree. Refusing to describe her own discovery — my mother, who had never met a superlative she didn't footnote.

Something was wrong in the valley past the Cordillera del Viento. Something she couldn't or wouldn't put in a letter that might be read.

I sat with that until the window went grey, and I'll be honest about what I felt, because a person should be honest at least once per chapter: I felt relieved. Not worried first — relieved. Because worry would have meant staying, and staying was the one country I'd renounced citizenship in, but *worry from a distance* — worry with a train ticket in my pocket and a duffel packed and a legitimate professional pretext — that was the perfect compromise. I could go toward her without going *to* her. I could investigate the problem instead of having the conversation. I could be filial at range, the way I'd been filial my entire adult life: concerned, informed, and nowhere to be found.

The morning boat to the mainland left at six. I was on the quay at five-forty with my bag and my tools and the waterproof pouch warm against my spine, watching the fishing crews load ice, and Señora Ibáñez came down to see me off because of course she did, she'd known the moment I paid for the month in advance instead of the week.

"You run," she said. Not cruelly. The way you'd note the tide.

"I travel," I said. "There's a difference."

"In my experience," she said, "the difference is only how far the running gets you before you start again." She pressed something into my hand — a twist of paper containing almonds, because she fed everyone whether they consented or not — and patted my cheek with a hand like a dried cod. "Eat these on the water. And write to the one who wrote to you. Running people are bad at answering letters. It's the first thing they stop doing."

The boat pulled out into a sea the color of hammered tin, and Puerto Salado shrank behind us — the cannery, the crane, the trench I'd never fill in properly, the saucer with its smiling broken rim still half-buried in the dark, waiting for someone with thinner principles than mine to pour concrete over it. I ate the almonds. They were good. Everything anyone gives you to eat on a departure is good; that's the cruelty of departures.

Somewhere east of me, past the coast and the cordillera and a river I'd have to fight my way up, my mother was standing in a valley full of things she refused to describe, writing letters she wasn't sure would reach anyone, surrounded by funding that was *complicated*.

I told myself I was going for the artifact money. Salvage consultants on emergency expeditions commanded absurd day rates, and I had eleven weeks of wages and a landlord in the next port expecting rent. That was the story. Money, professional curiosity, a chance to see a mountain range I'd only ever traced with one finger on a map.

The letter rode against my spine the whole crossing, and I did not take it out, and I did not answer it, and I did not — despite composing seventeen drafts in my head between the mainland dock and the bus station — write back.

Running people are bad at answering letters. She'd have understood that. She'd taught me the steps herself, after all, in her way: every childhood summer a new dig, every friendship a two-month acquaintance, love expressed as logistics and shared work and never, ever as staying. I hadn't invented flight. I'd inherited it, the way you inherit eye color, or a nose, or the specific way your mother crosses her t's like she's striking them through.

The bus went inland. The coast fell away. And in my pocket, folded along its creases, the letter waited — along with the coordinates tucked at the bottom of the last page that I hadn't mentioned yet, the ones written in her surveyor's shorthand, the ones that said, beneath the small talk and the open door and the eat-vegetables:

*Last transmission: grid reference follows. If you're reading this, Gideon, come find out why I stopped sending them.*

I'd tell myself it was about the money. I'd keep telling myself that right up until the moment it stopped working.
