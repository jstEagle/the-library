## 2. Silence on Channel Nine

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like a joke Elena would have appreciated and I did not.

I know this because I remember the day with the embarrassing precision of someone who has spent years pretending not to keep track. Tuesday, the fourteenth of March. The salvage dig at Puerto Viejo had run long — we were pulling a drowned warehouse out of the harbor mud, and the mud was winning — and I came back to my rented room smelling of brine and diesel to find an envelope wedged under the door. Cream-colored stock. My name typed, not handwritten: *G. Reyes*. No return address, but I knew the postmark before I turned it over. I'd have known it blindfolded, probably, the way you know a voice through a wall.

Cordillera del Viento. The high country. Her country, for the last two years, according to the rumor mill of people who still spoke to both of us and had learned not to mention either.

I sat on the bed with the envelope in my lap for a long time. Long enough that the light moved across the floorboards and the gulls outside changed their argument from one topic to another. There's a particular paralysis that comes with mail from a parent you've been avoiding — it's not dread exactly, because dread implies you don't want what's coming. It's more like standing at the edge of cold water. You know it will hurt. You also know, in some stubborn animal part of yourself, that you'll feel better once you're under.

I opened it eventually. Of course I did. I'm weak in very specific ways.

* * *

The letter itself was three pages, single-spaced, in Elena's cramped field hand — the writing she used when she was working, all forward momentum and no decoration, letters leaning into each other like porters on a narrow trail. She wrote the way she dug: fast, thorough, and with an absolute refusal to acknowledge anything uncomfortable unless it could be dated and catalogued.

*Gideon —*

*I hope this finds you employed. I hope it finds you at all; your addresses have the lifespan of mayflies.*

That was her idea of an opening. Three years of silence, and she led with a joke about my mailing habits. I read it twice before continuing, which is the kind of thing I tell myself I didn't do.

*We're upriver now, past the last of the checkpoints. The Sereno is low this year — Sef says the dry season has teeth — so we're making slower progress than planned, but slow is fine. Slow is where the good stratigraphy lives. You'd appreciate the geology here. The whole valley is a held breath.*

*Sef Maren, our pilot — boat pilot, he'd correct me — says I should explain who these people are when I write, since you've never met them. Fine. Marcus Hale is financing the expedition. He comes recommended by people whose recommendations I no longer fully trust, which I find I can live with. Money is money, and the valley doesn't care whose checkbook opened the road to it.*

There it was. The first wrong note, though I didn't hear it as one yet. I read it as Elena being Elena — brisk, unsentimental, filing a human being under "logistics" the way she'd filed me, once, under "dependent."

*The site is promising beyond anything in my career, and I say that knowing I said it about Tulum and was wrong. This is different. The tomb complex sits above the valley floor in rock that shouldn't hold it, and the local community — descendants of the builders, as far as we can tell — has known about it forever and told no one. Think about that. Four hundred years of knowing, and silence. There's a lesson in it I suspect I deserve.*

*Now the small talk, because I promised myself I'd include some:*

*Is the harbor work treating you well? You always liked water that fought back. Are you eating? You never ate when you were working, and you were always working. Have you stayed anywhere longer than six months? Don't answer that last one. Or do. Either way, write back care of the mission compound address below, or better — if the radio is behaving, channel nine, evenings, 1900 hours. I'll be listening. I'm always listening, Gideon. That was always the problem between us, wasn't it? One of us listening too hard and the other one leaving anyway.*

*Coordinates, in case the mail dies before I do: 12°44'S, 71°19'W, elevation call it 2,800 meters. If you ever wanted to see what I actually do all day —*

*— Elena*

*P.S. The valley has a second sunrise most mornings. Light comes off the eastern ridge and hits the tomb face before the sun clears it. The locals call it the mountain greeting its dead. I thought of you when I saw it. I think of you more than the letters suggest.*

I put the letter down.

Then I picked it up and read it again, because the first reading had gone by in a blur of something I refused to name, and the second reading was where the archaeologist in me took over from the child. And the archaeologist noticed things.

She'd given coordinates. Elena Voss did not give coordinates. In thirty years of fieldwork she had guarded site locations the way other people guard children — jealously, obsessively, with a paranoia she called professionalism. A location in a letter was a confession. It meant she was afraid the letter might be the only thing that got out.

And the small talk. *Have you stayed anywhere longer than six months? Don't answer that.* That wasn't small talk. That was a woman who understood exactly how her child operated, reaching through three years of silence to grip a wrist. *If the radio is behaving, channel nine, evenings.* She was giving me a schedule. Mothers give schedules when they want certainty. When they want to know there will be a next time.

And the postscript. God help me, the postscript. *I thought of you when I saw it.* Elena Voss did not waste words, and she had spent five of them on a sentence that meant everything she'd never said at the kitchen table in Cusco eleven years ago, the night I left, when she'd looked at my packed bag and said only, "You'll be back when the season ends," as if I were a supply line and not a person.

I should have written back that night. I want to be honest about this, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now whether I consented to it or not. I should have sat down at the desk by the window with its view of the harbor cranes and written back, even just the small talk, even just *yes, I'm eating, no, I haven't stayed anywhere, the harbor work is fine.*

Instead I folded the letter along its original creases, put it in the drawer with the other letters I'd never answered — there were four, spanning eight years, each one a small archaeological layer of guilt — and went to bed telling myself I'd deal with it after the warehouse dig wrapped.

I was very good at telling myself things. It's the core competency of a person who has made flight into an identity. You develop a whole administrative apparatus for avoidance. Filing systems for unfinished conversations. Excuses with provenance and chain of custody.

For nine days, the letter sat in the drawer, and every evening at 1900 hours — I checked my watch, I couldn't help it — I was somewhere without a radio, and I let that be the reason.

* * *

On the tenth day, the reason died.

I was in the harbor office arguing with the salvage foreman about compression rates on the pump when a runner came in looking for me — one of the dock kids who carried messages for coins, a sharp-faced girl named Isa who had appointed herself my personal courier sometime that winter. She handed over a telegram flimsy with the solemnity of someone delivering a verdict.

It was from a Dr. Amara Okafor, whom I'd never heard of, writing on behalf of the Voss Expedition, Sereno River. The message was fourteen words long and I have them memorized the way you memorize the shape of a room you woke up screaming in:

*RADIO DARK ELEVEN DAYS STOP LAST TRANSMISSION RECEIVED DAY TWENTYTWO STOP ADVISE.*

Eleven days. Eleven days of silence on a channel where a woman had promised, in ink, to be listening. And the telegram had been sent to me — not to a university, not to a government bureau, not to any of the institutions with helicopters and budgets and jurisdiction. To me. Which meant either they had no one else, or someone in that expedition had decided that the estranged child was worth a telegram, and I found I didn't want to examine which possibility was worse.

I stood in the harbor office holding the flimsy while the foreman kept talking about pumps, his voice going on like weather, and I did the math the way you do math when you're trying not to feel anything: eleven days dark, plus however long the telegram took to reach a coastal town with one postal clerk and a mule. Call it fifteen days of nothing. Fifteen days in country where rivers eat boats and mountains eat radios and men with guns eat whatever's left.

Elena had been silent for half a month, and the first person officially notified was the daughter who hadn't written back.

I excused myself. I walked out onto the pier and stood there among the fish guts and the mooring lines and I let myself feel it for exactly as long as it took to smoke half a cigarette, which is my version of a breakdown — brief, scheduled, and followed by immediate practical activity. Then I went back inside and quit the job.

The foreman took it well, in the sense that he swore at me in two languages and then shook my hand, because salvage men understand leaving. Everyone at a salvage dig understands leaving. It's the whole grammar of the work: you raise what you can, you mark what you can't, and you go.

* * *

Here is the part where I'm supposed to say I went for love. That the letter cracked something open, that the postscript undid me, that I booked passage north because a mother I'd spent eleven years outrunning had finally said *I think of you more than the letters suggest*, and some buried thing in me surfaced all at once like the warehouse timbers coming up out of the harbor mud, black and waterlogged and unmistakable.

That's a good story. I almost believed it myself, somewhere around hour six of packing.

But the truth — and I've had occasion since to learn the difference — is that I built myself a much more comfortable story, and I built it fast, and I furnished it nicely, and I lived in it for the entire journey north. The story went like this:

*Expeditions go dark all the time.* Radios fail. Batteries die. Antennas come down in storms. The Sereno country was notorious for equipment deaths — humidity alone killed electronics the way malaria killed the careless. Eleven days was anomalous, yes, but anomalies were my business. I was, professionally speaking, the person you hired when the record didn't make sense.

*And there's money in it.* This was the load-bearing beam of the whole structure. Elena's letter had mentioned artifacts — a tomb complex, promising beyond anything in her career. Finds like that meant salvage rights, consulting fees, authentication work. Whoever recovered or verified that material would stand to make more in a season than I made in five years of drowning warehouses. If the expedition was finished — and expeditions that lose contact for half a month usually are, one way or another — then there would be a scramble for the site, and I knew the terrain of those scrambles. I could get there first. I could document properly, stake claims cleanly, and come out of the Cordillera del Viento with enough money to stop doing this. Whatever *this* was. Whatever I'd been doing for eleven years, hopping dig to dig like a man crossing a river on stones that sank behind him.

*So really,* the story concluded, *this is a career decision. Purely practical. The mother is incidental. The letter is incidental. The fact that I've read it nineteen times is — irrelevant. Irrelevant and unconnected to anything.*

I told this story to the harbormaster when I settled my debts. I told it to Isa when she asked where I was going, and she looked at me with the flat skepticism of a twelve-year-old who has watched adults lie to themselves professionally, and said, "You're going for the lady. Your mamá." And I gave her an extra coin to forget she'd said it, which fooled neither of us.

I told it to myself again on the coastal steamer north, standing at the rail while the town I'd lived in for seven months — seven months, a personal record, and isn't it funny that I broke it fleeing toward her instead of away — shrank behind us. I told it in the bus over the coastal range. I told it in the river port of Puerto Cobre, standing in the heat with a duffel bag and a letter in my pocket and a telegram in my jacket, watching the brown water of the Sereno slide past the pilings like something with somewhere to be.

The story had one flaw, and the flaw was this: practical people don't carry letters they haven't answered. Practical people don't know transmission schedules by heart. Practical people don't stand in strange ports doing arithmetic on elevations — 2,800 meters, she'd said, call it 2,800 — trying to calculate how far a woman could walk in fifteen days if she was walking, and how little distance she'd cover if she wasn't.

But the story held. It held because I needed it to hold, the way a roof holds — not because it's true, but because underneath it is weather, and the weather was a mother in a valley with a second sunrise, thinking of me, going silent on a channel I'd never once tuned.

Puerto Cobre smelled of diesel and river rot and frying plantains, and the ferry office had a notice board, and the notice board had a card on it, fresh-pinned, the ink still sharp:

**RESCUE PARTY — VOSS EXPEDITION — EXPERIENCED FIELD MEN ONLY — TOP WAGES — INQUIRE HALE**

Hale. The financier. Money is money, and the valley doesn't care whose checkbook opened the road to it.

I stood in front of that card for a long moment, feeling the story shift under me like ballast settling, and I told myself one more time — the last time, as it turned out, that the story would work — that I was here for the artifact money, and the wages were good, and it was purely practical to sign on with the only organized party heading upriver.

Then I picked up my duffel and went to inquire of Hale.
