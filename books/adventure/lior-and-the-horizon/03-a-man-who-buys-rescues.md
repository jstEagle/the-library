## 3. A Man Who Buys Rescues

Puerto Cobre smelled the way river ports always smell: diesel, fish gone past their argument with freshness, and the sweet rot of fruit nobody had gotten to in time. I'd been there four hours when I understood that the town had exactly one street worth walking and one bar worth drinking in, and both of them were watching me.

I should explain how I got there, because it happened faster than a decision has any right to happen.

The letter from Elena sat on my bunk at the salvage dig for two days before I opened it. Then the second message came — not a letter this time but a forwarded transmission, sent by a woman named Ines Ocampo who identified herself as the expedition's logistics coordinator and who wrote like someone composing under a deadline and a gun: *Radio contact lost eleven days ago. Last coordinates attached. Family being notified. If you are receiving this, Dr. Voss listed you as next of kin.*

*Next of kin.* Three years of silence and I was still the emergency contact. There's a particular kind of insult in that — being trusted only with catastrophe — and I sat with it for a long time that night, listening to the coastal rain do its impression of indifference.

By morning I'd quit the salvage job. The foreman didn't even argue; he just looked at my packed bag and said, "You're one of those," the way you'd identify a migratory bird. He was right. I was one of those. I told myself the money mattered more than anything else — Elena's expedition had insurance, salvage rights, equipment worth recovering, and someone competent would need to be on-site to catalog whatever they found. That was the story I told myself on the bus, on the ferry, on the rusted coastal freighter that dropped me at Puerto Cobre's single gray pier.

The story held up fine until about noon, which is when I met Marcus Hale.

* * *

He found me rather than the other way around, which should have been my first clue. I was standing at the harbor office trying to look like someone who knew what he was doing, studying a chart of the Sereno River that was older than I was, when a voice behind me said, "You have her hands."

I turned. The man was maybe fifty-five, silver at the temples with the precision of a barber rather than age, wearing field clothes that had never once been near a field — khakis pressed sharp enough to filet a fish, boots so new they creaked when he shifted his weight. Everything about him said *money that had dressed itself as competence.*

"Gideon Reyes," he said, extending a hand. "Marcus Hale. I financed your mother's expedition."

Her hands. I looked down at mine — dirt in the knuckle creases, a healing cut across two fingers, the nails of someone who digs for a living. Whatever resemblance he thought he saw, it wasn't flattery. It was reconnaissance. He'd studied me the way you study a map before buying the land.

"Mr. Hale." I shook the hand. Firm, dry, calibrated. "You're fast."

"Fast?"

"The radio's been dark eleven days. You've got boats chartered, men hired, supplies stacked on the pier." I nodded toward the far dock, where crates were being loaded onto two flat-bottomed river boats by men who moved with the unhurried efficiency of people paid by the day. "Either you're very good or you started before anyone knew she was missing."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not guilt, exactly. Recalculation. A man checking whether I was clever enough to be useful or too clever to be comfortable.

"I'm very good," he said, smiling. "It's why Elena chose me. Well — that and my checkbook. Come have a drink. We should talk."

* * *

The bar was called El Faro, though the only lighthouse within two hundred kilometers was a broken one on the point, and the beer was cold in the way of places that owned exactly one functioning refrigerator and guarded it like a shrine. Hale took the corner table without asking, back to the wall, sightline to the door. Men who sit that way have either been soldiers or read about them.

He ordered for both of us without consulting me, which I noted, and got my order wrong anyway, which I noted harder.

"Elena spoke of you," he said. "Rarely. But when she did—"

"She didn't." I drank. "Whatever you're building, build it on something true. She hasn't spoken to me in three years. She wrote me a letter because her satellite phone protocol requires a next-of-kin designation and she never bothered to change it."

Hale absorbed that with the smoothness of a man who'd been lied to professionally and had developed an appreciation for craft. "Then why are you here?"

"Money."

The word landed between us and neither of us blinked. I watched him decide to believe it, because believing it was convenient. A grieving child would want answers, would ask questions, would need managing. Someone here for money could be bought.

"The expedition is insured," he said. "Equipment recovery, salvage rights on any significant finds, plus the rescue stipend from the university's contingency fund. There's also—" he paused, delicately, "—a private interest willing to pay a finder's fee for the safe return of certain personnel."

"Certain personnel."

"Dr. Voss primarily."

"And the rest?"

"Are covered," he said, in the tone of a man closing a drawer.

Here is what I knew by then, assembled from the harbor office gossip, the chart clerk's loose tongue, and forty minutes of watching Hale's men load those boats: there were eleven of them. Eleven armed men for a rescue mission up a river where the primary dangers were rapids, malaria, and boredom. Two of them carried rifles slung with the casualness of long familiarity. One — a broad, silent man who went by Tomás and answered questions with shrugs — wore a shoulder holster under an unbuttoned shirt and stood where Hale stood, the way a shadow stands where the body stands.

Rescue parties don't need eleven guns. Retrieval operations do.

"You loved her work," Hale said then, leaning forward, and his voice changed — softened, warmed, took on the grain of sincerity. It was a good performance. I want to be fair: it was a genuinely good performance. "Everyone did. She had a way of seeing what a landscape was hiding. I've funded thirty expeditions and she was the only one who ever made me feel like the money was the least interesting thing in the room."

And there it was. The wrong note.

Not a false word — every word was plausible, warm, even generous. It was something underneath. When people talk about someone they actually admire, they talk about specifics. The way she argued with a dating report. The time she was right about the stratigraphy and insufferable about it. Hale talked about Elena the way investors talk about a stock that performed well: admiration with no intimacy in it. He'd spent years writing checks to her and had never once listened to her, and now he was performing having known her to a man who hadn't spoken to her in three years, and we were both pretending this constituted knowledge.

"You're wondering about the guns," Hale said.

I hadn't asked yet. I raised an eyebrow, granting him the point.

"The Sereno isn't safe. River pirates above the third checkpoint, and the last survey team lost a boat to them. My insurers require armed escort." He spread his hands, reasonable as arithmetic. "You've traveled upriver before?"

"Once. With a mapping crew."

"Then you know. The rifles are paperwork, Mr. Reyes. Nothing more."

Nothing more. I looked out the window at his men on the dock. One of them was checking the magazine of a rifle with the absent tenderness of a man checking on a sleeping child. Paperwork.

* * *

He made the offer over the second beer, and it was generous — insultingly, deliberately generous. Five times the going rate for a field archaeologist, hazard pay, a bonus contingent on recovery of personnel. He slid a folded paper across the table and I didn't touch it.

"Why me?" I asked. "You've got eleven men and a budget. What does one estranged child add?"

"Legitimacy." He said it without shame, which I'll grant him; shame would have required him to notice it was shameful. "A family member on the rescue changes how it reads. To the university, to the press, to the families of the staff. And—" the warmth again, that calibrated warmth, "—Elena will need to see a familiar face when we find her. People coming out of situations like that can be fragile. Disoriented. They say things that aren't reliable. It helps to have someone whose judgment she trusts."

There. Second wrong note, louder than the first.

*They say things that aren't reliable.* Said about a woman who hadn't been found yet, in a sentence shaped like concern. File that away, I told myself. Put it in the grid square with the eleven rifles and the pre-chartered boats and the pressed khakis. The picture forming wasn't pretty, but archaeology teaches you patience with ugly pictures — you don't get to see the whole mosaic just because you've found three tiles.

"When we find her," I repeated. "You're confident she's alive."

"Absolutely." No hesitation. Too little hesitation. "Elena Voss has survived worse than eleven days off the radio. She's the toughest person I've ever funded."

"Funded," I said. "Not met."

His smile stayed exactly where it was, which was its own kind of answer. "The river's leaving Thursday," he said, standing, dropping bills on the table without looking at them. "I'd like you on the first boat. Think about the number tonight. And Gideon—" he paused at the edge of the light, and for half a second the polish thinned and something older and hungrier looked out, "—she'd want you there. Whatever else is true between you two. She'd want her child there."

He left. I sat with the beer and the folded paper and the sound of the river doing its slow brown work at the end of the street.

She'd want her child there. Maybe. Probably, even — I couldn't claim to know anymore what Elena wanted, that muscle had atrophied years ago. But I noticed what Hale had done with the sentence. He'd handed me the one bait I couldn't weigh rationally, wrapped it in sentiment, and set it down gently, the way you set down something you're afraid might go off.

* * *

I walked the docks until midnight. This is a habit from the dig — walk the ground before you commit to it, let your feet learn what your eyes miss. The crates on Hale's boats weren't marked with expedition numbers; they were marked with a shipping company I didn't recognize and a port of origin that wasn't anywhere near the coast. Medical supplies, the manifest said. I counted the cases. Nobody packs that many antibiotics for a two-week river run unless they expect casualties, and nobody expects casualties on a rescue unless they've already decided what kind.

Tomás was smoking at the rail of the nearer boat. He watched me watch the crates, and after a while he said, in Spanish, "You're the son."

"I'm the son."

He shrugged — everything was a shrug with Tomás — and flicked his cigarette into the water. "The river's high this year," he said. "Fast water kills more people than bullets do." Then he went below, and I stood there translating the warning into whatever language it had been sent in, because it definitely hadn't been small talk.

Back at the room I'd taken above the chandlery, I laid it all out on the bed like finds on a sorting table. Eleven armed men. Pre-positioned supplies. A financier who talked about a missing woman like a portfolio holding. A phrase — *they say things that aren't reliable* — already waiting for a living Elena to contradict. A bonus structure that paid for personnel recovered and said nothing, not one word, about artifacts or records or whatever Elena had actually gone up that valley looking for.

Which meant either Marcus Hale was the most thorough amateur in the history of river rescues, or the rescue was the cover story and something else was the cargo.

I thought about going home. I want that on the record, wherever records go. I stood at the window with the harbor lights bleeding into the river and I genuinely considered turning around, letting the professionals handle it, sending money instead of myself. Flight was the family trade; I was better at it than she'd ever been.

But somewhere up that river was a woman who'd written me a letter full of small talk with a plea buried in it like a sherd in fill, and whether I'd forgiven her or not — whether I ever could — nobody else was going to read that letter correctly. Nobody else knew that when Elena Voss wrote *"the valley is more beautiful than the photographs suggest, I almost understand why you always keep moving"* she was telling me, in the only dialect we'd ever shared, that she was afraid.

Thursday morning, the first boat left the pier at six. I was on it.

Hale shook my hand at the rail, warm as a heating pad, and said, "Welcome aboard. We'll have her home in no time."

And I smiled and said, "Looking forward to it," and both of us lied beautifully, and the Sereno took us north into the green dark, carrying its cargo of guns and grief and whatever it was we each thought we were going to find.
