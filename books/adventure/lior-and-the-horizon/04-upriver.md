## 4. Upriver

The Sereno River does not flow so much as it conspires. It winds through the lowlands in a series of lazy, treacherous bends, brown-green and opaque, hiding its snags the way a card sharp hides his ace. Our boat — a flat-bottomed barge of a thing with an outboard motor that sounded like a dying hymn — was called *La Paciencia*, which struck me as either a joke or a prayer.

I stood at the rail on the first morning with coffee that tasted like it had been filtered through a boot, watching the banks slide past: cattle, then cane, then jungle pressing down to the waterline like it wanted to drink.

"You're Voss's kid." The voice came from behind me, and I turned to find one of Hale's men — the big one, the one with the shaved head and the forearms of a man who'd done more lifting than reading. He had introduced himself yesterday as Dario, in the tone of a man who introduces himself so you'll know who to be afraid of.

"Gideon," I said. "And yes."

"Doctor Voss." He said it carefully, tasting the title. "She pay you to come? Or you paying your own way?"

"Why do you ask?"

He shrugged, all innocence, which is how I knew it wasn't. "Just making conversation."

"Try harder," I said, and turned back to the river.

He laughed — a good-natured sound, deliberately good-natured, the way a dog's wag can be deliberate — and wandered off. I watched him go and made a note in the mental ledger I keep for exactly this purpose: *Dario. Testing. File under: why.*

Because that was the thing about this rescue party. We were eleven people bound upriver to save six, and half of us seemed to be auditioning for something. Hale had hired porters, two mechanics, a medic named Ilsa who chain-smoked and said almost nothing, and four men whose job descriptions were vague enough to be legally interesting. They carried more ammunition than medical supplies. I'd counted. Counting is what I do instead of sleeping.

* * *

Sef Maren found me at dusk on the second day, or perhaps I found him; with Sef it was always hard to say who sought out whom. He was the pilot, sixty-odd years of river in his face, a man built like driftwood — knotted, weathered, lighter than he looked. He sat beside me on the cargo crates without asking, which I appreciated. People who ask permission before sitting usually want something.

"You eat?" he said, and produced from somewhere in his shirt two plantains and a lump of salted fish wrapped in banana leaf. It wasn't an offer so much as a fact of the universe, like gravity. I ate.

"The river's high," he said, after a while. "Early rains up in the cordillera. That's bad for them, good for us. Rapids run fast but the rocks drown."

"That sounds like a metaphor."

"Everything's a metaphor if you're tired enough." He chewed, spat neatly over the side. "You're the archaeologist's kid."

"I'm the salvage diver's kid, actually. She's the archaeologist. I just dig where they tell me things got lost."

"Mm." He looked at me with eyes the color of the river itself — nothing reflected, everything absorbed. "Hale says she's alive. You believe him?"

"I don't believe anything Hale says. I believe the radio went dark, and I believe dark radios are usually boring until suddenly they aren't."

"Smart answer." He said it the way you'd say *wrong answer* to someone you liked. Then, quietly, without looking at me: "You know this is his third trip up the Sereno?"

"I didn't."

"First one, five years back. Survey work, he said. Came back with samples and no problems. Second one, three years ago — mining concession, he said. Came back short." Sef held up two fingers. "Two men. Porters. Families got money, families got letters, letters said the men drowned. River's full of drowned men, nobody argues." He folded the fingers down. "But I pulled bodies out of this river for thirty years, and I never once pulled out a body wearing boots still laced. Men who drown kick their boots off. These two — the story said drowned, but their gear came back dry-stored. Folded. Like it was returned."

The plantain sat heavy in my stomach. "Maybe the story's true anyway."

"Maybe." He rose, joints cracking like green firewood. "You dive wrecks, yeah? You know how to read a site?"

"A little."

"Then read this one." He nodded toward the stern, where Hale stood talking to Dario, both of them looking — not at us, but at the space we occupied, the way you look at a door you're deciding whether to lock. "Read it slow. And Gideon—" he paused at the rail, "—when a man tells you a story twice, listen for what changes."

* * *

The checkpoints began on day three.

There were four of them between Puerto Cobre and the last navigable water, and every one of them was a study in rot. The first was a floating platform of gray planks manned by two soldiers and a dog that had clearly given up on the whole enterprise of authority. The soldiers wore uniforms that had been camouflaged by time rather than design, and their rifle bolts moved with the reluctance of men who hadn't been paid since the last government.

Hale handled them like currency. Papers, stamps, a fold of bills passed with a handshake so smooth the dog didn't even bark. At the second checkpoint the officer wanted to inspect the cargo, and Hale opened the crates marked *AGRICULTURAL EQUIPMENT* to reveal — agricultural equipment, actually, though I noticed the false bottoms were riveted, not screwed, and I noticed that Hale noticed me noticing.

At the third checkpoint, the officer was young and honest, which made him the most dangerous kind. He read the manifest twice, looked at our eleven passengers, and asked why a rescue expedition needed four men carrying military-pattern rifles.

"Bandits," Hale said pleasantly. "The upper Sereno's gone wild since the garrison withdrew."

"There are no bandits on the upper Sereno," the officer said. "There are farmers with grievances."

"Then we won't need the rifles," Hale agreed, smiling, and paid him enough to make the rifles a theoretical problem for a future administration.

That night I lay in my hammock under the canopy deck and listened to the river talk to itself, and I did arithmetic. Six days to the head of navigation, if the rapids allowed. Eleven days of silence from Elena's camp before the letter even reached me — and the letter had taken another nine to arrive, which meant the radio had been dark for twenty days, maybe more. Twenty days is a long time to be alive in the cordillera. It is also, I reminded myself, a long time to be dead, and dead women don't write letters pleading for someone to bring her spare reading glasses, of all things. Small talk. Elena Voss did not do small talk. Every sentence in that letter had been load-bearing, and I'd spent three nights mapping which beams held up which walls.

*Coordinates. Come yourself. Bring nothing you'd mind losing.*

Bring nothing you'd mind losing. From a woman who had once catalogued a broken potsherd with the tenderness other people reserve for infants.

* * *

On the fifth day the river stopped pretending to be gentle.

We hit the first real rapids at dawn — the *Cola del Diablo*, Sef called it, the Devil's Tail, a quarter mile of water trying to become a waterfall in installments. Sef took us through standing up, barefoot, one hand on the tiller, reading the water the way I read stratigraphy: layers, breaks, the places where the surface lied about what lay beneath. Twice the hull screamed over rock and everyone grabbed everything, and both times Sef's face didn't change, because a pilot's face is the last thing on a boat that's allowed to break.

Afterward, shaking, I helped bail water while Dario watched me with that same pleasant blankness.

"You handle yourself okay," he said. "For a professor's kid."

"I'm not a professor's kid. She's a doctor. Different animal entirely."

"Yeah?" He crouched, forearms on knees, amiable as a landslide. "What's she like? The famous Dr. Voss. Hale talks about her like she hung the moon."

"She's like a dig site," I said. "You spend years getting through the top layer and then you find out the interesting stuff is deeper down and older than you expected, and also there's a chance you'll break something getting to it."

Dario laughed, genuinely this time, and I hated that I liked him a little. That's the trick of men like Dario — they make themselves likable precisely so you'll hesitate when the moment comes. I filed it away. *Dario. Likable. More dangerous than the unpleasant ones.*

* * *

Sef's second interlude came that night, moored against the bank below the last set of rapids, where the water ran loud enough to cover a conversation.

He brought me fish again. I was starting to suspect it was how he checked pulses.

"Tomorrow we beach," he said. "Head of navigation. After that it's mule trail and feet." He fed a scrap to the boat cat, a scarred orange tyrant named Almirante who had claimed the fuel crate as sovereign territory. "You've been counting."

"Habit."

"What's the count?"

"Fourteen long guns, two crates with false bottoms, and a satellite phone Hale uses only at night, facing away from the boat."

Sef nodded slowly, unsurprised. "Last trip," he said, "the two men who didn't come back — one of them was my cousin's boy. Tomás. Nineteen. Strong as cable." He said it flatly, the way you report a depth sounding. "His mother still sets a place at the table. Not grief. Habit. Habit's worse."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be careful. There's a difference and it matters." He looked at me then, full on, and the river was in his eyes again. "I'm telling you this because you're going to figure it out anyway, and I'd rather you figured it out with me than alone. Something's wrong with this rescue, Gideon. Rescues come back heavy — with people, with gear, with the sick and the hurt. This one's built to come back light. Light and quiet."

"And you took the job anyway?"

"I took the job because my cousin's boy died on the last one and nobody asks questions on this river unless somebody's on the boat to ask them." He stood, brushing off his trousers. "So ask yours. Quietly. And Gideon — tomorrow, when we beach, watch who Hale sends ahead first. A rescue goes in together. Anything else has scouts."

* * *

We beached at noon the next day, at a mud landing where the forest came down to the water like a crowd pressing against a barrier. The head of navigation: beyond it, the Sereno broke into white water no boat could argue with, and the Cordillera del Viento stood up behind the haze, blue and enormous and indifferent.

Hale gathered us on the bank with the efficiency of a man who had rehearsed this speech. Mules, loads, order of march. And then, exactly as Sef had promised, he sent Dario and two others ahead — "to scout the trail and confirm the camp's position" — a full hour before the rest of us would move.

A rescue goes in together. Anything else has scouts.

I stood in the mud with my pack on my shoulders and watched those three figures shrink into the green, and I felt the unease in my chest stop being weather and start being architecture. Something solid, load-bearing, built to hold weight.

Sef appeared at my elbow, adjusting a mule's harness that needed no adjustment.

"Well?" he murmured.

"Scouts," I said.

"Mm." He tightened a strap. "Then I guess we'd better figure out what they're scouting *for*."

Behind us, Hale clapped his hands and told us to move out, cheerful as a man announcing lunch — and for just a moment, before he turned, his eyes swept the tree line where his men had vanished, and I saw what was underneath the cheerfulness.

It wasn't worry.

It was anticipation.
