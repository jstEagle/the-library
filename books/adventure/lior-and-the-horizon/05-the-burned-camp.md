## 5. The Burned Camp

We smelled it before we saw it.

Sef cut the engines a full bend before the camp came into view, and the smell drifted downriver to meet us — wet ash and char and something underneath that I didn't want to name until I had to. Smoke that's had time to think about what it's done. Not the sharp, panicked smell of a fire still eating. This was old grief, soaked by at least one rain.

"Nobody talk," Sef said, which was unnecessary, because nobody was talking.

Hale's boat pulled alongside ours. He stood at the rail in a clean field jacket that had never met honest mud, binoculars raised, and his face did something complicated — a performance of dread with, I thought, a flicker of satisfaction running underneath it like a vein of ore. I'd spent three years learning to read stratigraphy in faces too. It's the same skill. You look for the layer that doesn't belong.

"There," Hale said quietly. "The lower camp. Elena insisted on establishing it at the confluence — said the porters needed a staging point below the rapids." His voice caught on *Elena*, professionally, the way a man catches a sleeve on a nail he's walked past a hundred times.

I said nothing. I'd learned on this river that my mouth was a liability. Every time I opened it around Hale, I heard my mother's cadence coming out of it, and I wasn't ready to hand him that.

The bank came up gray. That was the first wrong thing, and I felt it in my teeth before my brain caught up: the whole clearing was gray, not black. A campfire burns hot and local — you get a black ring, a scatter of charcoal, the jungle creeping back in within weeks. This was different. This was a clearing that had been *painted*.

* * *

Hale wanted to go in fast — six of his men fanning out through the treeline with their rifles pointed at nothing, boots churning the ground into soup. I put myself in front of him at the edge of the clearing, which took more nerve than I expected it to, because two of those rifles swung toward me before he waved them down.

"You'll walk on the evidence," I said. "All of it."

"The evidence is ash, Dr. Reyes."

"Gideon." I let him use no title; it was a small rebellion, the kind you can afford. "And the evidence is exactly not ash. Ash is the last chapter. Everything that matters happened before it." I crouched at the clearing's edge, where the trampled mud gave way to ground that hadn't been touched, and looked at the surface the way I'd looked at a thousand surfaces in a thousand holes: patiently, letting it speak first.

Here's what the ground said.

It said: *rain.* A real rain, a day or more of it, after the burning — every ash patch softened at its edges, charcoal leached into gray halos, footprints filled with standing water that had since gone to skin. The fire itself had burned in weather that was dry but not drought-dry; the surrounding leaf litter was scorched only within a radius you'd get from accelerant, not from a spreading blaze. Someone had brought fuel. Someone had *placed* fuel.

I stood up and walked the perimeter in a wide arc, staying off the soft center, and Hale watched me with an expression I couldn't file yet. Curiosity, maybe. Or appraisal. The way a man looks at a tool he hasn't decided whether to buy.

The tents had burned where they stood — four dome frames, warped aluminum, the nylon long gone. But here was the thing about tent frames: they collapse inward when they burn with their fabric, outward when they're dragged or disturbed. These had collapsed inward, neat as diagrams. Except the fifth position. There was a fifth flat spot in the grass, dead pale where a floor had blocked the light, and no frame at all. One tent had been struck and carried off before the fire. You don't loot a dying camp. You loot a camp you've already emptied.

The supply crates were the next chapter. Six of them, split open along the seams — not smashed, *opened*, pried with something flat and then scattered, their contents gone. Rope, tinned food, the medical kit's empty case with its red cross faded to pink. But a crate of surveying instruments sat untouched ten feet away, lid still latched, and when I knelt beside it I could see why: water stain across the top, old, from before the fire. It had been left out in the rain, forgotten or dismissed. Looters take what's valuable. Whoever hit this camp had taken what was *useful* and left what was *expensive*, which is a very specific kind of thief. A thief with a shopping list.

Then the radio.

It lay near the cold fire pit, and I have to be honest — seeing it stopped me. Elena's radio. The big field transceiver she'd hauled across two continents and one bad marriage, the one she talked to me through when I was nine and she was in the Atacama and the static made her voice sound like it was arriving from another century. Eleven days of silence had been an abstraction. This was a box of glass and copper with her fingerprints, metaphorically, all over it, and someone had beaten it to pieces against a rock.

But even grief has to make way for craft, and the craft was screaming at me.

The radio hadn't died in the fire. It was twenty meters from the burn zone, lying in grass that was unburnt. It had been carried *out* of the fire's path — or rather, it had never been in it — and then destroyed. Deliberately. Thoroughly. With the valve housing cracked open and the power cells removed. And the rock it had been broken against had chips of fresh paint on it, the expedition's stenciled markings, meaning the blows had landed hard enough and recently enough to leave transfer.

You smash a radio to silence a camp. You carry a radio away from your own fire and smash it to leave a *message*. This one said: *the expedition's voice ended here.*

Except it hadn't. Because the fire was theater, and theater needs an audience, and there was only one audience this stage could be built for.

Us.

* * *

I found Sef at the riverbank, coiling line with the aggressive calm of a man who wants his hands busy so his face doesn't have to do anything.

"Walk the north edge with me," I said.

"I don't walk edges. Edges are where the snakes are."

"Sef."

He sighed and came. We moved along the treeline, away from Hale's men, who were conducting a search of the ruins with all the subtlety of men kicking over stones looking for scorpions. Twenty meters on, Sef crouched without being asked. He'd seen it too, then. Good. I was starting to trust his eyes more than my own, which was either wisdom or exhaustion.

A footprint. Just one clear one, pressed into the clay where the canopy drip kept the ground soft, at the exact margin where a careful person would walk if they wanted to approach a camp without being seen from it. Boot tread, military pattern, deep heel — a heavy man, or a man carrying weight. And beside it, half-erased by the post-fire rain, the ghost of a second print. Smaller. Lighter step. A porter's sandal, maybe, or—

Or Elena's boots. She wore short leather hiking boots with a distinctive worn patch on the outer left heel, a habit of her stride I could have identified in a police lineup of footwear. The print was too degraded to swear to. I swore to it anyway, privately, in the place where I kept things I wasn't ready to say out loud.

"Days old," Sef murmured. "Four? Five? Rain since."

"The fire's older than the message about the fire." I kept my voice level. "Whoever burned this wanted it found late. Wanted us — or whoever came — to arrive already grieving."

"And the looting?"

"Staged. They took rope, food, medicine. Left instruments, left the survey gear, left—" I nodded back toward the wrecked radio. "Left the one thing guaranteed to break the hearts of anyone who came looking. This isn't a raid. It's a *set design*. Somebody built a tragedy here, and they built it for people who'd read it the way civilians read it — smoke means dead, broken radio means silent, scattered crates mean violence. Any rescue party arrives, sees this, radios home: *expedition lost, all hands.* Case closed. No searches. No questions."

Sef was quiet a moment, working the coil of line through his hands. "Or," he said, "somebody attacked the camp, took what they needed, and you're doing what archaeologists do. Finding patterns in dirt because patterns are more comfortable than bodies."

"Where are the bodies, Sef?"

"That's my point. Where are they?" He turned to look at me, and his face had none of its usual river-worn humor. "Sixteen people in that expedition. Cooks, porters, Elena Voss herself. Camps like this leave remains. Bones in the ash pit. Teeth. Something. I've seen villages burn. Villages leave *worse* than bones." He let that sit. "This camp doesn't even leave blood. You know what that means?"

"It means nobody died here."

"It means nobody died here *that they wanted us to find*." He stood, slung the line over his shoulder. "Those aren't the same sentence, Gideon. Don't go confusing them just because one of them lets you sleep."

I didn't sleep anyway, that night, but not for the reason he meant.

* * *

Hale convened us at the fire pit after dark — his men in a crescent behind him, Sef and me and the two hired hands who'd come upriver on our boat arranged opposite, the whole tableau lit orange and shadowed like a production of something grim. He'd recovered his composure somewhere between the clearing and sundown, and what he offered us now was gravity, measured out in careful spoonfuls.

"I want to be direct with you both," he said. "What we found today changes the mission. This is no longer a resupply run or a check-in. As far as I'm concerned, we are searching for survivors — or for answers." He let his gaze rest on me. "Dr. Reyes. Your professional assessment."

There it was. The trap laid so politely I almost admired it. If I said *staged*, I was telling Hale — and his six armed listeners — that I suspected the truth had been manufactured, which put me in the category of people who knew too much. If I said *tragedy*, I was signing my mother's death certificate in front of witnesses and making the whole party turn back downriver, mission accomplished, everyone home by Christmas.

So I gave them the stratigraphy instead. The truth, layered like sediment, each stratum harmless on its own.

"The fire was set with accelerant, not spread naturally," I said. "The looting was selective — consumables taken, valuables left. The radio was destroyed away from the burn zone, deliberately, as a signal. And there are no human remains, which a sixteen-person camp should produce even in a worst-case scenario." I let that land. "My assessment is that this site was arranged. Whether it was arranged to cover an attack, or arranged *as* an attack, or arranged to convince rescuers the expedition is dead — I can't tell you yet. But I can tell you the people who did this were organized, unhurried, and thinking about an audience."

Silence. One of Hale's men shifted his weight. Somewhere across the river, a howler monkey made its opinion known.

"Audience," Hale repeated.

"Rescuers. Relief parties. Anyone who'd come up this river asking questions and stop asking them once they saw ash." I held his eyes. "Which raises the obvious question, Mr. Hale. Who benefits from the world believing Elena Voss is dead?"

I watched the question go into him like a probe into undisturbed soil. Something shifted — a fraction of a degree of recalibration, the look of a man revising a file. Then he smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and I understood that I had just told him exactly how dangerous I was, and that he would now adjust accordingly.

"Rival academics," he said lightly. "Grant committees. The usual vultures. Elena made enemies the way rivers make silt — constantly, and without effort." He clapped his hands once, briskly. "We push on at first light. The main camp is four days up-valley, past the rapids. If there are survivors, that's where they'll be." A pause, delicately placed. "And if there aren't — well. We'll owe them the truth, whatever it turns out to be."

*Truth*, in his mouth, sounded like a word he was trying on for size. Like a coat he intended to wear only until something better came along.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to its honest self and Hale's men had rotated into watch shifts, I sat at the edge of the ruined camp with my notebook and drew what the ground had told me, because drawing is how I think and because paper keeps secrets better than my face does.

One tent missing. Consumables taken, valuables ignored. Radio executed for effect. No remains. Footprints at the treeline — one heavy boot, one light sole I chose to believe belonged to my mother, because belief is a tool like any other and tonight I needed it.

If the expedition had been taken alive — and every line of evidence bent toward that, however much Sef warned me not to lean on it — then the burning wasn't an ending. It was a *door closing*. Someone wanted the outside world to stop looking, which meant the outside world was never supposed to reach the high valley. Which meant whatever was up there — whatever Elena had found, or was close to finding — was worth more than sixteen lives' worth of silence.

And Marcus Hale had outfitted this "rescue" in nine days, with armed men and a schedule too tight for grief.

I closed the notebook and looked up at the stars, the same stars that presumably hung over my mother wherever she was, and I felt the two things Sef had promised me arrive together, right on schedule: grief, cold and specific, and under it, rising like groundwater, the thrill. The puzzle had teeth. The puzzle had a shape. And some traitorous part of me — the part that had made flight into an identity, the part that had never once stayed — leaned toward it the way a plant leans toward a window.

Somewhere upriver, in the dark, my mother was either dead or a prisoner, and either way the answer was four days upstream, guarded by men whose employer paid for rescues the way other men paid for funerals.

I wrote one last line in the notebook, small, in the margin where I keep the things I mean:

*They staged a death. So somebody's alive enough to hide.*
