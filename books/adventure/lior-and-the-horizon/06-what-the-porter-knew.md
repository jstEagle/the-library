## 6. What the Porter Knew

I have run boats on the Sereno for nineteen years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things worth teaching: the river always wins eventually, the government checkpoints are only dangerous on Tuesdays, and a man who counts his money twice before paying you is counting it twice because he intends to take some of it back.

Marcus Hale counted his money four times.

He did it at Puerto Cobre, he did it at the rotting checkpoint at Isla Verde — which was, mercifully, not a Tuesday — and he did it again last night at the camp below the Raudal de los Ahogados, the Rapids of the Drowned, a name the river earned honestly and defends annually. He counted out wages to his men by lantern light, and I sat by my boat pretending to check a fuel line that did not need checking, and I watched him pay each man with the same careful, generous smile, and I thought: *here is a man buying something. And it is not a rescue.*

Rescues are frantic things. Rescues are men shouting and gear left behind and someone's cousin crying into his hat. This was an inventory. Hale moved through his expedition like a landlord through a property he already owned, and his men — twelve of them, too many of them armed, all of them hired inside a week — moved like employees who had been told the bonus structure depended on discretion.

And then there was the Voss woman's child.

Gideon Reyes. I'd ferried enough archaeologists up this river to know the type, but Gideon was a variant I hadn't catalogued: the archaeologist who hates archaeology. Who stands over a midden like it owes them money. Who looks at every dig site the way I look at a boat I've just bought — calculating how soon they can leave it.

They'd been on my boat five days now, Puerto Cobre to the upper gorge, and in five days Gideon had asked me exactly two questions about their mother's expedition and roughly forty about Hale. Where he slept. What he ate. Which men took orders from him directly and which were hired muscle who'd never met him before this week. I answered what I could and lied about the rest, because a pilot learns early that the passenger who asks where everyone sleeps is not making conversation.

But tonight, after the wage-counting, after the lanterns went down to embers, after Hale's sentry walked his circuit with the bored rhythm of a man being paid to be bored — tonight, a sound came up from the rocks below the camp.

A groan. Then, worse, a word.

"*Agua.*"

* * *

I found him wedged in a crevice above the waterline, maybe forty meters downstream, where the bank steepened into black stone slick with spray. A porter. Expedition canvas jacket, Sereno River Survey stenciled on the breast in letters that had been washed to ghosts. His left leg was wrong below the knee — broken, badly set or badly rebroken, wrapped in strips of shirt gone brown with old blood. He'd been there days. The flies told me that before his smell did.

"Quiet," I said, when he tried to speak again. "Water first."

He drank like a man apologizing to the canteen. Then he looked past me, up the slope toward the ember-light of the camp, and his whole body did something complicated — hope and terror fighting for the same handful of muscle.

"You're with them," he whispered.

"I'm the boat," I said. "Boats are neutral." It wasn't true. Boats are never neutral; they go where they're pointed, and the pointing is everything. But it bought me a second of calm, and I needed him calm, because I needed him talking, and because his eyes had gone to the machete on my belt in a way I didn't like.

"What's your name?"

"Tomas." A cough. "Tomas Quispe." He said the surname like it was a rope thrown across a gap, like it should mean something to me. It would later. That night it was just a name.

"Tomas. Who left you here?"

His jaw worked. "The advance team. Hale's men. We came through ahead — six of us, porters, carrying crates up the gorge trail. Crates we weren't allowed to open." He stared at his ruined leg as if it belonged to the story rather than to him. "At the fork below the pass, the boss — a man called Ferrer — he got a radio message. After it he was different. He made us dig a hole and bury two of the crates. Then he said the rest of us were done, contract complete, payment at the coast. They gave us half and started back down." His voice flattened. "Then one of them hit me. I don't even know why. Maybe because I saw which fork they took. Maybe because it was easy."

"And the others?"

"Went on down. Or tried to. The river took Beto at the crossing." He said it without drama, the way people on this river say such things, because drama is a luxury the Sereno doesn't stock. "I crawled up here. I thought — the main party comes this way eventually. Someone would find me."

Someone had. The wrong someone, probably. But he was talking, and I am a greedy man for talk, so I asked the question I'd been carrying since Puerto Cobre like a stone in my boot.

"Tomas. The Voss expedition. Do you know what happened to them?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Above us, the sentry's boots crunched gravel, paused, crunched on. Tomas waited until the sound faded.

"They're alive," he said.

The night got very quiet around those two words, the way a room goes quiet when someone says the thing everyone has been carefully not saying.

"The doctor, her staff, the students — alive. Up in the high valley. The valley of the Second Sun, my grandmother calls it. There are men holding them there. Not bandits. Hired men. Contracts, rations, a supply line down the gorge trail." He swallowed. "Hale's men. Hired months ago. Before the expedition ever lost contact."

I sat down on the wet rock, because standing suddenly seemed ambitious.

"Say that again. Slowly."

"Hale hired them in the spring. Through a labor agent in Cobre — I carried the payroll myself, twice, up to the confluence. I didn't know what it was for then. Money moves, you don't ask it where it's going." His hands shook around the canteen. "The expedition wasn't lost. They finished the approach in June. Found what they found. And then the valley closed around them, and the radio went dark because a man named Osorio smashed it with a rock, and I know because I was there, I carried the rock's owner's tent—"

"Stop." My own voice sounded strange to me. "You're telling me the rescue is fake. That Hale knew where they were the entire time."

"He didn't lose them," Tomas said simply. "He's retrieving them. There's a difference. You lose a thing when you don't know where it is."

Above us the sentry stopped walking. We both froze, Tomas pressing himself deeper into the crevice, me becoming very interested in the fuel line I had already checked. The boots resumed. Gravel, gravel, pause. A man paid to be bored, doing his job badly, thank God.

I helped Tomas drink again and thought about what a fool I'd been, and how much company I had in it. Because I had known. Not known-known. Pilot-known. The way you know a current is wrong before your instruments agree. Twelve armed men for a rescue. A medic who packed more ammunition than gauze. Hale's questions on the boat — not *how fast can we reach the last coordinates* but *what's the water level at the confluence in October, Sef, and how long does the high trail stay open*. Logistics of occupation, dressed as logistics of mercy.

And underneath it, the older thing. The thing Sef Maren tells no one: Hale's last expedition up this river, six years ago, came back short two people and long on silence, and I piloted the boat that brought the survivors down, and I watched Marcus Hale stand on my deck explaining the deaths to the families with such fluent sorrow that I nearly believed him myself. Nearly. The fluency was the problem. Grief stumbles. His grief had rehearsed.

Now here was a broken porter in a crevice handing me the missing page.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I did on the wet rock while Tomas dozed against my shoulder, feverish, muttering names — Beto, his mother, a valley, a sun that rises twice:

If I tell Gideon Reyes what Tomas told me, the party fractures. Maybe that night. Gideon is not subtle — I've watched them lie to Hale for five days with all the grace of a heron landing in a tin roof — and if Gideon knows, Hale will know within a day, and then it's guns on a gorge trail with eleven days of food and a wounded man who cannot walk.

If I don't tell Gideon, I deliver them — and their mother, alive, God help us all — straight into a retrieval operation wearing a rescue's clothes. I hand Dr. Elena Voss to the man who has been holding her for months, and I do it smiling, for wages.

There was a third option, of course. The one sensible men take. Put Tomas in the boat's hold under the tarp, run downriver at first light, report to the naval post at Cobre, let institutions handle it. I want to tell you I considered it seriously. What I actually did was consider it for about nine seconds, during which I remembered that the naval post at Cobre has a radio that works on Tuesdays and a commandant who plays cards with Hale's labor agent, and I laughed out loud, alone, on a rock, in the dark, which is the kind of laughter that should worry a man.

So it came down to Gideon.

And here is the part I keep turning over, the part that kept me awake after Tomas finally slept: I barely know them. Five days. A fare paid in cash, a passenger who asks where everyone sleeps. By every rule of the river — and the river has rules, carved older than any government — you carry the passenger, you protect the passenger's skin, and you do not bleed for them. Loyalty is for crew. Family. The dead you owe.

But I had watched Gideon Reyes read the burned lower camp like a text, crouched in the ash with their sleeves rolled, finding the lie in it — *staged, days old, deliberate* — and I had seen their face when they said it. Not the face of a person hunting a paycheck. The face of a person who had spent three years practicing not caring and had just been caught red-handed caring anyway.

And I had watched them look at Hale. Really look. With that flat, patient attention, like someone reading an inscription they already half-translate. Gideon didn't trust Hale. Gideon was waiting for permission not to.

Maybe I was the permission. Maybe that's vanity. Pilots are vain; we spend our lives believing the river needs us specifically.

Tomas stirred and gripped my wrist with a hand light as a bird's. "The valley," he murmured. "My grandmother's people. The doctor was good to us. She learned our words. She asked about the second sun." Fever-bright eyes found mine. "Don't let them erase it. Whatever she found — don't let them erase it."

*I'm a boat,* I almost said. *Boats don't decide what gets erased.*

But boats go where they're pointed. And somewhere between Puerto Cobre and this crevice, without signing anything, I had let myself be pointed.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold off the water. I carried Tomas up the slope on my back — ninety kilos of pilot, sixty of porter, and the math still worked, barely — and installed him in the hold beneath the fuel tarp, dosed with willow bark and the last of my good rum, which hurt me more than it hurt him.

Then I went to find Gideon Reyes, who was already awake, already watching the trailhead, already wearing the face of a person counting exits.

"Sef," they said. "You were out late."

"Found something." I glanced back at the camp — at Hale's tent, at the sentry rubbing sleep from his eyes, at Ferrer, the advance-team boss, eating breakfast fifty meters away with the relaxed appetite of a man whose dirty work is currently buried in a hole below the pass. "Something you'll want to hear. But not here, and not loud."

Gideon's eyes did the thing they do — that quick, total inventory, reading me the way they'd read the ash of the burned camp. Looking for the lie. Finding, I suppose, whatever it is people find instead.

"Walk with me," they said. "I need to check the mooring line."

It was a good lie. Terrible execution, perfect instinct. The mooring line was fine and we both knew it, so we stood at the bow in the dawn mist with the Rapids of the Drowned growling upstream like a dog that smells weather, and I told Gideon Reyes everything Tomas Quispe had told me on the wet rock in the dark.

I watched the knowledge land. Watched it move through them like a flood stage rising — denial first, then the terrible quick recalculation, the whole architecture of the trip quietly rebuilding itself around a new fact. Rescue becomes retrieval. Mother becomes hostage. Employer becomes jailer. I watched them arrive at each truth in order, and I watched them refuse to say any of it aloud, because saying it aloud on an open riverbank is how people die.

"So," Gideon said at last, very quietly, eyes on the water. "We're not the rescue party."

"No."

"We're the retrieval crew."

"Now you've got it."

They were silent a moment. Then they smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen on a human face — small, cold, and absolutely steady, the smile of someone who has just been given permission.

"Well," Gideon Reyes said, turning back toward camp, toward Hale, toward the double life they were about to start living in earnest. "Let's go make sure Marcus sleeps well tonight."

Upstream, faint under the river's growl, I heard — or thought I heard — the far-off crack of a rifle echoing down from the direction of the pass. Once. Just once.

Neither of us mentioned it. Both of us heard it.
