## 7. The Turn at the Confluence

The trouble with learning a terrible truth on a river is that there's nowhere to put your face. No door to close, no room to walk into and fall apart in private. Just the deck of the *Mariposa*, the brown water sliding past, and Marcus Hale three meters away asking if I wanted coffee.

"I'd love some," I said, and smiled at him with the whole face, the way you do when you've just decided to become a liar for the foreseeable future.

Sef had told me at dawn, down in the engine well where the diesel throbbed loud enough to swallow words. The porter — Tomás, his name was Tomás, though he'd been "the porter" to Hale's men right up until he was a bleeding problem left in a ditch — had talked through cracked lips while Sef pressed water to his mouth. Elena's expedition wasn't lost. It was held. In the high valley. By men Hale had hired months ago, before the expedition ever went silent, before the letter, before any of it. The rescue was a retrieval. My mother wasn't missing; she was inventory.

And I had said, "Okay," which is what cowards and strategists both say, and then I'd gone up on deck and drunk Hale's coffee and asked him intelligent questions about fuel reserves like a person who did not know.

Here's what nobody tells you about lying: it's not the big lies that grind you down. It's the small ones, the dozens per hour, the *good morning* and *sure, whatever you think* and *I slept fine*. Each one is nothing. Together they're a second job you work all night without pay.

* * *

The confluence came at midday, where the Sereno met a tributary the maps called the Río Oscuro and Sef called by a name that predated the maps. The water went from brown to a strange banded green-brown, two currents arguing, and above it the Cordillera del Viento stood up out of the haze like something that had been waiting for us specifically.

Hale stood at the rail with binoculars, and I stood next to him, because that's what a loyal expedition member does, and I watched my mother's prison take shape on the horizon and said, "Beautiful country."

"It is," Hale said. "Shame about the people."

I let that one go past. There would be hundreds like it, and if I flinched at each one I'd be dead of honesty by Thursday.

The plan, such as it was, formed itself out of necessity rather than genius, which is how most plans form and why most plans fail. We couldn't take Tomás with us openly — Hale believed Tomás dead or abandoned, and the man could barely stand. We couldn't tell the rest of the party, because among Hale's fourteen men there were maybe four who weren't his, and we didn't know which four. So: play along upriver, get within reach of the foothills, and slip away at night with Tomás and whatever map Sef could steal from Hale's cabin. Find the valley first. Get to Elena before Hale's "rescue" became something else.

It required me to spend every waking hour being exactly the person Hale thought he'd hired: Gideon Reyes, mercenary archaeologist, estranged from the famous mother, in it for the salvage rights and the money. The beautiful thing — the awful thing — was that this character required almost no acting. I'd spent years building him. All I had to do was stop hiding him.

"So," Hale said over lunch, tinned beef and hard bread on a crate between us, "your mother. You two close?"

"Not even slightly." I chewed. "She sent me a letter because she needed a body who could read stratigraphy. That's the whole relationship."

"That must sting."

"It did. For about a decade. Now it's just weather." I shrugged, and the shrug cost me more than anything I'd done on this river, because underneath it my hands wanted to break his jaw. "What's the split on artifacts? Fifty-fifty after expenses?"

His eyes flickered — not suspicion, just the reflex of a man doing arithmetic he'd already done. "Sixty-forty. My capital."

"Forty of a tomb's worth of gold is still forty," I said, and laughed, and hated the laugh, its easy rotten warmth.

That night I lay in my bunk listening to the river and rehearsing tomorrow's lies the way other people rehearse arguments they'll never have. Somewhere below, Sef turned the wheel through the dark, and somewhere ahead, behind stone walls I hadn't seen yet, my mother was alive and counting days she didn't know were numbered differently than she thought.

* * *

Hale got sharper around day three.

I should have expected it. Men like Hale aren't stupid — that's the trap of them. They're stupid about people, about the things that can't be bought, but about process, about risk, about the mechanics of a job going sideways, they're scalpel-sharp. And something had changed in his advance team's radio schedule, some check-in that came back wrong, because on the third morning he started eating breakfast facing the deck instead of the view.

"Gideon." He sat down across from me uninvited. "Walk me through your read on the burned camp again."

I'd given this testimony once already, back at the lower camp, standing over ash: staged destruction, deliberate, someone wanting the world to believe the expedition dead. I gave it again, word for word nearly, because consistency is the spine of a lie. "Fire set from the outside of the tents, supplies carried off not scattered, radio smashed with one clean blow instead of a frenzy. Whoever did it was tidying up a story, not venting rage."

"And who tells stories?"

"Governments. Insurers." I sipped my coffee. "Financiers."

He smiled at that, appreciating the edge, and I realized I'd made an error — too clever, too comfortable needling a man I was supposed to fear moderately. A real estranged child wouldn't spar with him. A real one would be brittle.

"You think your mother's alive," he said. Not a question.

"I think the camp says alive. Dead men don't need their deaths staged twice." I let some genuine exhaustion show, which was easy, because I had a surplus. "Look, Hale. If she's alive, fine, we pull her out, she signs her share over to me out of guilt, everyone wins. If she's dead, I inherit the claim anyway. Either way I'm motivated. You don't need to worry about my feelings."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I don't."

But after that he worried about them constantly. He assigned Reyes — no relation, a squat man with a shotgun and a sense of humor like wet sand — to "assist" me at survey work, which meant stand behind me and watch my hands. He started asking casual questions at dinner: where had I trained, who had I worked for, did I keep in touch with anyone. Building a file. I answered everything honestly except the parts that mattered, which is the only sustainable way to do it, and even so I could feel the double life settling into my shoulders like a load I'd have to carry up a mountain.

* * *

Sef and I developed a language out of nothing, the way people do under pressure. A question about ballast meant *are we safe*. An answer about ballast meant *not tonight*. When Sef said the current was "shifting early this year," it meant Hale's men had searched the crew quarters. When I complained about the coffee, it meant I'd found something in Hale's cabin worth knowing — which, on the fifth night, I had: a ledger page, copied in my own hand in ninety seconds by lamplight while Reyes smoked on the foredeck, listing payments dated eight months back to names I didn't recognize and one I did, a name Tomás had given us. Hale hadn't hired his muscle after the silence. He'd hired it before. The rescue was theater that had been written before the tragedy it claimed to respond to.

I folded the copy into a oilcloth packet the size of a cigarette case and began looking for a way to get it to Sef.

This proved harder than stealing it. On a boat with thirty feet of usable deck and a watcher assigned to me, passing a note is a logistical operation worthy of a heist film, except in heist films nobody has dysentery and the lighting budget is better.

The chance came at the confluence's upper reaches, where the river narrowed and the *Mariposa* had to warp through a set of rapids by winching along a line Sef's shore crew rigged. Everyone was needed. Even watchers. Even me, hauling on a wet rope with the spray coming off the rocks cold enough to hurt, and Sef ten feet away on the bank holding the line's anchor point.

I couldn't speak to him. Reyes was beside me, grunting, pulling. But the packet was inside my glove, and the rope was wet, and when we swung the line wide for the next wrap I let my end drag through the shallows near Sef's boots — a natural mistake, rope catches on everything — and crouched to free it, and my hand closed over his ankle for half a second, pressing the flat little weight of oilcloth against his bootlace, and then the rope pulled and I staggered up and swore at it convincingly and kept hauling.

Sef never looked down. That was the discipline of the man. He finished the wrap, spat in the river, and said, loudly, "Current's shifting early this year."

Not tonight, then. But soon.

* * *

They nearly caught us two nights later, and it was entirely my fault, and I've replayed it enough times since to know exactly which moment I lost control of it.

We'd tied up below a rotting checkpoint — one of the old government posts, now just a roofless concrete box with a flagpole and a family of goats — and Hale had posted a night patrol, two men walking the bank in opposite directions, meeting at the bow every twenty minutes. Standard. Predictable. What wasn't predictable was that Sef and I had gotten greedy. Tomás's fever had spiked that evening, and Sef had medicine in the hold, but getting to Tomás meant crossing the open aft deck, and we'd worked out a signal system — a note passed at the changing of the patrol, when both walkers were at maximum distance from the stern — and I'd volunteered to take the watch-count myself so Sef could sleep before his turn at the wheel.

So there I was at two in the morning, counting footsteps in the dark, holding a note that said *fever worse — quinine in the green duffel — burn this*, waiting for the gap, and the gap came, and I moved — and the patrol didn't pass each other. One of them had stopped. To relieve himself against the checkpoint wall, ten meters short of the meeting point, which threw the whole rhythm off by ninety seconds, and I was already in the open, halfway across the deck, with a piece of paper in my hand and no story for it.

I did the only thing available. I kept walking. I walked the way a sleepless person walks, aimless, hunched, and when the man straightened up and turned, I was standing at the rail staring at the water like a philosopher, and the note was gone — dropped over the side? No. Held flat against the rail under my palm, which was worse, because he came over.

"Reyes." His flashlight found my face. "Something wrong?"

"Can't sleep." I gestured at the river with my chin. "Thinking about my mother."

It was, absurdly, the truest thing I'd said in a week, and it worked, because truth has a texture that lies can't fake and tired men recognize it. He grunted, sympathized in the way men do when emotions appear unexpectedly — a pat on the shoulder that nearly broke my collarbone — and walked on toward the bow to complete his circuit.

I stood there until my heart rate returned to something mammalian. Then I looked down. The note was still under my palm, damp with river air, and beneath it, caught on a rusted cleat, a scrap of paper I hadn't put there.

Sef's handwriting, in the dark, at some earlier hour: *Patrol changed. New route. Wait for my signal.*

He'd known. He'd known the pattern had shifted and he'd left me a warning I'd almost been too busy to find, and the note in my hand was suddenly evidence of how far behind I was running in a game where the other player never slept.

I fed both papers to the river and stood a long time watching the black water take them.

* * *

In the morning, Hale invited me to look at his maps.

It was friendly. That was the worst part. He spread them on the chart table in his cabin — the stolen map, the survey sheets, the valley approaches — and asked my professional opinion on routes, and I gave it, honestly, competently, pointing out the pass he should take and the slopes that would kill his mules, and he listened and nodded and thanked me, and only afterward, walking out, did I understand what the session had been. He hadn't wanted my opinion. He'd wanted to see where my finger hesitated. Where I lingered. Which route I argued *against*.

Because a man planning to run will steer you away from the way he means to go.

Had I hesitated? Over the western approach, maybe — the old salt road country, the route no map showed, the one Sef was already asking Tomás about in low voices below decks. Maybe. I couldn't remember, and not remembering was its own kind of terror. The double life doesn't leave you room to audit yourself. Every gesture gets performed so long it stops leaving records.

At noon, Hale stood at the rail beside me again, watching the mountains come on, and said, conversationally, "You know what I admire about you, Gideon?"

"My modesty."

"Your pragmatism." He passed me the binoculars. "Most people, when they learn the world isn't what they were told, they get sentimental. They want the world to apologize. You just recalculate. I saw it the day you boarded." He smiled at the horizon. "People who recalculate are the only people worth hiring."

I took the binoculars and raised them toward the high valley, toward the smoke I could almost pretend was cooking fires, toward the barred window I hadn't seen yet and already couldn't stop seeing, and I said, "Thank you, Marcus. That means a lot."

And the river carried us on toward the foothills, and behind us the last of the dry season burned off the ridgelines, and I began, quietly, to count nights until the dark would be long enough to lose fourteen men in.
