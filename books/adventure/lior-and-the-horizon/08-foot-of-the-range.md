## 8. Foot of the Range

The Cordillera del Viento announced itself three days before we reached it, the way bad news does — first as a rumor on the horizon, then as a fact you couldn't stop looking at. From the last ridge of foothill scrub, the range stood up against the sky like a wall built by something with more ambition than skill, its peaks still white in the dry season, its lower slopes the color of old bone.

"Beautiful," said Vásquez, one of Hale's men, spitting with the reverence of a man who'd been paid to appreciate scenery. "And dead past the passes by the first week of the rains."

"Then we'd best be quick," Hale said, and smiled at me, and I smiled back, and between us passed one of the small counterfeit courtesies I had by now grown expert in. Three weeks upriver had taught me the grammar of Hale's smile: it meant *I am watching you*, or *I am pleased with you*, or *I have decided to let you live a while longer*, and the trick was that you never learned which.

We made camp at the foot of the range in the last of the afternoon light, in the lee of a rock outcrop the porters called, in their own tongue, something Sef translated as *the place where the wind sleeps*. It did not sleep. It moaned over the stones all night like a man with a grievance, and I lay in my bedroll listening to it and thinking about maps.

I had stolen the map two nights before. I want that on the record, since I've spent most of my life insisting I'm not a thief: I stole it, deliberately, from Hale's own survey case, while he was at dinner performing magnanimity for the porters. It was a beautiful thing — hand-inked, annotated in two languages and a third that might have been a private shorthand — and it showed something the official route did not: a line of old salt-traders' paths threading the foothills, paths that bypassed the main pass entirely. Paths a small party could take and a large one could not follow.

Hale thought I was his. That was the whole architecture of my survival: a man who buys rescues believes everyone can be bought, and I had accepted his wage without haggling, which in his arithmetic made me honest. I had even, in a moment of inspiration I still didn't entirely understand, told him a truth — that I wanted to find my mother before he did. He'd liked that. It fit his model of the world. A daughter competing with the financier for the archaeologist, all of us animals at the same trough. He'd never once considered that I might want my mother for the unfashionable reason, the one that didn't come with a price tag.

The truth, deployed correctly, is the best lie there is. Elena taught me that, in a way, though she'd be appalled to hear it.

* * *

Sef found me at the fire after the others turned in. He sat down without asking, the way he always did, and poured himself coffee he hadn't made, and for a while we watched the flames do their small useless work against all that dark.

"Tomorrow," he said. Not a question.

"Tomorrow night. The watch changes at the second hour; Vásquez drinks. The new man is the one who cried at the burned camp — he'll see a ghost in every shadow and report nothing."

"You've been counting his drinks."

"I've been counting everyone's drinks. It's the only mathematics I'm good at." I fed the fire a stick. "The porter — Tomás — can he walk?"

Sef was quiet a moment. The porter Hale's advance team had left to die, the one we'd carried for six days on a ruined ankle, the one who'd told us everything: the expedition alive, held at the high valley, the rescue a retrieval operation with my mother as the collateral. "He says he can run," Sef said. "He's lying. But he'll do it anyway, and sometimes that's the same thing."

"Then that's settled."

"Gideon." He said my name the way he always did, flat, like a stone set down on a table, and I braced. "You understand what we're doing. Once we go, there's no coming back to this party. Hale won't forgive it. Hale doesn't forgive."

"I'm counting on him not forgiving. A man who forgives sends one tracker. A man who doesn't sends six, and six men moving fast through the foothills at night make noise."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else for a week. I'm very tired and I'd like to think about something else, if you have a suggestion."

Sef looked at me across the fire, and there was something in his face I couldn't read — not disapproval, not quite approval either, but the look of a man checking his instruments one last time before weather he doesn't trust. "You know what's strange about you?" he said. "You've spent your whole life running away. Anyone can see it. You pack before dawn, you leave positions people would kill for, you've got the lightest baggage of anyone I've ever carried a boat with." He stood, brushing off his trousers. "But this is the first time I've seen you run *toward* something. It looks different on you. You keep almost smiling."

"I don't smile."

"No," he agreed, and walked off into the dark, and I sat there furious to discover he was right.

* * *

The next day was the longest of my life, and I have had some competitors. We broke camp at dawn and climbed through foothill country that got stonier and more beautiful by the hour, Hale's party strung out in a line like beads on an ugly necklace. Hale rode when the terrain allowed, which it rarely did, and dismounted with the good humor of a man whose plans are proceeding. He talked to me as we walked. That was the worst part — he *talked*, amiably, about markets and mountain weather and the economics of remote labor, and once, delicately, about my mother.

"Dr. Voss always struck me as a woman who understood leverage," he said, stepping over a streambed without looking at it. "Whatever she's found up there, she'll have grasped its value immediately. It's why she'll cooperate. Sensible people do."

"You make her sound like an investment."

"Everything is an investment, Ms. Reyes. Some people are just embarrassed by the paperwork." He glanced at me, mild as milk. "You'll understand when we get there. Family reunions are simpler when everyone's interests are aligned."

I smiled my counterfeit smile and thought about the map folded against my ribs, and about the drainage of the foothills, and about how many hours of darkness we'd have, and I did not push him into the streambed, though I want it noted that the thought had real architectural merit.

The dry season was burning out around us as we climbed. The grass on the lower slopes had gone the color of straw and then the color of ash, and the air had that taut, waiting quality of a place that hasn't decided whether to burn or flood. The porters muttered about it. Even the wind had changed its complaint — less grievance now, more warning.

By afternoon we'd made the last camp Hale intended before the pass proper, a shelf of ground above a dry wash, and I watched him stand at its edge looking up at the range with an expression I recognized from the river: the look of a man measuring something that could not be measured in money, and being annoyed about it.

* * *

We left at the second hour of the night, as planned.

I will not pretend it was graceful. Tomás's ankle had healed to the consistency of a promise, and the first half mile was a slow, breathless negotiation between his courage and his body, with Sef carrying him piggyback across the worst stretches and me scouting ahead with a shuttered lamp and my heart going like a bird in a closed room. The moon was a thin blade, no help and no hindrance. The wind had finally, insultingly, gone quiet, so that every sound we made seemed broadcast to the entire Cordillera.

We went not up, but sideways — along the contour of the foothills, toward the first of the salt-traders' paths, which the map promised began at a split rock shaped, according to the annotation in that third language, like a sitting woman. We found it by luck and lamplight, a boulder that did indeed have a hunched, patient silhouette, and behind it a track so faint it might have been an animal trail or a trick of the moonlight.

"That's it?" Tomás whispered, from Sef's back. "The old salt road? My grandmother carried salt on that road. She said the mountain women used to sing from the peaks so the traders wouldn't lose the way."

"Does it look like a road?"

"It looks like nothing. That's the point. Roads that look like something get found." He said it with the serene authority of a man quoting his grandmother, and I decided on the spot that his grandmother was the finest cartographer I would never meet.

We made perhaps four miles before the moon set and the dark became total, and then we did the thing I'd argued for and Sef had resisted: we stopped. Cold camp, no fire, wedged into a hollow beneath the sitting woman's shadow, taking turns at watch. Sef thought we should push through the dark and buy distance. I thought a party of three, one of them lame, feeling its way blind over broken ground, would leave a trail of broken ankles and noise, and noise was the one currency we couldn't afford. In the end Tomás settled it by falling asleep mid-sentence, and we stopped.

I took the second watch, which meant I lay awake for the first one, staring at stars so dense they looked like spilled salt, listening to the mountain breathe, running the arithmetic of pursuit. Hale would discover us missing at dawn. He would be angry, then calm, which was worse. He would read the ground — he was good at that, I'd give him that; a man who buys things learns to read what can't be bought — and he would see three sets of prints going sideways instead of up, and he would guess the salt road, or he would guess wrong and split his men, and either way he would send someone after us, because Hale did not leave loose threads. Loose threads, in his arithmetic, were investments in future problems.

And beneath the fear — I'll be honest, since this account has otherwise been too flattering to me — beneath the fear was something I hadn't felt since I was a child on my mother's digs, something I'd have denied under oath. It was the feeling of the night before a trench opened. The trowel in hand, the grid strung, the unknown under the soil. Terror and appetite in the same breath. For eleven years I had run from everything, and running had a flavor I knew too well: ash, and the specific loneliness of bus stations. This was different. Every mile of this flight was aimed at something. My mother was at the end of it, alive, imprisoned, furious — probably already composing the argument she'd have with me about proper excavation protocol — and I was going to her, and I was going *away from* a man who deserved to be fled from, and the two motions together made a kind of music.

I almost smiled. I caught myself doing it and stopped, on principle.

* * *

Dawn found us moving again, and the foothills found their voice. The wind came back with the sun, moaning over the stones, and under its cover we made good time — six miles, seven, the salt road revealing itself gradually as a road after all: a cut here, a retaining wall there, the bones of an old trade route showing through its skin of scrub like a dig site showing through topsoil. Tomás, riding Sef's back less and walking more, pointed out things his grandmother had told him: the cairn where traders left offerings, the overhang where they slept, the spring that was bitter in the dry months and sweet in the wet.

At midday we crossed a saddle and looked back, and that was when I saw them.

Two figures. Far below and behind, at the edge of the last camp, tiny as insects, but moving with the unmistakable economy of men who knew exactly what they were doing. One of them stood at the split rock, the sitting woman, and even at that distance I could see him crouched over the ground, reading it.

"They found the prints," Sef said. He didn't sound surprised. I don't think Sef had been surprised by anything since the Sereno.

"Two. He split his men."

"Or those two are the fast ones and the rest are behind." Sef lowered the field glasses. "The fast ones are the problem. The slow ones just make you tired."

I did the arithmetic again, and the arithmetic was bad. Two trackers, fresh, moving light, on a trail they could read. Three fugitives, one lame, carrying everything they owned, on a trail that only looked like nothing if you already knew it was a road. The math said they'd have us by nightfall, or the night after, and I found — and this surprised me — that I wasn't afraid in the old way. Not the ash-and-bus-station fear. This was cleaner. This was the fear of a puzzle with a clock on it.

"Then we don't run," I said. "We walk. We walk like traders. Tomás — your grandmother's road. Where would she have camped tonight? Where would she have *stopped*, unhurried, like a woman with salt to sell and no one chasing her?"

Tomás blinked at me, then at the trail ahead, and something shifted in his face — the porter's flat deference giving way to something older and prouder. "There's a shelter past the bitter spring," he said slowly. "Stone walls. My grandmother said the mountain women built them, the ones who sang from the peaks. If we camp there like it's ours—" his chin came up "—like we've always camped there—"

"—then anyone reading this trail reads a salt caravan, not a flight," I finished. "We cook. We leave leavings. We sleep like people who intend to be here tomorrow."

Sef looked at me for a long moment. "You want to *forge* a trail."

"I want to *excavate* one in reverse. It's the same skill. You just work backward." I was already walking. "Come on. We've got a reputation to build, and we've got until nightfall to build it."

Behind us, small and patient, the two figures came on. Ahead, the range stood against the sky, white and waiting, and somewhere past its teeth my mother was alive, and angry, and mine to find. For eleven years every road I'd taken had been pointed away from something. This one was pointed through.

I did not almost smile this time. I smiled.

And then the wind shifted, and brought down from the high country a smell I knew from the burned camp — woodsmoke, old and cold, and beneath it, faint and wrong, the smell of fresh ash.

Someone had made a fire on the salt road ahead of us. Recently. And we were walking straight toward it.
