## 9. The Pass of Teeth

The map failed us at noon, in the way maps do — not all at once, but politely, a little at a time, like a friend who stops returning your calls.

Sef had warned me. "Paper lies up here," he'd said that morning, tapping the stolen sheet with one blunt finger while the porter, Tomás, tightened the straps on his pack and pretended not to listen. "The salt road men drew this from memory, and memory is a liar with good handwriting."

He wasn't wrong. The pass was supposed to run east through a corridor between two ridgelines, marked on the map with a confident dotted line and the words *paso angosto* in someone's careful hand. What we found instead was a forest of stone. Wind-carved spires, some of them three men tall, crowded together in formations that looked less like geology and more like architecture — columns, arches, doorways leading nowhere. The wind had been sculpting this place for ten thousand years, and it had opinions about how we should proceed.

"Which way?" Tomás asked.

I turned a slow circle. Every direction looked identical: pale stone, blue shadow, sky so hard and high it felt like it might crack if I shouted at it. "The map says left."

"The map," Sef said, "also says there's a river here." He toed the dry gravel. "I don't see a river."

"We follow the sun line," I said, because saying something was better than admitting I had no idea. "Elena taught me that. When terrain contradicts the map, trust the sun and the slope. Water cuts down; passes cut across."

It was strange, how easily her name came out now. Three days ago I couldn't have said it without my chest tightening like a fist. Now it was just a tool, another instrument in the kit. *Mother as compass.* There was probably a dissertation in that for somebody.

We went left.

* * *

By mid-afternoon we were lost in earnest.

Not the romantic kind of lost, either — not the kind you put in memoirs. The ugly kind, where you've backtracked twice past the same spire with the split crown, where Tomás has stopped volunteering suggestions because he's noticed what I haven't wanted to notice: that the person he's following doesn't know where she's going.

I climbed the tallest spire I could find — handholds worn smooth as river stones, the rock humming faintly under my palms like it remembered being sung to — and looked out over the maze. From above it was worse. The spires ran in every direction, hundreds of them, a petrified crowd frozen mid-migration. Somewhere south and below us, Hale's men were climbing. I couldn't see them, but I could feel them the way you feel weather coming in your bad knee.

"Gideon." Sef's voice, low, from below. "Come down. Now."

I came down faster than I'd gone up.

He pointed. Between two spires maybe eighty meters off, a flicker of movement — there and gone, the color wrong for stone. Dust-colored. Man-colored.

"One?" I whispered.

"At least one. Maybe a scout. Maybe the point of a longer knife." He crouched, making himself small against the rock. "They move quiet. Hale hires quiet."

Tomás had gone very still. He was fifty-three years old, a porter by trade and a father of four by confession, and he had walked away from Hale's advance team with a bullet graze on his arm and a debt to Elena Voss he refused to explain. He looked at me now with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the arithmetic of a person calculating odds they don't like.

"How far to the far side of the pass?" he asked.

"If the map were honest? Four hours. Since it isn't?" I shrugged. "Tomorrow. Maybe the day after."

"And they are behind us by how much?"

"Half a day yesterday. Less today. They know these mountains better than we do."

Sef spat, carefully, into the gravel. "Then we don't sleep tonight. We walk the dark hours and let them chase our echo."

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It was also, as it turned out, the last plan we would get to choose for ourselves.

* * *

We walked until the light went from gold to copper to nothing, picking our way through the spires by feel and by the stars Sef read like a dockside schedule. The cold came down with the dark, the kind of altitude cold that gets inside your clothes and settles in your bones like it pays rent. Twice we heard things moving behind us — or thought we did. At altitude, at night, the wind makes a convincing imitation of everything.

Near midnight we found water — a seep running dark and thin down a channel between two spires — and stopped to fill bottles, and that was when the night broke open.

I heard it first as a sound I couldn't parse: a wet click, a grunt, the scrape of boots on stone. Then Sef shouted a word in river cant that needed no translation, and a shape detached itself from the black of a spire and came at us low and fast.

Everything after that happened in pieces.

A flashlight beam swinging wild. Tomás shoving me sideways — the old man, the *porter*, shoving me out of the line of whatever was coming. The crack of the beam hitting rock. My own hands doing something I hadn't decided to do, grabbing the man's wrist, his weight, the smell of him — tobacco and wool and fear. We went down together onto the gravel and I learned quickly that he was stronger than me and knew exactly what he was doing, and that knowing what you're doing is worth maybe half a second in a real fight, which is not enough.

Then Sef was there with a length of rope and no philosophy whatsoever, and the man went still under our combined weight, breathing hard, pinned.

The flashlight had died. In the starlight I could see the tracker's face: young, maybe twenty-five, cheeks windburned raw, eyes flat as coins. One of Hale's quiet ones. A coil of thin cord hung from his belt, and I didn't want to think about what it was for.

"Where are the others?" I asked.

He said nothing. His eyes moved — to Tomás, to Sef, measuring, cataloguing. Reporting, even now, even pinned. Professional to the marrow.

And then Tomás made a sound.

I turned and the old man was sitting down. Not collapsing — sitting, deliberate, the way you sit when your legs have filed their resignation. His hand was pressed to his side, and in the starlight the wet on it was very dark.

"He had a blade," Sef said quietly. "In the other hand. I never saw it."

Neither had I. Neither had Tomás, apparently, until it was in him.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about wounds in the field: the bleeding is never the dramatic part. The dramatic part is the decision.

Tomás's wound was a long shallow slice along the ribs — bad, not fatal, the kind of cut that kills slowly if you let it, or heals clean if you rest it. Rest. At altitude. With a tracker's team somewhere behind us in the dark, and a pass we couldn't find, and snows coming weeks early.

"You can't carry me," Tomás said. He said it before anyone else could say it, which told me he'd already done the math too. "You can't, and you shouldn't. Leave me the rope, the knife, the water. I'll follow slow. I know how to hide in stone — I carried loads through worse than this for eleven years."

"No," I said.

"Gideon—"

"I said no." I heard my own voice come out flat and hard, a stranger's voice, and underneath it something older: every departure I'd ever made, every dig site I'd slipped away from before dawn, every person I'd left before they could leave me. All of it rising up at once and refusing.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing I'd built my whole life around not learning: leaving is easy. Leaving is *free*. You pack before dawn, you decline the permanent position within the hour, you drift to the next horizon, and you tell yourself it's independence, it's self-preservation, it's the family curse. Nobody ever has to see you stop.

Stopping costs. Stopping means sitting in the dark with a bleeding man and a dead map and hunters on your trail, means spending hours you don't have, means betting everything — Elena, the valley, all of it — on the chance that mercy isn't just sentiment dressed up as strategy.

But I sat down anyway. Next to Tomás, in the freezing gravel, in the maze of teeth.

"Sef," I said. "Get the kit."

* * *

We worked by feel and by the shielded glow of a match-light lantern, Sef holding the light, me cleaning and closing the cut with the suture kit I'd carried for six years and used twice. My hands were steadier than I expected. Dig hands. They know how to be precise when everything else is chaos — it's the one inheritance from my mother I've never resented.

Tomás talked while I worked, because he was frightened and talking was how he held it off. About his daughters. About the coffee he grew on a quarter-hectare that produced more pride than profit. About how Elena Voss had paid porters' families double when the lower camp burned, out of her own money, and how no patrón had ever done that, not once in thirty years.

"So you see," he finished, as I tied off the last stitch, "you cannot leave me. Not because I am worth it. Because she would come back for me, and she would find you sitting here, and there would be a scene."

Despite everything, I laughed. It came out cracked and half-frozen, but it was a laugh.

"She would absolutely make it a scene," I agreed.

We couldn't stay where we were — the fight had been loud enough to carry, and the tracker, trussed to a spire with his own cord, would be found eventually by whoever came looking. But we couldn't move fast either. So we compromised: a sheltered hollow between two fallen slabs, half a kilometer on, where the wind couldn't reach and the stars couldn't see us. Sef rigged a litter from the rope and a pack frame. Tomás walked the first hundred meters out of sheer stubbornness, then submitted to being carried, hating every second of it.

I took first watch, because I couldn't have slept anyway.

The tracker — we'd left him alive, bound and furious, with water within reach and a lecture from Sef about what happens to men Hale forgets — would talk when his people found him. Which meant they'd know our direction by morning. Which meant we'd bought maybe six hours with our mercy, and spent a night we couldn't afford.

I sat with my back against cold stone and did the accounting. And here is what I found, there in the dark, listening to Tomás breathe: the ledger didn't balance the way I'd always assumed. All those years I'd believed stopping was the expensive choice, that motion was safety, that the horizon was a place where debts couldn't follow. But the truth was the opposite. Every place I'd fled had cost me something I never got back. This — the stitches, the wasted hours, the old man snoring under a scrap of tarp — this had cost me one night.

One night. Against a man's life. Against the version of myself who would have kept walking, and had to live with her afterward.

The eastern sky was still hours off when Sef relieved me, but the stars had wheeled enough that I could guess the time. Before I crawled into my bedroll, I looked back the way we'd come, toward the spires standing black against the fainter black of the sky.

"Hold on, Mamá," I said, to no one, to the mountain, to the woman in a barred window I hadn't reached yet. "I'm coming slower than I planned. But I'm coming."

Dawn brought the answer to a question I hadn't asked yet. Sef shook me awake, and his face told me everything before he said a word.

"There's smoke," he said. "Behind us. And ahead—" He paused, and for the first time since Puerto Cobre, the pilot looked genuinely afraid. "Ahead, Gideon. There's someone on the ridge watching us. And they're waving."
