## 10. Valley of the Second Sun

The map lied about the descent.

Not in the way maps usually lie — by omission, by optimism, by drawing a river where there is actually a cliff — but in the way that mattered most: it showed the valley as a place you could arrive at. As if arrival were a thing that happened to you, rather than something the valley permitted.

We had been climbing down through cloud for six hours. The porter — Tomás, though he'd only given us the name two days ago, like a man paying out rope slowly — walked ahead of us with his bandaged arm cradled against his chest and an expression I'd come to think of as his listening face. Sef was behind him, then me, then the pack mule, who had opinions about scree and shared them constantly with her hooves.

"You feel that?" Sef said.

I felt cold, exhaustion, and a bruise on my hip shaped exactly like a rock I'd fallen on in the pass. "Be specific."

"The air." He lifted his chin. "It's warm."

He was right, which annoyed me. Somewhere below the cloud line the wind had changed its mind. It came up at us now soft and smelling of green things — wet earth, some flowering shrub I didn't know, woodsmoke. Woodsmoke meant people. People meant either rescue or capture, and after nine days of running from Marcus Hale's men across the roof of the Cordillera del Viento, I'd stopped pretending to myself that I knew which one I wanted.

Then the cloud tore open below us, the way a curtain tears — all at once, without dignity — and there it was.

I have stood in tombs older than most languages. I have watched dawn come up over a desert city that no living person had named correctly in two thousand years. None of it prepared me for the valley of the second sun.

It sat in a bowl of mountains like a coin in a cupped hand, terraced green to the waterline of a lake so still it looked poured rather than gathered. The light did something wrong there — the peaks around it caught the morning and threw it down into the bowl, so that the valley lay in full sun while everything above it was still gray. A second sunrise, arriving late and from the wrong direction. The terraces stepped down in perfect concentric curves, planted with maize and something blue-flowering I couldn't identify, and stone channels carried water between them with the patient geometry of a system that had been maintained for longer than my entire civilization's presence on this continent.

And threaded through it all, roads. Not paths — roads, paved, edged, running straight where straightness served and curving where the land asked them to. Roads are an argument. These argued that the people who built them intended to be here forever.

"Madre de Dios," Sef said quietly.

Tomás said nothing, but he had stopped walking, and he took off his hat, which told me more than any words would have. You don't bare your head to scenery. You bare it to a church, or a grave, or your grandmother's house.

"This is it," I said. "This is what she found."

"This is what she *disturbed*," Tomás said, and put his hat back on, and started down.

* * *

They knew we were coming before we were halfway to the first terrace. I don't know how — no dogs barked, no horns sounded, no sentry shouted from a wall. But by the time we reached the lowest field, six people were waiting for us on the road, arranged in a loose crescent that managed to be both welcoming and unmistakably a checkpoint. Four men, two women, dressed in a mixture of woven wool and machine-made cloth, which is its own kind of archaeology: a culture that takes what it wants from the world and keeps what it keeps.

An old woman stood at the center of the crescent. She was small and upright and her hair was white as salt, braided and wrapped, and she looked at me the way I look at a stratigraphy profile — reading layers, checking for disturbance.

"Gideon Reyes," she said. Not a question.

"How—"

"Tomás sent word ahead from the pass. There is a boy who runs messages faster than fear does." Her eyes went past me to Sef, to the mule, to Tomás's bandaged arm, cataloguing damage. "You are the daughter of Elena Voss."

"Son," I said, because apparently even at the bottom of a hidden valley at the end of the world I was going to spend energy on that correction.

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An assessment, filed away. "Ama Quispe," she said. "You will eat. You will sleep. And tomorrow we will talk about why you ran down a mountain ahead of armed men instead of walking in behind them as their guest."

"We're not—" I started, and stopped, because I genuinely didn't know how to finish that sentence. We were not Hale's guests, true. We were also not anyone's guests. We were three fugitives and a mule, and the mule was arguably the most legitimate member of the party.

"You will eat," Ama Quispe repeated, in a tone that closed the subject the way a trowel closes a trench, and turned, and the crescent turned with her, and we followed.

* * *

The village — I hesitate to call it a village; it was too deliberate, too old, too well-kept, a town wearing the modest clothes of a village — held perhaps four hundred people. Stone houses with steep thatched roofs, whitewashed doorways, a plaza with a fountain that worked, which in my experience of remote settlements puts it ahead of half the towns with governments. Children watched us from rooftops with the frank appraisal of children everywhere, and one of them, a girl of maybe seven, fell into step beside me and walked with me for a hundred meters before deciding I wasn't interesting enough to keep.

They fed us in a long low hall near the plaza: soup with potatoes whose names I didn't know, roasted maize, a dark grain cake, tea that tasted like altitude and honey. I ate the way I always eat after days of terror and short rations — mechanically, gratefully, without tasting much — but part of my mind was already working the room the way it works a dig. The hall was old. Older than the walls around it. The roof beams were cut in a style I'd seen once, in fragments, in a museum drawer in the capital, labeled *provenience unknown*.

Provenience unknown. That phrase has haunted me my whole career. It means: this object was torn out of its story and now sits in a drawer telling nobody anything. Looking up at those beams, I understood that somewhere in a dozen museums there were drawers full of provenience-unknown fragments of this place, and that every one of them had been carried out over the mountains by men who told themselves stories about discovery.

"You're doing the thing," Sef said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your eyes go distant and your jaw sets and everyone around you knows you've stopped being a person and become an instrument."

"I don't do that."

"You absolutely do that. I've watched you do it at checkpoints, at rapids, and once, memorably, at a plate of fried plantains." He tore his grain cake in half and handed me a piece I hadn't asked for. "Eat. Then sleep. Tomorrow you can be an instrument."

But there was no sleeping, not really, not yet. Because while we ate, Tomás had gone quiet in the way of a man gathering courage, and when the bowls were cleared he leaned toward me and said, low, "The compound. You should know where they are keeping them."

* * *

The mission compound sat on a rise at the valley's northern edge, and I use the word *mission* the way the valley used it — as a name for a wound. I could see the shape of its history from the road: a chapel with a collapsed bell tower, a cloister walk with arches bricked shut, barracks added later by hands that cared nothing for proportion. The Spanish had built it, or forced its building, four centuries ago, and it had failed at whatever it was built to do, and been abandoned, and been absorbed — the way the valley seemed to absorb everything — into its own long argument with time.

Now it had new occupants, and they had not been absorbed.

From the cover of a terraced wall two hundred meters out, I counted. Eight guards visible, rotating on a schedule that was disciplined enough to tell me they were professionals and lax enough to tell me they weren't expecting trouble. Two men on the chapel roof with rifles. A truck under a tarp in the courtyard — a truck, which meant there was a track in and out of this valley that our stolen map had not shown me, which meant Hale's advance team had known ways into this place that predated the map, which meant the map itself might have been bait. I filed that thought where I file thoughts that make me want to lie down.

And at a barred window on the second floor of the cloister wing, a figure moved.

I want to tell you that I recognized her instantly — that blood called to blood across two hundred meters of evening air. I didn't. What I saw first was posture: a woman standing at a window with her arms crossed, weight on one hip, head tilted at the exact angle of someone reading a document that has insulted her. I have seen that posture across dinner tables, across airport terminals, across the widening gap of three silent years. My body recognized it before my mind consented to.

Elena Voss. My mother. Alive, ambulatory, and — judging by the way she was pacing the width of her cell like a caged animal conducting a survey of its cage — furious.

Something in my chest did a complicated structural failure. Relief, grief, rage, and a fourth thing I had no name for, some mineral deposit laid down over years of not-caring that cracked all at once and let everything through.

"She looks well," Sef said carefully.

"She looks like she's about to write a strongly worded letter to her captors."

"That's how you know she's well."

I laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and then pressed my fist against my mouth because the sound wanted to become other sounds. Nine days. Nine days of burned camps and forged notes and the Pass of Teeth and Tomás bleeding on the scree while I learned — finally, expensively — that stopping costs less than leaving. All of it aimed at a barred window, and now here it was, and she was forty meters and eight guards and one locked door away, and she was pacing.

She stopped at the window. She looked out — not at us, not sensing us, nothing so cinematic; she just looked out, at the valley, at the second sun going gold on the terraces, with an expression I couldn't read at this distance and could read perfectly anyway, because it was the expression she wore at every site she ever loved: *how does this work, and how soon can I understand it better than anyone alive.*

That was the woman who chose a site over a child. That was the woman whose letter had arrived like a hand from a drowning dream. That was the woman I had crossed a mountain range for while telling myself I hadn't.

"Gideon." Sef's hand on my shoulder. "We can't stay on this wall. The rotation comes back around in ninety seconds."

I knew that. I'd done the count myself. I stayed four seconds too long anyway, memorizing her — the gray in her hair that hadn't been there three years ago, the way she pushed off the windowsill, the fact of her, breathing, furious, alive.

Then I slid down behind the wall, and we went.

* * *

Ama Quispe was waiting for us when we returned to the hall, sitting alone by the banked fire, and I understood immediately that we had not slipped out unnoticed, that nothing in this valley slipped anywhere unnoticed, and that our little reconnaissance had been permitted rather than missed.

"You saw them," she said.

"Some of them. Through a window."

"And now you want to go in tonight with fire and noise and get them all killed." She said it without heat, the way you'd note that water runs downhill. "The young always want to go in tonight."

"I want—"

"You want your mother. I understand wanting. Sit down."

I sat. The fire smelled of some resinous wood, and outside, the valley's second sun was dying in the ordinary way, sliding off the western peaks, letting the bowl fill with blue shadow and early stars.

"Marcus Hale's men came to us in the spring," Ama Quispe said. "Before your mother's expedition. They came asking questions about ruins in the high country, offering money for guides, offering more money for silence. We gave them neither. So they found other guides — men from outside, men who owe nothing to stone." She stirred the fire. "When Dr. Voss came, she came differently. She wrote to us first. A letter, carried over the pass, asking permission. Do you understand? In four hundred years, since the ones in black robes came over the ridge, no one has asked us permission."

I thought of my mother's letters — the few I'd received, the fewer I'd answered. Formal, precise, emotionally armored documents that nonetheless always contained, buried in a paragraph about logistics, one sentence that was trying to be an olive branch and usually came out sounding like a footnote. Apparently she'd gotten better at it. Apparently she'd saved her best letter for strangers.

"She found the tomb," I said.

"She found the door to the record house. Whether there is a tomb behind it depends on what you think a tomb is for." Ama Quispe looked at me across the fire, and her eyes in the firelight were the color of the lake at dusk. "Your mother thinks it is for remembering. The man Hale works for thinks it is for owning. Both of them are partly right, which is why one of them is in a cage and the other is coming up the mountain with guns."

"When?" I asked. "How long do we have?"

"He moves when the snows threaten to close his retreat. The storm season is early this year. Days, not weeks." She rose, joints cracking like green branches, and stood over me with the fire at her back. "Tonight you sleep. Tomorrow you learn what your mother found, and why men with trucks and rifles are willing to burn a valley to erase it. And then, daughter of Elena—" her mouth did that not-quite-smile again, "—son of Elena, we will decide together what is worth fighting for. Here, decisions are made together. Even by people who run down mountains."

She left. Sef, who had been feigning sleep with the transparency of a man who misses nothing, opened one eye.

"So," he said. "Any chance you tell me what's in that tomb?"

"Nope."

"Because you don't know, or because you've already decided it's yours to protect?"

I pulled my blanket up and stared at the roof beams — old beams, provenience known, cut by hands that had meant to stay — and realized with a small internal shock that I didn't know the answer either.

Above the mission compound, on the rise, a guard's lantern swung past the barred window where my mother slept, or paced, or read some document that had insulted her. Three years of silence, and now forty meters of stone and eight men with rifles between us, and somewhere down that darkening valley, a truck track led out to a world where Marcus Hale was counting the days until the snows.

Days, not weeks. I closed my eyes and, for the first time since the coast, did not start planning the route of my leaving.
