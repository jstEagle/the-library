## 11. Reunion, Interrupted

They took my boots on the fourth day.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last thing I own. Not the watch — they let me keep the watch, which was either an oversight or a message. Not the notebook, though they read it twice and returned it with a page dog-eared at the entry where I'd sketched the door lintel of the tomb. The boots. Marcus Hale's men took my boots because a woman without boots does not walk out of a compound, and a woman who cannot walk out of a compound will, eventually, sit down and negotiate.

It was the smartest thing anyone did to me in that valley, and I have resented it with a purity of feeling I usually reserve for bad peer review.

Let me go back further, because captivity has made me a narrator whether I like it or not. There is nothing to do in a room but arrange events into sequence, and I have spent forty years arranging events into sequence for grant committees, so I am, God help me, good at it.

* * *

The tomb was found on a Tuesday. I remember because Tomás Ochoa — our surveyor, a man who believed every important discovery should be preceded by a decent lunch — had just finished his empanadas when the ground gave way under Renn's left boot near the eastern scree field. Not dramatically. No chorus of angels. Just a soft collapse, like the earth clearing its throat, and then a smell coming up out of the dark: cold stone, dry dust, and something older underneath, the smell of sealed air, air that had been waiting.

I went down first. Of course I went down first. My daughter would tell you that was characteristic, and she would not mean it as a compliment, and she would be right.

What we found was not treasure. I need you to understand that, because everything that followed — the men with guns, the compound, the boots — happened because other people could not understand it. It was a record. A treaty, cut into slate panels taller than I am, stacked in niches along a corridor like books in a library built by giants. Two peoples: the valley folk and the lowland kingdom whose names the Spanish mangled beyond recovery. Boundaries. Water rights. A shared stewardship of the high pastures, sworn before witnesses whose genealogies ran down both walls of the antechamber.

And here is the thing that made my hands shake, here is the thing I wrote in my notebook in handwriting that deteriorated panel by panel: the boundaries described in that treaty match, almost line for line, the boundaries claimed today by the Confederación de los Altos — the resistance movement the government calls bandits and the government the resistance calls thieves. If the treaty were real — and it was real; I have spent my life learning to tell real from forged, and this was as real as bone — then the government's entire legal architecture for the land grab up here collapsed. Every concession, every decree, every smiling minister's map. Void. Or inverted: put the same document in different hands, and the resistance stops being a rabble and becomes a nation with a four-hundred-year-old deed.

One stone library. Two political detonations. Whichever side held it would hold the Cordillera.

I did what I thought was right, which is to say I did what I always do: I documented. Photographs, rubbings, translations begun that same night by lamplight while Tomás argued about ethics and Renn argued about weather and I argued with both of them because arguing is how I think. I sent word downriver — coded, cautious, addressed to no one — that we had found something that required protection, not publicity.

Marcus Hale received that message within six days.

I know now that he had men on the river before we'd finished the preliminary survey. I know that the "porters" he'd hired in the spring included three who reported to him monthly. I know all of this the way you know the shape of a betrayal after the fact: perfectly, uselessly, like reading a dig report for a site that's already been looted.

They came up through the pass in the last week of the dry season. Twenty-two men, rifles wrapped in burlap, walking in the loose staggered file of people who have done this before. Hale himself came a day behind them, in a clean shirt, carrying a bottle of decent pisco and an apology for the intrusion.

"We're concerned about your safety," he said, standing in my tent with his hands clasped behind his back like a man visiting a hospital ward. "The valley's not secure. The Confederación has been active."

"The Confederación hasn't come within thirty kilometers of us," I said.

"Which is why you've been safe." He smiled. He has a very good smile; I'll grant him that. It's the smile of a man who has never once been surprised by an outcome, only inconvenienced by one. "Dr. Voss. Elena. You've found something significant. Significant things require management."

"Management," I repeated.

"Custody," he amended, as if that were better.

They took the compound — the old mission, all whitewash and iron bars, the church's one concession to this valley before everyone with a cross moved on — and they took our radios, and they took our boots, and they put us in rooms with shutters that opened onto a courtyard where Hale's men smoked and laughed and cleaned their weapons with the unhurried competence of professionals. And every second day, Hale came to my room and asked me, pleasantly, inexorably, where the master panels were stored.

Because here is what Hale understood that I had underestimated: the treaty was only worth what its original could command. Copies could be disputed. Photographs could be dismissed as fabrication. But the stones themselves, sitting in a court or a ministry or a private vault in the capital — those were power you could hold. He didn't want me dead. Dead archaeologists make martyrs and attract expeditions. He wanted me compliant, and compliance, he believed, was a matter of time and discomfort.

He was wrong about me, but he wasn't stupid, and I have learned — late, expensively — that the two are often confused.

* * *

Eleven days after the radio went dark, I heard the boats.

You hear the Sereno before you see it, even up here — the river talks to itself around the bend below the valley mouth, and any change in that voice carries. That morning the voice changed: more engines than the supply run, shallower draft, the particular stutter of a boat being pushed harder than it likes.

I stood at my shutter and watched them come up the trail from the landing, and I counted nine figures, and then I stopped counting, because one of the figures walked in a way I knew.

You do not forget how your child walks. You may forget birthdays, anniversaries, the name of their first pet, the promises you made — I have forgotten all of these, and I will answer for it — but the walk stays. Gideon walks like someone perpetually leaving a room: weight forward, shoulders angled toward the nearest exit, eyes doing the arithmetic of doors. Even at eight years old, even in our own kitchen, Gideon walked like that. I used to think it was energy. It took me twenty years to understand it was preparation.

"They brought a stranger," Tomás said at the next door, low, through the wall we'd learned to talk through. "Young. Hale's treating them carefully. Carefully, Elena. He doesn't do careful."

"I know," I said.

"You don't know. You can't—"

"It's my child, Tomás."

Silence from the wall. Then, very quietly: "Then God help Hale."

I wanted to believe that. I lay on my cot that night and rehearsed it, the way I rehearse lectures: Gideon arrives, Gideon sees, Gideon understands, Gideon and I stand together against Hale and his twenty-two rifles. In the rehearsal it worked beautifully. Rehearsals always do. That is their function and their cruelty.

Because there was another rehearsal running underneath, the one I didn't invite: Gideon arrives, Gideon looks at me through the bars, and says, *Three years, Mamá. Three years and one letter.* Because that was true, too. I had sent exactly one letter in three years, and I had filled it with small talk — the weather on the river, the quality of the coffee at Puerto Cobre, a joke about Tomás's empanadas — because the real letter, the one that said *I found something that will get us killed and I don't know whom to trust and I am sorry, I have been sorry for twenty years, I chose stones over you and I would choose them again and I hate that I would choose them again* — that letter had no safe address. So I sent the small talk and told myself it was prudence.

Prudence. Yes. Let's call it that.

* * *

On the ninth night after the boats came, something scratched at the base of the east wall.

Not rats. I had become a connoisseur of the compound's rats; they scratched with purpose, in straight lines, along the skirting. This was irregular. Deliberate. Coming from outside.

The drainage culvert ran beneath the east wall — a colonial-era channel, half-collapsed, that carried snowmelt from the ridge down through the mission's foundations and out into the orchard. I had catalogued it in my first week the way I catalogue everything: as a feature, as a possibility, as a thing to note and set aside. Someone else had noted it too.

I knelt. The grate at the culvert's inner mouth was rusted through at one corner, and through the gap came a sliver of starlight and a smell of wet earth, and then — this is the part I will carry to my grave, whatever grave I get — a voice.

Low. Roughened. Careful.

"Mamá?"

Not *Doctor Voss*. Not *Elena*. Not even my name, which Gideon hadn't spoken aloud to me since the fight in Lima, the fight I started, the fight I won, the victory I have regretted every single day since. *Mamá.* One syllable of it, anyway, folded small so it would fit through a drainage grate.

I got down on my knees on a stone floor in a room where my captors had taken my boots, and I put my eye to the gap, and there was a face. Thinner than I remembered. A new scar through one eyebrow. Eyes that looked at me the way you look at a site you've excavated before and found disappointing.

"Gideon," I said. And then, because I am who I am, because forty years of habit will outlive gunfire and heartbreak and everything else: "Are you alone? What's your approach vector? How many—"

"Oh, for—" A breath, exhaled hard through the grate. "Hello to you too."

"That's not — I'm glad you're alive. I'm—" I pressed my forehead briefly against the cold stone. "I'm glad you came."

"Wasn't sure I did, honestly. Wasn't sure it was for you." A pause, and I could hear the sentence being dismantled and rebuilt behind it, the way Gideon rebuilds sentences when the truth gets too close to the load-bearing walls. "There's money in artifact retrieval. That's what I told myself."

"And now?"

"Now I'm lying in an orchard talking through a sewer, so draw your own conclusions."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — a short, ugly, relieved sound that I immediately smothered in my sleeve because the guard walked the east wall every eleven minutes and I had counted, God help me, I had counted. But Gideon heard it. I saw it land, saw something shift in that face at the edge of the starlight, some expression I recognized from a photograph I kept in a drawer for years after Gideon left: my child, aged nineteen, caught mid-laugh at someone else's joke, surprised by their own happiness.

Then the expression closed, and the next words came out flat and prepared, and I understood that we were going to have the argument now. Here. Through a hole in a wall. With a guard on an eleven-minute circuit. There was never going to be a better time. There was never going to be a quiet kitchen and a pot of coffee and a civilized hour. We had missed all of those trains, and this culvert was the platform we had left.

"Eleven days," Gideon said. "The radio went dark and you waited eleven days to send a letter, and the letter was about *coffee*."

"The letter was a code—"

"It was small talk. You're the best field archaeologist of your generation and you sent me small talk, because God forbid you ask me for anything. God forbid you say *come*, or *help*, or *I'm afraid*."

"I said the coordinates—"

"You said the coordinates the way you'd cite a footnote!" The whisper cracked and steadied, which was worse. "I spent three years telling myself you didn't write because you didn't need anything from me. That was fine. That was survivable. And then the letter comes and it turns out you didn't write because you were managing me. Like a grant committee. Like a — like one of your sites. Keep the stakeholders calm, disclose nothing material—"

"That is not fair."

"No," Gideon said, and for a moment the voice was younger, much younger, eight years old in a kitchen in Cusco while I packed a crate of sherds instead of attending a school play. "No, it isn't. Fair would take longer than eleven minutes."

Somewhere across the courtyard a rifle bolt slid home. My count said four minutes gone.

"Hale has the expedition staff in the west rooms," I said, fast now, all business, because business was the only language we had ever both spoken fluently and I hated myself for reaching for it and reached anyway. "Tomás, Renn, the Oviedos, six porters. He executes threats slowly — one prisoner punished per infraction, publicly. He wants the master panels. They're hidden. He doesn't know where."

"Do *you* know where?"

A beat. Through the grate, my child's eyes narrowed, and I saw the exact moment the archaeological mind engaged — the mind I had trained, the mind I had shaped, the mind that had learned suspicion at my own dinner table watching me deflect funders and rivals and, eventually, family.

"You know," Gideon said slowly. "You've known the whole time. You could buy everyone out of here tomorrow and you haven't, because the panels are leverage and you can't stop *managing* long enough to spend it."

"The panels aren't mine to spend," I said. "That's the entire point of them, Gideon. They belong to the valley. To Quispe's people. If I trade them to save six lives, I hand Hale the power to erase a nation, and if I destroy them to spite him, I bury the only proof these people ever had. Either way I decide for them. I have decided for other people my whole life. I am *not doing it again*."

The silence that followed was long enough for two circuits of the guard. When Gideon spoke again, the anger had burned down to something quieter and more dangerous, the way anger does in my family: it goes underground and becomes method.

"Ama Quispe," Gideon said. "The elder. She showed me the archive. I know what the treaty is, Mamá. I've known for four days." A breath. "Sef thinks there's a way out. The salt road, over the shoulder of the ridge, before the snows close it. We're working on it. All of it. The prisoners, the record, Hale — all of it."

"*We*," I repeated.

"Don't make it strange."

"It isn't strange. It's—" I stopped. The true sentence sat there between us, enormous and embarrassing: *it's the first time in twenty years you've said we and meant me.* I let it go unsaid. Some truths you ration. "Be careful in the orchard. The guard's circuit is eleven minutes, but the dog adds ninety seconds when it's windy — it loses the scent lines."

A pause. Then, dry as the dust in the tomb: "Ninety seconds. Of course you've timed the dog."

"Somebody raised me to count things."

The starlight shifted. Gideon was moving back from the grate, and I felt the panic rise, absurd and maternal and three years overdue — *wait, don't go, stay, I have questions, I have — there are things I—*

"Gideon."

The figure paused at the edge of the culvert's mouth.

"The letter," I said. "The coffee. The small talk. It was the only envelope I knew wouldn't be opened by the wrong hands. It was the only way I could reach you at all. It was never — I was never managing you. I was holding the door open the only way I knew how."

Another silence. Longer. Somewhere above us the wind changed, and far off, up on the black shoulder of the Cordillera, I heard — or imagined I heard, or perhaps neither of us imagined it — the first thin whistle of a season turning early.

"Keep counting the dog," Gideon said at last. "We move in three nights."

And then the starlight was just starlight, and the culvert was just a culvert, and I knelt alone on a stone floor in my stockinged feet, with three nights stretching ahead of me like a trench waiting to be dug — knowing that somewhere out in the dark, my child was counting guards and timing dogs and planning a war, and that the first words we had exchanged in three years had been an argument, and that somehow, impossibly, it had felt like a conversation.

Three nights. Outside, the wind rose off the ridge, and it sounded less like wind every hour, and more like snow.
