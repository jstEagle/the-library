## 13. Snows on the Ridge

I have spent my whole life reading water, so let me tell you what I know about weather: it does not negotiate. A river will give you a warning — a change in the current, a smell of silt, the birds going quiet. The sky over the Cordillera del Viento gave us nothing so courteous. It simply turned the color of an old bruise and began, without ceremony, to end the dry season three weeks ahead of schedule.

I was mending a net that had no business being this far from any water when I saw it. The cloud bank coming over the western ridge like something poured. Not building — *pouring*, slow and total, the way floodwater comes around a bend.

"Quispe," I said to the old woman grinding maize beside me, because I'd learned her name was Ama Quispe and not "the elder," and because in my experience people correct you once about their name and remember forever whether you bothered. "How long?"

She didn't look up from her work. That was my first answer. People who look at bad news are still deciding whether to believe it. People who don't look have already done the arithmetic.

"Two days," she said. "Maybe three for the pass itself. After that, nobody crosses until spring."

"Nobody."

"The mountain closes its door." She ground the maize with the patience of someone who had outlived every deadline she'd ever been given. "It has closed it on better-prepared men than the ones up at the mission."

That was the thing about Ama Quispe. She never said anything cruel. She just said things that were true, and let cruelty take care of itself.

* * *

The arithmetic went like this: we were maybe forty hours of hard walking from the pass, if the porter's leg held, which it would not. Elena Voss and her people were locked behind the compound walls, and Hale had doubled his sentries since the night Gideon nearly got caught passing notes — a night I still had nightmares about, mostly involving the sound of boots on gravel stopping directly behind me while I stood there holding a folded paper like an idiot holding a folded paper. We had one route out, the one on Hale's stolen map, and Hale knew that route as well as we did, because it was his map.

In other words: our entire plan depended on using the enemy's own road, at the enemy's own pace, through a door that was closing.

"Then we find another road," Gideon said.

We were in the back room of the weaving house, which had become our war room by the simple process of being the only room where Quispe's people could talk without Hale's ears. Gideon stood over the stolen map with that look I'd come to know — jaw set, eyes doing that archaeologist thing where they read the paper like it was a trench wall, layer under layer under layer.

"There is no other road," I said. "I've been looking. There's the pass, there's the river gorge, and the gorge is a coffin with scenery."

Gideon looked up. And here is what I will say about Gideon Reyes, and I say it as a man who has ferried smugglers, surveyors, and one memorable fugitive dentist across the Sereno: they are the most stubborn person I have ever carried, and I include the time I carried a mule that refused to disembark for six hours.

"You said no map shows another way," Gideon said. "You said *no map*. Not no way."

"I said that?"

"You said the valley people must have gotten salt in and wool out before there were maps. Before there were roads. Somebody walked something."

I opened my mouth to explain all the excellent reasons this was a stupid line of reasoning, and found I couldn't remember any of them, because the reasoning wasn't stupid. It was just the kind of thinking that only works if you've never been taught what's impossible.

I went to ask the old people.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hidden valleys: the hiding is a full-time job. Every person in that valley over sixty had a story about a path their grandfather used, a route that stopped being spoken of when the soldiers came or the mine men came or whoever came, because a secret path only stays secret if you stop admitting it exists. They hadn't forgotten the paths. They had just agreed, collectively, for generations, to pretend.

It took me most of a day and two cups of chicha I did not want to get old Tomás talking. Tomás was ninety-one and mostly deaf and had been a salt trader's boy, back when salt moved over the mountains instead of around them.

"The salt road," he said, when I finally got the words into his good ear. His face changed. Not nostalgia exactly. Something more careful. "My father walked it. You do not walk it in storm. You do not walk it slow. It goes high — higher than the pass — along the ridge where the wind has eaten the stone thin. There are places where two men cannot walk abreast, and places where you look down and see the clouds *underneath* you." He looked at me then, both milky eyes finding me with unnerving accuracy. "Why does a boat man want a mountain road?"

"Because the map roads belong to bad men."

He nodded slowly, like this confirmed something he'd suspected about maps for ninety years.

"It starts," he said, "at the notch above the black lake. Where the condors nest. But listen—" and he gripped my wrist with a hand like a root, surprisingly strong, "—the road does not forgive. My father lost two brothers on it. One to the wind. One to his own fear, which made him step wrong. If you take frightened people onto that road, the road will keep them."

I thanked him. He waved me off and told me to bring him real coffee next time, and I promised, and we both knew the odds on that promise, and we shook hands anyway.

* * *

I scouted it alone. This was not bravery; it was bookkeeping. If the salt road killed me, the plan lost a pilot who couldn't fly anything anymore anyway, and kept everyone else. Good trade. I've made worse.

The climb to the notch above the black lake took me the better part of a day, and the last part of it I did on my hands and knees, because Tomás had undersold the wind. It doesn't blow *at* you up there. It blows *through* you, like you're the interruption and it's the natural state of the world. I crawled the last two hundred meters with my cheek against stone that was older than any country, and when I came over the notch and saw the road itself, I sat down hard and laughed, once, in a way that probably sounded unhinged to the condors.

Because it was beautiful. That's the obscene part. A shelf cut into the cliff face, wide enough for a laden llama, running along the ridge like a stitch seam, with the whole world falling away on either side. Somewhere down there, centuries ago, men with stone tools had decided the mountain needed a road, and the mountain, apparently, had agreed. The clouds really were underneath us. I could see their shadows moving on the valley floor below like slow fish.

And I could see, clear as a chart, why it had never been on a map. Because a map implies an invitation, and this road was the opposite of an invitation. It was a dare.

I walked the first kilometer of it, testing, counting. Places where the shelf narrowed to less than an arm's span. Places where frost-heave had tilted the stones. One place where the outer edge had simply fallen away into the sky, leaving a gap you'd have to step over with nothing but wind on both sides of the step. I marked each one in my head the way I mark snags in a river channel, because that's what it was — a channel. A channel with a current of wind instead of water, and a bottom you'd never reach to inspect.

It was passable. Barely. In good weather, with steady people, in single file, it was passable.

Which left the weather, and the people.

* * *

The weather settled its own account first.

I came down off the ridge at dusk with the taste of iron in my throat and the wind still ringing in my skull, and the valley had gone strange — that greenish light, that pressure behind the ears. By midnight the first snow was falling on the peaks, and by dawn the pass, our pass, the map pass, the easy pass, wore a white cap that had not been there the day before.

Gideon took the news standing very still, which is how Gideon takes everything that would make a normal person sit down.

"So it's the salt road or nothing," they said.

"It's the salt road or wintering here," I said. "And wintering here means Hale finds us eventually, and the record leaves this valley inside somebody's pocket, and everything Quispe's people bled for walks out over the mountains in a banker's coat."

"And the prisoners?"

"That's the part I keep not saying out loud."

But Gideon was already looking at the map again, or past it, at the compound visible as a smudge of tile roof in the middle distance, and I watched them do the math I'd been avoiding. Because here was the ugly truth of the clock: it wasn't just closing on us. It was closing on Elena Voss and eleven other people sitting in a building with a man who was running out of reasons to keep them alive.

Hale had come for a document. Documents can wait for spring. Witnesses cannot.

* * *

We heard the shot at midmorning.

One shot, flat and small across the distance, and every bird in the valley went up at once, and I knew — the way you know a river's dropped a client before anyone tells you — that something had ended.

By afternoon we knew what. One of the expedition's porters, a boy called Andrés who'd been caught slipping food to the prisoners through the compound fence, had been taken to the compound gate and shot there, in view of the valley, and left lying where he fell. Hale sent a man down under a white rag to deliver the message verbatim, because Hale was the kind of man who liked his threats notarized: *any further contact between the valley and the compound, and the next one dies inside the walls.*

They brought Andrés home on a door. I helped carry him. I had carried enough things across the Sereno that my arms knew the work even when the rest of me wanted to be anywhere else, and I walked with my eyes on the door's grain and thought: this is what the clock costs. Not days. Boys.

Ama Quispe stood over the body for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was level as still water.

"He chose," she said. "Remember that. He was not made to do it. He chose, knowing the price, because a hungry prisoner mattered more to him than his own breathing. That is the ledger we keep here. Not the deaths. The choices."

Then she looked at me, and then at Gideon, and said the thing that turned my stomach and steadied me in the same breath:

"Now you understand your enemy. He counts days too. And a man who is losing his count becomes careless, and a careless man can be beaten — but not by people who wait politely for spring."

* * *

That night we laid it out in the weaving house: Gideon, me, Quispe, and two of her people whose names I'll carry to whatever grave gets me — Marta, who was built like a door and moved like smoke, and young Cusi, who at nineteen had already survived more than I had at twice that.

The salt road. Two days' walk to the notch, one day across the ridge, one day down the far side to the river headwaters, where — and here was my contribution, the one thing I could actually offer — there was water deep enough for a boat. I'd seen it on the way in and dismissed it, because pilots dismiss rivers the way priests dismiss sin: professionally, constantly. But there was a gorge below the snowline, and water in it, and if we could get a boat up there — drag it, portage it, whatever the mountain demanded — we could put twelve people on moving water instead of twelve people on a cliff edge in a storm.

"Twelve people," Marta repeated, "on a road where two men cannot walk abreast. In winter."

"In late autumn," I said, "technically."

Marta looked at me the way you look at a man who has just offered you technicalities as a life raft.

"Frightened people shouldn't go on that road," I said. "Tomás told me. So we don't send frightened people. We send people who've decided. There's a difference, and the road knows it."

Gideon had been quiet through all of this, working the edge of the map with one finger, and now spoke without looking up.

"Hale won't sit still for it. Once we move, he'll know within hours. He'll come after us with everything he has, because he can't afford to let witnesses reach the far side of that range — and he knows the general direction even if he doesn't know the road."

"So we don't all move at once," I said. "We move in pieces. First the scouts — me, Marta, Cusi — we go ahead, fix ropes at the bad gaps, cache supplies on the far side. Then the prisoners, in small groups, spread over a day so no single loss ends it. Then—"

"Then someone has to be last," Gideon said quietly. "Someone has to make sure Hale is looking the wrong way when the last group steps onto the road. Someone he trusts to be standing right there."

The room went quiet, because we all understood what Gideon was describing, and we all understood who fit the description. Gideon Reyes, who had played double agent for two weeks, who Hale half-trusted and fully watched, who was the only one of us who could stand in front of that man and hold his eyes while the others vanished into the storm.

"No," I said.

"It's not a request, Sef."

"It's a terrible idea wearing a brave coat."

"It's the only coat we have." Gideon finally looked up, and there was something in their face I hadn't seen in all the weeks I'd known them — not fear, exactly, and not resolve either. Something more tired than either. "I spent ten years running from everything, Sef. Every town, every dig, every person who might stay. Do you know what I learned? That running is easy. Running is the laziest thing a person can do and call it survival." A breath. "This is the first time in my life I'm running *toward* something. Toward my mother, toward the truth, toward the far side of a mountain. I'm not going to flinch at the last step of it."

I wanted to argue. I want it noted, wherever notes are kept, that I wanted to argue, and that the only reason I didn't was that I'd seen Gideon read ground and read people and read that map like scripture, and I had never once seen them wrong about a thing they'd committed to. Annoying, that. Being right all the time. It should be illegal.

"Fine," I said. "But I'm fixing a rope at every gap on that ridge, and the last one is yours to clip into. You don't cross that road unroped, Gideon Reyes. That's my price. I've buried enough clients."

For the first time in weeks, Gideon smiled. It was a small smile, worn at the edges, but real.

"One rope," they said. "Deal."

* * *

I went out at first light to look at the sky, because that's the last thing a boat man does before committing to water, and it's the first thing I do before committing to anything now.

The storm was coming. Not the gray trickle of yesterday — the full wall of it, stacked up behind the western peaks like freight, blue-black at the base, silent, patient. Three days, maybe four, before it reached the valley floor. Five before it sealed the ridge entirely.

Five days to empty a compound, cross a road that ate Tomás's uncles, run a boat through an unmapped gorge, and put a mountain range permanently behind us.

I stood there a long moment, feeling the wind arrive ahead of the cloud like a bill collector, and I thought about Andrés on his door, and Tomás's father's brothers somewhere under that ridge, and the twelve people behind a compound wall whose time was being measured now not in days but in a madman's patience.

Then I went to find Marta and Cusi, and start packing rope.

The clock had become the enemy. Fine. I'd raced worse.

I just hadn't raced one that could bury the whole course.
