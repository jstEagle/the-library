## 14. A Bargain with Thieves

I've negotiated with looters before. I've haggled with border officials whose palms were drier than the artifacts they wanted bribed for. I once spent four hours in a customs shed in Valparaíso convincing a man that a crate of potsherds was, legally speaking, agricultural equipment. So I thought I knew what it sounded like when power opened its mouth.

I didn't. Power, it turns out, sounds like reasonableness. It sounds like a man pouring you coffee.

Marcus Hale had claimed the mission compound's one intact room as his office — a former sacristy with a cracked saint missing her hands, watching us from her niche like she'd seen this exact conversation play out a hundred times and had stopped taking notes around the second century. He'd set two chairs on either side of a table made from a door laid across crates. Two chairs. Not three. The arithmetic of the seating told you everything about who he considered a person and who he considered a variable.

"Sit," he said, and poured from a battered thermos into tin cups. "It's terrible coffee. The expedition's supplies were better, but my men have been drinking them." He said it the way another man might say *the weather's been poor* — an apology that wasn't one, a confession designed to sound like small talk.

I sat. Outside, the storm that had closed the pass scratched at the shutters like something wanting in. Somewhere below, in the valley's dark, Ama Quispe's people were moving — I could feel it the way you feel weather coming, though I couldn't have said what told me. And somewhere under this compound or near it, Elena was locked behind a door I'd found through a drainage culvert, and the last thing we'd said to each other through three years of silence and six inches of stone was an argument about whether I'd ever been capable of finishing anything.

So. Coffee with a thief. My mother's life on the table. Let me tell you how steady my hands were: steady enough to hold the cup. That's all I'll claim.

"You've been busy," Hale said. "The porter. Maren. The map." He ticked them off like items on an invoice he'd already audited. "I want you to know I'm not angry. Anger is for people who are surprised."

"And you're not surprised."

"I read people for a living, Reyes. Before I read ledgers, before I read contracts. You came upriver because of your mother — everyone pretends otherwise, but they always come for someone. You played along because you're good at it. Better than most." He leaned back. "But here's what you haven't figured out yet, and I'll give it to you free, because I respect the work: you're not good enough at it to beat me. Nobody is. That's not arrogance. That's actuarial."

I drank the terrible coffee. It tasted like it had been brewed twice, which, given the supply situation, it probably had.

"I'm not here to beat you," I said. "I'm here to make a trade."

His eyebrows did something polite. "Go on."

"Elena Voss. The expedition staff. The porters you've got locked in the granary. Safe passage down the salt road, out ahead of the snows, no pursuit. In exchange, my people and I walk away, and you never see any of us again. No reports. No courts. No names in anyone's mouth."

"That's it? You give up nothing?"

"I give up the only thing worth anything," I said. "My silence."

He smiled at that — genuinely, I think, the first real expression I'd seen on him since Puerto Cobre. "Silence. Yes. People always offer me their silence like it's gold. It isn't, Reyes. Silence depreciates. Every year you keep a secret it gets heavier and more expensive to carry, until one day you're drunk in some port town and it falls out of you for the price of a story and a bottle. I don't buy silence. I buy things that hold their value."

"Then name your price."

And here is the part I will be honest about, because if I can't be honest in my own head then this whole account is just another performance: there was a moment — half a breath, maybe less — when I hoped he'd say money. When I hoped he'd say *the site*, or *first claim on the excavation*, something with edges I could work around. Because a deal solves everything. A deal means nobody bleeds on the ridge, nobody freezes above the tree line, Sef walks out of whatever hole they're keeping him in, Elena sleeps tonight without bars between her and the sky. A deal is the shape every exhausted person dreams the world actually comes in.

"The record," Hale said.

The word landed in the room like a dropped tool.

"The treaty," he said, when I didn't answer. "Whatever Voss found in that tomb — the document, the inscription, the rubbing, the translation, however she's carrying it. All of it. That's the price. That, and I never think about any of you again."

"They told you it's treasure," I said. "Gold, effigies—"

"Don't insult both of us." He set down his cup with a small precise click. "I've known what this was since before your mother went upriver. Why do you think the funding took eleven months to arrange? Do you know how hard it is to finance an archaeological expedition honestly? It's nearly impossible. Nobody legitimate wants a dig; they want what a dig finds. And what this dig finds isn't gold. Gold I could sell. What this dig finds is a piece of paper older than every land claim in three hundred miles, and paper like that doesn't get sold, Reyes. Paper like that gets *handled*."

"Handled."

"There are men in the capital — reasonable men, men with ministries and letterhead — who have spent two years building a legal structure on the assumption that this valley belongs to no one worth consulting. Your mother's little treaty makes their structure a house of cards. They don't need the valley. They need the proof of it to stop existing." He spread his hands, a man showing you the simple geometry of a complicated world. "I'm not destroying history. I'm preventing a war over it. There's a difference, and the difference is that I get paid."

Outside, the wind found a loose shutter and worried it. The handless saint watched.

"And if I say no?"

"Then tomorrow morning my men start moving prisoners down to the lower compound, which sits on scree, above a drop, in weather that's about to get much worse. Accidents happen on scree. Especially to older fieldworkers with bad knees." He wasn't threatening. That was the horror of it — he was *scheduling*. "You'd have your justice eventually, I suppose. An inquiry. Two years of depositions. By which time the ministry's structure is finished, the valley is a survey number, and your mother's legacy is a footnote in someone else's report. Or—" he shrugged, "—you give me paper, and everyone walks home."

Everyone walks home. Say it fast and it even sounds true.

"I need time," I said.

"One night." He stood, gathering the thermos, already halfway to being done with me. "Sunrise. And Reyes — a word of advice, freely given, from one professional to another. Whatever cleverness you're planning, weigh it against this: I have offered you the clean exit. Men like me only offer it once. After that, everything gets expensive."

At the door he paused, and for a moment the polish thinned and I saw the thing underneath, the actual animal, cold and patient as ice forming.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your mother refused too. First day. Same face you're making now."

Then he was gone, and I sat alone in the sacristy with two empty cups and the storm, and I made myself name what I'd felt in that half-breath when he named his price. Not fear. Not even anger.

Relief. For one obscene instant, some part of me had been *glad* — glad that the choice was finally simple, glad that someone else had drawn the line so I wouldn't have to draw it myself, glad to be offered a bargain where the cost was somebody else's history instead of my own comfort. That's how it gets in, I understand now. Not through greed. Through tiredness. Corruption doesn't recruit; it *accommodates*. It speaks fluent exhaustion. It knows exactly how much a person can carry and offers, gently, to take the heavy end.

I poured out the rest of the coffee onto the floorboards, which was childish, and satisfying, and changed nothing.

* * *

Sef found me an hour later in the granary loft, where I'd gone to count our assets: one stolen map, one wounded porter who could walk if walking meant suffering, one pilot with river hands and no mountain sense, one elder's granddaughter named Yma who knew the salt road and hated Hale with the clean heat of someone who'd lost family to his last expedition. Four of us against his thirty, minus the two or three of his men who'd been looking at the valley's children lately like men doing arithmetic of their own.

"Well?" Sef said.

"He wants the record. The whole treaty. Sunrise tomorrow."

Sef was quiet a while. Down in the granary, the freed porters slept in shifts, and someone was humming very low, an old song with a rhythm like water.

"So we give him a copy," Sef said at last. "A forgery. Buy the night, walk the prisoners out under the storm, let him find out on the ridge."

"That's the plan I already hate."

"It's a good plan."

"It's a plan where I sit across from him and lie with a straight face and shake his hand, and he *believes* me, and for one night I am exactly the kind of person he thinks everyone is." I heard my own voice go sharp and stopped it. "Sorry. It's not the lying. I've lied to border guards and grant committees and myself. It's that it works. Do you understand? It *works*. He'll take the deal because the deal is plausible, because a man like me selling out a valley for my mother's life is the most believable sentence in the world. And some morning ten years from now I'll still be able to hear how easy it was."

Sef looked at me the way he looked at rapids — reading the water under the water.

"Gideon," he said. "You know what my last captain used to say? The river doesn't care what kind of man you are. It cares what line you run. Run the line clean and the kind of man you are sorts itself out later." He tapped the map between us, the old salt road inked in by Yma's sure hand. "Tonight we run the line. Tomorrow you can be as disgusted with yourself as you like. Disgust keeps. Guilt keeps. Dead mothers don't."

I laughed — a short, ugly, grateful sound — because that's the other thing about bargains with thieves: they make you grateful to the people who help you commit them.

We worked through the dark hours. Yma forged the translation pages from memory and a scrap of the genuine text Elena had passed me through the culvert, her pen moving with a copyist's patience, aging the paper with tea and ash. Sef laid out the march order for the prisoners. I wrote the note that would go to Elena — *salt road, third bell, trust the girl with the lantern* — and folded it small enough to swallow, in case.

Near dawn I went back to the sacristy alone, and set the forged record on the door-table between the two cups, and waited for Marcus Hale to come collect his prize.

He arrived with the light, stamping snow off his boots, and picked up the pages with a scholar's care that would have fooled anyone who hadn't watched him smash a radio with a rock.

"Pleasure doing business," he said, and offered his hand.

And I took it — warm, dry, firm, the handshake of a man closing a sale — and smiled the smile of a person who has just solved everything, and felt the truth of it settle into me like a swallowed stone:

It had been easy. God help me, it had been *easy*.

Behind him, through the shutter cracks, the storm was lifting off the ridge — early, wrong, a clear cold dawn where there should have been whiteout — and somewhere on that ridge, I would learn within the hour, a man named Vasquez was holding Elena's own field notebook up to the new sun, reading my forgery line by line, and beginning, with a scholar's patience far more dangerous than Hale's, to smile.
