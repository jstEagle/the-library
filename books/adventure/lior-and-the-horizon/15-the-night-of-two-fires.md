## 15. The Night of Two Fires

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before you do something unforgivable or magnificent, and I have never learned to tell them apart until afterward. That night in the valley it was the silence of wind holding its breath above the ridge, the storm Ama Quispe had promised gathering itself somewhere beyond the pass like a debt collector who knows exactly where you live.

I sat in the elder's house with a cup of maize beer going cold in my hands and rehearsed the plan for the eleventh time, because that is what I do instead of praying.

"The decoy fire goes first," Sef said. He'd drawn the mission compound on the floor with a stick — the chapel, the barracks Hale's men used, the long dormitory where Elena and her people were kept, the gate on the south wall where the hinges had rusted to lace. "It has to look accidental. A lamp knocked over, nothing more. If they think sabotage, they lock everything down and we've burned our one night."

"And if they think carelessness?"

"Then they'll be too busy saving their own mattresses to count prisoners." He tapped the gate with the stick. "You cut here. I take the rear wall."

"You're not taking anything alone," I said.

He looked at me the way he always did when I tried to mother him — a boat pilot of forty-odd years, a man who'd run the Sereno's worst rapids backwards, being told by a person half his age and twice his foolishness what he could survive. "The porter's still limping," he said. "Your mother can't run. Someone has to be the last one out, Gideon. It's arithmetic."

"It's heroics dressed up as arithmetic."

"Everything worth doing is." He smiled, and for a moment I saw the man who'd warned me about Hale back in Puerto Cobre, back when my biggest problem was lying to myself about why I'd come. "Besides. If it goes wrong, you'll need someone captured who talks slow. They'll never get a word out of me before dawn."

I should have argued harder. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone: I should have argued harder.

* * *

We moved at the hour when even dogs give up pretending to guard things. The valley at night was a different country — the tomb's terraces invisible, the peaks around us just darker shapes against a sky bruising over with cloud. No stars. Ama Quispe had read the sky three days ago and said the snows would come early, and she'd been right about everything else, so I'd stopped doubting her the way I'd stopped doubting the river's current after watching it eat a barge.

Tomas came with me to the gate. The wounded porter, the one Hale's advance team had left to die on the trail, had insisted on coming despite a leg that bent in places legs shouldn't bend. He carried the bolt cutters Sef had liberated from the compound's own tool shed, which struck me as the correct kind of poetry.

"Hinges are worse than we thought," he whispered, working at the rusted metal. "Might be faster to lift the whole thing off."

"Faster is loud."

"Loud might be fine tonight." He nodded toward the mission, where the first thread of smoke was already rising from the east wing — Sef's work, a lamp and oil and a prayer to whatever god looks after arsonists with good hearts. "Fire makes its own noise."

He wasn't wrong. Within minutes the smoke thickened, and then a shutter of orange light bloomed behind the chapel windows, and then the compound woke up all at once — shouting, boots, a whistle blowing three sharp notes that I would later learn meant *fire, east wing, all hands*. Men poured out of the barracks carrying buckets with the desperate energy of people whose pay didn't cover dying in a blaze.

The gate lifted off its lower hinge with a groan Tomas muffled against his shoulder, and we were through.

* * *

Inside, the world was firelight and chaos, and I have done enough salvage digs to know that chaos is just order wearing a bad disguise. The guards ran east toward the flames. The dormitory door — locked, because Marcus Hale did not rise to the rank of monster without learning the basics — yielded to the crowbar in four hits that sounded, in my memory, like cannon fire but were probably no louder than the burning building fifty meters away.

Elena Voss stood in the doorway of the darkened dormitory with her hair loose and her eyes already cataloguing me — height, weight, exhaustion, blood on my knuckles — the way she used to catalogue potsherds.

"You're late," she said.

"Traffic on the salt road."

For one absurd second I thought she might smile. Then she turned and did the thing I couldn't have done: she organized twenty frightened people into a silent column in under a minute, her voice pitched low and certain, hands moving — *you, carry him; you two, front; nobody speaks past this point.* Three years of estrangement, and her competence hadn't aged a day. It was infuriating. It was the most familiar thing I'd ever seen.

We went out through the gate in ones and twos, hugging the compound wall, and the fire roared behind us and covered us like a mother covering a child's cry. The decoy had worked better than planned — too well, maybe, because the east wing was genuinely going up now, and I found myself thinking about the expedition's field notes, Elena's fifteen years of Sereno survey data, all of it presumably ash by morning, and feeling something between grief and relief. Some records burn clean.

We reached the tree line. We reached the first terrace. Behind us the compound was a carnival of orange, and ahead of us the old salt road climbed into blackness, and for perhaps ninety seconds I let myself believe the whole thing had worked.

That's when the whistles started again — different rhythm now, three notes and a fourth, and I understood the new word without anyone translating it: *prisoners.*

* * *

What followed was not a chase so much as a negotiation between our fear and the mountain's steepness. Elena set a pace that shamed people thirty years younger. The freed staff stumbled and were caught and steadied. Tomas, leg screaming, refused every offer of an arm until I simply took his elbow without asking, and after that he leaned on me with the grace of a man deciding pride was a luxury item.

Sef was nowhere behind us.

I told myself the rear wall route was longer. I told myself he'd loop wide, come in from the north terrace, meet us at the salt road's first switchback the way we'd agreed. I told myself a lot of things at a rapid clip, the way you talk to yourself when the alternative is turning around.

At the second switchback, the storm arrived — not gradually, the way storms do in civilized countries, but all at once, a white wall of sleet and wind that erased the ridge behind us and made the path under our feet a rumor. Ama Quispe's people materialized out of it with lanterns hooded to slits, guiding us onto the old road, counting heads with soft, urgent voices in Quechua.

They counted twenty-three.

We had left with twenty-four.

"Elena." My voice came out wrong. "Where's the last of your staff? The botanist — Ríos—"

"He's here." She counted her own people with a touch to each shoulder, quick, professional, and finished. Her face changed. "Gideon. Who's missing?"

"Sef Maren."

She didn't know the name, but she knew my face, and whatever she saw there made her put a hand on my arm — the first time she'd touched me in three years, and it took a catastrophe to arrange it, which if you think about it too long will break your heart, so don't.

"We go back," I said.

"No," said Ama Quispe's nephew, Mateo, who commanded the escort. "Storm's closing the road. You go back, the mountain keeps all of you."

"He stayed behind so we wouldn't have to. That was the arithmetic." I heard my own voice crack on the word and hated it. "I told him it was heroics."

"And he went anyway," Elena said quietly, "because he decided you were worth the trade. Which means the only way to insult him is to die making it worthless."

I have rarely been argued with so effectively by a woman I was actively furious at.

* * *

We learned what happened to Sef two hours later, at the shelter cave where the salt road ducked under the ridge, from a runner Ama Quispe had sent down through the storm — a boy of maybe sixteen with ice in his eyelashes and news delivered flat, the way bad news travels best.

Hale's men had caught him at the rear wall. Not fleeing — standing, they said, between the guards and the escape route, talking slowly, buying seconds with words the way he'd promised he could. He'd bought enough of them. Every prisoner got out. And then they'd taken him back inside the compound, and the fire had been put out, and Marcus Hale now had exactly one piece of leverage that mattered.

The boy finished and looked at me expectantly, as if there were instructions attached.

There weren't. There was just the cave, and the storm hammering the ridge like it wanted in, and Elena sitting down beside me without asking permission, and the terrible arithmetic of victory: everyone saved except the one man who'd made it possible, and a hole torn in the middle of the night exactly the shape of a boat pilot who talked slow.

"He'll bargain," Elena said. "Hale doesn't kill assets."

"No. He trades them." I stared into the hooded lantern. "And I know exactly what he'll ask for."

Because I did. I'd known since the culvert conversation, since the archive in the stone, since the moment I understood that Hale hadn't come for gold at all — he'd come for paper. A treaty, centuries old, carved in stone and copied in bark, that dissolved his employers' claims to this valley like sugar in rain. He wanted it erased. And now he had the one person I couldn't afford to lose, and I had the one thing he couldn't afford to leave existing, and the storm had closed every road except the one that led back up to the ridge where men like Marcus Hale did their best thinking.

"He'll want an exchange," I said.

"Then don't give it to him."

"That's easy for you to say. You didn't promise him anything."

"I didn't promise anyone anything," Elena said, "for fifteen years. Look how well that worked out." She pulled her coat tighter. "Whatever you're planning, Gideon — plan it honestly. It's the only inheritance I've got left to give you."

Outside, the snows came down on the pass like a verdict, early, exactly as promised, sealing us in with a hostage and a liar and a choice.

I started planning.
