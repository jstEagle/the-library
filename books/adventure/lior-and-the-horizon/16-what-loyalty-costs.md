## 16. What Loyalty Costs

The salt road was older than the mission, older than the pass, older — Ama Quispe's people said — than any name anyone still used for it. It ran along the shoulder of the mountain like a scar that had healed into a path, white with crushed stone in places, black with old pack-animal grease in others, and it climbed. God, how it climbed.

I had spent thirty years telling students that archaeology was a conversation with the dead, and I had never once mentioned that most of the conversation was conducted on your knees, at altitude, with a storm chewing at the back of your neck.

My legs had gone past burning into something more philosophical about four hours ago. The freed prisoners moved ahead of us in a strung-out line — two of my grad students, the surveyor Okafor, three porters who had chosen, in the chaos of the gates, to come with us rather than stay with Hale's men. Sef was not among them. Every time I thought of that, of the pilot turning back into the compound's searchlight so the rest of us could reach the treeline, my chest did something structural and unsound.

Gideon walked beside me. Not ahead, not behind. Beside. They had arranged it that way without appearing to arrange anything, which was their gift — logistics as emotional avoidance. Give Gideon Reyes a column of exhausted people and a mountain, and they would manage both beautifully and feel nothing, or appear to.

The storm was coming down off the ridge behind us in a grey wall. Ahead, the road bent around a spur where someone centuries ago had cut windbreaks into the rock, shallow alcoves like the ribs of some buried animal. Gideon called a halt there, checked faces, counted heads, redistributed weight from the weakest packer to the strongest, and only then sat down next to me with the deliberate casualness of a person approaching a landmine they'd been told was theirs.

"You should eat," they said, handing me a strip of dried meat I didn't want.

"So should you."

"I ate."

"Liar," I said. "You've been lying since you were six. You've gotten worse at it, actually. At six you committed."

Something flickered across their face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would have been if either of us had been raised in a house with more of them. They chewed the meat. Below us, the valley we'd escaped was already swallowed by cloud. Somewhere inside that cloud was Hale, and Sef, and everything we hadn't finished.

"Three years," I said.

"What?"

"Three years since you spoke to me. Eleven days of silence before that, and then you show up on a boat full of armed men, and in all that time — " I stopped. Recalibrated. This was not how I wanted to do this. I had rehearsed nothing, which was unlike me; I rehearsed everything, always, which was precisely the problem between us, historically speaking.

"In all that time," I tried again, "you never once asked me why."

Gideon looked out at the cloud. "Why what?"

"Why I took the Sereno contract. Why I went back into the field at fifty-eight with a bad knee and a funding gap. You read the letter. You came. And you never asked the question any reasonable child asks, which is: *Mother, what were you thinking?*"

"I'm not reasonable," Gideon said. "You made sure of that."

* * *

There it was. Three years of silence and eleven days of terror, distilled into one sentence delivered flat as a trowel edge. I felt it land exactly where it was aimed, because Gideon had always been a precise excavator. They didn't dig wide. They dug narrow and deep, straight down to the thing you'd buried deepest, and then they stood over the pit and waited for you to climb down after it yourself.

"All right," I said. "Then let's not be reasonable."

"Elena." They used my name. Not Mother. They'd been doing it since the culvert, since our first words in three years had been an argument about whether I'd been held captive or had simply failed to send word — an argument I had lost, incidentally, because they were right. "We're four hours from a firefight. Maybe less. Maybe we should save it."

"For when?" I asked. "For the descent? For Puerto Cobre? For the airport in the capital, where you'll shake my hand and take the consulting fee and vanish again for another three years?" I heard my own voice go sharp and hated it, because sharpness was my mother tongue too. "No. We're doing it now, on this mountain, because now is the only terrain I've ever been able to hold."

Gideon stared at me. And then, to my astonishment, they laughed — one short, cracked note, no humor in it at all.

"*Terrain,*" they said. "You're going to make our relationship a site report."

"I'm going to make it honest, which is the closest I know how to get."

The wind came up the road and found the alcove and went through us. Down-valley, thunder muttered like a bureaucrat clearing his throat. Gideon pulled their knees up and wrapped their arms around them, and for a moment they looked sixteen again, sitting on the porch of the field house in Oaxaca while I packed for a season I'd promised — promised — would be my last.

"The dig that broke us," Gideon said. "That's what you want to talk about."

"That's where the trench is."

"Fine." They said it the way they said *fine* when it wasn't. "Here's what I remember. I was seventeen. You told me you'd be home for my graduation. Then the stratigraphy got interesting. Then the funding committee extended the season. Then there was a burial chamber, Elena, an actual intact burial chamber, and I watched you choose it. On the phone. I could hear it in your voice — you weren't even sorry. You were *lit up*. You sounded happier talking to me about potsherds than you ever sounded talking to me."

"That's true," I said.

They blinked. I think they had expected a defense. Twenty years of expectation, and I handed them a confirmation instead, and it knocked them sideways harder than any argument would have.

"It is true," I repeated. "I chose the chamber. I would choose it again. That's the part I've never said out loud, because the honest version of me is worse than the version you invented. You needed me to be a monster who abandoned you, because a monster can be left cleanly. What you actually had was a mother who loved her work more than she knew how to love a person, and that doesn't leave clean. It leaves residue. It gets into everything."

Gideon's jaw worked. "Don't do this."

"Do what?"

"Don't hand me absolution wrapped in confession. You don't get to be the tragic figure here. I'm the one who spent ten years running from every job, every town, every person who stayed longer than a season, because the one person who was supposed to stay taught me that staying is a trick. That everyone leaves when something shinier turns up. Do you know what that does to a person? It makes them leave first. It makes leaving the only safe move on the board. I have been playing that move my entire adult life and I am *tired*, Elena, I am so tired—"

Their voice broke. Not dramatically. Just a hairline fracture, quickly plastered over, the way they'd been plastering it over since they were seventeen. I reached out and put my hand on their arm, on the rope-burned sleeve, and they didn't pull away, which after three years counted as an embrace.

"I know," I said. "I know exactly what it does. Because I learned it from my mother, who left me with an aunt for two years while she chased a shipwreck off Crete. I swore I'd never be her. And then a burial chamber opened under my feet and I became her so fast I didn't even hear the door close."

The rain arrived then, thin and needling, and nobody in the alcove complained, because complaining required energy none of us had. One of the porters rigged a tarp without being asked. Gideon watched him do it with that look they got — the appraising look, the one cataloguing whether a person could be trusted — and I realized with a small cold shock that they were cataloguing me too. Still. Even now. Even here.

"There's more," I said. "And you're not going to like it, and I need you to hear all of it before Hale finds us, because if I die on this road with it still in my mouth, I'll haunt you, and I'll be insufferable."

"God forbid," Gideon said hoarsely.

"Hale didn't find my expedition. I found Hale." I made myself keep eye contact, because looking away would have been mercy and I hadn't earned it. "Eight years ago. After the Oaxaca scandal, after the university dropped me, I couldn't get funding anywhere legitimate. Hale's consortium approached me. Clean money, they said. Diversified interests. I asked questions — I want you to believe that, I asked real questions — and I got answers that were smooth enough to swallow. And when the answers started getting rougher, three years ago, when I saw the names attached to the second tranche of funding, names connected to the land concession filings in this province — I kept taking the money anyway."

Gideon had gone very still. "You knew."

"I suspected. Then I knew. Then I told myself knowing wasn't complicity, which is the sentence every coward says to themselves right before they become one." My hands were shaking, and not from the cold. "I told myself I'd document everything and expose it later, once the discovery was secure. That's the oldest lie in my profession, Gideon. *Later.* Later is where ethics goes to be mummified. I fed your rescue party to a man I knew was a predator because I was ashamed, and because part of me hoped you'd never learn what I'd done, and because — " my voice caught — "because I wanted to see you. Even under those conditions. Even wrong. I wanted my child in the same valley as me one more time."

The rain ticked against the tarp. Somewhere below, the storm was eating the switchbacks we'd climbed.

"So," I said. "There it is. The whole stratigraphy. No clean floor. I chose the chamber. I chose the money. I chose the silence. Layer on layer, and every layer has my fingerprints on it, and you can hate me for any of them you like. But I'm offering you the truth, Gideon, because it's the only currency I have left, and I'd rather bankrupt myself honestly than buy you back with another performance."

* * *

Gideon didn't answer for a long time. Long enough that Okafor drifted over, assessed the temperature of the conversation with a glance, and drifted away again with the survival instinct of a man who'd worked for my family before.

When Gideon finally spoke, their voice was low and stripped of all its armor.

"Do you know what I thought, on the boat? When Sef told me Hale's last expedition came back short two people? I thought — good. Good, maybe this is the thing that finally makes it simple. Maybe I rescue you, maybe I don't, but either way I get to stop carrying it. Either way there's an ending." They shook their head slowly. "And then I found you alive, and it wasn't simple at all. It was worse. Because you were worth rescuing. Do you understand how much easier my life would have been if you'd just been a monster?"

"Yes," I said. "It would have been easier for me too."

"And the money. The Hale money. Did it touch the tomb? Did it touch — " they gestured vaguely eastward, toward the valley, toward the archive in the stone that Ama Quispe had shown them — "did it touch *that*?"

"No. The valley funded itself. The valley has been funding itself for five hundred years." I almost smiled. "Hale's money bought boats and bribes and the men with guns. It bought the cage, not the treasure. Which means when we give the record to Ama Quispe's people — and we are giving it to them, Gideon, all of it, publicly, whatever it costs my name — Hale's employers lose their claim, and my reputation burns with his. Both things happen in the same fire. I've made peace with that. Mostly. At three in the morning I make less peace with it."

Gideon was quiet again. Then they said, in a voice I hadn't heard since they were small — the voice they used for findings, for things uncovered carefully:

"The letter you sent. The plea disguised as small talk. You wrote *tell me about the garden you never plant.*"

"You noticed that."

"I've read it forty times. I thought it was sentiment. It wasn't, was it. It was a test. You were asking whether I'd become you."

The truth of it landed between us like a struck flint. "Yes," I admitted. "And you answered it. You came up a mountain instead of planting a garden. So I still don't know."

"Neither do I," Gideon said. And then, quieter, looking down at their own mud-caked hands as though reading marks in them: "But I stopped moving. Up there, in the pass, when Tomás was hurt — I stopped. First time in my life stopping cost less than leaving. I keep waiting for it to feel like a mistake. It hasn't yet."

I looked at my child — at this sharp, guarded, magnificent stranger who shared my jawline and my stubbornness and my talent for self-exile — and I understood that absolution was not mine to give or receive today. Today we had only done the harder thing. We had opened the ground between us and looked at what was actually in it, unlovely and unmummified, and neither of us had run.

"Gideon," I said. "Whatever happens on this ridge tomorrow — and something will happen, Hale won't let us walk — I need you to know that the last three years of silence were the most expensive purchase of my life, and I regret the price, and I would pay it again badly, and I intend to spend whatever years I have left doing it better. Badly, but better. That's the whole offer. Take it or leave it."

Gideon exhaled. Looked at me. And for one unguarded second, the sixteen-year-old and the thirty-year-old occupied the same face at the same time.

"Badly, but better," they repeated. "That's going on your headstone."

"I'd settle for *she documented her own crimes.*"

That got the ghost of a laugh out of them, and then it died, because Gideon had gone rigid, head cocked, listening to something below us in the rain — voices, or wind shaped like voices, coming up the salt road from the direction of the compound.

They rose, hand already on the stolen map, and the commander's face slid over the child's face like a shutter.

"That's not wind," Gideon said quietly. "Get everyone up. He's found us."
