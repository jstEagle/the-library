## 18. The Exchange on the Ridge

The storm had been circling us for two days like a dog deciding whether to bite, and by the morning of the exchange it had made up its mind. Clouds came down the Cordillera in gray tongues, swallowing the spires of the Pass of Teeth one by one, and the wind carried that particular mineral smell I'd learned to dread — snow, coming early, coming whether we were ready or not.

Hale had chosen the ridge himself. Of course he had. A man who buys rescues also buys terrain. The saddle between two wind-carved fins of rock was narrow enough that his six men could watch every approach and wide enough for theater. He wanted witnesses to his reasonableness. That was always Hale's tell — the performance of fairness, polished until it gleamed.

I came up the eastern trail with Elena at my shoulder and Ama Quispe's nephew, Tomás, carrying the record case. Sef's pilot jacket hung off Tomás's frame, a detail Hale would notice or wouldn't. Behind us, somewhere below the cloud line, the salt road wound down toward the valley with eleven freed expedition staff already walking it under guard of Quispe's people. That part had worked. Whatever happened on this ridge, that part had worked.

Elena hadn't spoken to me since dawn. Not from anger — we'd spent our anger already, up on the salt road, trading truths like people trading blood types in an emergency. Her silence now was something else. She kept glancing at my hands.

"You're gripping it too tight," she said finally. "The case. A man watching you will read your knuckles before he reads your face."

"I'm not nervous."

"You are. But you're also proud of what's in there, and pride looks exactly like guilt when you're holding something you shouldn't have." She adjusted her collar against the wind. "Loosen your fingers, Gideon. You're giving him a gift."

I loosened my fingers. Three years of estrangement, and my mother's first piece of maternal advice in all that time was counterintelligence coaching. It should have been funny. Maybe it was. I couldn't laugh and walk at the same time.

* * *

Hale stood at the center of the saddle with his coat buttoned to the throat and his scholar, Dr. Imre Vass, hovering at his elbow like a crow waiting for a sheep to die. Vass had been Hale's authenticator on three continents, according to Sef, and had once declared a forged Maya stela genuine because the forger had been better than the original artist. He was not, in other words, a man to be underestimated. He was also, I noted with some satisfaction, wearing city boots on mountain ground, and he hated every step of it.

Sef knelt between two of Hale's men, wrists bound in front, face bruised along one side but eyes sharp as chipped flint. When he saw me, he didn't smile. He looked past me, counting my escort, counting theirs, doing the arithmetic he always did. Then his gaze flicked down — once, deliberately — to the case in Tomás's hands, and back up. *Don't look at it again,* I willed him. *You've told them everything they need.*

"Gideon Reyes." Hale spread his arms as if greeting family at a wedding. "You brought your mother. Sentimental. I confess I expected you to keep her safely downhill."

"She insisted."

"Of course she did." His eyes went to Elena, and something moved behind them — not warmth, exactly. Recognition. Two people who had spent years pretending the other didn't exist. "Dr. Voss. Your field reports were always exemplary. It's a pity about the conclusions you drew from them."

"The pity," Elena said evenly, "is that you funded me for six years hoping I'd never reach any."

"And you did. Which brings us here." He gestured, and one of his men stepped forward to take the case. I held it a moment longer than necessary — theater for theater, since the case contained forty pages of my own careful forgery, aged with tea and ash and copied from fragments Elena had memorized before the compound fell — then let it go.

"Count it," I said. "You'll want to."

"Oh, I'll do more than count it." Hale crouched over the case right there in the wind, lifting out the wrapped bundle with the reverence of a priest handling relics. Vass leaned in beside him, gloved fingers already working at the bindings.

I watched them the way I'd learned to watch a dig come apart — not with hope, but with attention. Every exchange is a stratigraphy. You lay your layers carefully and pray no one digs below the surface fill.

Vass dug below the surface fill.

It took him four minutes. I know because I counted, matching my breathing to the seconds, while the wind rose around us and the first flakes came down — not snow yet, just the sky shedding its nerve. Four minutes of Vass turning pages, frowning, tilting the record against the flat gray light. Then he said something low to Hale, and Hale's face did the thing I'd been dreading: nothing at all.

"Gideon." Hale stood. He sounded almost gentle. "Who wrote this?"

"A scribe of the valley people. Sixteenth century. You'll want to verify the—"

"It's good work." Vass held up a page, and even from ten feet I could see the flaw he'd found — the ink sitting wrong, or the fiber, some detail only a man who'd spent his life touching lies would catch. "The pigment is correct. The aging is competent. But this passage—" he tapped it, "—quotes a treaty clause that appears nowhere in the fragmentary sources. Because whoever forged it invented it. Clever invention. Plausible invention. But invention."

The ridge went very quiet, the way places go quiet before avalanches.

"You see my problem," Hale continued, conversationally, tucking the forged record back into its wrappings like a disappointed customer returning merchandise. "If this were genuine, I would honor our bargain to the letter. I'm a reasonable man, Gideon. Ask anyone I've ruined. But a forgery tells me something worse than refusal. It tells me the real record still exists, hidden somewhere, and that you thought I was stupid enough to trade a living hostage for paper mache."

"Sef isn't paper mache," I said. "He's standing right there."

"That's the other problem." Hale sighed, and nodded to his men. "He's seen the valley. He's seen the faces. He's flown half your escape route. I can't return him to the world, Gideon. I'm sorry. Truly. I had hoped to avoid this."

Two things happened then, and I've replayed the order of them a thousand times since, because the order mattered.

First, Elena moved. Not away — *toward*, stepping in front of me with her arms slightly out, the way she used to stand between me and my father's temper in another life, another country, and I understood in one sick flash that she had known this might happen and had come anyway, had chosen to be present for it rather than safe without it.

Second, the mountain answered.

The snowline had been descending all morning, patient as water finding a level, and it chose that moment to arrive — not as flakes anymore but as a wall, a white curtain rolling over the western fin of rock so fast the men nearest it shouted. Visibility dropped from a hundred yards to twenty in the space of a breath. Somewhere above us, the deep groan of wind through the Pass of Teeth changed pitch, the sound of the high country closing its accounts for the season.

And in that white confusion, Sef moved.

I never saw how he got his bonds off — later he claimed he'd been working the knots since dawn with a splinter from the firewood, which was either true or exactly the kind of lie Sef enjoyed letting people believe. What I saw was his shape rising out of the whiteness like something surfacing from deep water, one bound wrist swinging into the jaw of the man nearest him, and then he was running — not downhill, where Hale's men were, but *up*, toward the pass, because Sef Maren understood something the rest of us took years to learn: sometimes the way out is the way nobody sane would go.

"After him!" Hale's voice cracked across the saddle. "After—"

A rifle discharged, wild, the report swallowed instantly by the storm. Someone screamed — not hit, I think, just terrified by the noise bouncing off the stone fins. And then the ridge dissolved entirely, six armed men and three of us and one fleeing pilot all reduced to shapes and shouts in a roaring white blindness.

I grabbed Elena's sleeve. She grabbed mine. We ran blind, both of us, following the sound of Sef crashing through the drifts ahead, and behind us I heard Hale shouting coordinates, landmarks, the names of features on a map he'd studied and I hadn't — because that was the terrible thing about Marcus Hale, the thing I'd underestimated from Puerto Cobre onward. He wasn't a fool. He was a prepared man having a bad day.

"The case!" Elena shouted against the wind. "Gideon, the forgery — he still has it!"

"I know!"

"Do you understand what that means? Vass authenticated the *style*. If Hale shows that forgery to anyone — a court, a ministry, my own colleagues — and claims it's the treaty, and the real record surfaces later and contradicts it—"

"Then the real record becomes deniable." My stomach turned over as the arithmetic completed itself. Our forgery, meant to buy a night, had instead handed Hale a weapon: a fake so good that its exposure could poison the genuine article. "We didn't compromise the hiding place. We compromised the *record*."

Ahead, Sef's shape resolved out of the white, waving us toward a cleft in the rock — the old salt road's upper switchback, barely a goat track, invisible until you were inside it. We squeezed through single file, the storm pressing in behind us like a tide filling a footprint.

* * *

We gained maybe four hundred meters of elevation before the wind forced us into the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, where we huddled with our backs to the stone and let the mountain shout at us. Sef's hands were bleeding where the cords had torn the skin. Elena wrapped them with strips of her shirt, efficient and wordless, and he let her, which told me more about his condition than any complaint would have.

"They'll follow," Sef said. "Not tonight. Tonight even Hale's men aren't stupid enough. But tomorrow, if the storm breaks — they'll pick up our trail at the cleft."

"Then we don't stop tomorrow either."

"Gideon." He flexed his fingers under Elena's bandaging, testing them. "Nobody's following anybody through that pass in this. Not them, not us. The question isn't whether they chase. The question is where the real record is, and whether their map knowledge beats ours to it."

It didn't. That was the one card left in my hand, and I played it aloud because there was no one left to bluff. "Ama Quispe has it. In the valley. Buried somewhere even I don't know."

"Then Hale can't get it either," Elena said. "Not through the pass. Not now."

"No," I agreed. "But he doesn't need the pass. He needs a court. And he's holding forty pages of very convincing fiction that says the treaty says whatever he wants it to say." I leaned my head back against the stone and laughed, briefly and without any humor in it at all, because the alternative was screaming. "Do you know what I keep thinking? I keep thinking about how easy it was. Forging it. Sitting in the mission by lamplight, copying hands, inventing clauses. It took me two days. And it nearly worked. And if it *had* worked, I'd have handed a liar the perfect instrument and called it justice."

"You did what the night required," Elena said.

"The night required courage. I gave it cleverness. There's a difference, and the mountain just billed me for it."

Sef snorted — pain or laughter, probably both. "Welcome to flying. Every plan survives until weather."

Below us, faint through the roar, I heard it: voices. More than two. Moving — not toward the cleft, but along the ridge line, parallel, the way you'd move if you knew these stones the way Hale's trackers were starting to know them. And threaded through the wind, almost lost, a sound that stopped my heart colder than the storm ever could: the crack of ice shifting in the pass above, the deep structural groan of the Teeth themselves under their first new load of snow.

The snows hadn't just arrived early. They'd arrived *wrong* — heavy, wet, unstable, the kind of season that buries passes and keeps them buried until spring.

We had maybe a day, perhaps two, to get down the salt road with eleven fugitives, a wounded porter waiting below, and a valley full of people whose secret was now worth more dead than alive to the man hunting us. And somewhere in that white chaos behind us, Marcus Hale was regrouping with a forged treaty in his case and a scholar at his elbow, preparing to do the thing prepared men do.

Turn disaster into procedure.

Elena touched my arm. "Which way?"

I looked up at the pass groaning under its new weight, then down into the white nothing where the salt road ought to be, and understood that cleverness had officially left the building. What remained was the oldest instrument there is: put one foot in front of the other, protect the people behind you, and refuse — absolutely refuse — to stop moving.

No. Not that last part. I'd done enough of that for one lifetime.

"One foot," I said, and stepped into the storm.
