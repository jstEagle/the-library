## 21. Descent

The dead travel lighter than the wounded. That's the first thing the mountain teaches you on the way down.

We wrapped Tomás Quispe — no relation to Ama that anyone could confirm, though everyone insisted otherwise by the second day of mourning — in his own blanket and lashed him to a litter frame Sef built from a broken paddle and two hours of profanity. He rode at the front of the column where the path was widest, because the living agreed without discussion that the dead should not have to wait for narrow places. The wounded came after: the porter Mateo with his splinted leg, two of Hale's men who had chosen to stay with us rather than freeze on the ridge, and Elena, whose ribs had been cracked in the compound weeks ago and who had said nothing about it until the storm passed and there was no one left to be brave for.

I walked at the back. It's where I've always walked. But this time it wasn't so I could see the exits.

* * *

The Sereno in its lower reaches is a brown, patient animal. Going up, six weeks ago, it had fought us every kilometer — rapids like arguments, checkpoints rotting into the current, Hale's men watching me with the flat attention of men deciding whether you're furniture or a problem. Going down, the river seemed almost embarrassed by all that. It carried us in long slow bends past banks of red clay and bamboo, and the boat pilots Sef hired at the confluence were cousins of cousins who asked no questions beyond "how many" and "how much," which are the only questions worth asking on the Sereno.

Sef stood at the tiller of the lead boat with the particular stillness he gets when he's thinking about something he doesn't want to say out loud. He'd been quiet since the ridge. Quiet in the way of a man recalculating what people are worth.

"You could sleep," I told him, settling onto the cargo crate beside him. "The river's doing the work."

"The river's never done the work. The river just lets you think you did." He adjusted the tiller a degree. "Your mother snores."

"She does not."

"Like a landslide. Small one. But geological." He glanced at me. "She's in the stern of my boat because she says your steering makes her nervous. That's the first honest thing she's said in three years, according to her."

I looked aft. Elena was sitting against the gunwale with her arms around her knees, watching the water go by with an expression I recognized from childhood dig sites — the look of a person reading a landscape the way other people read letters from home. She caught me looking. Neither of us waved. We'd used up our ration of waving somewhere around the salt road, when she'd held my hand hard enough to leave marks and neither of us had mentioned it afterward.

Three years of silence, then three weeks of shared catastrophe, and we still hadn't figured out how to have a conversation that wasn't either an argument or an autopsy. But we were in the same boat. Literally. It was a start.

* * *

We reached the burned camp on the fourth day.

I'd known we would pass it. I'd been dreading it and wanting it in equal measure, the way you want to reread the last page of a book that hurt you. The camp sat on the bluff above the river exactly as we'd found it going up: blackened tent frames, the looted supply pit, the smashed radio scattered across the ground like the carcass of something that had once sung.

But now I read it differently. Six weeks ago I'd knelt here in the ash and seen a staged destruction — deliberate, days old, someone's careful fiction of a dead expedition. Now I saw the rest of it. The scuff patterns where men had dragged crates toward the water. The boot prints overprinting each other in the clay, too many boots, boots that didn't match expedition issue. And near the fire pit, half-buried where the rain had worked it loose, a scrap of canvas with a stenciled number I now knew belonged to Hale's advance team.

They'd burned their own staging camp behind them. Tidied up. Left the world a story instead of a fact.

Elena crouched beside me at the fire pit without being asked. Her breath went shallow — the ribs — but she stayed down, turning the canvas scrap over in her fingers.

"He stood right here," she said. "Hale. When they brought me through. He looked at this camp like a man looking at a finished invoice."

"You remember that?"

"I remember everything about those weeks. You don't forget the inventory of your own captivity." She tucked the scrap into her jacket. "Evidence?"

"Evidence." I hesitated. "Though I'm not sure anymore what it's evidence FOR. The valley has the treaty record. Hale's going downriver to face whatever justice the porters' families decide to build. Do the courts need this too?"

Elena looked at me for a long moment, and I braced for the old argument — the one we'd been having since I was nineteen and she chose a trench in Turkmenistan over my graduation, the one about what things cost and who pays. Instead she said:

"Ask the valley. Not me. Not the courts. Ask them what they want done with the story of what happened here." She brushed ash from her knees and stood carefully. "That's the whole lesson, Gideon. That's the thing I got wrong for thirty years. It was never mine to give away."

My mother, admitting error. I checked the sky for the second sun. It declined to appear.

* * *

We buried Tomás at the confluence, on the low bluff where the two rivers married, because his family's village was another week down and Sef said the dead shouldn't ride the rapids. Ama Quispe's people had sent two runners with us as far as the foothills, and one of them — a wiry, unimpressed young man named Cusi who had appointed himself Mateo's nurse and my conscience — spoke the words over the grave in the valley tongue, then translated them into Spanish for the rest of us.

"They say: he walked up carrying nothing and comes down carrying everything. They say the mountain kept him too long and the river will keep him gently."

Nobody spoke after that for a while. The river filled the silence, brown and patient, and I thought about how many people the Sereno had carried this season — Hale's mercenaries going up with guns and lies, Elena's expedition going up with hope and instruments, all of us coming back down changed into different cargo.

Elena stood next to me at the graveside. She didn't take my hand this time. She just stood close enough that our shoulders touched, which, in the currency of my family, was worth more.

"I used to bring you to sites," she said quietly. "You were eight. Nine. You'd sit on the spoil heap and sort potsherds by color and you'd stay for hours. Everyone said, Elena, your child has the patience of an archaeologist. And I thought — good. Good, I'll teach them everything." Her jaw tightened. "And then I taught you the wrong lesson first. I taught you that the work matters more than the people standing in it."

"You taught me the work," I said. "Somebody else taught me the leaving. I had plenty of instructors."

"That's generous."

"It's accurate. Generous would be saying it wasn't mostly you."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, like she'd surprised herself — and then pressed her hand to her ribs and laughed again anyway, wet-eyed, and I put my arm around her very carefully, the way you handle something you've excavated, knowing how many centuries it took to make and how little it takes to break.

* * *

It was on the fifth night, moored below the last set of rapids, that Elena laid the map on the crate between us and started talking about the future as if it were a place we might actually go.

The map was hers — hand-drawn, annotated in three colors of ink, the valley rendered in loving detail along with the tomb's location, the mission compound, the terraces above the river. Someone else's map would have marked the site as X MARKS TREASURE. Elena's map marked it the way she marked everything: with questions. *Unexcavated? Structural integrity? Drainage? Who lives here NOW?*

"With their consent," she said, tapping the valley. "Not a concession, not a permit bought from some ministry that's never seen the place. Consent. They read every document before we publish. They approve every loan list. If they say a chamber stays closed, it stays closed forever, and that goes in the agreement in writing, in their language and ours."

"And the treaty record?"

"Stays theirs. We publish its existence, its significance, maybe selected images if Ama Quispe agrees — enough to kill the land grab permanently. The original never leaves the valley." She looked up at me. "Which means the site can never be a state project. Which means funding will be a nightmare for the rest of my life."

"You say that like it's a selling point."

"It IS a selling point. Any dig worth doing should be slightly impossible. Filters out the people who'd ruin it." She rolled the map closed and then, uncharacteristically, didn't move it away. Her fingers rested on it. "I'd want you on it. As co-director. Not because you're my child. Because on the ridge, in the whiteout, when cleverness ran out — you were the one who kept thinking clearly. I've watched a lot of archaeologists under pressure. Most of them get faster and stupider. You got slower and smarter."

I looked at the river. A kingfisher — or something like one; I'd stopped cataloguing species somewhere around the Pass of Teeth — went skimming low over the water.

"I don't do permanent," I said. It came out automatically, the way you recite your own alibi.

"No," Elena agreed. "You don't. Yet."

"That's not fair. You can't just — 'yet' me. Like it's a symptom."

"Isn't it?" She said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Gideon. You spent six weeks running TOWARD something for the first time in your adult life, and you haven't stopped talking about the valley since we crossed the tree line. You learned four hundred words of the language from Cusi on the boat. I counted."

"They were useful words."

"What's the word for garden?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Cusi had taught me it on the third day, unprompted, grinning like he'd planted it there himself.

"...There's a word for garden," I admitted.

"There's a word for garden," Elena repeated, "and you know it, and you're pretending that means nothing." She finally rolled up the map and handed it to me. "Take it. Think about it. You don't have to answer on a riverboat. God knows I've made every major decision of my life in the field and regretted most of them. Take the map. Take a month. Take a year if you need it."

I took the map. It was heavier than paper should be.

* * *

Puerto Cobre appeared on the ninth day the way bad memories do — gradually, then all at once. The same rusted cranes, the same fish-scale stink, the same cantina where Marcus Hale had bought me a drink and lied to me with the smooth confidence of a man who'd never once been interrupted mid-sentence by consequences.

He wasn't there, of course. He was a week behind us, walking down under guard with the men who hadn't chosen the valley, headed for whatever reckoning the porters' families and the river towns would construct out of grief and arithmetic. Sef had gone ahead by fast boat to carry word — "so nobody shoots anybody on sight," he'd said, "and so somebody who isn't me tells the widow in Santa Rosa what happened to her husband" — and the whole port already knew we were coming. Word travels faster than boats on the Sereno. It always has.

What struck me, stepping off onto the mud-slick dock, was that I expected to feel the itch. The old pull. Puerto Cobre was exactly the kind of town I'd always left within the hour — a job offered, a bag packed before dawn, the horizon doing its usual trick of looking like an exit.

Instead I stood on the dock holding a rolled map and felt... nothing pulling. Just the ordinary weight of my own feet on the planks.

Cusi materialized beside me, because Cusi always did, carrying Mateo's crutch and an expression of profound skepticism that I'd come to think of as his resting face.

"You are staring at the town like it owes you money," he observed.

"Just checking the exits."

"How many?"

"Four. Same as always."

"And?"

"And I don't want any of them." I heard myself say it and waited for the panic, the old claustrophobia of staying. It didn't come. It just... didn't. "Huh."

Cusi nodded sagely, as if I'd passed a test I hadn't known I was taking. "Ama Quispe says the ones who run are running from a door that's already open. She says it in fewer words. She thinks I talk too much."

"Ama Quispe is wise."

"Ama Quispe is old and bored and enjoys messes. It's why she likes you." He handed me the crutch and jerked his chin toward the shore, where Elena was helping Mateo down the gangplank, arguing cheerfully with him about the structural properties of splints. "Your mother is coming to the valley in the spring. She told the elder runners this morning. They laughed at her for asking permission. She didn't understand why that was funny."

"I explained seasons to her once. Didn't take."

Down the dock, a boy was running toward us through the fish stalls, waving a telegram flimsy overhead, shouting my name in a voice that cracked on the second syllable. Behind him, at the mouth of the port road, stood a woman in traveling clothes with dust on her boots and a lawyer's leather case in one hand — someone from the capital, someone with documents, arriving ahead of schedule the way trouble and hope both do.

Elena saw them at the same moment I did. Our eyes met across the deck of the beached boat, and for the first time in three years, we moved toward each other instead of away.
