## 1. The Weight of Water

The Verrow ran brown that morning, the color of old tea left too long in the pot, and Isandro Vane stood at the wheel of the Kestrel watching it slide past in long, sullen ribbons. Dawn had come up gray behind the eastern bluffs, a light without conviction, the kind of morning that promised nothing and delivered exactly that. He had been on the river since before first light, easing the boat down through the narrows above Millbrook Bend, and now the channel widened ahead of him, spreading out lazy and slow toward the lower country where the water forgot it had ever been young.

Forty years the Kestrel had been working this river. Forty years of cargo runs and fuel stops, of mud banks and mooring lines, of men who paid their debts and men who didn't. She was a stern-wheeler gone to seed, her paddle wheel still turning with a sound like a slow heartbeat, *thrum, thrum, thrum*, but her paint had faded from proud red to something like dried blood, and her deck planks creaked in a language only Isandro understood. He knew every complaint she made. He knew which rivet sang when the current pushed hard against her port side, and which stair groaned when the humidity climbed, and how her boiler coughed twice before settling into its work rhythm, like an old man clearing his throat before telling a story he'd told a hundred times.

He was forty-one years old and he felt every one of those years in his hands this morning. The wheel was slick under his palms despite the cold, and he realized he'd been gripping it too tight again, the way he did when he was thinking about things he couldn't fix.

The debt. Always the debt.

His father's debt, though his father had been eleven years in the ground and past caring what anyone collected. Four hundred crowns, originally, borrowed against the Kestrel's hull to keep her running through the famine winter when cargo was scarce and fuel was dear. Old Vane had meant to pay it back. Old Vane had always meant to pay everything back—meant to fix the wheel housing, meant to re-caulk the hull, meant to take Isandro fishing up at Lake Verrow like he'd promised when Isandro was nine. His father had been a man made entirely of intentions, and when he died of the lung fever they'd buried him with all of them unpaid.

The Provision Company had bought the note within the month. They always did. Debts were a crop to the Company, and they harvested patiently, and they never let a field lie fallow.

Isandro had paid down two hundred and sixty crowns over eleven years. Small payments, careful payments, always in person, always at the Company office in Corrow's Landing, always counting out the coins himself so there could be no dispute, no arithmetic error that might grow interest like mold. He kept the receipts in an oilcloth packet beneath the floorboard by his bunk, and some nights, when the river was quiet and sleep wouldn't come, he took them out and read them like scripture. *Received of I. Vane, twenty crowns. Received of I. Vane, fifteen crowns.* Each one a small stone laid against a wall that never seemed to get shorter.

One hundred and forty crowns remained. It might as well have been fourteen hundred.

"Captain."

The voice came from behind him—Tobin Marsh, his engineer, or as close to an engineer as the Kestrel could afford these days. Tobin was sixty if he was a day, a barrel-chested man with forearms like dock rope and a bald head that caught whatever light the sky offered. He came up the stairs from the engine deck wiping his hands on a rag that had started life white sometime in the previous decade.

"Boiler's holding," Tobin said. "But she's grumbling on the starboard line. I patched it at Greyford, but that patch is living on borrowed time."

"Everything on this boat is living on borrowed time," Isandro said. "Including us."

Tobin grunted, which for Tobin was agreement, disagreement, and philosophical reflection all at once. He leaned on the rail beside the wheelhouse and spat into the water, an act of such practiced precision that the spittle landed almost exactly where his last one had.

"You hear anything from the Landing?" Isandro asked.

"I hear things." Tobin's voice went carefully neutral, which was how Isandro knew the news was bad. "Man came through on the northbound packet yesterday asking after you. Asking after the Kestrel."

Isandro's hands tightened on the wheel. "Company man?"

"Collector. Name of Dreele, they said. Working the lower river this season." Tobin paused. "They say he's thorough."

*Thorough.* In the Company's vocabulary, that word had teeth. Isandro had known collectors before—the fat, comfortable ones who took their payments and stamped their books and moved on, and the other kind, the ones who understood that a debt was not just money but leverage, and leverage was not just money but power, and power was the only thing the Provision Company had ever truly sold. The thorough ones didn't want your payments. They wanted your dependence.

"He'll be at Corrow's Landing," Isandro said. It wasn't a question. Collectors worked the landings; it was where the money flowed, where boats stopped because they had no choice. A riverman could avoid a lot of things, but he couldn't avoid the landings, not if he wanted cargo, fuel, news, or a night's sleep tied to something solid.

"Probably," Tobin said. "You could run wide of it. Take the east channel past the shoals, put in at Teller's Ford instead. Cargo's thinner there, but—"

"And give them reason to say I'm dodging?" Isandro shook his head. "No. You don't outrun a debt, Tobin. You just teach it to follow you faster."

Tobin looked at him for a long moment with those river-colored eyes of his, eyes that had watched forty years of water go by and remembered most of it. "Your father used to say something like that."

"My father said a lot of things." Isandro turned the wheel a few degrees, feeling the Kestrel answer with the sluggish obedience of a large animal being asked politely to move. "Mostly he said them instead of doing them."

The current took them then, the river bending south around a long gravel bar where herons stood in the shallows like gray sentries who had forgotten what they were guarding. Mist clung to the water in patches, burning off slowly, and through it Isandro could see the far bank: cottonwoods dropping their leaves one by one into the current, the water carrying them away with the same indifferent patience it carried everything—driftwood, silt, drowned branches, once, years ago, a man he'd tried and failed to save. The river took it all and gave nothing back and asked nothing either. There were days he envied it.

There were days he wished he could be as invisible as the water. That was the whole art of his life, really, the one skill he had perfected while everything else went to seed: staying small. Staying unremarkable. Paying what was owed, taking what cargo paid, keeping the Kestrel moving and his name off any list longer than a manifest. He had learned it young, watching his father be *known*—known at every landing, greeted by name, toasted in every tavern, beloved and broke and finally dead at fifty-two with nothing to show for a life except a boat full of debts and a son who had drawn the opposite lesson with both hands.

Be nobody. Keep the wheel steady. Stay invisible.

It was a good philosophy. It had kept him alive. It had also, he sometimes admitted in the hour before dawn, kept him from very much else. No wife. No children. No friends closer than Tobin, and Tobin was paid in wages and whiskey and the particular loyalty of a man who had nowhere better to be. The Kestrel's cabin held one extra bunk that had never once been slept in by anyone Isandro had chosen to have aboard. He told himself he preferred it that way. Most days he believed it.

* * *

Corrow's Landing rose out of the mist by midmorning, a sprawl of warehouses and rope walks and tin roofs going orange with rust, the Company's flag hanging limp above the customs house like something that had given up trying. The landing itself was a long timber apron stained black by decades of tar and river water, and it was crowded with the usual traffic—deckhands rolling barrels, a fishmonger arguing with a buyer, two boys fishing off the pilings with the patient hopelessness of boys everywhere.

Isandro brought the Kestrel in slow, backing her thrust against the current until the hull kissed the fenders with a groan he felt through the soles of his boots. Lines went out. Tobin killed the engine, and the sudden silence rang in Isandro's ears the way silence always did after a day of the wheel's heartbeat.

He found the collector within ten minutes, because he was fairly sure the collector had found him first.

Dreele was waiting at the foot of the gangplank, standing precisely where Isandro would have to pass, and Isandro understood immediately that this was not accident but architecture. The man was younger than Isandro expected—thirty perhaps, maybe thirty-five—with a narrow face and pale, unhurried eyes and clothes that were cut well enough to cost money but worn carelessly enough to suggest he didn't need to impress anyone here. He held a leather ledger under one arm the way another man might hold a Bible, or a weapon.

"Captain Vane." His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. "Corvus—" he stopped himself, smiled faintly, corrected, "Dreele. Provision Company, collections division. I believe you've been expecting me."

"No," Isandro said. "I've been avoiding you. It's different."

Something flickered behind the pale eyes—not offense, but interest, the way a man looks twice at a horse that bites. "Honesty. Refreshing. May we speak?"

"We can speak right here."

A small smile. "Here is fine."

Isandro came down the gangplank and stopped three steps up from the boards, so that they were nearly eye level, a small geometry of advantage he doubted the collector missed. Up close, Dreele smelled faintly of ink and something herbal, like a man who chewed fennel to settle his stomach, and his ledger was bound in green leather with the Company's seal stamped in gold—a set of scales over a sheaf of grain, the emblem of an outfit that had started as grain brokers and ended up owning half the river.

"Your account," Dreele said, opening the ledger with a soft crack of new binding. "One hundred and forty crowns outstanding, principal. Interest accrued since the last payment, thirty-one crowns, four bits. Total obligation, one hundred seventy-one crowns, four bits." He looked up. "That's assuming you intend to pay today."

"I intend to pay today."

"Then we're already having a better conversation than most." Dreele produced a pen from inside his coat, licked the nib with the delicacy of a man tasting wine. "How much?"

Isandro had counted it out twice before docking, coins sorted into cloth rolls in the strongbox under his bunk. "Fifty crowns."

"Fifty." Dreele wrote it down without comment, then sat back on his heels, considering. "Captain, do you know what happens to accounts like yours? Accounts that pay steadily, faithfully, year after year, and never quite finish?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Nothing. Nothing happens to them. They persist. They become fixtures. And fixtures, Captain, are valuable—to us, certainly, but also, I'd argue, to you. An open account means the Company has a continuing interest in your wellbeing. Your boat stays insured against our displeasure. Your landings stay open. Your name stays clean." He closed the ledger gently, like a man tucking in a child. "A man who pays off his debt becomes merely a customer. A man who carries one remains... family."

"I'd rather be a customer."

"I'm sure you would." Dreele tucked the ledger away. "But here's my difficulty, Captain. My superiors have flagged certain vessels on the upper river for expanded service. Expanded service means expanded attention. And expanded attention means that next season, someone senior to me will be looking at your account, your routes, your cargo manifests, and your father's history with the Company—which, between us, includes certain irregularities I have so far chosen not to itemize." He let that sit in the air between them like smoke. "I mention it only because I'd hate to see a man of your... discipline... caught flat-footed by a less courteous auditor."

"That sounds like a threat dressed in polite clothes."

"It's a forecast," Dreele said mildly. "Weather, Captain. I deal in weather. Whether you carry an umbrella is your affair." He stepped back from the gangplank and inclined his head, courteous as a dancing master. "Fifty crowns received. Receipt at the customs house by noon. Do give my regards to your engineer—he and I share an acquaintance in Greyford, I'm told."

He walked away down the boards, unhurried, nodding to the fishmonger, pausing to admire the boys' fishing lines, a man entirely at home in a world he intended to own a little more of each year.

Isandro stood on the gangplank a long moment, listening to the Kestrel creak behind him, feeling the number in his chest where numbers lived—one hundred and twenty-one crowns, four bits, plus whatever *irregularities* his father had salted into the past, plus whatever *expanded attention* meant when it arrived wearing someone senior's face.

* * *

He paid the customs house at noon, as instructed, and got his receipt, and stood a moment afterward in the shadow of the warehouse awning watching the river. The mist had burned off completely now, and the Verrow lay bright and hard in the midday sun, glittering like hammered tin, beautiful the way knives are beautiful. Barges moved on it. Packet boats. A Company gunboat, gray and low-slung, sliding upstream against the current with the arrogant ease of something that owned the water it displaced.

Expanded attention. He could see it coming the way you could see weather coming down a valley—still distant, still harmless-looking, but moving, and moving toward him.

"Tobin," he said that evening, as they cast off into the gold-and-copper light of late afternoon, the Kestrel's wheel turning its slow heartbeat against the current, "we're going to need more cargo. Better-paying cargo. Whatever's honest."

"And if honest doesn't pay enough?"

Isandro watched the bank slide past—the cottonwoods, the herons, the gravel bar already falling behind—and said nothing for a long moment.

"Then we'll worry about that when it asks," he said finally.

They ran downriver through the evening, the Kestrel's lamp throwing a small yellow circle onto dark water, invisible, unremarkable, exactly as he'd taught himself to be. But that night, anchored in the willows below the Landing, Isandro lay awake in his bunk listening to the river move in the dark, and he thought about how invisibility was a kind of debt too—paid out in loneliness, in the empty bunk, in the years that went by unlived—and how the interest on that debt was starting to come due.

Two days later, at a muddy trading post called Sallow's Rest, he would find a dying man collapsed on his dock.

But that evening he didn't know it yet. That evening there was only the river, and the wheel's slow heartbeat, and the weight of the water going by.
