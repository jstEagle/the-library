## 2. A Stranger at the Landing

The rain had been falling on Saltmarsh Landing for three days without conviction—too steady to be called a squall, too thin to wash anything clean. It came down in a gray veil that turned the river the color of pewter and made the trading post's boardwalk shine like an old man's scalp. Isandro Vane stood at the rail of the *Kestrel* and watched it fall, a mug of chicory going cold in his hand, and told himself he would be gone before noon.

He was always telling himself that.

Saltmarsh was the kind of place the Verrow River forgot on purpose—a smear of mud and commerce where the current slowed and everything the water carried eventually snagged: driftwood, silt, drowned men's hats, and men who had given up swimming. The trading post itself was a long shed of gray timber with a tin roof that sang when it rained, and beside it a dock of blackened pilings that flexed underfoot like something alive. Three barges were tied up ahead of him, loaded with resin barrels and misery. A dog slept in a puddle because the puddle was drier than the ground.

Isandro drank his chicory anyway. It was the small rituals that kept a man upright—the mug at dawn, the logbook entries in his own careful hand, the lie to himself about leaving by noon. Twenty-two years on the river had taught him that survival was mostly a matter of small rituals performed faithfully while larger disasters arranged themselves elsewhere.

"Captain Vane." The voice came from the dock, wet and apologetic. Odo Merrick, the post's factor, stood below with his collar up and his ledger held over his head like a shield. "Got your manifest. Resin, salt pork, nails. And—" he paused, the way he always paused when there was money in the sentence, "—a gentleman asking after northbound passage."

"No passengers." Isandro said it the way other men said good morning.

"He's paying in dust."

Isandro set down the mug. That was the trouble with the lower Verrow: everyone knew what you were short of. "How much dust?"

"Enough to make a man forget his rules." Merrick shrugged, rain running off his chin. "Course, I'd want my cut for the introduction."

"You'll get nothing but my continued silence about those resin barrels you're not declaring, Odo, and we'll both sleep fine." He pulled his oilskin hood up. "Where is this gentleman?"

Merrick's face did something complicated. "That's the thing," he said. "I don't know as *gentleman* is the word anymore."

* * *

The man was lying at the far end of the dock, half in the shadow of an overturned skiff, and for a moment Isandro thought he was looking at a pile of rags someone had abandoned. Then the rags coughed.

He was old—that much was clear even before Isandro crouched beside him. Old the way driftwood is old, bleached and split and lighter than it should be. His beard was white shot through with river-gray, crusted at the corners with something that wasn't mud. His hands lay open at his sides, palms up, fingers curled like dead ferns, and every finger was mapped with scars and stained to the knuckle with ochre and graphite—the hands of a man who had spent decades scratching at stone.

His eyes were open too. That was the worst part. They were fixed on the gray sky with an expression of profound irritation, as if the weather were a personal affront he intended to file a complaint about.

"He came in yesterday," Merrick said behind him, keeping his distance the way people keep their distance from sickness and bad luck. "Walked in, bought flour and a tin of matches, paid fair. Sat by the stove all afternoon. This morning we found him out here. Wouldn't come inside. Says—" Merrick lowered his voice, embarrassed, "—says the roof sings too loud."

"Delirious?"

"Sensible as a judge, far as I can tell. Just stubborn." Merrick shifted. "Doc Halloway's downriver at Bexley. Two days' run. And Captain—I can't have him dying on my dock. Bad for trade. People talk."

People talk. Isandro looked at the old man and felt the familiar machinery of self-preservation begin to turn: *not your problem, you've got a collector hunting you already, you can't afford charity, the last time you took in a stranger it cost you four months' cargo and a broken rib.* The machinery was well-oiled. It had kept him alive and invisible for twenty-two years, and it deserved his respect.

The old man turned his head. His eyes found Isandro's with unsettling precision, and he smiled—a dry, cracked smile, like paper tearing.

"There you are," he said. His voice was gravel wrapped in wool. "Been waiting for the sound of your boat since yesterday. Kestrel-class engine, third cylinder knocks on the upstroke. You ought to have that seen to."

Isandro blinked. "You know engines?"

"I know *your* engine." The old man coughed again, deep and wet, and Isandro heard the rattle in it and understood that this was not a man recovering from anything. "Kestrel. Good hull. Bad luck follows her, though. Or maybe she just goes looking for it." He tried to sit up, failed, and settled for turning his head to spit neatly over the dock's edge into the river. "Name's Lior."

"Congratulations," Isandro said. "I didn't ask."

"No. You didn't." Lior's eyes glittered with something that might have been humor. "That's how I knew you were the one."

"The one for what?"

"For carrying me." The old man said it simply, without drama, the way a man reports the weather. "I'm done walking, Captain. Done floating on my own bottom, too. I need a boat heading upstream, and yours is the only one at this landing with a captain who doesn't work for the Provision Company. I checked."

Something cold slid down Isandro's spine, beneath the oilskin, beneath the skin. "Everyone works for the Provision Company one way or another."

"Not yet, they don't." Lior closed his eyes. For a moment Isandro thought he'd lost him right there, and felt—an unwelcome, unbidden lurch—something like alarm. But the old man's chest still moved, shallow and rattling. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, and the rain seemed to lean in close around them. "I've got gold enough to pay your passage. More than enough. And I've got something else worth more than gold, and I need it carried upriver before the calving opens the high channel. That's all. That's the whole of it. Carry me and carry it, and you'll never owe another soul on this river again."

Isandro stood up slowly. His knees cracked. Down the dock, Merrick was pretending very hard to study his ledger, which meant he'd heard every word, which meant by nightfall the whole landing would know there was a dying prospector on the *Kestrel*'s berth talking about things worth more than gold.

That was how it started. That was always how it started. Not with violence—with a sentence spoken too loudly near a man who couldn't mind his own business.

"Get him inside," Isandro said. "My boat. The forward hold has a bunk. If he dies aboard, you're digging the hole, Odo, not me."

Merrick's eyebrows went up. "I thought you said no passengers."

"I said no passengers who can walk." Isandro crouched again, took one of those scarred, stained hands—it weighed almost nothing, bird-light, terrifyingly light—and draped the old man's arm across his shoulders. Lior smelled of woodsmoke, stone dust, and the sweetish stink of fever. "Up you get, old man. And if you throw up on my deck, the deal's off."

Lior laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough bent him double, and Isandro held him up against his own shoulder and waited it out, staring at the rain sliding off the pilings, wondering—not for the first time in his life, but with unusual force—why he could never manage to do the sensible thing even when the sensible thing was standing right there holding the door open for him.

* * *

The forward hold of the *Kestrel* smelled of turpentine and canvas and forty years of cargo that had left its ghost behind. Isandro laid Lior on the bunk beneath the porthole, propped him on folded sailcloth, and stood back with his arms crossed, feeling like a man who had just signed something.

The old man slept—or sank, rather; there was a difference, and Isandro had seen enough of both to know it. His breathing came in long, laboring pulls, like a man hauling a rope hand over hand. In sleep his face lost its irritation and became something younger and more worn, a face that had spent a long time being disappointed by the world and had made peace with it.

Isandro went through his pockets. Not proudly—he told himself it was prudence, standard procedure, any captain boarding a stranger would do the same—but his hands weren't entirely steady, and he knew the difference between prudence and fear, and this was fear.

What he found: a tin cup, dented. A folding knife worn to a sliver. A lump of flour-stained cloth containing, when he unwrapped it with his heart hammering, three flakes of gold no bigger than fingernail parings—and, beneath them, a packet of papers wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed string. He weighed the packet in his palm. Maps, probably. Claims. The usual litter of a prospector's life.

He should have put it back. He told himself he was putting it back. Instead he thumbed the knot loose and looked.

The top sheet was a map. Hand-drawn, grease-marked, rendered in a fine sure hand that used hatching for elevation and tiny patient dots for scree fields. It showed the upper Verrow—the country above Greyford, above Thornmouth, above the last survey marker Isandro had ever seen or heard of. It showed the glacier. And past the glacier's blue snout, drawn in ink so fresh it still shone faintly, a small valley with a spring marked at its head, and beside the spring, in letters small and fierce, two words:

*It lives.*

Isandro put the packet down on his knee and sat in the dim hold listening to the rain drum the deck above and the third cylinder knock its broken knock, and he felt the particular cold that comes not from weather but from understanding. Men died for maps. He had watched it happen once, years ago, from a safe distance he had purchased with silence, and he had sworn—there had been blood involved in the swearing, though not his own—that he would never again be within arm's reach of whatever a man like this carried.

"It lives," he said aloud, and the words sounded foolish in the empty hold, and true, and foolish again.

Above him, boots crossed the deck. Nell's knock—two quick raps, the signal she'd invented herself the first week she'd been aboard, meaning *trouble* or *food*, depending on her mood. He tucked the packet back into Lior's coat, buttoned the coat, and climbed the ladder.

* * *

Nell stood at the gangway with rain streaming off her hood, eleven years old and built entirely of elbows and opinions. Behind her on the dock, two figures were coming through the rain at a pace that Isandro recognized in his bones: long coats, flat hats, walking like men who had never once hurried for weather because weather worked for them.

Provision Company.

"Cap," Nell said, low and fast, "they asked Odo who's berthed here. Odo pointed at us."

Of course he did. Isandro put his hand briefly on her shoulder—felt her go still under it, the way she did, absorbing steadiness like dry ground absorbs rain—and stepped out onto the gangway to meet them.

They stopped at the foot of it. The taller one had a face like a closed ledger and rain beading on his hat brim, and he smiled the way creditors smile.

"Morning, Captain. Company business. We're looking for a prospector—older fellow, white beard, came through here yesterday. Might be traveling under the name Lior, might not. We understand he may have sought passage."

"Passage where?" Isandro asked.

"That's rather our question."

"Then I can't help you. Nobody's bought passage off me today. I'm loading resin and I'll be here till the tide turns, and after that I'm running cargo down to Bexley, same as every month, same as the Company's own schedule says I am." He let the boredom show in his voice, let it settle over the exchange like silt. "You're welcome to search the landing. Odo will sell you the privilege."

The tall one studied him for a long moment. Rain ticked on their hats. Somewhere behind Isandro, muffled by the hatch, a man began to cough—the deep, rope-burning cough of the dying—and Isandro's heart dropped through the deck plates into the river.

The agent's head tilted, very slightly, toward the sound.

"Cargo hold?" he asked pleasantly.

"Barrels," Isandro said. "Resin weeps when it gets damp. Sounds worse than it is." He held the man's eyes and thought about nothing at all, which was the hardest thinking he ever did. "Anything else? Tide won't wait on conversation."

A pause. Then the agent touched his hat brim. "Safe travels, Captain Vane. The Company values its independent operators." The way he said *independent* made it sound like a diagnosis. They turned and walked back up the boardwalk, unhurried, and Isandro watched them until the rain took them, and then a while longer.

Nell appeared at his elbow. "Was that a lie?"

"Which part?"

"All of it?"

Isandro looked down at her—at the sharp little face, the eyes that missed nothing and forgot less—and felt the day tilt beneath him, the way a deck tilts when the current shifts and you realize the river has plans of its own.

"Go make soup," he said. "There's a man in my hold who's going to need it."

She went. Isandro stayed on the gangway a moment more, watching the pewter water slide past the pilings, carrying everything downstream except, apparently, him. Upstream lay the glacier, and the calving, and a valley with a living spring, and two agents of the Provision Company who would now remember his name and his boat and the sound of a cough.

Below decks, faint through the planks, the old man's breathing hauled on, hand over hand, like a man pulling a rope up from somewhere very deep.

Isandro went down to see what he'd gotten himself into.
