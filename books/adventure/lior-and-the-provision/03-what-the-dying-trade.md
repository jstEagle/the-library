## 3. What the Dying Trade

The fever broke once in the night and Isandro let himself believe it was over.

He had put Lior in the bunk beneath the wheelhouse—the one he'd slept in himself for six years, though he told no one that—and stripped the wet clothes off the old man's back with the brisk efficiency of a man handling cargo. The body underneath had been a map of its own: ribs like river stones, a scar across the belly that some surgeon had closed badly and long ago, and on the left forearm a brand half-healed into white scar tissue. Isandro had seen that mark before, once, on the wrist of a Provision Company clerk who came aboard to count crates and never smiled. It meant something. He decided not to know what.

By morning the fever had come back like a tide, and with it came talking.

Isandro stood at the wheel with the Kestrel sliding down the green corridor of the lower Verrow, mist peeling off the water in ropes, and listened to a dying man argue with people who weren't there. Lior's voice was thin but stubborn, the voice of a man who had spent decades being right in empty country where being right kept you alive. He named rivers Isandro had never heard of. He counted things—distances, days, the number of men who'd gone up with him and the smaller number who'd come back. At one point, very clearly, he said: *Not the gold. You never understood. It was never the gold.* And then he wept, quietly, the way old men do, as if crying were just another chore to be gotten through.

Isandro adjusted the throttle and did not go below.

There was a discipline to it, this not-going. He had built his whole life on it the way you build a levee—one sack of refusal at a time, packed tight, until the water finds nothing to grip. Take on cargo. Deliver cargo. Take payment. Pay what could be paid, dodge what couldn't. Keep the Kestrel floating and your name small and your history sunk so deep in the river mud that even you can't dredge it up. That was the whole architecture. A dying prospector in his bunk, calling out numbers in the dark, was a crack in the levee, and Isandro knew exactly how cracks went.

But at midday, when the mist burned off and the sun lay flat and hot on the water, he went below anyway. He told himself it was to check the bilge.

Lior's eyes were open. They found Isandro in the dimness with an accuracy that was worse than fever—clear, patient, unsurprised.

"You didn't sleep," the old man said.

"Captain sleeps when there's time for it."

"There's never time for it. That's how they get you." Lior tried to sit up, failed, tried again, and Isandro—hating himself a little—put a hand behind his shoulders and helped. The old man weighed nothing. He weighed less than the coil of rope Isandro carried one-handed. "Water."

Isandro gave him water from the tin cup wedged by the bunk. Lior drank half and held the rest, turning the cup slowly, watching the surface tremble.

"I'm going to die on your boat," he said. Not a question. Not even, quite, a complaint. A surveyor's note. *Elevation falling. Ground unstable.*

"People die in beds all over the Verrow," Isandro said. "You're not special."

That got a sound out of him that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been rationed out of him years ago. "No," he agreed. "I'm not." His hand moved on the blanket, patting, searching, and Isandro watched him find the oilskin packet that had ridden against the old man's chest since the dock—wrapped, tied, stained dark at one corner with something that wasn't grease alone. Lior held it against his sternum like a man holding a wound shut.

"I know what you're doing," Isandro said. "Don't."

"Do you? Tell me, then."

"You're going to offer me something I don't want, for a promise I won't make, and then you're going to die before I can give either one back." Isandro leaned against the bulkhead, arms crossed, and made his voice flat as the horizon. "I've run this river eleven years. I've had men die aboard twice. Both times they tried to hand me something first. A ring. A letter. People think a boat is a priest. It's not. It's a box that floats."

"And yet you took me aboard."

"The alternative was stepping over you every morning until you stopped breathing. I have a reputation to maintain."

Lior looked at him for a long moment. Fever brightened his eyes but didn't blur them; whatever was burning him up was burning away everything except the essential parts, and apparently the essential parts included a kind of terrible patience. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone intimate, the voice of a man sharing a campfire rather than a deathbed.

"Eleven years running cargo," he said. "And before that?"

"Before that I was younger."

"You're Vane." The name landed in the little cabin like a dropped anchor. "Son of Aldous Vane."

The levee held. Isandro felt the pressure of the water behind it and kept his face still. "Aldous Vane owed money to everyone between Greyford and the sea. If you're a collector, you're twenty years late and dressed wrong."

"I'm not a collector." Lior's thumb worked at the knot on the oilskin packet. "I knew your father. Upstream. Before the Company bought his debts and his silence both." He paused. "He was trying to reach the source too, near the end. Did you know that?"

*The levee held,* but Isandro heard, distantly, the first sack shift and settle. "My father drowned drunk above Thornmouth," he said. "Whatever he was reaching for, the river got there first."

"He wasn't drunk." Lior said it gently, which was somehow worse. "He was scared. There's a difference, and the river knows it, and so did he."

Isandro pushed off the bulkhead and stood straight. "I'm going to check the fuel line," he said, "because this conversation has developed a leak."

"Vane." The old man's hand shot out—not strong, but quick—and caught Isandro's wrist. The grip of a dying man is a strange thing; there's no force in it, only gravity, only the plain fact that soon there will be nothing left to hold with. "Hear the trade. That's all. Hear it, and refuse it if you like. I'll die easier for having made the offer, and you'll lose nothing but five minutes you'd have spent listening to the engine anyway."

The engine, as it happened, was running beautifully. Isandro stayed.

* * *

Lior unwrapped the packet on the blanket between them, and the smell rose up out of it—tallow, pine resin, woodsmoke, the cold mineral breath of high country. Inside the oilskin was a second wrapping of waxed canvas, and inside that, folded into eighths, a sheet of heavy paper gone soft as cloth with handling, stained at the folds with years of grease from the hands that had opened and closed it, opened and closed it, in tents and wind and the blue shadow of ice.

"Unfold it," Lior said.

Isandro did, because refusing now would have been its own kind of answer, and he wasn't ready to give any answer at all.

The map was drawn in iron-gall ink gone brown, over pencil, over more pencil—a palimpsest of surveys, corrected and corrected again, the work of years compressed onto one sheet. The Verrow ran up the middle of it like a spine, and around the spine clustered annotations in three different hands: soundings, bearings, notes on current and gravel bar and the moods of the gorge. And past Thornmouth, past the mapped world's ragged northern edge, the river continued in a different style entirely—dotted lines, guessed lines, lines annotated in a cramped, fierce script: *ice bridge holds 6 weeks after calving, no more. Do not trust the east bank. Water rises 4 feet overnight, cause unknown.* And at the top of the sheet, where maps put north or a cartouche or a king's face, there was a small circle drawn freehand and filled in with such dense cross-hatching it had gone nearly black, and beside it, in letters pressed deep enough to score the paper:

*THE SOURCE. DO NOT SELL. DO NOT TELL. CARRY.*

Isandro looked at it for a long time. He was not a cartographer, but he had spent eleven years learning what rivers did to paper representations of themselves, and he knew honest work when he saw it. This was honest work. This was a document that had cost somebody everything, and the somebody was lying in front of him with a pulse like a moth against a window.

"It's real," he said finally. "Whoever drew it believed every line."

"I drew most of it. The rest I inherited, same as I'm offering to you." Lior's voice had gone quieter. The effort of the unwrapping had cost him; sweat stood on his forehead despite the chill of the cabin. "Twenty-two of us went up the year of the great calving. Four came back. Three of those died within the season—bad ends, mad ends. I'm the fourth, and I've been running downriver ever since, looking for—" He stopped. Started again. "Looking for someone who wouldn't want it."

"That's a strange thing to shop for."

"It's the only thing worth shopping for." Lior's hand crept across the blanket and rested, light as a bird, on the edge of the map. "Everyone wants it, Vane. That's the whole trouble. The Company wants it sealed because what's in that valley unseals everything else—their charters, their monopolies, the towns they burned to keep the secret. Prospectors want it because they think it's gold. My own partners wanted it, and it ate them alive from the inside out. What I need—" the breath caught, held, released— "what I need is a man who'll carry it upstream and give it to the river itself. Or bury it. Or burn it. Someone who understands that some cargoes are only safe when they're delivered nowhere."

Isandro began, carefully, to fold the map along its worn creases. "Then here's my counteroffer. I'll take you to a doctor at Greyford. Good one, ex-Army, doesn't ask names. Whatever this is—"

"This is thirty years of high country and a winter in my lungs. Your doctor will charge you a week's freight to tell me what I already know." Lior closed his eyes. Opened them. "Carry it upstream. Before calving opens the route—that's the window, six weeks, maybe seven, when the ice bridge stands and the gorge is passable. Miss it and the route closes for a year, and the year after that the Company's new survey ships come up from the delta, and then it's over forever. They'll find it, Vane. They always find it eventually. Unless someone makes sure there's nothing left to find."

"No."

It came out of him faster than he'd planned, and he let it stand. "No," he said again, steadier. "I don't know what's in that valley and I don't want to. I run cargo. Grain, timber, salt, machine parts. Things that stay things. I don't run promises upstream through a glacier for dead men, and I especially don't do it for free, and I *especially* don't do it because a stranger knows my father's name." He set the folded map down on the blanket, equidistant between them, a border neither would cross. "Greyford's two days. I'll get you there. After that, this stays aboard until I can hand it to whoever buries you, and then it's not mine and never was."

Lior studied him without anger. That was the worst part—the absence of anger, the absence of pleading, the look of a man checking a bearing he'd already trusted.

"You say you run cargo," the old man said. "Things that stay things. But you took me aboard knowing I was nothing but trouble, and you're feeding me, and you haven't charged me a copper, and you're standing there angry because some part of you already knows the answer." He laid his hand flat on the oilskin. "Being needed isn't a trap because of what people ask, Vane. It's a trap because of what you find out you're willing to give."

"I'm not willing to give anything."

"Not yet." Lior's eyes drifted toward the porthole, where the green water slid by, going somewhere with enormous patience. "But you will watch me worsen. Two days to Greyford. Maybe I last two days. Maybe not. And every hour, you'll ask yourself whether the promise costs you anything at all—whether it's really so heavy, carrying a piece of paper north—and every hour the answer will get lighter, and lighter, until one morning you pick it up just to see how it feels in your hand." He smiled, faint and terrible. "I did the same thing. So did your father. Nobody refuses this cargo, Captain. They only delay accepting it."

Isandro left the cabin. On the way up the ladder he told himself the conversation was over, that he had refused cleanly, that a man was entitled to his refusal the same as his fare.

The engine hummed. The river ran south while they fought it northward, and Isandro stood at the wheel and thought about his father—not the drowning, which was the story he kept polished for his own use, but the years before it, the long absences, the returns with hollow cheeks and burning eyes and pockets full of ore samples he'd never show anyone. *Scared,* Lior had said. *Not drunk. Scared.* Isandro had spent twenty years building a father out of the drowning, simple and final, a man the river had finished. Now some fevered stranger had handed him a different father entirely, one who'd been reaching for something, and Isandro hated him for it with a purity that frightened him.

* * *

The worsening came fast after that, the way it does, all at once and then gradually and then all at once again.

By evening of the first day Lior could no longer stand without help, and Isandro found himself steadying the old man on the short walk from bunk to deck, feeling the fragile architecture of him, the way a chimney feels before it falls. By the following dawn the lucid hours had shrunk to islands, and between them the fever talked—counting, naming, arguing with the dead. Isandro cooked rice porridge he couldn't get the old man to eat. He sponged the burning skin with river water. He did these things without deciding to do them, which was the trap working exactly as advertised, each kindness laying the next one down like a plank.

On the second afternoon, in a clear hour, Lior asked to be brought up on deck.

Isandro carried him up—carried him, this grown man light as a bundle of reeds—and settled him in the wheelhouse doorway where he could see the river. The Verrow ran wide and brown-green here, cattle watering on the far bank, the smoke of a landing town smudged against the heat haze. Greyford by nightfall, if the current held.

"Good river," Lior said.

"It's a river."

"You'll take the map north." The old man said it without inflection, a fact observed from altitude. "When I'm done wasting your time."

"I told you no."

"You did." Lior's head lolled against the doorframe. His eyes were closed but his mouth kept moving, unhurried, a man dictating a letter he had plenty of time to finish. "You'll tell yourself it's not a choice. That there was never a moment where you said yes. That's fine. That's how the good ones do it. The ones who want it—gold-hungry men—they choose, and choosing poisons them. But a man who never chooses..." The breath whistled. "A man who never chooses just wakes up one day already carrying it. Like a debt. Like a son inherits."

"Stop talking about my father."

"I'm talking about you." The old man's hand found Isandro's sleeve and gripped it with the last of his strength, and his eyes opened, and they were clear as winter stars. "One promise. Carry it upstream before the calving. Don't read it more than you must. Don't sell it, don't spend it, don't let anyone love you for having it. Just carry it. Give it to the ice or the fire or the deep water—anywhere it can't be owned. That's the whole trade. That's everything I have left to ask of the world." His grip tightened. "Say no again if you mean it. Say it slow. I'll take no for an answer if it's true."

Isandro opened his mouth.

What came out—or what failed to—was interrupted by the sound of Lior's breath going wrong, a wet rattle like water finding a new channel, and the old man's weight sagging into his arms, and the hour of clarity closing over him like ice. Isandro lowered him to the deck boards and knelt there with the river running loud beneath the hull and the fever taking the old man back down into its depths, and he realized his hands were shaking, and he realized something else, worse:

He had been about to say yes.

He hadn't decided to. That was the horror of it. The word had simply been there, loaded, waiting for the smallest clearance—and Lior had known, had *measured* him, the way you'd measure a channel before running it at night.

Below him, faintly, the dying man began to count again. Distances. Days. The number of miles remaining.

Isandro got up, went to the wheel, and put the Kestrel's nose hard into the current, driving for Greyford as if speed could outrun a bargain already struck. Behind him, in the wheelhouse doorway, the oilskin packet lay where he'd set it down that morning—except he had no memory of setting it there, and it sat square in the center of the doorway, precisely where a man would have to step over it to take the helm.

He stepped over it. For now, he stepped over it.

Ahead, the smoke of Greyford Landing thickened on the horizon, and with it—though Isandro could not have known it—the shapes of three strangers waiting at the dock, and further out, riding low and gray in the channel haze, a Company launch flying no flag at all.
