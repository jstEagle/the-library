## 4. The Promise

The fever broke at midnight, and Isandro knew then that Lior was going to die.

He had seen it before—on the Kestrel, in the years when he'd carried more than cargo, when men came aboard whole and left in canvas. The body had a way of announcing itself before the end, a strange clarity that settled over the dying like river fog burning off. Lior lay on the narrow bunk in the stern cabin, and his breathing had changed. Not the wet, tearing rasp of the fever hours, but something slower now. Deliberate. As if each breath were a coin he was counting out carefully, knowing the purse would not refill.

Isandro sat in the chair he'd dragged from the galley, a lamp turned low between them so the oil would last. He had not slept. He did not expect to.

"You're still here." Lior's voice was a ruin of itself, scraped thin as driftwood. His eyes found Isandro in the lamplight, pale and startlingly clear.

"Boat's anchored," Isandro said. "Nowhere to be till morning."

"Liar." A ghost of a smile moved across the old man's cracked lips. "You could've put me ashore at Miller's Bend. Left me with the sisters at their mission house. You didn't."

"The mission house charges by the day."

"There it is." Lior's laugh became a cough, and the cough went on too long, and Isandro leaned forward without thinking to steady the man's shoulder until it passed. When it was done, Lior sagged back against the pillow like something emptied. "You're a hard man, Vane. But you're not a cruel one. There's a difference. Most people never learn to see it."

"Don't flatter me. I'm not going to give you anything."

"You already have." Lior's hand crept out from under the blanket, trembling, and rested on the arm of the chair. His skin was paper over bone, cool despite the stove Isandro had fed all night. "You gave me a clean place to die. Do you know how many men don't get that? How many just go into the water, or the mud, or some ditch off the towpath, and the river takes them and nobody ever says their name again?"

Isandro said nothing. Outside, the Verrow moved past the hull with its endless low whisper, that sound he knew better than any voice, better than his own heartbeat. It had been his lullaby and his alibi for eleven years. It asked nothing of him. That was why he loved it.

"I want you to take the map upstream," Lior said.

"No."

"You haven't even heard—"

"I heard enough two nights ago." Isandro stood, needing to move, and crossed to the small porthole where the black water showed a smear of starlight. "I don't carry promises, old man. I carry freight. Timber, grain, whatever pays. What you're asking—that's not freight. That's a noose with a map tied to the end of it. The Provision Company doesn't send collectors after men who owe money. They send them after men who know things. And I have spent eleven years being a man who knows nothing."

"You know nothing," Lior agreed softly. "That's what makes you perfect for it."

"That's not a compliment."

"It's the truest one I can give." The old man struggled up onto one elbow, and the effort cost him visibly—a sheen of sweat, a tremor through the wasted arm. "Listen to me. I'm not asking you to understand. Understanding comes later, if it comes at all. I'm asking you to do one thing. Take the map upriver. Past Thornmouth. Past the White Reach, when the calving opens the gate. Deliver it to the source, or deliver it to someone worth trusting, or bury it in the ice—I don't care which, anymore. I cared once. Now I only care that it doesn't die with me in your cabin like a rat in a wall."

"And if I say no?"

Lior looked at him for a long moment. In the low light his face seemed carved from the same stuff as the hills they'd passed that afternoon—all ridge and hollow, weathered down to essentials.

"Then I'll die anyway," he said. "And the map will be yours whether you want it or not, because dead men don't own things, and you'll be the one holding it. So say no if you need to, Captain. But say it knowing what you'll be carrying out of this room either way."

Isandro turned back to the porthole. The stars on the water wavered, broke, reformed. Somewhere far downstream a night bird called, three notes falling away like coins dropped down a well.

"I made my life small on purpose," he said finally. "You understand that? Small boat. Small debts. Small lies that don't hurt anybody. Every year the net gets a little tighter—the Company, the taxes, the tolls—but I stay under it. I stay *small*. And you come aboard bleeding your big secret all over my deck, and you want me to stand up inside that net."

"Yes."

"Why? Why me? You could've flagged down any boat on this river."

"Because any other captain would have already sold me to Hale's people for the reward." Lior sank back down, exhausted by the effort of propping himself up. "You had me two days in your hold and never went through my pack. Don't deny it—I watched you look at it and walk away. Twice. A man who doesn't search a dying stranger's belongings is a man who understands that some things aren't his to take. That's the whole of it, Vane. That's the entire reason. I trusted the shape of you."

Isandro's jaw tightened. He remembered those moments—the oilskin pack wedged in the corner, the way his fingers had actually twitched toward the buckle the first night, and how he'd made himself turn away. He'd told himself it was pragmatism. Plausible deniability. If he didn't know what the old man carried, he couldn't be accused of knowing.

It hadn't been pragmatism. He'd known that even then.

"I'll get you to Greyford," he said. "There's a doctor there. A real one, not a tooth-puller. If anyone can pull you back from this, it's—"

"No doctor." Lior's voice had gone quiet in a new way. Final. "Greyford is a Company town. Company doctors write reports. I'll not spend my last days as an entry in Corvus Hale's ledger." He closed his eyes. "Leave me be, Captain. Let an old man rest. We'll talk again at dawn, if dawn wants us."

* * *

Dawn wanted them both, though only one of them knew it yet.

The light came up gray-gold over the eastern bluffs, and the mist rose off the Verrow in slow columns, and Isandro stood at the rail with cold coffee going colder in his hands, watching the bank slide past: willow and alder, red clay cutbanks, a heron standing its patient vigil in the shallows. Ordinary country. Country that asked nothing.

Behind him, in the cabin, Lior's breathing had gone so quiet that Isandro kept turning to check it, the way a man checks a candle flame in a draft.

He went below when the light was strong enough to read by. Lior's eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling beams, and when Isandro entered they rolled slowly toward him.

"Still here," Lior whispered.

"So are you."

"Not for long." The old man worked his hand free of the blanket again, and this time Isandro took it. He told himself it was to keep the man from straining. He knew, even as he did it, that it was something else—something he would examine later, or more likely never. The hand in his weighed almost nothing. It was like holding a bundle of dry reeds.

"I never told you my name's real name," Lior said. "Or where I came from. Or the names of the six men who went up with me, four years ago, and didn't come down." His breath caught, held, released. "Doesn't matter. Names are for people who get graves. I only need you to know one thing about the map."

"Don't," Isandro said. "Don't make a speech. Save your breath."

"A debt owed to everyone." The words came out with terrible slowness, each one placed like a stone in a crossing. "That's what it is. Not treasure. A *debt*. Everyone who drinks from the Verrow, everyone who fishes it, floats it, buries their dead along its banks—they're owed something, and the Company has been stealing it for thirty years, and the map is the proof and the payment both." His grip tightened with a strength that surprised them both, sudden as a fish's last kick. "Promise me. Not the Company. Not the river. *Me.* Carry it up. Before the calving. Promise me, Isandro Vane, son of Aldous Vane, who also died owing money to men with ledgers—"

"How do you know my father's name?"

But Lior wasn't listening anymore. His eyes had gone past Isandro, past the bulkhead, to something at a great distance, and his lips were moving through words that had no sound, and then the movement stopped, and the grip eased, and the hand in Isandro's became a thing rather than a man.

The lamp had burned nearly dry. In the gray light through the porthole, the cabin looked exactly as it always did—and entirely different, the way a room changes when a living presence leaves it. Isandro sat for a long time, still holding the hand, because letting go felt like a second death and he could not bring himself to cause it.

When he finally laid the hand down on the blanket and folded the fingers closed, he noticed that his own were shaking.

* * *

He buried him on the north bank, where the ground rose above flood line among a stand of birches whose leaves were just beginning to turn.

It took him most of the morning. The soil was root-bound and full of stone, and the spade from the cargo deck was meant for moving grain, not grave-digging, and twice he stopped to rest with his hands on his knees, breathing hard, telling himself this was foolishness—that the proper thing, the sensible thing, was the river. Captains buried strangers in the river. It was quick, it was clean, it left no marker for Company men to find and interrogate.

But the river took everything and returned nothing, and Lior had spent four years walking toward something and dying short of it, and Isandro found—he could not have explained it, and did not try—that he could not give this man to the current like so much spoiled freight. The old man had asked for a clean place to die. He should have a clean place to lie.

So he dug. He dug until the hole was deep enough to keep the animals honest, and he wrapped Lior in the wool blanket from the bunk because the old man owned nothing else worth shrouding, and he lowered him down with a rope slung under the shoulders because his arms alone wouldn't bear the weight anymore.

He did not pray. He had nothing to pray to, and suspected Lior hadn't either. Instead he stood at the head of the grave with his hat off, looking at the birches, and said:

"I don't forgive you for this. I want that understood. Wherever you are, if anywhere—you put this on me, and I didn't ask for it, and I want it known that I know that."

The birches said nothing. A crow lifted off one of them and beat away south.

"But you got a grave," Isandro went on, his voice roughening. "Out of me. A man who owes everybody money gave you the only thing he had that was free. So we're square on that part. Just that part."

He filled the grave. He set a flat stone at the head, and after a moment's thought he used the point of his knife to scratch into it the single word LIOR, and beneath it, smaller: HE WALKED UP. It wasn't much. It was more than the river would have given.

Then he went back to the boat, and the map was waiting for him.

* * *

The oilskin pack sat where it had sat for two days, in the corner of the stern cabin, patient as a debt collector. Isandro stood in the doorway a long while, looking at it.

He had promised nothing. That was the thing he kept returning to, turning over like a stone in his pocket, hunting for the smooth side. Lior had asked; Isandro had refused. Refused clearly, refused twice. Whatever happened now was not breach of promise. It was simply inheritance—the way silt belongs to whoever owns the bank it settles on.

Except.

*Promise me, Isandro Vane.*

He had not said yes. But he had not let go of the hand, either. He had held it while the old man died believing—or hoping, which might be worse—that the yes was coming. And there was a version of Isandro Vane, a version he did not like and did not visit often, that understood perfectly well that silence in the presence of a dying man's hope is a kind of answer. That the refusal he'd spoken aloud had been for his own protection, filed like a ship's manifest, while the truth underneath had been legible to both of them the whole time.

He crouched and picked up the pack. It weighed almost nothing. That seemed obscene.

The buckle was cold under his fingers. Inside: a tin cup, dented; a folding knife worn to a sliver; a journal bound in oilcloth, swollen with damp; and beneath these, wrapped in a second layer of waxed linen, the map.

He unwrapped it on the chart table. It was drawn on sailcloth, grease-stained at the corners, dense with survey marks in a fine, sure hand—soundings, bearings, elevations, notes in a shorthand he couldn't read. The river ran up the center of it like a spine, and above Thornmouth the familiar country ended and the lines continued into blankness, into territory no commercial chart acknowledged, up and up to a place marked with a single symbol: a circle, and within the circle, another circle. Like an eye. Like a well.

Like a debt.

Isandro stood over it for a long time. Through the hull he could feel the current leaning on the keel, patient, indifferent, ready to carry him downstream to Greyford and the doctor he no longer needed and the small life he had built so carefully inside the net.

He thought about leaving the map in the grave. He actually climbed the bank with it once, stood over the fresh-turned earth with the waxed linen in his hands, and imagined setting it down in the dark beside the man who'd carried it four thousand miles. Let the debt be buried with the debtor. Clean. Final.

But Lior had called it a debt owed to *everyone*, and you cannot pay a debt to everyone by hiding it in the ground. And there had been six men, four years ago, who hadn't come down. And somewhere downstream, sooner or later, Corvus Hale's ledger was going to notice a dead prospector last seen boarding the Kestrel, and Isandro understood—with the flat, cold certainty of a man reading water ahead of a snag—that the map in his possession was now the only thing standing between the Provision Company and a story with no witnesses.

If he surrendered it, it died with him. If he destroyed it, six men stayed nameless forever, and whatever the Company had poisoned to keep quiet stayed quiet. If he carried it—

If he carried it, he was no longer small.

Isandro folded the map along its old creases, careful as a man handling a wound, and slid it inside his coat, against his chest, where he could feel it with every breath. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

He cast off at midafternoon. The Kestrel swung her bow upstream against the current, engines muttering, and the Verrow took the hull and pushed back, as it always did, as it would every mile of the long climb ahead.

At Greyford Landing, he told himself, he would take on passengers. Freight. Anything that paid. Nothing had changed.

Three figures waited on the dock.
