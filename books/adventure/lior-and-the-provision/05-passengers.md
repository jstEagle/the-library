## 5. Passengers

Greyford Landing announced itself the way a sick man announces himself: by smell before sight. Sefa caught it from the rail of the *Kestrel* as they came around the last bend—tar and rotting sawdust and the sweet-sour reek of fish offal dumped too close to the water, all of it layered over the cold mineral breath of the river itself. She had been to a hundred landings like it in her old life, and she had sworn she'd never stand on another one.

Yet here she was, one bag in her hand, watching the town assemble itself out of the morning fog: a crooked row of stilt houses leaning over brown water, a timber crane gone green with moss, a saloon whose sign had lost its letters until only *THE ——— AR* remained. Behind it all, the Verrow ran wide and slow here, patient as a creditor.

She had chosen this boat because it was small. Small boats went unnoticed; small boats did not keep passenger manifests worth stealing. The man at the wheel—the captain, Vane, according to the paint flaking off the wheelhouse door—had not asked her a single question when she'd stepped aboard at dawn two landings back, only held out a palm for her coin and jerked his thumb toward the deck. That suited her fine. Questions were how people got found.

But he watched her. She felt it the way she felt weather coming—a pressure behind the ears. When she'd unrolled her charts that first night to check their position against the river's course, she'd caught him looking at them through the wheelhouse glass, and there had been something in his face she recognized because she'd worn it herself for years: not greed, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man seeing a tool he knew how to use and wished he didn't.

Sefa kept her charts rolled now. She stood at the rail with her hands in her pockets and let Greyford come to her, and she told herself the familiar lie: *just passage, just distance, just until the name stops following me.*

The *Kestrel* kissed the pilings with a groan of old wood. Lines went out. Somewhere behind her, the captain said, "Two hours," in a voice like gravel poured slowly, and she understood it was addressed to everyone and no one.

She went down the gangplank into the mud of Greyford, and if anyone in that town had ever heard of the cartographer who put a river on a map where no river existed—the map that drowned eleven men—she would know within the hour, and she would be gone before the second hour closed.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. She had made it herself, and her maps were always accurate.

It was the only thing about her that was.

* * *

Bram felt the boards of Greyford Landing flex under his boots and thought, absurdly, of church floors.

He hadn't been inside a church in six years, but his body remembered the way certain floors were built to bear weight without complaint, and these planks had that same honest give. He liked that. He liked most things that did their work quietly—good rope, a well-hung door, an axe with a true handle. It was why he'd lasted as long as he had in the army, and why he'd stopped lasting all at once, on one particular morning, in one particular valley, when quiet work had turned out to be something else entirely.

He shook the thought off the way a dog shakes off water. It came back anyway. It always came back.

The landing was half awake. Dockhands hauled crates marked with the Provision Company's ironwheel stamp, and Bram kept his shoulders loose and his eyes down as he passed them, because a man who had worn the Company's grey uniform—even briefly, even at the bottom of the ranks—learned to recognize the stamp the way a beaten dog recognizes a belt. He'd torn the uniform up years ago. The habit of flinching had proven harder to destroy.

He needed work. That was the whole of his ambition now: work hard enough and long enough that his hands were too tired at night to remember anything. He'd followed that rule from one end of the Verrow country to the other, and it had mostly held, except for nights like last night, when he'd dreamed of the valley again and woken with his fists clenched so hard his knuckles ached.

The riverboat looked like an answer. Not a grand one—the *Kestrel* was a working boat, patched and repatched, her paddle housing scarred, her smokestack trailing a thin uncertain ribbon—but she was loading cargo, and loading meant lifting, and lifting was what Bram was for. He stood at the edge of the activity until the captain came down the plank, a lean man with river-grey eyes and the kind of stillness Bram associated with men who had decided long ago not to be surprised by anything.

"Looking for the captain," Bram said. "I'm strong. I don't talk much. I'll take pay in passage or coin, whichever's cheaper for you."

Vane looked at him for a long moment. Bram had learned to endure being looked at; big men got looked at their whole lives, usually by smaller men deciding whether to be afraid.

"Can you handle a winch?"

"Yes."

"Crates go aft. Don't drop anything I own."

"That's all?"

Vane was already turning away. "You want a speech, hire a preacher."

Bram almost smiled. He took off his coat, folded it, set it on a piling, and went to the winch, and for the next hour the world narrowed to the beautiful simple grammar of weight and leverage and rope. His shoulders burned. His mind went blessedly white. This, he thought. This is enough. Let this be enough.

He was halfway through the third crate when he noticed the girl.

She was small and quick and she moved through the dock crowd like water finding cracks—around a fishmonger's elbow, under a crane arm, along the shadow line of the warehouse wall. She wasn't begging. He'd seen beggars; they made eye contact, performed their need. This girl avoided every eye in Greyford as though eyes were things that could grab you. She drifted toward the *Kestrel*'s cargo stack, paused, examined the sky with elaborate boredom, and then slipped into the gap between two crates with the smoothness of long practice.

Bram's hands kept turning the winch. His heart had started hammering like he'd seen a ghost, which was nearly true—he'd been that child once, moving through garrison towns the same way, invisible on purpose, because visible children near soldiers ended up in places children shouldn't be.

He said nothing. Telling the captain was the right thing, probably. But right things had a way of getting people hurt, and besides—it wasn't his boat, and it wasn't his business, and the girl could hide wherever she liked as far as he was concerned.

Still, as the loading went on and the crate gap stayed quiet, he positioned himself, without appearing to, where he could see it. Just in case. Some habits weren't about the army at all.

* * *

Nell had been inside the crate gap since before the sun cleared the ridge, and she had a system, and the system was working, and she wished everyone—including her own stomach—would stop acting like it wasn't.

Rule one: get aboard before the crew does. Done. Rule two: pick a hiding place with air and room to shift, because cramps make noise. Done—she was folded into the space between a crate of nails and a crate of dried apples, and the apple crate had a split corner, and she had already eaten three apples very slowly and quietly, core and all, because evidence was the thing that got you caught. Rule three: stay hidden until the boat was so far from shore that putting back was more trouble than tolerating you. Then emerge, look pathetic but useful, and negotiate.

She'd run the system twice before. Once it worked beautifully, all the way to Marlow Bend. Once it failed at the first lock, and a lock-keeper with bad teeth had dragged her out by the collar and put her ashore with a swat and a lecture about how girls traveling alone got taken, girl, didn't she know what happened to girls—and Nell had known, better than he did, which was why she hid.

Greyford smelled terrible. Nell catalogued its smells the way other people counted sheep: tar, fish guts, wet rope, someone frying onions, horse, mud, and underneath everything the river, which smelled like stone and cold and something older. She liked the river smell best. Rivers didn't care where you'd come from.

Through the crack between the crates she watched the passengers come aboard. A woman with a canvas roll slung across her back, walking like the roll was full of knives—or maybe like she was. A big man with forearms like mooring posts who took over the winch and made it look easy. And the captain, Vane, going up and down the plank with his ledger, taking coins, not asking questions. Nell approved of captains who didn't ask questions. It meant they had things they didn't want asked about either. Those were the best kind of people to travel with, because they understood the arrangement: nobody looks too hard at anybody.

Her stomach growled, low and long, and she pressed both arms against it and breathed through her nose. Apples. She'd had apples. Be grateful. Gratitude was free and hunger was temporary and the boat was leaving, she could feel it in the deck's faint tremble as the engine below woke up, coughed, settled into its deep steady heartbeat.

Then footsteps stopped directly outside her gap, and a voice said, "You going to eat those cores quietly, or do I have to listen to you chew all the way to Thornmouth?"

Nell froze so completely she forgot to breathe.

The voice belonged to the big man. He wasn't looking at her—she could tell through the crack that his eyes were forward, on his work—but he'd spoken clearly, and clearly to her, and her whole body rang with alarm like a struck bell.

Options tumbled through her head: deny it, bolt, cry, lie. She rejected them all in order. Denial was stupid when the evidence was literally in her lap. Bolting meant jumping into a moving river. Crying was for emergencies. Lying—

Lying, then. But a good one.

"I bought those apples," she said, keeping her voice flat and bored, the voice of a person with nothing to hide. "From the market. On the dock. Before we left."

"Mm." The winch creaked. Rope paid out. "Market opens early in Greyford."

"It opens whenever it wants. Nobody owns the market."

Another pause. She could hear him deciding something, the way you could hear weather decide. Then he shifted, and his broad back moved between her gap and the rest of the deck, casual as anything, blocking her from view of the wheelhouse.

"There's a blanket in the coil by the stern rail," he said quietly. "When it gets dark and the deck's empty. Don't say I told you."

Nell stared at the back of a stranger's head and felt something crack open in her chest that she didn't have a name for and didn't trust one bit.

"Why?" she said.

"Because I hate chewing noises," the big man said, and turned the winch, and said nothing else.

* * *

Sefa came back aboard as the lines were cast off, and she felt the *Kestrel*'s motion change beneath her feet—the small shudder, the gathering, the slide away from the pilings—and something in her chest loosened that she hadn't known was clenched. Greyford shrank. The stilt houses leaned together like conspirators, then like children waving, then like nothing at all, just shapes in the haze. The name she was running from stayed behind on the muddy bank, at least for today.

She climbed to the bow rail, where the spray was finest and no one stood, and allowed herself the day's single indulgence: she unrolled her chart of the middle Verrow across the capstan head and studied it.

Not her official chart. Her real one—the one she drew at night, from memory and obsession, the one with annotations no survey office would ever sanction. *Fog banks collect below the Split, mornings, spring.* *Old channel, west fork, dries in August but holds fish year-round—villagers net there illegally.* *Depth soundings unreliable past Thornmouth; current shifts after heavy rain upstream.* A living map. A map that admitted the river was alive. It was the kind of mapping she'd believed in once, before belief got people killed, before the Commission stripped her credentials with a rubber stamp and a sentence read aloud in a dry room while she stood very straight and did not cry until afterward, alone, in the stairwell.

The river bent ahead into the afternoon light, and Sefa traced its curve with one finger, and hated how much she loved it.

"You're a surveyor."

The captain's voice, close behind her. She hadn't heard him cross the deck, which should have been impossible—she'd trained herself to hear people coming, and this man moved like the river moved, constant and quiet.

"I'm a passenger," she said, rolling the chart with deliberate slowness.

"Passengers don't mark soundings in the margins." He nodded at the roll. "And they don't hold them like they're afraid someone will read over their shoulder."

Sefa turned. Up close, Isandro Vane had the kind of face that gave away nothing on purpose, and tiredness everywhere else—in the creases, in the eyes, in the way he stood like a man carrying something below decks that weighed more than any cargo.

"And what would you do if I were?" she asked. "A surveyor."

"Do?" He looked genuinely puzzled by the question, and that puzzled her more than suspicion would have. "Nothing. It's your paper." He glanced at the river, then back. "Just—there's a stretch above Thornmouth where every chart I've ever bought disagrees with the water. If yours doesn't, I might ask you a question someday. That's all."

He walked away before she could answer, and Sefa stood at the rail with her heart doing something complicated and unwelcome.

*A question someday.* No one had asked her a professional question in four years. They'd asked her plenty of others—how she could have signed off on a channel that didn't exist, whether the bribes were worth eleven lives, whether she slept at night—all questions designed as verdicts. But a question about the water, about the actual living water, as if her knowledge were still worth something to somebody—

She unrolled the chart again. Found Thornmouth. Stared at the reach above it, where her careful ink faded into blank paper, the great unmapped throat of the upper Verrow that no licensed surveyor was permitted to enter, by decree of the Provision Company, whose ironwheel stamps she could suddenly see everywhere once she looked: on the crates stacked aft, on the fuel drums lashed amidships, on the manifest the captain carried.

Everyone on this boat was running from something, she thought. The big man lifted like a man doing penance. The captain steered like a man avoiding pursuit. Even the river itself seemed to be fleeing—always downhill, always away, hurrying toward some lower, safer place.

And somewhere behind them, in the fog Greyford had already swallowed, Sefa couldn't shake the feeling that something was following all of them anyway. She'd spent four years learning to sense being hunted the way sailors sense storms, and the air tonight had that taste: pressure without wind, silence with intent.

Below her feet, muffled by planking, she heard a small sneeze—from inside the cargo, unless she missed her guess—and heard the big man cough loudly to cover it, badly.

Sefa almost laughed. Almost.

Then she looked north, up the darkening river, toward Thornmouth and whatever waited beyond it, and the laugh died in her throat.

Far downstream, barely visible in the last light, a boat with no lights was holding the middle channel. Keeping pace. Keeping distance.

Keeping watch.
