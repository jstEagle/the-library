## 6. Company Men

The fog came off the Verrow that morning like a held breath, and Isandro Vane knew before he saw the launch that his luck had run its course.

He'd felt it building for days—the way the river traffic thinned below Greyford, the way the fuel barges stopped answering his hails, the way even the gulls seemed to give the Kestrel's wake a wide berth. Word traveled on water faster than any telegraph. Somewhere downriver, someone had paid for that word, and someone else had carried it, and now it had caught up with him, riding a gray wake out of the gray morning with three men standing in it like nails driven into a plank.

"Company," Bram said from the cargo deck. He said it the way other men said *storm* or *sandbar*—a fact of weather, not a question.

Isandro set the wheel and watched the launch come on. Provision Company gunmetal, Provision Company flag snapping wet at the stern, and at the bow a figure who did not move as the boat rocked, which was somehow worse than if he had braced himself. The river moved under everyone. It did not move under this man.

"They'll want to board," Sefa said, appearing at his shoulder with a chart case hugged to her chest like a shield. Her voice was level. Her knuckles were not.

"They'll want to search." Isandro turned the wheel two degrees, bringing the Kestrel's nose toward the channel marker, all business, nothing hidden in his posture except everything hidden in his hold. "So we give them a clean deck and a boring captain. Nell—"

"I'm here." The girl materialized out of the crate shadows where she'd been living since Greyford, eyes bright, hair tied back with a length of tarred twine. She looked, Isandro thought, exactly like what she was: a stowaway who'd been fed and half-adopted by accident. Which meant she couldn't be anywhere near the map.

"Forward cabin. Under the floorboard with the loose nail. You know the one."

"I know the one." She went without argument, and he loved her a little for that, and hated himself for the word *loved*, because love was just another debt, and debts were what had brought these men up his river.

* * *

The launch came alongside with a bump Isandro felt through the hull, soft and deliberate, the touch of something that wanted him to know it could hit harder. A gangway plank came across. And then Corvus Hale stepped onto the Kestrel's deck as though he were stepping into his own parlor.

He was not what Isandro expected. He was not big. He was not scarred or loud or cruel-looking. He was a narrow, tidy man in a gray coat that fit him the way a ledger fits a column of figures, and his face was the face of an accountant who had never once been surprised by a number. His eyes did the only moving on him—a slow inventory sweep, bow to stern, that took perhaps four seconds and missed nothing, including the fresh oil stain near the winch, including the mended rail, including, Isandro was certain, the exact weight of the boat sitting in the water versus the weight she should have been carrying.

"Captain Vane." Hale's voice was quiet enough that Isandro had to lean toward it, and understood immediately that this was deliberate. "Corvus Hale, Provision Company, Office of Recovery. I have a warrant concerning the effects of one Lior Anselm, prospector, deceased approximately eleven days ago at a landing forty miles downstream."

"You're well informed."

"It's my profession." Hale produced a folded paper but did not open it, holding it the way a priest holds a book he knows by heart. "Your name appears in his effects. In a ledger. He owed you passage money, apparently. Or you owed him. The entry is ambiguous."

"He owed me nothing. I gave him a ride because he was dying on my dock." Isandro kept his hands loose at his sides, kept his voice in the register he used for customs men and fuel brokers: bored, cooperative, faintly put upon. "Buried him myself, up past Miller's Bend. You're welcome to dig him up and check."

"That won't be necessary." Hale's gaze drifted past Isandro's shoulder, along the deck, cataloguing. "Though I confess I'm curious about your passengers. A cartographer whose license was revoked in Harrowgate. A deserter from the Third Rifles with a bounty notice that's six weeks stale. And a child no one has reported missing, which is its own kind of interesting."

The fog pressed close around the hull. Somewhere inside the Kestrel, Isandro knew, Nell was lying under a floorboard listening to every word, and Bram was standing very still by the cargo hatch with the stillness of a man calculating distances, and Sefa was watching from the wheelhouse with her jaw set like a woman memorizing a face for later.

"They work for their passage," Isandro said. "Same as anyone on this river. If the Company has warrants for them, serve them. Otherwise they're crew, and crew are my business."

"Everything on this river is the Company's business, Captain. That's rather the point of the Company." Hale finally opened the warrant, more ceremony than necessity. "I'm authorized to search for documents, maps, correspondence, or mineral specimens belonging to the deceased. You'll permit my men twenty minutes."

"And if I don't?"

Hale looked at him then—really looked, for the first time—and Isandro felt the look go through him like a sounding line dropped into dark water, measuring depth, finding bottom, finding it farther down than expected.

"Then I note your refusal in my report, and the next boat that boards you carries soldiers instead of clerks." He smiled, and it was the worst thing about him, that smile: small, precise, entirely without warmth, a signature on a form. "I'd prefer the clerks. They make less mess."

* * *

They searched.

Isandro stood on his own deck like a guest while two agents in gray went through his boat with the methodical patience of men who had done this a hundred times and would do it a hundred more. They opened his lockers. They ran their fingers along the seams of his bunks. One of them took apart the galley stove pipe and shook it over a spread tarpaulin, and ash sifted down like dirty snow, and Isandro thought, with a strange calm, *there goes my whole life, sifted onto a tarp, and none of it is what they want.*

Because the map wasn't there. The map was sewn into the lining of Lior's old coat, hanging on a peg in the forward cabin, directly above the floorboard where Nell lay breathing slow and shallow, and the agents walked past it twice, and both times Isandro's heart performed some ugly gymnastic maneuver that he hoped did not show on his face.

It showed, maybe. Hale was watching his face. That was the trouble with Hale—he didn't watch lockers. He watched people.

"Rough season?" Hale asked, conversationally, standing beside Isandro at the rail while his men worked. Down in the hold, an agent's boot scraped across the very boards above Nell's head.

"Every season's rough."

"Mm." Hale produced a tin of mints, offered one, was refused, took one himself. "You know what I admire about this river, Captain? It forgets everything. Bodies, wrecks, debts, massacres—it takes them all down to the delta and spreads them out flat, and in a year there's nothing left but mud and new growth. Very forgiving geography." He let the mint click against his teeth. "The Company isn't the river, though. The Company remembers. That's the entire value we offer. We are memory, made permanent. Every debt, every claim, every promise—filed, indexed, retrievable." He turned his head slightly. "You understand promises, I think. Your father did."

Something cold slid down Isandro's spine, vertebra by vertebra. "My father's debts died with my father."

"Debts don't die, Captain. They migrate. Interest is a kind of migration." Hale consulted a small notebook, unhurried. "Four hundred and twelve marks, adjusted annually since your father's death. The Company has never pressed it. Do you wonder why?"

"Charity."

"Patience." Hale closed the notebook. "A hook is only useful if the fish believes the water is free. But the fish also believes, somewhere in its small fish heart, that the line will come tight someday. Doesn't it."

In the hold, the bootsteps stopped. Isandro heard the agent call softly to his partner—something about ballast weight, something about *she's riding heavy for her cargo manifest*—and Isandro made himself breathe, made himself smile, made himself be the bored captain down to the marrow.

"She's an old boat. She leaks. She rides however she pleases."

"Hmm." Hale watched him for a long moment, and whatever he saw—or didn't see—he filed away; Isandro could practically hear the drawer sliding shut. Then he raised his voice, mild as milk: "That's time, gentlemen. Anything?"

The agent from the hold emerged, dusting his knees. "Nothing relevant, sir. Personal effects, ship's papers, a dead man's coat—" he nodded at the peg, and Isandro's pulse went white for a half-second— "nothing with writing on it worth reading."

"The coat stays," Hale said, without looking at it. "It smells of the grave."

And then he was done. He gathered his men with a glance, stepped to the gangway, and paused there with one hand on the rail, turning back with that small precise smile.

"A courtesy, Captain, since you've been so cooperative." He reached into his coat and produced a small brass disc, the size of a coin, stamped with the Provision Company's seal—a scale, a river, a crown. He set it on the capstan without ceremony, the way a man sets down a drink he doesn't intend to finish. "Registration token. New policy. Boats operating above Thornmouth are required to carry one. Displayed, it means you're in good standing. Not displayed—" the smile again— "it means you have something to hide, and boats with something to hide get boarded again. And again. And eventually by the wrong sort of boarding party. Keep it visible."

"I don't run above Thornmouth."

"No," Hale agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

He went down the gangway. The launch pulled away into the fog, unhurried, its wake spreading in long gray lines, and Isandro stood at the rail until the sound of its engine faded into the river's older sounds—water on wood, wind in the rigging, the creak of a boat that had carried too much for too long.

* * *

They found the brass disc where Hale had left it, gleaming on the capstan like a small cold moon.

Nell picked it up before anyone could stop her, turning it in her fingers, reading the inscription on the rim aloud: "*Memoria firma.* What's that mean?"

"'Steadfast memory,'" Sefa said quietly. She hadn't touched it. She stood back from it as if it were a snake she'd identified by its markings. "It's a surveyor's motto. Old imperial grant companies used it when they were mapping land they intended to take."

Bram spat over the rail. "They left a spy-token on my deck."

"It's not a spy-token," Isandro said, and then stopped, because he picked it up and weighed it and understood exactly what it was. Brass, yes. But dense. Too dense. He'd handled enough smugglers' hardware in his years on the river to know a sealed compartment when he held one, and this coin had one, and somewhere inside it—listening, recording, remembering—was whatever the Company used instead of ears. "It's worse. It's a listener. Or a tracker. Maybe both."

"So throw it in the river," Nell said.

"And confirm we've got something to hide." Isandro turned the disc over. "He told us to keep it displayed. He wants us to either wear it or refuse it. Either answer tells him something." He looked up at the wheelhouse, where Sefa stood with her arms crossed, and at Bram, who had gone very still in the way Isandro was beginning to recognize as the shape of bad news forming. "We keep it. We display it. And we assume everything said on this deck from today forward belongs to the Company."

Silence, then. The fog was lifting off the water in rags, and the far bank came back into existence tree by tree, and the Kestrel rocked gently at her mooring as if nothing had happened, as if three gray men hadn't walked through her holds and shaken her ashes onto a tarpaulin and left a piece of themselves behind.

"He knew about my father," Isandro said at last. He said it to the river, mostly. "Kept the debt alive all these years. Four hundred marks. He called it patience. Called me a fish." His hand closed around the brass disc until the stamped edges bit his palm. "Twenty years I've kept my head down, paid nobody attention, been nobody. And it turns out being nobody was just another kind of bait."

Sefa came down from the wheelhouse. She stood beside him at the rail, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and looked out at the water the way she looked at charts—as if it might resolve into sense if studied long enough.

"He'll report the boat," she said. "Weight discrepancy, unregistered passengers, a dead prospector's coat. None of it actionable. All of it noted. Hale doesn't strike me as a man who acts—he strikes me as a man who waits for things to become inevitable."

"Then we make sure they don't." Isandro opened his hand. The disc sat in his palm, catching the strengthening light. Upstream, past the bend, past Thornmouth, past the frozen gate he hadn't yet admitted he intended to reach, the river ran high and fast with snowmelt, running toward the sea the way everything ran, the way debts ran, the way promises ran once you made them. "We run the calving window early. Before his paperwork becomes a fleet."

Bram's head came up. Sefa turned. Even Nell went quiet, sensing the shape of the thing before understanding it.

"You said you didn't run above Thornmouth," Bram said carefully.

"I said a lot of things this morning." Isandro dropped the disc back onto the capstan, displayed, obedient, a lie worn openly where Hale's memory could watch it. "Most of them were true yesterday."

Below decks, in the forward cabin, under the floorboard with the loose nail, the map waited in the dark with the patience of everything the Company had ever failed to bury—and Isandro Vane, who had spent twenty years learning how to stay invisible, felt the last of his invisibility slide off him like a coat he no longer needed, and found, to his own surprise, that underneath it he was not afraid.

Or rather: he was afraid, thoroughly and completely, the way a man is afraid in high water—but the fear had changed its shape. It had stopped being the fear of being caught, and become the fear of failing, and those were not the same animal at all. One made you hide. The other made you cast off.

He put his hand on the wheel.

"Get us underway," he said. "Greyford's behind us, and everything ahead of us is upstream."
