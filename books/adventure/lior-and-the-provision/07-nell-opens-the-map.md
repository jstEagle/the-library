## 7. Nell Opens the Map

Nell had learned early that the best hiding places were the ones nobody wanted to look at. Under a floorboard was too obvious. Behind a loose brick, worse. But the space between things—the gap where the world forgot itself—that was where secrets lived. She'd hidden her whole self in such gaps for three years: between crates on the Greyford docks, between the hours when the harbor watch changed, between one name and the next.

So it was no accident that she found the map.

It happened on the fourth day out of Greyford, in the long gray afternoon when the river ran slow and brown and the crew had all gone quiet with the heat. Isandro was at the wheel, shoulders set in that way he had, like a man carrying something heavy just behind his collarbones. Sefa sat at the bow rail with her charts, though Nell had noticed she wasn't really reading them—just moving her finger along coastlines she already knew, the way you might touch an old scar. Bram was below, hammering at something that didn't need hammering, because Bram hammered when his thoughts got loud.

And Nell was supposed to be scrubbing the deck.

She was, technically. She had a brush and a bucket of gray water and a stretch of planking that needed her attention. But the Kestrel's deck was full of gaps, and Nell's hands had a habit of finding them. There was the seam behind the wheelhouse where the caulking had rotted. The hollow under the stair tread where Isandro kept his spare pipe. And there—her favorite discovery of the week—a loose panel beneath the chart locker, warped just enough that its corner lifted like a shy eyelid when you pressed it right.

She hadn't opened it before. That mattered to her. She'd found it two days ago and made herself walk away, because taking things from Isandro felt different than taking things from strangers. Isandro fed her. Isandro had looked at her across the galley table on the first night and said, *You eat like someone who expects the plate to be taken away,* and then he'd put half his own supper on it without another word. You didn't steal from a man like that.

But looking wasn't stealing. Looking was just curiosity, and curiosity wasn't a crime anywhere she'd ever been, whatever the magistrates said.

Nell glanced aft. Isandro's back was turned, a dark shape against the pale sky. Sefa was squinting at her charts. The river murmured against the hull, patient as a confessor.

She pressed the panel's corner. It lifted with a soft wooden sigh.

Inside: a coil of tarred rope, a tin of lamp oil gone thick, and a leather tube, longer than her forearm, capped with brass at both ends. The brass was greened with age, but the caps were tight, and the leather had been oiled recently enough that it still smelled faintly of tallow and something else—something sharp and mineral, like the air off a struck flint.

Nell knew what a map tube was. Every dock rat did. She also knew what it meant that this one was hidden in a place Isandro Vane clearly thought no one would ever look.

Her heart started doing something complicated against her ribs.

*Put it back.* That was the sensible voice, the voice of survival, the voice that had kept her alive through two winters on the Greyford streets. *Put it back and scrub your deck and be nobody.*

But underneath the sensible voice was another one, older and hungrier, the voice that had made her stow away on the Kestrel in the first place instead of signing on to some safe, suffocating life in a laundry or a counting house. That voice said: *You have spent your whole life pressed up against other people's lives like glass against glass. Here is a door.*

She took the tube.

It was heavier than it looked. She carried it low, against her hip, casual as anything, and walked to the far side of the deck where the shadow of the cargo crane fell broad across the planks. Her fingers were suddenly clumsy on the brass cap. It resisted, then gave with a gritty click, and she tipped the tube.

The map slid out into her hands like something alive.

It was not paper, exactly—or it was paper once, but it had been folded and refolded so many times that the creases had become part of the geography themselves, ridges and valleys running through it like the lines of a palm. Grease stains bloomed across it in amber continents. Someone had repaired a tear with a strip of linen glued down so carefully it was nearly invisible. And drawn over all of it, in ink that had faded from black to the color of dried blood, was the Verrow River.

Nell stopped breathing.

She knew the Verrow. Of course she did. She'd grown up on its lower reaches, could draw its muddy loops in her sleep, knew every landing from Saltmarsh to Greyford the way she knew the freckles on her own wrists. And here they were, unmistakable—the wide splay of the delta, the dogleg at Thornmouth, the twin islands below Greyford that the locals called the Sisters because they flooded apart and rejoined every spring.

But the map kept going.

Past Greyford, past the last survey marker Nell had ever seen inked on any official chart, the river continued—drawn in a hand that grew finer and more certain as it went, as if the farther upstream the cartographer traveled, the more the river revealed itself and the more lovingly it was recorded. The tributaries branched like veins. The gorge country appeared in cross-sections, shaded with hundreds of tiny strokes to show the walls closing in. And above it all, near the top edge where the paper had gone soft as cloth from handling, the river rose into mountains rendered so densely they seemed to shimmer—and there, at the very crown of the drawing, a small symbol Nell didn't recognize: a circle with rays around it, like a child's sun, inked and re-inked until the paper beneath had worn thin.

Someone had traced that sun so many times it had nearly gone through the page.

"Nell."

She flinched so hard she nearly dropped it. Sefa stood at the edge of the crane's shadow, her charts forgotten under one arm, her eyes—not angry, not yet, but sharpening—fixed on the map.

"That's not yours."

"I wasn't stealing it," Nell said quickly. "I was—I only looked. I was going to put it back."

Sefa crossed the deck without hurrying. Up close, Nell could see the tiredness carved under her eyes, the salt-chapped skin, everything that the crisp way she held herself tried to hide. She held out her hand. Nell gave her the map, and hated how much it hurt to let go of it, like handing back a window after years in a cellar.

But then Sefa did something unexpected. She didn't fold it away. She stood there in the shade, holding the edges delicately, the way you'd hold a wounded bird, and her breath came out slow through her nose.

"Where did you find this?"

"Under the chart locker. There's a loose panel." Nell winced. "I wasn't snooping for him. I just notice things."

"I know," Sefa said absently. "That's why I asked." She turned the map toward the light, tilting it so the raked sun caught the ink at an angle, and Nell watched something extraordinary happen to her face. The wariness drained out of it. What replaced it was naked, unguarded hunger—the look of a starving woman set before bread.

"Oh," Sefa breathed. "Oh, you beautiful old liar."

"Is it real?" Nell whispered. "I mean—it looks real. But people fake things. I've seen fakes."

"This is not a fake." Sefa's finger hovered above the river's course without touching it, tracing the air an inch above the ink. "Look here. Thornmouth. See how the soundings are written? Not numbers—little crosses, each one weighted by current speed. That's a prospector's notation. They chart by feel, by drag-line, because they can't afford proper instruments. No academy teaches it. No academy would accept it." Her finger moved upstream. "And here—the gorge sections. These hachures are drawn from observation, not projection. Whoever made this *stood* on those walls. Look at the shading on the north faces—he understood the light comes from the south up there, off the ice. He drew the shadows falling the wrong way compared to any textbook convention, because he drew what he *saw*."

"You can tell all that?"

"I spent eleven years learning to tell exactly that," Sefa said, and there was something raw in it, something that sounded almost like grief. "Before they took my seal. Before—" She stopped. Straightened. "This is genuine. This is a working survey, made by hand, over years. Do you understand what that means? Everything past Greyford is blank on every official chart. The Company holds the survey monopoly on the upper Verrow. Nobody is *supposed* to have seen what this man saw."

"Then how did he see it?"

Sefa didn't answer right away. Her thumb had come to rest near the top of the map, beside the little rayed sun, and Nell saw her hesitate there the way you hesitate at a door you're not sure you should knock on.

"There are stories," Sefa said finally. "Dock stories. Drunk stories. A source above the ice. Something the Company seals the valley for—not because there's nothing there, but because there's too much." She laughed shortly, without humor. "I always filed those with the mermaids and the ghost barges."

"And now?"

"Now I'm holding a hand-drawn survey of a river that officially ends forty miles above Greyford," Sefa said quietly, "and the man who drew it was good. Better than good. Some of these notations are techniques I've never seen, and I thought I'd seen everything." She looked up, and her eyes found Nell's with sudden intensity. "Do you know who else has seen this?"

"Just me. And whoever hid it." Nell hesitated. "Captain."

Something moved across Sefa's face—calculation, caution, and underneath both, that hunger again, banked now but burning. "Isandro's been strange since Greyford. Since before Greyford. Carrying weight he won't name." She began, carefully, to refold the map along its ancient creases. "We should wait for him to—"

"No."

The word came out of Nell before she'd decided to say it, hard and clear, and it surprised them both. Sefa raised an eyebrow.

"He'll fold it up and put it back in the hole," Nell said. "I know him. He keeps everything in holes. And then we'll go on downriver forever, hauling cargo and dodging collectors, and nobody will ever—" She stopped, hearing her own voice crack, and pushed on anyway. "I spent three years being nobody's business. I'm done with it. If there's a door, I want to walk through it while it's open."

For a long moment Sefa just looked at her. Then, slowly, she smiled—not the polite, distant smile Nell had seen from her before, but a real one, crooked and tired and lit from inside.

"You know," she said, "they stripped my seal and struck my name from the guild rolls, and everyone said I'd never hold a legitimate chart again." She spread the map flat on top of a cargo crate, weighting the corners with four iron washers from her pocket. "They were right. I never will." Her hands smoothed the old paper flat with a tenderness that made Nell's throat tighten. "But this—this isn't legitimate. This is better."

* * *

By the time Isandro came off the wheel for the evening watch change, the map had become a kind of altar.

Sefa had cleared the crate entirely, laid the chart open under the swaying lantern, and was deep in the survey's upper reaches, muttering measurements, sketching comparisons in the margin of one of her own charts. Bram had come up from below, drawn by the smell of lamp oil and the sight of Sefa actually *excited*, which he'd apparently never witnessed, and now stood at the crate's corner with his big arms crossed, frowning at the mountains as if they'd insulted him. Nell sat cross-legged on the deck where she could see everything, having decided that confession was safer than concealment now that the thing was out in the open.

Isandro stopped at the foot of the wheelhouse stairs. Nell watched him take it in—the lantern, the crate, the map, the three of them arranged around it like moths—and watched something close behind his eyes, a shutter coming down over a window.

"Well," he said. His voice was flat as the lower river. "I see we're having a meeting."

"It was my fault," Nell said, before anyone else could speak. "I found the panel. Sefa just—"

"I know whose fault it is." Isandro came forward slowly. He looked at the map for a long time without touching it, and Nell saw his jaw work once, twice, like a man chewing on words he couldn't afford to swallow or spit out. "I told myself I'd bury it with him if it came to that. Carry it somewhere safe and forget I had it." He exhaled through his nose. "Turns out 'somewhere safe' lasted four days."

"The source," Sefa said. Not a question. "Lior's map to the source."

Isandro's head came up sharply. "You know that name?"

"Everyone knows that name, Captain. As a story." Sefa tapped the rayed sun at the map's crown. "As a *fact*, apparently, known to exactly one dead prospector and one riverboat captain who's been lying to us since Greyford."

The word *lying* landed on the deck between them and lay there. Bram shifted his weight. Nell held her breath.

"I never lied about anything that was any of your business," Isandro said quietly.

"No," Sefa agreed. "You lied about something that's about to get all of us killed, which is worse." But there was no venom in it—only a kind of fierce, exhausted honesty, and Nell realized with a start that this was the first true conversation she'd heard on the Kestrel since Greyford. Everyone else had been performance. This was the boat's real keel showing.

Isandro looked at each of them in turn—at Sefa bent over the map like a woman remembering her own name, at Bram standing rigid with his fists knotted, at Nell, last, longest. And whatever he saw in her face made him close his eyes briefly, the way a man does when he loses an argument with himself.

"He was dying when I found him," Isandro said at last. "On my dock at Miller's Bend. Fevered, starved, half gone. He traded me this map for a promise—to carry it upstream before the calving opens the passage. I told him no." His mouth twisted. "He died anyway, with my hand in his, saying the map was a debt owed to everyone. I buried him on the bank and I took the thing I swore I wouldn't take, and I've been telling myself every day since that I'd find a way to be rid of it."

"Have you?" Bram asked. His voice was rough. "Found a way?"

"No." Isandro looked down at the map, at the little sun with its worn-through paper, and something in his face cracked—just a hairline, just for a second, but Nell saw it, and she thought Sefa saw it too. "Every day I don't burn it, I'm choosing it. I just haven't had the nerve to say the choice out loud."

The river lapped against the hull. Somewhere on the bank, a night bird called, once, and got no answer.

"So say it," Nell said.

Isandro looked at her. In the lantern light his eyes were unreadable, dark water over dark bottom—but he didn't look away, and he didn't laugh, and he didn't send her below decks, and Nell understood that something had just shifted in the geometry of the Kestrel, some load transferring silently from one point to another.

"Upstream," Sefa said softly, her fingers resting at the edge of the mapped world, where the ink ran out and the blank paper began. "Through the gorge. Through the ice. To the place that isn't on any chart but this one."

Nobody spoke. The lantern swung. And Nell felt it rise in her chest, warm and terrifying and brand new—not hope exactly, something more practical than hope, more like a heading—the first spark of a purpose shared by four people on a small boat on a large river, with the whole unmapped world waiting above them.

Isandro reached out and, very gently, closed his hand over the map.

"We vote tomorrow," he said.
