## 8. The Vote Below Decks

The Kestrel lay at anchor in a backwater below Greyford Landing, her hull ticking as the night cooled, her single lantern swaying at the end of its line so that shadows swung across the deck like slow pendulums counting out the hours Isandro wasn't sleeping.

He'd told them at supper. Not all of it—he'd never tell all of it—but enough. The dying man on his dock. The map pressed into his hands with fingers already going cold. The promise he'd made with a nod instead of words, because words would have made it real, and then the promise he'd broken by taking the map anyway when Lior's breath stopped and there was no one left to refuse.

They had gone quiet the way water goes quiet above a drop.

Now it was past midnight, and he found them gathered in the hold without him having called them there—Sefa cross-legged on a cargo crate with her drafting kit open beside her like a surgeon's tray, Bram leaning against the bulkhead with his arms folded and his jaw set, Nell sitting on the deck itself because she said chairs made her feel like she was being watched. The map lay between them under the swing of the lantern light, grease-stained and soft as old cloth from years of being handled by hands that had hoped to die rich and instead died on a stranger's dock.

Isandro came down the ladder slowly, and they watched him come, and none of them spoke until his boots touched the deck.

"You could have told us before Greyford," Bram said. "Before we signed on."

"I didn't know I was going to tell you at all."

"You knew." Sefa's voice was quiet but it had an edge to it, the edge of a blade laid flat against skin. "You've known since the dock. You've been steering around it for two hundred miles like a snag you don't want to name."

He couldn't argue with that. He sat down on the crate opposite Sefa, and the lantern swung, and the shadows swung with it, and for a long moment the only sound was the river moving against the hull—the patient, indifferent sound of water that had carried a thousand boats and would carry a thousand more and cared nothing for what any of them hauled.

"Here's what I know," Isandro said finally. "Lior came down from the high country half-dead, and the only thing keeping him upright was getting that map off his chest before his chest stopped working. He called it a debt owed to everyone. I thought he was raving." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I'm not sure anymore. I read the journals he had tucked in his pack. There are pages missing—torn out, deliberate—and the ones that remain talk about a spring. Not gold. Something else. Something the Provision Company has spent thirty years making sure nobody finds."

"The Company poisons towns," Nell said. It wasn't a question. She'd been the one to find the journal pages first, spread across her knees in the bow like a storybook, reading by firelight while the rest of them pretended not to be listening. "It says so right here. Marrow Bend. Little Ashford. They burned them and called it fever."

"Burned towns leave records," Bram said flatly. "Ashes leave witnesses. If the Company did half of what those pages say, we'd have heard."

"We *have* heard," Sefa said. "We just called it something else. Every surveyor who went upriver and didn't come back. Every mapping expedition the Provision Company bought out and buried. I know three names of men who were paid to lose their notes, because one of them taught me cartography before he drank himself into the Verrow." She looked down at the map, and her hand hovered over it without touching, the way you hover over a flame. "This is Lior's hand. I've seen his early work in the Company archives—before they struck him. The projection style is unmistakable. This is genuine, Isandro. This is the source of the Verrow, drawn by the last man who ever saw it and lived."

"And what happens if we're the next ones?" Bram pushed off the bulkhead and paced the length of the hold—four steps, turn, four steps back, a caged animal's arithmetic. "You want to run upstream. Past Thornmouth, past every checkpoint the Company keeps between here and the ice. With a gunboat already sniffing our wake. For what? A spring?"

"For the truth of it," Sefa said.

"The truth gets people killed. I've seen it." Bram's voice dropped, roughened. "I've *made* it happen."

The hold went still. Nell looked up at him. Isandro kept his eyes on the map, giving the man the mercy of not being watched while he decided how much to say.

Bram stopped pacing. His fists opened and closed at his sides. "I told you I deserted. I didn't tell you why." He swallowed. "There was a village on the Serrano fork. The Company said it was harboring river pirates. We were ordered to burn it and let the river do the rest. There were children, Captain. There were children running for the water, and my sergeant laughed, and I—" His voice cracked and he hated it; Isandro could see him hating it. "I stood there. I didn't fire. But I stood there, and standing there was enough. Standing there is always enough. So don't ask me to sail toward another thing the Company wants burned. Don't ask me to stand there again."

Nobody moved. The lantern swung. Somewhere above them, the anchor chain murmured against the bow roller.

"I'm not asking you to stand anywhere," Isandro said at last. "That's why we're doing this now, in the dark, like cowards. Because whatever we decide, we decide together, and nobody gets dragged."

"That's not true," Nell said.

Every head turned. She was eleven years old and sitting on the deck with her knees pulled up and her chin resting on them, and she looked at each of them in turn with the terrible patience of someone who has been invisible long enough to learn exactly how visible people are.

"You'd drag me," she said. "If you vote to turn south, you'll drag me south, and I'll jump ship at the first landing, and I'll walk back up the towpath alone, and I'll probably die, because I'm eleven and I don't know anything about rivers except what I've learned in two weeks on this boat. So it's not nobody gets dragged. It's just deciding who does the dragging." She shrugged, small and brutal. "I say we go. I say we go *now*, tonight, before Hale's boat comes back."

"Nell—" Isandro started.

"He'll come back," she said, talking over him, which she only ever did when she was frightened, though her voice didn't shake. "The agent with the gray coat. Hale. He marked us. You lied to him and he smiled while you did it, and men like that smile because they already know the ending. If we stay here, we're a boat full of witnesses sitting still. If we run, at least we're a boat full of witnesses running." She unfolded herself and crawled forward on her knees to the map, and put one small finger on the inked line of the upper Verrow where it vanished into the glacier's white nothing. "And besides. Somebody owes this man Lior. He gave you the only thing he had, and you took it, and you can't give it back because he's dead. The only way to pay him is to finish it."

Isandro looked at her finger on the map—at the small, chapped finger of an orphan girl pointing at the edge of the known world—and felt something shift in his chest that he had spent ten years welding shut.

"Sefa," he said. "Your name. Tell them what it's worth."

Sefa's jaw tightened. She closed her drafting kit with a click that sounded loud in the hold. "My name isn't worth anything," she said. "That's rather the point. Twelve years ago I surveyed the middle Verrow for the Company, and my charts were used to site three toll stations and a garrison. I drew beautiful maps and men with rifles used them to decide which villages paid and which villages burned. When I objected, they struck my certification, blacklisted me, and circulated word that Sefa Marun falsifies surveys. Do you know what that means, for a cartographer? It means I am professionally dead. No guild will touch me. No captain will hire me." Her laugh was short and had no humor in it at all. "Except you, and only because you didn't check."

"I checked," Isandro said quietly. "I hired you anyway."

She looked at him then, and something passed between them that neither of them would have named—not in the hold, not with the others watching, maybe not ever—and then she turned back to the map and her voice steadied.

"If I chart the source—if I bring back a verified survey of the headwaters of the Verrow—that's not just a map. That's the discovery of the age. Every guild in the civilized world would fight to certify it. My name would be on it. *Sefa Marun, surveyor.* Not the woman who falsifies charts. The woman who found the source." She shook her head slowly. "I've told myself for twelve years that I don't care about the name. And then I saw this map, and my hands shook, and I learned that I lied."

"So it's greed," Bram said. "All of it. The dead prospector's debt, the girl's adventure, your reputation. Greed wearing different coats."

"It's not greed to want to be whole," Sefa said.

"It is when other people bleed for it."

"And what would you have us do?" Sefa rose to her feet, and the lantern light caught the gray in her dark hair, the lines around her eyes that hadn't been there two weeks ago. "Turn south? Go back to hauling tin and hiding? Hale finds us in a month or a year, and we hand over the map, and he burns it, and the Company goes on poisoning towns, and Bram stands there again, everywhere, forever, because there is nowhere left to stand that isn't next to something the Company is doing?" She pointed at the map. "Or we go up. We go up and we see it with our own eyes, and then—we decide. Maybe Isandro's right. Maybe it should be destroyed. But we'll decide knowing, instead of deciding scared. That's all I'm asking. That's everything I'm asking."

* * *

They argued until the third hour of the morning.

Isandro let them. He'd learned on the river that a crew argues twice—once in anger, once in truth—and you can't get to the second argument without surviving the first. So he sat on his crate and listened while Sefa laid out the calving season, the narrow window in late summer when the glacier's mouth split wide enough for a boat the Kestrel's size, and the months of river between here and there. While Bram counted the Company checkpoints on his scarred fingers: Thornmouth, the fueling station at Corry's Eddy, the ice-line patrol. While Nell, who was supposed to be asleep and knew it and stayed anyway, corrected Bram's count twice, because she had memorized the map in a single evening and remembered everything, always, the way some children remember everything because remembering was the only inheritance they'd ever been given.

At some point Isandro realized he had stopped thinking of them as passengers.

It happened somewhere in the middle of Bram's fourth circuit of the hold, watching the deserter argue geography with a disgraced cartographer while an orphan girl annotated from the floor, and the thought arrived with the quiet force of a keel touching gravel: *these are my people.* Not crew. Not cargo. People. The family he had refused to admit he needed, assembled out of wreckage, arguing by lantern light about whether to sail toward the thing most likely to kill them all.

He almost laughed. He didn't, because it would have come out wrong.

"All right," he said, and the hold went silent. "Here's how it is. I made a promise to a dying man, and then I broke it, and I've been telling myself the breaking was an accident. It wasn't. I wanted the map. I've wanted out from under my father's debt since I was sixteen, and a treasure fell into my lap wearing a dead man's face, and I took it." He stood. His knees ached. Everything ached, lately. "So I don't get a clean vote. Whatever I want is dirty. Which means the rest of you decide, and I'll steer whatever you choose—south to Greyford, north to the ice, or straight into Hale's gunboat if that's what wins. One condition."

"Name it," Sefa said.

"Whatever we choose, we choose all the way. No half-measures, no one waking up in a week wishing they'd voted different and poisoning the boat with it. We vote, and it's done, and we live inside the decision like a hull. Agreed?"

Three nods. Even Nell nodded solemnly, though her eyes were shining.

"Then vote. All in favor of running the river upstream to the source."

Sefa's hand went up immediately, steady as a drawing line. Nell's shot up after, arm straight, chin high, as if she'd been waiting her whole life for someone to ask her the question.

Bram looked at his own hands for a long time.

"I think it's a mistake," he said. "I think we're sailing into the exact shape of the worst thing that ever happened to me, and I think the Company will kill us for it, and I think Lior's debt isn't ours to pay." He exhaled, long and ragged. Then, slowly, like a man lowering a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body, he raised his hand. "But I'm done standing there. Whatever 'there' turns out to be. So—all in favor. God help us."

"Carried," Isandro said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. "Unanimous. We run the river north."

Nell made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and threw herself at him, arms around his waist, and he stood stiff as a mast for a moment before his hand came down—awkwardly, gently—on top of her head. Sefa began rolling the map with the reverence of a priest handling scripture. Bram turned to the ladder, then stopped.

"Captain," he said. "Thornmouth rapids are three days north. If we're running them, we should run them at dawn light, with the water low. Which means we weigh anchor tonight."

Isandro looked at the lantern swinging its slow pendulum. At the map disappearing into its oilcloth. At the three faces turned toward him in the dark—not passengers anymore, not strangers, something with no honest name yet but with weight, with ballast, with the particular gravity of people who had chosen each other in a flooded hold at three in the morning.

"Weigh anchor," Isandro Vane said.

And far downstream, unseen in the black water, a gunboat with gray paint and no lights showed a single spark of a signal lamp toward the shore, and waited for an answer.
