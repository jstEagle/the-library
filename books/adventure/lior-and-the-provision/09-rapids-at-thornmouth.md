## 9. Rapids at Thornmouth

Bram had heard the Thornmouth rapids described a dozen ways in his life, and every one of them was a lie by omission. Men spoke of the noise, mostly—the roar you could hear two miles off, like a mountain clearing its throat. They spoke of the white water, the standing waves, the rocks that showed their teeth only when it was too late to matter. What they never mentioned, what no one ever thought worth saying, was the smell of it: cold stone and crushed spray, a green mineral breath that rose off the churning water and filled your chest before you'd even made the decision to run it.

He smelled it now, standing at the Kestrel's rail with his hands wrapped around a line he wasn't sure he'd be able to hold when it counted, and his mouth went dry as river dust.

"Thornmouth," Isandro said from the wheelhouse, and there was nothing in his voice at all. That was the thing about Vane that Bram had been learning, day by day, mile by mile upriver: the man saved all his feeling for later, banked it like firewood against some winter Bram couldn't see coming. Right now, with the roar growing teeth behind them and Hale's gunboat a gray smudge on the water a half-mile back, Isandro sounded like a man discussing freight rates.

"I've seen it," Sefa said. She stood beside the wheelhouse door with her charts rolled under one arm, her hair tied back so tight it pulled her eyes into slits. "Surveyed the approach in my old life. The channel narrows to forty yards past the split rock, then opens again below the falls shelf. If we keep left of the seam—"

"If we keep left of the seam, we live." Isandro nodded once. "Right of it's the washing machine."

"The what?" Nell asked. She was perched on a crate with her knees drawn up, and she asked it the way she asked everything—hungry for the word, wanting to own it.

"The water goes round and round," Bram said, before he could stop himself. He hadn't meant to speak. He'd spent three weeks on this boat perfecting the art of being furniture. But Nell looked at him with those quick dark eyes, waiting, and the words came out of him the way they used to come out of him when he was a boy, before the army taught him that talking got people killed. "It turns you over and over and holds you down. You don't drown fast in a hole like that. You drown slow."

Nell considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Then we go left."

"Left," Isandro agreed. "Bram, I want you on the bow line. When I call for it, you throw the coil to the cleat on the starboard side and make fast. Sefa, you're my eyes above—I'll need the channel called out loud, because I won't hear myself think in there. Nell—" he paused, and Bram watched him soften by a degree, the way a man softens toward something small and breakable, though Nell would have bitten anyone who said so aloud, "—you go below. Latch yourself in the aft cabin. Hands over your ears if you want. That's not cowardice. That's sense."

"I'm not going below," Nell said.

"Nell."

"You'll need hands. Everyone always needs more hands than they think." She slid off the crate and stood there, small and immovable as a fence post, and Bram felt something twist in his chest that he didn't have a name for. He knew what it was to be ordered into danger. He'd lived inside other men's orders for six years, and the orders had led him to a burning village and a rifle that wouldn't rise to his shoulder. This was different. This girl volunteering herself, this captain letting her—there was a trust in it that made his throat hurt.

Isandro held her gaze a long moment. Then: "You stay behind me. Whatever happens, you stay where I can reach you. That's the price of staying topside."

"That's not a price," Nell said. "That's just sensible."

* * *

The gunboat came on through the morning haze like a rumor getting worse.

Bram saw it first—he'd learned to see ships the hard way, learning which silhouettes carried guns—and he didn't say anything for a full minute because saying it wouldn't change anything. It was Hale's boat; he'd know that wedge-shaped bow anywhere now, the way it cut the water without hurry, without doubt. The Provision Company didn't chase. They followed. They followed the way winter follows autumn, certain that everything green would come to them eventually.

"They're holding back," Sefa observed, glass raised. "Hale knows the rapids. He's letting us go in first."

"He wants us to clear the channel for him," Isandro said, "or die showing him where not to go. Either way he wins." He turned the wheel a spoke. "So we don't die, and we don't show him anything. Bram—bow line."

Bram moved. His body remembered how to move when his mind wanted to stand still; the army had given him that much, at least, had burned obedience into his muscles so deep that even after he'd deserted, even after the shame had eaten him hollow, his hands still knew how to work. He coiled the line, checked the cleat, checked it again. The Kestrel's deck shuddered beneath his boots as the current began to take hold—not gently, not like the lower river's slow brown pull, but like a hand closing around the hull.

The banks changed. That was the thing that frightened him most, he realized: the banks. On the lower Verrow the shores had been lazy with willow and mud, forgiving, wide enough to lose yourself in. Here the gorge walls rose sheer and gray, streaked with old flood-marks, and the river between them had gone the color of hammered tin. It moved fast. It moved like it had somewhere to be and no patience for anything in its way.

"Half a mile," Sefa called. "Split rock dead ahead. Left of the seam, Isandro. Left."

"I heard you the first time."

"You'll hear me again."

The roar swallowed whatever Isandro answered. And then they were in it.

Bram had been in artillery barrages. He had knelt in frozen mud while shells walked across a hillside toward him, close enough to feel the ground punch the air out of his lungs, and he had told himself afterward that nothing on any river could frighten him the way that had. He had been wrong. Artillery was chaos, but it was chaos aimed elsewhere; you were an accident of its attention. The Thornmouth rapids were not chaos. The Thornmouth was purposeful. Every wave stood up like it had been waiting for the Kestrel specifically, every rock wore its foam like a hood drawn back to watch them pass, and the noise—God, the noise—it came up through the deck plates and into his bones until his ribs buzzed with it.

"LEFT!" Sefa screamed, both arms out. "SEAM'S MOVING—LEFT, HARD LEFT!"

The wheel spun. The Kestrel heeled, and Bram threw himself flat as a wall of spray came over the bow, cold enough to make his skull ache. Through the white blindness he heard the engine scream—a high, ragged note he'd never heard it make—and felt the whole boat slew sideways, stern swinging, and understood with a clarity that had nothing to do with thinking that they were going over.

Not might. Going.

The starboard rail dipped toward the water. Cargo shifted below with a groan like a dying animal. Nell—where was Nell—she was supposed to be behind him, behind Isandro—

She was at the cargo netting, both hands buried in the rope, feet braced, and the river had her hair streaming straight out behind her head like a black banner. Her eyes found his across the tilting deck. She didn't scream. She just looked at him, and in her face was a question he'd spent years avoiding: *what are you going to do?*

Bram did not think. Thinking was for men who had time.

He crossed the deck in four strides as the rail kissed the water. The river poured green and freezing across the planks around his knees. He got one arm through the netting beside Nell, hooked the other around her middle, and threw his weight backward—away from the water, away from the drop, back toward the high side that was already becoming the low side. The world tilted. The sky swung. For one endless second the Kestrel hung in the balance like a coin someone had flipped, and Bram hung with it, Nell's heart hammering against his forearm faster than any heart should go, and he heard himself shouting words he didn't remember deciding to shout:

"*PORT! PORT, NOW, ALL OF IT!*"

Whether Isandro heard him or whether Isandro had already seen it himself, Bram never learned. But the engine note changed—dropped, caught, roared—and the wheel must have gone hard over because the Kestrel shuddered, wallowed, and then, with the reluctance of a great animal changing its mind, began to roll back. The starboard rail climbed. Water sheeted off the deck in cataracts. The boat righted itself by degrees, groaning the whole way, and slammed down into the trough beyond the seam with a impact that threw everyone still standing off their feet.

Bram landed on top of Nell, cushioning her with his arms, taking the deck plate on his shoulder hard enough to numb it to the wrist.

For a moment nobody moved. The roar continued around them, enormous, indifferent. They were through the seam. Ahead, impossibly, the channel opened—forty yards of gray violence narrowing to a throat and then spilling out into a long, tumbling stretch where the river, having made its point, let them go.

"STARBOARD CLEAT!" Isandro's voice, raw now, cracked open at last. "BRAM!"

Bram hauled himself up. His shoulder screamed. He grabbed the coil, threw it, missed, threw again, and the loop settled over the cleat on the second try. He made it fast with fingers he couldn't feel, and then he simply stood there, soaked to the skin, watching the gorge walls slide past, listening to the sound of his own breathing come back to him in pieces.

They ran the rest of it in silence. There was nothing to say that the river wasn't already saying better.

* * *

Below the falls shelf, the Verrow widened and slowed, and the Thornmouth's roar faded behind them into a long grumble, like a giant muttering in his sleep. Half their cargo was gone—crates of salt pork, two barrels of nails, the entire consignment of dried apples that Nell had guarded like treasure since Greyford. It lay scattered along the seam rock or spinning away downstream, and no one suggested going back for it. Some prices you paid without counting.

They anchored in an eddy beneath a cliff furred with pine, and for a long time the four of them simply worked. Work was mercy. Bram bailed. Sefa wrung out the charts and laid them to dry on the cabin roof, weighted with stones. Nell inventoried what remained, calling out losses in a voice that wobbled only slightly. Isandro disappeared into the engine room and emerged an hour later black to the elbows, announcing that the impeller had survived, barely, and that the Kestrel had taken water in places water had no business being.

"She'll hold," he said, and then, quieter, almost to himself: "She held."

They ate cold pork on the foredeck as the light went amber and long. Nobody spoke about the rapids. Everybody was speaking about the rapids, in the glances they traded, in the way Nell sat closer to Bram than she needed to, in the way Sefa kept touching Isandro's sleeve as if confirming he was solid.

It was Nell who broke it, finally, around a mouthful of bread. "Bram shouted port."

"I did," Bram said.

"Nobody told him to."

"No," Isandro said. He was looking at Bram across the little circle of lamplight, and his face had done something it rarely did—opened. "No, nobody did."

Bram stared at his hands. They were steady now, which surprised him. During the war his hands had shaken for days after every engagement, and the doctors had said it was nerves and the chaplain had said it was sin, and neither of them had known what they were talking about. His hands were steady because—for the first time since the village, since the smoke and the screaming and the order he refused—his hands had done exactly what they were for. Not killing. Holding. Holding a line, holding a girl, holding a boat together with rope and weight and stubbornness.

"I heard her," he said finally. "In the water. I heard her looking at me." He shook his head, embarrassed. "That's not—I know how that sounds."

"It doesn't sound like anything," Sefa said gently. "It sounds true."

Isandro leaned forward. "You want to tell me why a man who moves like you do—fast, sure, no wasted motion—spent three weeks on my deck trying to be invisible?"

The question hung there. Below them the river chuckled against the hull. Somewhere upstream, faint as memory, the Thornmouth still grumbled.

Bram looked at each of them in turn. The girl who'd trusted him with her whole small weight. The woman who'd named the truth of him without flinching. The captain who'd steered them through a mile of white death and then asked an honest question like it was the most natural thing in the world.

He thought about the village. He thought about the order, and the rifle, and the morning he'd walked away from all of it carrying nothing but his name—which wasn't even his name anymore. He thought: *they will use this. Anyone could use this.* And then he thought about Hale's gunboat, patient on the gray water, following the way winter follows autumn, and understood that they were already being used, hunted, marked—that silence had stopped protecting him the moment he'd stepped aboard the Kestrel.

"My name isn't Bram," he said. "And I deserted from the Provision Company's colonial regiment four years ago." The words came out of him steadily, like water finding its level. "There was a village called Ashen Ford. We were ordered to burn it. I wouldn't. I've been running downriver ever since, doing labor, keeping quiet, trying to be furniture." He looked up. "I'm done being furniture."

Nell reached over and put her small hand on his big one, right there on his knee, as casually as if she did it every day.

"We figured it was something like that," she said. "Took you long enough."

Isandro laughed—a short, rough sound, surprised out of him—and clapped Bram on the good shoulder. "Ashen Ford stays on this boat. That's the whole of it. Past that, you're Bram, you're crew, and tomorrow you're on the bow line again, because I've never seen anyone throw a coil like that with a dead arm."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then she unrolled one of the drying charts and turned it toward the lamplight. "Then you'll want to see this," she said. "Before we anchor for the night." Her finger traced the river ahead, up through the widening valley, to a place where her careful ink lines simply stopped—where the survey ended in a blank space and a single notation in an old clerk's hand: *Provision Company lands. Survey closed by order.*

"And past that?" Nell asked.

Sefa's jaw tightened. "Past that is where the map begins. And look—" she pointed astern, downstream, toward the darkening water they'd come from.

Far back down the long reach, a light had appeared. One light, riding low, moving with the unhurried certainty of something that had all night and all season. The gunboat, through the dusk, running the Thornmouth after all.

Hale had cleared the rapids. And he was still coming.
