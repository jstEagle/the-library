## 10. Hale's Offer

The fueling station at Marrow Bend was less a station than a wound in the riverbank—a scar of gray pilings and rusted cranes where the Provision Company had once dredged coal from the shallows and abandoned the effort when the seam ran thin. Now it sold diesel, rope, tinned meat, and silence, in that order. The Kestrel came alongside just past noon, her hull riding low on the last of their water, her engine coughing like an old man climbing stairs.

Isandro felt the change before he saw it. The dockhands—three men who normally moved with the boneless indifference of the underpaid—were standing straighter than they should have been. Watching him moor. Not helping.

He tied off anyway, because a man who stopped tying off his own lines was a man advertising fear, and Isandro Vane had spent eleven years making sure no one could read anything off him but boredom.

"Keep the crew aboard," he said to Bram over his shoulder. Low. "All three."

Bram's jaw worked. "You're not going alone."

"I am." He met the deserter's eyes, and something passed between them that neither would have named a month ago. "If I'm not back in an hour, cast off and run Thornmouth backward if you have to."

"You'd never make it through Thornmouth backward," Sefa said quietly from the wheelhouse door. She had heard everything, of course. She always did. Her hands were folded, her face composed into the particular stillness she wore when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "So don't be late."

Isandro almost smiled. Almost. Then he stepped down onto the planks of Marrow Bend, and the smell of the place rose up to meet him—diesel and creosote and the cold mineral breath of the river coming down from the high country, carrying, faintly, a sweetness he had learned to dread. Ice melt. They were closer to the glacier now than any honest cargo run required.

Corvus Hale was waiting for him under the crane.

Not approaching. Waiting. That was the thing about Hale that set him apart from every other Company man Isandro had ever dealt with: he never hurried. Collectors hurried. Enforcers hurried. Hale simply arranged himself in a location and allowed the world to come to him, the way a spider doesn't chase so much as maintain.

He stood in a coat too fine for the mud, charcoal wool buttoned to the throat despite the mild day, his hat brim casting a clean line of shadow across his eyes. Beside him, two of his men leaned against a stack of fuel drums with the studied looseness of men who wanted you to notice they were armed without making it a threat. A third man sat on the bollard nearest the Kestrel's line, close enough to touch the rope, far enough away to claim he hadn't.

Isandro walked up and stopped six feet out. Close enough for conversation. Far enough for dignity.

"Hale."

"Vane." Hale's voice was pleasant, unhurried, almost warm—the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "You're burning more fuel than your manifest suggests. Running heavy, are we? Passengers?"

"Cargo."

"Mm." Hale turned his head slightly, and Isandro followed the glance to the Kestrel, where Nell's small face was pressed against the wheelhouse glass before vanishing. Something flickered at the corner of Hale's mouth—not quite a smile. An inventory. "Cargo that watches."

"What do you want?"

"Straight to business. Good. I've always respected that about you." Hale took a slow turn along the dock, hands clasped behind his back, boots finding the dry boards with unerring precision while his men splashed through the puddles without noticing. "Do you know what I admire most about you, Vane? You're a careful man. Careful men are rare. Most people want things so badly they leak wanting all over everything. But you—" he stopped, faced Isandro fully, "—you've spent your whole life wanting nothing. It's remarkable. It's also, I'll admit, inconvenient."

"For whom?"

"For me, currently." Hale produced a cigarette case, opened it, did not take one, closed it again. The gesture had the quality of ritual, or rehearsal. "Your father owed the Provision Company four hundred marks when he died. Interest has carried it to a sum I won't insult us both by naming aloud. Eleven years you've paid the minimum, kept your head down, kept your boat moving, kept yourself invisible." He said *invisible* the way another man might say *cowardly*, gently, as though offering a diagnosis rather than an accusation. "And then, this spring, a prospector named Lior Ash falls onto your dock at Greyford, half-dead, and dies aboard your boat with a document in his possession that my employers have spent thirty years and considerable blood trying to recover."

The river slapped against the pilings. Somewhere behind the crane, one of the dockhands shifted his weight, and the boards creaked.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Isandro said.

"No," Hale agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. That's what makes you good at this." He began walking again, slowly, and Isandro fell into step beside him without deciding to—an old habit, following the money, keeping the conversation moving so it couldn't settle around his neck. "Here's what I find interesting, though. Lior Ash came downriver from the north. He passed through four landings. At each one, he asked after boats heading upstream. At each one, he was refused. And then he reached Greyford, and a man with debts and a fast boat and a reputation for minding his own business took him aboard, and three days later that man is running the Verrow against the current, in calving season, with a cartographer whose license was revoked for falsifying surveys, a soldier who deserted his post, and a child."

The word *child* landed harder than the rest. Isandro kept his face still, but inside, something cold slid into place.

"They're drifters," he said. "I take work where I find it."

"Of course you do." Hale stopped at the end of the dock, where the river widened and the current ran silver-bright over the drowned foundations of the old coal works. He looked out at the water with something that might, in a lesser man, have been called wistfulness. "Let me tell you a story, Captain. There was a town upriver once. Call it a company town—Company-built, Company-run, Company-fed. Two hundred souls, give or take. And one summer, a survey team found something in the high valley, and the town became inconvenient. Not hostile. Inconvenient. Too many witnesses, too many mouths that might talk, too many people who might go looking for what their neighbors had seen." He paused. "The Company didn't burn it. Burning leaves evidence. The Company bought the grain contracts, and the winter did the rest. By spring there were forty-one survivors, and they signed relocation papers and were scattered across three provinces, and the maps were amended, and today if you ask after that town, official records will tell you it never existed."

Isandro said nothing. His heart was beating hard and slow, the way it did before rapids.

"The man who negotiated those grain contracts," Hale continued, "was promoted. He's retired now, on a lake, with grandchildren. A decent man, by every account. He never ordered anyone killed. He never touched a weapon. He simply understood that some knowledge is a load-bearing wall, and you cannot pull it out without the whole structure coming down on everyone." He turned back to Isandro, and for the first time his pleasantness dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was worse—patient, reasonable, almost kind. "I am not a cruel man, Captain. I want you to understand that. Cruelty is inefficient. I'm offering you efficiency."

"Which is?"

Hale reached into his coat and drew out a single sheet of paper, folded once, and held it between two fingers without extending it.

"A full discharge. Four hundred marks and eleven years of interest, struck from the ledger. Your father's name cleared. The lien on the Kestrel released—she becomes yours outright, free and clean, first boat you've ever owned without the Company's hand in her hold. And a stipend, modest but real, enough to refit her properly, hire a proper crew, run honest cargo for the rest of your life." He let the paper hang there in the light. "In exchange for a piece of stained cloth that means nothing to anyone who isn't willing to die for it."

The number did its work. That was the obscene part, Isandro thought later—that it worked at all. Eleven years of waking before dawn to check the water level, eleven years of flinching at Company letterhead, eleven years of watching collectors board his boat and handle his things with the casual ownership of men who believed, correctly, that everything he had belonged to them in fact if not yet in law. Eleven years of being *theirs*, and here was a piece of paper that would make him free, and all it cost was a dead man's map and a promise made to a dying stranger who had no one else in the world.

*Carry it upstream,* Lior had whispered. *Before the ice opens.*

But Lior was dead. Dead men didn't collect debts. And the Kestrel was leaking, and Nell needed shoes that fit, and Sefa deserved a chart table that wasn't a plank on two crates, and Bram—

Bram would leave. Bram had voted against the source from the start. Bram would take his pay and walk into the hills and Isandro would never see him again, which might even be a kindness.

Four hundred marks. Eleven years. One lie to a corpse.

"How do I know you'd honor it?" Isandro heard himself say. His voice sounded distant, level, a pilot's voice reading water he didn't trust.

Hale's expression softened into something like approval. "Because I've never broken a contract in nineteen years of service. Ask anyone downriver. Corvus Hale is many things, but he pays what he promises." He extended the paper at last, holding it out across the space between them, and the wind off the river stirred its corner. "Take it. Read it. Have your cartographer review it—she'll confirm the terms are exactly as stated. I don't hide knives in contracts, Captain. Knives are for people who need them. Contracts are for people like us."

Isandro reached out.

His fingers were an inch from the paper when Hale spoke again, idly, the way a man comments on weather, and the words went through Isandro like cold water through a hull seam.

"There is, of course, the matter of your passengers. The Company's position is that anyone who has seen the document must be... debriefed. Thoroughly." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "The girl especially. Children repeat things. Teachers ask questions. It's nothing personal—it's hygiene."

The wind came down the river, smelling of ice.

Isandro's hand stopped.

It didn't retreat. It simply stopped, hanging in the air between them, and in that suspended inch he saw it all with terrible clarity: the discharge paper, the clean boat, the refit, the honest runs—and beneath it, like ballast below the waterline, the shape of a small grave somewhere upriver, dug by efficient men in good coats, for a girl who stowed away among cargo crates because anywhere was better than nowhere. And beside it another grave for a woman who'd lost her name and would lose her life for a survey she never made. And another for a deserter who'd already run from one massacre and would not run from a second.

And Hale watching him, patient, pleasant, already composing the report.

"You'd kill a child," Isandro said. Not loudly. The words came out flat and precise, each one placed like a foot on wet rock.

Hale tilted his head, genuinely puzzled by the objection. "I'd *prevent* a child from becoming a vector for catastrophe. There's a difference, though I understand it's a subtle one." He sighed, and for a moment—just a moment—something almost human crossed his face, something tired. "Captain, I've watched men drown in that girl's position before. It's never pleasant. Which is why I'm offering you this alternative *first*. Most men in my position wouldn't bother. Do you understand what a courtesy this is?"

"Yes," Isandro said quietly. "I understand exactly what a courtesy this is."

He withdrew his hand.

The motion was small—six inches, maybe less—but Hale read it instantly, and the pleasantness returned to his face like water closing over a stone, complete and unbroken, as though the offer had never been refused because it had never truly been made.

"Naturally," Hale said, "you'll want time to consider. These decisions shouldn't be rushed." He folded the paper away into his coat with the same unhurried care he'd used to produce it. "I'll be at the station office until evening. After that, I'm afraid, the offer expires—not out of malice, but because my orders require me to proceed upstream regardless, and the Company's methods become considerably less negotiable once the ice opens." He adjusted his hat brim against the light. "You're a careful man, Vane. Be careful *well*. There's a difference between caution and delay, and only one of them keeps a family intact."

The word *family* in Hale's mouth was a knife wrapped in silk, and they both knew it.

Isandro turned and walked back down the dock. His legs felt strange, too light, as though the debt he'd carried for eleven years had briefly been lifted and then returned double-weighted. Behind him he could hear Hale's men shifting, the creak of the bollard where the third man sat near the Kestrel's line, and he made himself walk at the same unhurried pace all the way to his gangplank, because a captain who runs advertises guilt, and a captain who strolls advertises nothing at all.

Sefa was waiting at the top. She searched his face, and whatever she found there made her step aside without a word and follow him into the wheelhouse, pulling the door shut against the wind.

"Well?" she said.

"He offered me everything." Isandro braced both hands on the wheel, staring out at the gray water, at the pale line of the high country where the glacier waited. "Debt gone. Boat clear. Money to refit. All of it. Everything I've wanted for eleven years, on one sheet of paper."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. "And?"

"And then he told me how they'd handle the witnesses." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Efficiently. Hygienically. Starting with Nell."

The silence in the wheelhouse had weight, like deep water.

"He talked about killing a nine-year-old girl the way I'd talk about scrubbing barnacles," Isandro said. "No anger in it. No cruelty, even. Just... housekeeping." He laughed, short and joyless, and it sounded wrong in the small space. "Eleven years I've hated that debt. I'd have sold a lot for a piece of paper with my father's name struck off it. And he put it right in front of me, Sefa. Right in front of me. My hand was out."

"But?"

"But I kept seeing her shoes." He shook his head. "Nell's shoes. Two sizes too big, stuffed with rags at the toes. She stuffs them every morning so nobody hears her coming, so she can move quiet, like she learned to do somewhere before us." He stared at the wheel, at his own knuckles white around the spokes. "That's what eleven years of debt taught me, Sefa. How to move quiet. How to survive by staying small. And I nearly traded a kid who does the same thing for the chance to stop doing it myself."

Sefa reached out and put her hand over his on the wheel. Her fingers were cold from the wind, ink-stained at the edges, steady.

"So what do we do?" she asked. "He gave you until evening."

Isandro looked out the wheelhouse window. Down on the dock, Hale had rejoined his men, and the three of them stood together in the weak light, unhurried, patient, maintaining. The man on the bollard was still within reach of the Kestrel's line. Beyond them, upriver, the water ran bright and cold toward the frozen gate, and somewhere past it lay a spring that drove men mad with wanting, and a promise made to a dying man, and whatever truth the Company had poisoned towns to bury.

"We cast off," Isandro said. "Now. Before evening. Before he decides patience is wasted on me."

"He'll follow."

"He'll follow." Isandro was already moving, throwing open the throttle housing, checking the fuel gauge, the water line, the engine temperature—his hands finding their work the way they always did when his mind wanted to come apart. "Then we give him a river worth following. Wake Bram. Tell Nell to stay below and stay quiet. Tell her—" he hesitated, and the hesitation cost him something, "—tell her nothing. She's a child. Let her be a child for one more hour."

Sefa squeezed his hand once and left.

Isandro stood alone in the wheelhouse a moment longer, looking at the spot on the dock where Corvus Hale had stood, and understanding, finally, what Lior had known in his bones at the end: that the map was never treasure. It was a test. Not of whether Isandro Vane could get rich—but of what he was willing to become to stay safe.

He had almost failed it. His hand had been out. It would be a long time before he forgave himself for the inch.

The Kestrel's engine caught on the second try, coughing, then settling into its old laboring rhythm, and Isandro swung her bow upstream into the current as the afternoon light went thin and gold over the water. Behind them, Marrow Bend receded, and on its dock a figure in a charcoal coat raised one hand—not waving, Isandro noted. Checking a watch.

Hale had seen them go. Hale had expected nothing else.

And far ahead, past the last honest bend of the Verrow, the glacier waited with its mouth closed, counting down to the only days when it would ever open—and somewhere in the ice, Isandro knew now, other things were waiting too. Things the Company had buried. Things that had driven Lior Ash down a dying river with a map and a prayer.

The current pulled against the hull, and the Kestrel pushed through it, and Isandro Vane stood at the wheel with a clean conscience and a dirty soul, running toward the one thing in the world the Provision Company feared more than exposure:

A witness who refuses to sell.
