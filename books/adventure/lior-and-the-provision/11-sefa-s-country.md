## 11. Sefa's Country

The maps ended on the third morning past Thornmouth.

Sefa felt it before she saw it — a kind of silence in her hands, the way a musician feels a string go slack. She had been charting since dawn, working at the little folding table Isandro had rigged for her beneath the Kestrel's awning, and her pencil had been moving in long, confident strokes: bend, shoal, bluff, bend again. Then she reached for the survey folio to confirm her position against the Company charts, and there was nothing left to confirm against. The last printed sheet ended at a place called Weir's Rest, a fueling station that had burned down eleven years ago, and beyond it the paper was blank except for a faded stamp: TERRA INCOGNITA — SURVEY SUSPENDED BY ORDER OF THE PROVISION COMPANY.

Suspended. That was the word they used. As if the river itself had been paused mid-sentence, like a man interrupted.

She sat back and looked up, and the country looked back.

It was not empty country — that was the first lie of the blank page. The Verrow ran broad and green-gray here, sliding between hills that wore their forests like old coats, too heavy for their shoulders. Mist stood in the hollows. Somewhere upstream a loon called, that sound like grief remembering itself. There were no markers, no cut banks blazed with Company paint, no mile posts. Just water, and willow, and the enormous indifferent sky going pale at its eastern edge.

"End of the world," Bram said from the cargo deck, where he was re-lashing crates that didn't need re-lashing. He said it lightly, but his hands didn't.

"No," Sefa heard herself say, and her voice surprised her — steady, almost warm. "Beginning of mine."

Nell looked up from where she sat cross-legged on a crate, mending a net with the fierce concentration of someone who had learned every skill by watching. "Yours?"

"Nobody's ever charted this reach properly." Sefa smoothed the blank margin of her own paper. "The Company suspended surveys after the source was sealed. Everything above Weir's Rest is rumor and dead men's notes." She tapped the pencil against her lip. "Which means everything I draw from here on is the first drawing."

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. She hadn't meant to feel it either — that slow lift under her sternum, like a boat coming off its moorings. For six years she had been a woman who used to be a cartographer, the way a ruin is a house. Disgraced over a boundary line she hadn't fudged but hadn't fought hard enough to defend; stripped of her credentials; hired and fired and hired again by men who liked to say *of course you understand we can't put your name on anything*. She had learned to make herself small on other people's maps.

But here, past the last inked contour, smallness was impossible. Here the country did not know she'd fallen. It only knew whether she could read it.

Isandro came up from the engine well wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white, and she watched him take in the scene — Nell's question hanging, Bram's knuckles tight on the lashing, her own face doing something she couldn't control. He didn't ask what had passed between them. He just looked at the water ahead, then at her open chart, and something moved through his expression like current under ice.

"How far does your paper go?" he asked.

"It doesn't." She turned the folio toward him, the blank sheet facing out like an open palm. "From here, I make the paper go."

He held her eyes a moment longer than the question required. Then he nodded, once, and went forward to take the wheel, and Sefa discovered she was smiling at a blank page like a girl.

* * *

They ran through the afternoon into country that grew stranger and more beautiful by the mile. The hills closed in, then opened onto a valley so wide the far side was a blue suggestion. The water shallowed and braided, and Isandro threaded the channels with a concentration Sefa found herself studying when she should have been studying the banks — the small economy of his hands on the wheel, the way he listened to the river with his whole body, head tilted, as though the water were speaking a language he'd half forgotten and was ashamed of how much he still loved.

She charted until her fingers ached. Bend after bend, island after island, each fix taken twice, each sounding logged. It was the work she had been made for and kept from, and it fit her hands like a tool returned after long borrowing.

Toward dusk the wind died and the river went mirror-still, and Isandro called for anchorage in a willow-choked slough off the north bank. They ate beans and smoked fish and Nell's flatbread, which was improving, and Bram told a story about a mule he had known in the army that was smarter than his entire command, and for an hour the Kestrel was simply a warm lit box floating in the dark, full of voices.

Then the others turned in, and Sefa took the midnight watch, because she had asked for it.

* * *

The night was enormous. No moon worth naming — a thin paring of light low in the west — and so the stars came out the way they only come out far from towns: all of them, in their thousands, in ranks and rivers, the Milky Way arched overhead like the spine of some vast pale animal. Down on the water the stars doubled themselves, so that the Kestrel seemed to float not on a river but on the space between two skies.

Sefa sat in the stern with her sextant across her knees and worked.

This was the part of the craft no one taught anymore, the part the Company's steam-driven survey launches had made obsolete: finding yourself by the sky alone. She took Polaris, corrected, reduced. She shot Altair and Deneb and the slow wheel of the northern crown, and with each sightline she felt something in her chest unknotting that had been tied for years. The stars did not care about her disgrace. The stars had never read the inquiry findings. They hung where they hung, patient and exact, and if she was honest with them they would tell her exactly where she was — which was more than any man on any committee had ever done.

"You're smiling at the sky," Isandro said quietly, from the shadow by the wheelhouse. "That's either madness or religion."

She hadn't heard him come out. He lowered himself onto the bench beside her, leaving a careful handspan of space, a mug of chicory steaming in the cold. He handed it to her without being asked.

"I'm navigating," she said. "Properly. By sextant and star table, the way they taught me before the Company decided instruments were cheaper than skill." She took a sip. The chicory was terrible and hot and perfect. "Do you know what they do now? Trilateration from fixed shore stations. Wire signals. A man sits in an office in Corvane and knows where his boats are to the yard, and nobody aboard needs to know anything at all." She looked up. "I spent six years being unnecessary on purpose. Being made unnecessary. And now—" she gestured at the whole burning vault of the night, "—now I'm the only person on this river who can find us in the dark."

"And how does that sit?"

She considered lying, or deflecting, which were the two currencies she'd traded in for years. But the night was too big for small change.

"Like putting weight down," she said. "You know how you carry something so long you forget it's heavy? And then someone takes it, and your arm floats up?"

Isandro huffed — not quite a laugh. "I know the feeling from the other direction. Weight keeps arriving."

"Your father's debt."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, maybe, that she knew; or maybe just the old pain recognizing its name. "Bram talks."

"Bram worries," Sefa corrected. "There's a difference. He worries about you the way Nell worries about all of us — loudly, at night, when he thinks no one hears him pacing."

Isandro looked out at the black water. A fish rose somewhere upstream, a soft circular kiss on the surface, rings spreading toward the boat and dissolving.

"My father bought the Kestrel's hull with borrowed money," he said at last. "Then borrowed against her, then borrowed again. When he drowned, the debt didn't drown with him. Provision bought the paper within the month. They've owned a piece of me ever since — my cargo, my routes, my name on their ledgers." His jaw worked. "A man can live a long time owing money. What wears you down is owing *permission*. Every decision goes through their office first. You start asking permission to exist."

"Yes," Sefa said. Simply. Because she understood it exactly — different ledger, same chain. "And you've never told anyone that. Not in those words."

"Have you ever told anyone about the inquiry?" he returned. "What actually happened with the Halvern boundary?"

The name landed like a stone in still water. Sefa stared at him.

"How do you know that name?"

"I read. You talk in your sleep — no," he raised a hand, "you don't, actually, that was Bram speculating and I'm telling you now he's wrong. You mutter coordinates. And one night in Greyford, before you came aboard, I heard you arguing with a harbormaster about a survey mark. You said, 'Halvern's line was drawn from a false meridian and everyone signing it knew.' Then you saw me listening and stopped." He shrugged. "I remember things. It's most of what I'm good for."

Sefa set the mug down slowly. The stars wheeled, indifferent and kind.

"The Halvern commission," she said, and her voice came out lower than she intended. "Six years ago. Two provinces, one river border, and a mineral concession waiting on the outcome. I was the junior surveyor. I found that the original meridian had been established from a station that had settled — four minutes of arc, no more. Four minutes. But four minutes put a silver seam on one side of the line instead of the other, and there were men in Corvane who had already sold the silver." She wrapped her hands around the mug. "I filed a correction. My director thanked me, praised my rigor, and filed it in a drawer. When the concession was granted anyway, I went over his head. In writing. With proofs." She laughed, short and bitter. "You know what happens then. Not scandal. *Procedure.* An inquiry into my conduct. Questions about my temperament, my thoroughness, my — objectivity. A woman, objecting. The finding was 'no actionable error,' which is what they call a lie when they want it to sound like weather. My certification was suspended pending review. The review never concluded. It's still pending. I expect it will be pending until I die."

The river slid past in the dark, carrying its cargo of stars.

"They didn't destroy you because you were wrong," Isandro said quietly.

"No. They destroyed me because I was right, and right was expensive." She looked at him. "Why are we saying these things to each other?"

"Because it's three in the morning," he said, "and the stars don't repeat themselves, and neither do we."

* * *

They sat with that awhile. It was, Sefa thought, the first honest silence she had shared with another adult in years — not the loaded silences of people deciding what not to say, but the easy quiet of people who had said the thing and found the world still standing.

Above the eastern hills, the first suggestion of dawn was a gray thread, hours away yet. The cold deepened. Without quite deciding to, Sefa shifted along the bench until her shoulder rested against his. He didn't move away. He didn't move closer, either — just let the contact be true, the way a bearing is true.

"In Corvane," she said, "there was a man. After the inquiry. He was kind, and I was lonely, and I spent two years pretending that was enough." She felt rather than saw Isandro go carefully still. "I'm not telling you that to warn you off. I'm telling you because I've decided I'm done pretending things are enough."

"That sounds like a dangerous decision, on a boat this size."

"It is. Everything true is." She turned her head and found him already looking at her, his face half starlight and half shadow, and in his eyes none of the weary guardedness she'd catalogued the day she boarded — just a man, tired and attentive, meeting her without armor. "You could tell me to mind my station, Captain."

"Sefa." Her name in his mouth sounded like a place he'd been looking for. "I've spent ten years keeping everyone at oar's length because everyone I've let close has cost me. Debt, grief, one way or another." He exhaled. "And then I filled my boat with strays anyway, and I keep catching myself glad. It's the most reckless thing I've ever done, and I once ran the Kestrel through Thornmouth with a hole in her bilge."

"You did *what*?"

"Later. It's a good story and it requires brandy." The corner of his mouth lifted. "My point is — I'm not good at this. I'll be bad at it. I'll steer away when I shouldn't."

"Noted," Sefa said. "I'll chart around it. It's what I do."

And then, because the night was ending somewhere behind the hills and neither of them would get this exact darkness again, she leaned in and kissed him — or he kissed her; afterward they would disagree, amiably, for years. It was brief and salt-and-chicory flavored and slightly cold at the edges, and when it ended she kept her forehead against his and listened to the river.

"Well," Isandro said finally, unsteadily, "that's going to complicate the chain of command."

"Good," Sefa said. "Command chains are how the Company thinks. We're not the Company."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter and rose. Sefa stayed on deck past the end of her watch, working while the light strengthened, fixing their position from the fading stars and laying down the day's first lines on her chart. Behind her she heard Isandro wake the others; heard Nell's sleepy outrage at the cold; heard Bram grumbling toward the engine well. Ordinary sounds. Family-shaped sounds, though she wasn't ready to use that word even inside her own skull.

By full light the river had changed character again — narrower now, swifter, the banks rising into gray stone shoulders. And ahead, maybe six miles as the loon flew, the water disappeared into a white wall that at first Sefa took for cloud.

She got the glass on it. Lowered it. Got it again, and made herself breathe.

Ice. A glacier's snout, ramming down from the high country to meet the river, blue-white and ancient, sealing the valley wall to wall. The Verrow emerged from beneath it in a single furious green channel, but above — the map Lior had drawn, the route they meant to run — everything beyond that wall was locked.

"Weeks early," Isandro said beside her. She hadn't seen him come forward. His voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a man setting down a weight he'd only just picked back up. "Calving won't open that for a month, if the journals are right. Maybe more."

Sefa swept the glass along the ice face, reading it the way she read everything now — professionally, hungrily, refusing to be afraid. The glacier was vast, but glaciers were also clocks. She could see the meltwater staining, the crevasse fields, the tension written in the ice like handwriting.

"It'll open," she said. "Ice always tells you when. Until then—" she turned back to her chart, to the clean white space beyond the last contour, and felt the strange steadiness of a woman standing exactly where she was made to stand, "—until then, Captain, I suggest we anchor, and I suggest you let me teach your crew the stars. If Hale comes up that channel, I want to hear him before he sees us."

Behind them, far downriver, so faint it might have been imagination, came a sound none of them could name yet — a long metallic cough echoing off the stone, like an engine working hard against the current.

Bram's head came up like a deer's.

Sefa raised the glass astern, and held her breath.
