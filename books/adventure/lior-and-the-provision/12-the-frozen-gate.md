## 12. The Frozen Gate

The river ended the way a sentence ends—abruptly, mid-thought, against a wall of blue ice.

Bram felt it before he saw it: a change in the Kestrel's motion, the water thickening beneath the hull like soup going cold, the current losing its argument with itself. He stood at the bow with a boathook he hadn't needed in three hundred miles and watched the channel narrow between shoulders of gray stone, and then the stone gave way to something that wasn't stone at all.

The glacier's mouth filled the horizon from bank to bank. It was not white, as he had always imagined ice to be. It was blue—the deep bruised blue of deep water, of shadowed steel, of a bruise a week old—and it rose out of the river in a sheer face forty feet high, seamed and grooved and dripping, exhaling cold like a living thing asleep.

"Sweet mercy," Nell breathed beside him.

"Get back from the rail," Isandro said, but there was no force in it. He had gone still at the wheel, both hands on the spokes, staring up at the face of the thing as if it might stare back.

Sefa came forward with her charts rolled under one arm, unrolled them on the deck box, weighted the corners with two fists of rivets and a tin cup. She looked at the ice, looked at the paper, looked at the ice again. Bram watched her jaw tighten by degrees, the way a rope tightens around a cleat.

"We're early," she said.

"How early?" Isandro asked.

She turned the chart toward him without answering, and Bram, who could not read a survey to save his life, read her face instead. Weeks. The word was written all over her in the way she smoothed the paper flat twice when once would do.

"The calving won't start until the meltwater gets under the tongue," Sefa said finally. "The river does the work for us—eats away the footing until the whole lower shelf lets go and floats. That's the window Lior wrote about. Three days, maybe four, when the channel's open." She pressed her palm flat against the chart. "We're six weeks ahead of the melt."

Nobody said anything for a while. The Kestrel drifted, and Isandro let her drift, backing water gently so the bow didn't touch the ice. Up close the glacier made sounds—a low internal grinding, like millstones turning under the floor of the world, and every few minutes a crack, sharp as a rifle shot, that made Nell flinch and Bram's hand remember the weight of a rifle it no longer carried.

"So we wait," Nell said.

"So we wait," Isandro agreed.

* * *

They made camp in a gravel eddy half a mile downstream, where an old flood had left a shelf of stones above the high-water line, sheltered on three sides by a shoulder of rock. Isandro ran the Kestrel's stern line to a boulder the size of a chapel and rigged the awning over the cargo well, and by the second evening they had something that resembled a life: a fire ring, a drying line for socks, Nell's collection of interesting rocks arranged along the gunwale like a museum nobody visited.

Bram knew how to make camp. He'd been making camp since he was fourteen years old, since the first time a sergeant kicked him awake in frozen mud and told him home was wherever the column stopped. He split wood, hauled water, dug the latrine trench downwind, and did all of it quickly and competently and without talking, because that was what he was for. Strong hands. That was what Isandro had said the day he took him aboard at Greyford, looking at Bram's arms and not his eyes. Strong hands, and no questions asked.

Six weeks was a long time to be useful.

By the fourth day the woodpile was absurd—a rampart of split spruce stacked in ranks like soldiers awaiting inspection—and Isandro told him to stop splitting wood before he ran them out of forest. By the sixth day he had recaulked seams that didn't leak, sharpened every blade aboard including Sefa's letter opener, and rebuilt the stove's firebox with plate iron scavenged from a wrecked scow they'd found wedged in the willows. The stove drew better than it ever had. Nobody mentioned it.

On the seventh day Nell caught him trying to straighten the nails he'd pulled from the scow, one by one, hammering each crooked one flat on a flat stone, and she sat down across the fire from him and just watched, chin on her knees, until he put the hammer down.

"You don't have to do that," she said.

"Do what."

"That." She waved at the nails, the woodpile, the whole arrangement of him. "Work all the time. Like if you stop, something bad happens."

Bram looked at the fire. The glacier cracked somewhere upstream, a long rolling report that echoed off the canyon walls and came back wrong, doubled, like two glaciers arguing.

"Something bad already happened," he said.

Nell, being eleven, considered this with the seriousness it deserved and then said, "You could tell us about it. If you wanted. Sefa says carrying things alone makes them heavier."

"Sefa say that?"

"She says it about maps. A survey you keep to yourself is just paper. It only becomes a map when somebody else can read it too." Nell shrugged, a small precise shrug she'd learned from Sefa, Bram realized, the way kids learn everything, by theft. "I think people are probably the same."

He didn't answer. But that night, when Isandro took first watch and Sefa lay in the bow cabin with her charts, and the fire burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones, Bram sat up longer than he meant to, listening to the ice grind, and found that he could not sleep at all. His body had learned to sleep anywhere—in mud, in rain, standing up against a wall—but here, in safety, with nowhere to march at dawn, sleep wouldn't come. Safety, it turned out, was the thing his nightmares had been waiting for. They'd had nothing to do while he was busy.

* * *

It came apart on the ninth day, over fish.

Sefa had rigged a net across a side channel where the grayling ran in the evenings, and they ate well—fish crisped in bacon fat, wild onions Nell dug from the bank, tea brewed from spruce tips that tasted like Christmas smelled. There was even a bottle of plum brandy Isandro produced from some hidden compartment with the sheepish air of a man revealing a vice he'd promised himself he'd given up. He poured four tin cups, thin measures, and they drank, and the fire popped, and for an hour the camp was almost warm in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

It was Nell who broke it, because Nell always broke things open; it was her nature, the way some children are born to locks and others to doors.

"Bram," she said, "were you in the war? The real one, on the Marrow? Sefa says your boots are army issue and your hands are callused like a musket grip, and Isandro won't say, and I've been wondering for weeks."

"Nell," Isandro said, a warning note.

"It's all right." Bram heard his own voice come out level, which surprised him. He'd expected it to shake. Maybe it shook somewhere underneath, where it didn't show, the way the glacier moved somewhere underneath, where you couldn't see. "It's a fair question. I've been circling it myself for nine days."

He turned the cup in his hands. The brandy was gone but he kept turning it, watching the firelight swim in the empty tin.

"I was in the Provision garrison at Cold Harrow," he said. "Third Rifles. You'd know it as the fort that burned—I expect there were stories. There usually are, about places like that, and most of them are wrong in the details and right in the shape."

Sefa had gone very still. She was the one who understood institutions, who had been chewed by one herself; Bram saw her recognize the shape of what was coming before he said it, and he loved her a little for not stopping him.

"There was a town near the fort. Kelstern. Little place, ferry crossing, maybe two hundred souls. The Company claimed the partisans were using it—storing arms in the church cellar, running messages through the ferryman. Maybe true. Probably partly true. Doesn't matter now." He set the cup down carefully, precisely, the way he did everything, because precision was the only discipline left to him. "Orders came down in the winter. Not from any captain I ever met—from above, from a man with a Company seal and no name I was allowed to know. The order was to make an example. Those were the words. Make an example. And the example was to be made of the whole town, because an example of one house, the officer explained, is just arson."

The fire cracked. Downriver, water chuckled over gravel, indifferent, going about its business.

"Some men refused. They were arrested on the spot, and I don't know what happened to them, and that's another thing I carry—that I don't know, that I didn't ask, that I stood in the ranks and did my arithmetic and decided I'd rather be a coward alive than a martyr dead." His voice stayed level. It was eerie to him, hearing it. "I stood in the ranks. That's the whole of my crime, if you want the small version. I stood where I was told to stand, and I held the weapon I was told to hold, and when the order came I—" 

He stopped. Nine days of circling, and here was the center, and it was smaller and colder than he'd imagined.

"I raised my rifle," he said. "I aimed it at a door. There was smoke coming out from under the door, and light inside, lamplight, and behind the glass I could see a shadow moving. Somebody walking around in their own kitchen. And I aimed at the door because aiming at the door meant I wasn't aiming at the shadow, and I told myself that mattered. I told myself right up until the volley that it mattered, that I never fired at a person, that my shot went into wood."

"Did it?" Nell asked softly. Not accusing. Just asking, the way children ask, because they haven't learned yet which questions adults have agreed not to ask.

"I don't know." The truth of it came out of him like a splinter coming out, slow and tearing. "That's the hell of it, girl. In a volley of thirty rifles you can't hear your own shot. I'll never know. Every night since, some part of me has been doing the sums—thirty rifles, one door, two hundred people—and the sums never come out clean. So I deserted. Walked out of the lines two nights later, swam the Marrow in February, lost two toes to it, and I've been running downriver ever since, taking work where hands are wanted and faces aren't remembered." He looked up, finally, and made himself look at each of them, because laying it down meant laying it down in front of witnesses or it didn't count. "That's why I didn't want to come up here. That's why I voted against the source. Because everything the Company buries, it buries with men like me holding the shovels, and I know exactly what they'll send if they decide this valley needs making quiet. I've been one of them. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you so you know what's sleeping next to you in this camp."

Silence, except the river and the far-off groan of the ice.

It was Isandro who moved first. He got up, crossed the gravel, and sat down beside Bram—not facing him, but shoulder to shoulder, both of them looking at the fire, the way men sit when words are too heavy to survive eye contact.

"In Greyford," Isandro said, "there was a woman named Odile Ferran who ran the debt ledger for the Provision office. Nice enough woman. Polite. She'd take a man's boat and his father's name and write it all down in a fine copperplate hand, and she'd say, sorry, Vane, rules are rules, and mean it, and sleep soundly after." He turned his cup. "Took me years to understand the trick of it. The Company doesn't need monsters. Monsters are expensive and unreliable. All it needs is ordinary people who follow the form. The form does the killing." He knocked his cup lightly against Bram's. "You followed the form. Then you broke it. Most men never get as far as breaking it."

"I raised the rifle."

"And then you swam a frozen river to get away from what you'd been part of. I've known a lot of men who stayed warm and dry and never regretted a thing." Isandro's voice was low and rough. "I'll take the deserter with the frozen toes over the comfortable man every day of my life."

Sefa spoke from across the fire, quiet and clear. "At the Survey Office, they falsified my charts. Took my names off my own coastlines and put the Director's nephew on them. I spent five years being furious at the men who did it. It took me longer to be angry at myself—for staying, for signing, for telling myself the small compromises didn't matter." She met his eyes across the flames. "Shame is just grief with nowhere to go, Bram. You give it somewhere to go, it gets lighter. That's all any of us are doing out here."

Nell crawled over and sat down on his other side, close, the way she did when she'd decided something, and leaned her head briefly against his arm—the whole weight of her, trusting, brief as a bird landing.

"I'm glad you told us," she said. "Now it's a map."

Bram laughed—or something started as a laugh and ended somewhere else, in his chest, tight and hot, and he bowed his head over his empty cup and let it happen, because they were kind enough to look at the fire and pretend they hadn't seen a grown man come apart over fish and plum brandy. Shame laid down like a heavy pack. He felt the absence of it on his shoulders the way an amputee feels the missing limb—still there, still aching, but no longer weighing him down with every step.

They sat together a long time. The brandy bottle made another round, thinner measures still. Somewhere past midnight the others drifted to their bedrolls, and Bram took the watch he'd traded for without discussion, wrapped in a blanket, the rifle-less hands quiet in his lap.

* * *

Near dawn, the mountain spoke.

Not the usual cracks and grinds—those had become the valley's breathing, familiar as a snoring shipmate. This was different. This was a boom that came up through the ground, through the gravel, through the soles of his boots and the bones of his legs, and Bram was on his feet before his mind caught up, old training firing along nerves that had never quite demobilized.

Upstream, at the face of the Frozen Gate, a seam of pale light had opened along the ice.

He woke Isandro with a hand on his shoulder, and they stood together at the waterline watching the glacier shift—a groan, a shudder, a sloughing of snow from the crest of the face that fell a hundred feet and burst against the river in a long white detonation. The water shivered. The Kestrel tugged at her mooring line like a dog smelling rain.

"Too early for calving," Isandro murmured. His breath hung in the freezing air. "Way too early."

"Then what was it?"

Isandro didn't answer right away. He was looking at the ice with an expression Bram had learned to dread over the past weeks—the look of a man doing sums he doesn't like.

"Meltwater's not the only thing that moves a glacier," he said at last. "Weight moves it. Something up-valley's pushing." He turned, and in the gray pre-dawn his eyes went to the Kestrel, to the awning, to the sleeping shapes of Sefa and Nell. "Or someone's getting ready to come through from the other side."

Behind them, faint but unmistakable on the still morning air, came a sound neither of them had heard in weeks and neither of them would ever forget: the low, patient throb of a Provision Company engine, somewhere downriver, closing the distance.
