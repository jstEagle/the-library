## 13. Nell's Discovery

The waiting had a sound to it.

Nell had learned that in her first days aboard the Kestrel — that every place made its own noise if you lay still enough to hear it. The lower Verrow had sounded like flies and rope creak and Isandro muttering figures in his cabin at night. The middle river had sounded like Bram's snores through the bulkhead and Sefa's pencil scratching against paper. But here, at the frozen gate of the world, the waiting sounded like the glacier itself: a long, low groan beneath everything, like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep, too big to wake all the way.

She was bored past the edge of boredom and out the other side, into a bright, humming restlessness that made her fingers itch.

The Kestrel sat wedged in the shallows of a gravel bar where the river went narrow and green-white with glacial silt, half a mile below the ice wall that sealed the passage upstream. The wall was beautiful in a way that frightened her — a cliff face of blue-green ice taller than any church she'd ever seen, streaked white where centuries of snowfall had pressed itself flat. It did not look like something that could open. It looked like something that had been closed since the beginning of the world.

"Stay within sight of the boat," Isandro had said that morning, in the voice he used when he was pretending not to worry. "And take Bram."

Bram had come as far as the first bend in the shoreline and then stopped, arms crossed, staring up at the ice with an expression Nell couldn't read — or could read too well, maybe, because it was the same look he got when anyone mentioned the war. He'd planted himself on a boulder like a fence post and said he'd watch the waterline.

So it was Nell alone who noticed the gap.

It was barely a gap at first — a shadow in the rock where the glacier's toe met the canyon wall, a seam of darkness no wider than her shoulders. Wind came out of it, cold and mineral-smelling, carrying a faint sweetness underneath, like snow over old woodsmoke. She stood before it for a long moment, doing the arithmetic every orphan learns early: how much trouble, weighed against how much wonder.

Wonder won. Wonder always won with her. That was what Sefa said, laughing, tapping her on the nose. *You're a compass needle, girl. You can't help pointing.*

Nell turned sideways and slipped into the dark.

* * *

The cave opened almost at once, the way secrets do — one breath of squeezing terror and then room. She struck a match from the tin she'd filched from the galley weeks ago (Isandro knew; he'd never said anything, which was its own kind of kindness) and lit the stub of candle she carried rolled in waxed cloth in her coat pocket. A girl who'd slept in cargo holds learned to keep light close.

The flame showed her a corridor of stone and ice, wide as a barn, sloping gently upward. The walls were ribbed with frozen meltwater, and when she breathed on them they gleamed like the inside of a shell. Her footsteps rang strangely, doubled and tripled, so that it sounded like three girls walking instead of one, and she found herself whispering hello to them just to hear it come back.

The glacier-groan was louder in here. Not frightening, exactly. More like being inside a throat that was deciding whether to sing.

She walked for what felt like a quarter hour, following the slope, until the corridor bent and the candlelight caught something that didn't belong.

Cloth. A scrap of canvas, gray with age, hanging off a ledge of rock.

Nell's heart did a small, strange hop. She knew that canvas. She'd spent a whole feverish night aboard the Kestrel wringing it out and folding it, back when Lior was dying in the bunk across from hers, his hands shaking too hard to hold a cup. It was tent-canvas, army-surplus, patched at the corner with a square of red flannel — because Lior had told her once, in one of his lucid hours, that red flannel was the only thing that kept the mountain spirits from stealing your dreams, and he'd said it grinning, but he'd also sewn it on himself, careful as a surgeon.

Beyond the ledge, the cave widened into a chamber, and the chamber held a camp.

A rusted stove, its pipe vented through a crack in the ceiling that breathed daylight down like a pale blue ribbon. A bedroll gone stiff with frost. Crates stacked against the wall, their stenciled letters worn to ghosts. And everywhere — everywhere — the small archaeology of a man who had lived alone with a secret for years: a tin plate with a spoon fused to it by ice. Boots resoled with strips of tire. A line strung with drying herbs gone brown as autumn. A chess set carved from antler, mid-game, frozen in the same position it must have held for years, two kings facing each other across a board neither player would return to.

Nell stood very still in the doorway of Lior's hidden life and felt the way you feel standing in a church — hushed, and slightly trespassing, and unable to leave.

"Hello," she whispered to the three echoes of herself. None of them answered.

She touched nothing at first. Then she saw the journals.

They were in a waterproof oilskin roll tucked behind the stove, six of them, seven, bound in oilcloth and tied with wire, and the topmost one had *PROPERTY OF L. VESS* burned into the cover with a hot nail, the letters wobbling the way letters do when a hand is cold or old or both. Nell knew she should go back. She knew Isandro would want to know, would want to see, would want to decide — that was the shape of him, the captain-shape, always deciding so nobody else had to carry it.

But her fingers were already untying the wire.

* * *

The early pages were a surveyor's diary, dry as gravel. Elevations. Bearings. Notes on ice depth and rock strata, written in a cramped, disciplined hand. Nell skimmed them the way she skimmed Sefa's charts — admiring the neatness without understanding the meat.

Then, four pages into the third journal, the handwriting changed.

It slanted. It hurried. Words were crossed out and rewritten in the margins, and the ink changed color halfway down a page, which meant he'd run out and kept going with something else, which meant he hadn't wanted to stop even long enough to find more ink.

Nell sat down cross-legged on the frozen floor with the journal in her lap and the candle between her feet, and she read.

*I have been a fool. I came up here thinking I was chasing gold. Every man who comes up here is chasing gold. The Company lets us chase it because the chasing keeps us blind.*

*There is no gold. There is something better than gold, and worse than gold, and the Company has known about it for thirty years.*

*The spring is real. I have seen it. I will write this plainly so that whoever finds these pages cannot mistake me for a madman: there is a spring in the high valley above the ice, and the water carries minerals in concentrations I have never seen recorded — ores suspended in solution, wealth enough to buy and sell the Provision Company ten times over. It comes out of the mountain glowing. I am not drunk. I am not mad. It GLOWS.*

Nell read that word twice. *Glows.* She thought of the map hidden beneath the Kestrel's floorboards, the grease-stained lines Lior had traded his life's last warmth to deliver, and she felt the cold of the cave reach through her coat for the first time.

She turned the page.

*But here is what the Company knows and I did not: the spring poisons everything downstream of it. The minerals concentrate in the water table. Wells go bad. Cattle die with blue froth at the mouth. Children are born wrong, or not at all. I have walked through three abandoned towns in the last month — Harrow Bend, Cold Sallow, and a place whose name has been scraped off the signposts — and in each one the story is the same. The Company bought the land cheap. The Company moved the people along. And then the Company posted men at the head of the valley to make sure no prospector, no surveyor, no curious fool ever followed the water home to its source.*

*In Harrow Bend there is a churchyard with forty-one graves dated within a single summer. Forty-one. I counted twice. The stones say fever. The Company's men told the survivors it was fever.*

*It was the water.*

Nell's breath made a small cloud and hung in front of her face, and she watched it hang there, because looking at anything else suddenly felt impossible and looking at the page felt worse.

She was nine years old when her mother died, and eleven when the workhouse turned her out, and she had learned young that the world was full of hard things. But those had all been hard things that happened — accidents, sicknesses, the ordinary cruelty of luck. This was different. This was a thing that had been *done*, deliberately, quietly, with ledgers and lawyers and men posted at the heads of valleys. Somebody had sat at a desk, in a warm room, and decided that forty-one graves in Harrow Bend were cheaper than honesty.

She thought about Corvus Hale's gray eyes and his gray gloves and the way he'd smiled at her on the deck of the Kestrel like she was furniture. She thought about the gunboat that had shadowed them upriver. She thought about Isandro lying smoothly to Hale's face while her own heart hammered under the crates, and how she'd understood even then, without knowing why, that the Company was not a company at all. It was a mouth. And mouths only ever wanted one thing.

She kept reading. She couldn't not.

*I tried to tell a newspaper man in Greyford. He listened very politely and then reported my presence to the Company office within the hour. I know because the Company's men came to my lodgings that night and I left through the window with nothing but this journal and my boots.*

*They do not need to kill many people. They only need to kill the ones who talk, and make sure everyone watches. That is the whole architecture of it. Fear does the rest of the work for free.*

*So now I sit in the ice with my secret and my bad heart, and I think about what a man owes. I owe the dead of Harrow Bend. I owe the dead of Cold Sallow. I owe every prospector the Company has fed lies to and every family that drank the water and buried their children and believed it was God's will or bad weather. The Company calls the source a liability. I call it a debt owed to everyone.*

That phrase landed in Nell's chest like a dropped anchor. *A debt owed to everyone.* Lior had whispered it at dawn with Isandro's hand in his, and she hadn't been there — she'd been asleep in the hold — but Isandro had told them, that night below decks when he'd confessed everything, his voice rough as river gravel. She remembered Sefa going very still. She remembered Bram looking at the floor.

She remembered deciding, in the dark, that she would follow this promise anywhere it went.

The last entries were short. His handwriting was failing — the letters sprawling, gaps where he'd lost his thread and picked it up again. The final entry was three lines:

*If someone reads this, the spring must not be claimed. Not by the Company. Not by us. Not by anyone. It does not make men rich. It makes men hungry, and hunger like that eats the world.*

*I have drawn the map true, because a false map kills honest people. But I have given it away, because a true map in my pocket dies with me.*

*Whoever you are: be careful what you carry downhill.*

Nell closed the journal and sat in the cold and the candle-hiss and the enormous slow breathing of the glacier, and she cried — not loudly, not the way children cry to be heard, but the silent, scalding way she'd taught herself in the workhouse, tears running straight off her chin onto the oilskin cover, where they froze almost at once into tiny bright beads.

She cried for Lior, dying alone up here with forty-one graves on his conscience that weren't even his. She cried for the children of Harrow Bend, whose names weren't even on their stones. She cried a little bit for herself, age eight, drinking well water in a town she couldn't remember the name of, and wondering, with a chill that had nothing to do with the ice, whether her mother's cough had been a cough at all.

And underneath the crying, quiet as a match being struck, something else was happening. Something hard and clean and new.

She was angry.

Not the hot, splashing anger of a child throwing a cup. This was a deep-water anger, cold and steady, the kind that could turn a paddlewheel for years. The Company had killed towns and called it fever. The Company had chased a dying old man through the ice to bury a truth. The Company had put gray-eyed men with gray gloves on boats to hunt a piece of paper — because a piece of paper was all that stood between them and everyone forgetting.

Well. Nell Vane — she'd given herself the surname herself, off a gravestone in Greyford, and she'd never told anyone that either — Nell was done being furniture.

* * *

She took the journals. All of them, wrapped back in their oilskin, wired shut, stuffed inside her coat where they rode against her ribs like a second heartbeat. She took the antler chess kings too — one for each pocket — because it seemed wrong to leave them facing each other forever in the cold, and because she thought Bram might understand, in his wordless way, about two kings and a game nobody finished.

At the mouth of the cave she paused and looked back once. In the candlelight the camp looked almost peaceful, a man's life folded neatly around an absence, waiting. She blew out the candle and the dark took it back.

Outside, the light hit her like a blow after so long in the blue gloom — the low sun blazing off the ice wall, the river running green and glassy, the whole frozen world glittering as if nothing ugly had ever been done in it. Bram was still on his boulder. He straightened when he saw her face.

"You were gone long," he said. Then, reading her the way he read weather: "What is it? What'd you find?"

"Lior's camp," Nell said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "He lived up here. For years." She patted the lump of journals under her coat. "And Bram — it wasn't treasure he was running from. It wasn't gold at all."

Bram went very still, the way he did when old things stirred.

"It's the Company," she said. "It was always the Company. They poisoned the towns, Bram. Whole towns. And they're coming up this river to make sure nobody ever—" 

She stopped.

Because across the water, far downstream, sunlight flashed off something that wasn't ice and wasn't water. Once, twice. Deliberate. Rhythmic.

A signal mirror.

And beside her, she felt Bram stop breathing.
