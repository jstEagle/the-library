## 14. Betrayal at the Ice Line

The cold came down off the glacier at dusk like a verdict.

Isandro felt it first in the deck plates beneath his boots, a deepening chill that had nothing to do with the season and everything to do with the wall of blue ice two miles upstream, where the Kestrel sat moored in a pool of meltwater as still and pale as milk glass. They had been waiting nine days. The glacier had not moved. The route had not opened. And something in the air had begun to rot—not literally, not yet, but in the way of all things held too long in one place: tempers, rations, hope.

He stood at the rail with a mug of coffee gone bitter from sitting too long, watching the light die on the ice line. It was the hour he liked least and trusted most. In the dark, a man's face told you nothing. But his hands—his hands always told the truth, if you watched long enough. Twenty years of running cargo past customs men and debt collectors had taught him that much. You didn't read what people said. You read what they did when they thought no one was looking.

Which was why, when Bram slipped out of the hold an hour after full dark, moving quiet as spilled oil, Isandro set down his mug without a sound and followed him.

* * *

Bram went aft.

That was the first wrong thing. There was nothing aft but the lazarette, the spare line, and the old ship's boat they'd lashed upside down against the rail to keep it clear of snow. Nothing worth visiting at midnight, unless a man wanted privacy—and privacy, on a boat ninety feet long with four souls aboard, was a thing you had to manufacture, not find.

Isandro kept to the shadow of the deckhouse, boots silent on planking dusted with frost. He'd learned to walk quietly before he'd learned to swim; his father had seen to that, back when quiet feet were the difference between a collector finding them and a collector passing by. Some inheritances you couldn't refuse no matter how far upriver you ran.

Bram crouched beside the overturned dinghy. His broad back blocked Isandro's view of whatever he was doing, but Isandro could see the shape of his shoulders—the particular hunch of a man bent over something small and precious. A minute passed. Two. The glacier groaned somewhere out in the dark, a long tectonic complaint that rolled across the water and made the mooring lines hum.

Then Bram raised his arm, and the moonlight caught it: a small rectangle of glass, angled toward the southern sky.

A signal mirror.

Isandro's blood went cold in a way the night hadn't managed in nine days.

He knew the flash when he saw it—a hard, deliberate wink of reflected light, three quick pulses, pause, three more. Not random. Not a man checking his teeth or admiring his own eyes. Code. Someone downstream, someone with a glass trained on this stretch of river, was being answered in the only language that could cross ten miles of frozen dark.

Hale.

The name arrived in Isandro's mind without drama, the way bad news always does—already seated, already comfortable, as though it had been waiting there the whole time.

He should have felt rage. What he felt instead was a terrible, clarifying sadness, the kind that comes when a structure you've been living inside shows you its cracks. Because he had known. God help him, some part of him had known since the journals came out of the ice cave, since Nell had spread Lior's pages across the galley table with her cold-reddened hands and read aloud about towns bought and burned and drowned so that one company could keep one secret. He had watched Bram's face through all of it. He had seen the color leave it.

He had told himself it was horror. Horror at the Company. Horror at what men would do.

It had been recognition.

* * *

"Put it down, Bram."

His voice came out low and even, and it still broke something in the silence. Bram went rigid, the mirror frozen mid-flash, and for three heartbeats neither of them moved. Steam rose off the water. Somewhere under the ice, the river breathed.

Then Bram turned, slow, the way big men turn when they know running is pointless. He set the mirror on the rail with exaggerated care, as if gentleness now could undo anything.

"Cap'n." His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "How long you been standing there?"

"Long enough."

"It's not—" Bram stopped. Started again. "You don't understand what those journals mean. For me."

"I understand exactly what they mean for you." Isandro stepped out of the shadow, letting the moonlight have him. "Thornmouth. Twelve years ago. The Provision garrison at the ferry crossing. You were there."

Bram flinched as if struck. "Who told you?"

"You did. Not the words. Everything else." Isandro moved closer, stopping an arm's length away—close enough to talk, far enough that the conversation could go either direction. "The night you told us why you deserted. You said they ordered you to clear the crossing. You said you refused. I believed you then."

"I *did* refuse."

"And then what? You stood there while others didn't?" Isandro shook his head slowly. "No. That's not it either. I've had twelve years to learn every way a man can be present at a thing without touching it. You weren't a bystander at Thornmouth, Bram. You were a hand. Maybe a reluctant one. Maybe a weeping one. But the ferry burned, and the people on it drowned, and your name is in whatever ledger Hale keeps of who did what."

The big man's breath came out in a white plume that shook. "The journals name the unit," he said finally. "Page forty-one. Lior copied the garrison roster off a requisition form. My name's in ink, Cap'n. In ink, in a dead man's handwriting, in a book Nell reads like scripture." His voice cracked open. "She looks at me different already. Since she found those pages. You think I don't see it?"

"She's eleven."

"She's eleven and she'll live to be eighty, and every year she'll remember me a little worse." Bram pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "I ran from Thornmouth to get away from it. Ran from the army, ran downriver, ran onto your boat because you didn't ask questions. And now the whole thing's caught up, sitting right there in the hold in a dead prospector's journal, waiting to hang me. So yes. I signaled. I signaled because Hale doesn't want the map. He wants the *journals*. He said—I heard him say at the fueling station—he said evidence burns easier than boats."

"You talked to Hale?"

"I flashed him two nights ago. First time. Just asked if he'd take me off alive." Bram lowered his hands. His eyes were wet and hopeless in the moonlight. "He said come alone, bring the books, and the Kestrel passes free. No one else has to burn."

"And you believed him."

"I *wanted* to believe him." The confession came out raw. "There's a difference, Cap'n, and I've been telling myself it's a bigger difference than it is."

* * *

Isandro looked at him for a long time.

Below them, faintly, he could hear Sefa turning in her bunk, the creak of the frame he'd learned to sleep through. Nell's breathing, high and quick, a child's rhythm even in sleep. The sounds of the family he had never asked for and could not now imagine doing without—and one member of it standing in front of him with a signal mirror in his pocket and Hale's promise in his ear.

The easy thing—the thing the old Isandro Vane, the one who'd spent years keeping his head below the gunwale, would have done—was to put Bram off the boat. Take his gear, take his share of nothing, and point him at the ice. Let the cold do the arithmetic. A man who signals Company gunboats forfeits any claim on the people he endangers.

But the old Isandro had died somewhere between Greyford Landing and the White Reach, and the man standing here now understood something the old one never had: that Bram wasn't betraying them out of malice. He was betraying them out of terror, which was worse, because terror could live in anyone. Terror lived in Isandro too. Terror was the reason he'd almost taken Hale's deal at the fueling station, the reason his hand had actually risen toward the map when Hale slid the debt papers across the table. If Bram's fear made him a traitor, then Isandro's temptation made him one too, and the only difference between them was that Isandro's moment had passed unspent and Bram's had not.

"You're going to tell them," Bram said. It wasn't a question. "Sefa and Nell. Come morning."

"Yes."

"They'll never trust me again."

"No," Isandro agreed. "Not for a while. Maybe never. Trust isn't a rope, Bram. It's a bone. When it breaks, it knits, but it knits crooked, and it aches in cold weather the rest of your life." He picked up the signal mirror from the rail, turned it over once in his fingers—cheap tin, Company issue, probably lifted from a supply crate years ago—and put it in his coat. "I'm keeping this."

"What are you going to do with me?"

"I honestly don't know yet." Isandro looked out at the glacier, a white wall faintly luminous against the stars, holding back everything behind it. "That's the truth. The old me would know. The old me had a whole ledger of punishments. But the old me never had a crew."

"Cap'n—"

"Go below, Bram. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow you'll sit at that table and say what you just said to me, word for word, to Sefa and Nell, and you'll let them look at you however they need to. That's the sentence. That's all of it." He paused. "And if the glacier opens tomorrow—if we run the channel—you'll be at your post, because I'm not leaving a man on the ice, even a man who sold our heading to the devil. Do you understand why?"

Bram's jaw worked. "Why?"

"Because Lior called the map a debt owed to everyone. Everyone, Bram. Not everyone except the ones who fail." Isandro turned away, then stopped. "One more thing. Did you tell him where the journals are hidden?"

"No." It came fast, and Isandro believed it. "I never got that far. I asked about terms first. He wanted proof I had them."

"So he knows we have them. He doesn't know where." Isandro exhaled. "Small mercies. Go."

Bram went. Isandro stood alone at the rail until the sound of the hatch faded, then took out the mirror and hurled it, sidearm, far out over the black water. It skipped once, twice, and sank without a sound, taking Hale's channel of communication down with it.

Nine days, maybe less, until calving. Hale would come whether or not the mirror flashed again. The question—the only question that mattered now—was whether the Kestrel would be ready to run when the ice gave way, and whether the four of them could stand on the same deck knowing what one of them had done.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He climbed to the wheelhouse instead and sat in the dark with the chart spread out under the dim shielded lamp, tracing the channel they'd run when the glacier opened—though Sefa's pencil marks, her careful soundings, her neat annotations in a cartographer's hand that trembled only when she was tired. He traced them and tried to think like a captain instead of a wounded man, and mostly failed.

Near dawn, soft footsteps on the ladder. Sefa, wrapped in a blanket, hair loose, eyes immediately finding his face and reading it the way she read water—for depth, for current, for what lay hidden underneath.

"You've been up all night," she said.

"Yes."

"Something happened."

Isandro closed the chart. For a moment he considered lying—lying was a skill, and he was good at it, and there were hours yet before he'd promised Bram his reckoning. But Sefa was looking at him with the particular patience of a woman who had lost her own name once and rebuilt it from honest timber, and lies, he had learned, curdled fastest in the presence of people who valued truth above comfort.

"Bram signaled Hale," he said. "Two nights ago. Again tonight. A signal mirror, off the stern. He wants to trade Lior's journals for safe passage out."

Sefa went very still. Outside, the first grey light touched the ice line, and the glacier groaned—a long, grinding exhalation, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"He's frightened," Isandro went on. "The journals name him at Thornmouth. He thinks if Hale gets them, he lives and we pass free. He actually believes that." He laughed, short and without humor. "The worst part is I understand him. That's the worst part, Sefa. I sat across from Hale and nearly did the same thing with the map."

Sefa crossed the wheelhouse and stood beside him at the window, shoulder almost touching his. Together they watched the light grow on the ice.

"What do we do?" she asked.

"We wake Nell. We put Bram at the table, and he tells her himself, because he owes her that and I won't rob him of the paying." Isandro rubbed his eyes. "And then we vote. Same as before. All four voices. Even his."

"Even his?"

"*Especially* his. If we decide his fate without him, we become the kind of people who decide fates in back rooms. That's the Company's trade, not ours." He straightened. "But Sefa—whatever we decide, it happens today. Because last night, after I took the mirror from him, I watched that glacier for an hour, and I swear to you the sound it's making has changed. Deeper. Like something leaning."

As if the mountain had heard him, the ice spoke again—that same low, tectonic groan, longer this time, rolling down the valley and setting the meltwater trembling against the hull. Far upstream, in the blue heart of the glacier wall, something cracked. The report came flat across the water, sharp as a rifle shot, and then another, and then a sound like distant thunder that did not stop.

Sefa's hand found his in the dark of the wheelhouse.

"That's not settling," she whispered. "Isandro. That's the beginning."

Above the ice line, in the paling sky, a thin plume of snow-dust rose from the glacier's face and hung there, glowing, like a flag going up over a country about to change hands.

They had hours. Maybe a day. And somewhere downstream, closing at the speed of a gunboat's engines, Corvus Hale was coming to collect.
