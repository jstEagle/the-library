## 15. The Calving

The glacier spoke first in the night, and Isandro woke believing it was thunder.

He lay in his bunk in the Kestrel's stern cabin, listening to the sound roll down the valley—a long, grinding groan that seemed to come from the bones of the world itself, deep enough that he felt it in the planking beneath him, in the fillings of his teeth, in the old scar along his ribs. Then came a second sound, sharper: a crack like a rifle shot, then another, then a stuttering volley of them, echoing off the valley walls until it was impossible to tell where one ended and the next began.

He was on deck before he remembered to dress properly, boots unlaced, coat thrown over his nightshirt, the cold hitting him like a slap of river water. The others were already there. Sefa stood at the bow rail with her arms wrapped around herself, her breath streaming silver in the starlight. Nell was pressed against the wheelhouse door, eyes enormous. And Bram—

Bram stood apart, as he had stood apart for three days now, at the edge of the lamplight where the shadows could hold him. His signal mirror was gone—Isandro had taken it, had held it up to the fire and watched the man's face collapse—but Bram's hands were still curled around the shape of it, as if some part of him hadn't yet understood it wasn't coming back.

The glacier filled the head of the valley like a wall built by something that hated travelers. By day it was blue-white and terrible; by night, under the stars, it glowed faintly, a drowned light trapped in ten thousand years of ice. As they watched, a seam near its base split open with a sound like the sky tearing, and a block of ice the size of the wheelhouse sheared away and fell into the meltwater lake below. The splash reached them a full three seconds later, a soft, obscene *whump* that rolled across the water.

"The calving," Sefa said quietly. "It's starting."

"Days?" Isandro asked.

She tilted her head, reading the glacier the way she read rivers—the tilt of the ice face, the color of the meltwater, the rhythm of the cracking. "Three. Maybe four if we're lucky. After that it seals again for another year." She turned to look at him, and in the dark her face was all angles and honesty. "It has to be soon, Isandro."

He nodded and said nothing, because what was there to say? Three days. Hale's gunboat was somewhere behind them in the fog of the lower channel—he'd seen the smoke two mornings ago, a gray smudge against the gray sky—and somewhere ahead lay the source Lior had died carrying a map to, the spring that drove men mad with wanting, the debt owed to everyone.

And here, at the edge of the lamplight, stood the man who had sold them all to buy back his own guilt.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, the kind of morning when the river looked like poured pewter and the air tasted of iron. Isandro found Bram on the aft deck, sitting on an overturned crate with his elbows on his knees, staring at nothing. He didn't look up when Isandro approached. He'd stopped looking up at anyone three days ago. He ate what Nell left for him by the wheelhouse door and spoke only when spoken to, and even then barely, as though words were things he no longer trusted himself to carry.

Isandro sat down on the crate opposite him. For a long time neither of them said anything. The glacier cracked and groaned in the distance, patient as a clock.

"I've been thinking about leaving you here," Isandro said at last.

Bram's shoulders didn't move. "I know."

"There's a trading post forty miles downstream. Old Ferro's place. He'd take you on for the winter. You'd be safe from Hale there—Ferro doesn't ask questions and doesn't answer them either."

"I know that too."

"And you'd deserve it." Isandro kept his voice level, almost gentle, the way you speak to a man whose leg is caught in a trap. "You know what Hale does to witnesses, Bram. You heard him say it at the fueling station. If he catches us because of your mirror, he won't just take the map. He'll line us up on the deck and—" He stopped. There was no need to finish. They had both seen what Company men left behind at Thornmouth.

"I know," Bram said, and his voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "I've been knowing it since before I did it. That's the worst part, Vane. I knew exactly what I was doing while I was doing it. Same as before." His hands opened and closed around nothing. "I told myself it was different this time. That I'd learned. That these people—" his jaw worked, "—that you lot were worth more than my fear. And then the journals came out of those caves and I read what the Company did to those towns, and I thought: they did that to *towns*. What would they do to me? To Nell? And my hands started shaking and I couldn't stop them, and the mirror was right there, and I—" He finally looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and hollowed out, a man who had been hollowing himself for days. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not stupid enough to ask that. I'm just telling you I know what I am."

The glacier boomed in the distance, long and low, like a struck bell.

Isandro looked at him—at the deserter who had once hauled Nell out of the flooding cargo hold without hesitation, who had worked the winches through the Thornmouth rapids until his palms ran blood, who had carried Lior's grave-marker stone three miles up the bank because he said the old man deserved something heavier than driftwood. None of it erased the mirror. But none of it was erased *by* the mirror either. That was the thing about people Isandro had spent years refusing to learn: they were not sums. They did not balance.

"My father used to say a debt is a rope," Isandro said. "You can pull with it or hang with it, but either way somebody's holding the other end." He rubbed his jaw, feeling the week of stubble there. "I owe a dead man a promise. You owe four living ones a chance. I don't see how leaving you on a dock squares either account."

Bram stared at him. "Vane—"

"We leave with the next high water. Tomorrow, maybe the day after, depending on what the ice does." Isandro stood, knees cracking, suddenly very tired. "If you're aboard, you work. Harder than anyone. You don't touch the wheel, you don't touch the chart table, and you sleep where I can see you. And if you so much as look at a signal glass sideways, I'll put you over the side myself and let the river sort you out."

For a moment Bram just sat there, and Isandro saw something move across his face that might have been relief, or shame, or the strange grief of being forgiven before you felt you deserved it. Then the big man nodded, once, hard, like a man accepting a sentence.

"Aye," he said. "Aye, Captain."

"Don't call me that."

But Bram was already standing, already moving toward the engine room with a purpose Isandro hadn't seen in him since the ice caves, and Isandro stayed on the crate a while longer, watching the glacier calve in the distance, and admitted to himself what he'd never say aloud: he didn't feel forgiving. Not yet. Maybe not for a long time. But forgiveness, he was learning, wasn't a feeling. It was a heading. You set it and you steered by it and you trusted the feeling would catch up eventually, the way dawn always did, even on the grayest mornings.

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and the Kestrel had never looked so much like a dying animal being groomed for one last race.

Sefa worked over the charts by lamplight, annotating the channel through the calving zone with notes in her small, precise hand: *ice field shifts hourly — trust the water color, not the eye — green water means depth, white means death.* Nell ferried supplies and questions in equal measure, her energy the only bright thing on the boat, though even she had gone quiet whenever she passed Bram, and Isandro saw her watching him with a complicated expression—not hatred, not yet forgiveness either, but the fierce arithmetic of a child deciding whether a person could still be loved.

Isandro himself went over the engine with Bram, and it was strange, working shoulder to shoulder with the man, passing tools back and forth in the oily dark of the engine room, saying nothing that mattered and everything that didn't. *Hand me the nine-sixteenth. She's running hot on the port cylinder. Hold that there.* The ordinary liturgy of work, which was perhaps the only prayer either of them knew anymore.

By late afternoon the glacier's mood had changed entirely. The cracks came faster now, overlapping, continuous, a drumroll building toward something. The meltwater lake at its base had risen visibly, brown with rock flour, and great slabs drifted down-valley past the Kestrel's anchorage, some of them big enough to swamp a boat, all of them hissing softly as they melted.

"It's going to let go soon," Sefa said, joining him at the rail. She smelled of lamp oil and pencil shavings, and her hair was escaping its braid in gray-streaked wisps. "The whole face. When it goes, the channel opens, but it also means the ice upstream is shifting. We'll have maybe sixty hours of navigable water. Less if we're slow."

"Hale?"

"Smoke again, an hour ago. Closer." She paused. "He'll have seen the calving too. He knows the window same as we do."

Isandro watched the distant wall of ice shudder and shed another house-sized block. "Then we go tonight."

"Tonight." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "Through the calving zone. In the dark."

"In the dark, with the ice falling, with a gunboat behind us and a sealed valley ahead." He turned to her, and despite everything—despite the fear sitting in his chest like ballast—found himself almost smiling. "You've navigated worse."

"I've never navigated anything like this," she said. But her hand found his on the rail, and her grip was steady. "Neither have you. That's rather the point, isn't it? We do it anyway."

* * *

They cast off at moonrise, such as it was—a thin rind of moon behind cloud, giving the world a washed-bone pallor. The Kestrel moved up the lake toward the glacier's face, and the closer they came, the more the sounds changed: less thunder now, more tearing, a vast wet ripping as the ice face loosened sheet by sheet into the water.

Nell stood at the bow with a lantern, calling depths by the lead line, her voice clear and young and steady in a way that made Isandro's chest ache. Sefa was at the chart table, then at the windows, then at the chart table again, navigating by instinct and starlight and thirty years of ruined maps. Bram was below, feeding the boiler with a fury, as though he could outrun his own betrayal on steam pressure alone. And Isandro had the wheel, as he always had the wheel, the worn wood familiar under his palms.

The first true calving came at midnight.

The whole lower face of the glacier let go at once—a cliff of ice half a mile wide sliding into the lake with a roar that swallowed thought. The wave it raised came at them like a moving hill, and Isandro had one heartbeat to turn the Kestrel's bow into it, shouting for everyone to hold on, and then the world was water and noise and the terrible creaking of an old hull asked to do one more impossible thing.

They rode it out. Barely. When the spray settled, Nell was soaked and laughing—a wild, ringing laugh—and Sefa had not moved from the chart table, and Bram came up the ladder two steps at a time to check the hull seams, reporting in a voice that shook only slightly: "Holding. She's holding."

Beyond the settling chop, where the glacier's face had stood, there was now a gap. A channel. Dark water threading between walls of blue ice, opening into the unknown upstream.

"The window," Sefa breathed. "It's open."

Isandro rang the engine bell twice—full ahead—and the Kestrel limped toward the gap with her boilers straining and her seams weeping and her crew of broken, borrowed souls standing watch in the freezing dark.

Behind them, far down the lake, a searchlight bloomed against the clouds like a false dawn.

Hale had found the window too.

* * *

The channel narrowed as they pushed upstream, the ice walls rising sheer on either side, ancient and luminous, glowing faintly from within as though the glacier were a lantern with something alive inside it. Chunks of ice the size of sheep drifted past, ticking against the hull. Every few minutes the glacier groaned overhead and everyone flinched, and every few minutes after that, nothing happened, and everyone exhaled, and the Kestrel pressed on.

It was near the narrowest point of the passage—walls close enough that Isandro could have thrown a bottle against either side—that the ice ahead began to move.

Not calving. Sliding. A shelf of it, a whole ramp of shattered blocks, detaching from the eastern wall and slumping across the channel with a grinding slowness that was somehow worse than any explosion. In an hour, maybe two, the way would be shut.

"Sefa?"

Her eyes moved across the water, the ice, the closing gap. "We make it or we don't," she said simply. "Full ahead. Steer for the green water, port side of the slide. And pray the old girl holds together."

Isandro rang the bell. The Kestrel surged forward into the narrowing dark, ice shrieking along her hull, the gap ahead shrinking with every hammering heartbeat, the searchlight astern growing brighter, and Isandro Vane gripped the wheel of his dying boat and steered straight into the last open water he might ever see.
