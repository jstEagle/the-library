## 16. The White Reach

The glacier opened at dawn with a sound like the world's spine cracking.

Sefa was on deck before her boots were fully laced, chart case clutched to her chest like a child, and she stood at the rail watching a wall of ice the height of a cathedral lean, groan, and shed itself into the river in a thunder of white ruin. The spray reached them a full minute later—a cold breath off the water that smelled of stone and ancient dark—and when it passed, the channel ahead lay open: a corridor of black water walled in blue-green ice, winding away north until the mist took it.

"Days," Isandro said beside her. His voice was rough from a night without sleep. "Maybe three. Maybe less."

"Then we don't waste one," Sefa said.

She had spent the night doing what she had once done for the Royal Survey, what she had done for eleven years before a superior's forged signature ended her career: she had built the river in her head. Lior's journals gave her the bones—the bends, the shallows, the two narrows he'd named in a hand that grew shakier with every page. Nell had copied the relevant passages three times over by lamplight while Bram sat apart, silent as furniture, and Isandro had stared at nothing for hours. But charts were only ever a promise. The river would decide how much of it to keep.

Now she unrolled her own work across the pilothouse chart table—pencil lines on butcher paper, corrections inked over corrections, the whole thing looking like a wound that kept being reopened. Isandro studied it without touching it.

"You've marked six hazards."

"I've marked six I know about." She tapped the third bend. "Lior called this one the Grinder. Ice debris collects here after a calving. If we hit it at speed—"

"We won't hit it at speed."

"If we *don't* hit it at speed, we lose steerage in the current and drift into it anyway." She looked up at him. He looked terrible—hollowed, gray around the eyes, a man carrying more weight than any hull could hold. She softened her voice despite herself. "Isandro. There is no slow way through this. There's only the way through."

He was quiet a moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he did when he'd stopped arguing with himself and started agreeing with someone else.

"Call your stations," he said.

* * *

The Kestrel entered the White Reach an hour after sunrise, and within twenty minutes Sefa understood that everything she had prepared was a letter written to a stranger.

The channel did not hold still. It breathed. Walls of ice calved on both sides—not the great cathedral collapses of dawn but constant, grinding exhalations, house-sized blocks shrugging loose and plunging into water already crowded with floes. Each impact sent a swell down the corridor that lifted the Kestrel's bow and dropped it like a toy in a bath. Freezing spray coated everything: the rails, the winches, Nell's hair, the glass of the pilothouse windows, so that Sefa navigated increasingly through a world seen through rippled ice, wiping a porthole clear with her sleeve every few seconds and finding the river changed each time.

"Starboard!" she shouted. "Hard starboard—floe field closing the left channel!"

Isandro threw the wheel over. The old boat groaned, listed, and threaded between two drifting slabs close enough that Sefa heard them scrape the hull—a long, shrieking kiss of wood and ice that went through her teeth.

"Bilge!" Bram's voice from below, already hoarse. "We're taking water! Slow!"

*Slow* meant dead. In this current, with this traffic, a boat that lost way became a log in a millrace. Sefa bent over her chart, hands shaking—from cold, she told herself, only cold—and made the calculation no surveyor should have to make: which leak could they afford.

"Keep her at speed," she called down. "Pump in shifts. Bram, you're first shift, then me."

She heard him swear, heard the pump begin its laboring rhythm, and put it out of her mind because ahead the channel bent hard to port around a spur of blue ice and beyond the bend, according to Lior, waited the Grinder.

* * *

It was worse than the journals described.

The narrows were choked—not with floes but with bergy bits, the shattered leavings of the morning's great collapse, riding the current in a tumbling white herd. And through the middle of it, where the deepest water ran, the current moved fastest, which meant the only path through the Grinder was straight down its throat.

Sefa stood at Isandro's shoulder, one hand on the wheel housing, reading the water the way she'd once read tide tables: the V of smooth current between the chaos, the darker seams where depth lived, the telltale boil where something waited just under the surface. Her mouth had gone dry. Her heart was going like a pump itself.

"There," she said quietly. "Between the big doublet and the submerged shelf. Hold that seam. Don't chase the smooth water past it—that's the trap, it looks open and it's not, there's a fin of ice under it."

"How do you know?"

"Because the current folds around it. Look—" she pointed, "—the ripple pattern. Water doesn't lie, Isandro. It tells you everything if you speak it."

He glanced at her then, and even with death floating all around them, even with the bilge pump knocking below like a failing heart, something in his face made her breath catch. It wasn't gratitude exactly. It was recognition—the look of a man discovering that the person beside him was exactly who he'd begun to hope she was.

"Hold the seam," he said, and put the Kestrel into the Grinder.

For four minutes that lasted a season, they ran it. Ice ground along both sides of the hull with a sound like enormous teeth. A slab the size of a hay wagon rose directly ahead and Isandro feathered the throttle back—against every instinct, trusting her—and let the current swing the stern just wide enough, and the slab slid past their rail close enough to touch. Nell, lashed to the cargo ring amidships, was laughing. Actually laughing, high and wild, and some buried part of Sefa thought: *that girl will never be afraid of anything again, and God help whoever deserves it.*

Then the stern struck something soft and heavy, the wheel kicked in Isandro's hands, and the Kestrel began to spin.

"Floe under us—" Isandro fought the wheel. "Rudder's fouled—I can't—"

"Stern to the wall!" Sefa was already moving, pointing. "Let it take you—use the wall to pivot—starboard quarter against the ice, *now*, or we broach!"

He did it. It was the kind of maneuver no sane pilot attempts—driving his wounded stern deliberately into a moving wall of ice—but he trusted her voice over his own instincts, and the Kestrel kissed the ice wall, shuddered, and swung nose-downriver like a door on a hinge. The rudder cleared. The engine caught. They shot out of the Grinder's throat into open gray water, listing, leaking, alive.

In the ringing silence afterward, Isandro set the wheel and said, without looking at her:

"Marry me."

Sefa blinked spray out of her lashes. "What?"

"After. When we're through. Marry me, or I'll spend the rest of my life trying to find water complicated enough to impress you, and there isn't any."

She laughed—a cracked, freezing, joyful sound—and pressed her shoulder against his arm because her hands were too numb for anything better. "Ask me again when the boat stops sinking."

"It's not sinking. It's *taking on character*."

Below them, the bilge pump knocked on.

* * *

They lost the Kestrel by inches, over hours.

That was the thing no one told you about a dying boat—it didn't go down in one dramatic gasp. It died the way a man dies of cold: gradually, then suddenly, and mostly in the dark. Through the afternoon Sefa rotated through the pump with Bram, hauling the handle in a rhythm that matched her heartbeat, watching the water in the bilge rise and fall and rise a little higher each time. The hull had taken a crack below the waterline somewhere aft of the engine—ice strike, probably, during the spin—and no amount of caulking they could do underway would close it.

By late afternoon the list was visible. By dusk it was a fact of life; they'd shifted the remaining cargo to port, and the deck sloped underfoot like a hillside.

Nell found Sefa at the rail during the second watch change. The girl's face was smudged with coal dust and her eyes were red-rimmed, though whether from spray or something else, Sefa couldn't say.

"She's not going to make it, is she," Nell said. Not a question.

Sefa considered lying and rejected it. This girl had earned truth the way the rest of them had earned their scars. "No. I don't think so."

"The spring—is it far? Past the gate?"

"A day's walk, maybe two, once we're through the channel. Assuming we can land the boat before she goes under."

"Then she got us here." Nell said it simply, the way children state enormous things. "Lior picked her. She got us all this way." She looked up. "You'll chart where she goes down? So we can find her again someday?"

Sefa's throat closed. She nodded, and the girl squeezed her arm once—Bram's grip, learned from him, she realized—and went below to spell Isandro at the pump.

Sefa stayed at the rail a long time, watching the ice walls slide past in the failing light, luminous green-blue even now, beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful. She thought about the map folded against Isandro's chest. About the source waiting at the end of all this white. About fame, and names restored, and the survey she'd dreamed of since she was Nell's age. And she found, examining herself honestly as the cold found every seam in her coat, that what she wanted most in the world was no longer on any map. It was standing at a wheel ten feet away, refusing to give up on a sinking ship because the people aboard weren't done yet.

*Love,* she thought, *is just competence pointed at someone.* You learn their shoals. You read their currents. You keep them off the rocks. Everything else was poetry.

* * *

Night came down like a lid. The channel widened—Lior's journals promised a lake beyond the last narrows, a mile of open water before the landing below the source valley—and Sefa navigated the final miles by the ghost-glow of the ice itself, the walls shedding enough frozen light to read her chart by. The Kestrel limped. The pump never stopped. Somewhere behind them, faint as a headache, Isandro swore he could hear Hale's gunboat engines echoing off the ice, though Sefa couldn't confirm it and chose not to try.

At the mouth of the lake, the channel forked around a central island of packed ice, and the eastern passage—deeper, according to Lior—was also the one flowing toward a shelf that groaned ominously in the wind.

"Western passage," Isandro said, studying her chart. "Shallower, but stable."

"Eastern saves us forty minutes."

"And costs us what, if that shelf lets go?"

Sefa looked at the eastern gap—at the shelf above it, vast and blue and trembling faintly with each gust—and felt the old arrogance rise in her, the surveyor's certainty, the hunger to be right faster than anyone else. She let it rise. She watched it. And she let it go.

"Western," she said.

Isandro took them through the western passage at half speed, the keel whispering over bottom sand, and as they cleared the island the eastern shelf calved with a roar that rolled across the lake like artillery, burying the deep channel under a hundred tons of fresh ice.

Neither of them said anything. Isandro just reached over and squeezed her hand once, hard, and steered on.

They made the southern shore near midnight, nosing the Kestrel into a shallow cove of gravel and grounded ice, and ran her aground gently, deliberately, the way you'd lay down a friend who was too tired to stand. The list was bad now. The pump was losing its argument. Isandro went below alone—for a minute, two, three—and when he came back up his eyes were wet and his jaw was set like poured concrete.

"She held," he said, to all of them, standing on his tilting deck in the green glow of the glacier. "Longer than she had any right to. We walk from here."

They unloaded in the dark: crates, rope, the journals, weapons, food. Nell carried the lantern. Bram carried twice what anyone asked of him, saying nothing, working like a man doing penance with his spine. Sefa stood on the shore with her chart case and turned once to look at the Kestrel—her old bones, her stubborn heart, leaning against the ice like a faithful dog too exhausted to follow.

And then, across the black water of the lake, from the channel they'd come through, came a sound that froze her harder than the wind ever could: the low, patient thrum of an engine under load, and a searchlight beam sweeping the ice walls, drawing closer.

"They followed us through," Bram said flatly. "Through the Grinder. At night."

"He wants that map," Isandro said quietly, "more than he fears that river."

Sefa opened her chart case, found the pencil stub, and began, by lantern light, to draw the only route left to them—up the valley wall, on foot, with the source above and Corvus Hale below, and the Kestrel keeping her lonely watch in the cove behind them, already becoming a story they would tell for the rest of their lives.
