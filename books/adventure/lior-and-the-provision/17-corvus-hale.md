## 17. Corvus Hale

Bram heard the gunboat before he saw it—a sound like a knuckle cracking inside the mountain, rolling down the channel and arriving a half-second before the truth of it did. He was on deck with the ice pole, keeping the Kestrel's starboard side off the blue wall of the bend, and the sound went through the pole and into his hands and settled somewhere behind his sternum where all his worst knowledge lived.

He knew that sound. He had spent four years trying to forget it, and here it was again: the flat cough of a Company engine tuned for shallow water, running hard.

"Hale," he said, and his voice came out smaller than he meant it to.

Isandro was at the wheel already, of course. The man moved like weather—you didn't see him decide anything, you just looked up and there it was. He cut the throttle and the Kestrel swung her nose toward a shelf of gravel and old ice at the channel's edge, a gray tongue of shore no wider than the boat herself.

"Off the water," Isandro said. "Now. Sefa, take Nell onto the shelf. Bram—"

"I know." Bram was already hauling the rifle case from under the wheelhouse bench. His hands knew the latches better than his conscience did. That was the thing about the army; it never left you, it just waited in your fingers.

The gunboat came around the bend bow-first, throwing a wake that slapped the ice walls on both sides like applause. She was painted Provision gray with the black chevron of the Survey Enforcement Division on her flank, and she was bigger than Bram remembered, or maybe he was smaller. A swivel gun sat forward under an oiled tarp. Corvus Hale stood at her rail with his hands folded behind his back, the way a man stands when he has never once needed to grab something to keep his balance.

Through the speaking trumpet, Hale's voice arrived without hurry.

"Captain Vane. You've made excellent time. I'd hate to see it wasted."

* * *

They grounded the Kestrel on the shelf and got behind her hull, because a wooden boat is poor armor but it is some armor, and because standing in the open felt like volunteering. Sefa pulled Nell down behind the cargo crates they'd lashed amidships, and the girl's face over the crate edge was white as the glacier around them. Bram crouched at the bow with the rifle across his knees and discovered he was shaking—not from cold. From recognition. This was the shape of every bad morning he'd ever had: men with guns on one side of a narrow place, and him in the middle of it, holding something he'd been ordered to hold.

"You can't win this," Hale called. His voice carried strangely well off the ice, clean and reasonable, a voice built for ledgers. "I have thirty rounds of brass and a swivel gun, Captain. You have a boat that's already dying. Give me the map and the journals, and I'll put you all ashore below the calving line with your debt burned and my personal word you'll be left alone."

"Your word," Isandro called back, "is worth what you paid Lior for his."

A pause. Then, almost gently: "Lior sold himself to us twice, Captain. Once when he signed his claim over, once when he ran. We were patient both times. Patience is expensive. Eventually someone collects."

Bram watched Isandro's back. The captain didn't answer right away, and Bram understood why—the offer was good. It was so good it hurt. Debt erased, crew alive, whole future unburdened. Bram thought about the signal mirror in his coat pocket, the one he'd raised toward Hale's camp two nights ago, the betrayal sitting in him like swallowed glass. Part of him wanted to stand up right now and finish what he'd started. Walk out on the ice, hand over the map, end it. At least then the shooting would stop and everyone could hate him honestly instead of politely.

But then he saw Nell's eyes over the crate rim, fixed on him—not accusing, just watching, the way she watched everything now, having learned too young that adults lie—and Bram found that he could not move. Shame is a strange anchor. It held him better than courage ever had.

"The map isn't yours," Isandro said finally. "It never was. And neither is what's up this river."

"What's up this river," Hale said, "is a hole in the Company's ledger the size of a province. Everything else is sentiment." He turned his head slightly, and though Bram couldn't see his eyes at that distance, he felt them land on the boat, counting. "Mr. Ashen. You're a deserter. You know how this ends for men like you. Come out, and I'll enter you as recovered property instead of a corpse. The forms are kinder."

*That's me*, Bram thought. *Recovered property.* He'd been property since he was sixteen, first to the regiment, then to his own guilt, and the strange thing was how calm he felt hearing it named aloud. Something in him went very quiet and very clear, the way it had gone once before—at Thornmouth, at the massacre he still couldn't say the name of—and he had run from that clarity his whole life because he'd known even then what it would cost.

He stood up.

"Bram—" Sefa's voice, sharp.

"It's fine," he said, which was a lie, and walked out onto the ice.

* * *

The channel ice near the shelf was old and thick, ridged where the current had buckled it, and Bram walked along the ridge line with the rifle slung and his hands visible, the way you approach a checkpoint. The gunboat idled twenty yards off the ice edge, her prop wash grinding slush. Hale watched him come with the patience of a man watching a transaction complete itself.

"That's sensible," Hale said. "Sensible men live long."

"I want to ask you something first." Bram stopped at the ice edge, close enough to see the gun crew now—two men at the swivel, one at the helm, Hale amidships. Close enough to see that Hale's boots were good leather and his coat had no mending in it anywhere. "Thornmouth. Eleven years back. There was a town on the survey route that wouldn't sell its water rights. What happened to it?"

Hale's expression didn't change, which was itself an answer. "Towns come and go."

"There were children."

"Taxes go unpaid, Mr. Ashen. Someone always pays them." Hale leaned on the rail. "You weren't there. Why do you care?"

Because I was somewhere like it, Bram thought. Because I stood in a line of men just like these and did what the order said and told myself afterward for four years that following orders was a kind of innocence. Because last night a girl showed me pages from a dead man's journal and I read what my silence bought.

"Last chance," Hale said. "The map."

Bram reached into his coat. Hale nodded to his men, satisfied, and that nod—that small administrative dip of the head—was the thing that decided it. Not rage. Not heroics. Just the sight of a man ordering a death the way he'd order freight.

Bram drew the signal mirror.

He'd been thinking about it since the night Isandro caught him, since the family fractured and then, impossibly, hadn't—since Isandro had brought him anyway through the calving gate, forgiveness chosen before it was felt, and Bram had carried that unearned mercy like a wound that wouldn't close. He'd been thinking about what a mirror does. It doesn't make light. It only aims what's already there.

He angled it at the sun and flashed it into the eyes of the helmsman.

The man flinched back blind, and the gunboat's nose swung, just for a heartbeat, just enough—her port quarter kissed the ice ridge and ground along it with a shriek of timber on frozen rock. In that heartbeat Bram dropped the mirror, shouldered the rifle, and fired three times into the stern, low, where the rudder post came down through the hull. He'd serviced those posts for six years in the engineers' corps. He knew exactly what a rudder hinge looked like from outside, and exactly what a bullet did to one.

The first round sparked off iron. The second punched through. The third he sent into the tillage linkage as the boat slewed, and then the world answered him all at once: the swivel gun roared, the shelf behind him erupted into gravel and shattered ice, and something took him across the side like a door slammed by God.

* * *

Pain arrived late, the way it does when the body is busy. He was on his back on the ice with the sky wheeling above him, white and enormous, and his left arm wasn't answering. There was a wet heat spreading under his coat that the cold was already turning to syrup. He heard shouting—Isandro's voice, raw, a word that might have been his name—and the crackle of rifle fire from the shelf where Sefa was returning fire with more discipline than he'd ever credited her, covering him, dragging him home by bullets.

The gunboat was in trouble. He could see it even flat on his back: she was circling wide and wrong, her stern swinging loose, the helmsman hauling the wheel through dead water. Without her rudder she was a promise the river intended to break. The current here ran hard against the outer bend, and the outer bend was icefall country—seracs leaning over the channel like rotten teeth.

"Get him off the ice!" Isandro. Hands under Bram's shoulders then, hauling, and Nell there too, impossibly, her small fists in his collar, and together they dragged him up the shelf while the swivel gun thundered again and a round took the Kestrel's wheelhouse apart in a bloom of splinters.

From the shelf, Bram watched the rest through a graying haze. The gunboat, steering by engines alone, misjudged the bend. Her bow caught the ice wall a glancing blow, spun her stern out into the main current, and the river—took an interest. She swept downstream sideways, gathering speed, Hale standing at her rail still giving orders to men who could no longer execute them, and then the current set her toward the shallows below the point and she grounded hard on a gravel bar with a sound like a coffin lid.

Not sunk. Not destroyed. But lame, and listing, and a long day's repair from being dangerous.

For a moment the channel was silent except for the glacier groaning somewhere above them, patient as arithmetic.

Then Hale's voice came across the water, and Bram had to give the man this: it hadn't lost anything. No rage, no shake. Just recalculation.

"You've cost me a rudder, Mr. Ashen. I'll note it in your file." A pause, and the sound of men working, axes maybe, rigging. "Understand what you've done. You've delayed an accounting, not escaped one. I will go downriver, I will return with the fleet tender and forty men, and I will seal this valley with every soul in it inside. That is not a threat, Captain Vane. It is a schedule."

The last thing Bram heard clearly was Hale saying, almost conversationally: "Bring the map to me before then, and I'll let the girl keep her name out of the report."

* * *

They got him into the wheelhouse—or what was left of it—and Sefa cut his coat open with a boat knife while Nell held a lantern steady and Isandro steered them off the shelf and back into the channel, because staying was dying. The bullet had gone through the meat of his shoulder and out the back, missing the bone by luck or by whatever passes for luck among men like him. Sefa packed it with snow boiled down to salt water and bound it tight, her hands quick and certain, and she never once looked away from his face while she worked, which was somehow worse and better than any gentleness.

"You idiot," she said quietly.

"Yeah," Bram said.

"You signaled them. Two nights ago. And then you—" She stopped, jaw working. "Why?"

Bram looked past her, through the broken window, at the white valley sliding by. He thought about the mirror in the snow, shot through now, probably, or lost. He thought about orders, and the men who give them, and the small administrative nods.

"Because I've been on his side of the water my whole life," he said. "Figured it was time I found out what the other side costs."

Sefa tied off the bandage harder than she needed to. "It costs blood."

"I know." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Cheaper than I expected, actually."

Nell was crying without any noise at all, the way orphans cry, efficiently, and Bram reached over with his good arm and put his hand on her head like she was something worth guarding, because she was, because they all were, and that was the entire revelation, arriving eleven years late and costing him a shoulder.

"They're coming back with a fleet," Isandro said from the wheel. His voice was level but Bram had learned to hear the load-bearing strain underneath it. "Days, not weeks. Whatever we're going to do up there, we do it fast."

Above them, far up the white reach, the source waited, luminous and corrupting, a spring that drove men mad with wanting. And below, gaining nothing yet losing nothing, a lame gunboat limped toward the fleet that would come to bury them all.

Bram closed his eyes and let the pain carry him under, and his last thought was a strange, light one: *the file on me is going to be very thick, and none of it will be true anymore.*
