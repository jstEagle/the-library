## 18. The Source

They climbed for two days out of the calving channel, and the river climbed with them—thinner now, angrier, a white thread stitching itself through stone that had never learned to be gentle. The Kestrel was behind them, dead in the flooded dark of the approach, and Isandro did not let himself think about her. Not yet. Grief was cargo he could not afford to carry up this grade.

Bram went first, because Bram always insisted on going first since the ice, as if he could put his body between his people and whatever came next. Sefa followed with the compass and Lior's journal strapped against her chest like a second heart. Nell came third, light-footed on the scree, and Isandro took the rear, which was where captains belonged when there was no longer a boat to command—the last man off the deck.

The air changed on the second morning. He noticed it before he understood it: a sweetness, faint and mineral, like the smell of a struck flint or water running over iron. Nell stopped so suddenly that Sefa nearly walked into her.

"Do you taste that?" the girl said. "In the air. It tastes like—" She frowned, hunting for the word. "Like coins held in your mouth too long."

"Spring's close," Bram said, and spat, as if to clear the taste away. It didn't work. Isandro tasted it for hours after, coating his tongue, settling into the back of his throat like a promise someone else had made on his behalf.

They crested the final shoulder of rock at midday, when the sun stood high enough to pour straight down into the valley beyond, and Isandro Vane—who had run the Verrow for nineteen years, who had seen the river at flood and frozen, who believed he had already seen everything water could do—stopped walking and forgot how to breathe.

* * *

The valley was small. That was the first shock. After all of it—the rapids, the ice, the deaths and near-deaths—he had imagined something vast, a cathedral of a place worthy of the journey. Instead it was barely half a mile across, a bowl of grey granite cupping a pool no larger than the Kestrel's hold.

But the pool *glowed.*

Not reflected light. The sun was overhead and the water shone from beneath, a pale gold-green luminescence that moved in slow coils, like smoke underwater, like something alive turning over in its sleep. Where the spring overflowed—a modest trickle spilling over a lip of white stone—the runoff ran luminous too, threading down the valley floor in veins of light before sinking into the gravel and vanishing. The rocks around the pool were stained in rings: ochre, rust, a green so deep it looked painted. Minerals laid down over centuries, patient as sediment, beautiful as sin.

"It's warm," Nell whispered. She had drifted forward without anyone noticing, drawn the way iron filings are drawn, and now she crouched at the pool's edge with her hand hovering above the surface. Steam curled off the water despite the cold. "It's warm and it's glowing and it's—" She laughed, a small startled sound. "It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen."

Isandro felt it too. He would not pretend otherwise, not even to himself. Something in his chest had gone loose and golden, some knot he'd carried since his father's funeral loosening strand by strand, and a voice that was not quite his own whispered: *here, here is where the debt ends, here is where everything you lost comes back to you.* His hands ached to touch the water. His mouth watered. He wanted, with an intensity that frightened him, simply to stand here forever in the light of it.

He caught Sefa's eye across the pool and saw the same hunger banked behind her expression, carefully controlled, the way she controlled everything—and that frightened him more than his own wanting did.

"Nobody touches the water," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Nobody drinks. Nobody fills a flask."

"We hauled water up the last three miles," Bram said. "We're fine."

"Say it anyway."

Bram looked at him a long moment, then nodded once. "Nobody touches."

Nell withdrew her hand slowly, reluctantly, the way children pull away from a stove they've been warned about but don't quite believe in.

* * *

They made camp well back from the pool, at the valley's mouth, where the sweet mineral taste was thinnest. While Sefa and Bram rigged a shelter against the wind, Isandro sat with Lior's journal on his knees and read the final pages by the flat white light of the afternoon.

The old prospector's handwriting deteriorated as the pages turned. The early entries were neat, surveyor-precise—dates, elevations, sketches of the approach. Then the letters began to lean. Then to sprawl. Then to fracture into fragments, sentences abandoned mid-thought, whole passages crossed out and rewritten in a hand that shook.

*Found it at last. More than I dreamed. The water carries ore in solution—you can see it, gold and something else, something that shines green. A man could scoop fortune out with a cup.*

Two pages later:

*Vasquez drank from the pool against my warning. Says he feels twenty years younger. Says we should stay. I want to leave. Vasquez does not.*

Then:

*Vasquez will not leave. He stands at the edge all night watching the light. He speaks to it. He has stopped speaking to me. He says the spring told him we are rich, we are rich, we are rich, and when I asked how a spring can talk he looked at me like I was the mad one.*

Then, in a hand gone almost illegible:

*I tried to carry some out. Filled a flask at dawn. By noon I had drunk it. I do not remember deciding to drink it. I remember the weight of it in my hand and then I remember the taste and then I remember nothing until the flask was empty and I hated myself and started filling it again. This is what the Company knows. This is why they poison towns. Not to hide gold. Gold is only money. This is worse than money. This takes the wanting you already have and feeds it until there is no room left for anything else. I have watched a good man forget his own daughter's face because the light was prettier.*

The last entry was dated eleven days before Lior collapsed on Isandro's dock. The handwriting was steady again—steadier than it had been in fifty pages—as if writing it had cost everything and left him clean.

*I am dying. The fever, or the mountain, or maybe just the years. Good. Dying men can walk away from things living men cannot. If you are reading this, I got out, and if I got out, you can too. Do not claim it. Do not chart it. Do not sell it. There is no price that survives contact with this water. Every man who ever owned it was owned by it instead. The Company sealed the valley not out of greed but terror—they know better than anyone what their treasure does, and they will kill to keep it buried because a secret this ugly cannot survive daylight.*

*Carry the map upstream. That is all. Carry it, and then decide what a debt owed to everyone requires.*

Isandro closed the journal and sat for a long time with his hands flat on its cover, feeling the wind move through the valley mouth, tasting copper on every breath.

* * *

He walked to the pool alone at dusk.

He hadn't planned to. Or perhaps he had, and had simply declined to admit it, the way he'd declined to admit for four hundred miles that he intended to keep the map. The light was different at dusk—deeper, slower, the gold-green coils turning like the insides of a shell held to the ear. Up close he could see the ore glittering in suspension, flecks of brightness drifting in the warm current, wealth beyond counting suspended in water that wanted to be counted.

He knelt at the edge—not touching, keeping a hand's width between himself and the surface—and took the map from inside his coat.

Lior had drawn it in grease pencil and ink on sailcloth, and it had survived rapids, ice, fire, and betrayal. It showed the approach channel, the glacier gate, the calving window, the valley. It showed everything Hale's fleet would need. In another hour it would be dark, and tomorrow they would begin the long walk out, and every mile they carried this cloth was a mile it could be taken from them.

He understood, kneeling there, what the true promise had been. Not *carry it upstream.* Lior had known Isandro would come this far; had gambled a dying man's last coin on the character of a stranger with kind hands and a closed face. No—the promise was this moment. The promise was a man standing at the source with the map in his hand and the choice finally, fully his. Lior couldn't order it destroyed. An order could be refused. But a debt—a debt owed to everyone—had to be paid freely or not at all.

*A debt owed to everyone.* Everyone downstream. Everyone who would drink the Verrow's water for the next thousand years. Everyone who might someday find this valley and stand where Isandro stood, feeling exactly this loosening in the chest, this golden untying, this voice whispering *here, here is where everything comes back.*

Because that was the trap, and he saw it now with terrible clarity: the spring didn't look like corruption. It looked like redemption. It offered each man precisely the thing he most lacked. For Vasquez, youth. For Lior, fortune. For Isandro, standing knee-deep in his own exhaustion—an end to debt, an end to running, a home. It would offer Sefa her name restored in every atlas in the world. It would offer Nell a childhood's worth of safety in a single glowing handful. It would offer Bram absolution, though no water on earth could give him that.

And it would lie. It would give a little and take everything, the way the Company gave wages and took towns.

Isandro held the map over the water. The light rose up through the sailcloth and turned it translucent, and for a moment he could see both sides at once—the route in, the way back—overlapping, indistinguishable.

His hands did not shake. That surprised him. Nineteen years of running, and at the end of it, the steadiest moment of his life.

"Not tonight," he said aloud, to the pool, to Lior, to himself. "Tonight I sleep on it. You've waited ten thousand years. You can wait one more day."

Because there was one conversation he could not avoid, and he would not destroy the map behind her back. Whatever came at dawn—whether Sefa cursed him or thanked him or walked out of this valley without him—he owed her the argument first. Love that decided for the other person wasn't love. It was just another kind of owning, and he had come all this way to stop being owned.

He folded the map along its old creases and put it back inside his coat, against his heart, where it beat a little faster than he did.

Behind him, at the camp, he heard Nell laugh at something Bram said, bright and ordinary, and the sound carried down the valley and broke across the glowing water like a stone.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow the argument. Tomorrow the choice.

But when he rose and turned back toward the firelight, Isandro Vane found himself glancing at the pool one last time—and the pool, he could have sworn, was watching him back.
