## 19. What Power Wants

Sefa had not slept. None of them had, but she wore it differently than the others—Bram sat by the fire like a man waiting to be sentenced, and Nell had finally gone down under a blanket near the spring's outflow, exhausted past dreaming, but Sefa lay on her back on the cold stone with her eyes open, watching the light.

The spring did not behave like water should. It rose from a basin of pale rock in the heart of the valley, and it glowed—not reflected light, not moonlight caught and thrown back, but something generated from within, a soft blue-white luminescence that pulsed slow as breathing. The minerals that gave it that glow were worth more per ounce than gold. Lior's journals said so. The Company's burned towns said so. And every hour she spent looking at it, Sefa felt the old hunger wake in her chest and stretch, like a cat that had been asleep for years.

She was a cartographer. She had been a cartographer before she was a fugitive, before the Provision Company stripped her name off every survey she'd ever signed and handed her work to men who hadn't earned it. She had spent eleven years drawing coastlines and riverbeds for other people's fortunes, and the one thing she had ever found that was truly hers—truly *new*—was lying forty feet away, glowing in the dark, and Isandro wanted to erase it.

She could hear him moving behind her. He'd been awake too; she'd heard him turn over a dozen times, heard the creak of his bedroll, the small sounds of a man wrestling something he couldn't win against. Finally his boots crunched on stone and he came to sit beside her at the edge of the basin, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you."

"I will." He didn't move to. He watched the water the way she'd watched it all night—with that careful, guarded expression she'd learned to read somewhere between Thornmouth and the ice line. Not wonder. Calculation. A man measuring a thing for its weight and its danger both.

"Isandro." She turned toward him. In the spring's light his face looked younger and older at once, the lines around his eyes deepened by the glow, his mouth set in the way it set when he'd already decided something and was only waiting for the right moment to say it. "I know what you're going to do with the map."

"Do you."

"I've known since we came through the gate. You didn't bring us here to find it. You brought us here so you could end it." She kept her voice low, so as not to wake Nell. "You're going to burn it. Or drop it in the water. One of those."

He was quiet a long moment. "And if I am?"

"Then I'm asking you not to."

* * *

The argument did not begin as an argument. It began, as the worst ones do, as a conversation between two people who loved each other, sitting in beautiful light, each certain they were being reasonable.

"I'm not asking you to sell it," Sefa said. "I'm not asking you to give it to the Company or dig the valley full of shafts. I'm asking you to let me chart it. Coordinates, depth soundings, the mineral composition if we can test it. A record. That's all. Put it somewhere no one can use it unless they already know what they're looking for."

"And where's that?" Isandro asked. "Where do you put a record that Corvus Hale can't find? That man has half the Company's money behind him and a fleet coming upriver. You think a locked drawer in Greyford will stop him? You think anything short of nothing will stop him?"

"Then I keep it myself. I memorize it. Paper burns, but—" she tapped her own temple, "—this doesn't. I carry it. When I'm old, when Hale is dead and the Company's fallen apart the way they all eventually do, I write it down and I give it to someone who deserves it. Medicine. Metallurgy. There are things in this water that could change lives, Isandro. Lior's journals say the spring water itself heals wounds. *Heals wounds.* And we're going to drown it because one company poisoned three towns?"

"Because one company poisoned three towns *for it*," Isandro said. "That's my whole point, Sefa. It isn't the spring that kills people. It's the wanting. You're looking at it right now and telling me you can hold it in your head for fifty years and never once be tempted."

The words landed harder than he probably meant them to. She felt them land—felt the truth under the accusation, which was that she *was* tempted, had been tempted since the moment Nell spread the map across the Kestrel's deck and Sefa recognized the survey style and felt, for the first time in six years, that the world still had room in it for her name.

"That's not fair," she said quietly.

"It's completely fair. It's the fairest thing I've ever said to anyone." He wasn't angry. That was the terrible part—he spoke with the flat gentleness of a man stating weather. "I've watched you look at this water all night. I know what it's doing, because it's doing it to me too. Every man Lior wrote about started exactly where you're sitting. Reasonable. Careful. Certain they were different."

"I *am* different."

"Everyone says that."

* * *

They moved away from the basin eventually, without deciding to—the way you step back from a fire that's too hot—and walked up the valley floor among the frost-split boulders while the sky went from black to the color of iron. The spring's glow fell behind them, and Sefa felt its absence like a hand lifting off her shoulder, and hated how much lighter she felt, and hated herself for feeling lighter, because it proved his point.

"Tell me about your name," Isandro said.

She stopped walking. "What?"

"Your name. Before. What it would mean to get it back." He turned to face her. In the pre-dawn grey his eyes were nearly black. "That's what this is really about. Not medicine. Not metallurgy. You want to stand in front of whatever's left of the Survey Guild with a chart nobody else alive has seen, and watch them say your name out loud again. *Sefa Marun surveyed the source of the Verrow.* Am I wrong?"

She wanted to hit him. She wanted it physically, her fist closing at her side, because he was not wrong, and cruelty done accurately is worse than cruelty done carelessly.

"No," she said. "You're not wrong."

"Good. Then say the rest of it. Say what happens after the Guild says your name."

"After—" She faltered. "After, I'd be restored. My work would matter. Everything I lost—"

"Would come back. Yes. And then what? You think the Company lets a published chart of their worst secret sit in a Guild archive? The day your map exists, Hale doesn't need our testimony anymore. He needs a clean copy, and there are exactly two ways to get one." He held up two fingers. "Buy it, or bury it. And Sefa—you, specifically you—they'd never buy. So they'd bury. And everyone who knew where the copy came from gets buried alongside it. Me. Nell. Bram, if he lives. Everyone you love dies so your name can live."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it? Ask Lior. Oh, wait." His voice cracked on it, just slightly, and she saw that beneath the flatness he was bleeding too. "Ask the three towns. Ask the surveyors the Company sent in first, the ones whose names aren't on anything anymore. You told me yourself, reading those journals—every person who drew this place died badly. Every single one. And you think you'll be the exception because your reasons are better?"

"My reasons ARE better!" It came out louder than she intended, echoing off the ice walls, and somewhere below them Nell stirred in her blankets. Sefa lowered her voice to a shaking whisper. "Lior was a prospector chasing gold. The Company are monsters chasing profit. I'm talking about *truth*, Isandro. The world is allowed to know things. The world SHOULD know things. Burying knowledge because knowledge is dangerous—that's what the Company does. That's literally their entire method. Seal it, hide it, kill whoever saw it. And you want us to do the same thing with cleaner hands and call it virtue?"

For a long time he didn't answer. The glacier groaned somewhere above them, a deep tectonic complaint, and loose snow whispered down the far wall of the valley.

"Yes," he said finally. "Yes, I do. Because there's a difference, Sefa, and it matters. They seal it to own it. We'd seal it to free it. A thing nobody owns can't be fought over. A thing nobody knows can't make anyone crazy. Lior called it a debt owed to everyone—I used to think he meant the treasure. He didn't. He meant the *ending*. The mercy of it staying closed." He stepped closer. "You want to tell the truth. I understand. But some truths are a loaded weapon lying in the street, and picking it up to show people how heavy it is doesn't make you honest. It makes you the next body."

"And who made you the judge of which truths stay buried?" Her voice broke on the last word. "Who died and gave you the map, Isandro? Because as I recall, a dying man put it in your hands and asked you to carry it upstream, and you've decided that meant destroy it, and you never once asked the rest of us—"

"He asked ME." Now he was loud too, and neither of them cared. "He held my hand and he died and he asked ME, and maybe that was a mistake, maybe he should have given it to a better man, but he didn't, he gave it to a debtor and a smuggler who knows exactly what people do for money because I've BEEN the person people do things for money, and I am telling you—if that chart exists anywhere, in any form, in ink or in memory, then someday, someone I love is going to be standing where Hale can reach them, and Hale is going to say: tell me what you know, or watch them suffer. And you'll tell him. Anyone would. Anyone human would. The only safe map is no map!"

"The only safe life is no life!" she shouted back. "By your logic we should burn every chart I ever made! Rivers drown children—burn the charts! Passes avalanche—burn them! You don't get to decide the world stays ignorant because ignorance is safer, Isandro, that's not protection, that's just a smaller cage—"

"NELL IS DOWN THERE."

The words came out ragged and raw, and the echo rolled away up the valley, and then there was silence except for the glacier and the wind and Sefa's own heartbeat in her ears.

"She's twelve years old," Isandro said, much quieter now. "She stowed away on my boat because there was nowhere else. She found the map. She read the journals. She thinks this spring is magic. And if your chart exists, then everything that happens to her for the rest of her life happens downstream of that chart. Every collector, every agent, every desperate man with a gunboat. You'd be writing her into the story forever. Is that the truth you want? Truth that costs Nell?"

Sefa stood very still. The anger was still there—it would not leave, not fully, not tonight—but underneath it something cold moved, something that looked at the shape of the future he was describing and could not find a flaw in it. She searched for one. She searched honestly, the way she'd once searched for errors in her own surveys, checking every bearing twice, and the bearing held. If the record existed, it would be hunted. If it were hunted, the people she loved would be leverage. There was no version of the ledger that balanced.

But oh, God, it hurt. It hurt like losing a limb. Eleven years of disgrace, and the one redemption within reach, and she had to choose between it and them, and the choosing felt like dying.

"If I agree," she said slowly, "if I let you destroy it—I need something from you."

"Anything."

"Don't say anything. You haven't heard it yet." She made herself look at him. "When it's done—when the map is gone and the approach is flooded and none of it can ever be undone—you let me grieve it. Properly. You don't comfort me, you don't explain to me why it's right, you don't touch me and tell me I made the noble choice. I get to be angry at you for as long as the anger lasts. Maybe years. Maybe forever. Can you live with that?"

Isandro's jaw worked. She watched him understand what she was offering—what it cost—and watched him refuse to pretend it was cheap.

"Yes," he said. "I can live with that."

"And one more thing." Her voice steadied, and to her own surprise, something in it turned hard and clear, the way it did when she worked. "If we're destroying it, we do it right. No half measures, no 'the map burns but I remember the bearings.' You were right about me, Isandro, and I hate you for it, but you were right. So I'm going to stand at that spring and I'm going to forget it on purpose. Every coordinate. Every depth. I'm going to take the best map in my head and I'm going to let it go, and if you ever catch me sketching it in the dirt at midnight, you have my permission to throw the sketch in the fire without asking." She exhaled. "That's my price. Witness me forgetting."

He reached for her hand. She let him take it, but she didn't close her fingers, not yet. Some doors open slower than others.

"Sefa," he said. "For what it's worth—I'd have chosen you over the name. If it had been my choice to make for you. I'd have chosen you every time."

"I know," she said. "That's the only reason I'm choosing it too."

* * *

They walked back down together as the sun cleared the eastern ice wall and struck the valley, and the spring blazed up like a piece of fallen sky, luminous and gorgeous and utterly indifferent to what they had decided about it. Bram was awake now, sitting up, watching them come with the wary eyes of a man who'd heard shouting in the night. Nell sat beside him, hair wild, hugging her knees, and her gaze went from Sefa's face to Isandro's and back again with the terrible quick intelligence of children who always know when something has ended.

"Well?" Nell said. "What are we going to do?"

Sefa looked at the spring one last time—at the light rising out of the earth like a promise, like a door, like everything she had ever lost coming back to offer her hand—and she made herself look away.

"We're going to need the journals," she said. "All of them. And the map. Bring everything to the water's edge."

Nell's face fell. Bram exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a pack he'd carried for years. And far down the valley, faint but unmistakable, carried on the cold morning air, came a sound that froze them all where they stood:

An engine. More than one. Coming up the frozen channel.

Hale had found the gate.
