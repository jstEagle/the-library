## 20. The Flooded Approach

The spring did not want to be left alone.

Isandro felt it the way a man feels eyes on his back in an empty room—a pressure at the nape of his neck, a low hum beneath the soles of his boots that was not quite sound and not quite silence. The water pooled in its basin of blue ice like something alive, luminous with that pale mineral glow, casting light upward onto their faces as though it were studying them. He had stood at its edge for what felt like an hour, map folded in his fist, and the longer he stood, the more he understood Lior's last journal pages: *it does not tempt you with gold. It tempts you with certainty.*

He turned away from it deliberately, the way you turn your back on a fire you've decided not to warm yourself by.

Sefa still stood at the rim. She hadn't spoken since their argument ended—not with resolution, but with exhaustion—and her face in the spring's light was the color of old bone. Nell sat hunched against a boulder, arms around her knees, Bram beside her with his bandaged shoulder and his ruined hand cradled in his lap like something borrowed. None of them looked whole. None of them would again.

"Say it," Sefa said quietly. "Whatever you've decided. Say it out loud."

Isandro unfolded the map.

It had come apart along its creases in the White Reach, grease and water and time working at it until it hung in soft panels, Lior's inked river running through them like a vein through paper skin. Up close he could see the prospector's careful hand—the soundings, the annotations, the little skull drawn at the glacier's mouth with the words *turn back* beneath it in a script gone shaky near the end. And past all of it, the source itself, marked with a symbol Isandro now understood: not a treasure mark, not an X, but a circle with a spiral inside. A whirlpool. A warning dressed up as a destination.

"A debt owed to everyone," he said. "That's what he told me. I thought he meant I owed him. Or the river owed him. Some accounting between two men." He smoothed one panel against his thigh. "He meant this. The map is a debt. And debts don't get paid by keeping them."

"You could bury it," Nell said. Her voice was small in the vast cold hush of the valley. "Under rocks. Nobody would—"

"The Company dug men out of graves for less than this." Isandro looked at her gently. "You read the journals, Nell. You know what they did to Marrow's Bend. They'll dig until they find it, or until there's nothing left of them to dig with. Paper buried is paper waiting."

"Then burn it," Bram said. "Right here. It'd be ash before the wind finished taking it."

"And Hale's fleet comes anyway, next season, the season after. They have half the survey already—that's why they killed everyone who ever held the other half. Burning the map buys us nothing but our own ignorance." Isandro crouched at the basin's edge, where the outflow channel ran narrow and shallow toward the ice shelf that sealed the valley's throat. He could hear it now, up close—the water moving behind the shelf, patient as a debt collector. "But the mountain doesn't need convincing. The mountain just needs permission."

Sefa came to stand beside him. For a long moment she said nothing, and he let her have the silence, because he knew what she was giving up. Not fortune. Something rarer than fortune. A name written back into the record. The first true chart of the Verrow's headwaters, in her own hand, the thing that would have made Sefa Reyes mean something again in every hall where maps were hung.

"If we do this," she said finally, "there's no proof any of it happened. No proof we were here. No proof I—" She stopped. Started again. "The world will go on believing the source is a story drunk prospectors tell."

"The world believed that before us," Isandro said. "It can keep believing it. That's the payment. That's the whole price—we pay it with our names instead of our lives."

She laughed then, short and bitter and real. "Lior paid with his life."

"Lior paid with everything he had. So do we." He rose. His knees cracked in the cold, and the sound seemed enormous in the valley. "Everyone gets a say. This isn't mine alone. It never was—not since Greyford Landing, not since the vote below decks. So. All in favor of drowning the approach and ending this for good?"

Nell raised her hand first, fast, the way children vote—whole body committed. Then Bram, slow, wincing as his ruined arm shifted. Then Sefa, after a breath long enough that Isandro felt his heart stop in the gap of it.

"All opposed?"

Silence. The spring glowed. Somewhere deep in the glacier, ice settled with a groan like an old man turning over in bed.

"Then let's finish what a dying man started," Isandro said.

* * *

The Kestrel lay where the calving channel had broken her, wedged against a shelf of blue ice a quarter mile down the valley, listing hard to port, her stern flooded to the rail. Seeing her from above—small, wounded, absurdly faithful—did something to Isandro's chest that he refused to name. Twenty-two years. She had carried grain and gunpowder, fugitives and coffins, and every year of his father's shadow, and she had never once put him ashore against his will.

They spent the afternoon stripping her. There wasn't much worth taking; the rapids and the ice had already taken their tithe. But they salvaged what they could—the brass fittings, the ship's bell that had rung his father's name across three decades of river, the small iron stove from the galley, coils of rope, the medicine box, Nell's crate of books wrapped in oilcloth. Bram rigged a sledge from a hatch cover and runners of salvaged plank. It was grim, cold work, hands too numb for finesse, and nobody spoke much except to warn of loose footing.

It was Nell who found the figurehead.

The Kestrel had never had one—or Isandro had always assumed she hadn't, the bow too battered to remember ornamentation. But there, split free of the hull where the ice had torn her open, lay a carved bird no bigger than a gull, wings swept back, painted once in faded green. His father must have set it there before Isandro was born. He turned it over in his hands a long time, feeling the worn smoothness of it, and then he handed it to Nell without a word, because some things are lighter when someone else carries them.

By late afternoon the sledge was loaded and the sky had gone the color of pewter, low clouds dragging themselves over the valley rim. The wind had picked up out of the north, carrying the smell of snow. Good, Isandro thought. Snow would cover their tracks. Snow would help erase them.

He stood at the bow one last time. Put his palm flat against the wet planking, the way you'd touch the shoulder of an old horse.

"You did right by me," he told her. "Better than I deserved."

Then he climbed down and didn't look back, because looking back was how men talked themselves into drowning.

* * *

The ice shelf sealing the valley's throat was a wall forty feet high and, by Sefa's estimate, nearly a hundred thick where it bridged the gorge—a rampart the calving season had built and never dismantled, holding back the spring's outflow the way a cupped hand holds water. Behind it, the meltwater had gathered all winter into a lake that pressed against the ice with quiet, astronomical force. All it lacked was a flaw. A place to begin.

Isandro had found the flaw that morning, while the others slept: a crevasse in the shelf's landward flank, dark and breathing cold air, running back into the ice like a keyhole waiting for its key. The question was what to put in the lock.

"Powder," Bram said. They crouched at the crevasse's mouth, wind keening over them. "We've got the blasting charges off the Kestrel's cargo—half case, maybe more. Set them deep, collapse the shelf from inside. It'll go all at once."

"It'll also announce itself to anyone within twenty miles," Sefa said. "If Hale has scouts on the ridge—"

"Hale's scouts are a day behind us and limping," Isandro said. "Bram made sure of that." He glanced at Bram, who looked away, jaw tight. Whatever it had cost him at the bend in the channel, disabling the gunboat's rudder under fire, he hadn't spoken of it and Isandro hadn't asked. Some payments you don't itemize. "No. Powder's too clean. Too quick. We need it to fail slowly enough that the water does the work—crack the shelf and let the lake tear it open. Otherwise we're just making noise."

"So what, then?" Nell asked. "We knock on it?"

"Fire," Isandro said. "And patience."

They built the pyre through the gray afternoon, hauling deadfall from the stunted pines at the valley's edge, stacking it in and around the crevasse until the hole was choked with wood. Isandro added the last of the lamp oil, soaked the pile until it reeked, and then stood over it with the match while the others watched from thirty yards back, silhouetted against the dying light.

He thought about saying something ceremonial. Words for the occasion. But everything true had already been said—to Lior's grave on the riverbank, to the crew below decks, to Sefa in the dark of the night watch, to himself a hundred times on a hundred watches. What remained was just work.

He struck the match and threw it.

The pyre went up with a roar that echoed off the valley walls, orange light leaping against the blue ice, heat washing his face like a hand. He fed it until his arms burned and the smoke columned straight up into the windless dark, and then he retreated and they waited, four figures in the freezing night, watching fire fight ice.

Ice fought back. That was the thing nobody told you about glaciers—they were not passive, not inert. As the heat worked into the crevasse, the shelf groaned and shrugged, shedding slabs, sealing its own wound in places. Twice Isandro thought the fire had died for good, and twice they crept back with more wood, more oil, feeding it through the small hours while the temperature fell and their breath froze in their beards and eyelashes. Nell fell asleep sitting up against the sledge. Bram stayed awake out of stubbornness, feeding the fire one-armed, refusing help.

Near dawn, the ice gave up the argument.

It started as a sound like a rifle shot—a single crack that snapped across the valley and came back twice. Then another. Then a dozen, overlapping, running up and down the shelf's length, and Isandro felt the ground shift beneath his boots, a deep tectonic shudder, and he was shouting them back, back, farther back, and they ran for the high ground at the valley's side as the whole face of the shelf began to move.

It did not collapse. It *failed*—the way a dam fails, the way a promise fails, all at once and forever. A seam opened down its center and the lake behind it found the seam and widened it with the casual violence of ten thousand tons of water given somewhere to be. The shelf buckled, tilted, slid in white thunder into the gorge, and the flood came through behind it—a brown-white wall of meltwater and shattered ice, sixty feet wide, tearing down the approach canyon with a voice like the end of the world.

The sound went on and on. Long after the initial burst, the water kept coming, scouring the canyon, ripping out the talus slopes, erasing the trail, erasing the campsites, erasing every trace that anything had ever walked this way. Spray rose in clouds that caught the dawn light and turned to drifting rainbows. The ground shook steadily, a heartbeat too large to be a heartbeat.

Isandro stood on the ridge with the map in his hand and watched.

When the flood's first fury had passed and the canyon ran full-bore with churning meltwater—impassable, unchartable, unrepeatable—he took the map's panels one at a time and fed them into the current. They went reluctantly, catching the wind, plastering wet against rock, and then the water took each one and folded it under and it was gone. Lior's careful soundings. The little skull. The spiral mark. The words *a debt owed to everyone*, which Isandro had copied onto his own heart years too late to matter and exactly soon enough.

The last panel he held a moment longer. It showed the lower Verrow, the river he knew, the mudflats and landings and the small gray town where he'd been born under his father's shadow. He let the wind have it.

"There," he said. Nobody heard him but the water, and the water agreed.

* * *

The Kestrel went down an hour later.

They had moved upriver along the ridge to watch the flood pass through the wider valley, and so they saw it happen: the surge reaching the wounded boat where she lay wedged against the ice shelf, striking her beam-on, rolling her off her rest like a hand brushing a fly from a table. She righted once—his stubborn, faithful girl, righting herself even now—and then the water took her properly, pressed her under the surface, and drove her against the shelf until the hull gave. He heard it even over the flood's roar, a groan of parting timbers that he felt in his teeth, in his chest, in the place where twenty-two years lived.

She sank in nine feet of water, mast-down, her bell still lashed to its bracket, ringing once—a single clear bronze note bubbling up through the flood—as if announcing her own arrival wherever she had gone.

Nell was crying. He realized it distantly, the way you realize weather. Then Sefa's hand found his, cold fingers lacing through colder ones, and he understood that he was crying too, or something adjacent to crying, something that had no better name and needed none.

"She kept every promise you ever made her," Sefa said against his ear, under the roar.

"No," Isandro said. "I kept hers. She just carried me while I did it." He squeezed her hand. "Same difference, maybe."

Bram said nothing at all. He stood at attention, the way soldiers stand for burials, and held that position until the flood subsided to a broad, violent, permanent river where the approach had been—a river that would run for a hundred years and cut deeper every one of them, a river with no banks any mapmaker would ever bother to visit.

The valley behind them was already changing. Meltwater ponded in new shapes, ice groaned and resettled, and the spring—somewhere up the hidden channel, beyond the ridges—went on glowing for no one. Isandro felt its pull fade with every step they took away from it, like a fever breaking, like a hand unclenching. By the time they reached the saddle at the valley's head and looked back one final time, he felt nothing but the ordinary human grief of leaving a beautiful place, and underneath it, astonishing and unfamiliar, something light.

Not happiness. He knew happiness; it was a fair-weather friend. This was older and quieter. Liberation, maybe. The specific weightlessness of a man who has finally paid everything he owes and discovered that what remains—four people, a sledge, a carved bird, a long walk downstream—is not a loss at all.

"We're poor," Nell announced, wiping her face with her sleeve. "Like, actually poor. We don't even have a boat."

"We have legs," Bram said.

"We have each other," Sefa said, and rolled her eyes at herself even as she said it, and Nell laughed through her tears, and even Bram's mouth twitched.

Isandro shouldered the front harness of the sledge. The load was heavy and would grow heavier over the weeks of walking ahead, down out of the ice, through country none of them had crossed, toward rivers where people used their real names and rivers where they wouldn't. His shoulders accepted the weight the way they accepted all weights: without complaint, without ceremony.

"One condition," he said, setting off. "Nobody tells me I'm a hero. I've seen what happens to heroes on this river."

"What happens?" Nell asked, falling in beside him.

"They end up walking," Isandro said, and pointed his chin down the long white slope, where the morning sun was climbing and the meltwater ran bright and free toward a sea none of them would ever see. "Come on. Debt's paid. Let's go find out who we are when we're not running from anything."

Behind them, far below, the new river thundered on, keeping its secret, keeping theirs.
