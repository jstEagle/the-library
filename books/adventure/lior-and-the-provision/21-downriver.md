## 21. Downriver

Nell learned to count in weeks the way other children counted in coins.

The first week out of the valley, she counted blisters. She had eleven on her left foot and nine on her right, and Bram said that was a fair trade for having walked away from a glacier with all her toes still attached. He said it while carrying her on his back across a scree field, because her boots had split at the seams and Isandro had wrapped them in strips cut from the Kestrel's old ensign, and even wrapped they filled with grit like little hourglasses measuring how far she could go before she cried.

She didn't cry. She'd decided somewhere in the ice caves—maybe when she found Lior's journals, maybe before that—that crying was a thing she would do only when there was time for it, and there was never going to be time again.

* * *

They walked downriver because the river was the only map left.

Sefa had burned nothing, but she might as well have. The charts she'd made of the upper Verrow—the ones she'd argued so hard to keep, the ones Isandro had argued harder to destroy—had gone into the flood with everything else, soaked to pulp in the Kestrel's cabin as the water came down the valley like a held breath finally let out. What Sefa carried now was in her head, and what was in her head was dangerous, and so she did a strange thing: every night at the fire, she taught it away. She drew the river from memory in the dirt with a stick—the bends, the shallows, the seam of blue stone where Lior's spring had bled its light into the water—and then she swept it flat with her palm and drew something else. A bird. A boat. Four stick figures holding hands.

"Memory is a chart you can't confiscate," she told Nell one night, low, while the men argued about whether the fish they'd caught were safe to eat this close to the old Company posts. "But some things shouldn't be carried whole. So I'm breaking them up. Giving the river away a piece at a time, to people who'll only ever hold one piece."

"To who?"

"To anyone we meet. The ferryman at Harlow Bend. The woman drying nets at Colder's Reach. By the time anyone thinks to ask us where the source was, we won't be able to tell them—not all together, not in order. It'll be like asking the rain where it came from." She smiled, and it was a tired smile but a real one. "That's not destroying the truth, Nell. That's planting it where it can't be dug up."

Nell thought about that for three days, walking. She decided it was the most cartographer thing she'd ever heard, and that when she grew up she wanted to know things the way Sefa knew things—which was to say, completely, and then to give them away on purpose.

* * *

The second week, she counted meals.

There were fewer than there should have been. They'd come off the mountain with almost nothing: two blankets, a dented kettle, Bram's knife, Isandro's coat, Sefa's instruments in their velvet-lined case (she would not leave those; Nell understood without being told that the case was the last honest thing from Sefa's old life), and forty-one feet of good rope salvaged from the Kestrel's deck winch. No money. No cargo. No boat.

Isandro had stood on the shore the morning after the flood and watched the water where his boat had gone under, and he hadn't said anything, and Nell had taken his hand because someone had to, and his hand had been shaking, and he'd let her hold it until it stopped.

"We're poorer than we started," he'd said finally. It wasn't self-pity. It was inventory. He said it the way he used to read the depth gauge—flat, factual, already calculating what came next.

"You've got us," Nell said.

He'd looked down at her with an expression she couldn't name then and could name now, a year later, having seen it a hundred times since: it was the look of a man discovering that the ledger he'd been keeping his whole life was in the wrong currency.

* * *

They ate fish when Bram could catch them. They ate cattail roots, which tasted like wet chalk and forgiveness. Once, near an abandoned homestead with a collapsed roof and a garden gone wild, they ate an entire patch of onions raw, sitting in the dirt, laughing until Isandro had to put his head between his knees, and Nell filed that evening away in the place she kept the things that proved the world was worth walking through.

At the homestead, Sefa found a child's slate hanging on a nail by the door, and she gave it to Nell without ceremony, the way you hand someone a tool they were always going to need. Nell practiced letters on it at night by firelight. *Kestrel*, she wrote. *Lior*. *Spring*. Then she licked her thumb and erased them, one by one, learning early the discipline of letting words go.

* * *

The third week, she counted bridges—or rather, the absence of them.

The Provision Company had been busy on the middle Verrow. Every landing had a notice board, and every notice board had the same paper nailed to it, weather-curled and ink-bled:

**BY ORDER OF THE PROVISION COMPANY — TRAVEL ABOVE THORNMOUTH IS PROHIBITED. PERSONS FOUND IN VIOLATION WILL BE DETAINED FOR QUESTIONING. REWARD OFFERED FOR INFORMATION CONCERNING THE WHEREABOUTS OF SURVEY EQUIPMENT STOLEN FROM COMPANY ASSETS.**

Below that, a sketch of Sefa's face, ten years out of date, and a description of a riverboat called the *Kestrel*.

Bram tore the first one down and burned it. Isandro stopped him at the second. "Leave them," he said. "A notice nobody answers is worse for them than for us. Every empty board tells the Company their secret's already loose."

It was true, though Nell didn't understand how he knew it until later. Word travels downriver faster than boats. Somewhere below them, in the towns they'd passed through on the way up—in Greyford, in Thornmouth, in all the muddy landings where they'd taken on cargo and passengers and trouble—the story was already moving. Not the true story; nobody had the true story, not whole. But pieces. A prospector who died with a captain's hand in his. A gunboat that came back down through the calving channel with a shattered rudder and half its rail torn away, its crew tight-lipped and its agent Corvus Hale gray-faced and silent. A glacier that calved out of season and sealed a valley that maps had never admitted existed.

People stitched pieces together the way they always did. And the stitchwork they made was better than the truth, because the truth had four tired walkers in it, and the stitchwork had a legend.

* * *

On the twenty-third day, they met the fleet.

They heard it first—a sound Nell felt in her teeth before her ears caught it, a deep mechanical pulse rolling up the water like a heartbeat too big for any animal. They got off the trail and into the alder brush above a long gravel bar, and Nell parted the branches, and there it was.

Three steam launches in echelon, black-hulled, funnels feathering gray smoke, each one flying the Provision Company's white-and-gold pennant. Deck guns covered in canvas. Men lining the rails with the particular stillness of soldiers approaching something they've been ordered to fear.

And behind them, riding a barge lashed between the second and third launch, more men. Dozens. Survey tripods. Crate after crate marked in stenciled letters Nell could read now, thanks to the slate: *EXPEDITION SUPPLY — UPPER VERROW DIVISION*.

"They brought an army," Bram breathed beside her.

"They brought a funeral," Isandro said.

Nobody moved for a long time. The launches ground past the gravel bar, close enough that Nell could see the faces of the men at the rails—young, mostly, the way Company men were young, hired for hunger and kept for obedience—and then the lead launch's bow swung toward the valley mouth, toward the gap in the mountains where the river ran narrow and white, and Nell held her breath along with everyone else.

The fleet entered the gorge.

An hour later—it took Nell a while to understand why the wait felt so strange—they came back out.

Not all of them. The lead launch came back out, moving slow, listing slightly, its deck crowded with men who weren't standing at the rails anymore but crouched behind them. The other two followed. The barge turned in midstream with a ponderous, graceless effort, and the whole formation came back downriver the way a hand comes back from a stove.

Through the alder leaves, Nell watched a man in a gray coat stand at the stern of the lead launch, very straight, looking up at the valley mouth through a brass spyglass for a long, long time.

Even at that distance, she knew the set of his shoulders. Corvus Hale had boarded the *Kestrel* once, back when the world was smaller, and had spoken to Isandro in a voice like a ledger being balanced, and had smiled exactly once, and Nell had hated him instantly and completely and had never forgotten the shape of him.

He lowered the spyglass. He said something to an officer. The officer shook his head.

Then Hale did the thing Nell would think about for years afterward, the thing she'd try to describe to herself on sleepless nights and never quite get right: he turned and looked at the north bank of the river—at the alder brush, at the general green anonymity of it—and held there, just a moment, as if he could feel four sets of eyes on him the way you feel weather coming.

He couldn't see them. There was no way he could see them. But he raised one hand anyway—not a wave, not quite a salute, something older and barer than either—and held it up like a man taking an oath or surrendering to something, and then the launch's engine note deepened and the fleet went on downriver and around the bend and was gone, and the pulse of it faded until the loudest thing in the world was the river itself, doing what rivers do, which is forget.

"He knows," Bram said quietly.

"He suspects," Isandro said. "There's a difference. Suspecting costs him nothing. Knowing—" He shook his head. "Knowing would require proof, and proof requires a witness, and every witness he sends up that gorge is going to find a wall of ice where a door used to be. Let him suspect. Suspicion doesn't pay debts."

* * *

That night, camped on the gravel bar with the fire built small and low, Nell asked the question she'd been carrying since the flood.

"Isandro. Your debt. To the Company. Is it—is it gone?"

He was mending a tear in his coat with sail thread, because a man like Isandro mended with sail thread no matter what the garment was. His needle paused.

"The debt was leverage," he said. "My father borrowed against the *Kestrel*'s route license—upstream rights, the high river, the runs above Thornmouth. That's what the Company actually owned. Not money. Access." He pulled the thread through, snug. "You can't collect on a road that doesn't exist. Hale can wave my father's paper at me until the day he dies. There's no boat to seize, no route to revoke, no source to tax. As far as the Company's ledgers are concerned, Isandro Vane owes them payment on a bridge they burned themselves." He almost smiled. "I spent nine years rowing upstream against that debt. Turns out all I had to do was drown it."

"You don't sound happy about it," Nell said.

The needle paused again. Firelight moved on his face, and for a moment he looked younger and older at once, the way he did when he talked about the past.

"I spent nine years being owed," he said. "Being owed is a kind of ballast. You always know your weight in the world." He tied off the thread and bit it clean. "I'll learn to float."

From across the fire, Sefa said, without opening her eyes, "He floats fine. I've seen him."

Bram snorted. Isandro's ears went red, and Nell laughed until her blisters hurt, and that was the night she understood that they were not four people traveling together. They were something else, something with no name on any chart, and the namelessness was the point.

* * *

The fourth week, she counted strangers.

Because word had traveled ahead of them, and the word had grown legs. At Colder's Reach, a net-mender pressed a sack of smoked trout into Sefa's hands and wouldn't take payment, only asked, shyly, if it was true about the ice. At Harlow Bend, the ferryman—a broad, sunburnt woman named Odile who ran a flatboat with a cracked bell—refused their last coin for passage and instead paid *them*, two bits apiece, "for the story," and Nell realized with a jolt that they were the story now, that she was inside the thing other people would tell.

They gave Odile the piece of the river that Sefa had assigned to her: the bend below the falls, the shallow crossing, the blue seam in the rock. Nothing more. Odile listened with her head tipped, nodded once, and said, "I'll remember it wrong, probably," which was exactly right, and Sefa's eyes over the woman's shoulder were bright with something that might have been grief or relief or both wearing the same coat.

By the time they reached the low country, where the Verrow went wide and brown and patient, Nell had given away pieces too. She told a farmer's daughter about the ice caves and the way light came through them green. She told a lock-keeper's son about a dog named Kestrel she'd invented on the spot, because every story needed a dog. She kept the true pieces—the journal pages she'd copied onto her slate in her careful new handwriting, the ones about the towns the Company poisoned, the ones about the men the spring drove mad—folded up small inside her shirt, next to her heartbeat, where she intended to keep them for the rest of her life.

Some truths you plant. Some truths you carry. Sefa had taught her the difference without ever saying it aloud.

* * *

On the thirty-first day, they reached the sea.

Or the estuary, anyway, where the Verrow gave itself up to salt water in a hundred braided channels, and the air changed taste, and gulls wheeled over mudflats glittering like spilled coin. They stood at the top of the last bluff, all four of them, dirty and thin and sunburnt, wearing clothes that had been mended so many times they were more thread than cloth, and looked at the water.

"Well," said Bram.

"Well," said Isandro.

Nell looked up at him. "What now?"

Isandro Vane, riverboat captain, debtor, keeper of drowned secrets, put his hands in the pockets of his ruined coat and considered the horizon with the same expression he'd worn reading depth gauges, and then he did something she had never seen him do in all the weeks and months and hard miles since a dying man had put a map in his hands.

He laughed. Out loud. Both hands free, head back, no ballast anywhere in him.

"Now," he said, "we find work."

* * *

But that was later—that was the next part, the part with the ferry and the new names and the small bright ordinary years. On the bluff above the estuary, with the wind coming off the water and the whole long river lying behind them like a sentence finally finished, Nell took out her slate one more time.

She wrote four words. She looked at them a long moment.

Then, because some lessons you keep forever, she licked her thumb and erased them—but slowly, so she could feel each letter go, the way you feel a scar in winter.

The words had been: *We walked out.*

And under them, fainter, in the ghost of the slate where erased writing lives: *all four.*
