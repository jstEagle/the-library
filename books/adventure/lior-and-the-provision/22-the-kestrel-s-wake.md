## 22. The Kestrel's Wake

The river had a different name here, and so did he.

Isandro Vane had been Isandro Marr for eleven months now—Marr, a name Sefa had found in an old atlas of a country neither of them had ever seen, meaning *shore* in a dead language, which felt right for a man who had spent his whole life on the water and never once belonged to it. The ferry was called the *Lark*, though the people of Aldenmere mostly just called it "the crossing," because that was what it did: carried farmers and schoolteachers and sacks of grain and the occasional nervous bride across two hundred yards of slow brown water between the village and the orchard road.

Two hundred yards. He could have thrown a stone across it in his younger days, or at least convinced himself he could. After the Verrow—with its thousand-mile throat, its ice, its hunger—the Lark's little river felt like a held breath finally let out.

He stood at the tiller in the gray-gold light of early autumn, watching mist lift off the water in ropes, and did what he did every morning now, which was nothing. Nothing but steer. Nothing but listen to the river say its one long syllable against the hull. There was a luxury in it that still embarrassed him, a year on. He kept waiting for the feeling to wear off, this lightness, this sense of having set down something enormous without ever quite seeing his hands do it. It hadn't worn off. Some mornings he woke with his heart hammering, certain the Provision Company's gunboats were coming around the bend, certain the debt, the map, the glacier, all of it had been a fever dream he'd wake from back on the lower Verrow with collectors circling like gulls. And then he'd hear Nell laughing at the mooring line, or smell Bram frying bread in the cabin, and the hammering would slow, and he'd remember: the Kestrel was under forty feet of ice and stone. The map was pulp. The debt was a story told about a man who no longer existed.

The bell rang from the far bank—a farmer with a cart of late apples—and Isandro swung the wheel and went to fetch him.

* * *

They had found Aldenmere by accident, the way they seemed to find everything good. They'd walked out of the high country through most of a summer, four ghosts moving down out of the mountains with empty pockets and frost-scarred hands, taking work where work offered, sleeping in barns, saying as little as possible. By the time they reached the lowlands, the story of the flooded valley had already outrun them—there were newspaper men in the river towns talking about a catastrophe in the northern ranges, a glacial collapse, a lost survey expedition, the Provision Company's shares dropping like a stone in a well. Nobody connected four tired travelers to any of it. That was the strange arithmetic of disappearance: the more terrible the thing you left behind, the less anyone looked for you in the small places.

Aldenmere was a small place. It had a ferry that needed running and no one to run it since old Master Corb had drowned the previous spring—not dramatically, Isandro gathered, just stupidly, the way rivers take men who stop respecting them. The village council interviewed them on a bench outside the alehouse: a scarred man who steered like he'd been born at a tiller; a woman with ink-stained fingers who drew maps of the surrounding farms so precise the council asked twice if she'd been paid by someone official; a big quiet fellow whose hands could mend anything with a rope in it; and a girl of maybe thirteen who read everything she could get hold of and asked questions like small knives.

"You'll want references," the council head said.

"We don't have any," Isandro said.

"Then you'll want to stay anyway," the man said, "because nobody else wants the job." He spat thoughtfully into the dust. "River's not much. Two hundred yards of nothing. But it's ours."

Isandro had looked at the water then—slow, brown, patient, carrying leaves instead of ice floes—and felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton.

"We'll stay," he said.

* * *

The morning rounds went as mornings went. The apple farmer, Hobb, rode across grumbling about wasps and prices and tipped Isandro double anyway, the way he always did, pretending it was a mistake. Two children going to the schoolhouse on the far side hung over the rail counting fish until their teacher pulled them back. A woman with a sewing basket asked Sefa—it was Sefa's week on the passenger bench—whether the mist meant rain, and Sefa gave her a forecast so detailed and confident, built off cloud shapes and wind direction and some private mathematics of her own, that the woman repeated it in the village for a week as gospel. It rained exactly when she said it would.

Bram had taken over the maintenance shed, and the shed had never been better ordered in its life. He coiled rope in patterns Isandro suspected only soldiers knew. He had put on weight. His hands had healed crooked—the cold had gotten into the fingers on the glacier, and two of them would never fully straighten—but he'd taught himself to work around it, and if it slowed him, no one in Aldenmere ever saw it. Sometimes at night Isandro heard him talking in his sleep, short clipped words in a voice like a younger man's, giving orders or refusing them. He never asked. On the glacier, Bram had bought his redemption with blood and a ruined hand and the rudder-line of Hale's gunboat, and whatever debts remained in him were his own to pay down. But Isandro noticed that Bram had started going to the village chapel—not to pray, as far as anyone could tell, but to sit in the back during services, among people, doing penance simply by allowing himself to be near other human beings. It was, Isandro thought, as honest an atonement as any priest could prescribe.

And Nell—

Nell had grown three inches and learned to swim and won the village reading prize two terms running, and had appointed herself, without election, the Lark's first mate, purser, navigator, and chronicler. She kept a logbook. Of course she kept a logbook. She recorded crossings and cargo and weather and the names of every regular passenger and the day the kingfisher came back to the reed bank and the exact shade of the water in October versus November. Isandro had read it when she wasn't looking, once. In the back pages, past the ferry business, she wrote other things—fragments, memories, the taste of glacier melt, the sound the ice made at night like a giant turning over in bed. She was writing it all down. Not to publish. Not yet. Just so it would be true somewhere besides inside them.

That was the thing about Nell. She understood before any of them that a secret doesn't die just because you bury it. It ferments.

* * *

It was evening when she asked. The last crossing was done, the Lark tied up at the village side, lanterns lit along the bank because the nights were drawing in. Sefa was below deck—below cabin, really, the Lark being modest enough that "below deck" meant three steps down into a room that smelled of tea and lamp oil—working on a chart of the river valley she was making for the council, free, because she wanted to and because no one was paying her to falsify anything. Bram was on the bank arguing cheerfully with the blacksmith about the correct way to hang a gate. Isandro sat on the roof of the cabin with his boots dangling, watching the first stars come out over the water, and Nell climbed up beside him the way she always did, without asking, folding herself down next to him like a bird settling.

For a while they just watched the river. It was the kind of silence they'd earned, the kind that didn't need filling.

Then she said, "Do you think we'll ever tell anyone?"

He didn't pretend not to know what she meant. There was no one else it could be about. For a year the source had lived among them like a fifth crew member—unmentioned, unmentionable, present in every pause. The spring in the hidden valley, luminous and wrong, the mineral wealth that had poisoned towns and bought massacres and driven Lior half across a continent to die on a stranger's dock. The map Isandro had burned in the flood-torrent along with his boat and his name. Hale's fleet arriving, so the newspapers said, to find a sealed valley and no answers.

"Tell anyone what?" he said, but gently, giving her the opening rather than closing the door.

"The spring." She hugged her knees. "What it was. What the Company did—all those towns, the people in Lior's journals. Everyone thinks it was just a mining accident. Everyone thinks the valley flood was just ice doing what ice does." Her voice had that edge it got when she was working up to something. "Sefa says the truth matters. She almost charted it, remember? She wanted the world to know where it was. And I keep thinking—if the truth mattered enough to nearly kill us for, doesn't it matter enough to say out loud? Someday?"

Isandro took his time. He'd had a year to turn this over, and he still hadn't finished.

"Hale will tell it eventually," he said. "Or the Company will, when it suits them, some sanitized version. Or some prospector will find the outflow seep and start a rumor, and the rumor will grow legs. Truths like that don't stay buried forever, Nell. They're too heavy. They press up through the ground."

"Then why not us? Why not first?"

"Because of what happened to the last man who tried to carry it alone." He nodded at the water, at the dark, at the memory of a feverish old man on a muddy dock trading a map for a promise. "Lior spent thirty years knowing the truth and thirty years dying of it. Not the fever—the knowing. It ate him. He couldn't hold it and he couldn't give it away and he couldn't use it, because using it made you like them." He turned to look at her. In the lantern light her face was fierce and young and unbearably familiar, and he thought, not for the first time, that he had crossed a forbidden river and survived a calving glacier and sunk his own boat with his own hands, and none of it had frightened him as much as the idea of this girl growing up carrying what he carried. "The source corrupts everyone who claims it. You know that. But here's what I figured out standing on that ice, holding the map over the water: the story corrupts people too, if you try to own it. If it's yours—your discovery, your secret, your leverage—then it owns you right back. The only way I found to be free of it was to give it away completely. To the river. To the ice. To each other."

Nell frowned at the current. "To each other," she repeated.

"We already did—we told each other."

She was quiet a moment, working it through, and he watched her arrive at it the way she arrived at everything, all at once, like a fish rising. Every night on the glacier, every argument, every confession—Bram's massacre, Sefa's disgrace, Isandro's father and the debt and the promise made too late—they had spoken the whole truth to the only people in the world who could bear it. Four witnesses. That was the record. That was the archive. Not paper, not charts, not newspapers: four people who knew, walking around loose in the world, keeping each other honest.

"So the telling isn't finished," Nell said slowly. "It's just... it only has an audience of four."

"For now."

"And someday?"

"Someday," Isandro said, "when telling it costs more than it gives—or less, depending on the year—you'll decide. Not me. You've got the logbook. You've got the words. Lior picked me because I was passing by, but I think—" he smiled despite himself— "I think what he really wanted was to hand it downstream. To somebody young. Somebody who'd live long enough to know what to do with it."

Nell looked at him for a long moment, and then she leaned her head against his shoulder, briefly, the way she permitted herself affection in rationed doses, and said, "You're getting sentimental, Captain Marr."

"Don't tell anyone."

"Too late. It's going in the logbook."

* * *

Below them, the cabin door opened and Sefa came out into the evening, squinting up at the roof. "Are you two plotting up there?"

"Always," Nell called down.

"Come eat, both of you. Bram caught trout and he's insufferable about it." Sefa paused, then added, softer, just for Isandro, in the tone she saved for things that were true: "There's a letter came on the afternoon coach. No name on it. Postmarked from the capital."

The lightness didn't leave him all at once. It drained slowly, the way warmth drains from a hull at dusk. He climbed down and took the envelope from her hands—cheap paper, official seal broken already because Sefa was Sefa—and read it twice under the lantern.

It was from a historical society. Or claimed to be. It said they were compiling an account of the northern expedition disaster, the flooded valley, the lost survey party, and would welcome any testimony from persons with knowledge of the region. It said the Provision Company had recently entered receivership amid scandal. It said, in a postscript in different handwriting, smaller, pressed hard into the page:

*We know four people came down out of those mountains. We are not the Company. We are not looking for treasure. We are looking for the truth, and we suspect it is safer with you than it has ever been with anyone. No reply necessary. But if you ever choose to speak, we will listen.*

Isandro read it a third time. Then he handed it to Sefa, and watched her face do the complicated arithmetic it always did—truth against safety, duty against love—and then he folded it carefully and put it in the tin box under his bunk, where Lior's map had once ridden, where Nell's logbook pages would go someday, where the family kept the things it wasn't ready to carry openly but refused to throw away.

No reply. Not tonight. Tonight there was trout and lamplight and Bram's insufferable triumph, and a river saying its one long syllable, and home—not a place, never again a place, but the three people on this boat and the girl on the roof writing it all down.

But as he doused the lantern and the dark came in soft over the water, Isandro Vane—who had been many names and would perhaps be others—understood that the story was not over. It was only resting, the way a river rests between rapids. Somewhere downstream, in a year or ten, a girl with a logbook and a knife-sharp mind would decide the world was ready. And when she did, she wouldn't be carrying it alone.

None of them ever would again.
