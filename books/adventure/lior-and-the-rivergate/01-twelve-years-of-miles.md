## 1. Twelve Years of Miles

The Kestrel didn't like cold mornings, and neither did I, but the medicine wouldn't wait and the strip at Two Rivers was nothing but frost and gravel at first light. I ran my preflight by feel more than sight — left wing strut, check; oil cap, snug; the patch on the cowling I'd riveted myself two winters back, still holding. Twelve years of flying had taught me that a plane tells you everything if you listen, and that people mostly don't.

"Morning, Fairweather." Old Sam Okafor came up the dock road with two thermoses and his breath steaming ahead of him like a small ghost. "You're loaded?"

"Loaded."

"You're crazy."

"That too." I took the coffee because refusing it would have started a conversation, and conversations at six in the morning have a way of becoming obligations.

Sam watched me strap in through the open door, that particular look men get when they want to say something true but settle for something useful instead. "Weather's clean to the divide," he said. "After that, who knows."

"Who ever knows."

I taxied out onto the ice-glazed gravel and let the engine come up slow, the way you coax an old dog off a porch. The Kestrel shuddered, coughed, caught — a sound like a promise being reluctantly kept — and then we were rolling, and then we were airborne, and Merrow's Landing fell away beneath me: the sawtooth rooftops, the fish plant smoking its gray ribbon into the dawn, the river running wide and brown toward a coast I hadn't seen in twelve years and never intended to see again.

Some men fly to get somewhere. I flew to be between places. There's a stretch of sky where nobody can ask you anything, and I'd made it my address.

* * *

The interior opened up under the wheels like a book nobody had finished writing. Rivers braided and unbraided. Ridges wore their snow like old men wear hats they've stopped caring about. I flew the river because the river is the only honest map up here — it goes where it goes, and every settlement since the beginning has bent its knee to that fact.

Two hours in, I crossed the Serrated Reach, white water stitching through black rock, and gave it the respect it deserved with a thousand extra feet. A pilot named Calloway had gone down there in '09 trying to shortcut a fuel run. They found pieces of him for three years afterward. The river keeps what it takes.

At the divide the weather did what Sam said it might. Cloud stacked up against the peaks like a debt coming due, and I threaded the pass with my teeth set and one eye on the altimeter, the Kestrel grumbling about the turbulence the way she grumbles about everything — takeoffs, landings, Tuesdays. She's a 1948 bush plane that has been crashed twice, rebuilt once properly and once improperly, and repainted so many times that her original color is a matter of theological dispute among the strips. She drinks oil. She leaks in rain. She has never once, in all her moods, left me walking.

That's more than I can say for anyone else.

* * *

The strip at Two Rivers is a gravel bar with ambitions, and the man who ran it was a half-Chilcotin trapper named Eli Starlight who kept a radio powered by a car battery and a grudge. He met me at the edge of the strip with the medicine crates already counted, which told me he'd been waiting a while.

"Flu's bad upriver," he said, instead of hello. "Keswick's got four kids down with it. Old woman Naniche won't take the shots but she'll take the pills if you tell her they're for her heart and not her lungs."

"They are for her lungs."

"She doesn't care what they're for. She cares what story comes with them." He helped me haul the crates onto his sled, hands cracked from cold. "You staying? Got trout in the smoker."

"I've got Keswick before dark."

"You always say that." He looked at me the way he does sometimes, sideways, like he's reading weather off a horizon I can't see. "Twelve years, Fairweather. You ever think about landing somewhere?"

"I land somewhere every day."

"You know what I mean."

I did know what he meant, which was why I didn't answer it. Instead I checked the tie-downs on the crates and asked about fuel drums and whether the river ice was holding at the bend, and by then the moment had passed, the way moments do when you're good at passing them.

But flying out, climbing back over the divide with the sun going long and gold behind the peaks, I thought about what he'd said, and I did what I always do with thoughts like that: I put them in the same drawer as everything else I don't open. It's a big drawer. It holds a coast I won't fly over, a name I won't say, a sister whose voice I can't remember anymore no matter how hard I press my eyes shut, and a decision I made at twenty-six that felt like survival and has since begun, in quiet moments, to feel like something else.

Comfortable loneliness. That's what Eli would call it if I ever gave him the words. A cabin with one chair. A plane with one seat's worth of living. A calendar with no dates marked except fuel deliveries and freeze-up. You can build a whole life out of not-being-somewhere, and I had, and most days the walls held.

Most days.

* * *

The rumor economy of the frontier strips works like this: information is currency, accuracy is optional, and everything trades at a premium the farther upriver you go. By the time a story travels from Merrow's Landing to Two Rivers to the mission at Cold Fork, it has changed owners so many times it barely remembers its own name — but it remembers what matters, which is who's buying.

I heard three rumors on this run alone.

One: the mining consortium — Corvus Consolidated, silver logo, lawyers like a plague of locusts — had bought up water rights along the upper river, quietly, through shell companies, the way you buy a thing you don't want the owner to know he's selling. Nobody knew why. The upper river has no gold worth the drilling and no timber worth the cutting. But consortiums don't buy water rights for scenery.

Two: a prospector named Tobin Hale had been seen at the Cold Fork strip, alone, half-starved, asking strange questions about headwaters. Hale was a legend of the old days — the kind of man who went into the interior for two years and came out with pelts and stories and a laugh you could hear across a lake. Except the laugh was gone now, according to the story, and the stories themselves had gone strange. He talked about a gate. He talked about water you could drink that would give you back your life. Every pilot who repeated this detail did so with the particular smirk we reserve for the Rivergate — the source of the great river, the spring that rises whole from the earth, the place that appears on no map and in every drunk conversation at every strip from here to the coast. We joke about it the way sailors joke about mermaids. It's how we keep from admitting we've all, somewhere in the small hours, wondered.

Three: somebody was offering money for pilots willing to fly survey runs up the headwaters, no questions, cash on delivery. Good money. The kind of money that buys silence along with it.

I turned these over during the leg to Keswick, watching the river unspool below me, and filed them in the drawer with everything else. Rumors are weather. You note them, you respect them, you don't build your house on them.

* * *

Keswick sits on a bluff above a slow bend, four cabins and a church that doubles as a schoolhouse, and by the time I landed the whole population had turned out, which is to say eleven people and a dog. The flu had hit the Halloran family hardest — four kids down, flushed and miserable, and their mother running on nothing but worry and boiled willow bark. I handed over the medicine, held a squirming eight-year-old while Sister Anne gave the shot, and stayed an hour longer than I meant to because the youngest, a girl named June with hair like a crow's wing, asked me if I'd ever been scared in the plane.

"Every day," I said.

"Really?"

"Really. Being scared just means you understand what you're doing. It's the ones who aren't scared you should worry about."

She considered this with enormous seriousness. "Do you have anybody?" she asked, the way children ask, the way children are allowed to ask, the question adults spend entire lives politely not asking each other. "Like family? Waiting for you?"

"Nope," I said, cheerful as a man signing his own receipt. "Just me and the Kestrel."

She looked at me with pity, actual pity, seven years old and sorry for me, and I laughed it off and walked out to preflight in the dark, and stood there a minute longer than the preflight needed, looking at the stars coming out over the ridge like sparks off a grinder.

Just me and the Kestrel. Twelve years of miles. And the drawer full of things I don't open sitting right there behind my ribs, same as always, locked same as always, heavy same as always.

I could have stayed the night at Keswick. Sister Anne offered. The Hallorans offered their floor. Any of it would have been warm and human and exactly the kind of thing a man building a life does.

I took off instead. Last light, cold engine, a hundred miles of dark river between me and my own bed. Because that's the arrangement I made with myself a long time ago: keep moving, keep the tank full, keep the drawer shut. Deliveries, not visits. Strips, not homes. The sky between places is the only place I've never broken anything.

The Kestrel climbed into the blue-black and leveled off, and the river ran silver under the moon, going somewhere, the way rivers do, without ever once looking back.

I used to think that was freedom.

Somewhere over the divide, with the fuel gauge steady and the night clear and nobody in the world knowing or caring where I was, I passed a campfire on a gravel bar — one small orange point in all that darkness, a man sitting alone beside it, and I felt something twist in my chest that I refused, as always, to name.

And that was when the radio crackled.

Not Merrow's traffic. Not weather. A voice I didn't know, thin with distance and static, saying a strip name I almost missed — Cold Fork — and a word that cut through the static like a hook through water:

"Medic. We need a medic. Man's down at Cold Fork. Prospector. Bad off."

I sat with my hand on the mic for three full seconds, telling myself it wasn't my run, wasn't my problem, that Cold Fork was forty minutes the wrong direction and the light was dying and the drawer should stay shut.

Then I banked north, because some promises you make before you know you're making them, and because twelve years of miles had taught me exactly one thing I couldn't put in a drawer:

Out here, when a man calls, somebody answers. Or nobody does.

I pushed the throttle forward and flew into the dark.
