## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

The strip at Kettle Flats wasn't on any chart worth trusting. It was a scar of gravel some mining crew had graded forty years ago and then abandoned to the alder, and it was exactly the kind of place Isandro Fairweather liked to land, because nobody with money ever came looking for him there.

He brought the Kestrel in low over the treeline, checking the wind sock — a rag of orange nylon someone had tied to a dead spruce, faded to the color of weak tea — and set down with the usual three bounces that told him his tires were getting old and so, probably, was he. Twelve years of flying medicine runs and mail sacks and the occasional rich man's whim into the interior, and his body had started keeping its own logbook: left knee from the hard landing at Sorrel Creek, shoulder from the winch cable at Grayling, a permanent ringing in the right ear from an engine backfire he'd never gotten looked at.

The plane rolled out and stopped. The silence after shutdown was the good kind, the kind you could drink.

Kettle Flats had one structure left standing, a trapper's cabin with its roof caved in on the north side, and beside it, half in the shade of the alders, lay a man.

Isandro saw him before he even opened the door — a shape too still to be resting, too arranged to be dead. He climbed down with his medical kit under one arm, because up here you learned to carry the kit the way a city man carried a phone. The shape resolved into an old prospector, canvas coat gone gray as the gravel, boots worn through at the heels, a pack lying open beside him with its contents scattered like the man had been searching for something and run out of strength halfway through.

"Hey." Isandro knelt. "Can you hear me?"

The old man's eyes opened. They were yellow. Not the yellow of age — the yellow of a liver filing its resignation, gold-dust fever they called it in the camps, though the doctors downriver used longer words. Isandro had seen it twice before. Both men had died.

"Fairweather." The voice was a dry rasp, but it knew his name. That meant word traveled faster than he did, which was always an uncomfortable thing to learn about yourself. "You're the one who flies."

"Sometimes. Who are you?"

"Tobin Hale." A cough took him, deep and wet, and when it passed he lay gasping like a landed fish. "You're going to Merrow's Landing."

It wasn't a question, and Isandro didn't confirm it, because Merrow's Landing was where he refueled before the long leg south, and the less anyone knew about his routes, the better. "I'm going wherever I'm going. You need a hospital. There's a clinic at Fort Anselm, four hours if I push the Kestrel—"

"No hospitals." Hale's hand shot out and closed on Isandro's wrist with a grip that had no business being that strong. His fingers were stained to the second knuckle, not with dirt but with something finer, a glitter worked into the skin's creases like it had grown there. Gold dust. Years of it. "Listen to me. I'm past listening distance from anything that can save me, and I know it, and I've made my peace with it, or most of it. What I haven't made peace with is dying with this undone."

He let go of Isandro's wrist and fumbled inside his coat. What came out was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with copper wire, and small enough to hold in one palm. He worked the wire loose with fingers that shook, and Isandro, against every instinct toward self-preservation he'd cultivated over twelve careful years, helped him.

Inside the oilcloth was a map. Hand-drawn on what felt like hide, inked in something brown that might have been berry juice or might have been blood, folded into eighths until the creases had gone soft as cloth.

"I don't want your map," Isandro said. "I don't want whatever's at the end of your map. Old men up here give pilots maps the way drowning men grab ropes, and the maps are always wrong, and the pilots who believe them are always dead."

"This one isn't wrong." Hale's eyes fixed on him, fever-bright and terribly clear. "I've spent thirty years proving it isn't wrong. And I'm asking you not to follow it. I'm asking you to deliver it. There's a difference, and the difference is whether you live."

* * *

Isandro got the old man into the shade of the cabin's standing wall, propped him against his pack, and gave him water from his own flask in small sips while Hale talked, because talking seemed to be the only thing keeping him upright.

The story came out in pieces, the way stories do from men who've rehearsed them alone too long. Tobin Hale had come into the interior thirty-one years ago with a partner and a theory about the river's source. The partner was dead within two years — fell, Hale said, though his eyes slid sideways when he said it, and Isandro filed that away without judging, because everyone up here had a way a person fell. Hale had kept going alone. He'd found things. He wouldn't say what things. He'd marked them on the map, all of them, in a hand that got more cramped and more obsessive the older the markings got, decades of return trips layered over each other like tree rings.

"And your daughter," Isandro said, because Hale had said *daughter* twice now, the way a man says a word he's afraid will stop working if he repeats it too often. "Maren."

"Maren." The name came out cracked. "She was nine when I went upriver the last time. Nine years old, standing on the dock at Merrow's Landing, and she asked me when I'd be back, and I told her before the ice. I told her before the ice, Mr. Fairweather, and then I didn't come back before the ice, and then I didn't come back the next year either, and somewhere in there I stopped being a man who was coming back and became a man who was gone, and I don't—" Another cough, worse than the last. He spat pink into the gravel. "I don't have an excuse. I want that understood. Whatever else is in my journals, whatever I wrote about the river and the finding, I don't have an excuse. But she deserves to hear it from my own hand instead of from nothing at all, which is what she's heard for twenty-two years."

Twenty-two years. Isandro did the arithmetic without wanting to. A girl of nine, now a woman of thirty-one. A woman who had spent her whole adult life being the daughter of a man who vanished, which in the settlements was a particular kind of orphan — not mourned, just unexplained.

"Why me?" Isandro asked. "There are traders on this river. Mail boats. The mission flight comes through twice a month."

"Because the mail boat gets robbed, the mission pilot talks, and the traders sell anything that isn't nailed down." Hale's mouth twisted into something that had once been a smile. "And because you're the only pilot on this river who doesn't ask questions. I've watched you for three seasons, Fairweather. You land, you unload, you leave. You fly like a man running from something, and a man running from something can be trusted with a secret, because he wants no entanglements of his own."

That landed harder than it should have. Isandro stood, brushed off his knees, and looked out at the strip, at the Kestrel sitting in the sun with its paint peeling along the cowling, at the treeline pressing in from every side like the interior was a tide that never finished coming in.

"I'll take you to Fort Anselm," he said. "The clinic. That's the offer. That's the whole offer."

"There's no time and we both know it." Hale patted the ground beside him, and Isandro, cursing himself, sat back down. "Gold-dust fever gives a man maybe two days, and I've already spent one of them crawling here because I heard at Sorrel Creek that the Fairweather plane was working the eastern strips this week. I spent my last good hours getting to a place where a plane could find me. I'm not spending what's left in a clinic bed with a nurse reading me scripture." He held up the map. "Take this to Maren Hale, Merrow's Landing, the house above the chandlery with the blue door. Tell her it's from her father. Tell her—" He stopped. His jaw worked. For a moment Isandro thought the fever had taken him mid-sentence, and then he realized the old man simply couldn't finish it, that the sentence had defeated him for twenty-two years and would defeat him now.

"Tell her I'm sorry," Hale said finally. "No. Don't tell her that. She'll have heard sorry from every drifter on the river by now, and it won't weigh anything. Tell her the truth is in the map. All of it. Everything I couldn't say. She'll understand when she sees it. Or she won't, and she'll hate me properly instead of hating a ghost, and honestly, Fairweather, either one would be a mercy compared to what I left her with."

* * *

They sat through the afternoon while the light went long and amber across the flats. Isandro ran through his medical kit — saline, antibiotics, the fever powders that were mostly aspirin and hope — and Hale refused all of it except the water, waving the rest away with the economy of a man who'd rationed everything for three decades, including his own body.

Instead he talked. About the river, mostly, in fragments Isandro couldn't assemble and suspected weren't meant to be assembled. About a gorge where the wind spoke. About water so clear it hurt to look at. About a spring that rose whole from the earth, no stream feeding it, no outlet visible, the river beginning in the middle of nowhere like a sentence starting without a first word.

Every pilot on the interior knew those stories. They were the jokes you told around fuel drums at the end of a long season — the Rivergate, the Source, the place where the great river came out of the ground carrying everybody's memories downstream. Nobody claimed to have seen it. Nobody claimed to have looked for it, either, not seriously, because the men who'd looked seriously had a habit of coming back strange, or not coming back, or coming back rich in ways that didn't show. It was frontier folklore, same as the white moose and the lake that froze in July. You laughed about it and checked your fuel gauges.

But Tobin Hale wasn't laughing. Tobin Hale was holding a map drawn on hide with thirty years of obsessive markings, dying of a prospector's disease with gold dust grown into his knuckles, and saying *the truth is in the map* like it was a weight he'd carried so long his spine had bent around it.

"You should burn it," Isandro said, as the sun dropped behind the spruces and the cold came up out of the ground the way it does in the interior, sudden and total. "Whatever's on it. Burn it and die easy."

"Nothing dies easy up here." Hale drew the oilcloth back around the map with hands that could barely manage the folds. "Least of all secrets. Secrets keep themselves alive; that's the whole trouble with them. They wait." He looked up, and in the last light his yellow eyes had gone calm in a way that frightened Isandro more than the fever had. "So. Will you take it?"

Everything in Isandro's twelve-year education said no. No deliveries, no promises, no packages that weighed more than their postage. He'd built a life on the principle that the interior gave you exactly as much trouble as you agreed to carry, and he had never once regretted traveling light. There was a reason he lived alone in a hangar at the edge of Merrow's Landing with a plane and a kettle and nothing on the walls. There was a reason he'd left the coast, and the reason had a name he hadn't spoken aloud since, and every promise he'd ever broken had started exactly like this — with a dying stranger's eyes and a thing pressed into his hands.

"Yes," Isandro said.

Hale exhaled, long and shuddering, like a man setting down a load at the top of a hill. "Thank you." He pressed the bundle into Isandro's palm and closed Isandro's fingers over it with both of his ruined hands. "One more thing. If anyone asks — anyone — you never met me. You found an empty cabin and flew on. There are people who want what's on this map, and they're not prospectors, and they're not poor, and they don't take no for an answer, and I'd rather my daughter grow up hating me than die knowing me. Do you understand?"

"Not even slightly."

"Good." The ghost of the smile again. "Understanding comes later. It always does."

* * *

Tobin Hale died an hour after midnight, quietly, between one breath and the next, while Isandro sat wrapped in a tarp against the fuselage of the Kestrel pretending to sleep. Isandro buried him at dawn in the soft ground behind the cabin wall, digging with the folding shovel from his survival kit through the frost line and down, laying the old man in with his empty pack at his head because it seemed wrong to separate a prospector from his pack even now. He stacked stones over the grave until his arms ached, said a few words that weren't prayers because he'd lost the habit, and stood for a moment in the morning cold listening to the interior make its enormous indifferent noise.

Then he walked back to the plane, stowed the shovel, and sat in the pilot's seat with the oilcloth bundle in his lap.

It was lighter than it should have been. That was the thing he kept noticing. Something that had bent a man's life double for thirty years, something that had killed him by inches in the bush, and it weighed almost nothing — a few folds of hide, a few ounces of ink and obsession. Trouble always traveled light. That was how it got aboard.

Isandro turned the Kestrel into the wind, ran up the engine, and lifted off the scarred gravel of Kettle Flats into a sky scrubbed clean and blue. Below him the river appeared through breaks in the canopy, wide and brown and patient, running south toward Merrow's Landing and a blue door above a chandlery and a woman who had spent twenty-two years waiting for a father who was never coming back.

He'd promised to deliver a map. Just that. A delivery, a signature, a handshake, and back in the air by nightfall.

He was halfway to the Landing before he admitted he hadn't looked at the map yet — and that some part of him, the part that had flown twelve years in straight lines to avoid exactly this kind of curve, was already wondering what was on it.

He told himself he'd deliver it sealed. He told himself that the whole way south.

He was lying, and the river below him seemed to know it, running beside him the entire way like it was keeping him company, or keeping an eye on him, or — and this was the thought he finally had to say out loud, alone in the cockpit, just to hear how ridiculous it sounded —

like it was waiting for him to open the map and read where it began.
