## 3. What the Map Shows

The Kestrel's cabin smelled of avgas and old leather and, now, faintly of gold dust — a dry mineral sweetness that had come off Tobin Hale's clothes and hands and seemed to have settled into the upholstery like it intended to stay. Isandro sat in the pilot's seat with the dome light off and the hangar doors cracked to let in the last blue hour of evening, and he unrolled the map across his knees for the fourth time since leaving Hale's strip.

He told himself he was checking it for practical reasons. Fuel stops. Water crossings. Ridge lines that would matter if Maren Hale turned out to be the kind of daughter who wanted more than a parcel delivered.

He was lying to himself, and he knew it, because his hands were careful in a way they only got when something mattered.

The map was drawn on waxed canvas, the kind prospectors used because paper rotted in the interior damp. Tobin Hale had worked on it over years, clearly — the ink changed character three times, from a young man's confident strokes to an old man's trembling annotations squeezed into margins. Rivers were inked in blue-black, ridges in brown, and distances marked not in miles but in days: *two days' walk, bad water; one day, good gravel bar; half day, keep to the east bank or lose the trail.*

It was a walking map. A map made by someone who had gone the last stretch on foot, which meant somewhere along this river there was a stretch no boat could run and no plane could land.

Isandro traced the route with one finger. It started at Merrow's Landing, followed the great river — the Serpent, the maps called it, though nobody upriver called it anything but *the river*, the way you don't name the air — upstream past the trading villages, past the Serrated Reach where the water came down through broken granite like a saw blade, past the Fork where the channel split and every guide in the territory had a different opinion about which branch was real.

Then the annotations stopped being about navigation and started being about something else.

*Third fork, take the dry channel.* In Tobin's younger hand. Then, decades later, cramped into the margin beside it: *still there. still singing.*

Isandro sat very still.

He knew that phrase. Every pilot who flew the interior knew that phrase, the way sailors know the shape of a storm cloud without ever wanting to meet one. It came out in the strip bars after the second bottle, when the fuel drums were paid for and the stories got loose. The Rivergate. The headwater. The place where the Serpent rose whole out of the earth instead of trickling down from some snowfield like a respectable river. The place where the water was so clear you could see your own face looking back at you from twenty feet down, and where — this was the part said quieter, with a glance at the door — the water remembered.

*You drink there,* old Odile Brant was supposed to have said once, back before she gave up running the upper river, *and you remember everything. Everything you lived. Everything somebody else paid to forget.*

Pilot talk. Strip-bar theology. The interior was full of places that didn't exist: the white buffalo valley, the lake that burned, the mine that ate its own shaft. The Rivergate was the best of them because it had geography attached — a gorge, a spring, a basin shaped like a cupped hand — and geography made a lie durable. You could argue about a spirit. You couldn't argue about a gorge.

Except Tobin Hale hadn't been telling a story in a bar. Tobin Hale had spent forty years and most of his blood walking toward this spot, and he'd drawn it with the precision of a man mapping something he intended to find again, and in the margin beside the basin itself — a small circle, shaded, with lines radiating out like a child's drawing of the sun — he had written four words in the steadiest hand on the entire map:

*Do not drink twice.*

Isandro set the map down on his knees and looked out through the crack in the hangar doors at the runway, the dark line of forest beyond it, the first stars coming up over the ridge like sparks off a grinder.

Every joke has a source, he thought. Somebody started the Rivergate story. And every story this old, this consistent, this geographically stubborn, has either a hundred liars agreeing on details no liar would bother with —

— or a truth that people agreed to stop talking about.

He'd heard the second version too, once, from a grizzled government surveyor drinking alone in Merrow's Landing while Isandro waited out weather. The man had been three sheets down and mean with it, and when a young pilot at the next table made the usual joke about flying up to see the Gate, the surveyor had put his glass down with a click like a round chambering and said: *There's a reason the consortium holds every water lease on the upper Serpent and pumps nothing. There's a reason their survey planes fly that corridor twice a month over empty country. Ask yourself what a company that greedy is protecting, boy, and then ask yourself why you've never met anyone who's been there and come back talking.*

Then he'd paid his tab and left, and everyone had laughed, and Isandro had laughed too, and for twelve years he had kept the laugh going whenever it came up, because that was what you did with the interior's ghosts. You fed them jokes and they stayed harmless.

Now one of them was lying across his knees in Tobin Hale's handwriting.

* * *

He did what he always did with a problem he couldn't solve standing up: he went to work on the Kestrel.

The hangar at Two Rivers strip was his for another week under a barter arrangement — hangar space and fuel against a season of medicine runs — and the Kestrel sat in the lamplight with her cowling open, engine half-dressed, waiting for the valve inspection he'd promised himself he'd do tomorrow. He wiped tools. He torqued things that didn't need torqueing. He checked the oil twice and found it fine both times and checked it a third time anyway.

Twelve years ago he'd have taken the map straight to someone. There had been a time when Isandro Fairweather solved problems by talking them out loud, by dragging other people into them, by believing that two heads and shared weight beat one head carrying everything. That man had flown out of a coastal strip one morning with a full tank and a promise he couldn't keep, and the man who landed that afternoon was the one who'd been flying these runs ever since: solitary, punctual, pleasant in the way of locked rooms. Comfortable loneliness. He'd built it plank by plank and called it a life, and it had held up fine until a dying prospector had pressed a piece of waxed canvas into his hands and said *my daughter* with eyes like a man handing over a live grenade.

Because here was the thing he kept circling back to, the thing that had his stomach tight as a spun prop: the map wasn't addressed to him. It was addressed to Maren Hale. Which meant Tobin hadn't wanted the Gate protected. He'd wanted his daughter to have it — or to have the choice about it — and he'd trusted a stranger with the delivery because there was no one else left.

And if the Gate was real, then Isandro Fairweather was currently the only man in the interior holding a map to the most valuable square mile of land on earth, and he had already refused money for it once, which meant the people who wanted it now knew exactly how much trouble he intended to be.

He closed the cowling. Latched it. Stood there with his palm flat on the warm aluminum like he was taking the plane's pulse.

"Okay," he said to the hangar, to the map, to Tobin Hale wherever dead prospectors go. "Let's say I believe you."

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightjar called its three-note question and got no answer.

"Let's say it's real. Water that keeps memory. A company would burn the territory down for that — not to sell the water. To control it. To decide who remembers what." He thought of the debt-bonded miners down at Corvus's camps, men who signed contracts they couldn't read and forgot, year by year, why they'd ever come north. "Or worse. Not to control it. Just to make sure nobody else ever finds it."

That was the scenario that fit the consortium's fingerprints. They didn't need the Gate working. They needed it buried. A monopoly on forgetting required only that remembering stay impossible.

And a dying prospector had just handed the only map to a bush pilot with a reputation for keeping his word.

Isandro laughed, short and humorless, and picked the map up again. Fine. He'd deliver it. He'd deliver it fast, low-profile, and be gone before anyone in Merrow's Landing finished asking questions. One flight south, one handshake, one unburdening. That was the deal. That was all the deal had ever been.

He was folding the canvas along Tobin's creases when he noticed the last annotation — small, in the oldest ink, tucked into the bottom corner where a man might write something he didn't want found:

*If you're reading this, Isandro, she'll want to go. Don't let her. Some debts are paid by staying away.*

Isandro stood motionless for a long moment, the lamp buzzing, the nightjar calling its unanswered question out in the dark.

Tobin had known who'd carry the map. Had known it before Isandro himself had landed on that strip. Which meant the old man had chosen him — not because he was convenient, but because he was the kind of man who wouldn't let a girl walk into the interior alone, and therefore exactly the kind of man who'd have to be warned off in writing.

"Some debts," Isandro read aloud, testing the words, hating them. "Yeah. I know a little about those."

He folded the map away into the oilskin pouch, and the pouch into his flight jacket, and he was still standing there deciding whether to feel manipulated or honored when he heard it.

* * *

An engine. High, steady, throttled back to a patrol cruise — the particular lazy drone of a turbine turning circles rather than going anywhere.

At nine at night, over Two Rivers strip, where there was nothing to survey and nowhere to land but his bartered hangar and a gravel bar full of frog ponds.

Isandro killed the lamp and moved to the door crack. The stars were out hard now, the Milky Way spilled across the ridge like flour, and against it — there. A running light, red then green as it banked, maybe three thousand feet up, tracing a slow orbit around the strip. Twin-engine. Clean airframe. The kind of aircraft that cost more per hour than Isandro made in a month.

Consortium colors, if there had been any light to see them by. But he didn't need colors. Nobody else in the territory flew circuits over nothing at night. The consortium's survey wing did it constantly over the upper Serpent corridor — the surveyor in Merrow's Landing had said so, drunk and bitter — and Two Rivers sat close enough to that corridor that a plane doing grid work could drift this far east without anyone filing a complaint.

Except it wasn't drifting. It was orbiting. Holding station over his hangar the way a hawk holds over a mouse it hasn't committed to yet.

Isandro stepped back from the door, out of the sightline, and stood in the dark with his heart going harder than the situation strictly justified. Think. If they knew about the map, they wouldn't circle — they'd land, and there'd be a car waiting at his strip tomorrow with a polite man and a bigger number than Renner had offered at the fuel depot. Circling was reconnaissance. Circling was *we think something's here, confirm before we commit.* Which meant they didn't know. Yet. Which meant he had a window, and windows in the interior closed fast, like the low-water season, like Tobin Hale's eyes.

The plane completed its orbit and swung away west, lights shrinking, the drone fading until the night took it back. Heading, Isandro noted, roughly along the Serpent corridor. Toward the Fork. Toward the third dry channel and the basin shaped like a cupped hand.

They weren't following him. They were patrolling the approach — the way you patrol a border you're afraid someone's going to cross. Which meant somewhere in a consortium office there was a file on the Rivergate thicker than any pilot joke, and somewhere in that file, sooner or later, a line item was going to read *Hale, T., deceased, map disposition unknown,* and then the circling would stop being overhead and start being personal.

Isandro looked at the hangar, at the Kestrel gleaming dully in the starlight, at the fuel drums he'd have to sign for in the morning from a depot that took consortium credit.

He'd planned to sleep on it. Deliver the map in two days, easy legs, weather permitting. That had been the plan right up until a turbine engine drew a slow circle around his roof and flew away toward the one place on earth he was now carrying directions to.

Plans, he'd learned twelve years ago, were just promises you made to yourself before the world weighed in.

He pulled the flight jacket on, map riding heavy against his ribs, and began pulling the cowl checks he'd scheduled for tomorrow forward into tonight. The Kestrel would be ready to fly at first light. Whatever waited in Merrow's Landing — a grieving daughter, a consortium net tightening around the upper river, a truth forty years deep — it would wait better with him already airborne.

Out over the ridge, faint as a memory, the nightjar called again.

This time, Isandro thought he heard a second bird answer — and hated himself for listening.
