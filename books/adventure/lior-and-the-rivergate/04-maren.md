## 4. Maren

The letter came on a Tuesday, which was the day the mail boat ran, and Maren Hale had learned long ago not to expect anything from her father on any day at all.

She was mending a net on the dock when the boat nosed in against the pilings — the *Cormorant*, old Pell at the tiller, his dog asleep among the crates. She almost didn't look up. Merrow's Landing lived and died by what arrived on Tuesdays: diesel parts, flour, letters from sons downriver who'd found work. Maren had stopped hoping for anything addressed to her years ago. Hope was a hook you left in the water too long; it only caught you.

"Maren Hale," Pell called, and there was something in his voice that made her set down the netting needle.

He held out an envelope, soft-cornered, water-stained, the handwriting on it cramped and slanting like a man writing against pain. Her name in her father's hand. The first thing Tobin Hale had sent her in eleven years, and it wasn't even a letter — just her name, and below it, in smaller script, someone else's: *Deliver by hand. Urgent.*

"Who brought this?" she asked.

"Fella flew it in yesterday. Bush pilot. Says he's got more than the letter." Pell's weathered face did something complicated. "Says he's got news of your pa."

The net slid out of her hands into the water and she let it go.

* * *

She spent the hours until she found him doing the thing she hated herself for: rehearsing. Standing in her kitchen over the cold stove, wiping her hands on her apron again and again, composing sentences. *Is he alive? Is he hurt? Why now? Why after eleven years does he remember he has a daughter?* She rehearsed anger because anger was easier to carry than the other thing, the raw and stupid hope underneath it, the little girl still living somewhere in her chest who had waited every Tuesday for eleven years at this same dock, watching the mail boat come in, watching it leave, watching the water where nothing ever came.

Tobin Hale had gone upriver when she was nineteen. Not died. Gone. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth. He'd kissed her forehead, said *there's gold up in the high country, girl, one more season and I'll come back with enough to buy you whatever you want,* and then the seasons had stacked up like driftwood and he had never come back at all. No letter. No word passed down through traders. Nothing but silence, year after year, until silence became its own answer, and Maren had to build a life around the hole where a father used to be the way you build a house around a stump — awkwardly, resentfully, always tripping on it.

She found the pilot at the Landing's single roadhouse, sitting alone at a corner table with a cup of coffee he wasn't drinking, a canvas flight bag at his feet like it might bite him.

He was younger than she expected. Late thirties maybe, sun-browned, with the particular stillness of men who spend their lives alone in small machines above big empty places. He looked up when she came in, and she watched him decide something about her before she'd said a word.

"You're Maren Hale," he said. Not a question.

"I'm told you have news of my father."

"Isandro Fairweather." He stood, offered a hand, and she took it mostly because refusing would have cost her more than touching him. His grip was dry and careful, like he was used to handling things that could break. "I fly medicine runs out of Corvus Field. Your father—"

"Sit down," she said. "And start at the beginning."

He started at the beginning. A remote strip called Larkspur, a man collapsed on the fuel drums, fevered, half-mad, gold dust crusted in the creases of his knuckles. How the man had gripped his sleeve with the last strength he had and made him swear an oath before he'd even tell him his own name. How he'd carried him to a mission clinic two hundred miles south, and how the doctors there had shaken their heads in that way doctors do.

"He's dying," Isandro said. "Maybe dead already. I couldn't stay — they were flying him down to the coast hospital, but—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "Maren, I'm sorry. He asked me to bring you this."

He unzipped the flight bag and drew out a oilskin packet, tied with waxed cord, and laid it on the table between them like a man laying down something heavy.

Her father's handwriting on the outside: *For my daughter. All of it. Everything I should have said.*

Maren didn't touch it.

Eleven years. Eleven Tuesdays. And now a stranger with kind eyes and a careful voice sat across from her holding everything her father "should have said," as if words could be shipped like freight, as if eleven years of nothing could be paid off in a single delivery.

"Did he say why?" she heard herself ask. Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. She'd expected it to shake. "Why now? Why send a stranger instead of coming himself?"

"He's dying, Ms. Hale. Men don't travel easy when they're—"

"Men don't travel easy when they're ashamed," she said. "They hire couriers."

Isandro flinched. Just slightly, just around the eyes, but she saw it, and some cold practical part of her noted that this man knew something about shame himself. It was in the way he carried it, the way she carried hers — close to the body, weight distributed so no one could see where it sat.

"I'm not defending him," he said quietly. "I'm just the pilot."

"No," she agreed. "You're just the pilot." She finally reached out and touched the packet, just one finger on the oilskin, feeling the shape of what was inside. Paper. Folded paper, several sheets, and something harder beneath them, angular. "What is it?"

"A map. Hand-drawn. And papers — notes, journal pages. He made me swear to put it in your hands and no one else's."

"And what's on the map?"

Something moved behind his eyes then, quick as a fish under ice. "I don't know exactly. Rivers, ridges. Someplace far upstream."

Liar, thought Maren. Or at least a man being careful with the truth, which in her experience amounted to the same thing. But she was too tired, too full of grief with nowhere to put it, to press him. She picked up the packet. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than anything she'd ever held.

"Thank you for flying it," she said, standing. "You can bill me for the fuel."

"That's not—" He exhaled. "Ms. Hale. Maren. There are people looking for this map. A survey plane followed me out of the interior. This morning a man named Renner — works for the Meridian Consortium — came to my strip asking questions. They want what your father found. Whatever's up that river, it isn't just gold."

She stood very still. Outside the roadhouse window, the river went on doing what it always did, sliding past the town without stopping, going where it went whether anyone watched or not.

"Then they can want," she said.

* * *

She read the map that night in her kitchen with the door barred and the lamp turned low, though she couldn't have said who she was hiding from. Maybe herself.

The map was beautiful. That was the first thing. Her father had always drawn beautifully — she remembered the margins of her schoolbooks covered in his sketches, birds and boats and the profile of her mother, dead these fifteen years, rendered in sure fine lines. This was the same hand, older and shakier but still sure: the great river rendered as a living thing, branching and braiding, annotated in his cramped voice. *Rapids — bad. Portage left.* *Village — good people, trade for salt.* And then, near the top of the sheet, past all the places any charted river ended, a place marked with a circle and three words:

*The Rivergate. I saw it.*

Maren sat back and laughed, once, a hard sound with no humor in it, because every child of the interior grew up on the story. The source of the great river, hidden in country no survey had ever mapped, where the water rose whole from the earth. The pilots joked about it in the roadhouses. The old people spoke of it differently — lower, slower, the way you speak of things that might be listening. Water that kept what it touched. Water that remembered.

Fairy tales, she'd always said. The kind of story men like her father told themselves to make the empty country feel like it had a heart.

*I saw it.*

Beneath the map were the journal pages, and she read them until the lamp burned low and the night birds started up, and what she read broke her open in ways she hadn't braced for. Because it wasn't a treasure account. It wasn't gold-fever rambling. Page after page, in her father's failing hand, was apology. Draft after draft of a letter to her, begun and abandoned, begun and abandoned — *Maren, I know you'll hate me, and you should* — pages crossed out so heavily they'd torn. He'd been carrying these words up and down the interior for years, unable to finish them, unable to send them, unable to burn them either.

And threaded through the apologies, fragments of what he'd seen at the Gate. What the water had shown him. *It gives you back everything,* he'd written. *Every hour. Every face. I sat at the edge and I saw your mother's kitchen and you at nine years old with that crooked braid and I drank anyway and God forgive me, girl, I saw it all and I still walked away, because seeing isn't the same as bearing, and I couldn't bear it.*

Maren put the pages down. Her hands were shaking.

He had seen her. Sitting at that impossible water, he had seen her — nine years old, crooked braid, the exact image she'd have chosen if she could have sent him one memory — and he had drunk, and he had known everything, and he had still chosen the empty country over her.

That was the truth she'd needed for eleven years, and now she had it, and it was worse than abandonment. Abandonment she could hate. This — this was a man who had known exactly what he was leaving, in perfect detail, forever, and left anyway.

She should burn it. She should burn the map and the pages and the whole rotten inheritance of it, and let the river keep its secret, and go on building her house around the stump.

She sat in the dark instead, and listened to the river, and by the time the sky went grey she knew what she was going to do, and the knowing felt less like a decision than like a flood — something that had been rising behind a weak place for eleven years and had finally, finally broken through.

* * *

She found Isandro Fairweather at dawn on the dock, checking the tie-downs on a battered blue-and-silver bush plane with *KESTREL* stenciled above the cabin door. He looked up as her boots hit the boards, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop working.

"I'm going upstream," she said.

He straightened slowly. "Maren—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me it's dangerous. Don't tell me the dry season's closing and the low water will strand anything bigger than a canoe. Don't tell me about Renner and the consortium and men with guns. I've spent eleven years being reasonable while my father disappeared up that river one lie at a time. I'm done being reasonable." She pulled the oilskin packet from inside her jacket and held it between them. "This is mine. My name is on it. And I am going to follow it to the end, and if you won't fly me, I'll take the trader barges as far as they go and walk the rest, and I'll probably die doing it, but I will die going toward him instead of standing on this dock every Tuesday waiting for a mail boat that never once brought me anything that mattered."

The river slapped the pilings. Somewhere down the shore a gull screamed and was answered.

Isandro looked at her for a long moment. She watched him do the arithmetic pilots do — weight, distance, risk, the odds of a load coming home — and she watched the arithmetic lose to something else, something he probably hadn't authorized.

"The dry season window closes in three weeks," he said finally. "After that, the upper reaches drop too low for anything, and the storms start. If we go, we go today. And I'm not promising to reach the Gate. I'm promising to try."

"We," she repeated.

His jaw worked. "There's a survey plane that's been circling since yesterday. If Renner's people find that map, they'll dam the headwaters and bury whatever your father saw under a reservoir, and no one will ever—" He cut himself off, shook his head. "I made your father a promise to deliver the map. Delivering it means keeping it out of their hands. That means going upstream myself, as far as it takes." He met her eyes. "So yes. We. But hear me, Maren Hale — I fly the plane, I make the calls, and if I say we turn back, we turn back."

"Agreed," she said, and lied, pleasantly, the way her father would have.

She went home for one bag — warm clothes, her mother's river charts, the journal pages, a tin of her father's favorite tea that she'd kept for eleven years without knowing why — and stood a moment in the doorway of the house she'd built around the stump. Then she locked it, and put the key in her pocket, and walked down to the dock where the Kestrel waited with its engine already coughing into life.

As she climbed into the cabin, Isandro nodded at the sky to the north, where a pale cross of wings hung motionless over the ridge line, too high to identify, too patient to be innocent.

"Hold on," he said. "We're leaving now."
