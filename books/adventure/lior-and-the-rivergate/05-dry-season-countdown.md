## 5. Dry Season Countdown

The rain stopped at dawn and didn't start again, and that was how I knew the season had turned.

In the interior you learn to read weather like a ledger. Twelve years of flying had taught me the arithmetic: every dry day was a debit against the river, and the river was the only road that mattered once you left the strips behind. By late morning the mist off the Merrow had burned away to nothing, and by noon I could see the waterline dropping on the pilings of the town dock — a hand's width of pale wood exposed where yesterday there'd been only current.

I stood in the open door of Odile's old boathouse, which doubled as my hangar when she wasn't using it for cargo, and looked at the Kestrel. She sat on her trolley with her cowling off, and I'd spent two hours the night before cleaning grit out of the air filter and finding nothing wrong with her, which was its own kind of unease. A plane that runs perfect is a plane waiting to surprise you.

On the workbench beside me, wrapped in oilcloth inside a canvas map case, Tobin Hale's map lay like something with a pulse.

I hadn't slept much. Not because of the map — or not only because of the map. Because of Maren Hale, who had taken it from my hands two days ago with fingers that didn't shake, read it in silence for a long minute, and then said, "He drew this before he got sick. This is years of work." And then, without looking up: "You're going upstream, then."

Not a question. I'd told her no anyway. It hadn't stuck.

Now I wiped my hands on a rag and told myself the day's business was simple: fuel the Kestrel, file my medicine run north to Kettle Strip, be gone before the heat made the air too thin to trust. Simple things. A man can survive out here on simple things if he never looks too hard at the complicated ones stacked behind them.

The fuel drums were stacked under tarps at the back of the boathouse. I dragged one into the light, cracked the bung, and smelled aviation gas and river mud and the particular green rot of the Merrow in dry season, and that was when I heard footsteps on the dock boards behind me — measured ones, unhurried, the walk of a man who has never in his life needed to hurry because other people do his hurrying for him.

I knew before I turned around who it would be. The whole Landing had been talking about the consortium man for a week. Corvus Renner. He'd come upriver on the company packet boat with two quiet assistants and a trunk nobody had seen opened, and he'd rented the good room above the trading post, and he'd bought drinks for every guide and pilot and dockhand within reach, asking questions in a voice so soft you leaned in to hear it and hated yourself after.

"Mr. Fairweather." He stood at the boathouse threshold with his hands clasped behind him, a lean man in a linen jacket that had no business being that clean this far upriver. Gray at the temples, maybe fifty, eyes the flat brown of river water in August. He smiled the way a ledger balances — everything accounted for, nothing given away. "I hope I'm not interrupting maintenance."

"You are." I set down the fuel hose. "Come back tomorrow."

"I'll try to be brief." He stepped inside uninvited, the way men like him always do, and looked around the boathouse with the pleasant appraisal of someone pricing the building and everything in it. His gaze crossed the workbench, crossed the map case, and did not pause there — which was itself a kind of pause, if you knew what you were looking at. "They speak well of you here. Fair prices. Reliable delivery. A man who doesn't ask questions about what he carries."

"That last one costs extra."

His smile widened a fraction. "So I've heard." He drifted along the wall, fingertips trailing the hull of Odile's spare canoe. "Twelve years on this river. That's a long time for a coast man. Most of you burn bright for three or four seasons and go home with your money or your scars. You stayed. Why is that, do you think?"

"I like the weather."

"The weather," he repeated, tasting it. "Yes." He turned to face me fully, and for just a moment the pleasantness dropped away like a mask slipping on sweat, and underneath it was something older and tireder and much more dangerous. "Mr. Fairweather. I'm going to speak plainly, because I respect plain men, and because we don't have as much time as the river pretends we do. Old Tobin Hale came down from the high country six weeks ago carrying something. He collapsed at the Fenwick strip. You found him. You flew him out — or what was left of him — and you flew his belongings to Merrow's Landing, to his daughter. Among those belongings was a document. A map."

My face did what twelve years of practice had taught it to do, which was nothing. "Tobin Hale was dying of gold-dust fever and exposure. I carried him forty miles with him coughing blood into my headset. What he owned went to his daughter, because he asked me and because it was right. If there was a map in it, it's hers now. Take it up with her."

"Oh, I intend to." Renner examined his fingernails. "But she'll listen to you. You're the one who brought it. There's a trust there. Trusts are useful." He let that sit, then reached into his jacket and produced an envelope — heavy stock, company watermark, thick enough that I could guess the number inside before he said it. "The Halcyon Consortium is prepared to offer you twenty thousand for the map's return. Unopened, unread, undamaged. Twenty thousand, Mr. Fairweather, for a piece of paper you've already delivered and hold no claim to. That is not payment. That is gratitude."

Twenty thousand. I'd flown eleven years on contracts that paid in fuel credits and IOUs and the occasional sack of coffee beans. Twenty thousand was a new engine, a new prop, a hangar with a real roof, five years of not counting drumfuls. My hands wanted the envelope the way hands want a rail when the ground tilts.

"And if I say the map isn't mine to sell?"

"Then we move to the second part of the conversation, which I confess I hoped to avoid." He set the envelope on the workbench anyway, square in the middle, deliberate as a chess move. "Halcyon holds your fuel contract. All of it — Kettle Strip, Fenwick, the mission stations, the whole northern line. Every drum that reaches this river comes through our depot at Corben's Bend. We also hold your insurance, such as it is, through our underwriters. And we hold, through various instruments I won't bore you with, the paper on the landing fees at four of the seven strips you use." He spread his hands, apologetic. "Refuse, and none of it vanishes overnight. I'm not a barbarian. But contracts come up for renewal, and renewals require review, and reviews take time, and meanwhile your customers learn to call Corben's Bend and are told, regretfully, that supply is constrained this season. Dry seasons are hard on small operators, Mr. Fairweather. Some of them don't survive to wet season."

I looked at him for a while. He let me look. Men like him enjoy being seen; it confirms they matter.

"You know what's on that map," I said.

"I know what Halcyon believes may be on that map." Something moved behind his eyes — quick, involuntary, like a fish turning under the surface. "A geological feature of significant commercial interest. Water rights in the upper basin. Nothing more."

"Nothing more," I said. "That's why you're offering twenty thousand for it."

"Prudence." He straightened his cuffs. "Mr. Fairweather, let me tell you something I tell very few people, since you strike me as a man who respects candor over comfort." He walked to the boathouse doorway and looked out at the river, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the broker's polish, worn down to something raw. "I was a prospector once. Younger than you. I walked the high country when it had no name on any map worth trusting. I saw things up there. Lost things. And I came back down having forgotten the most important year of my life — not misplaced it, not mislaid it. *Gone.* Clean as a page torn out." He turned back, and the pleasantness was reassembled, seamless. "Whatever Hale found, whatever he drew, it belongs in hands that understand what they're holding. Not in the hands of a bush pilot and a grieving daughter who will chase it upriver and drown in the Serrated Reach, or worse — find it. Do you understand? I'm not threatening you. I'm trying to save two lives and a great deal of suffering."

"The Serrated Reach," I said quietly. "You know the river well."

"I know everything worth knowing about that river." He picked up his hat from the bench where he'd set it — I hadn't seen him put it down — and settled it on his head. "The offer stands until sundown tomorrow. After that, the numbers change, and not in your favor." At the door he paused, half-turned. "One more thing, since you're a pilot. The low-water window on the upper Merrow closes in nine days. Maybe ten. After that, nothing larger than a canoe moves above the Fork until the rains. Whatever anyone intends to do up there, they'll do it soon, or not at all." He tapped the brim of his hat. "Good afternoon, Mr. Fairweather. Enjoy the weather."

His footsteps receded down the dock, unhurried, and were gone.

* * *

I didn't touch the envelope for a full hour. I fueled the Kestrel, checked the tie-downs twice, and sat in the pilot's seat with the door open, watching the river drop.

Nine days. He hadn't needed to tell me that part. Any pilot on this water could read the same ledger — the bars surfacing downstream of the Fork, the tributaries going to brown threads, the way the morning fog had thinned to nothing. The dry season wasn't coming. It was here, and it was eating the river a foot a day, and every hour I sat in Merrow's Landing weighing envelopes was an hour the upper Merrow got shallower.

That was the trap of it, and I had to give Renner credit — the man understood pressure the way engineers understand load. He hadn't threatened me first. He'd offered me money first, and then the threat, and then, worst of all, the truth: that doing nothing was also a choice, and it had a price too. Stay grounded, keep my contracts, and the map sits in a girl's room in Merrow's Landing until Halcyon decides to stop being polite about it. Fly, lose everything I'd built in twelve years — the contracts, the strips, the careful anonymous life I'd assembled out of schedules and deliveries like a man building a wall out of timetables.

Because that was the thing I couldn't say to Renner, couldn't say to anyone: the contracts weren't the real cost. The real cost was that flying north on my medicine run meant staying the man I'd been for twelve years. The man who left the coast. The man whose reasons lived in a locked room in his head that he kept locked on purpose, because the last time he'd opened it, opening it had cost somebody everything.

And Maren Hale was going upstream whether I flew her or not. I'd known it when she said it, in that flat voice with her father's map in her hands. She'd book passage with Odile Brant, or hire some drunk guide, or steal a canoe, and she'd die in the Serrated Reach, or worse — she'd live, and find whatever was up there alone, with no one watching her back but the river.

Duty. That was the word Tobin Hale had used, coughing blood into my headset at the Fenwick strip, pressing the map case into my hands with fingers like yellow candle wax. *Carry it to my daughter. Swear it.* And I'd sworn, because a dying man's oath is the cheapest currency in the interior and the hardest to spend.

I got out of the Kestrel and walked to the end of the dock.

Merrow's Landing in late afternoon: the packet boat loading salt and lamp oil, kids diving off the pilings, the smell of woodsmoke and drying nets. Ordinary. The kind of place that runs on small contracts and smaller mercies, the kind of place that would fold up like wet paper if Halcyon decided it wanted the whole watershed. Which it would. Renner hadn't come all this way for a map. He'd come because maps lead somewhere, and somewhere is what companies buy.

Maren was on the porch of the boarding house when I came up the hill, mending a pack strap by lamplight, her father's compass open on the rail beside her. She looked up when my shadow fell across the boards, and I watched her read my face the way pilots read sky — fast, total, no illusions.

"He came to see you," she said.

"This afternoon."

"And?"

I sat down on the rail across from her. Up close she had Tobin's jaw and none of his softness, and eyes that had already decided things I was still arguing about. "Twenty thousand for the map. And when I said no, he explained how my fuel contracts would evaporate, my insurance would lapse, and four of my landing strips would develop paperwork problems. Then he told me the low-water window closes in nine days."

Maren wound the strap around her fist and pulled the stitch tight. "Will you lose everything? If we go?"

"Everything I own fits in the Kestrel. It's the flying I'd lose. The work." I looked out past her, toward the darkening line of the hills where the river came from. "Twelve years I've built a life where nobody asks me questions. One map and one dying prospector later, a man in a clean linen jacket knows my whole ledger."

"Is that a no?"

"It's a man listing the price out loud so he doesn't pretend later he didn't know it."

She almost smiled. It was the first time I'd seen anything on her face that wasn't grief or iron, and it lasted less than a second. "My father chose the river over me," she said, tying off the strap. "For thirty years. I'm not going to make the same choice in the opposite direction — pick the safe life and spend the rest of it wondering. So I'm going. Tomorrow, if the water allows. Alone if it has to be that way."

"It won't be that way." I heard myself say it before I'd voted on it, which is how the truest things get said. I stood, and pulled Renner's envelope out of my jacket, and held it a moment — twenty thousand, heavy as ballast — then dropped it into the cold stove inside the boarding house door and shut the grate on it. "Fuel contracts can be rebuilt. Dead friends can't."

"We're not friends yet."

"No," I agreed. "But we're leaving at first light, and the Kestrel only seats two, so you'll want to pack light."

Maren looked at the stove, then at me, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch — not softness exactly, but the setting-down of a weight she'd been carrying alone. "Nine days," she said.

"Eight, by the time we're airborne." I turned for the stairs, then stopped. "Maren. The man in the linen jacket — Renner. When he talked about the upper country, he talked like a man describing a scar. Whatever your father found up there, Halcyon doesn't just want it. They're afraid of it. Remember that when it matters."

Above the Landing, the first stars were out over the ridgeline, and the river ran dark and a little lower than it had that morning, carrying everything it carried toward the sea — except us. Come sunrise, we'd be going the other way.
