## 6. Upstream

We left Merrow's Landing an hour before dawn, when the fog still owned the river and the consortium's man was surely still asleep in whatever air-conditioned room he'd rented to hate us from.

Isandro ran the preflight by feel, his fingers reading the Kestrel's skin like braille — control surfaces, pitot tube, the patch of fabric he'd sewn over the starboard wing root two seasons ago that had never quite matched the rest. The sky east of the strip was the color of old tin. He didn't love it. But the dry season didn't care what he loved, and every day they waited was a foot of water gone off the upper river.

Maren stood at the edge of the grass strip with her father's map rolled in a leather tube across her back like a quiver, watching him work.

"You check it that hard, it's going to file a complaint," she said.

"Complaints are how you know a plane trusts you." He wiped his hands on a rag that had been black since before either of them could remember. "It's the ones that stay quiet you worry about."

She climbed in without being told where to put her feet, which surprised him. Most passengers did the dance — the hesitation at the step, the grab for whatever looked solid, the apology. Maren just folded herself into the second seat, cinched the harness, and said, "Which one of these is yours?" tapping the headset box.

"The left. You get the right."

"Lucky me."

He taxied out through ground mist that swallowed the wheels and spat them back, swung the Kestrel into wind, and pushed the throttle forward. The engine caught its stride with its usual complaint — a grinding note on the low end that Isandro had been meaning to have a look at for eleven months — and then the fog peeled away beneath them and Merrow's Landing shrank to a smudge of tin roofs and woodsmoke, and then to nothing at all.

The river took them north.

* * *

From three thousand feet, the Rivergate country did not look forbidden. It looked like every other green wrinkle of the interior — ridgelines running their long sentences east to west, tributaries stitching through them in silver cursive, cloud shadow moving over it all like a slow hand. But Isandro had flown this latitude enough to know the difference between country that was empty and country that was unmapped, and the map spread across Maren's knees was drawing them into the second kind.

Tobin Hale had drawn it by hand, in pencil gone brown with age and handling: the river's course rendered in a draftsman's careful line, annotated in a cramped, patient script. *Shoals, dry-season mark.* *Bluff, good anchor rock.* *Do not land below the fork — mud takes wheels.* And past all of it, past the last named place on any survey chart Isandro owned, a small symbol like a keyhole, and beside it, in letters pressed so hard the pencil had nearly torn the paper: *THE GATE.*

"You're quiet," Maren said.

"I'm flying."

"You were quiet before we took off, too."

"That's called thinking."

"It's called brooding," she said. "I grew up with a man who did it. I know the posture." She tilted the map toward the window, checking it against the river below, and he noticed she held it the way her father must have — edges squared, landmarks tracked in order. She'd never met Tobin Hale, as far as Isandro knew, but she flew his map like his blood.

"There's weather building northwest," he said, because talking about weather was easier than talking about anything else. "Storm cells along the front range. We'll thread between them if the gaps hold."

"And if they don't?"

"We go around."

"Around adds hours."

"Around keeps us alive. Hours are negotiable."

She looked at him for a moment — he felt it more than saw it, the way you feel a change in trim — and then she laughed, short and real. "Tobin's journal says something like that. *The pilot's first job is refusing the trip.*"

"He wrote that?"

"In the margin. Underlined twice."

Isandro grunted and put his eyes back on the horizon. The storm cells were visible now, stacked towers of white going gray at the base, rain falling from them in curtains you could see a river die inside. The front range rose beyond them, ridges no survey had bothered to name because nothing worth naming lived there — or so the surveys believed.

An hour in, the first cell drifted across their line, and Isandro banked south to slip behind it, and the world went dark as a pocket.

* * *

The turbulence came without warning, the way it always does — one breath of smooth air, then the sky picked up the Kestrel and shook it like a dog with a rat. The seatbelt bit into Isandro's shoulders. Loose items in the cargo bay behind them rattled and slid. Rain hit the windshield sideways, hard enough to sound like gravel.

Maren didn't scream. She made a sound — a sharp exhale, almost a word — and then she planted both boots against the floor, gripped the map tube across her lap with both hands, and watched the rain hammering the glass with an expression Isandro could only describe as furious attention. Like the storm owed her something and she intended to collect.

"Talk to me," she shouted over the engine.

"Why?"

"Because your hands are doing something and I want to know if it's fine or not fine."

He checked the gauges, the gap ahead, the gray wall of the next cell. "It's fine. This is weather doing weather. It doesn't know we're here."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It's the truest comfort there is. Nothing out here is personal."

The cell passed. The light came back gold and enormous, and the river appeared below them again, wider now, braided around gravel islands, running fast and shallow toward the interior. Maren let out a breath and unclenched her hands one finger at a time.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. That was — "

"Routine."

"If you say routine one more time I'm opening my window."

"The windows don't open."

"Then I'm opening yours."

Despite himself, despite twelve years of practice at keeping passengers at arm's length and the whole world at slightly further than that, Isandro found his mouth pulling sideways. "There's coffee in the bag behind your seat. It's terrible."

"I've had worse."

"Nobody's had worse."

She dug it out, poured from the dented thermos into the cup lid, and handed him the first cup without asking. He took it. That was the thing he noticed later, turning the day over in his mind: she handed him the first cup, and he took it, and neither of them remarked on it, and somewhere in that unremarked exchange a contract got signed that neither of them had negotiated.

They flew on into the unnamed country.

* * *

By early afternoon the river had narrowed and the terrain had turned hostile in the quiet way high country is hostile — not dramatic, just indifferent. Ridges crowded the water. The channel bent through gorges where the current showed white teeth even from the air. Tobin's annotations got sparser and more urgent: *Narrow. Wind funnels after noon.* *Rock bar mid-channel, submerged in wet years.*

And then, over a ridge that matched nothing on Tobin's map or any other, the engine stumbled.

It wasn't dramatic. A skip, a cough, a return to full song — the kind of thing a pilot logs and worries about and mostly forgets. Isandro's stomach dropped anyway. He knew that sound. He'd been hearing its rehearsal for eleven months in the grind on the low end.

"What was that?" Maren said.

"Grit, maybe. Bad fuel. Could be nothing."

"Could be nothing," she repeated. "You say that the way doctors say it."

The engine skipped again. Held. Skipped a third time, longer, and this time when it caught, the note was wrong — thinner, strained, a voice singing through gritted teeth. Isandro's hands moved before his mind finished the thought: mixture rich, carb heat on, fuel selector checked. The needle on the fuel pressure gauge trembled like something frightened.

Below them the river ran through a canyon whose walls offered exactly nowhere to land, and then, mercifully, the canyon opened into a long valley, and the river braided wide across it, and there — half a mile ahead, a pale ribbon against dark water — a gravel bar.

"Isandro."

"I see it."

"Tell me what's happening."

"Engine's ingesting grit. Sand in the works, chewing itself. I'm putting her down on that bar while she'll still let me choose where."

"Can you?"

"No idea," he said honestly, and shoved the nose down.

The Kestrel dove for the valley floor. The engine surged, died, caught again with a bang that made the whole airframe flinch. Two hundred feet. The gravel bar swelled beneath them, studded with driftwood and glacial stones, too short, always too short, everything in this country too short. Isandro flared late and hard, and the wheels touched with a crack like a rifle shot, and the plane ran — bounced once, airborne again, touched again — and then the left main gear found a buried boulder and the world became noise.

Stone shrieked under the fuselage. The propeller chewed itself into fragments with a sound like tearing sheet metal. Something heavy came forward from the cargo bay and struck the back of Isandro's seat hard enough to snap his head against the headrest. Then the Kestrel slewed sideways, plowed a furrow in the gravel, and stopped.

Silence. The particular silence of a dead engine, which is louder than any engine.

"Maren." His own voice sounded far away. "Maren, talk to me."

"I'm here." A pause. "I think my coffee is everywhere."

He turned. She was white-faced, one hand pressed flat against the cracked windshield, a thin line of blood at her hairline where the map tube had swung into her. Her eyes were wide and completely clear.

"You're bleeding."

"So are you." She reached over and touched his forehead, and her fingers came away red. "Headset cut you. Or the seat. You're not dying, right? Blink twice."

"Blinking once is fine." He was already moving, killing the mags, shutting off fuel, the ritual of survival performed by hands that had done it in drills a hundred times and never once for real until now. No fire. No fuel smell yet. The fuselage had held. The gear hadn't.

He sat back and let his hands shake, because they were going to whether he permitted it or not, and it was better to spend the shaking now than to fight it.

"Well," Maren said. She looked out at the river running silver and indifferent past the wreck of their wings. "That was not routine."

"No," Isandro admitted. "That one wasn't."

* * *

They salvaged for two hours in the long afternoon light, working with the wordless efficiency of people who have recently agreed, without discussion, to trust each other with their lives. Isandro pulled the survival kit, the tool roll, the tent fly, the medical kit, the radio — the radio that crackled once when he tested it and produced only the dry hiss of country where nobody was listening. Maren hauled crates clear of the wreck, sorted what was soaked from what was saved, and bound the cut on her scalp with a strip from her shirt sleeve, tying it one-handed behind her head without asking for help.

"You've done this before," Isandro said, watching her knot it.

"Camping? My mother took me every summer. She said the wilderness is the only honest teacher." Maren glanced at the wrecked Kestrel, its propeller ending in splinters, its belly opened to the sky. "Though I notice she never mentioned the part where the lesson tries to kill you."

"She leaves that part off the syllabus."

They built camp above the high-water line on the bank, in the lee of a fallen spruce, and ate tinned meat and the last of the bread while the light went amber and then violet over the ridgeline. The river talked to itself all night, low and continuous, a language of water over stone.

Maren sat wrapped in the tent fly, the map open across her knees even though there was no light left to read it by. "How far to the Gate, walking?"

"By Tobin's marks? Four days upriver. Five if the Serrated Reach is as bad as he says."

"And the Kestrel?"

Isandro looked at her. She asked it plainly, no accusation in it, and he appreciated that more than she could know. Everyone else would have made it a question about blame.

"She needs a new prop, probably a new carburetor, and a week of work I can't do here. Maybe Odile Brant comes through. Trader woman, runs the middle river. If she passes, we send word down. If she doesn't—" He shrugged. "We walk."

"Four days."

"Five."

"Then we should start at first light." She said it like checking a box. No drama, no negotiation. Just the arithmetic of a woman who had decided where she was going years ago and was simply waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

Isandro fed the small fire another stick and watched the sparks climb. Twelve years he'd flown alone, delivered strangers, kept every promise small enough to keep. Now he was sitting on a gravel bar at the edge of the mapped world with a bleeding eyebrow, a broken airplane, and a woman who handed him the first cup of coffee, and he realized the loneliness he'd built his life inside had developed a door, and Maren Hale was standing in it, and she wasn't even looking at him. She was looking upriver, into the dark, at a place her father had drawn a keyhole beside.

"Get some sleep," he said. "I'll take first watch."

She lay down without argument, and within minutes her breathing slowed into sleep — deep and immediate, the sleep of someone who trusted the person on watch. Isandro sat by the fire and listened to the river and did not examine why that trust sat in his chest like something warm and dangerous.

It was near midnight when he heard it: a sound carried down the valley on the night wind, faint but unmistakable.

An engine. Not his. An aircraft, running somewhere over the western ridge, throttled low, circling.

He killed the fire with a handful of dirt and sat motionless in the dark, listening, while the sound swept slowly along the river's course — searching — and Maren slept on behind him, unaware, and the stars wheeled over the unnamed country, cold and unconcerned, and Isandro Fairweather understood that they were no longer alone.
