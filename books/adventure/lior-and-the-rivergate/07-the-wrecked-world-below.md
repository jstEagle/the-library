## 7. The Wrecked World Below

The Kestrel sat on the gravel bar like a heron with a broken wing, nose-down, one float crumpled, the propeller bent into a shape Isandro refused to look at directly. Grit had gone through the intake on the forced landing — he'd heard it, that awful grinding cough, felt the engine die a half-second before the river rose up to meet them. He'd put her down hard and straight, which was the only mercy the morning offered.

Maren stood hip-deep at the edge of the bar, hauling crates toward dry ground with the methodical fury of someone who had decided grief could be carried in boxes. She hadn't said much since the crash. Neither had he. There was a language pilots and passengers developed after a wreck — silence first, inventory second, blame never, or last.

"Fuel's contaminated," he said finally, siphoning a sample into a tin cup. It came out gray-brown, glittering faintly with silt. "Even if I could patch the intake, she's not flying on this."

"So we walk."

"We're four hundred miles from Merrow's Landing and two hundred from anything you'd call a road." He screwed the cup shut. "We don't walk. We wait for the river to bring us somebody."

"And if it doesn't?"

"It will." He looked upstream, where the water ran fast and shallow over the gravel spine of the bar. "This is a trade route. Old one. Every pilot jokes about the Rivergate and every trader knows more than they say. Somebody comes through."

Maren wiped her hands on her jeans and studied him. She had her father's jaw, Isandro realized — Tobin Hale's face rebuilt by a younger hand, harder around the eyes. "You keep saying *somebody* like it's a prayer."

"It is."

* * *

They salvaged through the afternoon. The Kestrel's cargo bay had taken the landing better than her pride: medical crates intact, radio cracked but breathing — static, mostly, and once, faintly, a voice speaking Portuguese before dissolving again. Maren organized the supplies into loads while Isandro stripped what he could from the engine, working with his hands because his hands were calmer than his head.

It was Maren who found the tracks.

"Isandro." Her voice had changed. He came around the tail of the plane and saw her crouched at the waterline, pointing at the mud. Boot prints, wide and flat, half-washed but recent. And beside them, a long drag-mark like something heavy had been hauled to the bank.

"Barge," he said. "Poled or winched off the bar. Within a day."

"You sound relieved."

"I am relieved."

"You also sound like a man who knows exactly who's coming." She stood, brushing mud from her knees. "You've been expecting this since we crashed. You knew there were traders on this stretch."

He didn't lie to her. That was becoming a discipline, the way checking fuel levels was a discipline. "I hoped. Hoping and knowing are different currencies out here."

The trader came at dusk.

* * *

She arrived the way weather arrives — announced first by sound, a low diesel chug around the downstream bend, then by the barge itself, riding low and loaded, tarped cargo stacked like a floating village. A woman stood at the stern wheel, broad-shouldered, silver-haired, wearing a canvas coat that had been patched so many times the patches had their own patches. She brought the barge alongside the gravel bar with the casual precision of someone who had done it ten thousand times, killed the engine, and looked them both over without hurry.

"Wrecked," she said. Not a question.

"Grit in the intake," Isandro said.

"Gravel bar south of the Serrated Reach. Everyone eats it sooner or later." She spat thoughtfully over the side. "You're Fairweather. Fly medicine up the Vessel line. I've hauled your fuel drums."

"Odile Brant."

"Odile Brant." She said her own name like a man signing for a delivery. Then her eyes went to Maren, to the map case slung across her back, and stayed there a beat too long. "And you'd be carrying something."

"We're going upstream," Maren said.

Odile laughed — a short, barkless sound. "Everybody going upstream says it like that. Like it's simple. Like the river hasn't eaten better plans than yours." She jerked her thumb at the barge. "Get your gear aboard. I'm running as far as the Fork by Thursday, and I'll take you that far, but I'm not taking you past it, and I'm not taking you free. Fairweather can work it off. My winch cable's frayed and my deckhand quit at the last settlement."

"That works," Isandro said.

Maren glanced at him. "You're sure?"

"No," he said honestly. "But it beats walking."

* * *

The barge was called the *Patient Magpie*, and she smelled of diesel, dried fish, and old rope. Odile fed them river cat stew that night under a tarp strung between cargo stacks, rain starting somewhere above the gorge walls and drumming soft on the canvas. Isandro worked the frayed winch until his shoulders burned; Maren helped Odile re-tarps the load against the wet. By full dark they were underway, the Magpie pushing upstream against a current that seemed personally offended by their presence.

It was Maren who asked the question, because Isandro wouldn't.

"The Rivergate," she said, sitting across the cookbox from Odile. "My father drew a map to it. Before he died. Do you know it?"

Odile stirred the stew pot without looking up. The firelight moved on her face like water. "I know stories."

"Tell them."

The old woman ladled three bowls and handed them out, taking her time. When she finally spoke, her voice had dropped into the register people use for things they half-believe and fully fear.

"There's a place where the river starts whole. Not a glacier melt, not a seep — the whole river, rising out of the ground like breath. The water there is clear past clear. You can see your own face in it, and the face looks back like it knows you." She blew on her stew. "That's the Gate."

"And the water?" Maren leaned forward. "What does it do?"

Odile ate a spoonful. Made them wait. Isandro recognized the technique; traders used it on prices too.

"Memory," she said at last. "The water holds memory. Drink it, and everything comes back. Everything you ever saw, everything you ever lost, everything anyone ever tried to make you forget. Whole lives, poured back in one swallow." She looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were pale as fish bellies. "That's the story. And here's the part the stories don't like to tell: it doesn't just give back what *you* forgot. It gives back what others paid to forget. Secrets sold. Crimes buried. Names bought and erased. You drink, and you remember everything — including things that were never yours to carry."

The rain ticked on the tarp. Somewhere downstream, thunder muttered like an argument.

"People drink it?" Isandro asked quietly.

"Some." Odile set down her bowl. "There was a prospector, years back. Came through here with gold fever and a bad heart, heading upriver alone. Came back down six months later walking like a man carrying furniture — all his memories, stacked and heavy. He never told anybody what he saw up there. Just paid me passage to the coast and cried the whole way down the Reach. Quiet crying. The kind you do when nobody's supposed to notice."

Maren had gone very still. "What was his name?"

"Tobin Hale."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool. Maren's hand went to the map case at her back, gripping it through the canvas.

"He drank from it," she said. "He actually drank."

"If the stories are true, he did." Odile shrugged. "And then he spent thirty years not telling his daughter why he left. Which tells you something about what the Gate gives back. It doesn't give you peace. It gives you *everything*. And everything includes the parts you'd have paid good money to lose."

Isandro watched Maren across the fire. He watched hope and dread fight for her face, and neither one winning, and understood suddenly that the map in her hands had stopped being a dead man's directions. It had become a door. And doors, once known about, get opened — by her, or by whoever wanted what was behind it badly enough.

He thought about the coast. Twelve years ago. The thing he'd left there, folded up small and locked down deep, the thing he did not name even in his own head. If the water was real —

He caught himself reaching for the thought and pulled his hand back like it had touched a hot stove.

"You've seen it yourself?" he asked Odile. "The Gate?"

"No." She said it flat and final. "And I never will. I haul cargo. Cargo goes up, cargo comes down. What's at the top of the river stays at the top of the river, and the people who go looking—" she tapped her temple, "—come back different. Or don't come back."

"Renner will come looking," Isandro said. "Corvus Renner. Consortium man. He wants the source dammed and the power locked down. He offered me money for the map."

Odile's face changed — not fear exactly, but the wariness of a woman recalculating a route. "Renner." She said the name slowly, tasting it. "He was on this river before he wore a suit. Prospector's kid. Came up the Reach twenty-five years ago with a partner, both of them chasing the same rumor everyone chases." She paused. "Only one of them came back down."

"What happened to the other one?" Maren asked.

Odile looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw in Maren's face made her answer. "Nobody knows. But Renner came back alone, and rich, and quiet, and he never came upriver again. Until now." She gathered the bowls. "A man like that doesn't want the Gate for the water. He wants it so nobody else can have what happened to him. That's not greed. That's a wound with a budget."

She stood, joints cracking, and nodded upstream into the dark. "Fork's two days. After that you're on your own, and after that—" she shrugged, "—the river decides. Sleep. Both of you. The Magpie runs at dawn."

Maren unrolled her bedroll but didn't lie down. Isandro heard her moving in the dark, and then her voice, low:

"He knew. All those years, he knew what he'd forgotten — no. He remembered everything, and he still couldn't tell me." A pause. "Why would remembering make a man less able to come home?"

Isandro stared at the tarp ceiling, at the shadows the firelight threw there, and thought about a coast he'd fled and a promise he'd broken, and how some knowledge sits in a man like ballast — not lifting him, just keeping him down.

"Maybe," he said, "because the truth isn't always a gift. Sometimes it's just weight."

In the dark, Maren was quiet a long time. Then: "I'm still going."

"I know," Isandro said. "So am I."

Upstream, the current pulled against them, patient and cold, carrying everything it had ever been given toward a source that gave it all back.
