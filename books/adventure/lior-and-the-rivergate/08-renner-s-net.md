## 8. Renner's Net

Corvus Renner had closed rivers before.

Not with dams — not yet, not here — but with paper. A river was just water until you owned the checkpoints on its banks, and a checkpoint was just two men, a tarp, and a clipboard. He'd learned that in the southern concessions, where he'd strangled three watersheds in a single fiscal year without firing a shot. Men thought power looked like machinery. It didn't. It looked like paperwork, patience, and the slow understanding in a man's eyes when he realized every road out of his own country now had a tollbooth on it.

He stood at the window of the consortium's field office in Merrow's Landing — a converted cannery that still smelled faintly of old brine and diesel — and watched the river move. The Merrow. Fat, brown, unhurried. Feeding off a hundred tributaries that fed off a thousand creeks that all, eventually, if the maps and the myths were to be believed, drank from one place.

The Rivergate.

Renner did not believe in myths. He believed in hydrology, in flow rates, in cubic meters per second. But he had seen the survey data, and the data said something impossible: the upper watershed lost more water than it collected. The interior drank itself. Somewhere up there, the river rose whole out of the ground, and everything downstream of that spring was runoff from a miracle.

And somewhere upstream right now, in a battered blue-and-white bush plane with a reputation bigger than its airframe, a pilot named Isandro Fairweather was carrying a dead man's map toward exactly that.

"Sir." Pell, his assistant, stood in the doorway with a tablet hugged to her chest like a shield. "The strip contacts are confirmed. Kessik, Two Rivers, Brannock's Crossing. All three will hold any aircraft matching Fairweather's tail number."

"Payment?"

"Half up front, half on report. Standard."

"And the guides?"

Pell hesitated. That was the thing about Pell — she hesitated honestly, which made her useful and occasionally infuriating. "Two of the four took the money. The other two say the upper river isn't theirs to sell."

"Everyone's is theirs to sell," Renner said mildly. "They just haven't met their price yet. Double the offer. If they still refuse, put them on the list."

"The list" meant they'd never get a fuel contract, a supply barge, or a medical flight again. In the interior, that wasn't exile. It was death with extra steps.

"Yes, sir." She turned to go.

"Pell." He didn't look away from the river. "The bounty. Read it back to me."

"Fifty thousand for confirmed location of Fairweather's aircraft. One hundred for the map, intact. And—" she paused again, and this time he let her, because he wanted to hear whether she could say it without flinching, "—the woman. Maren Hale. Unharmed. Two hundred thousand."

Unharmed. As if harm were a thing you could invoice against later. Renner almost smiled.

"Good. Send it out on every channel we own. I want the whole river talking about nothing else by nightfall."

* * *

The whole river talked about nothing else by nightfall. Renner knew because he sat in the cannery office listening to the radio traffic roll in like weather — pilots gossiping, traders speculating, someone in Two Rivers swearing he'd seen the Kestrel lift off heading north-northwest into weather no sane man would fly. Fifty thousand dollars moved through a frontier like blood through a wound. You could feel the pulse of it.

He let them talk. Talk was free intelligence. Every rumor told him something: who was greedy, who was loyal, who was afraid. The ones who called in tips within the first hour were the ones he'd never trust with anything real. The ones who stayed silent were either honest or already bought by someone else, and both categories required attention.

What he didn't expect was the call that came at midnight, on the old frequency, the one printed on no consortium document.

A voice he hadn't heard in thirty-one years.

"Corvus." The voice was older, sanded down, but the cadence was the same — that lazy drawl that used to drive him crazy across a campfire. "You always did work late."

Renner sat very still. Outside, the river kept moving, indifferent as ever.

"Odile Brant," he said.

"Heard you were buying the river. Thought I'd come see what a man pays for a thing he already sold once."

* * *

She came to the office at dawn, which was a kind of insolence, and he received her alone, which was a kind of weakness, and they both knew it and neither cared. Odile Brant had aged the way driftwood ages — grayed, cracked, lighter than she looked, harder than she looked. She wore a trader's coat with forty years of patches and she sat down without being asked and put her boots on his desk.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look old."

"I am old. You're terrible. There's a difference." She studied him with eyes the color of shallow water over slate. "I heard about the bounty. I heard about the checkpoints. I heard you bought Kessik and Two Rivers for pocket change." She leaned forward. "I also heard you're offering two hundred thousand for a girl you want delivered unharmed. Corvus. I knew you when you couldn't deliver a mule unharmed. What happened to you?"

"What happened to me," Renner said, "is none of your business."

"It became my business when you started netting my river."

"Your river." He laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "Nobody's river. That's the point, Odile. That's always been the point. Water doesn't belong to anyone, so it belongs to whoever can hold it. The consortium can hold it. I can hold it."

"For whom?"

"For people who need it." He heard himself say it and hated how rehearsed it sounded, because it was rehearsed; he'd been saying some version of it to boards and ministers and mirrors for twenty years. "Villages downstream that lose their wells every dry season. Towns that watch their children drink from ditches while some chief's cousin sells permits. You think I'm doing this for the shareholders? Let the shareholders think that. What I'm building is control. Control means nobody upstream can dam it, poison it, or sell it out from under the people who drink it. You want the river free? Free is just another word for unguarded."

Odile listened to all of it with the patience of a woman who had heard men justify things across a hundred campfires, and when he finished she said only:

"You've been up there, haven't you. To the source."

The room went quiet. The river went on outside, brown and patient.

"That's a story," Renner said.

"So tell it."

And God help him, he found he wanted to. Thirty-one years, and he had never told it — not to the board, not to the doctors they'd flown in afterward, not to the priest in the coastal city who'd listened with professional sympathy and understood nothing. It lived in him like shrapnel. And here was an old woman with driftwood eyes who might be the last person alive who'd remember the world before he became whatever he was now.

So he told it.

* * *

He was twenty-three, and his partner was a man named Elio Sartorius, and they were prospecting the headwaters because everyone prospected the headwaters eventually — chasing the rumor that the river's source ran over gold the way other rivers ran over gravel. They had a canoe, six weeks of food, and a map Tobin Hale had drawn for them drunk, laughing, charging them nothing but a bottle, saying *you don't want to find it, boys, but you will anyway, so take the map and may it cost you less than it cost me.*

It cost more.

They found the gorge by following the whispers — wind through stone that spoke in voices, and Elio swearing one of them was his mother, dead nine years, calling his name in her kitchen voice. Renner had laughed at him. He remembered that. He remembered laughing, the exact sound of it, arrogant and bright, and then he remembered the basin opening below them like an eye.

Water so clear it looked like absence. Air thick with murmurs. And a thirst that came on him like a fever, sudden and total, a thirst that had nothing to do with his body.

Elio drank first. Renner watched him kneel at the edge and cup the water and drink, and watched his face change — watched thirty years of grief arrive in it at once, every loss landing simultaneously, his mother's voice and his mother's death and every day between. Elio wept and laughed and wept, and then he stood up and said, in a voice Renner barely recognized:

"They'll pay for this. Do you understand? Everything in here — they'll pay anything."

"Who will?"

"Anyone. Everyone. Memory, Corvus. Whole lives, bottled. You know what a dying rich man would give to hold his mother's voice again? You know what a guilty man would pay to *forget*?" Elio's eyes were wet and shining and utterly sane, and that was the worst part. "We're standing on the most valuable thing on earth."

They argued for two days. That was the part no one believed afterward — that there was no violence, no betrayal in the dark. Just argument. Elio wanted to bring the consortium in, build the infrastructure, start the trade. Renner wanted to seal the gorge and tell no one. And on the third morning Renner woke and Elio was gone — gone downstream with samples in wax-sealed jars and a notebook full of flow measurements, gone to the coast, gone to sell.

Renner followed. Of course he followed. He caught Elio in a port town, and they fought, and it was still just a fight, fists and fury, two young men destroying each other over a secret — and then Elio fell. Hit his head on the harbor steps. Died in Renner's arms on wet stone with the tide coming in, and the last thing he said was *the jars, save the jars,* and Renner saved the jars, because he was twenty-three and grieving and didn't know what else to do.

The jars held water. The water held memory. And the buyers — there were buyers within a month, once word moved through the right channels — the buyers taught Renner the lesson that organized the rest of his life:

Memory was the only commodity with infinite demand and finite supply.

He sold Elio's jars over five years. He sold a widow her husband's last day. He sold a war criminal his victims' faces so he could finally feel what he'd done. He sold a mother the sound of a child she'd given up. Each transaction he told himself was mercy, and mostly it was, and the money compounded, and the money built the network, and the network built the consortium's interest in the interior, and all of it — every contract, every checkpoint, every dollar — traced back to a boy kneeling at a spring, watching his best friend's face fill with ghosts.

And here was the thing he had never said aloud, the thing that had brought him up this river with clipboards and bounty notices instead of a canoe:

He had never drunk.

Thirty-one years. He owned the trade in memory and he had never tasted it. Because he knew what was in that water for him. He knew what the Gate kept for Corvus Renner: the sound of Elio's skull on the harbor steps. His own laughter, two days earlier, at a grieving man in a gorge. Every jar, every sale, every widow's tears he'd banked as profit. The Gate gave back everything, and everything, for him, was a ledger of the damned.

He wanted it anyway. That was the sickness of it. Some nights the wanting was so clean and total it felt like the only honest feeling he had left. To remember all of it — to hold it, all at once, and finally be a man who had paid for what he'd done instead of a man who'd invoiced it.

But he couldn't. Not yet. Not until the source was secured, controlled, owned — because if he knelt at that water as a free man, he might never stand up. And if he knelt at it as the man who owned it, who had bent the entire river to his will first — then drinking would be a coronation, not a surrender.

That was the plan. That had always been the plan. Dam the spring, pipe the water, meter the memory. Sell restoration by the liter. Build the world's first archive of souls and charge admission.

And then a dying prospector's map had fallen into the hands of a bush pilot with a conscience and a daughter with a grudge, and the whole architecture had developed a crack.

* * *

"—and that's why you'll fail," Odile said.

Renner blinked. He'd been talking longer than he intended. Much longer. The office was bright with full morning now, and Odile sat across from him with her boots still on his desk, having listened to thirty-one years without blinking.

"Why?"

"Because you think owning the water means owning what's in it." She took her boots down and stood. "The Gate doesn't sell, Corvus. It gives. All of it, to anyone who drinks. No metering. No ledgers. You can't dam that. You can only break it." She paused at the door. "Fairweather knows that. I can see it from here. The question is whether you figure it out before you're standing at the edge with your knees bending."

"Where are they, Odile?"

She smiled, and it was the smile of a woman deciding something. "You know what's funny? I ferried them aboard my barge two days ago and I never even asked their names. Didn't need to. A pilot who flies like the plane owes him money, and a girl with her father's jaw." She opened the door. "I'm not telling you where. But I'll tell you this for free, since you gave me a story for free: the Serrated Reach is running at eight inches under seasonal. Nobody shoots it in a canoe. Nobody."

After she left, Renner stood at the window a long time. Then he picked up the radio and called Kessik strip, Two Rivers, Brannock's Crossing, and the four hired boats waiting at the lower canyon mouth.

"Change of orders," he said. "Forget the aircraft. Forget the map. Watch the water. If anyone puts a canoe in above the Reach — anyone at all — I want to know within the hour."

He set the handset down and looked at the river, brown and heavy and going somewhere he couldn't follow yet.

"Hold on, Elio," he said quietly, to no one. "I'm coming to settle accounts."

Upstream, the dry season tightened its grip, and the water level fell another inch.
