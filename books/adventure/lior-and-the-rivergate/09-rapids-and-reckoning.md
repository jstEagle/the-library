## 9. Rapids and Reckoning

The Serrated Reach announced itself a full hour before we saw it — a sound like the river grinding its teeth.

We'd left Odile's barge at dawn, tied up below a bend she called the Widow's Elbow, where the channel split into three braids around gravel islands and none of them looked friendly. She'd given us her spare canoe, a battered cedar-strip thing with a patched hull and a paddle worn smooth by thirty years of other people's hands. She'd also given us advice, delivered while she smoked and watched the water with the flat appraisal of a woman who had buried friends in it.

"The Reach runs six miles through cut rock," she said. "Serrated because the limestone comes up in blades. You can't portage it — the walls are sheer. You run it or you climb out of this world entirely." She tapped ash into the current. "Stay left of the white fang at the second drop. Right side eats canoes."

"And after the second drop?" Maren asked.

Odile smiled without any humor in it. "After the second drop you'll be too busy to ask questions."

Now, an hour downstream, I could hear what she meant. The river narrowed ahead into a corridor of gray stone, and the sound coming out of it wasn't the lazy murmur we'd been riding for two days. It was a roar with texture in it, a churning, tearing sound, like something being ripped slowly apart.

I backed water and let the canoe swing into an eddy behind a boulder the size of a barn.

Maren knelt in the bow, hair tied back, sleeves rolled, looking at the whitewater the way she looked at everything — like it owed her an answer. Three days on the river had changed her. The woman who'd met me in Merrow's Landing with her arms crossed and her grief worn like armor had been replaced by someone leaner, hungrier, sunburned along the cheekbones, her hands blistered from the paddle and proud of it. She hadn't complained once. She hadn't asked to turn back once. Some part of me — the part that had spent twelve years flying alone so I'd never have to watch anyone else get hurt — found that terrifying.

"Scout it?" she called over the noise.

"From here we can see most of it." I pointed with my paddle. "First drop's that chute, center-left. Then a pool. Then the big one."

She squinted downstream. "The one that sounds like the end of the world."

"That's the one."

"Odile said left of the white fang."

"Odile said a lot of things." I shipped the paddle and studied the line again. The first chute was runnable — a tongue of green water funneled between two rocks, fast but clean. The pool below gave us maybe forty meters to set up for the second drop, where the whole river compressed through a gap barely wider than the canoe and detonated into standing waves. Somewhere in that chaos was the white fang, a fin of limestone Odile swore would peel a hull like fruit if you touched it.

I'd run water like this before. Once. A lifetime ago, on the coast, before the coast became a word I couldn't say. My hands remembered more than my head did, and right now they were remembering plenty.

"Isandro." Maren had turned to look at me. "You've gone quiet."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like." She shifted back against the thwart, facing me. "Talk to me. What are we actually looking at?"

I told her the truth, because lying to her had stopped working somewhere around the second day. "First drop is easy. Second drop is a coin flip. If we hit our line, we're through. If we're off by two meters, we're swimming, and swimming in there means the river keeps you."

She absorbed that. I watched her do it — watched the fear arrive and get processed and filed somewhere behind her eyes, and watched her come out the other side still sitting upright.

"My father ran rivers," she said finally. "That's what the letters never said, but the villagers downriver talked about him like he was half fish. He shot the Serrated Reach twice, Odile thinks." Her jaw tightened. "He did everything I'm doing. Everything except come home."

"Maren—"

"We should go before the wind picks up." She turned back to face downstream, and the conversation closed like a door.

I dug the paddle in and pulled us out of the eddy.

* * *

The first drop took four seconds and felt like falling off a roof into cold silk. The nose dropped, the green water swallowed us, the world went white and loud, and then we were through, both of us soaked, Maren whooping once — a raw, involuntary sound, the first unguarded noise I'd ever heard her make — before clamping her jaw shut again and paddling hard for the pool.

Forty meters of relative calm. My heart was going like a piston but my hands were steady, and I ferried us hard toward the left bank to set up the line.

"There!" Maren pointed. "The fang!"

It was exactly where Odile said it would be — a blade of pale stone jutting from the boil on the right side of the gap, water peeling off it in a V. Left of it, the channel looked almost civilized: a steep, plunging chute with a wave train beyond, big but honest.

"Left of the fang," I shouted. "Hard left. Then straight down the middle and don't stop paddling no matter what."

"No matter what," she repeated.

We came in hot. Too hot — the pool had less slack in it than it looked, and the current had opinions. I corrected, overcorrected, felt the canoe yaw as we crossed the eddy line into the drop, and for one clean instant the whole river seemed to hold its breath.

Then the Serrated Reach took us.

The drop hit like a fist under the keel. We plunged, rose, plunged again, and the third wave came at us sideways — a green wall with a curl on top — and I knew before it landed that we weren't square to it. It caught the starboard quarter and threw us. The world spun. Rock, water, sky, rock. Maren's voice, high and clear: "Isandro—"

The canoe wrapped around something submerged. I felt the hull flex, crack, and quit.

Cold took me all the way to the spine. I came up gasping inside the maelstrom, one hand still somehow on the paddle, and the first thing I saw was the canoe, upside down, pinned against the fang itself — and no Maren.

"MAREN!"

The river answered with noise. I got one stroke, two, scanning the foam, the holes, the sweep of the current toward the next drop — and then I saw her, twenty meters down, a flash of yellow shirt, surfacing and going under again, tumbled like laundry in a current that didn't care that she was the reason I was on this river at all.

Everything in me went very simple and very quiet.

I've thought about those ninety seconds many times since, and every time the memory comes back the same way: not panicked, not heroic, just stripped down to mechanics. Angle across the current. Ferry, don't fight. Let the river give you what it takes from someone else. I caught an eddy behind a blade of rock, sprinted along a gravel shelf with the water at my knees, and dove into the tail of the hole just as she surfaced again, closer now, her arms windmilling, her mouth open on a scream the roar ate whole.

Her hand hit mine like a bird striking glass. I grabbed her wrist, then her forearm, then hauled her against me and rolled us both downstream, away from the fang, away from the recirculating water that wanted to keep her forever. We came apart and came together and came apart, and the whole time I held onto one wrist and one wrist only, and the river hammered us against rock and tried to pry us loose, and I did not let go.

The current finally spat us into a long pool below the drop, and I swam for the gravel bar dragging her with me, and when my feet found bottom I stood up out of the water carrying her, because my legs decided that was happening whether either of us agreed or not.

I laid her on the stones. She coughed — a great racking convulsion — and rolled sideways and vomited river water, and then she was breathing, ragged and furious and alive, and I sat down hard beside her and discovered that my hands were shaking so badly I had to press them flat against my thighs to make them stop.

"You," she rasped, "let go of the paddle."

"What?"

"In the drop. When we spun. You let go of your paddle to grab me." She was on her hands and knees, dripping, glaring at me with red-rimmed eyes. "Nobody lets go of their paddle. That's the first rule. You nearly killed us both."

"If I hadn't—"

"You don't know that! Maybe we punch through. Maybe we swim together. Instead you—" Her voice cracked. She turned away, shoulders heaving, and for a moment I thought she was crying, but when she looked back her face was dry and terrible with something else. "Why? Why would you do that?"

And because the adrenaline had burned everything else away, because the river had already taken my canoe and my composure and most of my defenses, I told her the truth.

* * *

"Her name was Liana," I said.

Maren went still.

We were further up the bar, out of the spray, the wrecked canoe a sad ribcage of cedar wedged against the fang above us. The sun was working its way down toward the ridgeline. I hadn't planned to say any of this. I'd had twelve years of practice not saying it.

"My sister. Younger than me by three years. After our parents died she was — she was mine to look after. That's how I thought of it. Not a responsibility. A possession, almost. Something I was keeping safe." I picked up a flat stone and turned it over in my fingers. "I flew supply runs out of Puerto Oscuro back then. Good money, bad reputation, and a man named Esteban Reyes who paid triple for night landings nobody asked questions about. I knew what he was moving. I told myself I didn't, which is a different thing.

"Liana found out. She was nineteen and she had our mother's gift for seeing straight through people, and she told me to stop. One last flight, she said. Pay off the plane, fly one last load, and then take her up the coast like we always talked about. Show her the delta. She'd never seen the delta."

The stone in my hand was smooth and cool. I kept turning it.

"The last load was medicine," I said. "That's what the manifest said. It was medicine the way I was a church deacon. Reyes's men loaded the crates themselves, and Liana was there, and she begged me not to go. Not argued — begged. On the strip, in the rain, holding onto my sleeve. And I made a speech. I still remember making it. Duty, obligation, a man's word is his bond, all the furniture of cowardice dressed up as principle. I told her a promise was a promise." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I promised her the delta. I chose the crates instead.

"The plane went down in the storm off Cabo Rojo. Both pilots. The cargo burned where it sank, and whatever was in those crates never reached anyone, and two men I'd flown with for years died in salt water, and I — I'd been grounded. Food poisoning. The one time in my life my body refused to obey my schedule. I heard about the crash on the radio in a diner, eating soup."

Maren hadn't moved. The river roared behind us, indifferent.

"Liana didn't speak to me for a year. Then she spoke to me at the funeral, one sentence, and then she moved to the coast and I moved here, twelve hundred miles of interior between us, and I told myself it was distance, it was work, it was the dry season, it was always something. She wrote. Twice a year, then once. Then a letter came from someone else." I set the stone down with exaggerated care, the way you handle something that might go off. "Fever. Quick. I got the letter eleven days after they buried her. Eleven days, because I'd changed strips and hadn't left a forwarding address, because leaving no address was the closest thing to freedom I understood.

"So when you ask why I let go of the paddle —" I spread my hands. Water was still running off them. "Because I have already done the arithmetic where I keep hold of what I'm supposed to keep hold of and lose the person anyway. I have run that line. I know where it goes. I would rather drown holding your wrist than stand on the bank holding the manifest."

The silence afterward was enormous. The Serrated Reach filled it, roaring through its teeth, and Maren sat with her knees drawn up, watching me with an expression I couldn't read — grief, recognition, anger at having been handed something this heavy, all of it moving behind her eyes like weather.

"You never told anyone," she said at last. "Not Odile. Not the elders at the last village. I watched you talk to people for three days, Isandro. You tell them the weather and the fuel prices and nothing else."

"Telling people doesn't fix it."

"No." She looked out at the water. "But drowning alone in it, that doesn't fix it either. Believe me. I'm the family expert."

A laugh escaped me — small, surprised, real. She glanced over, and the corner of her mouth moved, and for a moment we sat there on the gravel like two survivors of a shipwreck, which is what we were, laughing at absolutely nothing, shivering in wet clothes while the river that had nearly killed us went on shouting about its own importance.

Then she leaned over, and her shoulder came to rest against mine, and neither of us moved away.

"She'd have liked you," I said quietly. "Liana. She didn't put up with men who made speeches either."

"Your father," I added, before I could stop myself, "walked away out of shame. I know that now. I've read enough of his trail to know it. But shame is just love with nowhere to put itself. I understand him better than I want to."

Maren was quiet a long moment. "Don't make me forgive him yet," she said finally. "I'm not done being angry. Anger is the only piece of him I brought with me."

"Keep it. It paddles well."

That earned me a real laugh, short and startled, and she punched my arm hard enough to mean it, and we sat there while the light went gold and then amber and then blue.

* * *

We salvaged what the river gave back. The waterproof duffel with the map survived, lashed as it was to my chest since Merrow's Landing — I'd slept with the thing strapped to me like a second heartbeat, and I wasn't stopping now. Two paddles washed ashore, one of them Odile's, cracked but usable. Half the food was gone. All of the canoe was gone; from the bar we could see it still wrapped against the fang, and it wasn't going anywhere, and neither, clearly, were we — not downstream, not without a boat.

It was full dark when Maren, poking through the driftwood at the edge of the bar, went suddenly rigid.

"Isandro."

I came over with the flashlight. Beyond the driftwood, half-buried in the sand, was a plank — planed wood, iron nails, river-polished. And another. And the curved ghost of a hull, long rotted, wide-beamed, built for cargo.

"Barge-sized," I said slowly.

Maren crouched, brushing sand from the wood, and froze. Carved into the plank, shallow and half-worn, was a mark I recognized from Tobin's map margins — a little hooked symbol like a fisherman's fly, the same mark that appeared at every campsite along his route.

"He came through here," she whispered. "In a boat. Through the Reach." She looked up at me, and the firelight and the fear and the hope were all tangled together in her face. "Isandro — if he ran this river in something bigger than a canoe, somebody built it for him. Somebody who knew these waters."

Downstream, faint on the night wind, carried impossibly far over the roar of the rapids, came a sound I'd been half-listening for since the crash: the low, patient throb of an engine on the water.

Not ours. Not Odile's.

Somebody else was on the Serrated Reach tonight.
