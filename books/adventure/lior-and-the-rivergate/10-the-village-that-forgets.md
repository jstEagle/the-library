## 10. The Village That Forgets

The village announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and river mud and something sweeter underneath, like crushed mint. Maren smelled it from the canoe while the Serrated Reach was still growling behind them, a day's paddle downstream, her arms still trembling from the rapids, her throat still raw from screaming Isandro's name when the water took him under.

He'd come up coughing. He'd held her after, both of them shaking on the gravel bar, and neither of them had said anything that mattered. Some things you didn't say. You just kept paddling.

Now the river widened and slowed, and around a bend of red clay bluffs the village appeared — forty-odd houses on stilts above the flood line, thatched roofs gone silver with weather, drying racks hung with fish that flashed like coins in the afternoon light. Children ran down to the bank before Odile's barge even finished its lazy turn, shouting words Maren didn't know, though she caught her own name in the middle of it, or rather Isandro's — *Fairweather* — passed along the shore like a coin changing hands.

"They know him," she said.

"Everybody knows everybody out here," Odile said from the tiller. "That's the problem with the interior. No place to be a stranger."

Maren watched Isandro's face as they came in. He was doing the thing he did — going flat and pleasant, the pilot's mask, the man who could land anywhere and owe nothing anywhere. But his hands were white on the gunwale, and she'd learned to read those hands now. Something about this place had its hooks in him already.

* * *

The elder who met them at the landing was a woman so old her skin had gone the color of the clay bluffs, fine-lined as cracked porcelain, with eyes that stayed startlingly black and young. She looked at Maren for a long time without speaking, and Maren felt examined the way she'd never been examined — not skin and clothes but something further in, some ledger being consulted.

"You are Hale's girl," the old woman said at last.

The words went through Maren like a fishhook. "You knew my father?"

"I knew a man who came upriver with his hands full of wanting." The woman turned, unhurried, and started up the plank walkway. "Come. You'll eat first. Then we talk. Hungry people lie."

Her name was Sefeya, and her house was the largest on the row, open-walled, smelling of smoke and dried herbs and old paper. There were no books. Instead the walls were hung with knotted cords, hundreds of them, dyed in colors that had faded into a language of their own — rust and indigo and bone-white, each cord dense with knots like a rope that had been through a storm.

"What is all this?" Maren asked.

"Us." Sefeya settled onto a woven mat with the care of someone handling her own skeleton. "Each knot is a remembering. Who we were. What happened. What we owed and what we paid. When one of us dies, the others take up her cords and learn them. So no one is only one life long."

Isandro stood near the door the way he always stood near doors — weight forward, exit mapped. "You're telling me you've got a written language made of string."

"I'm telling you we have a memory longer than any single head can hold," Sefeya said. "Yours lasts what — seventy years, if you're lucky, and half of it you lose to drink and grief? Ours goes back four hundred. Because twice a year, at the high water and the low, we drink."

She let that sit. Outside, children were shrieking over some game involving a stick and a hoop, and the sound seemed to belong to another world.

"Drink what?" Maren asked, though she already knew. She'd known since Odile's barge, since the stories told low over cards while the river slid past dark and endless. She'd felt the word move through her chest like a swallowed stone ever since.

"The water," Sefeya said. "Diluted. A cupful in a barrel of spring water, no more. Enough to sharpen. Not enough to drown." Her black eyes moved between them. "You've heard of the Gate. Everyone has heard of the Gate. Pilots joke about it over fuel drums. Traders swear they've seen it. Nobody comes here asking unless they intend to go."

"We're following my father's map," Maren said. "His markings. He was here — you said—"

"I said I knew a man full of wanting. I didn't say he drank." Sefeya rose, slow as a tree growing, and crossed to the knotted cords. Her fingers found one almost immediately, a cord dyed deep rust-red, and she walked her fingers down its knots like a blind woman reading a face. "But he asked. He sat where you're sitting, twelve years back or thirteen, and he asked us how to find it, and we told him what we tell everyone."

"Which is?"

"Don't."

* * *

They ate that evening in the open air of the communal house, fish and cassava and a bitter greens stew that Maren ate because refusing food in the interior was refusing everything else too. Around the fire sat perhaps thirty elders, gray-haired and straight-backed, and Maren realized with a slow chill that she couldn't guess their ages. They might have been sixty. They might have been a hundred and twenty. Their faces had the strange smoothness of people whose faces had stopped mattering to them a long time ago.

After the meal, an old man rose and began to speak, and the others closed their eyes, and Maren understood she was watching the ceremony — not diluted water tonight, but the recitation, the cords taken down and read aloud. Names. Dates. A flood sixty years ago that drowned the east row of houses. A quarrel between two brothers resolved by a debt repaid across three generations. A woman named Ilset who had loved a trader and waited eleven years for him and then married the ferryman instead and lived well.

It should have been boring. It was the opposite of boring. It was like watching a river run backward — every small human thing lifted out of oblivion and set glowing on the shelf of the world, kept, kept, kept. Maren found tears running down her face without permission and wiped them angrily, and beside her Isandro sat very still, staring into the fire, and she knew he wasn't hearing the names. He was hearing other ones. Ones he'd never said aloud to her.

When the recitation ended, Sefeya came and sat beside her, close enough that Maren could smell the herbs on her skin.

"Now you ask me," the old woman said.

"How do you stand it?" Maren asked. "Remembering everything?"

Sefeya smiled, and for a moment the black eyes looked genuinely ancient, older than the village, older than the river. "We don't remember everything. We remember *enough*. That is the whole art of living, girl — knowing how much to carry. The Gate doesn't know that art. The Gate gives back everything, all of it, in order, with every wound still bleeding. You would drink and see your father's face not once but ten thousand times, from every angle, including the angles you were never meant to see. Including his side of it." She paused. "Including your side of it."

"My side of what?"

"Whatever guilt you're carrying right now, folded up small in your chest so you can walk. The Gate unfolds it. All of it. Every selfish thought you've ever had about the people you love. That's the price nobody puts on the map." Sefeya's hand, dry as bark, covered Maren's. "The water doesn't judge. It just shows you everything you actually were. Most people who drink it don't come back to villages like this. They go off alone with their perfect memories and sit inside them until they die. We chose the middle way. A sip, shared, together, so no one drowns alone in themselves."

"And my father? Did he understand that warning?"

Sefeya was quiet a long moment. "I'll show you something tomorrow," she said. "Tonight you sleep. Grief keeps better after rest. So does truth."

* * *

But Maren didn't sleep. She lay on a woven mat in Sefeya's house listening to the river breathe against the pilings, and past midnight she heard Isandro get up quietly and go out onto the plank walkway, and after a while she got up too and went to sit beside him in the dark.

The moon was down. The river was a black presence moving under them, and the village was a scatter of dying fires, and somewhere a dog was dreaming.

"I keep thinking about what she said," Maren whispered. "About seeing his side of it. If I drink, I could finally know why he left. Not guess. *Know.*"

Isandro was quiet so long she thought he wouldn't answer.

"When I was nineteen," he said finally, "there was a night I've spent twelve years trying not to remember. And I don't need magic water for it. I remember it fine. Every second." His voice was level, which is how she knew it was costing him. "Memory isn't the gift people think it is, Maren. Sometimes it's just the sentence you're serving."

"That's easy for you to say. You know what you lost. I don't even know that."

"No," he agreed softly. "You don't." And there was something in the way he said it — something that sounded almost like fear, or like a man standing at the edge of his own confession — that made her turn to look at him, but the dark had his face, and then he stood and said he'd take first watch, and the moment closed like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

In the morning Sefeya led them to a smaller house at the village's edge, half-swallowed by a fig tree, its door sealed with a crossbar thick with dust. The old woman lifted the bar with both hands.

"Tobin Hale slept here two nights," she said. "He asked his questions. We gave our answer. On the third morning he was gone, upstream, before light. But he left this. He said if a girl ever came asking after him, she should have it."

Inside, on a shelf, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age: a journal. Maren knew the handwriting before she opened it — the same cramped, furious script that covered her father's map, the letters leaning like men walking into wind.

The first page was dated thirteen years ago. The last entry was only three lines long, and Maren read it standing in the dusty room with the fig tree pressing at the window, and the words went into her like cold water:

*I saw it. I saw everything. God forgive me, I saw why I left her, and it was worse than I feared, and I walked away anyway.*

Below that, in fresher ink, added later:

*If Maren reads this — don't follow me. Some doors only open inward.*

Maren stood holding the journal and felt the ground tilt under everything she'd believed. Her father hadn't abandoned her blindly. He had *seen*. He had drunk from the Gate, seen his whole life laid bare including whatever drove him from her, and he had still walked away — and worse, he had known she might come looking, and he had tried to stop her.

Behind her, Sefeya spoke quietly. "There's one more thing, pilot. Your father — the one who flew him upriver, thirteen years ago. The villagers remember him. Tall man. Coast accent. Left his plane at Merrow's Landing and never came back for it."

Maren turned. Isandro had gone absolutely still, the color draining out of his face, and in that moment she understood that the ledger the old woman had consulted on the first day had been consulting him all along.

"That was you," she said. "Wasn't it? You brought him here."

Isandro looked at the journal in her hands, and then at her, and the pilot's mask was gone, and what was underneath looked young and terrified and twelve years tired.

"Maren," he said. "Before you read another word — there's something I have to tell you."
