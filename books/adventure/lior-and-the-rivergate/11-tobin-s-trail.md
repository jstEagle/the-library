## 11. Tobin's Trail

The markings started a mile past the village, cut into the bark of a riverside ironwood at the waterline. Isandro might have missed them entirely if Maren hadn't been the one to spot them — three shallow notches stacked like a crooked ladder, and above them a symbol he'd seen once before and never expected to see again.

He'd seen it on Tobin Hale's map. The mark for *safe water*.

"That's his," Maren said. Her fingers hovered over the notches without touching them, the way you'd approach something sleeping. "He used to carve these when I was little. On fence posts, on the porch rail. I thought it was just a habit." She laughed, but there was no humor in it. "I thought it was nothing."

"Prospectors leave trail marks their whole lives," Isandro said. "Doesn't mean he knew he'd be followed."

"He knew *I'd* follow him." She pulled her hand back. "That's what I can't figure out. Whether he wanted me to or was just too stubborn to stop doing it."

They were two days out from the village now, traveling light in the canoe Odile had traded them along with a week of supplies and a warning: *The river narrows past the old burn scar. Whatever Tobin left behind, the forest has been trying to take it back.* The elders' parting gift had been stranger — a small clay flask of water drawn from their ceremonial bowl, diluted so thin it was barely more than memory itself. Maren carried it against her chest like a talisman she hadn't decided whether to trust.

Isandro paddled. Maren watched the banks.

By midday they'd found four more markers, each one older than the last, the bark grown fat around the cuts. Some were fresh enough that sap had wept from them. Others had been swallowed nearly whole by twelve years of growing wood. Tobin had come this way more than once. That much was becoming impossible to deny.

The river narrowed as Odile promised, threading between ridges furred with second-growth pine, and in the afternoon the current picked up until Isandro had to work the canoe hard just to hold their line. Sweat ran into his eyes. He welcomed it. Paddling was simple. Paddling asked nothing of him except the next stroke, and the next, and he'd built a whole life out of tasks like that — tasks that kept his hands busy and his mind off the coast, off the sister whose name he still hadn't said aloud to anyone since the Gorge of Whispers.

Maren broke the silence first, because she always did.

"You heard her voice yesterday." It wasn't a question. She'd watched him stop dead in the middle of the portage trail, watched him sway like a man hearing his own name called across water. "In the gorge. You heard someone."

"I heard wind," Isandro said.

"You heard your sister."

The paddle dipped and pulled. The canoe held its line through a seam of fast water. He didn't answer, and she didn't push, and that was one of the things about Maren Hale that kept undoing him — she could wait out a silence like weather. She didn't need him to break open. She just needed him to keep moving in the same direction she was moving, and eventually the direction did the talking.

"I was nineteen," he said finally, when the river widened again and the current gentled. "My sister Liora. We grew up on the coast, fishing family, nothing special. When I left—" His jaw worked. "There was a night. A storm. My father wanted someone to run the boat up to the light station, and I went instead of her because it was my turn and I was the responsible one, and then halfway there I turned back because the barometer was falling and I told myself it was the smart call. Told myself she'd understand. She went alone."

The paddle strokes came slower now.

"She didn't come back. And I spent twelve years making sure I was never the responsible one again. Never the one who promised. Because promises are just the shape duty takes before it kills somebody." He glanced at Maren. "Your father made me promise to bring you this map. I've been trying not to think about what that makes me."

"It makes you a man keeping a promise," Maren said quietly. "There are worse things."

"There's a whole graveyard of worse things. That's how I know."

* * *

They found the camp on the third morning.

It was Maren who saw the smoke-blackened tin chimney pipe glinting through the alders, half a mile up a side creek where the water ran cold and clear over slate. The cabin — if you could call it that — was a low-roofed shelter of driftwood poles and sod, slumped at one corner like an old man leaning on a wall. Twelve years of winters had bent it, but hadn't broken it. Someone had built this to last, and then someone had walked away from it anyway.

Maren stood at the treeline for a long moment before she went in.

Isandro gave her that moment. He checked the perimeter instead, an old habit, though the only tracks around the site were deer and fox and the small busy signatures of marten. No one had been here in years. Whatever consortium survey crews had passed upriver, they'd missed this fold in the land, this creek, this quiet.

Inside, the cabin smelled of spruce resin and old iron. A bunk frame with rotted canvas. A woodstove gone to rust. And covering one entire wall, nailed up in careful rows, dozens of sheets of paper gone amber with age — sketches, mostly. Drawings of the river in every season. Drawings of birds. Drawings of a girl.

Maren stopped breathing.

She was younger in the drawings. Eight, maybe nine. Braids. A gap-toothed grin rendered in confident pencil strokes, over and over, from different angles — laughing, scowling, asleep with a book on her face. Tobin Hale had drawn his daughter hundreds of times, from memory, in a shack a thousand miles from her, and then he had nailed every single one to a wall where no one would ever see them.

"Oh," Maren said. Just that. One syllable that cracked down the middle.

Isandro moved toward the bunk, giving her the wall, giving her the girl with the braids. Beneath the bunk frame, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with wire, he found the journals. Four of them. Heavy-lidded with damp, their spines furred with mold, but intact. He set them on the woodstove's flat top and peeled back the oilcloth, and the first page stopped him cold.

*Tobin Hale. If found, read all of it or none of it. There is no honest way to read half.*

Maren came over slowly. Her eyes were red-rimmed but dry. She sat down on the bunk frame beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and together they began to read.

* * *

The early entries were prospector's arithmetic — assay notes, grubstake tallies, complaints about mosquitoes and the price of beans. Tobin's handwriting was cramped and impatient, a man writing only what he needed to remember. Then, three-quarters through the first journal, the handwriting changed. Loosened. Slanted downhill, as if the man holding the pen had stopped bracing himself.

*I found it.*

*Not gold. Better than gold. Worse than gold. The source. The actual source, the thing the old-timers drink toasts to and swear they've never seen. It rises out of the ground whole, a river born full-grown, and the water is so clear it hurts. I drank. God forgive me, I drank, because a man doesn't find a thing like that and not drink.*

*I saw everything.*

The entries after that were different animals entirely. Tobin wrote in bursts, sometimes pages at a stretch, sometimes a single line days apart. He wrote about his childhood in Merrow's Landing — the smell of tar on the wharf, his mother's hands, a dog named Biscuit he'd forgotten he'd ever loved. He wrote about Maren's mother, whom he'd never mentioned to Maren by name in any letter Isandro had seen, and the writing here went soft and slow, like a man walking on ice.

*Elena. I saw her again. Not a ghost. Memory. The real thing, the whole thing, every morning of her, every argument, the way she hummed when she braided the girl's hair. I had forgotten the humming. How does a man forget the humming?*

And then:

*I saw the day I left. All of it. Every reason I told myself and every reason that was true underneath. I left because I owed men money I couldn't pay and I was too proud to say so. I left because I believed she'd be better off learning to live without a gambler and a dreamer. I left because leaving was the one thing I was good at. I saw all of it at once, standing at that water, and I will carry it until I die, and I want to be clear in this record: the Gate did not show me anything I didn't already know. It just took away everywhere I could hide from it.*

Maren's hand had found Isandro's somewhere in the reading. He wasn't sure which of them had reached first. Her grip was tight enough to hurt and he didn't move it away.

The last journal was the thinnest. The entries spaced further apart, the handwriting deteriorating, the ink fading as if the man himself were running out. Near the end:

*I tried to go home twice. Got as far as the Landing both times. Stood outside my own daughter's window like a ghost at a funeral. Couldn't knock. You'd think a man who'd seen his whole life could face one door. But the Gate gives you everything including the guilt, and the guilt is the heaviest cargo there is. I know what I am now, completely, and knowing completely is not the same as being forgiven.*

*So I'm sending the map instead. Let the water do the talking. Let some pilot with steady hands carry what I couldn't.*

*If Maren reads this: I didn't leave because of you. I left because of me. There's a difference and it took a magic spring to teach it to me and I would give back every drop if it meant I'd knocked on that door.*

*The last entry was barely legible, the pen pressed so hard it had torn the paper:*

*Don't drink. Or drink. I don't know anymore. I got everything back and lost the only thing that mattered, and I can't tell you which mistake is which. Just know that whatever you find at the Gate, the finding isn't the healing. The healing is what you do after.*

Isandro closed the journal. The cabin was very quiet. Outside, the creek talked to itself over the slate, indifferent and eternal.

Maren was crying now, silently, the tears coming straight down without her face changing at all, as if she'd learned to cry from someone who'd taught her that grief should be efficient. Isandro put his arm around her shoulders and she let him, and for a while neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the wall of drawings hadn't already said better.

"He drew me," she said finally. "For twelve years. He remembered my face better than I remember his."

"He remembered everything," Isandro said. "That was the problem."

She laughed — wet, broken, real. "You sound like the elders."

"The elders are smarter than both of us."

She pulled free gently, crossed to the wall, and began taking down the drawings one by one, folding them with a care that bordered on ceremony. She saved the last one for final — the girl asleep with the book on her face — and tucked it inside her jacket, over her heart, next to the clay flask.

"We should burn the journals," she said. "Or take them. I haven't decided."

"Take them," Isandro said. "Burning's permanent. You might want to reread him someday."

She nodded. Then she went still, head cocked, listening — and Isandro heard it a half-second later, floating up the creek from the main channel. An engine. Not Odile's barge; he knew the diesel stutter of her tug by now. This was smoother. Tighter. The kind of engine consortium money bought.

He was moving before the thought finished forming, snuffing the stove's cold ashes out of caution even though there was nothing to snuff, gathering the journals, checking the canoe. Through the alders he could see the main channel, and on it, rounding the bend a quarter-mile downstream, a jet boat running dark and fast, two figures silhouetted in it, sweeping the banks with binoculars.

Renner's net, closing. They'd missed the checkpoints. Now they were being swept for.

"Maren." His voice was low and even. "We're about to have company. Canoe, now, quietly."

She stuffed the last drawing into her jacket and came. At the treeline she paused, looking back once at the sagging cabin, at the wall now bare of its hundred faces of her.

"Dad," she said softly, to the empty doorway. "Message received."

Then they slipped into the current, silent as leaves, and paddled north — toward the fork in the river they both knew was coming, and whatever was waiting for them there.
