## 12. Ambush at the Fork

The river fork announced itself an hour before we reached it — a change in the water's voice, the current splitting its sentence into two dialects. Odile stood at the wheel of the *Marguerite* with her boots planted wide and her chin lifted like she was smelling the air for rain.

"Left channel goes to Serrado," she said. "Deep water, wide banks, easy going for another hundred miles before it shallows out. Right channel climbs into the high country. Narrower. Meaner. Nobody runs it this late in the season."

"And which one does the map want?" Maren asked.

I had Tobin Hale's map spread on the barge deck, weighted at the corners with four coffee mugs because the wind upriver had opinions about paper. The old man's ink lines were shaky but sure of themselves, drawn by someone who'd walked every mile of them twice.

"Right," I said. "The right channel."

Odile spat over the rail, ceremonial as communion. "Then Renner knows it too."

That was the thing I'd been chewing on since dawn. A man like Corvus Renner didn't spend money on checkpoints and bribed guides without knowing where the road ended. He'd bought half the pilots on the lower river. He'd bought the fuel contracts out from under me with a phone call. He would have bought Tobin Hale's map twice over if the old man hadn't died first, and the only reason he hadn't already closed the net was that a river is a long animal and hard to strangle in one place.

But a fork — a fork you could put your hand around.

"How far to the fork?" I asked.

"Six miles." Odile turned the wheel a degree, unhurried. "And there's a bend about two miles out where the trees come down close to the water. If I were a man paid to stop a barge, that's where I'd stand."

Maren looked at me. In the three days since Odile had fished us off the gravel bar where the Kestrel sat dying, I'd learned to read her face the way I read weather — fast, and mostly right. What I saw there wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. She was already counting what this was going to cost.

"We could go left," she said quietly. "Pretend we're bound for Serrado. Let them take us at their leisure and slip away downstream tonight."

"The map says right."

"The map is sixty years old and drawn by a man who was losing his mind when he drew it."

"It's also the only map," I said, "and the dry season doesn't care what we pretend. Left channel drops two feet a week from here on. By the time we doubled back, there wouldn't be enough water under us to float a coffin."

Maren held my eyes a moment longer, then nodded once, the way people nod when they've lost an argument they expected to lose. She went below to check the supplies, and I heard her moving through the hold with that deliberate steadiness she used when her hands wanted to shake.

Odile watched her go, then looked at me.

"You love her yet?"

"That's a hell of a question to ask a man six miles from an ambush."

"I'm an old woman on a slow boat. All my questions are asked too early or too late." She adjusted the wheel a hair. "You didn't answer."

I folded the map along Tobin's creases. "Ask me after the fork."

* * *

The bend came exactly where Odile said it would, and the ambush came exactly where any sensible man would have put it.

We heard the outboard first — a thin, hungry whine upstream, wrong for these waters, too clean for a fisherman. Then a rifle shot cracked off the bluff above the bend, not aimed at anything, just a statement of fact: *we are here, and you are not getting past.*

"Hold!" Odile roared, and threw the *Marguerite*'s engine into reverse so hard the whole barge shuddered and groaned like a waking animal. The current took us sideways. Somewhere in the hold, crates shifted and broke loose.

Three men stepped out of the treeline on the near bank. Two carried rifles, slung casual across their chests the way hired men carry weapons they hope not to use. The third carried nothing at all. He wore a canvas hat and a smile that didn't reach his eyes, and he cupped his hands and shouted across the water.

"Brant! You're hauling cargo that isn't yours!"

"You're standing on a bank that isn't yours!" Odile shouted back, and I swear there was pleasure in it.

The negotiator laughed. "Renner pays double whatever Fairweather promised you. Triple. He just wants the girl's papers and the pilot's cooperation. Nobody has to bleed today."

"He always opens with that?" I muttered.

"Every time," Odile said. "It's how you know he means the opposite."

Maren came up from the hold carrying the map case strapped across her chest like a bandolier. Her face was pale and set. "What do we do?"

I looked at the geometry of it. The bend, the bluff, the three men on the near bank. And upstream past the fork, maybe a quarter mile, the right channel threading away between walls of green so dense you couldn't have walked through them with a machete and a week. There was a canoe lashed to the *Marguerite*'s stern — Odile's spare, a scarred aluminum thing older than both of us.

There was no way the barge made the fork. Not with men on the bluff, not with the channel narrowing ahead. Odile knew it. I could see her knowing it, watching the water with the flat calm of a woman doing sums she'd done all her life.

"Odile," I started.

"Don't." She held up one finger. "Don't apologize, don't argue, and don't waste my time being noble. Listen instead." She pointed with her chin at the canoe. "When I give the word, you three get in that boat and paddle for the right channel like your lives are rented. They'll shoot at the barge, because the barge is big and slow and worth shooting. Canoes are small and quick and annoying. By the time they figure out where you went, you'll be around the first bend."

"And you?" Maren asked.

Odile smiled, and for a moment she looked twenty years younger and twenty years meaner. "I'm going to run the *Marguerite* aground on the left channel in the most expensive, spectacular, inconvenient way I can manage. Cargo everywhere. Barge blocking the channel. Those boys will spend the rest of the day salvaging Renner's property instead of chasing yours." She shrugged. "Insurance will cover the hull. Nothing covers the look on their faces."

"They'll know it was deliberate."

"Of course they'll know. That's the point. Renner needs to understand that some debts can't be bought." She looked at me then, straight on, and her voice dropped into something quieter and harder. "My husband ran this river thirty years. Renner's consortium priced him out, bought his moorage, bought his contracts, and he drank himself dead on cheap rum watching their barges pass his window. So no, boy, I'm not sacrificing anything. I'm spending it. There's a difference, and the difference is that I choose where it goes."

The negotiator on the bank shouted again, patience fraying now. "Last offer, Brant! Then we take what we came for!"

Odile raised her hand in a friendly wave — and dropped it like a blade falling.

"Now!"

* * *

We hit the water running.

I went over the stern rail first, dragging the canoe's painter, and Maren followed with the supply pack clutched to her chest, and Odile shoved us off with one boot against the gunwale with a strength that belonged to a much larger woman. The current grabbed the little aluminum shell and swung it downstream, toward the men on the bank, and for one horrible second we were broadside to the rifles, naked on the brown water.

"Right channel! Paddle, damn you!" Odile screamed, and hauled the *Marguerite*'s wheel hard over, driving the barge toward the left channel at full throttle.

The first shot hit the barge's cabin. The second hit the water a yard short of our stern, throwing up a white feather of spray. I dug the paddle in and pulled until my shoulders burned, and Maren matched me stroke for stroke on the other side, no panic in her at all, just fury converted directly into motion. The canoe leapt forward. The current, sensing our desperation, decided to help.

Behind us the *Marguerite* thundered past the fork and drove herself onto the left channel's gravel shoal with a grinding, tearing crash that I felt in my teeth. Crates burst. Fuel drums bounded across the deck and splashed into the shallows, bobbing like fat yellow buoys. Odile stood at the wheel through all of it, hatless now, hair whipping, steering her dying ship onto the obstruction with the precision of a surgeon closing a wound.

On the bank, the hired men hesitated — and hesitation was all we needed. We shot the gap at the fork, and the right channel swallowed us.

The trees closed overhead. The noise of the ambush fell away behind us, replaced by water-sound and bird-alarm and the ragged bellows of my own breathing. Around the first bend, then the second, the channel tightening until the branches scraped the canoe's gunwales on both sides.

Only then did I let myself stop paddling, and only then did I feel it.

A hot line across my left arm, just above the elbow, wet through my sleeve.

"When did—" Maren was already reaching for me, turning in her seat, her hands quick and certain. She peeled the sleeve back. The graze was maybe four inches long, shallow, ugly, bleeding freely but not spurting. A bullet that had kissed me instead of biting. Another inch and it would have opened the artery, and we'd have been watching my life leak into the right channel of an unnamed river.

"One of theirs," I said stupidly. "Obviously."

"You didn't say anything." Her voice cracked on it. "You were paddling with a hole in your arm and you didn't say anything."

"Adrenaline's a liar. It tells you everything's fine." I watched her work — she tore a strip from the hem of her shirt, poured water from the canteen over the wound, tied it off with a knot that would have done credit to a field medic. Her fingers were steady now. Whatever shaking lived in her had gone somewhere deep, to be dealt with later, alone, the way she handled everything.

"There," she said. "You'll live. Unfortunately for you."

"Unfortunately?"

"So I don't have to feel grateful." But her hand stayed on my arm a moment longer than the bandage required, and neither of us mentioned it, and the river carried us on.

* * *

We ran until the light went bronze and the channel widened just enough to let us breathe. Then we pulled into a backwater eddy beneath a leaning fig tree and let the canoe drift in circles while the day finished dying.

From somewhere downstream, faint as a memory, we heard it: a single detonation, flat and final, rolling up the valley. Then another. Then silence.

Maren went rigid. "Odile—"

"No." I caught her wrist. "Listen. That's not gunfire. Gunfire comes in strings. That's demolition charges. Scuttling charges." I tried to make my voice believe what I was saying. "She told us. Most expensive, most inconvenient. She's sinking the *Marguerite* square in the mouth of the left channel, and every one of Renner's boats is going to sit there for a week while divers clear the wreck. That's not a death rattle. That's a door slamming."

Maren stared downstream, listening to the quiet where the explosions had been. "She gave us her boat."

"She gave Renner her grudge." I found I was smiling, and it hurt my face. "Best trade anyone ever made on this river."

We ate cold beans and hard bread in the bottom of the canoe, and I re-tied my bandage, and the mosquitoes rose off the water in the blue dusk like smoke given appetite. Maren sat close enough that her shoulder touched mine, and neither of us moved away from the contact, and the dark came down gentle as a hand.

"She'll get out," Maren said finally. "Downstream, on foot, whatever. She's too mean to die here."

"Too mean," I agreed. "It's the great survival trait. Talent fails, courage fails, meanness endures."

She laughed — a real laugh, brief and startled out of her, the first one I'd heard since Merrow's Landing. It did something to the air between us that I wasn't ready to name.

Then the laugh died, because the light on the water downstream changed.

A flashlight beam swept the channel below us — once, twice, methodical, patient. Men working the banks. Working *upstream*. They'd cut loose from the salvage operation and taken to the water, and they were coming up the right channel after us, and the channel was narrow, and the night was moonless, and there was nowhere left to run except the direction the map pointed.

Toward the high country. Toward the source. Toward whatever Tobin Hale had seen that had been worth dying with a map in his fist.

Maren's hand found mine in the dark. Her grip was cold and absolutely steady.

"Paddle," she whispered.
