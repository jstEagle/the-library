## 13. The Gorge of Whispers

The river gave out on us at dawn.

Not all at once — it thinned the way a voice does when someone walks away from you mid-sentence. One bend it was wide enough to carry Odile's old stories, and the next it was a brown ribbon threading through rock, and then it was gone under stone entirely, swallowed by a canyon that hadn't been on Tobin's map because Tobin had drawn his map for a man with wings, not boots.

"The Gorge of Whispers," Maren said, reading from her father's journal, her finger on a line of cramped handwriting. "He wrote: *Don't listen. It lies in your own voice.*"

"Comforting."

"He underlined it twice." She looked up at me across the fire's last coals. "What kind of man underlines something like that?"

"A man who listened anyway."

We stood at the lip of the thing and it went down and down, a slot of shadow cut two hundred feet into the plateau, narrow enough in places that I could have spat across it. The river ran somewhere at the bottom — we could hear it, a low mutter behind the walls — but there was no trail along the water. There was only a goat-track of a path along the rim, which meant carrying everything: the canoe, the packs, the salvaged supplies, the last of our clean water.

Three days, Odile had guessed, if the weather held. She'd said it the way she said most things, watching the sky instead of us.

I divided the load the way I divide everything — by weight, by necessity, by what could be abandoned without killing us. The canoe went first, upside down across my shoulders, its gunwales resting on a padded yoke I'd carved from a deadfall branch back at the wreck. Forty pounds of cedar and fiberglass that felt like eighty before noon. Maren took the packs in shifts, doubling back for them while I moved the boat ahead in stages, leapfrogging down the rim trail.

It was brutal work and I was grateful for it. Tired is a country I know how to live in. Tired doesn't ask questions.

The gorge started talking to us around midday of the first day.

At first I thought it was other travelers — voices carrying down the rim, distorted by the stone. Then I remembered there were no other travelers. There hadn't been since the fork, since Odile put her barge between us and Renner's men and told us to run and not look back, and I still hadn't decided whether the burning hulk we'd seen downstream was her cleverness or her funeral.

The wind came up the canyon from the south, and where it found the hollows in the rock it made sounds. That was all. Air through stone, shaped by chance into something almost like language. Almost like words.

Almost was the problem.

"Isandro." Maren had stopped twenty yards ahead, her head cocked. "Do you hear—"

"Wind," I said. "Keep walking."

But I heard it too. And underneath the wind, faint as breath on glass, I heard my name.

* * *

That night we camped in a fold of the rim where the rock curved around us like a cupped hand. I built a small fire — small, because smoke travels and so do men who follow smoke — and Maren laid out the last of the dried fish and the bitter tea Odile had traded us at the fork, and neither of us talked much, because the gorge was talking enough for both of us.

It was better after dark, somehow. Or worse. The wind dropped and the voices dropped with it, and the silence that replaced them was honest, at least. Honest silence I can live with.

Maren sat close, shoulder against my arm, studying her father's journal by firelight. She'd grown quieter over the past days since the camp, since the journals, since the page where Tobin Hale wrote about drinking from the Gate and seeing his whole life spread out like a surveyor's chart and walking away from his daughter anyway because he couldn't stand to be seen by her until he'd earned it. She carried that page now the way she carried everything — close, quiet, converted directly into forward motion.

"Tell me about her," she said.

I didn't pretend not to know who she meant. She'd earned the question days ago, on the Serrated Reach, when I'd pulled her half-drowned out of white water and shaken apart afterward and given her the shape of it without the details: *I lost someone once because I chose duty over love.*

"Lena," I said. The name came out rusty. Twelve years of not saying it will do that. "My sister. Younger by four years. She used to ride shotgun with me before she could legally sit in the front seat of anything, legs too short to reach the floor, feet swinging. She'd navigate off road atlases like they were treasure maps. Called every lake we flew over 'hers.'"

The fire popped. Down in the gorge, the river muttered.

"What happened?"

"I got an offer. Coast outfit, cargo contract, real money — the kind of money that buys out of a life instead of into one. Lena wanted me to stay. Not dramatically. She just asked, the way family asks, like the answer is already yes." I turned my cup in my hands. "And I said the coast run was temporary. Eighteen months, maybe two years. I said it like I believed it."

"And then?"

"And then she got sick, and I was three thousand miles away being temporary, and by the time I flew home—" I stopped. Some sentences don't have landings. "By the time I flew home there wasn't anyone to fly for."

Maren didn't say *I'm sorry*. I loved her a little for that. She just leaned harder into my shoulder and let the silence be what it was, and after a while she said, "Tobin walked away out of shame. You flew away out of grief. Seems like everyone in this story runs from the same direction."

"Everyone in this story drinks from the same river eventually," I said, and didn't sleep much after that.

* * *

The second day, the gorge stopped pretending to be wind.

It happened gradually, the way a fever builds. In the morning the voices were anonymous — a woman laughing somewhere below the rim, a child counting, a man calling coordinates in a cadence every bush pilot knows in his spine. By noon they had names. Not names spoken aloud; names *implied*, the way a familiar footstep in a hallway implies its owner before you see them.

By afternoon, the gorge knew things about me that no one on this river knew.

*Isandro.* My mother's voice, saying it the way she said it when I'd tracked mud into the house. *Isandro Fairweather, you wash those boots before you come in here.*

She'd been dead nine years. I'd scattered part of her ashes off the Kestrel's door over the delta she grew up on, and I remembered thinking, even then, even grieving, that the wind took her voice the same way it took everything else — evenly, without malice, without favor.

Now the wind was giving it back.

I set the canoe down on the trail and stood very still, listening to a dead woman scold me through two hundred feet of sandstone, and I understood, finally, why Tobin had underlined his warning twice. Because the voices weren't cruel. That was the trap. They weren't taunting. They were *warm*. They were every voice you'd ever ached to hear again, pitched exactly right, saying exactly the things you'd spent years pretending you didn't need.

"Maren." My own voice sounded wrong to me. Thin. "How far to the next landmark?"

She was ten yards ahead with the second pack. She turned, and whatever she saw in my face made her set the pack down slowly. "The narrows. Why?"

"Because I'm going to walk down there."

The words came out of me calm and reasonable, and that frightened me more than shouting would have. Because they were calm. Because some part of me had already done the math — the descent was maybe an hour, the river at the bottom was audible, the water was right there, and all I had to do was climb down and kneel and drink and have Lena's laugh in my ears again, my mother's kitchen, the coast runway at dusk, the whole drowned country of myself raised up like a sunken town in a drought year.

"It's a trick," Maren said.

"It's a gorge."

"You know what I mean." She came toward me, unhurried, the way you approach a spooked animal. "Isandro. Look at the trail. Look at where your feet are pointing."

I looked. My feet were pointing downhill. Toward the sound of water. Toward the sound of her.

"If you drink," she said quietly, "you remember everything. That's what the elders said. Everything — including this. Including me. Including the choice you're making right now, standing here with your feet pointed the wrong way. You'll remember choosing the water over the living. Do you want that on the list? Because the Gate doesn't edit. Your father proved that. He saw all of it, the good and the shameful, and it still took him eleven years to find his way back to me."

"That's different."

"How?"

"Tobin left because of what he'd done. I'd be going back for what I lost."

"No." She reached me, took my face in both hands, forced my eyes down from the rim and onto hers. Her eyes were Tobin's eyes, I realized — the same storm-gray, the same stubborn weather in them. "You'd be going back because remembering feels like having her again. But it isn't. It's a recording. It's Renner's vault full of bought memories. It's a barge of other people's yesterdays. Lena isn't down there, Isandro. Lena is wherever dead people go, and she is not in the water, and the thing in the water that sounds like her is the oldest lie this river tells."

The wind gusted up the canyon wall and the voices rose with it, and for one terrible clear moment I heard Lena herself — not my mother, *Lena* — young, laughing, calling across a lake she'd claimed as her own: *Come on, Sandro, the water's fine!*

The exact words. The exact pitch. The summer before the offer, before the coast, before everything.

My knees actually bent. I felt the gorge take my weight like it had been waiting for it.

Then Maren did the only thing that could have worked. She didn't pull me. She stepped in front of me, into the space between me and the rim, and said, in a voice that shook and held anyway:

"Stay. Stay here. With me. I'm not asking you to forget her. I'm asking you to stay."

* * *

I don't know how long we stood like that. A minute. Ten. The wind died the way wind does, all at once, and the voices fell with it, and the gorge was just a gorge again — hot stone, cold shadow, the honest mutter of the river far below.

When my legs worked, I picked up the canoe.

We didn't talk about it. We walked. The trail climbed away from the rim in switchbacks, and each switchback put another wall of rock between me and the water, and with each one the pressure eased, the way a headache eases, reluctantly, in stages. By evening I could carry the boat a full mile without setting it down. By dark I could think about Lena's laugh without my chest caving, which I counted as a victory, though it felt like a theft — like I'd traded a piece of her for passage through the stone.

We camped at the narrows, where the trail pinched between two leaning slabs, and Maren built the fire while I checked the canoe for cracks, and when she handed me the tea she said, "You would have gone, wouldn't you. If I hadn't been here."

"Yes."

"And if I hadn't been here at all — if you'd been alone on this trail—"

"Then I'd be kneeling at the bottom of that gorge right now," I said. "Drinking. Remembering. Walking out the other side a complete man with nothing left to want." I looked at her across the flames. "Which sounds like a bargain until you say it out loud."

She smiled, tired and crooked. "Tobin's journal says the Gate shows you your whole life. Every version of it." She turned a page. "He wrote one line I keep coming back to. Last entry before the camp. Want to hear it?"

"Say it."

"*The water gives you everything you were. It cannot give you anything you might still be.*"

I sat with that a long time, watching the fire eat down to coals, listening to the gorge breathe around us — just wind now, just stone, just air finding its way through the world honestly or not at all.

"Maren," I said finally. "When this is over. When the Gate is dealt with, one way or the other. When we're downstream and this is all story—"

"Don't." But she was smiling when she said it. "No promises of permanence. Just presence. Isn't that what you said at the fork?"

"Something like that."

"Then be present," she said, and lay down with her back against my side, and slept, trusting me with her unconscious weight the way she'd trusted me in the rapids, and I sat up keeping watch over the narrows and let myself want, for the first time in twelve years, a future instead of a past.

* * *

We came out of the gorge on the third morning, into light so sudden and open it hurt — the plateau spreading north in waves of dry grass, the river re-emerging from the stone ahead of us, young and shallow and running fast toward the high country. The source couldn't be more than two days' march now. Tobin's markings climbed the valley walls in old blazes, faded orange scars on the bark, leading us like a hand on the elbow.

I should have felt triumphant. Instead, standing at the mouth of the gorge with the canoe on my shoulders, I felt watched.

"Maren," I said. "The birds."

She followed my gaze. High over the plateau, a line of vultures circled — normal, ordinary, except they weren't circling carrion. They were holding station. Drifting in a slow, deliberate pattern above the river bend a mile downstream, the way birds hold station over something that is moving.

Over something that is following.

I set the canoe down and glassed the bend with Odile's battered binoculars, and the heat shimmer made everything swim, but not enough to hide the truth: six figures on the far bank, moving fast, and at their head a tall man in a dust-colored coat who didn't walk like hired muscle.

He walked like a man who owned the ground he stood on.

Renner had found us. And between him and the Gate, there was nothing left of the river to hide behind — only two days of open plateau, and whatever was waiting at the water that remembers everything.
