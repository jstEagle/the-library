## 14. Renner Catches Up

They smelled the smoke before they saw the fire.

Isandro had been reading the river all morning — the way it shallowed on the outside bends, the way the birds went quiet in stretches that felt wrong — and when the smell of woodsmoke drifted down through the gorge mist, he did what he always did: he stopped paddling and listened.

"Camp," Maren said.

"Too clean for a camp." He let the canoe drift against the current, holding station with short strokes. "Somebody's burning green wood. That's a signal."

The river bent left around a shelf of black stone, and beyond the bend the gorge opened into a wide pool where three tributaries had once braided together before the dry season strangled two of them to trickles. On the gravel spit at the center stood a canvas tent, a radio mast, and four men standing very still beside a fire that wasn't for cooking.

And leaning against the tent pole, arms crossed, watching them come like a man watching weather he'd ordered himself — Corvus Renner.

Isandro's hands went cold on the paddle. He'd imagined this meeting a dozen times since Merrow's Landing, usually at night, usually ending with him doing something brave and stupid. Now it was here, in daylight, and all he could think about was the distance across the water. Forty meters of open pool. No cover. One rifle among the hired men, maybe two.

"Don't beach," Maren murmured.

"We can't outrun them on the water. Current's against us past the fork."

"I know. I'm saying don't *beach* like we're coming to them."

He understood. He swung the canoe in a slow arc and brought it alongside the gravel spit sideways, so they stepped out facing the men rather than turning their backs, and even that small dignity felt like something worth having.

Renner hadn't changed since Merrow's Landing — same gray suit gone soft at the seams from weeks in the bush, same careful face, the face of a man who'd learned early that expressions were currency and spent them sparingly. But up close, out here, away from consortium offices and fuel contracts, Isandro noticed what he'd missed before: the way Renner's eyes moved. Not scanning threats. Scanning *them*. Like a man reading a document he already owned most of.

"Mr. Fairweather." Renner nodded. "Miss Hale. You've made better time than I projected. The Serrated Reach usually costs people more than it cost you."

"It cost enough," Maren said.

"Yes." Something flickered behind his eyes — not sympathy, exactly. Recognition. "I read the river reports. A canoe swamped at the third gate. Two travelers, one recovered by a trader named Brant." He paused. "The other recovered by you, Mr. Fairweather. At some personal risk, I understand."

"You have good sources."

"I have expensive ones. There's a difference, but it doesn't matter today." Renner gestured toward the fire, and one of the men set a folding stool near it — an absurd touch of civilization, canvas and aluminum in the middle of nowhere. "Sit. Both of you. I didn't bring you here to threaten you. I could have done that at any point in the last hundred miles. I brought you here because I've run out of reasons to pretend we want different things."

Isandro didn't sit. Neither did Maren. Renner accepted this without offense and remained standing himself, which Isandro noted was its own kind of calculation.

"The map," Renner said. "Tobin Hale's map. I've wanted it for eleven years. I've wanted what it leads to for longer than that. And now I find myself standing in a gorge talking to the two people carrying it, and I'm going to make you an offer that isn't money."

* * *

Maren had rehearsed hatred the way other people rehearsed speeches.

Since Merrow's Landing she'd carried her father's map like a coal in her chest — fury at Tobin for leaving, fury at herself for wanting so badly to understand why. She'd built a whole architecture of anger, load-bearing walls and all, and she'd assumed Renner would be easy to hate too. The suit. The consortium. The men with rifles who burned signal fires in gorges.

What unsettled her was that he looked tired. Not weak — she'd seen weak men in the Landing, men the river had finished with — but tired in a way that went down deep, like a well drawn from too many years.

"My father sold you something," she said. It wasn't a question. Odile had told them stories around the barge stove, and one of them had been about a young prospector who came downriver thirty years ago with empty eyes and a consortium contract, and how the old traders still argued about whether he'd been robbed or paid.

Renner's mouth moved — not quite a smile. "Odile Brant talks too much. Yes. Your father sold me memories. Not his own — mine. Or rather, he brokered the sale. There was a window, decades ago, when the Gate's water could be carried. Bottled, diluted, drunk downstream. It degraded within days, but a day was long enough. The consortium ran eleven expeditions before the source shifted underground and the trade died." He said it flatly, like a man reciting freight manifests. "I drank twice. Once as payment, once because after the first time I couldn't stop thinking about it."

"What did you buy?" Isandro asked quietly.

"My childhood." For a moment the careful face slipped, and underneath it Maren saw something raw and old. "My mother's kitchen. The sound the door made. My brother's name — I'd lost my brother's name, Mr. Fairweather. Do you understand what that's like? To know you had a brother and not be able to hold his face? I paid a season of claims for forty minutes of a kitchen I'd forgotten existed." He straightened. "So no, I'm not here to threaten you. I'm here because I'm the only man on this river who knows exactly what the Gate gives, because I've tasted it, and I know what it's worth, and I know what it costs."

"And the cost," Maren said, "is why there are villages along this river that drink it ceremonially and guard it like a wound."

"The cost is manageable. That's the consortium's entire argument, Miss Hale, and it happens to be true. Controlled access. Dilution protocols. Elders vetted, records kept. What we cannot manage is a free-for-all — every prospector, every pilot, every grieving soul on the continent walking upstream to drink themselves inside out. You've seen what uncontrolled memory does. Your father—"

"Don't." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "You don't get to say his name like you knew him."

"I knew him better than you did." It wasn't cruel. That was the terrible thing — it was delivered with the gentleness of a doctor setting a bone. "That's what I came to offer you. Not money. Not passage. Truth."

The gorge had gone quiet around them. Even the wind through the stone seemed to lean in.

"Tobin Hale drank from the Gate," Renner said. "Twenty-two years ago. Before he walked away from you, before he disappeared into the interior, before he became a rumor with a gold pan. He drank, and he remembered everything — including you, including the day you were born, including every reason he left. And then, because the consortium had buyers for verified Gate-memory in those years, he *sold it*. All of it. Every hour of his life, bottled and logged. I bought the lot eleven years ago when the estate of a dead surveyor came up for auction. I have been carrying your father's entire remembered life in a steel case ever since, waiting for the source to reopen so I could verify it." He looked at her, and his voice dropped. "It's reopening, Miss Hale. I can feel it in the water table. In three days, maybe four, the spring will run clear again. And when it does, I will give you his memories. Freely. Every one. You'll know why he left. You'll hear his voice telling you, in his own head, in his own words. All I ask in return is safe passage for my expedition to the basin — no sabotage, no warnings to the villages, no heroics at the water's edge. You walk in, you drink or don't drink, I do what I came to do, and everyone leaves whole."

* * *

Isandro heard the offer land in Maren like a depth charge.

He watched it happen — the way her breath caught and held, the way her hand found the strap of her pack and gripped it, the way she went very still, which he'd learned was what she did instead of shaking. Twenty-two years of an open question, and this man in a ruined gray suit was holding the answer in a steel case ten feet away.

"Maren." He said her name low, just for her.

"Don't." She didn't look at him. "Don't tell me what to think. Not you. Not anyone."

"I'm not going to."

"Everyone's going to." Her jaw tightened. "Odile warned me. The elders warned me. Everyone has an opinion about what remembering costs, and none of them had a father who vanished."

"That's fair." He kept his voice level, though his pulse was hammering. Because he understood — God help him, he understood better than anyone on this river. He had a sister in the back of his skull whose voice the gorge had thrown at him two nights ago, and if Renner had offered him twelve lost years in a steel case, Isandro honestly didn't know what he'd have said. "It's your choice. I mean that. Whatever you decide, I'll fly it, paddle it, or burn it. But I want one thing on the record first."

Renner raised an eyebrow. "Go on."

"He's lying to you somewhere in there, Maren. Maybe everywhere. Men who brokered memory sales don't hand over inventory out of kindness. There's a catch in this offer, and we haven't found it yet."

"There's no catch," Renner said. "There's a condition, and I've stated it plainly. Safe passage. That's all."

"No one states things plainly," Isandro said. "Not you. You closed the checkpoints on the lower river. You put a bounty on our heads. You burned Odile's barge—" he saw the flicker in the hired men's faces and knew he'd guessed right, and hated being right, "—and now you're standing in a gorge offering gifts. Either you need us for something you haven't said, or the gift costs more than you're pricing it. Probably both."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind Renner's eyes that might have been respect, or might have been the exhaustion of a man tired of being seen through. Then it was gone, filed away, and the careful face returned.

"Mr. Fairweather. May I tell you what I see, since you're so fond of plain speech? I see a pilot who fled the coast twelve years ago and hasn't told this woman why. I see a man kneeling at the edge of his own missing life two nights ago in a gorge full of voices, close enough to touch it." Isandro's blood went cold. "Yes. My men heard you. Sound carries in the Gorge of Whispers — that's rather the point of it. So don't lecture me about hidden costs, Mr. Fairweather. You know precisely what this water is worth, because you've been starving for it since before Miss Hale ever met you." He turned back to Maren, and his voice softened into something almost kind. "I'm not asking you to betray him. I'm asking you to choose yourself. For once in your life, choose the girl who waited at a landing strip for a father who never came. She's owed this. And unlike everyone else on this river, I can actually pay the debt."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted a long time.

One of the hired men shifted his weight. The fire popped. Somewhere above the gorge a bird called twice and thought better of it.

Maren looked at the steel case. It sat on a crate near the tent, dented, ordinary, the size of a bread box. Her father's whole life in there. His voice. His reasons. The answer to the question that had carved her hollow since she was nine years old and watched a floatplane dwindle into the fog without him on it.

She thought about the journals at Tobin's camp — the shaky handwriting, the pages where he'd written *I saw her face, I saw all of it, I couldn't stay* — and how she'd begun, just barely, to forgive a ghost. Would the memories finish that forgiveness or undo it? She didn't know. Nobody knew. That was the whole horror of the Gate: it gave back everything, including the parts that would break you, and it didn't care which broke first.

She thought about Isandro, standing beside her with river water still dried white on his boots, who had pulled her out of the Serrated Reach and then told her about a promise he'd broken and a person he'd lost, and who had never once, not for a second, tried to steer her choice. Who was probably dying inside right now, and holding the door open anyway.

That was the difference. That was the whole difference, laid out on a gravel bar between a steel case and a man.

"If I say yes," Maren said slowly, "you give me the case *now*. Not at the basin. Here. Before we move another step."

Renner's pause lasted half a second too long.

"Because you need us at the basin," she continued, and her voice steadied as she found the shape of it. "Don't you. The spring shifts underground — you said it yourself, the source moved once before. You don't actually know the way in the last miles. Tobin's markings do. My father's trail does. This was never a gift, Mr. Renner. It's a hiring fee. You're paying me in my own father to guide you to the thing you want."

"Miss Hale—"

"And if we reach the basin and you get what you want," she said, "what happens to the case then? What stops your men from taking it back? What stops you from deciding a witness who's heard everything I've heard is a liability?" She shook her head. "No. If you want guides, hire guides. Pay us in fuel, in supplies, in leaving the villages alone. But you don't get to sell a daughter her father's ghost and call it generosity."

Renner regarded her for a long moment. When he spoke again, the warmth was gone, and what remained was worse — efficiency.

"Then we arrive at the same place by a shorter road." He snapped his fingers, and the men began striking the tent with practiced speed. "You're going to the basin regardless. I'm going to the basin regardless. The only question is whether we walk the last miles together or whether my men walk behind you. I'd prefer together. The river's dying fast, the dry season won't wait, and I've lost enough people to it." He picked up the steel case himself and held it a moment, thumb resting on the latch, looking at Maren with an expression she couldn't fully read — part threat, part something older and sadder.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your father's first entry is dated the day you were born. He starts with your name. Whatever else is in there — whatever made him leave — it begins with love. I've read the manifest. I never listened to the contents." He turned toward the trail leading up from the pool, toward the high country and the dying tributaries and the source beneath them. "We move in twenty minutes. Mr. Fairweather — you're the best navigator on this river, and we both know it. Walk in front."

Isandro looked at Maren. She was pale, and her hands were trembling now that it was over, and she met his eyes and gave the smallest shake of her head — *not yet, don't talk yet* — and he nodded, and shouldered his pack.

Ahead of them, the tributary climbed into country no map had ever bothered with, and behind them, four armed men fell into step, and between those two pressures they started walking — toward the last water, with the man who'd bought a dead friend's soul carrying it in a steel case at their backs.
