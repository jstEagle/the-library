## 15. What Maren Chose

They gave her a tent of her own.

That was the thing Maren noticed first, in the gray hour after Renner's men pitched camp on the bench above the river fork — not the rifles racked against the pack frames, not the way Isandro's jaw went tight when they took his map case, but the tent. Green canvas, new, pitched apart from the others. A courtesy. A cage with the door left politely open.

"You can think," Renner had said, before he walked away into the dark to check his men's watch rotation. "That's all I'm asking. Nobody's rushing you."

Nobody's rushing you. As if her father's whole life — his whole *self*, every year he'd failed to send a letter, every birthday he'd missed, every silence she'd built her anger around like load-bearing walls — could be weighed overnight and signed for by morning.

Maren sat on her sleeping bag with the oilcloth packet in her lap and did not open it.

Outside, the river spoke in its low constant voice. Somewhere down the bank, one of Renner's men coughed in his sleep. The fire had burned to a red eye, and beyond it she could see Isandro sitting up against a log, arms crossed, watching her tent. Not hovering. Just there. He'd been there since Renner made the offer, since the words *your father's memories* landed in the space between them like a dropped stone, and he hadn't said a single word about what she should do.

She loved him for that. She also wanted to throw something at him for it.

The letter was inside the packet. Tobin's final letter — the one Odile Brant had pressed into her hands at the barge landing three weeks and a lifetime ago, saying only, *He asked me to hold this until you were close. Closer than Merrow's Landing. He knew you'd come.* Maren had carried it through the Serrated Reach, through the village that forgot, through Tobin's ruined camp with its journals full of shame, and she had not read it. She'd told herself it was because there hadn't been time. There had been time. There had been whole evenings of time, firelit and quiet, Isandro mending net or sharpening the machete, and every evening the letter had sat in her jacket like a coal she couldn't bring herself to touch.

Because once she read it, that would be all there was. The last thing her father ever said to her, sealed and finished, and after it there would be nothing new from Tobin Hale ever again.

Except now there was. That was Renner's poison gift: there *was* more. A whole man's worth more. Every memory Tobin Hale had carried to the Gate and traded away — sold, Renner said, decades ago, to a consortium clerk who paid in scrip and never understood what he was buying. Renner held them now. Renner was offering them like a merchant offering fabric: here, feel the quality of it. Your father at twenty. Your father holding you as an infant. Your father the day he walked out of Merrow's Landing and why, in his own voice, in his own remembering, unedited, true.

Not a letter. Not a guess. Him.

Maren unzipped the tent flap and looked out at the night. Isandro saw her and didn't move, didn't speak, just tilted his head slightly — *you okay* — and she shook her head, and then nodded, and then gave up on the grammar of it entirely and beckoned him over.

He came and sat beside the tent opening, close enough that their shoulders touched. He smelled of woodsmoke and river water and the graze-dressing on his arm needed changing, and she thought absurdly, *I could have forty years of this,* which was exactly the wrong thought to be having while deciding whether to buy back her dead father.

"He's still awake," Isandro said quietly. "Renner. I can see his lantern."

"I know."

"He'll wait as long as it takes. That's the kind of patience money buys."

"Isandro." She turned to look at him. In the dark his face was mostly shadow, the line of his jaw, the old scar near his temple she'd never asked about. "If I tell you what to do with your sister — if I told you drinking that water would give her back, would you do it?"

His breath caught. She felt it more than heard it.

"No," he said finally. It cost him; she watched it cost him. "No. Because what I'd get back wouldn't be her. It'd be me, watching her again. And I've already done that once."

"That's your answer about your grief. Mine might be different."

"It might," he agreed. "I'm not going to tell you it isn't." He paused. "But I'll tell you one thing for free, and then I'll shut up. Whatever's in that letter — whatever's in Renner's little bottle of your father — Tobin Hale already chose. He drank from the Gate. He saw everything, the whole life, and he still got on a boat and went downstream instead of east to Merrow's Landing. You know that now. You read his journals." His voice roughened. "So the question isn't whether he loved you. The question is whether you want to spend the rest of your life living in the past tense of a man who already answered."

Maren looked at him for a long moment.

"That wasn't shutting up," she said.

"No," he admitted. "It wasn't."

* * *

She waited until the camp slept.

Renner's men were professionals; they slept in shifts and lightly, but even professionals hit the dead hour, that trough between three and four when the body forgets its orders. Maren lay in her tent listening until the breathing patterns settled, until the sentry's footsteps found their rhythm along the bank, predictable as tide. Then she took out the oilcloth packet, and the letter, and struck a match low inside her cupped hands.

Tobin's handwriting had always looked like it was running somewhere. Even his name on the school forms she'd forged as a child — she'd practiced it off his old letters — slanted forward like a man late for something. This was no different. The paper was soft with handling, creased where it had been folded and unfolded many times before Odile ever took custody of it.

*Maren.*

*If you're reading this, Odile judged you ready, which means you're past the Reach, which means you're stubborn enough to scare me and proud enough that I'm glad of it. You get both from me. I'm sorry about the stubborn.*

*I drank from the Gate. I expect by now you know that. I expect you've read my journals and hated me for them, and you should, some. But I want you to hear this part from me and not from paper I wrote when my hands shook.*

*The Gate doesn't show you what happened. That's the lie everyone believes. It shows you what it FELT like, all at once, everything, no mercy and no order. I stood in that water and I was twenty again and sixty again and I was holding you the hour you were born and I was walking out the door of our house and all of it was happening NOW, all of it equal, and do you know what I learned?*

*I learned that I left because I was ashamed. Not tired, not restless, not any of the fine words I used. Ashamed. I'd gambled away our house and lied to you about where the money went and I could not stand to be seen by you, by anyone who knew me, one more day. The Gate showed me that truth so clean and bright I couldn't look anywhere else. And I walked away anyway. That's the part I need you to understand. It gave me everything and I still chose wrong. Knowing everything is not the same as being good. I proved it.*

*So if someone offers you my memories, girl — and someone will, because men like what I found don't stay secret forever — you tell them no. Not for my sake. For yours. My memories are mine, including the worst ones, especially the worst ones. You don't owe it to me to carry them. What you owe me is to live better than I did, and you can't do that looking backward.*

*You were the best thing I ever did and the one thing I did right, and I ran from you because I couldn't bear to ruin you too. Don't you dare take that as a reason to stop moving forward.*

*Your father,
who loved you badly and completely,
Tobin*

The match had burned down to her fingers. Maren shook it out and sat in the dark with wet cheeks and the letter trembling in her hands, and she realized she was laughing, quietly, horribly, the sound coming out of her like water finding a crack in stone.

*Don't you dare take that as a reason to stop moving forward.*

Even dead, even gone, even dissolved into rumor and river, the man could still tell her exactly what she didn't want to hear.

She folded the letter along its old creases and put it away, and she noticed that her hands had stopped shaking.

* * *

Renner was waiting at the cold fire when she came out of the tent at first light. He sat on a pack frame with a tin cup of coffee he wasn't drinking, and his eyes — pale, patient, the eyes of a man who had once been a prospector and had learned to wait out ground the way other men waited out weather — lifted to her face and read it the way he probably read ore samples.

"Well?" he said.

Behind him, Isandro stood between two of the hired men, hands loose at his sides, watching her with an expression he was trying very hard to make neutral and failing. The Kestrel's wrecked radio parts glinted in the bottom of a supply crate — she'd seen Isandro near it last night and said nothing, and he'd seen her see him and said nothing either, and that shared silence felt like the truest vow either of them had ever made.

Maren walked to the fire and stopped an arm's length from Corvus Renner.

"No," she said.

Renner's face didn't change. "Take the morning. Think it—"

"I've thought it. I've thought it for twenty-nine years, actually, every time a birthday passed without a letter. You're offering me a recording, Mr. Renner. A dead man's echo in a bottle. My father already stood in that water and saw everything he was, and you know what he did with perfect knowledge? He kept walking away." She pulled the letter from her jacket and held it up. "He wrote this before he died. He told me not to take the deal. He told me knowing everything isn't the same as being good. So no. I don't want his memories. I had his love, such as it was, badly delivered, and I've made my peace with the delivery. What I won't do is let you turn my father into inventory."

Something moved behind Renner's eyes — not rage, not yet. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own younger voice come out of someone else's mouth.

"You don't understand what you're refusing," he said softly. "I've *had* the Gate, girl. I know what it takes and what it gives. I spent thirty years hollow because someone else owns what I lost. I am offering you the one thing in this world that can't be bought anywhere else—"

"You're offering me what you wish someone had offered you," Maren said. "And I'm sorry for what was taken from you. I mean that. But you didn't come all this way to give me a gift. You came to buy passage with it, and you picked the wrong currency."

For a moment the camp was absolutely still. The river ran. A bird called somewhere upstream, three notes, descending.

Then Renner set down the cup he hadn't drunk from, and stood, and the recognition in his face closed over like ice forming across open water.

"Bind them," he said.

The hired men moved. Isandro fought — of course he fought, and for six or seven seconds it was beautiful, a shoulder into one man's sternum, an elbow that put another into the fire ring — but there were five of them and they'd been waiting for exactly this, and it ended with Isandro face-down in the gravel with a knee between his shoulder blades and cord biting his wrists, blood from a split lip spotting the stones.

"Don't hurt him," Maren said, and hated how fast it came out, how much it revealed.

"Hurt him?" Renner crouched beside Isandro, gripped a fistful of his hair, and turned his face up toward the light. "Mr. Fairweather is the only pilot alive who's flown this reach twice. And you—" he turned to Maren, and his smile was thin and cold as a blade's spine, "—you've read your father's trail marks better than any of my guides ever managed. You two aren't prisoners anymore. You're employees. You're going to lead me to the Gate, and you're going to do it quickly, because the dry season is eating my river a foot a day, and if we don't reach the source before the low water closes the gorges, I will spend the rest of my life as empty as I've spent the last thirty years. Do you understand me?"

Isandro spat blood into the gravel. "The Gate doesn't fix you, Renner. Ask your own memory-seller how it worked out for Tobin Hale."

"Oh, I intend to ask the Gate itself." Renner straightened, signaled his men up. "Cut them loose from each other. Rope them to the porters. We move in ten minutes." He paused, and looked back at Maren, and for just a moment the prospector showed through the consortium man, tired and haunted and old. "You could have been rich in ways you can't imagine, Maren Hale."

"I imagine plenty," she said.

They marched. Upstream, into the narrowing valley, past Tobin's old blazes fading on the trunks, the river climbing beside them toward the country where all maps went blank. Maren walked with her wrists roped and her chin up and her father's letter burning against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and ahead of her the gorge walls drew together, and the wind began to carry voices.

She didn't look at Isandro. She didn't need to. She could feel him behind her, steady as a rudder, and between them passed the silent arithmetic of two people counting the same odds: *five armed men, one narrow gorge, one sabotaged radio, and a source that drove a grown man to his knees.*

The Gate was a day away. Maybe less.

And the wind in the stones was starting to say her name.
