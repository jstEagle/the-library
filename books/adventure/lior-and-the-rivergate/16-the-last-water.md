## 16. The Last Water

The tributary died in front of them.

Isandro saw it first from the ridge — a silver thread gone to a smear of cracked clay, the pools shrinking to coins of brown water where the fish had already stopped moving. Below, the channel that Odile's charts had promised would carry them north was a scar of stones and sun-baked mud, running dry a full week ahead of anything reasonable. The dry season wasn't coming anymore. It had arrived, and it was hungry.

"Renner won't like this," Maren said beside him, binoculars down, voice flat with heat and fatigue.

"Renner doesn't like anything that isn't on his schedule."

They descended into the dead channel because there was nowhere else to go. The expedition — Renner, four hired men, Isandro, Maren, and the two porters who'd been paid enough not to ask questions — moved in a strung-out line along the gravel, boots crunching on stone that should have been underwater. The heat came off the rocks in visible waves. By noon Isandro could taste copper when he swallowed.

Renner walked at the front like a man who believed water obeyed him. He'd changed since the fork, since Odile's barge burned behind them and they'd fled up the wrong channel with her sacrifice still smoking on the water. He was quieter now. More certain. A man who had bought his way past every obstacle so long that he'd started to treat the landscape as just another negotiation.

But the landscape didn't take bribes.

They found the first pool at midafternoon — a foot of stagnant green water under an overhang of clay, warm as bathwater. The porters drank first, then the men. Isandro filled canteens through a cloth filter and dropped purification tablets in, watching the chemical fizz like something alive trying to escape.

Maren crouched beside him, keeping her voice low. "How far to the next reliable water?"

"If the map's honest? Two days." He glanced back at Renner, standing apart, studying the sky for his missing plane. "If it's not, we find out what we're made of."

"We're made of stubbornness and bad decisions."

"That's the good news."

* * *

That night the mutiny started small, the way all mutinies do — with a man sitting too far from the fire.

His name was Dekker, a broad-shouldered ex-soldier with a tattooed forearm and eyes that had gone flat somewhere south of here. He'd been Renner's most reliable man since the ambush at the fork. Now he sat outside the ring of firelight, cleaning a rifle that didn't need cleaning, and Isandro watched him do it for twenty minutes before Dekker spoke without looking up.

"You know how much water's left?"

Isandro did the math honestly. "Three days. Four if nobody sweats."

Dekker snorted. "Nobody sweats. Sure." He set the rifle across his knees. "I've done dry-country work before. Knew a crew up in the caldera country that ran out. You want to hear what that sounds like?" He didn't wait for an answer. "It sounds like nothing. That's the worst part. Men just stop talking. Then they stop walking. Then you're deciding who gets the last of it, and after that you're not a crew anymore, you're just animals waiting your turn."

"Then talk to Renner."

"I talked to Renner." Dekker's laugh had no humor in it. "He says the Gate is close. He says once we reach it none of this matters." His eyes finally came up, and there was something raw underneath the flatness. "You believe that? You've seen it?"

Isandro weighed the lie against the truth and chose something between them. "I've seen what it does to people who want it too much."

"Yeah," Dekker said. "Me too."

He walked away then, and Isandro sat alone by the dying fire, understanding that the expedition had just developed a fault line, and that he was standing on one side of it whether he liked it or not.

* * *

The radio was Renner's lifeline — a ruggedized unit lashed to a porter's frame, its antenna a whip of aluminum that caught the last light every evening when Renner called in position reports to the consortium's forward camp. Isandro had watched him use it for days. Twice-daily calls, short and coded, and each time Renner's hand rested on the set afterward like a man touching a talisman.

On the third morning, while the camp broke down around him and Renner stood at the ridgeline scanning the southern sky for the resupply flight that was supposed to meet them, Isandro knelt beside the radio pack.

Maren kept watch ten feet away, ostentatiously refilling a canteen, her body angled between him and the others. She didn't look at him. Her hands were steady. He loved her for that — for learning, over these hard miles, the specific grammar of complicity.

The sabotage took ninety seconds. He didn't break anything obvious. He loosened the antenna connection inside its housing until it would work intermittently, fail under vibration, and die completely within a day or two of rough handling. He pinched a hairline crack in the battery casing that would corrode the contacts slowly, invisibly. When he finished, the radio looked perfect. It would even transmit, sometimes, for another day or so — long enough that no one would trace the failure to a moment, a person, a choice.

Then he closed the housing and stood, and Maren fell into step beside him, and neither of them said a word about it.

He told himself it was strategy: cut Renner off from his masters, blind the consortium to their position, buy them hours or days. All true. But underneath the strategy was something simpler and less comfortable — the memory of Odile's barge burning, of Dekker's flat eyes, of everything this expedition had cost people who never got a vote. If the Gate died with the season, let it die unfound. Let the consortium's maps end here, in dead channels and empty air.

Some secrets were worth a man's soul. He was beginning to understand which kind his sister had thought hers was.

* * *

The radio died on schedule the next evening, mid-transmission, in front of everyone.

Renner's voice went from clipped to sharp to silent as he smacked the casing, checked the connections, tore open the battery compartment and found the corrosion blooming there like white mold. He looked up slowly, and his gaze crossed the circle of faces — Dekker, the other three men, the porters, Maren, Isandro — looking for the saboteur, finding nothing but exhaustion and thirst.

"No signal since noon anyway," one of the men offered. "Storm cells east. Probably nothing getting through regardless."

Renner stared at the radio a long moment. Then, quietly, in a voice that frightened Isandro more than shouting would have: "Pack it. We move at first light."

That night the camp split visibly. Dekker and two of the men built their own fire fifty yards downstream. The porters lay between the camps like stones in a border wall. And Renner sat alone with his dead radio, cleaning it with a rag, over and over, though there was nothing left to clean.

Maren found Isandro at the water's edge, where the last pool reflected a scatter of stars.

"He knows someone did it," she murmured.

"He suspects. Suspicion isn't proof."

"It doesn't need proof. It needs a target." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Isandro. What happens when a man like that runs out of things to control?"

He watched the stars shiver on the dark water. "He starts breaking things."

* * *

The truce happened on the fifth day, at the second-to-last pool, and it began with Dekker pointing a rifle at Renner.

Isandro heard the voices rise from downstream and came around the clay bank at a jog to find the scene laid out like a diagram of collapse: Dekker standing with the rifle half-raised, not quite aimed, the other two men flanking him with the wary neutrality of men choosing sides in real time. Renner facing them alone, hands empty, the porters vanished upchannel. Maren frozen at the edge of it, a canteen in each hand.

"Four days," Dekker was saying. "Four days you've promised us the Gate is over the next ridge. My map says—" 

"Your map is wrong."

"My map says there's a river crossing a day south of here. A day *south*, Renner. We've been walking north into a furnace because you've got a feeling."

"The source moves with the season—"

"Bullshit." Dekker's finger tightened on the guard. "I signed on for a survey job. Escort duty. Not dying in a dry wash so you can drink magic water and remember how much you love money."

What happened next, Isandro did without deciding to. He stepped into the space between them, palms open, and said the only true thing available:

"He's right about one thing. The Gate is close. I've seen Tobin Hale's markings. We're two days out, maybe three." He held Dekker's stare. "And I'm telling you the water's closer than that. There's a spring-fed pool in the box canyon ahead — Tobin marked it twelve years ago and those springs don't quit. Enough for everyone. But not if we spend the next hour deciding who shoots whom."

Silence. Heat shimmering off the clay. Dekker's rifle wavering between aim and rest.

"Why should we trust you?" one of the other men said. "You're his prisoner same as us."

"I'm nobody's prisoner," Isandro said. "I'm the only one here who's been to this country before. So here's the deal. Water first. Then anyone who wants to walk south can walk south, and I'll draw them a route myself. No hard feelings. Nobody follows."

Dekker lowered the rifle an inch. "And if there's no pool?"

"Then shoot me. I'll be the easiest thing left to hit."

A sound escaped Maren — half laugh, half sob, quickly strangled. And something in Dekker's face cracked, some old soldier's respect for a man willing to bet his own skin on his word. He slung the rifle. "Water first," he said. "Then we'll see."

Renner hadn't spoken through any of it. He stood apart, watching Isandro with an expression Isandro couldn't read — something between hatred and recognition, as if he were seeing, for the first time, the shape of the man he'd been chasing. As if he understood that the pilot had just taken command of the expedition without firing a shot, and that the men had followed him willingly.

* * *

Tobin's spring was exactly where the old prospector had marked it, cold and clear and running thin but running, seeping from a seam in the canyon wall into a basin fringed with ferns gone brown at the edges. They drank like animals and then like people, filling every container, soaking shirts, and the change in the camp was immediate and physical — men who hadn't met each other's eyes in days suddenly passing canteens back and forth.

Thirst, Isandro thought, was the great equalizer. It made brothers of enemies for exactly as long as the water lasted.

He was wringing out his shirt when Renner came to stand beside him at the spring, close enough to speak privately, far enough that no one else would hear.

"You sabotaged my radio."

Not a question. Isandro didn't insult them both by denying it.

"Corrosion," he said. "Happens."

Renner almost smiled. Almost. "Twelve years ago I would have killed a man for less. I want you to know that I know. And I want you to know why you're still breathing." He turned to look at the spring, at the men drinking, at Maren laughing tiredly with Dekker over a shared ration. "Because you just did what I couldn't. You held them together. And I need them held together for two more days." His voice dropped lower. "After the Gate, Fairweather, the account settles. Everything. But not before."

"Fine," Isandro said. "Two days. Then we settle everything."

They shook on it — two enemies sealing a truce with dry palms and no trust in it whatsoever, bound together by the oldest treaty in the world: the shared need for water, and the shared road toward something worse than thirst.

Above the canyon rim, the evening wind carried a sound none of them could name — faint, rhythmic, almost like voices. Maren lifted her head, listening, and Isandro felt the hair rise on his arms.

Two days. Maybe three.

The gorge was close, and it was already whispering.
