## 18. Everything I Ever Was

The basin opened out of the gorge like a held breath finally released.

Isandro came around the last bend of the portage trail with the canoe on his shoulders and stopped so abruptly that Maren nearly walked into the stern behind him. He set the canoe down without looking at it. His hands were shaking, and he watched them shake with the detached interest of a man observing someone else's body.

The water was wrong.

Not wrong the way a mirage is wrong, or a trick of light on limestone. Wrong the way a voice is wrong when you hear your own name spoken by a stranger. The spring rose from the center of the basin in a column so clear it was almost invisible — a pillar of water standing in water, fed from some artery of the earth that had been beating since before there was anyone to remember it. Where it broke the surface, the ripples moved outward in perfect rings, unhurried, as if the water had all the time in the world. As if time were something it kept in a jar.

The air above the basin was thick. Not humid — thick. It pressed against his eardrums. And underneath the sound of the spring, underneath the wind coming down the gorge, there were voices. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Murmuring in overlapping cadences, half-words, half-names, the acoustic texture of a crowded room heard through a wall. He'd been hearing them faintly for two days in the gorge. Here they weren't faint. Here they were *specific*.

He could almost pick out words. He could almost pick out—

*Lian.*

His sister's name, or close enough to it that his chest seized.

Maren set down her pack beside him. She didn't speak. Neither of them spoke. Renner's men had fallen back along the trail, muttering, refusing to come closer than fifty yards to the water, and Renner himself stood at the basin's edge like a man arriving at an altar he'd been exiled from for thirty years. His face was gray. His hands hung open at his sides.

But this chapter wasn't about Renner. This was Isandro's war, and he'd known it was coming since Odile first told her stories on the barge, since the elders in the forgetting village had looked at him with those patient, terrible eyes and said: *the Gate gives back everything, pilot. Everything. Are you sure you've counted what you carry?*

He hadn't counted. He'd spent twelve years not counting.

* * *

The basin was maybe forty meters across, ringed in white stone worn smooth as bone. No outlet visible — the river simply rose here, whole, and began its three-thousand-kilometer argument with gravity. The great river of the interior, the one every chart called the Serpiente, the one he'd been flying alongside and crossing and cursing for twelve years, started in a bowl of silence you could cup in your hands.

The light came down through a break in the gorge walls and hit the central column of water, and where it hit, the voices got louder.

Isandro walked to the edge before he decided to walk to the edge. That was the thing about the place — it didn't call you. It didn't need to. It just stood there being true, and truth did the pulling.

He knelt.

The stone was cool under his knees. The water at the rim was so clear that looking into it was like looking off a cliff — no bottom, no reflection he could trust, just depth going down and down into blue-black, and somewhere in that depth, movement. Shapes. Or memories. Or the idea of memories, wearing shapes the way actors wear costumes.

"Isandro." Maren's voice, quiet, behind him. "You don't have to."

"I know." His own voice sounded scraped. "That's the problem. Knowing is the only thing I've got left, and I'm about to trade it."

She crouched beside him but didn't touch him. She'd learned that about him on the river — when to put a hand on his shoulder and when to leave the space empty. He loved her for it. He loved her for a hundred things, and the cruelty of this moment was that every one of those hundred things was a reason to stay exactly who he was.

Because here was the arithmetic, laid out plain as a fuel gauge:

Drink, and he got everything back. Twelve years of fog, burned away. The coast. The last night. The storm. Liana's face, which he could no longer hold steady in his mind no matter how hard he gripped it — it slid and smeared like paint in rain, and every year it got worse, and every year he hated himself a little more for letting it go. Drink, and he'd have her face again. Her laugh. The promise he made her. And the other thing too — the thing he'd fled, the reason a decorated coastal rescue pilot had walked away from a career and a life and a name and ended up hauling medicine crates into strips that didn't appear on charts, alone, twelve years of miles with nowhere to land.

Don't drink, and stay hollow. Stay safe. Keep the wound bandaged with distance and routine and the particular anesthesia of always being in motion. Keep Maren — because the man she'd come to know on this river, the man who shot the Serrated Reach and pulled her out of the water and told her the truth about losing someone to duty — that man was built partly out of the hole where his past used to be. Fill the hole, and who knew what collapsed around it.

The water murmured. The voices murmured. Somewhere in the layered whisper he heard a woman laughing, young, and his whole body turned toward the sound like a compass needle.

"Lian," he said. Out loud. To the water. To whatever was listening.

The laughter didn't answer. But the depth shifted, and for half a second — less — he saw her. Not remembered. *Saw.* Dark hair plastered wet across her forehead. Seventeen years old and furious at him, the way she'd been furious at him that last night, standing on the dock in the rain.

Then it was gone, and the loss of it hit harder than the having of it, and Isandro Fairweather understood that he was already drowning.

* * *

Twelve years ago there had been a storm called Vespera, and a fishing boat called the *Cormorant Maid*, and a choice.

He'd told Maren the shape of it on the gravel bar after the crash, in the dark, with the engine ticking as it cooled. He'd given her the outline. Duty over love. A call he made, a boat he didn't turn toward, a sister who went out anyway because Isandro had told her the weather window would hold and then, when the mayday came from farther north, he'd flown north instead of south, because protocol said nearest-vessel-first and protocol had never met Liana Fairweather, who was seventeen and immortal and had borrowed their father's boat to prove she could run the coast alone.

The outline was true. The outline was also a cage, and he'd lived in it so long he'd mistaken it for a house.

Here is what the outline left out:

That he'd known. Some part of him, some animal part below the radio discipline and the checklists, had known the window wouldn't hold, had heard it in the barometer falling all afternoon, and had said nothing because saying something meant admitting he couldn't control the sky, and Isandro at twenty-six could admit anything except that. So he'd let his sister believe the lie he needed to believe himself, and when the sky tore open he'd done the correct thing, the by-the-book thing, the thing that saved four strangers and lost the one person whose whole life he'd promised to keep — promised on their father's grave, promised with his hand on her head like a benediction, *I've got you, Lian, I'll always have you* —

And then he hadn't.

And the worst part, the part that had eaten him alive on the flight north through Vespera's black walls, the part he'd never said aloud to anyone including God: in the moment the two maydays overlapped on the frequency, some cold efficient core of him had felt *relief*. One call. One decision. Clean. And he'd grabbed that relief like a man grabbing a handrail, and he'd flown north, and the relief had lasted eleven minutes, and the bill for it had been collecting ever since.

That was what the Gate offered to give back. Not the grief — he had plenty of grief; grief traveled light and never left. The Gate offered the *truth*. All of it. Every corner of the night he'd edited. Every rationalization stripped down to the cowardice underneath. The elders had warned them: *it gives back everything, including guilt.*

He knelt at the rim of the clearest water in the world and understood that he wanted it anyway.

God help him, he wanted it.

* * *

"Maren." He didn't turn around. If he turned around and saw her face, he'd lose his nerve, and he wasn't sure losing his nerve was the right outcome anymore, and that uncertainty frightened him more than the water did. "If I drink this, and I come up different — if I come up someone you don't—"

"Stop." Her hand landed on his shoulder after all, warm and certain. "You've spent twelve years deciding things alone. You told me that. On the Reach, in the water, you said the worst thing you ever did was decide alone." Her grip tightened. "So don't. Not this one."

"The choice is mine. Nobody else can drink my memory for me."

"No. But somebody can remind you who's waiting on the other side of it." She came around in front of him, kneeling into his sightline, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. She'd read Tobin's journals. She'd learned what her father had seen in this water and why he'd still walked away from her, and she'd chosen — alone, in the dark, with Renner's offer on the table — to take the living future over the recorded past. She knew the price of this water better than anyone breathing.

"My father drank," she said quietly. "And it destroyed him slower than a bullet would have. He saw everything, and he couldn't carry it, and instead of coming home to me he wrote letters to a daughter he was too ashamed to face." Her voice caught and steadied. "But that was Tobin. Drinking alone, carrying alone. That's the pattern, isn't it? That's the whole disease. Everyone who comes to this water comes alone, decides alone, drowns alone."

The voices in the air seemed to lean closer.

"So here's what I'm offering," Maren said. "Drink, don't drink — I'm staying either way. Whatever comes up out of that water wearing your face, I'll know him. Because I already do. The rest is just the parts you hid." She swallowed. "Even the ugly ones. *Especially* the ugly ones, Isandro. I grew up on ugly secrets. They don't scare me. Silence does."

He looked at her — really looked, the way he'd taught himself not to look at people, because looking was how you lost them — and the war inside him didn't stop. It changed shape. It stopped being *drink or don't* and became something older and simpler: *trust or don't*. Trust that a man could be fully known — the relief, the cowardice, the eleven minutes, all of it — and still be held.

Twelve years of miles, and every one of them had been a vote against that trust.

The water rang against the stone. Soft. Patient. It had waited ten thousand years; it could wait out one broken pilot.

"I'm scared," he said. It came out small. He hadn't said it out loud since he was a child.

"I know," Maren said. "Me too. About different things. That's kind of the point."

He laughed — a cracked, wet sound — and turned back to the basin, and the turning brought Renner into view.

* * *

Corvus Renner had not moved from his place at the far rim. He stood with the stillness of a man in the presence of something he'd sold his soul to once and recognized on sight. The gray had gone out of his face and something worse had replaced it: hunger. Old hunger, thirty years deep, the hunger of a man who knew exactly what the water tasted like and had spent three decades trying to forget that he knew.

He'd been a prospector. Odile had told them that much. What the elders' stories filled in was the rest — that Renner had drunk here as a young man, hungry for gold-vein knowledge, and the Gate had given him back his whole life in one swallow, and men had come behind him, men with contracts and ledgers, and they had found a way to draw the memory out of him like drawing venom from a bite. Bottled it. Sold it. Left him a man with holes where his childhood used to be, who remembered his mother's funeral but not her face, and who had clawed his way up the consortium's ladder ever since on the theory that whoever owned the source owned the cure.

Now he stood at the rim, and his hands were trembling, and Isandro — who knew what wanting looked like when it had curdled into need — felt the temperature of the moment drop.

Renner turned his head. Their eyes met across the basin, across the rising column of impossible water, and Renner smiled, and it was the loneliest expression Isandro had ever seen on a living face.

"You're going to drink," Renner said. Not a question. "I can see it. You've got the look. I had the look." He stepped closer to the rim, and his hired men behind him shifted uneasily, rifles loose in their hands, nobody wanting to be near the water. "Drink, pilot. Take it all back. And then, when you're whole — when you remember exactly who you are and what you did — you'll understand why I can't leave this place standing. Why *nobody* can be allowed to own it." His voice dropped, ragged. "Or maybe you won't. Maybe you'll be like the rest of them. Maybe everyone who drinks thinks they'll be the one who carries it."

The voices swirled. The column of water stood in its rings of light, ancient and indifferent, offering everything to everyone and keeping nothing.

Isandro looked at the water. At Maren. At the man he might become on the other side of one swallow.

Then he leaned forward over the rim, cupped his hands, and reached for the source of the Serpiente — while behind him, at the edge of hearing, Renner gave a quiet order to his men that Isandro was too far under to catch.

The water was cold beyond cold, and the moment it touched his lips, every voice in the basin went silent at once —

— and then they all spoke *his* name.
