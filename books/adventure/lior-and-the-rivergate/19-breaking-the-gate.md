## 19. Breaking the Gate

The basin opened before them like a held breath.

Isandro came over the last ridge with Renner's men a half-step behind and stopped so abruptly that Maren walked into his back. Below them, ringed by walls of pale limestone, the spring rose whole from the earth — not a trickle, not a seep, but the river itself, born in a single surge from a fracture in the rock floor, wide as a house, cold and impossibly clear. He could see forty feet down into the throat of it, to where the light bent and gave up. The water didn't move the way water moved. It *arrived*, steady and total, as if it had been rising there since before the mountains learned their names.

The air above it was thick. Not humid — thick, the way a room is thick when too many people have spoken in it. Isandro felt it against his skin like a hand laid flat on the back of his neck. Voices, or the memory of voices, or something older than either. He'd heard the gorge whisper for two days. This was the gorge's source, and it wasn't whispering anymore. It was *saying*.

"God," Maren breathed beside him.

He looked at her. Her face was wet — she'd been crying since the portage, he realized, crying without sound, without stopping, the way you cry when your body has given up asking permission. She was staring down at the water the way people stare at graves and altars, both at once.

Renner stood ten feet away, at the edge of the shelf, and he had gone very still. For three weeks Isandro had watched the man move like a blade being sharpened — all economy, all edge, every gesture aimed. Now Renner just stood there, and the stillness looked less like control than like surrender. His hands hung open at his sides. His mouth worked around words that wouldn't come.

"Mr. Renner," said one of his men — Voss, the big one, the one who'd mutinied first and hardest out on the dying tributaries. "Water's right there. You want us to—"

"Shut up." Renner's voice cracked on it. He took one step down toward the basin, then another, gravel sliding under his boots, and Isandro understood with a sick lurch of clarity what was about to happen and could no more stop it than stop the river.

Renner knelt at the water's edge.

He cupped his hands. They shook — Isandro could see it from twenty feet, the tremor running through the man's forearms like current through wire. And then Corvus Renner, who had spent thirty years and an unknown fortune buying his way back to this hole in the earth, dipped his hands into the source of the great river and drank.

He drank once. Twice. A third time, greedy, gasping, water running down his chin and into his collar.

Then he sat back on his heels and made a sound Isandro would hear for the rest of his life.

It wasn't a scream. Screams have shape. This was the sound a man makes when everything arrives at once — every debt, every face, every hour of every year he'd paid to forget, all of it surfacing together like bodies rising in a flooded mine. Renner folded forward until his forehead nearly touched the water, and he stayed there, rocking, keening, his fingers digging furrows in the mud, and the voices in the air seemed to lean down toward him, gathering, *recognizing*.

"Get him up," Voss said, but nobody moved. Nobody wanted to touch him.

Maren grabbed Isandro's sleeve. "Isandro." Her voice was low and fast. "Look at him. That's what it does. That's what it did to my father for eleven years, and he couldn't even tell me why."

"I know."

"Whatever you're going to do—" She turned and looked at him fully, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "Do it now."

* * *

He'd known since the gorge. Maybe since the village, maybe since Tobin's journals, maybe since the night in Merrow's Landing when he'd first unrolled the map by lamplight and felt the future tilt under him. There had never really been a choice, only a long postponement of admitting it. But knowing a thing and standing at the edge of doing it are different countries, and the border between them is crossed alone.

The plan lived in the pack on his shoulders: two sticks of gelignite Odile had pressed on him back at the barge with a shrug and a warning — *"the shelf over the aquifer is limestone, boy, it wants an excuse"* — plus a coil of fuse, a striker, and a prayer. Tobin's journals had described it: the spring didn't rise from the basin floor. It rose through a shelf of fractured rock capping a pressurized aquifer, a natural valve holding back water under the whole plateau. Break the valve wrong and you got a geyser. Break it right and the basin floor dropped, the spring found a hundred new channels underground, and the Rivergate became geology. Just geology. Water without witnesses.

It would take most of the charge. It would be loud enough to bring the whole gorge down on their heads if he misjudged it. And it would mean kneeling at the edge of the one water on earth that could give him back everything he'd lost — his sister's laugh, the coast, the reason he ran, the whole drowned country of himself — and turning his back on it forever.

He started down the slope while Renner wept.

Voss saw him. So did the other two, Delacroix and the kid they called Sparrow, and for a long moment the four of them regarded each other across the gravel like men meeting on a bridge too narrow for two directions. Three weeks ago they'd have shot him. Two days ago, after the radio died and the water ran out and Renner started talking to people who weren't there, they'd have shot him anyway. But now Renner was on his knees in the mud remembering thirty years at once, and the expedition had no more center of gravity, and men without a center look for the nearest solid thing.

"You're going to blow it," Voss said. Not a question.

"It's the only way it stays free."

Voss looked at the water. At Renner. Back at the water. Then he did the thing Isandro would never be able to repay: he turned his back, deliberately, and walked five paces away, and told Delacroix to check the packs.

"The consortium pays bonuses," Delacroix said uncertainly.

"The consortium's agent is crying in the mud, Del."

Isandro kept moving.

The shelf was exactly where Tobin's journal said it would be — a lip of pale rock at the basin's north rim, where the spring's overflow cut a channel on its way to becoming the river proper. He knelt on it and pressed his palm flat against the stone and felt, faintly, the pulse beneath: the aquifer breathing, tons per square inch, patient as sleep. Somewhere under his knees was enough water to drown the valley. He thought about that the way you're supposed to think about loaded guns.

He packed the charge into a crevice at the shelf's root, tamped it with river mud, and fed out the fuse with hands that were steadier than he deserved. Ten feet. Fifteen. Enough to reach the high ground, not enough to waste time.

And then, because there was no one left to perform for, because the moment demanded honesty the way the water demanded thirst, he let himself look at it.

The basin lay below him, lit by late sun, and the water was so clear it seemed less like liquid than like a window someone had forgotten to close. He could see the fracture where the river was born. He could see the light going down and down into blue. And rising off it, everywhere, the voices — not words, not quite, but the *shape* of words, the warmth of them, the way a kitchen holds the shape of everyone who ever cooked in it. He heard his mother in it somewhere. He was almost sure he heard Liana.

His sister. Twelve years dead in every way that mattered, twelve years a locked door in his chest, and here she was, one cupped handful away. Drink, and he'd have her back — her voice, her face at nine years old missing a tooth, the argument they'd had the last week, the promise he'd broken, all of it, everything, the guilt arriving in the same shipment as the grace because that was the price and the village elders had warned them true: *the Gate gives back everything.*

He could drink first. Thirty seconds. One handful. No one would know — Voss had turned away, Maren was watching Renner, the world was busy.

His knees actually bent. He felt them bend, felt his body making the decision ahead of his conscience, and that frightened him more than the explosive in his hand.

"Maren," he said. Loudly. To the air. To himself.

She was already looking at him. She'd been watching him the whole time; he understood that now. She came down the slope fast, gravel skidding, and stopped beside him at the shelf's edge, and she didn't say *don't*. She knew better. She'd read Tobin's journals too. She knew what forbidding a Hale from water accomplished.

Instead she said: "Whatever you choose, I'll carry it with you."

That undid him more than the voices had. Because it was an offer with no exit in it — stay and drown in memory together, or turn away together, but no version where he went alone. And going alone was the whole disease. Going alone was why he'd fled the coast. Going alone was the promise he'd broken.

He stood up. His knees ached with the cost of it.

"No," he said, to the water, to Liana, to himself. "Not like this. Not bought back. Earned forward."

He struck the fuse.

* * *

The fuse burned slow and vicious, spitting orange, eating its own length with a patience that mocked him. Isandro ran — up the slope, across the gravel, Maren beside him matching him stride for stride because of course she did — and behind them Renner had lifted his head from the mud, and his eyes, when they found the burning fuse, went from grief to arithmetic in the space of a heartbeat.

Thirty years of ruthlessness doesn't drown. It surfaces.

"*No—*" Renner came up off his knees like something resurrected, mud-sliding, screaming at his men: "*Stop him, stop him, do you know what that is, do you know what's worth—*" And Voss, magnificent, immovable, stepped into his path and simply stood there, a mountain in a canvas jacket, while Delacroix caught Renner's arms from behind.

Isandro hit the high ground and threw himself flat, dragging Maren down with him, her body half under his, his hand cradling the back of her skull against the stone.

"Cover your ears," he said. "Open your mouth."

The world went white.

It wasn't a sound so much as a fact — the detonation arrived through the ground before the air, a fist through the planet's bones, and then the shelf he'd knelt on thirty seconds earlier ceased to be a shelf and became a falling sky. The basin floor dropped. He felt it drop, felt the whole plateau shudder and settle like a house whose foundation has decided otherwise, and then the spring, cheated of its valve, did what pressurized water does: it screamed upward through every new crack at once, a forest of white columns, and the basin filled in seconds, boiling, churning, and the overflow channel became a torrent and the torrent became a wall coming down the valley toward the low ground.

"Run!" Voss bellowed, and even Renner ran.

They ran. All of them, enemies and allies and whatever Renner was now, sprinting up the goat track along the gorge wall while the flood took the valley floor below with a roar like the end of arguments. Isandro glanced back once — once — and saw the basin churning white where the clear water had been, the voices gone, *silenced*, the air suddenly, shockingly empty, like walking out of a crowded room into snowfall. The Gate wasn't destroyed. It was buried — a hundred feet of churning water over a shattered valve, the spring finding its way down into the dark aquifer, dispersed, anonymous, gone.

The river would run. The river would always run. But it would run without remembering.

* * *

The flood chased them half a mile up the gorge and lost interest. When it finally fell quiet behind them, they stood on a ledge above the transformed valley, six people gasping in the steam of their own breath, and counted themselves alive.

Delacroix had a gash on his scalp. Sparrow was laughing — high, unhinged, the laughter of a man whose employer had just stopped existing in any form he recognized. Voss leaned against the rock with his eyes closed. And Renner stood apart, at the ledge's edge, looking down at the white water, and he was not raging. That was the terrible part. Isandro had braced for rage, for the gun, for the last desperate card. Instead Corvus Renner stood very straight and very quiet, and when he spoke his voice was almost gentle, almost wondering, the voice of a man reading his own autopsy.

"I had a brother," he said, to no one. "I'd forgotten I had a brother. He died in my arms in '91 and I sold the memory of it for drilling rights." He laughed once, softly, horribly. "I've been walking around for thirty years lighter than a man should be and I called it *efficiency*."

Nobody answered him. There was nothing to answer.

He turned to Isandro, and his eyes were ruined and lucid, and he said: "You understand what you've taken."

"I understand what I've kept it from becoming," Isandro said. "A tap with a meter on it."

For a long moment Renner just looked at him. Then he reached slowly into his coat — and every muscle in Isandro's body clenched — and drew out not a weapon but a folded survey map, consortium-stamped, the basin marked in red ink with extraction schedules and dam elevations, thirty years of paperwork for a theft. He held it out over the drop. And let go.

The map turned twice in the wind and vanished into the flood-churned valley, into the water that remembered nothing, which was fitting, and Isandro thought: *that's the closest thing to mercy he has left, and he just spent it.*

"We're done," Renner said. "All of us. Go home." He started down the far side of the gorge alone, toward the long walk out, a man carrying everything, headed nowhere.

Voss spat over the edge, looked at Isandro, and said, "You owe me a bonus," and it was almost a joke, and Isandro laughed, and it hurt his ribs, and he didn't care.

* * *

Which left the small problem of the flood between them and downstream.

The trail along the gorge wall was passable but the low route was gone — Odile's rendezvous point, the canoe cache, all of it under eight feet of new river running fast and brown. Maren stood at the ledge's lip surveying it with the mapmaker's eye he'd watched grow sharper every day of the journey, and when she turned to him her face was streaked with dust and dried tears and lit from inside like a lamp.

"You blew up a geological formation," she said.

"It seemed urgent."

"My father spent eleven years trying to find that water again and you dropped a mountain on it." She was smiling. She was also shaking. He pulled her in before either of them could make it complicated, and she came, her fists closing in his jacket, her forehead hard against his jaw, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because the moment was already saying it.

"He'd have helped you carry the dynamite," she said into his collar. "You know that, right? After everything. He'd have carried it himself."

"Yeah," Isandro said. "I know."

Below them the river ran free and new through the broken valley, brown with the birth of it, going wherever rivers go when no one owns them. Somewhere downstream was Odile, and the village, and Merrow's Landing, and a wrecked Kestrel on a gravel bar that he was already, absurdly, aching to rebuild. Somewhere upstream, silence, permanent now.

Maren stepped back, wiped her face with both palms, and pointed down-trail with the brisk authority of a woman who had recently decided how her life was going to work.

"Two hundred miles to Odile's," she said. "And I'm not camping another night listening to you pretend you don't snore. Move, Fairweather."

He moved.
