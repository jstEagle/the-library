## 20. Downstream

The river we came up was not the river that carried us home.

I understood that in the first hour after the collapse, when the water beneath Odile's salvaged skiff turned from the color of tea to something clear as breath. The Gate had fed it for centuries — a slow seep of memory rising through limestone — and now the spring was buried under a hundred tons of shattered shelf rock, and the river was only water again. Cold, honest, ordinary. It moved past us without whispering.

Isandro sat at the stern with his arm bound and his face gray with exhaustion, watching the current the way a man watches a door he has just closed. He hadn't spoken since the basin. Neither had Renner.

Renner stood at the bow of his own boat, thirty meters behind us, and did not raise the rifle slung across his back. His men were gone — scattered into the bush or mutinied down to nothing somewhere in the dying tributaries — and what remained of Corvus Renner stood alone at the prow like a man reading a letter he wished he'd never opened.

He had drunk from the Gate. I'd watched him go under: the total recall, every year of his life arriving at once, unedited, unpaid for. What surfaced afterward wasn't the consortium's man. It was someone smaller, older, holding everything he had ever been and finding no one left to sell it to.

For two days he followed us downstream and did nothing at all.

* * *

We made camp the second night on a sandbar below the Serrated Reach, where the rapids had gone quiet and shallow, starved of their spring-fed surge. Isandro slept by the fire with his wounded arm cradled against his chest, and I couldn't sleep, so I walked to the water's edge.

Renner was already there.

I should have been afraid. A week ago I would have been. But the man crouching at the shoreline scooping water into his hands looked less like a hunter than like a mourner at an open grave.

"You shouldn't drink that," I said. "It's just river now."

"I know what it is." He let the water run through his fingers. "I keep drinking anyway. Habit." He almost smiled. "Twelve years ago they paid me to forget a woman named Ilse. My wife. She died in Merrow's Landing and I couldn't live inside the memory of it, so I sold it. The consortium bought my grief and gave me a career. Efficient trade. Everyone praised how well I took it."

The fire crackled behind us. Isandro stirred but didn't wake.

"Then your prospector's map led me back," Renner went on, "and I told myself I'd dam the source before it could undo me. And then I stood at the water and—" His voice failed, restarted. "And I remembered her funeral. I remembered that I didn't cry, because I was already negotiating with the men who'd bought my forgetting. I remembered being a coward at my own wife's grave." He looked at me, and his eyes were wet and ancient. "Your father drank too, you know. Did he tell you?"

"No."

"He saw all of it. Every year he'd spent, every mistake. And he still walked away from you." Renner shook his head slowly. "That used to confuse me. It doesn't anymore. Memory doesn't make you brave, girl. It just makes you accurate."

"Is that why you're letting us go?"

He was quiet a long time. The river ran past, dumb and clean.

"The consortium will send survey teams by August," he said finally. "They'll find a dry spring and a collapsed shelf and they'll spend ten years and a fortune proving there's nothing worth taking. Because there isn't. Not anymore." He rose, joints cracking, an old man suddenly. "You destroyed the only thing I ever wanted back. And it was the right thing to do. Both of those are true, and I'm going to have to carry them both, and I find I don't have the strength left to carry a grudge on top."

"So that's it? You just let us float away?"

"That's it." He turned back toward the water. "Tell your pilot—" He stopped. Started over. "Tell him the coast is a long way to run from. Some people never make it back."

* * *

We passed the village on the fourth day.

I smelled the woodsmoke before I saw the roofs — that green smoke of damp fuel, the smell of fish drying on racks — and my chest tightened so hard I had to grip the gunwale. This was the place. The village that forgets. Where the elders drank their diluted cups of Gate water and held centuries between them like a rope passed hand to hand down generations. Where Tobin Hale had come once, years ago, and been remembered.

Where Tobin Hale had asked them, I now knew from his journals, to forget him.

Isandro brought the skiff alongside the landing with the careful hands of a man who'd spent twelve years touching down on strips barely wider than his conscience. The elders were waiting on the bank. They always seemed to be waiting; I think they felt us coming the way they felt weather.

Sela, the eldest, the woman who had warned me that the Gate gives back everything including guilt, took one look at my face and then at the small canvas bag I carried, and she understood.

"You brought him home," she said.

"He asked for this." My voice cracked on the second word. "In his last letter. He wrote that if I ever found his trail, if I ever came down the river — he wanted his ashes here. Not with me. Here. He said this was the last place anyone knew him whole."

We carried him up the bank together, Sela and I, the canvas bag light as a bird's skeleton in my arms. Twenty years of a man reduced to something I could hold in two hands. Behind us Isandro hung back by the fire ring, respectful, hurting, letting me do this the way he'd learned to let me do everything on this journey — near enough to catch me, far enough to let me fall my own way.

The elders gathered in the longhouse as the sun went low. There were nine of them now; there had been eleven when we passed through heading upstream. The rope of memory grows shorter every year, Sela had told me once, and now the spring that fed it was gone forever, and these nine held the last of it.

They asked me to speak first.

"I didn't know him," I said, standing in the firelight with the bag in my lap. "That's the truth. I knew a father who left, and I built him out of anger, and the anger fit better than any real man would have. Then I read his journals and I met the people he'd helped and I found his camps and his mistakes and his shame, and I still didn't know him. But I know this: he crossed half the interior twice, sick and old and alone, trying to get back to a daughter he was too ashamed to face. And when he knew he was dying, he didn't send me gold. He sent me a map. He sent me the river." I looked around the circle of old faces. "He sent me here. So somebody would remember him out loud, because he couldn't bear to be remembered in silence."

Sela rose.

She was ninety-some years old and she stood like a young tree, and when she began to speak, the other elders joined her, voice by voice, a braided sound, a chorus of everything they carried.

"Tobin Hale came to us in the year the fish ran silver," Sela said. "He was forty-one and he had a scar on his left hand from a trap, and he laughed too loud at his own jokes because he was lonely and thought nobody noticed. We noticed."

"He fixed our roof beams and complained the whole time," said an old man whose name I never learned. "He complained beautifully. It was a kind of music."

"He drank from the cup we offered, diluted, a taste only, and he saw his life entire — the wife lost to fever, the baby buried in the coast town, the daughter growing up without him — and he wept for three days in the guest hut. And on the fourth day he asked us to let him forget."

My breath stopped.

"And we refused," Sela said gently, looking at me. "Because that is not our way. So instead he asked for something harder. He asked us to hold his memory for him. To keep him here, among us, so that somewhere in the world Tobias Hale was known — his jokes, his roof beams, his three days of weeping — even if his daughter grew up believing he was nothing. He said: let her hate a ghost. Let her build her own life free of the weight of mine. And he walked out of this village carrying nothing, and he never came back until he was ash."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the river ran on, remembering nothing.

"We kept him," Sela said. "Every elder since has spoken his name at every gathering, so that he would not vanish even from us. And now the spring is closed, and when we die, he goes with us. So we speak him one last time, aloud, complete, while the holding is still ours."

And they did. For an hour, maybe more, they told Tobin Hale back into the world. The scar on his hand. The songs he hummed off-key. The winter he traded his boots to a widow and walked barefoot in snow rather than see her children cold. The letter he wrote and never sent, which Sela recited word for word from decades of ceremonial keeping — a letter to me, written when I was nine years old, that said *I am not staying away because I don't love you. I am staying away because I love you badly, and you deserve better than bad.*

I cried the way I hadn't cried at the news of his death, or at his camp, or at his grave-less body. I cried like a girl with a father, which is what I was for one hour in a longhouse on a river that would never remember either of us.

At dawn we carried him down to the water. I opened the canvas bag at the landing and let the wind take him, and the ashes went out over the current — gray against gray, then gone — and the elders sang one verse more, quietly, and then it was finished.

Sela pressed her forehead to mine at the parting.

"The rope ends with us," she said. "But you are a knot in it, Maren Hale. Tie yourself to something living."

* * *

Renner's boat fell away at the fork below the village, turning toward the consortium's southern stations without a word exchanged, just a lifted hand from a hollowed man pointing himself home to a grief he'd bought and repurchased and could never put down again. I watched him shrink to a speck and then to nothing, and I felt no triumph. Only the strange mercy of seeing a wound acknowledged.

Isandro and I drifted the last days downstream through a changed country. The tributaries that had been dying on our way up were recovering — not from the Gate, but from rain beginning somewhere far north, the dry season breaking early, the river gathering itself from a hundred honest sources. Fish were running in the shallows. Birds we hadn't seen all journey came back to the banks as if summoned by the silence where the whispers used to be.

On the sixth night, anchored below the last gorge, Isandro sat across the fire from me and finally talked about the coast. About his sister. About the promise he broke and the twelve years of miles he'd flown to outrun it. Not all of it — some of it he'd drowned at the bottom of a basin of shattered stone along with my father's secret and Renner's wife and every other memory the river used to carry. But enough. Enough that when he reached across the fire and took my hand, it wasn't a pilot grabbing a passenger. It was a man choosing to stay within reach.

"Merrow's Landing has a decent airstrip," he said. "Bad coffee. Worse weather."

"Sounds terrible."

"It is. Completely." His thumb moved over my knuckles. "I'm thinking of being terrible there for a while. If you'll be terrible with me."

I looked at him — this man who had knelt at the water of everything he'd ever lost and chosen to break it instead of drink — and I thought about knots, and ropes, and tying myself to something living.

"I'll bring the maps," I said.

* * *

We rounded the final bend above Merrow's Landing on the ninth morning, and the town appeared the way towns do after the interior — impossibly small, impossibly safe, smoke rising straight from chimneys into windless air. The Kestrel sat on the strip where we'd left her, dust-caked, one cowled panel still open from the day we'd fled, looking less like a wreck than like a patient animal waiting to be called home.

There was a figure standing beside her.

Even at that distance I knew the posture — the squared shoulders, the hands jammed in pockets, the stance of someone who has been waiting a long time and intends to pretend otherwise. Isandro saw it too. I felt him go still beside me, felt the oar stop mid-stroke.

"Do you know her?" I asked.

Isandro was silent for three full strokes of the oar. When he answered, his voice was very quiet.

"She's my sister," he said. "And I have no idea how she found me — unless the river, even broken, keeps one last thing after all."

The current carried us toward the landing, toward the woman on the strip, toward whatever waited there — and Isandro Fairweather, who had run from the coast for twelve years, picked up the oars and rowed straight at it.
