## 21. Merrow's Landing

The Kestrel came home on a flatbed truck, lashed down with cargo straps that had last hauled diesel drums, and I stood in the yard of Merrow's Landing's one hangar and watched four men ease her off the bed like they were lowering a body into the ground.

She looked worse than I remembered. The gravel bar had torn open her belly, bent both props to uselessness, and the engine — my engine, the old radial I'd rebuilt twice with parts scavenged from three dead sisters — was a corroded fist of salt and grit where a heart used to be. Twelve years I'd kept her flying on stubbornness and baling wire. Now she sat in the tall grass behind Odile Brant's barge slip, and every time I walked past her I felt like a man visiting a hospital bed.

"You could buy new," Maren said. She'd taken to sitting on an upturned fuel drum outside the hangar while I worked, sketching the river in a battered notebook, her father's map folded inside the back cover like a bookmark she couldn't stop touching.

"No."

"You always answer like that."

"Because you keep asking questions with only one answer."

She smiled and went back to her drawing. That was the thing about Maren Hale I was still learning: she didn't push. She just stayed. Asked again tomorrow, or next week, patient as water working stone. Her father had been the same way, according to his journals — Tobin Hale had worn a canyon through the interior one season at a time, and he'd worn a canyon through his daughter too, slow and deep, and now she was learning to let the river run through it instead of damming it up.

I opened the cowling and started pulling cylinders.

* * *

Merrow's Landing in the wet season's first weeks was a town remembering how to breathe. The river had come back up after the dry season killed it to a brown trickle, and with the water came the barges, and with the barges came news, and the news was all about us.

I heard it secondhand at the Landing's single bar, which was really the front room of a woman named Petra Osei who sold beer warm and opinions colder. A survey crew from the consortium had come through asking questions, and Petra had told them exactly nothing, and then told everyone else everything.

"They wanted to know if you'd come back," she said, sliding me a beer I hadn't ordered. "Wanted to know what happened up there. Whether there was anything worth going back for."

"What'd you tell them?"

"Told them Fairweather crashed his plane and crawled out of the bush half-dead with a woman who looked like she could carry him." She shrugged. "Which is true as far as it goes."

Renner's men had left it at that. I understood why. Whatever Renner had seen at the Gate — whatever had emptied him out and filled him up in the same swallow — he wasn't talking, and neither were his people. The consortium's survey planes still crossed the high country some mornings, methodical as vultures, but they flew wider arcs now. They were looking for something that no longer existed, and somewhere in their ledgers the Rivergate had quietly become a line item marked *inconclusive*.

That suited me. Let them search. The spring was buried under forty tons of collapsed shelf rock, the basin drowned, the aquifer sealed. The river ran free and dumb and memoryless, the way rivers are supposed to run. Some nights I woke up sure I could hear the voices that used to live in that water — my sister's voice among them, saying things I'd never get to hear again — and some nights I woke up grateful. Most nights it was both at once, which I figured was as honest as a man gets.

Petra leaned on the bar. "You staying, then? Through the wet?"

"I don't know yet."

"Man fixes a plane for six weeks without flying it, he knows something he isn't saying." She took my empty glass before I'd finished it. "First one's free. Second one costs double, because you're famous now."

* * *

The reckoning I owed wasn't to Renner or the consortium. It was to a man named Elias Wren, who held my fuel contract, my strip rights, and — because frontier economies run on favors the way engines run on oil — most of my debts.

He came to the hangar on a Tuesday, a small round man in a clean shirt, and stood looking at the Kestrel's opened guts for a long time before speaking.

"Corvus Renner called me in September," he said. "Told me if I kept selling you avgas, the consortium would buy my whole operation out from under me and sell it back at rates I couldn't survive. Told me your contracts were done." He picked a wrench off my workbench, weighed it, set it down. "I told him to go to hell. Cost me two months of nervous stomach, but here we are."

"Elias—"

"He also offered me money for information about you. Where you went, who you flew, whether you'd ever mentioned a source on the upper river." Wren finally looked at me. "I want you to know I told him nothing, and I want you to know why I'm telling you this now instead of never: because I nearly did take his money. Winter before last, when your account ran thin. So we're square, you and me, and I'd thank you not to make a speech about loyalty."

"No speech," I said.

"Good." He turned to go, then stopped. "The girl. Maren Hale. She's been mapping the watershed for the villages downstream. Odile says she's good at it. Says the elders trust her." A pause. "Your account's current, by the way. Has been since you got back. I don't know how that happened and I've stopped asking."

After he left I sat under the Kestrel's wing for a long time, listening to the rain start on the tin roof, thinking about how many people had spent something small and real to keep me flying, and how long I'd mistaken solitude for self-sufficiency when it was really just debt I hadn't bothered counting.

* * *

Six weeks into the rebuild, the engine went back together.

Not the same engine. Two cylinders were beyond saving, and the crankcase had a hairline fracture I ground out and welded by lamplight over three nights, hands shaking, because welding a crankcase is either craft or blasphemy depending on whether it holds. The replacement parts came off a wrecked transport Odile found beached on a slough forty miles downriver — she towed the hulk to the Landing herself, grinning the whole way, refusing payment except for a promise that when the Kestrel flew again, I'd ferry her trade goods upstream for a season.

"Fair price," I said.

"Cheap price," she said. "You're a legend now, pilot. Legends work for stories, not money."

The morning she fired — I'd decided the Kestrel was a she the day I bought her and never examined why — Maren stood beside me at the hangar doors and the radial caught on the third blade and settled into that ragged, beautiful lope, and smoke rolled out of the stacks smelling of scorched oil and new life, and Maren grabbed my arm hard enough to leave marks.

"She sounds angry," she shouted over the roar.

"She sounds alive. Same thing."

We taxied her to the strip and I ran her up full throttle against the chocks and she pulled so hard the tail lifted and I had to haul her back down, laughing, rain in my face, twelve years of miles falling off me like mud off a boot.

Maren stood in the grass watching me laugh, and something changed in her face — not softening exactly. Recognition. Like she was seeing the man underneath the pilot for the first time, the one who existed between flights.

* * *

That night she came to the hangar with two beers and her notebook and sat on the workbench while I cleaned tools, and after a while she said, "When are you leaving?"

I wiped down a socket wrench that didn't need it. "Who says I'm leaving?"

"The plane's fixed. You're a pilot. Pilots leave. It's the whole architecture of you." She wasn't accusing. She was stating weather. "I've watched you look at that strip every morning like a dog watches a door."

"And you're asking because you want me to go?"

"I'm asking because I need to know which version of this I'm living in." She set the beer down. "The version where I'm a passenger, or the version where I'm a destination."

The rain picked up on the roof. Somewhere down the street Petra's generator coughed and caught. I put the wrench away and made myself stand still, which has never been my talent.

"When I was nineteen," I said, "my sister Liora and I had a plan. Coast town, both of us. She was going to school. I was going to fly mail runs until we'd saved enough for her to finish, and then—" I stopped. Started again. "There was a storm coming and a doctor needed getting to a settlement upriver and I took the flight because it was my job, my duty, all the words men use when they want to feel noble about leaving. The river came up while I was gone. She went out to secure the boat because I wasn't there to do it." I looked at my hands. "I've told this story maybe three times in twelve years. Each time it gets shorter. This is the longest version."

Maren didn't move. Didn't reach for me. Just stayed, the way she stayed, which I was beginning to understand was its own kind of reaching.

"At the Gate," I said, "the water would have given her back. Every argument, every bad joke, the sound of her voice. Everything I traded for a job well done. And I knelt at the edge of it and I wanted it so badly I couldn't breathe." I finally looked at her. "And then I broke it anyway. Not because I'm noble. Because if that water existed, men like Renner would own it, and owning memory is worse than losing it. But I won't pretend the sacrifice was clean. Part of me died on that rock shelf and part of me was relieved."

"Isandro—"

"Here's what I know now. I spent twelve years outrunning a night I can't outrun, and the running felt like freedom because I never stayed anywhere long enough to find out it was fear." I stepped closer. "I'm not promising you permanence, Maren. I don't believe in promises that big anymore. They're just debts with better handwriting. What I'm telling you is that tomorrow morning I'm going to wake up in this town, and the morning after that, and I'm going to fly the medicine run on Thursday and be back Friday night, and I'm going to keep being here until one of us decides otherwise — freely, out loud, no ambushes. Presence. That's the whole offer. It's smaller than forever and it's harder than leaving and it's mine."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she opened her notebook to the last page, tore it out, and handed it to me. It was a map — hand-drawn, careful, the watershed below Merrow's Landing rendered in her precise lines — and in the corner, where cartographers mark their datum, she'd written two names. Mine and hers. No date. No claim. Just coordinates.

"That's my whole offer too," she said. "Presence. Measured in miles, if you insist, but with somewhere to land."

I kissed her, and the rain hammered the roof, and the Kestrel ticked and cooled in the dark behind us like a heart slowing after effort, and neither of us said anything we'd have to take back later, because we hadn't promised anything except tomorrow.

* * *

Thursday I flew the medicine run. Vaccines and sutures and a crate of antibiotics for the village at Two Forks, plus a letter Odile's sister had been waiting six weeks to send. The river ran high and brown below me, fat with the wet season, braiding and rebraiding through country that no longer held any secret worth killing for. At the mouth of the gorge I tipped a wing — habit, or tribute, I couldn't tell you — and the wind through the stone made its old whispering sound, and for the first time it sounded like nothing at all. Just wind. Just rock. Just geography.

I was back before dark Friday, as promised.

Maren met me at the strip with her notebook and news: the elders' council at the forgetting village had asked her to come back for the whole wet season, to help them record — in ink, on paper, the ordinary mortal kind of memory — the names of their dead before the oldest of them went. She wanted to go. She wanted me to fly her.

"There's a catch," she said, climbing onto the wing strut while I tied down. "They'll ask you to stay for the ceremony. The one where they speak the names aloud. All night."

"All night."

"It's long. There's food. You'll be bored out of your mind by midnight and moved past words by dawn, if Odile's description holds."

I looked at her — rain-damp, certain, holding out a future the size of one weekend — and felt the old restlessness stir and settle, stir and settle, like an engine finding its idle.

"Fuel's on Elias," I said. "Tell them Fairweather's coming."

She grinned and dropped into the seat beside me, and I checked the mags, and the Kestrel answered, and together we pointed her nose at the wide gray water and the long unremarkable miles ahead.
