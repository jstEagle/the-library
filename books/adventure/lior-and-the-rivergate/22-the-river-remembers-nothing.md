## 22. The River Remembers Nothing

The Kestrel came out of the east at first light, riding the long gold band of morning that pooled along the river like spilled honey. Isandro had her trimmed out level, hands loose on the yoke, and for the first time in a year — maybe for the first time in twelve years — he wasn't flying away from anything.

Below him, the watershed unrolled in its new spring greens. The river ran high and loud with snowmelt, braiding through country that had spent a season drinking. He could see the flood marks on the banks from last year's wet summer, the one everyone said was the wettest in living memory, though living memory around here had gotten shorter lately. That was fine by him. Some things were better forgotten.

He keyed the radio. "Merrow's Landing, Kestrel, ten miles out, inbound with the medicine run."

Static, then Odile's voice, dry as driftwood: *"Kestrel, Merrow's Landing. You're late."*

"I'm early. Your clock's wrong."

*"My clock keeps better time than your promises, Fairweather. Come on down before the wind comes up the valley. And tell Maren the village elders are asking for her again. Third strip this week wants their maps updated."*

"Copy all." He grinned at nothing. "Tell them she'll be along when she's along."

"She's already along," Odile said, and there was something in her voice — amusement, maybe, or affection wearing amusement's clothes. "She beat you downriver two days ago. She's at the landing now, arguing with the schoolteacher about where the tributary actually forks."

Isandro laughed out loud in the cockpit, alone at four thousand feet, and it felt good to hear his own voice do that.

A year. Twelve months since he'd stood at the edge of a basin of impossible water with a detonator in his fist and every lost thing in his life waiting for him just below the surface. Twelve months since Renner had drunk and collapsed into the whole weight of what he'd sold away, and Isandro had chosen the future over the past and brought the rock shelf down on both.

Some nights he still dreamed about it. Not about the water — never about the water — but about the moment before, kneeling at the brink, hearing his sister Liora's voice in the air the way he'd heard it in the gorge, clear as a radio call. *Come find me, Sandro.* In the dreams he always knelt a little longer than he had in life. In the dreams, sometimes, he reached out and touched it.

Then he'd wake with Maren's breathing beside him, steady as an engine at cruise, and the dream would lose its grip. That was the trade he'd made. He'd stopped pretending he didn't understand the price. He just didn't regret paying it.

The strip at Merrow's Landing rose up to meet him — gravel, a little longer than it used to be because Maren had surveyed the extension herself and bullied three villages into sending labor. He flared the Kestrel onto the downslope, touched smooth, rolled out past the windsock, and shut her down in the shade of the fuel shack where the morning hadn't reached yet.

Maren was waiting at the edge of the strip, arms crossed, surveying tripod slung over one shoulder like a rifle. She didn't wave. She never waved. She just stood there with that look — the one that was half challenge, half welcome, and entirely hers — until he climbed down and walked over.

"You're late," she said.

"Odile says my clock's wrong."

"Odile's right. Your clock's been wrong since I met you." But she kissed him anyway, brief and sure, tasting of coffee and pencil shavings, and when she pulled back she tapped the fuselage where a fresh patch of aluminum gleamed over the old engine-ingestion scar. "How's she running?"

"Purring."

"Liar. She's got a hiccup above six thousand. I felt it through the seat when you landed."

He looked at her. She looked back, unrepentant. A year ago she'd never been in a small plane; now she could diagnose his engine by feel through the airframe. Somewhere along the mapping runs and the medicine hauls and the long slow rebuild of everything they'd broken or burned, she'd become the best backcountry pilot-adjacent mind on the river, and she'd done it without ever asking him to teach her. She just absorbed things. Like the watershed. Like him.

"Hiccup above six thousand," he conceded. "Probably a fouled plug. I'll check it tonight."

"Tonight," she repeated, and there was a weight under the word that had nothing to do with spark plugs. "Good. Because tonight we're eating at Odile's, and the elders from Serrano Bend are coming down on the barge, and they want to talk about the upper tributary."

"The one that dried up?"

"The one that *came back*." Her eyes went bright in a way he knew well by now — the way they lit when the river surprised her, which was often, because the river had been surprising everyone since the Gate died. "It ran dry for thirty years, Isandro. The old-timers all said so. Now it's running again, cold and clean, and nobody can say why. Except—" she lowered her voice, though there was no one within a hundred yards but gulls, "—except maybe we can."

He knew what she meant. When he'd blown the shelf, the basin hadn't just drained. It had *released*. All that water that had sat sealed under the rock for who knew how long, memory-soaked and motionless, had gone somewhere. Down. Into the aquifer. Into the ground itself. And the ground, apparently, had decided to give some of it back — not as miracle water, not as anything anyone could bottle or dam or sell, but as ordinary springs, seeping up in low places, feeding streams that had been dead since before Maren was born.

The consortium's geologists had come through twice trying to figure it out. They'd left with samples that tested as nothing but clean water and theories that amounted to nothing but guesses. Whatever the Gate had been, it wasn't anymore. It was geology now. It was rumor. It was a story pilots told each other at fuel stops, and every year the story got vaguer, and every year fewer people believed it, and that was exactly how Isandro wanted it.

"No map leads to it," he said. It had become something between a toast and a prayer between them.

"No map leads to it," Maren agreed. Then, because she was Maren: "Although technically the watershed survey covers—"

"*No map,*" he said, and she laughed and let it go, mostly.

* * *

They ate at Odile's that evening — the long table out back under the strung lights, the whole ragged family of the river gathered: Odile presiding with her barge captain's forearms crossed, the schoolteacher still grumbling about fork locations, two nurses off the medical circuit, a hydrologist from the coast who'd come up to study the returning tributaries and stayed because the food was better than his marriage. The elders from Serrano Bend sat at the head, old men and women with faces like river stones, and they talked late.

What they wanted was simple. The upper tributary was running. Their grandchildren were going to move up there, build again on land their families had abandoned a generation ago. They needed the channel mapped, the flood lines marked, a strip cleared for the medicine plane. They needed the future drawn onto paper so they could live inside it.

"We can have a preliminary survey done in three weeks," Maren said, sketching on the back of a supply manifest. "Full mapping by end of dry season. Isandro can fly the line for the strip — there's a bench of flat ground on the north bank, I saw it on the aerials."

"You've been looking at aerials of a river that came back last month," Isandro said.

"I've been looking at aerials of a river that came back last month," she agreed, "because someone has to know where things are before people need them. That's the whole job. That's the whole *point*." She looked around the table. "Tobin Hale drew maps his whole life and never once drew one for anybody who needed it. Everything he charted, he charted for himself. I'm going to spend the rest of mine doing the opposite."

Nobody argued. Nobody ever argued with Maren when she got that tone. Isandro had learned that in the first week and had built a life around respecting it.

Later, walking back along the darkened strip with the lantern light shrinking behind them, she took his hand without looking at it, the way you take a door handle in your own house.

"The elders asked me something today," she said. "At the head of the table, when you were getting dessert. Old Ama Sefu. She asked if I'd ever drunk from the returning springs."

Isandro's step slowed. "And?"

"I told her no." Maren watched the stars over the black line of the ridges. "She said that was wise. She said the water that comes back isn't the Gate — it's just water — but the *idea* of it travels, and ideas are stronger than water. She said people will start making pilgrimages eventually. Claiming the springs do things. Selling vials of it in the coastal cities within five years, probably." She snorted. "Ama Sefu thinks like a consortium agent. It's terrifying."

"What did you say to her?"

"I said that's why the survey matters. If we map the watershed honestly — every spring, every fork, every dead channel coming back — then the map itself becomes the true story. Better than rumor. Boring, even. Nobody makes pilgrimages to a boring map." She glanced sideways at him. "Your father would have hated it. Mine too, probably. Two men who needed the interior to stay mysterious."

"One of them's learning better," Isandro said. "Late, but learning."

They reached the hangar — his hangar now, leased from the village council at a rate Odile called criminal and Isandro called fair — and he rolled the doors open and lit the work lamp and pulled the Kestrel's cowl while Maren sat on a crate with the survey notes, reading aloud to him the way she did sometimes, the numbers and contour lines flowing over him like a second engine's hum. He found the fouled plug, cleaned it, gapped it, torqued it. Above them the hangar rafters held swallows' nests, and the swallows muttered in their sleep.

"Isandro." Her voice changed. He looked up from the engine. "The letter came today. While you were flying."

His hands went still on the wrench.

Not many letters found their way upriver. The ones that did were usually bills or grief. He set down the wrench and wiped his hands and held them out, and she gave him the envelope — cheap paper, coastal postmark, his name typed by someone who didn't know him.

He opened it standing under the work lamp with Maren beside him and read it twice.

It was from a law office in the capital. Short. Formal. It said that the estate of Corvus Renner had been settled, and that a bequest had been made, contingent on certain conditions, to one Isandro Fairweather, pilot, of Merrow's Landing.

Renner. Dead eight months now — they'd heard it thirdhand, some failure of the heart or the head, the obituary vague in the way obituaries are vague about men whose lives had a hole in the middle. The man who'd chased them up the river, who'd drunk from the Gate and drowned in his own total recall, who'd stood hollow-eyed at the end and let them go because, he'd said, he finally remembered why any of it mattered and none of it was this.

"He left you something?" Maren said quietly.

Isandro read the conditions aloud. The bequest was money — enough money to buy the hangar outright, to buy a second plane, to fund the survey for a decade. Enough to change the shape of everything they were building. And the condition was a single sentence, dictated by Renner before his death:

*To be released only upon proof that the source remains unmapped.*

Maren read it twice more. Then she started to laugh — a low, disbelieving sound that cracked into something else halfway through.

"He bought memories once," she said. "Spent half his life buying back what the Gate took. And at the end, the only thing he wanted to buy was *absence*. An empty space on the map." She shook her head. "He's testing us. From beyond the grave, the man is still trying to buy the river."

"Or he's trusting us," Isandro said. "Last thing he said to us was that he remembered why it mattered. Maybe this is what remembering looked like, for him."

Outside, far up the valley, thunder muttered — the wet season gathering itself somewhere beyond the ridges, months away still, but patient. The swallows shifted in the rafters. Maren took the letter from his hand, folded it along its creases, precise as she was with everything, and looked at him with the expression she wore when a decision was already made and she was merely informing the world.

"So we prove it," she said. "We finish the survey. We publish every fork, every spring, every channel — and we leave exactly one blank space on the map, in the high country, labeled *unsurveyed, seasonal flows unreliable*." She smiled, and it was Tobin Hale's smile, the mapmaker's smile, turned honest at last. "The most truthful lie ever drawn. No map leads to it. Not even ours."

Isandro looked at her — at this woman who had turned a dead man's map into a living one, who had taken the worst inheritance a father ever left and spent it building futures for strangers — and felt the old restlessness rise in him one final time, the twelve-year itch of miles and leaving, and felt it settle. Not die. Settle. The way a plane settles onto a strip when you get the flare exactly right: wheels kissing earth, momentum becoming stillness, stillness becoming home.

"A second plane," he said. "I could train a couple of the village kids to fly the med runs. Stay closer in."

"You? Grounded?"

"Not grounded." He pulled her in against the warm metal of the Kestrel's cowl, under the muttering swallows, with the letter in her pocket and the blank space on the future map. "Landed. There's a difference. Took me twenty years of miles to learn it."

She kissed him, and the work lamp swung gently on its cord, and the river ran on in the dark below the strip, carrying everything toward the sea except the one thing they'd given it forever to keep.

Above the hangar, the stars came out thick over the watershed — over the mapped channels and the unmapped one, over the springs that remembered nothing and the people who chose to remember everything themselves, out loud, together, one season at a time.

The Kestrel ticked as she cooled. Tomorrow there would be fuel to haul and elders to ferry and a tributary to chart. Tonight, for the first time in a life measured in miles, Isandro Fairweather was exactly where he was going.
