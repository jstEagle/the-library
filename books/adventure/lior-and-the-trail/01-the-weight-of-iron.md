## 1. The Weight of Iron

The theodolite's bubble sat dead center, and Mara Lockridge trusted nothing else in her life half so much.

She stood at the tripod on a shelf of granite two hundred feet above the Kettle River gorge, one eye to the scope, and let the instrument do what instruments did best: tell the truth without flinching. Below her, the morning fog was burning off the water in long silver ribbons. Above her, the spur they called Cinder Ridge climbed in broken steps of basalt and scree toward a skyline that had no business hosting a railroad and knew it.

"Reading," she said.

Hobbs, her chainman, crouched over the stadia rod fifty yards down the slope with his boots planted wide against the grade. "Eleven point six," he called back, voice flattened by wind. "Marked."

Mara wrote it down in the field book in her small, exact hand — the hand her father had drilled into her when she was nine years old, copying figures by lamplight while other children slept. *A number you can't read back is a number you never had*, he used to say. He'd been a section foreman for thirty-one years and died with creosote in his lungs and a pension that wouldn't have covered his coffin if the company hadn't quietly waived it, which was as close to tenderness as the Atlantic & Western ever came.

She loved the railroad anyway. That was the thing nobody understood — not the men who worked for her, not the superintendents who signed her pay vouchers, not Eli, least of all Eli. She loved it the way some people loved a difficult parent: knowing every flaw, loving it precisely because it was real. Iron rails did not lie. A curve laid at four degrees ran at four degrees or it threw the train into the river. The work rewarded honesty with survival and punished vanity with wreckage, and after eleven years of living out of survey camps and boarding-house rooms from the Coeur d'Alenes to the high desert, Mara had come to arrange her entire soul around that single dependable fact.

Everything else lied. The rails didn't.

"Again," she said, because Hobbs had shifted his weight and she wanted the reading clean.

He sighed the sigh of a man who had been re-measuring things since dawn, but he reset the rod, and she took it again, and it agreed within a tenth of a foot, and only then did she close the book.

* * *

By noon the fog had burned away entirely and the lie had arrived.

It came up the switchback trail in a cloud of dust behind a buckboard, wearing a linen duster too clean for the mountains and a smile that had been practiced in hotel mirrors. Deacon Pruitt, right-of-way agent for the Atlantic & Western's Mountain Division, dismounted before the wagon fully stopped, which told Mara everything about how badly he wanted to seem like a man of action.

"Miss Lockridge." He removed his hat, revealing hair oiled flat against a scalp pink with sunburn. "You're a hard woman to find."

"I'm exactly where my map says I am," Mara said. She didn't offer her hand. Her fingers were gray with graphite and granite dust, and she kept them that way on principle around men like Pruitt, who read clean hands as availability and dirty ones as an invitation to comment.

"So I see." He turned a slow circle, taking in the camp: two wall tents, the cook fire banked to coals, Hobbs and young Ferro stacking the range poles, the theodolite under its canvas hood like a sleeping bird of prey. His gaze snagged on the field book open on the folding table beside her transit case, and Mara watched him look at it the way a card sharp looks at another man's deck.

"Cinder Ridge," Pruitt said. "What's the word?"

"The word is eleven-point-six percent average grade over the first mile, with a maximum sustained grade of thirteen-four if we cut the shelf at full height." She said it evenly, the way she'd say the time of day. "The word is that the formation is fractured basalt sitting on a clay seam, and the clay seam weeps. Every cut we make will need timbering the whole length, and even then—" she tapped the field book, "—I'd want a drainage plan before I'd certify the alignment."

Pruitt's smile held its shape the way wet paper holds its shape. "And the cost per mile?"

"Higher than the division will want to hear."

"Miss Lockridge." He said her name gently, the way a man gentles a horse he intends to sell. "May I be frank?"

"You may be anything you like. Frank costs extra."

Behind him, Hobbs snorted and turned it into a cough. Pruitt ignored it with the discipline of long practice. He walked to the edge of the shelf instead, hands clasped behind him, and contemplated the gorge below as though the view were the reason he'd driven three hours up a road that wasn't fit for the buckboard.

"Do you know what happens to a division that misses its construction window?" he asked the horizon. "The bondholders start asking questions. The directors start asking questions. And the men who asked those questions start looking for someone to blame, and it is never, ever the man who signed the original estimate." He turned back to her. "It's the engineer who certified the route. The engineer whose numbers made the estimate possible."

"My numbers didn't make any estimate possible," Mara said. "My numbers said this spur was marginal last spring, when I flagged it in my preliminary report. If somebody in Denver built a budget on hope, that's not a surveying problem."

"No." Pruitt picked up her field book from the table — picked it up, actually lifted it, his thumb sliding along the page edge — and Mara felt something cold move through her chest that had nothing to do with the altitude. "But it could become a surveying problem. Or it could become a surveying solution. It depends entirely on how the final figures are written."

"Put the book down."

He did, eventually, and set it down carefully, aligned with the table's edge, which was somehow worse than if he'd tossed it. "There's a method," he said, conversational now, a man explaining the weather. "It's been used on every difficult line this company has built in twenty years. You carry your grades to the second decimal. You round your earthwork quantities down. You assume the rock fractures favorably — 'favorable fracture,' it's a standard phrase, it appears in textbooks. You note that seasonal seepage is expected to diminish. And when the line is built and the grades run heavy, why, the engines simply take them slower, and everyone agrees the mountain was harder than anyone guessed, and no one goes to jail, because no one broke a law. They just wrote optimistic numbers." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which made it worse. "Optimism, Miss Lockridge, is not fraud."

"It is when you know better."

"Everyone knows better. That's what makes it work."

* * *

She made him eat lunch at the cook fire before she answered, because she wanted the time and she wanted witnesses, and because a man who has eaten a camp meal with you finds it slightly harder to pretend afterward that you are a machine rather than a person. This was strategy, and she knew it, and she was honest enough with herself to know it was also loneliness wearing strategy's coat. Eleven years on the road. Eleven years of camps where the only conversations were about grades and gauge and whose turn it was to haul water. She had a trunk of letters from Eli she'd stopped answering two years ago, and a father's grave in a rail town she couldn't afford the fare to visit, and a reputation that traveled ahead of her like weather — *Lockridge, the woman engineer, precise as a watch, cold as one too* — and none of it, none of it, was a life. It was a career. She had confused the two for a long time, and the confusion had been comfortable, and comfort, she was beginning to suspect, was just armor that had rusted onto the body underneath.

Pruitt ate the beans and salt pork and complimented neither, and when the plates were cleared he leaned forward with his elbows on his knees.

"Denver wants the spur," he said. "Not wants — needs. There's ore traffic contracted, there's a lumber syndicate waiting on rates, there are men on the board whose names are on those contracts personally. If the spur dies, the Mountain Division dies, and if the Mountain Division dies—" he spread his hands, "—well. Divisions die, Miss Lockridge. Engineers get reassigned. To places like the Nebraska prairie, where the most interesting thing a surveyor sees all year is a fence post. You've been on the road eleven years. You must be tired of camps."

The wind came up the gorge and moved the fog-remnants through the pines, and somewhere below, the Kettle River ran loud and indifferent over rocks that had been refusing to move for ten thousand years.

"I'll tell you what I'll do," Mara said. "I'll write the report exactly as the ground gave it to me. Every figure. Every seep. Every fracture. And I'll write a recommendation section that lays out three options — abandon, tunnel, or build with full timbering and drainage at the true cost — and I'll let Denver choose with their eyes open. That's not optimism and it's not pessimism. That's engineering."

Pruitt looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something for him, because he nodded slowly and stood and brushed off his trousers.

"You know," he said, almost kindly, "a man in your position would have found a way to split the difference. Most of them do. It's practically the job description." He put his hat back on. "I'll be in Kestrel Falls through the end of the week, if you reconsider. The Falls is a dying little place, by the way — mines played out, half the men gone. Sad country. But the hotel has real coffee." He paused at the trailhead, one boot on the first switchback. "One more thing. There's a prospector been hanging around the lower valley, name of Craw. Amos Craw. Used to be one of ours, years back, before he went strange. If he comes up here bothering your crew, run him off. He's got ideas about these headwaters that don't match the company's, and ideas like his have a way of getting written down."

Mara said nothing. Pruitt took the silence for agreement, tipped his hat, and started down the switchbacks, and the dust of the buckboard rose behind him like a small dishonest cloud.

* * *

That evening she sat outside her tent with the field book on her knees and the camp quiet around her — Hobbs and Ferro asleep, the cook fire down to a red eye, the stars out in the brutal clarity of altitude. She reread the day's figures. Eleven-point-six. Thirteen-four. The clay seam, the weep, the fracture planes running wrong for a stable cut. The numbers were good. The numbers were true. And she sat there holding them and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, exactly what they were going to cost her.

Because Pruitt was right about one thing, and she hated him for it: the system did not punish the man who built the budget on hope. It punished the surveyor whose honest figures made the hope impossible. She had watched it happen to a bridge engineer on the Deschutes line, a decent man named Okafor who refused to sign off on pilings driven into river gravel, and who spent the next six years doing drainage surveys in a district so flat the water had nowhere to go either. She had told herself then that she would be different — smarter, more careful, more valuable, too precise to discard. Competence as a shield. She had believed it the way you believe things at thirty that you stop believing at thirty-five.

Her pencil hovered over the recommendation page. Three options, she'd promised him. Abandon, tunnel, or build true. She wrote them out, each with its cost, each with its risk, in her father's small exact hand, and when she finished she closed the book and tied it with the cord and set it inside the transit case, and latched the case, and sat with her hand resting on the leather a moment longer than the act required.

Below her in the dark, the Kettle River went on talking to itself, patient as anything that has already won.

She was nearly asleep when she heard the footsteps — not from the direction of the trail, but from upstream, along the water, uneven, dragging, the walk of a man whose body had been arguing with the mountains for decades and losing slowly. She came out of the tent with the lantern lit and found him at the edge of the firelight: an old man, gray-bearded, one arm cradled against his chest like something broken, his clothes stiff with river mud and his eyes burning with a fever that had clearly been riding him for days.

"Lockridge," he rasped. "Surveyor Lockridge. They said up the valley there was a surveyor named Lockridge." He swayed, caught himself on a pine, and held out his other hand — and in it, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with handling, was a rolled packet of paper tied with sinew. "Got something needs carrying to Kestrel Falls. Before the snows." His legs folded under him like a dropped tent, and Mara lunged forward and caught him before the firelight lost him entirely.

His skin was furnace-hot. His eyes found hers and held on with the desperate grip of a man handing off a weight he can no longer stand beneath.

"You'll take it," he said. Not a question. "Swear it."
