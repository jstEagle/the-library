## 2. A Stranger's Bargain

The man came out of the timber at dusk, the way trouble always did — quietly, and then all at once.

Mara Lockridge heard him before she saw him: a dragging footstep in the pine duff, a wet rattle of breath, and then the soft metallic clink of something heavy swinging against a belt buckle. She set down her transit notes, capped her ink bottle against the wind, and stood with one hand on the tent pole, listening. Three weeks in the high country taught a surveyor to catalog sounds the way she cataloged bearings. This one did not belong. Elk moved heavier. Wind moved softer. A man moving like that was either dying or dangerous, and sometimes both.

He stepped into the firelight and she had her answer.

He was sixty if he was a day, built like a fence post left too long in the weather, with a beard gone white at the chin and gray everywhere else, matted with burrs and pine pitch. His coat had been good once — canvas, double-stitched, the kind outfitters sold to men who expected their clothes to outlast them — but it hung off him now like bark off a dead birch. One hand pressed against his side, and even in the failing light Mara could see the dark stain spreading between his fingers, slow as sap.

"Evening," he said, and his voice was steadier than his legs were, which told her he'd been practicing the word for some miles. "Saw your smoke."

"You saw right," Mara said. "Come sit before you fall down."

"I'll stand." He said it the way other men swore oaths. Then his knees made the decision for him, and he sat down hard on the flat rock beside her fire ring, exhaling through his teeth.

Mara had worked enough camps to know the choreography. She put the kettle on, dug the good whiskey from her gear box — the bottle she saved for celebrating completed lines and drowning failed ones — and poured two fingers into a tin cup. He took it without thanks, drank half, and held the rest in both hands like something he meant to study rather than swallow.

"Name's Craw," he said. "Amos Craw."

"Lockridge. Mara Lockridge. I'm running a line for the Cascade & Northern." She nodded toward the stacked stakes at the edge of camp, the transit case, the rolled profiles under oilcloth. "You're a long way from anywhere, Mr. Craw."

"That's generally where I aim to be." He almost smiled. It cost him something; she watched him pay it. "You're the engineer? The one they say runs her numbers clean?"

Mara went still. Out here, forty miles past the last telegraph office, a stranger knowing her name and her reputation was not an accident. "Who says that?"

"Folks talk. Railroads talk louder." He turned the cup slowly. "They say when Lockridge gives a grade, the grade is true. That she won't fudge a cut or bury a bad bearing, not for a superintendent, not for a bonus, not for anything." His eyes came up to hers, and they were pale gray, the color of river water over slate, and very tired. "Is that so?"

"It's so," she said. "It's also why I've been passed over twice for chief engineer."

"Reckon it is." He drank the rest of the whiskey. Then he pulled his hand away from his side, and in the firelight Mara saw the wound properly — a long tear low across the belly, crudely stitched with sinew, weeping through the cloth. Not fresh. Days old. The kind of hurt a body fought for a while and lost to eventually.

"You need a doctor," she said.

"Need a lot of things." He said it mildly, the way a man reads a list of items he has no money to buy. "Doctor's six days behind me and I'm not going back to meet him."

"What happened?"

"Horse fell. Rockslide took the trail out from under us both." He paused, and Mara understood, in the way one understands things said plainly by people who have decided not to lie anymore, that there was more and that he would not be giving it. "Doesn't matter now. What matters is what I carried out."

* * *

She got him into her spare blanket roll inside the tent, propped against her gear chest because lying flat made him cough blood-flecked froth that frightened them both. She cleaned and re-dressed the wound with carbolic from her kit, working by lantern light while he watched her hands with an appraiser's attention.

"You've done this before," he said.

"On crews. Men get hurt around iron and powder." She tied off the bandage. "You should eat. There's beans and salt pork."

"In a minute." He reached beneath his coat, slow, showing her his empty palm first the way an honest man does, and drew out an oilskin packet tied with waxed cord. It was the size of a prayer book and worn soft at the corners. "This is what matters."

He unwrapped it on his knee. Inside was a map.

Not a company map — Mara knew those the way she knew her own signature, every convention and abbreviation, the ruled borders and stamped title blocks. This was drawn freehand on what looked like the backs of two ledger sheets, sewn together with thread, ink faded brown in places, fresh black in others. It showed a river system she recognized at once, because she'd spent three years fighting its lower reaches: the Silverthread, running down out of country no railroad had ever surveyed, through a gorge marked only with a warning in cramped capitals — NO LINE THROUGH HERE. TRUST ME. Above the gorge, the headwaters spread across the sheet in detail that made her breath catch: tributaries, elevations, a spring source circled twice and annotated in a hand gone shaky — THE WHOLE VALLEY DRINKS FROM HERE.

And there were ore markings. Dozens of them. Careful, methodical, dated across eleven years.

"This is the upper Silverthread," she said. "Nobody's mapped that. The government sheets just say *unexplored*."

"Somebody's mapped it." Amos Craw laid his cracked fingers on the paper with a tenderness she had seen men give only to children and instruments. "Eleven summers. Every bearing checked twice. I was a surveyor before I was a prospector — company man, ten years, Cascade & Northern same as you." Her head came up sharply, and he nodded. "That's right. Same road, different milepost. I ran preliminary lines up the north fork until I found what they wanted and wouldn't say so."

"Found what?"

"A body of ore big enough to hang a railroad spur on. Big enough to make a district." He coughed, wet and deep, and waited it out with his eyes closed. When he opened them again they were clearer, burning. "Big enough that when my report came in honest, they sent a second man to check it, and when his came in honest too, they stopped asking for reports and started asking questions about my loyalty. So I went into the hills and stayed there, and I kept mapping, because a map is the one thing a man can finish."

Mara looked at the map, and then at the man, and felt the cold arithmetic of it settle into place. A prospector driven out for honest numbers. An ore body in unsurveyed country. And somewhere below, in boardrooms lit by electric light, men who would pay a great deal to know exactly what this oilskin packet contained.

"There are people looking for you," she said. It wasn't a question.

"There are people looking for *this*." He folded the map along its old creases with great care. "Two of them crossed my trail four days back. We disagreed about whether I should keep carrying it." He touched his bandaged side. "I won the argument. Barely."

"And they'll come here."

"They'll come wherever the trail says a stranger went. They're thorough." He said it without drama, a fact among facts. "Which brings me to my bargain, Miss Lockridge, and I'll ask your pardon for the state of my manners, because I don't have time for manners."

"I haven't agreed to any bargain."

"No," he agreed. "But you will. Because you run your numbers clean, and I'm about to show you the number that matters most." He held up one finger. "Snow. First snows up here come early October, sometimes late September. After that, Kestrel Falls is shut off till thaw — no rider gets through the pass, no letter, nothing. Whatever happens to this valley happens before the snows, or it doesn't happen at all."

"Kestrel Falls," she repeated. "River town, below the gorge. What does it have to do with me?"

"Everything." He pressed the oilskin packet into her hands. His grip was still strong; the strength was the last thing to go in men like him, and it frightened her more than the blood had. "There's a woman there — Ada Voss, she wears a star. Tell her Amos Craw kept his promise, and this is his promise kept. She'll know. There are papers in the town records, filings, dates — she'll understand what the map means better than anyone alive, because she's been fighting the concession alone for two years and losing."

"The concession," Mara said slowly. "The Silverthread mining concession. The one the papers say expires next spring."

"Expires," Amos said, and laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough again, worse than before. When it passed he sagged against the chest, gray-lipped, and spoke in a whisper now, conserving everything. "That's the story they let the newspapers print. Here's the truth, engineer, and you can verify every word of it in the county filings: the permits carry a feasibility clause. If no certified engineer files a survey declaring the route workable within the term, the whole concession lapses. Forever. The claims die, the ground opens back up, and the valley belongs to whoever lives in it." His eyes fixed on hers. "Thirty days, Miss Lockridge. Thirty days left on the clock, and the only way the company keeps its grip is if an honest-looking engineer signs her name to a line through that canyon. They've been hunting for one for two years. Now they think they've found her, and she's sitting in my camp holding my map."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope a branch cracked under some animal's weight, and both of them turned toward the sound, and it was nothing, and it was everything.

"I'm not signing anything," Mara said.

"Not tonight." He smiled, and there was real humor in it, which was somehow the worst part. "Tonight you're going to do something harder. You're going to carry a dead man's mail."

"You're not dead."

"Not yet. Give it a week." He arranged it like an itinerary. "But I'm not crossing the gorge, and I'm not making Kestrel Falls, and we both know it, so let's not spend my last good hours pretending otherwise. Take the map. Take it to Ada Voss before the snows. That's the whole of the bargain. No payment, no obligation past the delivery — I know how you engineers hate open accounts."

Mara looked down at the packet in her hands. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the transit case, more than the crate of stakes, more than anything she had carried in nine years on the road. She thought about her line — her beautiful, doomed spur, the one the company men had asked her to fudge just last month, the one she'd refused to fudge and been punished for with silence and passed-over promotions. She thought about how easy it would be, tomorrow morning, to strike camp, ride downslope, hand this to nobody, lose it, forget it. Nobody would ever know. Nobody knew she had it now.

That was the trap of it, she realized. The debt was incurred precisely because no one would ever know if she defaulted. Character was what a surveyor measured when no instrument was watching.

"If I take this," she said, "and men come asking me — and they will — I want to understand what I'm carrying. Is it a claim? Is it evidence? What am I delivering?"

"Deliver it and find out," Amos said. "A map given freely tells more truth than a map taken under questioning. That's not philosophy, that's just how paper works." He leaned his head back against the chest. "Besides. You'll want to see the headwaters yourself before you decide anything. All of you clean-numbers people are the same. You won't trust a line you didn't run."

Despite everything — the blood, the hour, the cold coming down off the peaks — Mara felt her mouth twitch. "Is that so?"

"That's so." He closed his eyes. "Kettle's still hot, I hope. A man ought to die with decent tea in him."

* * *

He lasted four days.

She did not move camp. She told herself it was because breaking camp with a dying man aboard was impossible, and this was true, but it was not the whole truth, and she knew it. The whole truth was that Amos Craw talked, in the hours when the fever let him, and everything he said went into her the way water goes into dry ground.

He told her about Kestrel Falls: the millwright who'd lost two sons to the closed mines and still rang the school bell every morning; the sheriff, Voss, who'd come out from Denver after something Mara didn't press him on, and who read law at night by lamplight so the company lawyers couldn't walk over her; the way the whole town had voted down the company's first buyout offer to a man, and the way the second offer hadn't been put to a vote at all, just slipped to certain desperate households one envelope at a time.

He told her about the canyon above the falls — the one his map marked NO LINE THROUGH HERE — and how the company's own first survey, twenty years back, had called it unbuildable, and how that finding had never been filed, just buried, because a buried finding could be dug up and reversed by a man with the right signature.

"Yours," he said, watching her across the fire. "Or one like yours. That's all they lack. Everything else is bought and paid for — the ground, the politicians, the newspaper in the territory capital. They lack one honest engineer, and they figure every honest engineer has a price, because every dishonest one did."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then they find another way. Companies always find another way." He said it without bitterness, which made it land harder. "But a lapsed concession is a wall even money climbs slow. That's why the thirty days matter. That's why the snows matter. Time is the one thing out here they can't buy more of."

On the third night the fever climbed and he began talking to people who weren't there — a wife, dead twenty years, whose name was Ruthie; a partner lost in an avalanche whose name he couldn't retrieve and apologized to, over and over, for forgetting. Mara sat up with him, sponging his forehead with snowmelt, and when he surfaced near dawn he found her still there and studied her for a long moment.

"You could've ridden out yesterday," he rasped. "Map and all. Been fifty miles gone."

"I don't leave a line unfinished," Mara said.

"No," he agreed softly. "I didn't figure you did. That's why I picked your camp."

On the fourth morning he was clear-eyed and calm, the way men often were at the end, the storm having spent itself. He made her go through the map with him one final time, tracing each tributary, each elevation, explaining his shorthand — the little hooked crosses that meant tested float, the circles that meant springs, the single notation at the source itself, circled twice, that he touched but did not name aloud.

"When you see it," was all he said, "you'll understand why I spent eleven years keeping strangers away from it."

Near noon he asked her to help him sit up facing the fire, and she did, and he looked at the peaks for a while, blue and white and utterly indifferent, and seemed satisfied with the view.

"Bargain stands?" he said.

"You have my word," Mara said. "Kestrel Falls. Before the snows. To Ada Voss, into her own hands."

"That's all." He nodded, once, like a man closing a ledger. "Tell her—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell her the valley drinks from the source. Whatever else happens, whatever they offer, whatever papers get signed — the valley drinks from the source."

His breathing slowed through the afternoon, spaced further and further apart, like a train losing steam on a grade too steep to hold. Mara sat beside him and did the thing she could do, which was stay, and be precise, and not look away. Near dusk he exhaled one final time and did not draw the next, and the mountains went on being indifferent, and the fire needed feeding, and she fed it.

* * *

She buried him the next morning above the tree line, in ground she had to pick through frost to break, stacking a cairn over him because the earth was too shallow for anything deeper. She carved his name into a flat stone with her cold chisel — AMOS CRAW, SURVEYOR — and beneath it, after a long moment, the only epitaph she could think of that he would have approved of: HIS NUMBERS WERE TRUE.

Then she struck camp.

She packed the transit and the stakes and the oilcloth-wrapped profiles of her own abandoned spur, and she packed the oilskin packet separately, close against her body, where a pickpocket or a bullet would have to negotiate with her ribs to reach it. She stood a moment at the edge of the clearing, reading the country the way Amos had taught her to read it in four short days — the drainage patterns, the game trails, the long white shoulders of the peaks already wearing their first suggestion of winter — and calculated her route down to Kestrel Falls. Six days, if the weather held. Seven if it didn't. The snows were three weeks out at best.

Nine days of margin, on a delivery with no second chance. As margins went, she'd surveyed worse. She'd also trusted better.

She swung into the saddle and rode south, and behind her the cairn shrank into the hillside, and ahead of her the river ran down out of country no railroad had ever mapped, carrying snowmelt and secrets toward a town that did not yet know she was coming — and neither, entirely, did she know what else was riding the same trails, or how close behind her the first of them already was.
