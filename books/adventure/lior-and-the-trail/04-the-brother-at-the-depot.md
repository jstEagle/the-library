## 4. The Brother at the Depot

The train took the last mile of grade so slowly that Eli Lockridge could have walked it faster, and he spent that mile doing what he always did when a line ran badly: reading the roadbed through the floor of the car. He felt every joint in the rail, every soft spot where the ballast had been starved of stone, and he catalogued them out of habit, the way a doctor reads a face. The Kestrel Falls spur was dying. He could feel it dying under him, forty years of deferred maintenance singing up through the axles, and some cold professional corner of his mind noted that the company would let it die entirely within two seasons unless the new concession changed the arithmetic.

Which was why he was here.

He had the folder on his knee — the good leather one, the one their father had carried, the one with the brass corners worn to shine by a dead man's thumb. Inside it were the papers that made Eli, formally and on paper, the resident agent of the Meridian Consolidated Mining & Transportation Company's newest partner interest: the Silverthread Headwaters Concession, eleven thousand acres of high country above a town whose name he'd had to look up twice before boarding. The terms were summarized in an executive abstract four pages long. The full terms ran to sixty. Eli had read all sixty, alone, in a hotel room in Denver, and then he had read them again, because there was a clause on page thirty-one that he kept hoping would say something different the second time.

It didn't. It never did.

* * *

Kestrel Falls announced itself first as a smell — pine resin and river water and woodsmoke, the particular cold sweetness of a mountain valley in early spring — and then as a sound, the falls themselves, a long white roar somewhere north of the rooftops. The depot sat on a shelf of ground above the river, and it was small, smaller than the drawings in the company file had suggested, a single story of silvered boards with a loading dock built for ore cars that hadn't run in six years. Weeds stood waist-high between the rails of the yard. A water tower leaned over everything like a tired sentry.

Eli stepped down onto the platform with his bag in one hand and the folder under his arm, and the first thing he thought, absurdly, was that Mara would have hated this place.

No. That wasn't true. That was the lie he'd been telling himself since Denver, and he knew it was a lie even as he thought it, because the truth was the opposite: Mara would have loved it. She would have stood on this platform with her surveyor's eye already working, reading the grades and the sightlines, calculating what the valley could carry and what it couldn't, and she would have said something quiet and exact about the water tower or the rail joints, and she would have meant it as praise. His sister had never met a hard place she didn't want to understand from the inside. It was the thing he'd admired most about her, back when admiring her had still been allowed to be simple.

He asked the station agent — a stooped man with ink-stained cuffs who looked at the folder's brass corners with an expression Eli couldn't read — whether there was lodging in town, and the man said yes, the Kestrel House, and then said, without being asked, "You'll be the fourth company man this month."

Eli kept his face pleasant. "Is that so."

"Two lawyers and a fella measuring things." The agent stamped a ledger shut. "Town's getting to know the type."

There was nothing in that worth answering, so Eli tipped his hat and carried his bag down into the street.

* * *

Kestrel Falls was built on a slope, streets climbing away from the river in crooked terraces, and everywhere the water was present the way weather is present — in flumes running beside the boardwalks, in the sound under everything, in the way every window on the south side of Main Street seemed angled to catch the falls. It was, Eli admitted, walking it with his engineer's eye against his will, a well-sited town. Someone had understood the terrain here once. Someone had read the valley correctly.

He found the Kestrel House, took a room overlooking the street, and spent the afternoon doing what resident agents do: presenting himself. He called at the assay office. He called at the mercantile. He introduced himself to a bank manager who shook his hand with the warmth of a man handling a snake he'd been assured was tame. At each stop he gave the same speech, polished in Denver: Meridian Consolidated and its partners were committed to responsible development of the Silverthread headwaters, pending feasibility review; the concession represented jobs, a rail link, a future; Mr. Lockridge was authorized to hear concerns.

At the third stop, the assayer's clerk — a young woman with a smudge of blue mineral stain on her jaw — listened to the whole speech with her arms folded and then said, "Feasibility for what? You can't build to the headwaters. Everybody knows you can't build to the headwaters."

"The company believes the route is feasible," Eli said, "given proper engineering."

"Given proper engineering." She repeated it flatly, tasting it. "That's what the last fella said. The one who measured things. Before Amos Craw come down out of the hills half-dead and started telling everybody the company men were liars."

Eli felt the name go through him like a spike through a tie. *Amos Craw.* He knew the name. He had been required to know the name; it was in the file, page nine, flagged in red. Craw was the former company surveyor who had refused to certify the original ore reports eleven years ago, who had been quietly blacklisted, who had gone into the high country and become a rumor. The firm's position, stated in a memorandum Eli had signed receipt of, was that Craw's objections were those of a disgruntled employee and carried no technical weight.

Eli had signed the receipt. He had not believed the memorandum. He had signed anyway. There was a word for the interval between those two facts, and he suspected he was going to spend the rest of this assignment living inside it.

"And where is Mr. Craw now?" he asked, keeping his voice level.

The clerk looked at him a long moment. "Buried. Up on the bench, past the church. Died at a survey camp downriver three weeks back." Her eyes narrowed slightly. "Why? You know him?"

"No," Eli said, which was true, and which was also the most carefully constructed sentence he had spoken all day.

* * *

He saw her at dusk, on the porch of the sheriff's office, and the world tilted.

She was standing with her back to the street, talking to a tall woman in a badge — the sheriff, he learned later, Ada Voss — and even from across the street, even in the failing light, there was no mistaking the set of her shoulders. Mara had a way of standing that Eli could have picked out of a crowd of thousands: weight forward, hands loose, head slightly tilted as though listening to the ground itself. Their father used to say she stood like a bridge — like something designed to bear load. Eli had spent his whole life standing like a ledger instead, balanced, closed, nothing showing.

He crossed the street before he had decided to.

"Mara."

She turned, and he watched recognition arrive in her face in stages — surprise first, then disbelief, then something guarded and complicated that hurt more than anger would have. "Eli." She said it the way you'd say the name of weather coming in. "What are you doing in Kestrel Falls?"

"I could ask you the same." He gestured vaguely at the sheriff's office, the town, the whole improbable fact of her here, four hundred miles from any line she was supposed to be surveying. "Your office wired Denver saying you'd taken a personal leave. Nobody could tell me anything else. Do you know what I imagined?"

"What did the company imagine?" she asked, and it was a scalpel of a question, delivered mildly, and Eli understood in one cold drop that she had already heard the words *Meridian Consolidated*, already connected them to the folder under his arm, already done the arithmetic. She had always been faster than him. That had never been the problem between them. The problem was what each of them did with being right.

"The company," he said carefully, "has entered a partnership interest in the Silverthread headwaters. I'm here as its resident representative. Feasibility stage only. Nothing's decided."

"Nothing's ever decided," Mara said. "That's how it works. You decide it in a room in Denver and then you spend a year pretending it's happening to you."

"Mara—"

"Sheriff Voss, this is my brother." She said it without warmth and without apology, a formal introduction performed like a duty. "Eli Lockridge. Railroad man."

"Sister's told me about you," Sheriff Voss said, and her handshake was a measurement. "Not much, but some."

"I'd like to talk," Eli said to Mara. "Not here. Properly. You and me."

"You want to talk, walk with me to the assay office. I'm expected." She was already moving, and he fell in beside her, matching her stride out of old habit, and for fifty yards they walked in silence through the blue evening while the falls roared somewhere behind the roofs, and it was almost — dangerously almost — like the walks they used to take along the rights-of-way when they were young, when their father was alive, when the company was just a thing that paid for boots and schoolbooks and never a thing that came between them.

"I'm glad you're alive," Eli said finally. "I've been worried sick for three weeks. Whatever else is between us, that part's true."

Something moved in her face — a softening, quickly mastered. "I know. I'm sorry about the wire. I had reasons."

"Amos Craw's reasons?" He watched her stop walking. Watched her turn. Watched her decide, visibly, how much to give him.

"You know that name," she said.

"Everyone in this valley seems to know that name."

"That's not what I asked."

Eli looked at his sister in the last of the light — at the wind-chapped cheeks, the rope burn he could now see wrapped and healing on her left palm, the map case slung across her back that he would have known anywhere because he'd bought it for her twenty-first birthday — and he felt the page-thirty-one clause sitting in his chest like swallowed stone. *The certification of a qualified engineer shall constitute conclusive evidence of feasibility for purposes of permit renewal.* They hadn't sent him despite Mara being in the valley. Somewhere in a conference room in Denver, someone had looked at a personnel file and seen that the company's best surveyor was estranged from the company's fixer, and had concluded — correctly, so far — that Eli Lockridge could be relied upon to manage the situation. He was not here to represent the partnership to the town.

He was here, in part, to represent it to *her.*

"There's a version of the next five minutes," Eli said slowly, "where I tell you everything I know, and you ask me questions I can't answer honestly, and we both end up saying things we can't take back. And there's another version where we have supper at the Kestrel House, and you tell me why your hands are burned, and I listen, and I don't lie to you. Not tonight. Tonight I just don't volunteer."

Mara studied him for a long time. Down the street, a lamp was lit in the assay office window; the smell of supper smoke drifted from the boarding house; the river went on underneath everything, patient as geology.

"That's not nothing," she said at last. "From you, that's practically a confession." She shifted the map case on her shoulder, and he caught — just caught — the edge of heavy paper inside it, hand-drawn linework, a draftsman's hand but not hers. "Supper, then. But Eli — whatever you're carrying in that folder, you should know I've spent three weeks learning what these mountains cost people who treat them as a line item. If you're here to do that, we're going to be on opposite sides of it. I won't pretend otherwise for the sake of a meal."

"I wouldn't ask you to," Eli said.

And he meant it, in that moment, wholly — and he also knew, walking beside her toward the lamplight, that tomorrow morning he would send his first report to Denver, and that the report would omit certain things: that his sister was in the valley, that she carried a dead prospector's map, that she had already made allies of the sheriff and the assay clerk and possibly half the town. He would omit them because the firm had asked him to manage her, and because he still believed — God help him, he still believed — that he could stand between Mara and the machine he served, and absorb the compression, and keep everyone whole.

Their father had believed the same thing, once, about the same company. Eli pushed the thought down where he kept all the others, locked the ledger, and smiled at his sister as the lamp of the assay office lit them both.

Above the rooftops, unseen in the dark, the falls went on roaring — the sound, Mara had told him once when they were children, of a river that had never yet agreed to anything.
