## 6. Thirty Days

Ada Voss had learned to read weather the way other people read faces, and the sky over Kestrel Falls that morning promised nothing good. Low gray cloud pressed down on the ridgelines like a hand on a lid, and the Silverthread ran high and brown with early melt, loud enough that she had to raise her voice when she spoke to Mrs. Okonkwo at the dry goods store about the sandbags stacked behind her shop.

"Third flood this decade," Mrs. Okonkwo said, not looking at Ada but out the window at the river, as if it might be listening. "You'd think a body would get used to it."

"You don't get used to a river," Ada said. "You just learn its habits."

She meant it as comfort. It came out as warning, which was more honest anyway. Ada Voss was not a woman given to softening things. Eleven years wearing the badge in a town of four hundred souls — miners without mines now, widows without pensions, boys with nothing to do but throw stones at the company's survey stakes — had burned the softness out of her like resin out of pine. What remained was hard and clear and useful, mostly.

She was crossing the street toward her office when she saw the stranger come off the morning stage.

The stage from Meridian Junction arrived twice a week, carrying mail, dry goods invoices, and rarely anything worth the dust it raised. But this passenger stepped down with the particular posture of someone who had traveled hard on purpose — a woman in a canvas coat gone gray with trail grime, a surveyor's transit case slung across her back alongside a bedroll, boots worn through at the outer heel the way boots wear when their owner walks ridgelines for a living. She paid the driver, stood a moment in the road looking up the length of Front Street with an expression Ada recognized: the look of a person arriving somewhere they'd promised themselves they wouldn't stay.

Then the woman saw the sheriff standing there, and her chin came up a fraction, the way it does in people who've been stopped by authority often enough to have opinions about it.

"Sheriff?" she asked. Not a question about whether Ada *was* the sheriff — the star and the way the town bent slightly around her answered that. A question about what kind of sheriff.

"Ada Voss." She let the silence do its work. "And you'd be?"

"Mara Lockridge." The woman shifted the transit case on her shoulder. "I'm looking for — I had a promise to keep. To a man named Amos Craw."

The name went through Ada like cold water through a boot seam.

She kept her face still. Twenty years of poker with the miners at the Silver Dollar had taught her that much. But inside, something turned over, because Amos Craw had been dead six weeks by the town's best reckoning, and because his name was one of three names written in the ledger she kept locked in her desk drawer — the ledger of men who'd gone into the hills angry at the company and never come back down.

"Craw," Ada repeated. "You knew him."

"I carried him two days when his heart gave out on the trail above Miller's Fork. He didn't make it." Mara Lockridge's voice was level, but her hands weren't; they'd closed around the strap of her case until the knuckles whitened. "He gave me a map. Made me swear I'd bring it here before snowfall. I'm late. The passes held me up."

*Before snowfall.* The old man's phrasing, even secondhand. Amos Craw had talked like a man rationing time.

"Come inside," Ada said. "Off the street."

* * *

Her office smelled of coffee gone bitter on the stove and gun oil and the paper-and-ink smell of a place where too much history was filed in too few drawers. Ada poured two cups without asking, set one in front of the surveyor, and sat down across the desk from her with the deliberate weight of a woman settling in for something she'd been dreading for months.

"Tell me about him," Ada said. "Not the map. Him."

Mara blinked. Whatever she'd expected — suspicion, indifference, a demand for the document — it wasn't that. She wrapped both hands around the cup.

"I only knew him three days. He came into our camp half-dead already, though we didn't know it. Kept calling me by the wrong name — called me Ruthie, once or twice. I think Ruthie was somebody he'd lost." She stared into the coffee. "He was scared. Not of dying. Of the map getting lost with him. He made me repeat the promise back to him word for word, like a contract. Take it to Kestrel Falls. Give it to someone who'll use it right. He said — " she hesitated, then gave it plainly — "he said the town would know what to do, if it had time enough to do it."

"And did he say what the map shows?"

"He said it shows where the silver actually is. And he said that was the problem."

Ada nodded slowly. She got up, went to the drawer she kept locked, took out the key from around her neck where it lived against her skin, and opened it. From beneath a false bottom of old warrants she lifted a leather journal — Amos Craw's, recovered by a trapper from a cache near the man's last cabin, brought to her office eight months ago along with a shrug and a request for whiskey money. She'd read it a dozen times since. She set it on the desk between them.

Mara looked at it, then at Ada. "His?"

"His. Found after he stopped coming down." Ada tapped the cover. "I've been waiting for the rest of him to show up ever since. Looks like you're it."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Outside, the river roared its one note, and somewhere down the street a hammer rang against iron — Tom Bright repairing the depot roof, work that needed doing, work that could be finished, unlike most of what crossed Ada's desk these days.

"The map," Ada said finally. "Show me."

Mara unslung the transit case, popped its latches, and lifted out a packet wrapped in oilcloth. Inside was a sheet of heavy drafting paper, hand-ruled, covered in Amos Craw's cramped, precise lettering — elevations, bearings, stream courses, and a shaded body drawn in graphite that followed the upper Silverthread like a vein follows the wrist. Ada had spent enough evenings with mining maps to read one fast. This one she read slowly, twice, and what she read made her sit back.

"That's the headwaters," she said. "That's inside the watershed. Every claim staked on that body sits on ground that drains straight into the falls."

"Now you understand why he was scared."

Ada looked up. The surveyor's eyes were tired and steady and watching her the way Amos must have watched her — measuring whether the words would land.

"Explain it to me plain," Ada said. "Like I'm a jury."

So Mara Lockridge explained it plain. The Consolidated Argenta Mining Company — Ada's jaw tightened at the name; everyone in Kestrel Falls had tightened something at that name for fifteen years — had filed concession claims on forty acres of the upper Silverthread basin, claiming a rich ore body. The claims were legal on paper. The ore body itself was real; Amos's map proved it better than the company's own filings did. But the ore lay in the source gravel of the river itself, interbedded with the springs that fed the falls. To work it, you'd have to dam the source, divert the flow, and sluice the channel apart. Forty acres of silver bought with a dead river, and every farm, every well, every fishery downstream poisoned with tailings silt inside a season.

"They can't mine it without killing the valley," Mara said. "But they can hold it. Sitting on the claims keeps anyone else honest ground from being worked nearby, and it lets them sell the concession forward to investors who never look past the assay numbers. That's what the paper is worth. Not the silver. The story of the silver."

"And the town gets to drink the consequences either way."

"If it's ever worked. Or if the diversion fails during construction — which, on that gradient, is likely." Mara's mouth twisted. "I'm a railroad engineer, Sheriff. I build lines through country like this for a living. I know what a failure looks like before it happens."

Ada folded her hands on the desk. Here it was, then — the thing she'd been turning over all winter like a stone in a pocket. Time to take it out.

"Then you'll want to hear the part I haven't told anyone outside this room," she said. "Because there's a clock on all of it, and I think God or the devil put you on that stage this morning for a reason."

* * *

She laid it out the way she'd rehearsed it alone, nights, talking to the stove.

The concession permits — the whole bundle of Argenta's claims on the upper basin — were conditional instruments. Territorial law required that within eighteen months of filing, the holder demonstrate feasible access: a certified engineering survey showing a practical route for equipment, timber, and ore haulage to the claim sites. No certification, no extension. The permits would lapse, the claims would revert to open ground, and the whole rotten edifice of paper would collapse.

Eighteen months from filing came due in thirty days.

"Thirty days," Ada said. "As of yesterday. And here's the part that matters. Argenta has to file a feasibility survey signed by a licensed engineer — sworn, stamped, liable to prosecution if it's fraudulent. If they file one, the claims extend another five years, and they start building. If they *can't* file one — if no engineer will certify that a route exists that doesn't destroy the watershed — the claims die. Forever. Open ground. The valley stays whole."

Mara had gone very still. "They'll find an engineer. They always find an engineer. There's a man in Meridian Junction who'll sign anything for two hundred dollars and a bottle."

"There is. And Argenta's lawyers are already in Meridian Junction — I've had eyes on them since February." Ada leaned forward. "But here's what they don't have, Miss Lockridge. They don't have honest numbers. Their own preliminary surveys are garbage — done fast, done crooked, done by men who never climbed the gorge. The only accurate survey of that basin in existence is the one Amos Craw made before they drove him into the hills, and the copy of it is sitting on my desk. And the only other person alive who's seen that country close up just walked into my office carrying a transit."

Understanding moved across Mara's face like weather. "You want me to survey it."

"I want the truth surveyed. By someone whose signature means something." Ada held up a hand before the objection could form. "Hear the whole shape of it. If you certify a route exists, Argenta extends, and the river dies — maybe not this year, but eventually, and your name is on the death certificate. If you certify no route exists — if you file a formal finding that access is infeasible without destroying the watershed — the board rejects the permit renewal, the claims lapse, and Argenta's lawyers will spend ten years suing you personally into the dirt. They'll call you incompetent. They'll call you bribed. They may call you worse. Your career, whatever it is, whatever it cost you to build — they will burn it down to discredit the finding."

The stove ticked. Down the street the hammer rang and rang.

"And if nobody files anything," Mara said quietly, "the claims lapse on their own."

Ada shook her head. "No. Silence isn't refusal under territorial code. If no finding is entered — pro or con — the board grants an automatic extension for diligence. Argenta's counsel knows that loophole better than I do; it's why they've been slow-walking everything. The only thing that kills those permits is a documented finding of infeasibility. Signed. Sworn. Filed before the deadline." She met the surveyor's eyes. "By an engineer. Which means, whether either of us likes it or not, by you."

Mara Lockridge looked down at Amos Craw's map for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — flatter, careful, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Why me? You don't know me. For all you know I'm exactly the kind of engineer who signs for two hundred dollars and a bottle."

"Amos Craw picked you," Ada said. "Out of every soul on that mountain, he put his map in your hands and made you swear an oath over it. I've read that man's journal enough times to know he was a fine judge of character and a terrible one of odds. I'm betting he was right about the first." She paused. "Also, you flinched when I said the river dies. People who'd sell out don't flinch."

Something in the surveyor's shoulders let go — not surrender, Ada thought, but the loosening that comes before a decision. Mara rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger.

"How long does a proper survey take? That basin, the gorge approaches, the grade calculations, the sworn report?"

"Done honest? Longer than thirty days."

"Then I'd be racing the calendar the whole way up and the whole way back." Mara laughed — a short, hard sound with no humor in it. "Sheriff, do you know what you're asking? A full feasibility survey of canyon country in thirty days is the kind of job I'd refuse from any client on earth."

"I know exactly what I'm asking." Ada stood, went to the window, and looked out at Front Street — at the boarded windows of the assay office, at Widow Prentiss sweeping her step for the fourth time that morning because sweeping was the only work left to her, at the boys throwing stones at the red-and-white Argenta stake markers nailed to the corner post of the livery. "I'm asking you to race a calendar to save a town you rode into an hour ago. I'm asking you to put your name on a document that will make powerful men hate you. And I'm asking you to do it knowing that if you fail, none of it matters — the permits extend, the machines come, and everything we did was for nothing except that we tried."

She turned back. "That's the offer. Hope with a deadline attached. It's the only kind this valley's been issued for fifteen years."

Mara was quiet for a while. Then she said, "There's something you should know. My brother works for the railroad — the same railroad that's partnered with Argenta on the haulage concession. Eli Lockridge. He's — " she stopped, started again. "If the company sends anyone up that river, it'll be him. Or he'll be sent after *me*. We haven't spoken in four years, and the reasons haven't improved with age."

Ada felt the cold drop of it even before the surveyor finished — the sense of a door opening onto weather she couldn't control. Family was the one variable no sheriff could post a notice about.

"Then you'd better hope blood runs deeper than payroll," Ada said. "Or failing that, that it runs faster."

Mara almost smiled. Almost. She put out her hand instead, and Ada took it — a surveyor's grip, calloused, honest, trembling very slightly at the edges.

"I'll need provisions for two, mules, and dynamite permits I probably won't use," Mara said. "And I'll need to start tomorrow."

"Not tomorrow." Ada walked her to the door and pulled it open onto the gray, roaring morning. "Tonight. The season won't wait and neither will Argenta's lawyers. There's a man at the livery who rents stock, and I'll write you letters to folks upriver who can put you over the gorge crossings safe. Miss Lockridge — " she caught the surveyor's sleeve, one last thing, the thing she'd been carrying since the trapper handed her that journal — "Amos Craw died believing this town was worth his last three days of life. Don't make a liar of him."

Mara looked back at her, and past her, at the town — at the falling-down, stubborn, grieving collection of buildings and people that had taken in a stranger's dying wish without knowing it yet.

"No promises," she said. "Except the one I already made."

She shouldered her transit case and stepped out into the street, and Ada Voss watched her go — watched her stop in the middle of Front Street and turn a slow circle, reading the town the way she'd read the map, taking its measurements.

And that was when Ada saw the eastbound freight slowing on the far track, brakes singing, a private car riding behind the tender in company livery — and a man step down onto the depot platform in a good coat, scanning the street until his eyes found the woman with the transit case, and went carefully, deliberately blank.

Ada's hand found the drawer key at her throat.

*Thirty days,* she thought. *And the company's already here.*
