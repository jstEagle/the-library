## 7. Provisions and Old Wounds

The general store in Kestrel Falls smelled of lamp oil, cured venison, and the particular staleness of a building that had been argued in for generations. Mara stood at the counter with a pencil stub, working down a list she had written three times, each version shorter than the last. Hardtack. Salt pork. Coffee — real coffee, not the chicory the store offered at half price, because if she was going to walk forty miles of unmapped river country she would not do it with a headache on top of everything else. Rope, sixty feet of good hemp. A spare blanket apiece. Cartridges for the rifle she'd borrowed from Sheriff Voss, who had handed it over without ceremony and without a smile.

"You'll want the pemmican too," said the storekeeper, a broad woman named Hettie Brandt whose forearms suggested she did more behind this counter than weigh flour. "Amos Craw used to buy it by the sack. Said it was the only food that didn't complain going uphill."

Mara added it to the list. She had learned in four days in Kestrel Falls that Amos Craw's name opened doors and closed conversations both. People wanted to talk about him the way people want to talk about weather that killed someone — carefully, and looking sideways.

She was weighing out the coffee when the door swung open and let in the cold and her brother.

Eli Lockridge filled a doorway the way their father used to — shoulders first, hat brim low, an air of arriving even when he'd been standing outside for ten minutes deciding whether to come in. He wore company clothes now, she noticed. Not the railroad's blue serge but something finer, gray wool with a cut to it that said *expense account*. Four days he'd been in town, sleeping at the depot hotel, and every conversation they'd had had been conducted in the careful, level tones of two people defusing something.

"Voss says you're leaving tomorrow," he said, coming up beside her at the counter.

"Voss says a lot of things."

"She says the trail's bad above the gorge. Says nobody's been up past Amos's cabin since fall."

"Then we'll be the first." Mara weighed the last of the coffee into a tin and wrote the figure in her book. Her hand was steady. She made sure of it.

"Mara." He lowered his voice, and Hettie Brandt, with the practiced deafness of shopkeepers everywhere, began loudly rearranging barrels. "I've been thinking about what you're doing."

"Don't hurt yourself."

"I'm serious." He set his hand flat on the counter between them, and she saw the ring he wore now, a signet with the company's mark, the one their father had never lived long enough to see him earn. "Forty miles up a river canyon in thaw season, following a dead man's map, to file papers against a concession my own firm holds. You have to understand how that sounds from where I'm standing."

"From where you're standing," Mara said, "it probably sounds like arithmetic."

* * *

They took the argument outside, because some arguments are private property even when everyone in town can hear them through the walls. They walked down to the rail spur where the survey train sat cooling, iron ticking as it shrank in the evening cold, and stood among the ties like two people waiting for different trains.

"The concession expires in twenty-six days," Eli said. "If no feasibility survey is filed, the claims lapse. I know that. Everyone in this valley knows that. What you don't know—"

"What I don't know," Mara said, "is a great deal, according to you. You've been very consistent about it."

He flinched. It was small, and he covered it fast, turning to look down the empty track toward the tunnel portal where the mountains closed in, but she saw it, and she hated herself a little for aiming there and hating him more for giving her the target.

"I'm trying to protect you," he said.

"No. You're trying to manage me. There's a difference, and the difference is that protecting someone means telling them the truth even when it costs you." She pulled her coat tighter. The wind off the river had teeth in it; the melt was early this year and the Silverthread ran high and brown with snowmelt, loud enough that they had to stand closer than either of them liked to be heard over it. "You came here wearing that ring. You came here representing them. So tell me, Eli — what exactly is your firm's interest in whether I go up that river?"

For a moment she thought he would answer. Something moved behind his face — the old thing, the boy who used to show her the signal lamps and explain how the interlocking worked, patient with her because she was the only one who asked. Then it shut, the way a switch throws, and the man was back.

"My firm's interest is a railroad," he said. "A line through this valley that puts Kestrel Falls four hours from the coast instead of four days. That's jobs. That's a future for a town that's been dying since the mines sealed. And yes, the concession partner wants ore rights along the right-of-way, because ore pays for rails, and rails pay for everything else. That's not villainy, Mara. That's how the world gets built."

"That's how *you* get built," she said. "The world was already here."

He laughed, short and bitter, breath smoking in the cold. "There she is. Father's daughter."

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended, and once it was out she couldn't take it back, and she found she didn't want to. "Don't you dare put him between us. He's been dead nine years and you've spent all nine of them using him as a shield."

"And you've spent all nine using him as a knife." Eli turned on her then, and the depot lamp caught the anger in his face, and under the anger something rawer. "Every letter. Every visit. Every time I came home on furlough you had some new way to remind me that he died believing the mountain division would be mine to finish. That he died with my name in his mouth and the company's refusal letter in his pocket. You think I don't know? You think I went to them because I loved them?"

"Then why?" It tore out of her before she could dress it up. "Why, Eli? He was dying and you were in Denver shaking hands with the men who'd broken him. He asked for you. Ada Voss wrote to you — I paid the postage on that letter myself, I remember the cost of the stamp because I remember everything, that's my curse — and you sent back a telegram. Eleven words. *Regret circumstances prevent travel stop send details by post.*"

The river roared. Somewhere up the darkening valley a slide of loose rock went down into the water with a sound like a door slamming.

"I was nineteen years old," Eli said quietly. "And I had watched that man spend thirty years being right and end up with a pension of nothing and a headstone the union local paid for. I watched them refuse him the mountain division and give it to a man who'd never seen snow, and I watched him take it like a saint, and I watched it kill him anyway, slow, year by year, the way disappointment kills engineers. And I decided — nineteen years old, sitting in a boarding house in Denver eating beans — I decided I would rather be on the side of the men who win." His jaw worked. "You got to be his heir. You got the maps and the pride and the name. Somebody in this family had to eat."

"So that's what it was. Hunger." Mara looked at him and felt the strange double vision she'd carried since the day he stepped off the train — her brother's face worn over a stranger's choices. "All those years I thought you'd been seduced. Bought. It never occurred to me you were starving."

"It doesn't matter what it was. It matters what it is." He straightened, and the company man reassembled himself piece by piece, collar, shoulders, voice. "You're going up that river regardless. I can see it. You've got the look he used to get."

"What look?"

"The one right before he did something expensive."

Despite everything — despite the nine years and the telegram and the ring on his hand — Mara felt her mouth threaten to move. She killed it. "Then you have a choice to make, brother. You can go back to Denver and file whatever your firm tells you to file. Or you can come with me and carry half of sixty pounds of rope and find out what's actually up that river. But if you come, you come honest. No telegrams. No eleven words. If you know something about that concession — anything — you say it on the trail or you don't come at all."

Eli was silent a long moment. The lamp in the depot window threw his shadow long across the ties, all the way to her boots, so that his darkness touched her before he did.

"I'll come," he said.

"And?"

"And I'll tell you what I can." It was not the word *everything*, and they both heard the space where it should have been, and neither of them reached across it.

* * *

They provisioned until midnight. In Hettie Brandt's back room, by the light of a single lamp, they repacked the gear twice, Mara weighing each load in her hands like a surgeon balancing instruments, shifting cartridges from Eli's pack to her own when she judged his shoulder wouldn't hold the weight on a scramble. He watched her do it and didn't object, which was its own kind of surrender.

At some hour past eleven, Hettie brought them bread and drippings and a pot of the good coffee, and sat down uninvited, which was apparently her privilege.

"Amos bought rope from me the week before he came down the last time," she said, apropos of nothing, tearing her bread. "Sixty feet. Same as you're taking. He said a man ought to carry enough rope to get out of whatever he got into." She chewed, swallowed, looked at Mara with eyes the color of creek water. "He also said, near the end, that there was a surveyor coming. Didn't say who. Said she'd have his father's way of holding a pencil. You want to know what's up that river worse than any of us, Miss Lockridge. Just make sure wanting it doesn't cost more than knowing."

When she'd gone, Eli spoke without looking up from the rope he was coiling. "She's right about one thing. Whatever's up there, it's already changed you. Four days in this town and you talk like the river's yours to defend."

"It's not mine." Mara folded Amos's map into its oilcloth, wrapped it, and stowed it inside her shirt, against her ribs, where she'd feel it move when she breathed. "That's the whole point. Nothing up there is anybody's. That's why it's worth walking to."

Eli tied off the coil and set it down, and for a moment, in the guttering lamplight, he looked nineteen again — hungry, and proud of it, and sorry.

"We leave at first light," she said. "Sleep if you can."

* * *

They crossed the footbridge out of town as the sun came up behind the eastern ridge, the whole valley lit bronze and shadow, the Silverthread running loud and high below them. Sheriff Voss met them at the far end with the rifle and a paper packet of letters she asked Mara to post if she made it to the telegraph office at Copper Bend. She shook Eli's hand last, and held it a beat longer than courtesy required, and said nothing at all, which from Ada Voss was a paragraph.

The trail left the river after the first mile and climbed through lodgepole, and the town fell away below them — the rooftops, the millrace, the depot with its one lamp still burning — until Kestrel Falls was just a smudge of woodsmoke in a fold of the mountains, and then not even that.

They walked in silence for the first hour. It was not a comfortable silence, but it was a shared one, and Mara found she could breathe inside it.

It was Eli, near midmorning, who broke it. He had stopped ahead of her on the trail, looking back the way they'd come, and when she drew level with him he was wearing an expression she couldn't read.

"Mara," he said. "There's something you should know about the concession terms."

The wind came down the canyon, carrying the smell of snowmelt and pine and, faintly, from somewhere upstream, the smell of blasting powder.
