## 8. The Gorge Crossing

The sound reached them before the gorge did — a low, continuous thunder that Mara felt in her boot soles and in the fillings of her teeth, like standing too close to a locomotive at full steam. She had been hearing it for an hour, growing louder as the trail descended through lodgepole pine and scree, and by the time they broke through the last stand of trees onto a granite shelf, she understood why Amos's map marked this place with three careful X's and no route line at all.

The Silverthread gorge opened beneath them like a wound in the earth.

It was perhaps sixty feet across, sheer-walled, dropping two hundred feet to where the river ran white and furious with spring melt. The water was not blue or green but a churning bone-white, freighted with snowmelt and broken ice, slamming against boulders the size of boxcars and throwing spray fifty feet up the walls. The roar swallowed everything. When Eli said something beside her, she saw his mouth move and caught nothing.

She pointed at her ear and shook her head. He nodded and gestured forward.

And there it was — the bridge.

It hung across the gorge maybe forty feet above the water, sagging in the middle like a rope left carelessly over a fence rail. Two cables of braided rawhide and wire, gone gray with weather, anchored into iron rings driven into the rock on either side. Between them, slats of split cedar were lashed at intervals, half of them missing, half of the rest rotted to a softness that even from here looked like wet bread. A handrail of single rope ran along one side, drooping badly. The whole structure swayed gently in the wind that funneled up the gorge, creaking in a register that cut through even the river's thunder.

Mara stood at the edge of the shelf and made herself look at it the way she'd been trained to look at everything: not as a thing to fear but as a thing to measure. Span, roughly sixty feet. Cable diameter, maybe an inch and a quarter, and she could see fraying at the near anchor. Deck loading — well. That depended entirely on what crossed it and how.

They had too much gear. That was the arithmetic, plain and simple. Rope, pitons, the tent, blankets, flour and beans and salt pork for six more days, Eli's medical kit, her instrument case, the map and journal wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. Together it came to better than two hundred pounds, and there was no way around the gorge. Amos's map showed the trail continuing north along the east rim; the headwaters lay beyond. The bridge was the only crossing within three days' walk either direction, and they didn't have three days.

Eli tugged her sleeve and shouted directly into her ear, close enough that his breath was hot: "We lighten the load! Make two trips — gear first, then us!"

She shook her head. "One trip for gear means someone hauling it back across alone." But he was right about the principle. She held up a finger — wait — and knelt, unpacking the pack, sorting ruthlessly. The tent went back in. So did the spare blanket, half the flour, the coffee. What remained she divided into two loads, each light enough to be carried high on the shoulders, leaving both hands free for the cables.

When she stood, Eli was looking at the bridge with his jaw set in a way she recognized from childhood — the set he got before climbing the water tower behind the house in Laramie, before every reckless thing their father had dared him to do. Some things didn't change. Some things, apparently, never would.

* * *

He went first.

That had been argued about, briefly, in gestures and shouts, and Mara had won it — she was lighter, and if the deck failed under the first crossing they'd know before both of them were committed. But then Eli had pointed out, with maddening logic, that if the anchor rings were going to pull free of the rock, better they do it while only gear was at risk. And gear couldn't be trusted to hold a cable steady.

So: gear first, Eli second, Mara last. It was sound engineering. She hated it anyway.

Eli crossed with the first load bent double across his back, moving with a strange rolling gait, one foot placed carefully on each slat, hands gripping the side cables. The bridge sang under him — a low groaning note that rose and fell with each step. Halfway across, a slat let go entirely, dropping away into the gorge, and Mara watched it fall and fall and vanish into the white water without ever making a sound she could hear. Eli froze, then kept going. He reached the far side, dumped the load, and started back, and the relief Mara felt watching him step off onto solid granite was so sharp it embarrassed her.

Then it was her turn.

The first steps were the worst. The deck gave under her boots like a living thing, flexing and swaying, and the gorge yawned below with its white noise and its spray rising cold against her face. She fixed her eyes on the far anchor ring — don't look down, don't look at the water, look at the iron, iron doesn't move — and walked. One slat. Another. The cable hummed under her palms. Spray misted her face, cold as snowmelt, because it was snowmelt. Halfway across, the wind gusted up the gorge and the whole bridge swung sideways a foot, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world, and she stopped, arms wrapped around the cables, breathing.

*Measure it*, she told herself. *You've stood on trestles a hundred feet over canyons. You've walked the top chord of an unfinished bridge in a crosswind. This is nothing. This is arithmetic.*

It wasn't arithmetic. It was terror, and she breathed through it, and she moved.

She reached the far side. Eli gripped her arm hard enough to bruise, grinning, shouting something she couldn't hear over the river, and for one moment — one stupid, warm moment — they were just siblings again, two kids who'd survived something, laughing at the edge of a drop.

Then they went back for the second load, and that was when the wilderness stopped forgiving.

* * *

It happened on Eli's return trip, halfway across, with the second pack riding high on his shoulders.

Later, Mara would reconstruct it a hundred times, because that was how her mind worked — reduce the catastrophe to components, find the failure point, assign the cause. The cause was a cable. The upstream cable, the one bearing most of the lateral load, parted at a point eight feet from the far anchor where the braiding had worn to threads inside its casing, invisible from outside, invisible to any inspection short of cutting it open. She would learn all this afterward. In the moment, all she knew was the sound — a crack like a whip, sharp enough to cut through the river's roar — and the bridge lurching sideways like a struck animal, and Eli dropping to his knees on the tilting deck, and the remaining cable beginning to unwind its load toward the center of the span, toward the lowest point, toward the water.

"Eli!" She was screaming it and couldn't hear herself. "DROP THE PACK! DROP IT!"

He didn't. Of course he didn't — the pack held the tent, the food, six days of survival, and some stubborn Lockridge instinct wouldn't let go of it. She watched him struggle upright on the sagging deck, watched him take two more steps toward the far side, toward her, and then the remaining cable slipped its lashings at the near anchor — the rot she'd noted, the fraying she'd measured, the failure she'd catalogued and proceeded anyway — and the whole bridge folded.

It happened fast and slow at once. Fast: the deck collapsing into a V, slats sliding, the pack tearing free of Eli's grip and plunging. Slow: Eli, sliding down the slope of the fallen bridge, grabbing the single remaining handrail rope, hanging above the gorge with his boots kicking over two hundred feet of empty air, the white water reaching up its spray toward him like something hungry.

Mara was already moving.

She didn't remember deciding to move. Later she would search her memory for the decision and find only motion — herself running along the rim rock to where the near anchor's stump of cable still jutted from its iron ring, ripping the coil of climbing rope from her own pack, looping it twice around the stump and around her own forearm and hip in a friction hitch her hands tied without consulting her brain. Then she was at the edge, paying out rope, looking down at her brother hanging over the void.

He was fifteen feet below the rim, swinging gently at the end of the handrail rope, which was itself attached to the fallen bridge's wreckage and holding — for now. His face was white. His mouth was moving. She read the shape of it: *Mara. Mara.*

"I've got you!" she screamed, knowing he couldn't hear, knowing the words were for her as much as him. "I'VE GOT YOU!"

The handrail rope was tied off somewhere in the collapsed mess of the old bridge, and it was already creaking, fibers popping audibly even under the thunder. She had seconds. She looped the climbing rope again, took a turn around a granite horn, leaned out over the edge with her heels braced against a crack, and threw the coiled end down toward him. It fell short. She hauled it back, coiled faster, threw again — and this time the rope's end slapped against the rock wall beside him, and she screamed *GRAB IT* with her whole chest, and he did.

His weight came onto her line and the world became simple. There was the rope, burning through the leather of her gloves. There was her braced body, every muscle a cable itself. There was the count — haul, brace, haul, brace — and nothing else in existence. Not the company, not the concession, not the thirty days ticking down, not the fraud waiting at the headwaters. Just the rope and the weight and the arithmetic of friction and leverage, the one language she had ever fully trusted, saying: *you can hold him, you can hold him, you can hold him.*

His head came over the rim first, then his shoulders, and she dropped to her knees and got two fists in his collar and dragged him the last four feet onto granite as though he weighed nothing at all, as though she were not shaking so hard her teeth chattered.

They lay on the rock together, side by side, gasping. The river thundered on below, indifferent. The wrecked bridge hung in its V, swaying, shedding slats into the white water one by one, like the gorge quietly eating its meal.

* * *

They camped that night on the east rim, because there was nowhere else to go. The gear was gone — the tent, most of the food, the spare rope, Eli's medical kit, all of it at the bottom of the Silverthread. What remained was what Mara had carried against her body: the map, the journal, her instruments, a knife, matches in a tin, one blanket between them, and whatever they could gather.

Eli sat with his back against a boulder, still pale, hands wrapped around a tin cup of melted snow. He hadn't spoken since she pulled him up. Neither had she. The silence between them was different now — not the old armed truce of estranged siblings, but something rawer, newer, the silence of two people who had seen the same death and narrowly missed it.

Finally he said, quietly, under the river's distant growl: "I heard you. At the end. Through everything — I heard you counting."

"I wasn't counting."

"You were. Haul, breathe, haul. Like a switchman timing a yard." He almost smiled. "Dad used to do that. Out loud, when things went wrong. Numbers instead of panic."

Mara fed sticks to the small fire she'd built, sheltered in a hollow of stone. "Numbers don't panic," she said. "That's why I trust them."

"You trusted the bridge, too. I watched you measure it."

"And I found the rot. I found all of it." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "I catalogued every weakness on that span and we crossed anyway, because the schedule said cross. Because the deadline said cross." She stared into the fire. "That's the job, Eli. That's always been the job. You measure the risk, you file the numbers, and then somebody with a ledger decides whether your numbers matter."

Eli was quiet a long time. Firelight moved on his face, and she thought — not for the first time since the depot — that he looked like a man carrying something heavy in a place she couldn't see. "Maybe," he said finally. "Maybe the numbers aren't always wrong, Mara. Maybe sometimes the people reading them—"

"Don't." She said it flatly, and he stopped.

She spread the single blanket over them both and lay awake long after his breathing deepened, listening to the river. The gorge had taken their supplies and nearly her brother, and it had done it without malice, without even noticing — the way a stamp mill notices ore. The wilderness forgave nothing. It didn't punish either; it simply was, and everything that entered it gambled on terms it didn't write. She had spent her whole life believing that precision was armor against exactly this — that if you measured truly, calculated honestly, you'd be spared. The bridge had been measured. She had measured it herself.

And yet her brother was alive, breathing ten inches away, because she'd thrown a rope true.

So the lesson wasn't *be careful*. Careful hadn't saved anything. The lesson was sharper, colder, and it settled into her like a piton driven into granite: *the mountains will take what they take, and the only thing you control is whether you meet them honestly.* Fudged numbers, false markers, concessions built on paper lies — those were crossings made with rotten cables, and somebody always hung from the rope at the end of them. Amos Craw had known it. That was why he'd run rather than sign.

Tomorrow they'd climb the east rim with almost nothing, four days from the headwaters with three days of food, and she would not turn back. Fear sat in her chest like a coal, and she let it burn down into something harder. Resolve, maybe. Or recklessness wearing resolve's coat. Either way, it would carry her.

Near dawn she slept, and dreamed of ropes, and woke to frost on the blanket and Eli already up, scanning the trail ahead.

"Mara," he said, and something in his voice pulled her upright. He was looking north along the rim, where the morning light caught on something bright among the pines — a scrap of color where no color should be. "There's someone ahead of us. And they're not supposed to be here."

Downwind, faint but unmistakable, came the smell of woodsmoke — and under it, something else. Something chemical. Cordite, maybe. Or dynamite.
