## 9. The Claim Jumpers

Eli heard them before he saw them — the ring of iron on stone, carried down the canyon on a wind that smelled of pine pitch and coming rain. Three sharp strikes, a pause, three more. Anyone else would have taken it for a woodcutter. Eli knew better. Woodcutters didn't work in rhythm like that. Survey crews did.

He stopped at the head of the switchback trail and let Mara draw level with him, her pack creaking, her face still set in the particular hardness she'd worn since the gorge crossing. Two days had passed since he'd gone into the water and come out coughing river, and she hadn't once mentioned it — hadn't thanked him for pulling him out or blamed him for the gear they'd lost. She'd simply re-tallied their provisions that night by firelight with the same unflinching arithmetic she applied to everything, and announced they were two days behind schedule and would make it up through the high meadows. That was Mara. Grief and gratitude both went into the ledger, and neither was permitted to slow the line.

The sound came again. *Tink. Tink. Tink.*

"Prospectors," Mara said, squinting up the slope through the trees. "This far ahead of the deadline?"

"Maybe." Eli's mouth had gone dry. He knew the cadence of those strikes the way a man knows his own heartbeat. He'd stood beside crews making exactly that sound in Nevada and Colorado, staking claims in weather that would kill a mule, because the company paid triple wages for speed and asked no questions about whose ground it was. "Could be Amos Craw's old partners. Could be anybody."

"Mm." She didn't believe him. He could tell by the way she adjusted her pack straps — two short tugs, always two — and started up the trail without another word. She'd been reading him like a gauge all week, and every day the needle sat a little further from where he wanted it.

He followed her up through the lodgepoles, each step landing heavier than the last, because some part of him — the part that had been awake since the gorge, since before the gorge, since the night in Kestrel Falls when he'd read the telegram from Hollis & Vane and burned it in the depot stove — already knew what they would find at the top of this ridge.

* * *

They came out of the trees onto a bench of granite and scree above a bend in the Silverthread, and there they were: four men and a mule, working a line of stakes down the near slope toward the water. Three of them swung hammers at cedar posts; the fourth stood apart with a transit on a tripod, calling bearings in a flat, bored voice. They wore canvas dusters gone gray with dust, wide hats pulled low, and sidearms riding openly on their hips in the way men did when they wanted the hardware noticed before the faces.

Eli knew the man with the transit. Not well — a dinner in Denver six months ago, a handshake, a name he'd been trying not to remember since the canyon — but he knew him. Corliss. Hired gun with a surveyor's license, the kind of man firms like Lockridge & Vane kept on retainer for jobs that required instruments instead of rifles. Or both.

Mara dropped her pack and pulled the field glasses from the top. Eli watched her raise them, watched her go very still in the way she went still over a set of numbers that didn't balance.

"That's claim staking," she said quietly. "Proper staking. Corner posts, bearing trees blazed, location notices." She lowered the glasses. "Eli. Those aren't prospectors. Prospectors don't carry transits."

"Concession surveyors, maybe. The company's got thirty days to—"

"The company's survey is *mine*," she said. "I'm the engineer of record. There's one crew authorized on this drainage, and I'm looking at it." She turned to face him, and the wind off the river pushed a strand of hair across her eyes and she didn't bother with it. "So who are they, Eli?"

And there it was. The question he'd been carrying up the mountain like a second pack, heavier every mile, and now it stood in front of him wearing his sister's face.

"I don't know," he said.

It came out steady. He'd give himself that. Twenty years of boardrooms and depot platforms and dinners where men lied to each other across the soup course had taught him to keep his voice level while his stomach dropped, and he used all of it now. He met her eyes and lied to her with his whole face, and she looked back at him for a long three seconds, and then — God forgive her, God forgive *him* — she believed him. Or half-believed him. Enough to turn away.

"We should see those notices," she said. "If someone's filing claims inside the concession boundary before the permits even expire, that's fraud. That's something Ada Voss can take to the land office."

"Yes," Eli said. "It is."

He picked up his pack and followed her down the slope toward the stakers, and the weight of what he'd just done settled into him so gradually he almost didn't feel it — the way silt settles in a riverbed, grain by grain, until one day the channel has moved and the water runs somewhere it never meant to.

* * *

Corliss saw them coming when they were fifty yards out. Eli watched him register Mara first — the field glasses, the way she walked a slope like she owned the geometry of it — and then himself, and Eli saw the flicker of recognition pass behind the man's eyes and get shuttered fast. Professional. That was the word the partners in Denver had used about Corliss. *Professional.* It apparently meant a man who could look his employer's sister in the eye and see nothing.

"Well now." Corliss lifted his hat an inch, all courtesy. "Morning to you folks. You're a fair piece from any road."

"So are you," Mara said. "From any legal one. Those are location notices you're posting inside the Silverthread concession."

Corliss smiled like a man hearing a joke he'd already heard. "Concession's got twenty-six days left on it, ma'am, and after that it's open ground. We're just getting our ducks in a row. Nothing personal in it."

"Nothing personal," Mara repeated. Her voice had gone flat and precise, the voice Eli remembered from childhood, from the kitchen table, from the night their father's ledgers came apart in her hands. "You're staking the east bank of the source drainage. Do you know what's under that bench? Because I do, and I'd be interested to hear your assay numbers."

"I don't discuss assays with strangers on a hillside." Corliss's hand drifted, casual as anything, to rest on the butt of his revolver. The other three had stopped hammering. One of them, a big man with a beard full of gray, turned his hammer over in his hand and held it the way a man holds a thing he knows how to use. "Now, I'll say this friendly. This is active claim work. Strangers wandering through a staking crew's lines have a way of tripping over equipment. I'd hate for anybody to get hurt up here where the doctor's four days away."

"You're threatening us," Mara said.

"I'm advising you," Corliss said pleasantly. "There's a difference. Ask any lawyer."

Eli stepped forward. Every instinct he owned told him to stay back, stay small, stay a stranger — but Mara was squaring up to four armed men with nothing but her certainty, and if it came to shooting he was going to be standing next to her when it started, whatever else he was guilty of.

"Nobody needs trouble," he said. "We're headed upriver. We'll be glad to leave your lines alone if you leave ours."

Corliss looked at him then, really looked, and for one naked moment the professional mask slipped and something passed between them — acknowledgment, warning, a message sent and received. *You know who we are. You know who sent us. Keep walking.* Then the hat brim dipped again and the smile returned, bland as milk.

"Appreciated," Corliss said. "Safe travels."

Mara stood her ground a moment longer, jaw tight, and Eli could see her running the arithmetic — four guns, two of them hers and his, a canyon at their backs, twenty-six days on the clock. Then she bent, deliberately, and picked up a scrap of paper from beside the nearest stake. A location notice, carbon copy, the kind filed in triplicate. Corliss let her take it. Let her. That was the detail Eli turned over all afternoon, cold in his gut: the man had *let* her take it, which meant the paper said nothing incriminating, which meant the real papers were somewhere else, which meant this whole operation ran deeper than four men and a mule.

She read the notice twice on the spot, folded it into her breast pocket, and walked away without another word. Eli fell in beside her. Behind them the hammers resumed — *tink, tink, tink* — patient as rain, eating the mountain stake by stake.

* * *

They made six miles that afternoon, mostly in silence, camped at dusk in a hollow of aspens above the river. Mara built the fire small, the way she did everything, and cooked beans and hard bread without talking, and Eli sat across from her turning his hat in his hands and rehearsing sentences he would never say.

*I know who they are, Mara. I've known since Denver. The firm hired them in March. I countersigned the disbursement.*

That was the true sentence. It sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Here was what he had learned in the years since their father died, in the years of learning to be the kind of man who survived in rooms where Hollis & Vane made its plans: that truth was a currency, and spending it had costs, and timing was everything. If he told her tonight — *those men are mine, the firm sent them, I signed for them* — she would turn around tomorrow. Or she wouldn't turn around but she'd stop trusting him entirely, and he needed her trust for four more days, just four more days until they reached the headwaters and he could manage the situation, steer it, find some arrangement that saved the valley *and* the firm *and* her career *and* him, some elegant solution that existed only in his head and required, as a precondition, that she keep walking upstream beside him believing her brother was on her side.

He had gotten very good, these past years, at preconditions.

"Eli." She looked up from the fire. "Those men knew we were coming."

His hat stopped turning. "What makes you say that?"

"They weren't surprised. A staking crew surprised on their lines gets jumpy — hands on guns, voices up. These men were bored. Corliss talked to us like a landlord talking to trespassers he'd already measured for." She poked the fire, and sparks went up and died among the aspens. "Somebody told them an engineer was coming up this drainage. Somebody who knew the route."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the dark slope the river ran loud and indifferent, and Eli found that he could hear his own pulse in it.

"Kestrel Falls isn't exactly a secret," he said. "Ada Voss told half the town we were going. Half the town tells the other half. Any telegraph line—"

"There's no telegraph within sixty miles of here and you know it." She said it without heat, almost gently, and somehow that was worse. "Word came upriver ahead of us, Eli. From somebody who knew the route, the schedule, and the purpose." She held his eyes across the fire. "I'm not accusing you. I'm telling you what the evidence says, because evidence is what I have instead of family, most days."

The sentence landed harder than any blow in the canyon had. He made himself hold still under it. Made himself breathe.

"If I thought—" he began.

"I know what you think." She stood, brushed off her trousers, and took her bedroll to the far side of the fire, the side facing the river, the side that had been hers since the first night out. "Sleep, Eli. Long day tomorrow."

But she lay awake — he could tell by her breathing, too even, too controlled, a woman performing sleep — and Eli lay awake on his side of the coals, staring up through the black lattice of aspen branches at stars he couldn't name, and understood with perfect clarity that he had crossed a line today and there was no trail back over it. Complicity wasn't a single act, he realized. It was a direction. You took one step because the alternative cost too much, and then the ground you stood on became the ground you defended, and step by step you found yourself building the very thing you'd sworn you never would — a life with a locked room in the middle of it, and your own sister shut outside the door.

Their father had died with a locked room in the middle of his life too. Eli had promised himself at the graveside, nineteen years old and furious, that he would never become that man. And here he lay, on a mountainside above a stolen river, having become him in every particular except the coffin.

He must have slept eventually, because he dreamed — the old dream, the depot dream, his father's train pulling away with Eli on the platform holding papers nobody would sign — and woke gray-handed to frost on the bedroll and Mara already up, packing, her face closed as a ledger book.

She handed him coffee without a word. He drank it scalding and tasted nothing.

They broke camp and started upriver, and it was Mara who spotted it first, an hour on: smoke rising thin and straight from a fold of the valley ahead, too far from any trail, too deliberate for lightning. She put the glasses up and held them a long time.

"Cabin," she said finally. "Stone chimney. Somebody's living up here, or was." A pause, and then, quieter, with something in her voice he hadn't heard since Kestrel Falls: "Eli. The roofline. The cut of the door. I've seen this cabin before — on Amos Craw's map. He drew it with a little star beside it."

Eli felt the morning tilt beneath him. Amos Craw's map — the dead man's map, the reason for all of this, the paper in Mara's oilskin packet that half the territory seemed to want and Eli's own employers had ordered destroyed. And now a cabin from its margins, sitting warm and smoking in a valley where four armed men staked fraudulent ground with the patience of men who answered to someone with deep pockets and longer plans.

"Could be squatters," he said, and heard how thin it sounded even to himself.

"Could be," Mara agreed. She folded the glasses away and looked at him across the space between them, that widening country of things unsaid, and for a moment he thought she would ask him again — *who are they, Eli, who told them, who are you working for* — and he did not know, honestly did not know, whether he would lie a third time or finally break.

She didn't ask. She shouldered her pack and started downslope toward the smoke, and Eli followed his sister toward the dead prospector's cabin, carrying his secret like a bullet lodged where no surgeon could reach it, knowing that sooner or later — at the headwaters, at the deadline, at whatever waited in that stone-walled room — the wound would open whether he willed it or not.
