## 11. River Rising

The river woke before the town did.

Ada Voss stood on the porch of the sheriff's office at first light with a tin cup of coffee going cold in her hand, and she listened to it — that low, grinding voice beneath the usual chatter of water over stone. She had grown up on this river. She knew its moods the way a wife knows a husband's breathing: the thin whisper of August, the bright argument of October rain, and now this — the deep, patient growl of snowmelt coming down out of the high country all at once, a winter's worth of white let loose in a single week.

"Early," said Tom Quist, limping up the boardwalk from the assay office, his bad knee announcing the weather before the weather arrived. He squinted toward the bridge. "Melt came down off the divide three weeks ahead of itself. Warm wind out of the south all night."

"How high?"

"Crested the lower pilings around midnight." He spat thoughtfully into the dust. "Mill Street'll be under by noon if it keeps."

Ada drank her coffee and watched the brown water slide past the bridge timbers, already thick with foam and the corpses of small trees. Mill Street held eleven families, the feed store, the cooperage, and the church basement where they kept the town's records. It had flooded twice in her lifetime, both times shallow, both times survivable. But those floods had come in June, after the melt had spent itself gradually against the mountainsides.

This one had been sent all at once.

She didn't believe in coincidence. Twenty-two years wearing a badge taught you that the world was mostly coincidence, but the parts that weren't were the parts that killed people.

* * *

By nine o'clock the water was over the boards of Mill Street and rising an inch an hour, and Ada was standing hip-deep in the doorway of the Halloway house hauling a trunk with one arm while Ruth Halloway carried her youngest child on her back and her grandmother on her shoulders of pure stubbornness. The lower town had turned out the way towns do when there's no time to argue — everyone working, everyone shouting, a bucket line forming without anyone ordering it.

It was during the third trip that the wagon came down from the ridge road.

Ada saw it first because she always saw things first; it was the occupational disease of lawmen. A fine black carriage wagon, too clean for the mud, drawn by two matched bays that picked their way through the floodwater with the fastidiousness of animals who had never been asked to work. The man holding the reins wore a gray suit that would be ruined by noon and clearly didn't care. Behind him rode two more men in company colors, canvas dusters, sitting their horses like they'd been paid to sit them.

The wagon stopped at the dry end of Mill Street, close enough to watch and far enough to stay clean, and the man in gray unfolded himself from the seat with the unhurried grace of someone arriving at an auction he intended to win.

He found Ada ankle-deep in the street, a dripping ledger book under her arm.

"Sheriff Voss." He extended a hand she did not take. "Corbett Ashe, representing the Consolidated Silver and Copper Company. I'm told you're the authority here."

"I'm told a lot of things," Ada said. "Mostly wrong."

His smile thinned by a degree but held. Up close he had the kind of face built for banknotes — smooth, symmetrical, forgettable. His eyes went past her to the bucket line, the women carrying furniture, the men wading in the brown water, and something in the set of his mouth suggested he was doing arithmetic.

"Dreadful business," he said. "The flooding. Of course, the company holds no responsibility for acts of God—"

"Nobody said you did."

"—but we do feel a certain obligation. To the community." He produced a leather folio from inside his coat, dry as bone, as though the weather hadn't touched him. "Consolidated is prepared to make cash offers on affected properties. Generous offers. Above assessed value. Today, Sheriff. While the water's still rising and everyone can see plainly what their land is worth."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the folio.

"You came through a flood," she said, "to buy a drowning town."

"I came to offer *relief*," Ashe said mildly. "The distinction matters legally."

"It matters morally too. That's the part you're hoping nobody notices."

Behind her, the bucket line had stopped. Faces turned along the street — Ruth Halloway with her baby, old Sam Okafor standing waist-deep beside his cooperage with a hammer still in his fist, the Delgado boys on the church steps. They had heard. Towns that size hear everything; sound carries across water like a rumor.

Ashe noticed it too. He raised his voice pleasantly, pitched to carry. "Friends! I know these are hard days. Consolidated Silver and Copper wishes only to help. Fair prices, cash in hand, relocation costs covered. There's no shame in selling ground that fights you every spring."

"There's no shame," Ada said quietly, so that only he could hear, "in drowning either, if you go about it right. Now get that wagon off my street before I find a reason to impound the horses."

"You'd impound company property?"

"I'd impound anything standing in four feet of moving water that isn't helping bail."

He studied her then, really studied her, the way a card player studies a hand he'd expected to be weaker. And Ada understood suddenly what he was — not a buyer, not yet. A measurer. They'd sent him to see how much the town would go for, and whether the sheriff was a wall or a door.

"We'll speak again," he said, folding the folio away. "When the water's down and tempers are reasonable."

"Water won't go down," Ada said. "Not till the melt does. Three weeks, maybe more."

Something flickered behind his eyes — satisfaction, quickly buried. "Three weeks," he repeated softly, as though committing it to memory. "Well. We're patient people."

The wagon turned. The horses stepped delicately back through the mud, and the gray suit rode away up the ridge road without a spot on him.

Ada stood in the floodwater and felt the cold working up through her boots, and she thought: *three weeks.* The same number, give or take, that Mara Lockridge and her brother were somewhere upstream with a dead prospector's map and a deadline neither of them fully understood yet.

* * *

They met that night in the church, because the church was dry and the saloon was not, and because Pastor Eames had the only room in town big enough for everybody's anger.

Forty-one souls crowded the pews, dripping and exhausted, smelling of river mud and lamp oil. The water had crested at ten that night — eighteen inches below the floorboards of the lowest houses, a mercy nobody trusted — and now the town sat in candlelight and argued about what Corbett Ashe had left behind.

Because he had left things behind. That was the cleverness of it. Before the wagon ever rolled, his riders had nailed paper to every door on Mill Street and half the doors above it. Offers, printed neat, each one addressed by name, each one figured to the dollar. Sam Okafor's cooperage: four hundred dollars, double its assessment. The Halloways' place: six hundred and fifty. The church itself: nine hundred, marked *for relocation purposes.*

"They know our numbers," Sam said flatly. He was a big man gone gray, hands like split oak, and he held the offer sheet the way he'd hold a snake. "Assessed values, tax rolls, who owes what at the store. Somebody's been through our books."

"Somebody's been through everything," said Ruth Halloway. Her voice shook, but she kept it level. "Six hundred and fifty dollars. That's what my husband's life came to, by their figuring. He died in their mine and they paid me nothing, and now they want to pay me for the ground he's buried near, so they can dig it up."

The murmur that went through the pews was low and ugly and true. Eleven men of Kestrel Falls were dead in the closed mines — the Number Four collapse, three years back, the accident the company had called unavoidable and the widows had called otherwise. The mines had shut, the company had withdrawn, and the town had been left to bleed slow ever since. Everyone in that church had lost somebody or traded with somebody who had.

Pastor Eames lifted a hand. "The question isn't whether their price is fair. It isn't. The question is whether we can refuse it."

And there it was, laid open under the candles. Ada let the silence sit while folks looked at their hands.

"Talk plain, Sheriff," Sam said finally. "You've been quiet."

So she stood. She didn't go to the front; she stayed in the aisle where she was, because she'd learned that lawmen who take pulpits start sounding like preachers, and preachers start sounding like salesmen, and Kestrel Falls had had enough of both.

"Plainly, then," she said. "First thing: they don't need us to sell. Not all of us. A concession gives them mineral rights under our ground regardless. What they need is for us to be *gone* — or cheap enough to move — before anybody asks hard questions about what they intend to do upriver. A town full of witnesses is expensive. A town full of signatures is free."

"Questions about what?" asked young Peter Delgado.

Ada hesitated. She had promised herself she wouldn't say it until she was sure, but sure had arrived sometime that afternoon, standing in the floodwater watching a clean man measure her town.

"The headwaters," she said. "The source of this river. Amos Craw drew a map of it before he died and gave it to a stranger to bring us, and that stranger is up there right now with her brother, who works for the railroad that's backing Consolidated's claim. I don't know what the map shows. But I know what a company pays cash for drowned ground in spring, and it isn't kindness. If they dam or divert that source — and a mine up there would need the water handled somehow — then everything downstream changes. This flood becomes a trickle in August and a lake in April. Or worse."

The silence afterward had weight, like deep water.

"That's why the offers," Ruth said slowly. "Not greed. Housekeeping."

"They're tidying up," Ada agreed. "Before the mess."

Old Mrs. Ferro, ninety-one and sharp as a tack, spoke from the front pew without turning around. "My husband helped build this town. My sons are in the hill behind it. I will die in the house I paid for, and God can send whoever He likes to evict me."

A few people laughed, and the laugh broke something loose — not fear exactly, but the paralysis fear makes. Sam Okafor stood up, unfolding by degrees.

"Here's what I know," he said. "I've got a cooperage full of tools and no orders since the mines closed. Half this town's the same — skill and no work. So here's my proposal. We don't sell. Anybody who wants to sell, that's their business, and no one shames them — some of us have children going hungry, and I won't pretend courage is free." He looked around, daring anyone to disagree, and nobody did. "But the rest of us, we fix the levee at the mill race tomorrow. And the day after. And every day till the melt's done. We make this town worth keeping, and then we keep it."

"And when their enforcers come?" Peter Delgado asked. "You know they will. They always do."

Ada answered that one. "Then they come to a town that's organized, that's watching, that writes everything down. I'm deputizing six men tonight — temporary, sworn, paid in coffee. Every offer sheet gets copied into the record. Every rider, every wagon, every name. If Consolidated wants this valley, they'll have to take it in daylight, in front of witnesses, and explain themselves to a territorial judge. That's not much. But it's what we've got, and it's more than most towns think to use."

Mrs. Ferro raised her cane an inch, which in her parliament constituted a second. "Carried," she said.

* * *

Later, when the church had emptied and the candles were guttering, Ada sat alone at the clerk's table with the record book open and wrote down the day the way she wrote down everything — names, times, amounts, the license number of the black wagon, the description of Corbett Ashe, the exact wording of his offer. Evidence was just memory with discipline.

Then she took a fresh sheet and wrote slower.

*Mara Lockridge,* she wrote. *Upstream. Thirty days on the concession clock, less eight gone. If the stories about her brother are half true, she doesn't know yet what she's carrying besides a map.*

She thought about the woman she'd met three weeks ago — mud to the knees, a surveyor's case strapped to her back like a coffin she couldn't put down, asking careful questions and answering none. A precise woman. A proud one. The kind who believed the world ran on numbers and that honest numbers would save her.

Ada had seen that kind break before. She'd also seen them hold.

She sealed the letter to the relay station at Miller's Bend — the last point upriver the flood hadn't cut off — and addressed it to the care of any trail party heading for the headwaters. It said, in full:

*Concession expires in twenty-two days unless a certified feasibility survey is filed. No engineer's signature, no mine — the claims lapse forever. Company agents buying the town under cover of flood. Enforcers expected. Whatever you found up there, hurry down with it. — A.V.*

She read it twice, weighing every word the way she weighed testimony, then folded it small.

Outside, the river had dropped an inch. It would rise again tomorrow — the melt had weeks left in it, and so did the men in gray suits — but tonight it had given the town back its street, and the stars over the divide were hard and clear, and somewhere up in all that dark country a woman with a dead man's map was walking toward the same deadline Ada had just mailed downstream.

Ada blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark doorway, listening to the water grind past the pilings, patient as a creditor.

"Hold on, Lockridge," she said to the night. "We're keeping your light on."
