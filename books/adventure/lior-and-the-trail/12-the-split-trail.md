## 12. The Split Trail

The fork announced itself the way all important things did in the high country — quietly, without ceremony, as if it had been waiting all along for someone to notice.

Mara saw it first from a quarter mile off: two gray threads of trail diverging at the base of a granite shoulder where the valley pinched shut like a fist closing. The left-hand route climbed away to the north, switchbacking up through stands of whitebark pine toward a saddle she could just make out, snow-dusted even now in late spring. The right-hand route dropped instead, plunging down into a slot of shadow between canyon walls so sheer they looked cut with a blade.

She stopped and dug out Amos's map without being asked to by anything but habit. The paper had gone soft as cloth from handling, its creases worn to translucence. She spread it on a flat rock and weighted the corners with four stones, the way her father had taught her to weight survey plats against mountain wind twenty years ago, when she'd been small enough to ride in the instrument wagon and certain enough of everything to believe maps were promises rather than arguments.

*Two ways up,* Amos had written at this exact point, in a hand that had grown shakier toward the bottom of the page. *Saddle road is safe. Four days extra. Canyon is one day if the river's low and God is watching. I took the saddle both times. I knew men who took the canyon.*

She looked up at the sky, reading it the way she read a bridge truss — for load, for stress, for the places where failure would begin. High cirrus, thin as scraped ice, moving west to east. No build-up over the peaks yet. The river, when they'd crossed it that morning below the last stand of cottonwood, had been running high but not raging, brown-green with melt, carrying sticks and foam but nothing the size of a fence rail.

Four days. They had six left before the permits lapsed, and two of those were spoken for by the descent back to Kestrel Falls. Which meant the arithmetic was simple and merciless: the saddle route did not get them there. Not unless they grew wings.

"We're taking the canyon," she said.

Behind her, Eli had gone very still.

She heard it before she turned around — not words, but the absence of them. Her brother was a man who filled silence the way water filled a breach, always talking, always explaining, always three sentences ahead of whatever you'd said. In the eleven days since Kestrel Falls he had narrated every mile: the geology, the company's plans, their father's old stories retold with the edges sanded off. But now he said nothing, and Mara turned around slowly, and found him looking at the canyon mouth the way a man looks at a door he'd hoped was locked.

"Say something," she said.

"I'm saying it." He crouched beside the map, careful not to touch it, his hands hanging between his knees. He'd lost weight on the trail; the fine bones of his face had come forward, and in the flat light under the pines he looked younger than thirty-four and older than their father had ever managed to look. "I'm saying we should think about it. That's all. Four days is four days, Mara, but dead is dead."

"The saddle doesn't get us there."

"It might. We push harder. We—"

"We've been pushing hard for eleven days. You're limping. Don't tell me you aren't; I've watched you favor that ankle since the gorge." She kept her voice level, almost gentle, because she had learned on the railroad that the fastest way to lose an argument with a proud man was to win it loudly. "The canyon saves us three days. Maybe four. It's narrow, it's fast, and Amos marked a campsite halfway through where we can shelter if weather turns."

"Amos also died alone in a survey camp," Eli said. "Forgive me if I'm not building my travel plans on his judgment."

That landed harder than he probably intended it to, and she saw him realize it a half-second too late, see the flinch move through him like a tremor through cable under sudden load. Amos Craw was buried behind the Kestrel Falls church under a marker Ada Voss had carved herself, and Mara had promised a dying man she'd carry his map home, and her brother had just used that promise as a debating point.

"Eli."

"I didn't mean—" He rubbed his jaw. "Look. I've seen canyons like that. Down in the San Juans, when I was doing reconnaissance for the Durango extension. Slot canyons flash flood. One storm over the divide, fifty miles away, and you get a wall of water coming down a crack in the earth with no warning at all. You don't hear it until it's on you. Men die in those things, Mara. Good men, careful men, men who checked the sky."

"And men die on saddles, too. Rockfall. Weather. Horses going down." She stood, folding the map along its worn bones. "You argued for the gorge crossing at Silverthread, remember? You said the rope bridge was fine. And when it went out from under us, you went into the water, and I pulled you out, and the next morning you told me the wilderness forgives nothing and we should respect it. So respect it now. Respect the deadline most of all. Because if those permits lapse while we're taking the scenic route, everything we've walked through — your ankle, my hands, the gear at the bottom of the gorge — all of it was for nothing."

He was quiet again. That was twice now, and the second silence was worse than the first, because silences in her brother had always meant calculation. She could remember it from childhood — the way he'd go still and inward before lying about who had broken the window, or where he'd really been that afternoon, or why their father's good rifle had come home scratched. Eli's silences were workshops. Something was being built in there.

"Three days," he said finally. "You're sure about the arithmetic?"

"I'm never sure about arithmetic. That's why I check it." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Six days left. Two down. Four over the saddle, arriving a day late with nothing to show for it but a view. Or one day through the canyon, arriving with time to spare and a chance to actually look at what Amos drew."

He looked at the canyon for a long moment. The light had shifted while they talked; the shadows in the slot had deepened from gray to blue-black, and somewhere down inside it, faint as a memory, they could both hear the river working — a low continuous grinding, stone on stone, the sound the mountains made when they chewed.

"All right," Eli said. "The canyon."

And Mara, who had spent her whole life reading structures for the flaw hidden in sound material, felt the first cold drop of suspicion slide down her spine — small, singular, easy to dismiss, the way the first drop of a roof leak is easy to dismiss right up until the ceiling comes down.

Because he'd agreed too easily. After arguing. After the silence. He'd fought for the safe route with real feeling, and then folded in a single breath, like a man who had gotten what he actually wanted and needed only to be seen resisting it.

*No,* she told herself, shouldering her pack. *That's exhaustion talking. That's the gorge still in your blood. He's your brother.*

But the drop stayed cold, and it did not evaporate.

* * *

They made the descent into the canyon in the last hour of light, and it was like walking into the throat of the world.

The walls closed overhead until the sky was a ragged ribbon of violet, and the temperature fell ten degrees in as many minutes, the air going damp and mineral-cold, smelling of wet granite and pine rot and something older underneath — the smell of stone that had never once been warm. The trail, if it deserved the word, was a shelf of broken rock above the river, which ran faster here, compressed between narrowing walls, its voice rising from a mutter to a constant urgent argument. Spray hung in the air. Every surface was slick. Mara moved the way she moved on ballast slopes, testing each foothold before committing her weight, and behind her she heard Eli doing the same, his breathing loud in the confined space, his limp more pronounced now though he said nothing about it and she offered no mercy by mentioning it.

Twice the shelf gave out entirely and they had to climb, hands and boots finding cracks in the wall, packs scraping stone. Once they crossed a scree fan where a slab the size of a freight car had come down within the last year — she could tell by the unweathered brightness of the fracture faces — and she stood at its base looking up at the clean scar on the canyon wall above and thought, with a professional calm that frightened her a little, *that came off in one piece. That is how much force lives in this place, waiting.*

"You feel it?" Eli said quietly, coming up beside her. "Like the walls are listening?"

"I feel like the walls don't care whether we're here or not. There's a comfort in that."

"That's the saddest thing you've ever said to me."

"Then you haven't been paying attention for twenty years."

He laughed — a short, startled laugh, real despite everything — and for a moment they were children again on the porch of the section house, daring each other to touch the electric fence, and the cold drop of suspicion thawed a degree or two, and Mara let herself believe she'd imagined the whole thing.

They reached Amos's marked campsite as full dark settled into the canyon, a bench of gravel above the high-water line where a leaning slab formed a natural lean-to. Someone had been here before them — long ago, she judged. A ring of stones blackened with old fire. A tin cup gone to rust, crushed flat. Carved into the slab itself, low where a sitting man's hand would fall, a single word: *PATIENCE.* The letters were deep, cut with a knife by someone with time and fear in equal measure.

Eli built the fire while Mara checked the map by its light, tracing tomorrow's route. The canyon ran twelve more miles, straight as a rifle bore, and then opened into the headwater basin where Amos's inked lines converged like veins into a heart. One day. If the sky held.

She glanced up at the ribbon of violet overhead. Stars now, hard and close. No clouds. The barometer in her kit — she'd carried it since the spur line, out of habit, out of love — had held steady all evening.

"Sky's clean," she said.

"Mm," said Eli.

She looked at him across the fire. He was turning his boot toward the flames, drying the ankle, his face half gold and half shadow, and he was not looking at the sky at all. He was looking down the canyon, back the way they'd come, back toward the fork, with an expression she had no name for — not fear exactly, and not relief, but something with the shape of *waiting*. As though he expected something to arrive from behind them. As though he were counting.

"Eli. What are you looking at?"

"Nothing." Too fast. "Checking our backtrack. Making sure nobody followed." He smiled, and it was his depot smile, the one he wore for clients and superiors, polished and load-bearing and false. "Old habits. Company men are paranoid about being followed in claim country."

"Are you a company man, Eli?"

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, a boulder shifted in the current with a deep knocking sound, like a knuckle on a door.

"I'm your brother," he said. "That's the only title I've got left that matters."

It was a good answer. It was the answer she wanted. And that was precisely what made her lie awake half the night beneath the leaning slab, listening to the river grind and her brother breathe, turning the cold drop over and over in her mind until it had grown, by degrees she couldn't quite measure, into something with edges.

He'd known about the claim jumpers' timeline. He'd known the firm was staking ground ahead of schedule — she'd seen the fresh blazes on the trees two days back, bright-cut sap, markers that hadn't existed when the concession papers were signed. She'd assumed he hadn't noticed. She'd assumed a lot of things about her brother, all the way back to the day he'd chosen the company's telegram over their father's funeral.

And he had argued for the safe route. The slow route. The route that bought four days.

*For whom?*

She sat up in the dark. Across the dead fire, Eli slept on his side with his back to her, his pack tucked under his head — and propped against the slab beside him, half in shadow, his leather document case, the one he guarded like a ledger of sins. It was open an inch. Paper showed at the gap, pale in the starlight, and on the topmost sheet, even at this angle, even in this light, she could make out the corner of a letterhead she knew better than her own handwriting: the interlocked rails and winged wheel of the Cascade & Western.

Her own employer's mark. The same mark stamped on dynamite crates she hadn't found yet, in a canyon she hadn't entered yet, under rock she hadn't dug through yet.

Mara lay back down and stared at the ribbon of stars and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only in the hours when no daylight remains to soften anything, that the fork in the trail had not been the day's real decision. The real decision had been made somewhere else, weeks ago, in a boardroom she'd never seen, by men whose names she'd find written in that case — and her brother had simply been carrying the decision up the mountain in his hands, waiting for the place where it would weigh least.

The river spoke all night in its stone voice. And near dawn, far downstream — back the way they'd come, back toward the fork, back toward the world — Mara heard a sound that did not belong to any river.

A single sharp report, flat and hard, rolling up the canyon walls like a slammed door.

Dynamite.

Eli's eyes were already open.
