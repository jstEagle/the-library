## 13. Canyon of Echoes

The canyon announced itself before they saw it — a long, low groan of wind through stone, like the valley clearing its throat. Mara stopped at the lip where the trail dropped away and stood a moment with her hands on her hips, reading the rock the way she'd once read rail grades: the tilt of the strata, the pale scars of old slides, the talus fans spilled at the base of the walls like flour swept from a table.

"Four days around," Eli said behind her. "Or one day through."

"One day through," she agreed, "if the canyon lets us."

He came up beside her, and for a moment they stood shoulder to shoulder, two silhouettes against a slot of sky so narrow it looked cut with a knife. The Silverthread ran somewhere below them still, hidden by the walls, but they could hear it — a constant rushing whisper, the sound of water that had never once in its history been asked to stop.

Mara had made this decision at the fork and she had not liked making it. The safe route looped east over a saddle and down through timber, four days of steady climbing that would put them at the headwaters too late to matter. The canyon was a gamble dressed as a shortcut. She had told herself it was surveyor's judgment — she had read canyons before, crossed worse ones on foot with instruments strapped to her back. But some part of her, honest in the way she demanded her numbers be honest, knew she had chosen speed because speed felt like control, and control was the thing she had least of out here.

"Stay close to the wall," she said now, shouldering her pack. "If anything moves, you get under an overhang and you don't think about your gear. Gear can be replaced."

"And you?"

"I'll be ahead of you. I'm lighter."

"You're stubborn is what you are." He almost smiled when he said it, and for one strange second it was their father's smile looking back at her from a face twenty years younger, and Mara had to turn away and start down the switchbacks before something in her chest could crack open.

The trail into the canyon was barely a trail at all — a shelf of packed earth no wider than a coffin, cut into the south wall maybe forty feet above the river's churn. The walls rose sheer on either side, red and gray banded stone streaked with white mineral stain, and overhead the sky narrowed to a ribbon of blue so thin a hawk crossing it looked like a stitch being drawn shut. The air inside was cool and smelled of wet stone and pine pitch and something older underneath, something mineral and patient, the smell of rock that had been falling for ten thousand years and intended to go on falling for ten thousand more.

Their footsteps echoed strangely, doubled and trebled off the walls until it sounded like four people walking instead of two, then six, then a whole invisible company marching behind them. Mara found herself listening past the echoes, sorting real sounds from reflected ones the way she'd learned to sort signal noise from instrument readings. Water below. Wind above. Gravel ticking downslope here and there, small settling sounds, ordinary.

And once — just once — a sound that didn't fit. A flat metallic clank, somewhere upstream, gone before she could fix its direction.

She stopped. Eli bumped into her.

"What?"

"Did you hear that?"

He listened. The canyon offered back only its own hollow breathing. "Rock settling. This whole place is coming apart a grain at a time. That's how canyons work."

That was how canyons worked. She knew that better than anyone. She started walking again, but she filed the sound away the way she filed everything, in the precise cabinet of her mind, in a drawer marked *unexplained*, which was growing heavier every day they spent in this valley.

They walked for two hours through the winding dark. The ribbon of sky closed and opened, closed and opened. Twice they crossed scree slopes where the shelf had half-collapsed and they moved fast and light, packs unclipped and ready to shed, exactly as she'd trained him. Eli was a good trail man when he forgot to be a company man; she kept catching glimpses of the boy who'd followed her up the spoil heaps behind their father's section house, scrambling for the pure joy of altitude. Then he'd say something about tonnage or schedules or the firm, and the boy would vanish behind the man again, and Mara would look at the canyon walls instead of at him, because looking at him hurt in a way she hadn't yet decided what to do with.

They were halfway along the longest straight stretch — a quarter mile of shelf with the river roaring thirty feet below and the wall rising two hundred above — when the canyon spoke.

Not the wind-groan. Not the gravel-tick. A deep, structural sound, a crack like a giant knuckle popping, and then a rumble that Mara felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears.

She didn't think. Thinking was for afterward. Her body did what twenty years of reading failing ground had taught it: she threw herself at Eli, both hands slamming his chest, driving him off the shelf and into the shallow cave-mouth of an overhang she'd clocked twenty minutes earlier without knowing why she'd clocked it. They went down together onto gravel and cold stone as the world above them came apart.

It did not sound like falling rock. It sounded like the end of something — a tearing, grinding roar, the whole face of the wall shrugging loose at once, tons and tons of stone pouring down across the shelf and over the edge and into the river, and the dust hit them like a solid thing, blinding, choking, tasting of sulfur and ancient dryness. The concussion pressed on her eardrums. Stones rained past the overhang's mouth, some the size of her fist, one the size of a loaf of bread that struck the gravel a hand's width from her boot and split open like fruit.

Then, gradually, the roar thinned into rumble, the rumble into rattle, the rattle into silence — except the river, which roared on indifferent, and the dust, which hung in the canyon air like fog, turning the light amber and strange.

Mara lay with her cheek on cold stone and Eli's heartbeat hammering under her palm where her hand had landed on his chest, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

"Eli."

"I'm here." His voice was ragged with dust. "Mara. God. You—"

"Don't talk. Breathe slow. Check everything hurts before you move it."

She crawled off him and sat back on her heels, running her own inventory. Palms scraped. Left shin bruised deep, would stiffen. Ribs — she pressed carefully — sore but whole. Dust in her eyes, grit in her mouth, hair full of stone powder so she must have looked like something exhumed. Alive. All of her, alive.

Eli sat up coughing, gray-faced, his dark hair gone pale with dust. He looked at her with an expression she couldn't read — terror still draining out of it, and under the terror something else, something that looked almost like shame.

"The shelf," he said. "The whole shelf—"

"I know."

She crawled to the mouth of the overhang and looked out, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

The shelf was gone. Not damaged — *gone*. Forty feet of trail, a quarter mile back the way they'd come, simply erased, buried under a fan of fresh rubble that sloped from the shattered wall down into the river. Ahead, the same story: the slide had run fifty yards along the shelf, and where they needed to walk there was now a chaos of blocks, some as big as freight cars, tumbled and interlocked, with gaps between them that opened straight down onto whitewater.

Behind them, the way back was sealed. Ahead, the way forward was a question mark. And the overhang they crouched in was a pocket, not a passage — a few yards of shelter carved in the wall, ending in a blank face of stone.

"We can't go back," Eli said quietly.

"No."

"How far to the next trail junction?"

"Six miles. If the shelf holds all the way." She heard her own voice come out level, clipped, professional, and marveled distantly at the machinery of herself, still running, still producing numbers, while the rest of her stood in the wreckage of the last hour. "We go forward. Over the slide field. Slowly. We test every block before we weight it."

"Mara, that field—" He gestured at it, and his hand wasn't steady. "Those blocks aren't stable. One shifts under us and we're in the river."

"Yes."

"That's your whole answer? *Yes?*"

"It's the accurate one." She was already cinching her pack straps tight, clipping everything down, shedding nothing because they had nothing to shed. "You want comfort, wait till we're through. Right now I need your feet and your nerve, and I need them both working. Can you give me those?"

Something flickered across his face — hurt, maybe, at the flatness in hers, or guilt, though guilt about what she couldn't imagine. He nodded. "You know I can."

"Then stay three steps behind me and step where I step."

* * *

Crossing the slide field took them four hours and cost them everything they had.

It was the worst kind of ground, ground that lied. Blocks that looked seated were balanced on edges. Gaps that looked jumpable hid undercut lips. Twice Mara chose a route, tested it, and reversed, wasting twenty minutes each time, hating every minute, hearing the deadline tick in her skull louder than the river. The dust still hung in the canyon, fine and amber, softening every edge, so the whole field looked dreamlike, unreal, a landscape from a fever. Their own echoes came back wrong off the fresh rubble — flatter, closer, as if the canyon had moved its walls in to listen.

At the worst gap — eight feet of empty air between two monoliths, with the river boiling white below — Mara went first, arms out, walking a fallen slab that flexed under her boots like a plank. Halfway across, the slab shifted. Just an inch. Just enough. She froze with her heart slamming and her weight mid-transfer, one foot on the slab, one reaching for the far block, suspended over thirty feet of killing water, and for three full seconds she was nothing, held by nothing, committed to nothing, alive only because nothing had yet decided otherwise.

Then her reaching boot found the far block's edge and she poured herself onto it, flat on her stomach, arms wrapped around stone, breathing like a bellows.

"Eli. Your turn. Same line. Don't stop in the middle no matter what it does."

He crossed it better than she had — lighter, faster, refusing to let the shift spook him — and when he landed beside her he grabbed her arm, hard, and for a second neither of them said anything at all. The touch said it. *I've got you. You've got me. Whatever else is broken between us, not this.*

Then they were across, and the shelf resumed beyond the slide field, narrow and whole and mercifully boring, and the canyon began to widen toward evening, the walls leaning back, the ribbon of sky opening into a strip, then a band, then a plain grey-blue dusk.

They camped on a gravel bar where the river spread shallow and quiet, and Mara built a small fire because they needed hot food and because the cold coming off the water was bone-deep. Eli dozed against a log almost immediately, worn down to the stud, his hands still faintly trembling even in sleep. Mara sat up with her boots off, rubbing feeling back into her bruised shin, and told herself she should sleep too.

Instead she found herself replaying the clank. The metallic sound, upstream, before the slide.

Rock settling didn't clank. Rock didn't ring like iron on iron. And slides — she had read enough failed ground in her life to know the anatomy of one — slides usually had warning patterns, small sheds and sloughs beforehand, a canyon talking to itself for hours before it shouted. This one had come clean, sudden, total, right across the only route through the gorge, at the exact point where a shelf collapse would seal the canyon against anyone following them.

Or anyone ahead of them.

She turned the thought over and hated it and could not put it down. It fit too well with the claim jumpers' markers they'd found staked two days ago, fresh-cut and wrong, stakes driven by men who shouldn't have known the route. It fit with Eli's silences, the way he went careful and vague whenever she mentioned the firm. It fit with the simple arithmetic of it: someone wanted this canyon impassable, and now it was.

But a clank wasn't proof. A clank was a sound in an echoing place. She was a surveyor; she dealt in evidence, in measured lines and verified elevations, and she would not build a case on an echo.

She banked the fire and slept badly, and dreamed of falling stone.

* * *

Morning came up the canyon walls slowly, the light arriving last at the bottom like water filling a well. They broke camp in silence, ate hardtack and dried apples, and pushed on, and by mid-morning the canyon opened into a wider defile where the river slowed and spread and the going grew easy — so easy, after yesterday, that the ease itself felt suspicious.

It was Eli who spotted it first. He had gone ahead around a bend where the trail widened into a bench above the water, and Mara heard him stop, and heard the particular quality of his stopping — not the pause of a tired man but the freeze of a man seeing something he recognizes.

"What is it?"

"Nothing. A deadfall. Come look if you want."

She came around the bend and looked, and it was not a deadfall.

Set back against the base of the wall, half-hidden under a draped tarp and a screen of cut brush, sat crates. Real crates, sawn lumber, new wood still bright and unweathered, stacked three high and roped. Beside them lay a coil of fuse, a sledgehammer, and a canvas pack frame of the kind blasting crews used. The brush screening had been cut recently — the stumps pale and weeping sap.

Mara walked to the crates and stood in front of them, and the world went very quiet, the way it goes quiet after a rockslide.

The stencil on the nearest crate was crisp, black, professional. She knew the lettering style intimately. She had signed requisitions written in that lettering style for eleven years. She had watched that mark stamped onto shipping manifests in yards from Denver to the coast, had trusted it, had built her reputation on the reliability of everything it stood for.

**HARRINGTON & VOSS CONSOLIDATED — DYNAMIC No. 2 — 50 LB.**

Her old employer's mark. Fresh crates. Fifty pounds to a box, and there were six boxes visible under the tarp, and the tarp was sized for more.

Dynamite. In the gorge above Kestrel Falls. On the route to the headwaters, days ahead of any legitimate survey party, screened with cut brush by men who did not want to be found.

Grief arrived first. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that surprised her: not rage, not yet, but grief — a great slow wave of it, breaking over her from a long way off, the mourning of something she hadn't known was still alive in her. Eleven years. Eleven years of believing that the road meant something, that the numbers meant something, that the mark on the crate meant rails laid true and bridges that held and a country stitched together by people who checked their work. She had given the railroad her twenties. She had given it her father's memory, in a way — had followed the steel west because her father had laid steel, because the hum of a passing train was the closest thing she had left to his voice. And here, in a canyon a hundred miles from anywhere, was what the mark really stamped: *fifty pounds, keep dry, fuse separately.* Here was what her life's work had been carrying all along, under the freight, under the passengers, under the flag: this.

"Mara." Eli's voice, behind her, very careful. "It doesn't mean—"

"Don't." She didn't turn around. She couldn't look at him yet. Looking at him would require deciding what she saw, and she wasn't ready to decide. "Don't explain it to me. Not yet. Just — don't."

"They could be stolen. Blasters steal crates all the time, you know that, marks get—"

"*Eli.*" Still she didn't turn. The river ran past them, clear and cold and ignorant, headed for the falls, headed for the town, headed for all the people drinking it. "Yesterday. In the narrows. Before the slide. Did you hear a clank? Metal on metal?"

Silence. Long enough to be an answer.

"Eli. Look at me."

She turned then, and made herself see him — her brother, dust-streaked, hollow-eyed, standing in the amber morning light with his hands half-raised like a man caught between surrender and reach. And she watched him try to find words, watched the words fail, watched something behind his eyes give way — not into confession, not yet, whatever he was carrying was heavier than one canyon morning could crack — but into something rawer. Fear. For her, she realized. The fear was for her.

"Mara, I need you to trust me a little longer."

The sentence landed like a blade finding bone. *A little longer.* As if trust were a bridge toll. As if she owed him passage.

"Trust you." Her voice came out low and even, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "There are six hundred pounds of high explosives sitting in the watershed of a town full of people who fed me dinner and buried their dead with their own hands. There was a rockslide yesterday that fell like it was helped. And my brother — my brother, who works for the firm whose name is stenciled on those crates — asks me to trust him *a little longer*."

"I'm trying to protect you."

"From what?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. And in the space between, Mara understood that whatever he was protecting her from, he had decided it was worse than this — worse than her anger, worse than her contempt, worse than standing in front of dynamite with his sister's eyes on him. That knowledge frightened her more than the crates did.

"Carry one," she said finally, when it was clear he wouldn't speak. "One crate. Evidence stays with us. If something happens to us between here and the headwaters, I want it found on our bodies, stamped and dated, where an inspector can't lose it."

"That's fifty pounds."

"So am I. Move."

They rigged the crate into her pack frame with rope, and it rode against her spine like a verdict, and they climbed out of the defile toward the headwaters with the sun climbing red-gold behind them and the river running beside them, faithful, unknowing, carrying its cold clear water down toward Kestrel Falls — and somewhere downstream, Mara knew with a certainty that needed no instruments, men were already counting the days until they could make the water carry something else.
