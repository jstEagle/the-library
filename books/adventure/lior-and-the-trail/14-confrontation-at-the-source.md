## 14. Confrontation at the Source

The river began the way all great things begin — quietly.

Mara stood at the lip of the cirque where the Silverthread was born, a seep of snowmelt no wider than her boot, gathering itself out of a shelf of granite and running bright and cold over stones the color of old coins. Above it the headwall rose in pale terraces, patched with lingering snow, and the air was so thin and clean it felt like drinking. She had surveyed rivers for eleven years. She had never once stood at the top of one and simply listened to it start.

Behind her, Eli was quiet in a way that had nothing to do with exhaustion.

She had been counting his silences since the canyon. The rockslide had nearly buried them both, and when she'd dug him free — hands bleeding through her gloves, hauling slabs off his legs while the dust still hung like smoke — he had looked past her, up the talus slope, at something she couldn't see. She'd found the dynamite crates an hour later, half-crushed under the slide, stenciled with the mark she knew better than her own signature: *Lockridge & Vale Consolidated*. Her old employer's mark. The firm that had taught her everything. The firm that was paying her brother's salary.

She hadn't said anything yet. She'd wanted to be sure. And now, standing at the source of the river that Amos Craw had died trying to protect, she was sure.

"Eli." She didn't turn around. "Come look at this."

He came. She heard his boots on the scree, careful, deliberate — the walk of a man choosing each step. He stopped beside her, and for a moment they were just two siblings looking at water, the way they might have been as children on their father's farm, before the farm went under and their father went into the ground and Eli went to the company and Mara went to the rails.

"That's it," she said. "That's the whole beginning of it. A spring the size of a washbasin."

"It's beautiful," Eli said, and his voice cracked on the second word.

"Yes," Mara said. "It is. Now tell me why there are dynamite crates stamped with your firm's name forty feet below us, under fresh rockfall, on a trail we were told was impassable."

* * *

Eli had rehearsed this conversation for two hundred miles.

Not consciously — he wasn't a man who lied well, which was the whole trouble; lying badly was worse than not lying at all, because you carried the rehearsal with you like a stone in your boot. Every night at the fire, every morning breaking camp, he had composed sentences: *Mara, I need to explain. Mara, it isn't what you think. Mara, I did it for—* For what? For her? That lie had died somewhere back on the split trail, when he'd argued for the safe route and watched her eyes narrow, watched her do the arithmetic she was always doing, and understood that she already suspected. His sister had never once in her life failed to find the number hidden inside a thing.

So when she turned from the spring and faced him — mud-stained, rope-burned, her surveyor's case strapped across her chest like armor — he found he had no sentences left at all.

"The crates," he said. "I know about the crates."

"I know you know."

"They came up ahead of us." It came out flat, the truth falling out of him like gear off a broken pack. "A crew of six. They moved fast because they weren't carrying a full camp. They were told to stake the boundary markers early — before any survey was filed — so that when the permits were validated, the physical claim would already exist. Possession first, paper after. It's how the firm has always done it. I've watched them do it in three territories."

"Watched," Mara repeated. "Or arranged?"

The wind came down off the headwall, cold enough to make his eyes water, and he was grateful for it. Grateful for anything that gave him an excuse.

"Arranged," he said. "I signed the requisition. In Meridian, before I came to Kestrel Falls. I told myself—" He stopped. Started again. "I told myself it didn't matter, because the survey would settle it. Because if the route was feasible, then the mine was legal, and if it wasn't feasible, none of it would ever happen anyway. I told myself the markers were just insurance."

"And the trail argument. At the fork. Four days we didn't have."

Eli closed his eyes. "Yes."

"You were buying time for them."

"I was buying time for *you*," he said, and heard even as he said it how thin that was, how it rang hollow against the granite. "I thought — if the crew finished staking before we reached the source, you could turn back. You wouldn't have to see it. You could go home and never know what your name was going to be used for."

"My name." Mara's voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. He had grown up with that quietness; it was the sound of their father reading a bank notice. "What exactly is my name being used for, Eli? Say it plainly. You owe me that much, and you know it, and that's why you can't look at me."

He made himself look at her. Her face was weathered brown except where the rope burn crossed her palm, and her eyes were the same gray as their mother's, and standing there above the birthplace of the Silverthread she looked more like their father than Eli had ever dared to. Stubborn as bedrock. Precise as a plumb line.

"The concession expires in twenty-six days," he said. "You know that. What you don't know — what Ada Voss doesn't know — is that the permit has a clause. A feasibility rider. If a certified engineer files a favorable survey of the access route before expiration, the claims convert automatically. Full title. No review, no appeal, no inspector. The firm doesn't need to win an argument about the ore body. It only needs one signature on one page."

"One signature," Mara said.

"Yours." The word cost him everything he had. "They hired me to bring you here, Mara. Not to warn you. To deliver you. The whole expedition — the map, the deadline, the urgency — it was designed. You were always meant to stand right where you're standing, with your instruments in your hands, and certify the route. You were the instrument. That's all you ever were to them. That's all I let you be."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough that Eli counted his own heartbeats in it. Forty. Fifty. The spring murmured on beneath them, indifferent, patient, the way water is patient with stone.

Mara walked away from him. Not far — ten steps, twelve — to the place where the seep gathered into its first true channel, and crouched, and put two fingers into the current. He watched her hold them there until they must have ached with cold. He understood what she was doing. She was touching the thing they were talking about killing. She was making it real in her body before she answered him, because Mara Lockridge never spoke from anything she hadn't measured.

When she turned, her face was wet, and he couldn't tell if it was spray or tears, and he suspected she couldn't either.

"Amos Craw," she said. "Did you know about him?"

"No."

"He was your firm's surveyor. Twenty years ago. He found the ore body — the one in the concession filing, the one with the numbers so beautiful the directors wept over them. And then he ran the assays himself instead of letting the lab do it, and he found the ore sits inside the watershed. Directly inside it. The adits would drain straight into the source." She pointed down, at the seep, at the thread of silver beginning its long journey to Kestrel Falls. "Into that. He refused to sign the report. So they took his credentials, and they drove him into the hills, and he spent twenty years drawing maps by hand and waiting for someone honest to carry them down. We found his journal, Eli. In his cabin. I read every page by candlelight while you slept."

Eli's knees felt strange. He sat down on a boulder without deciding to.

"I didn't know," he said again, and this time it wasn't a defense, it was a wound. "I swear to God, Mara, I didn't know it was him. They never gave names. Just coordinates and deadlines. That's how they keep men like me working — you never see the whole shape of it. You see your piece. Your requisition, your schedule, your quarterly report. You tell yourself the rest is somebody else's conscience."

"Somebody else's conscience," Mara said. "And where did yours go?"

"With Father," he said, before he could stop it.

The words fell between them like dropped tools. Below them the young river bent around a spur of granite and hurried on, already committed, already gone.

"You think I don't know that?" Eli went on, and now it was coming up out of him the way floodwater comes, fifteen years of it, everything he had never said at the funeral or the reading of the will or the one terrible Christmas at the depot hotel. "You think I don't know exactly what I chose? You got the grief, Mara. You got to be angry and stay angry and carry him around like a torch. I got the ledgers. I was nineteen and the farm was mortgaged to the ridgepole and somebody had to feed us, and the railroad was the only thing in the territory paying cash, and I walked into that office hating them — hating them for what they did to him, for the spur line that killed him — and I shook the hand of the man who signed the contract, and I have shaken that hand every week for fifteen years. Do you understand? I didn't choose the company over family. I chose the company *because* it was family's killer. Somebody had to be inside it. Somebody had to watch. And then one day I looked up and I was the hand, Mara. I was the hand shaking other people's hands. And I told myself that was penance too."

"It wasn't," Mara said quietly.

"No," Eli said. "It was just a job with a wound inside it."

* * *

Mara stood at the edge of the spring and felt the two halves of herself pull apart along a seam she had always pretended wasn't there.

There was the engineer. Eleven years of her life lived by the compass and the level — the woman who could sight a grade to the eighth of an inch, who had laid rail across country other men called impossible, whose signature opened mountains. That woman understood, with a professional intimacy that frightened her, exactly what Eli was describing. She had seen the filings. The access route was *good*. She had known it since the gorge, since the canyon, since every mile of this trail her trained eye kept measuring despite her: a grade of four percent achievable along the eastern bench, ballast available at two cuts, a tunnel of less than nine hundred feet through the shoulder of the headwall. Buildable. She could write it down in an afternoon. She could build a railroad to this place, and the railroad would bring the stamps and the mills and the cyanide vats, and the town downstream would drink the tailings for a generation, and her name would be on the certification the way her father's name was on the spur-line contract that had sent him into the cut the day the hillside let go.

And there was the other woman. The one who had carried a dead prospector's map two hundred miles because a dying man asked her to. The one who had stood in Kestrel Falls and watched Sheriff Voss read the names of the lost off the mine office wall. The one who had held her fingers in the source of the Silverthread and felt, absurdly, that the water was holding back.

Love against duty. She had thought, climbing this valley, that she knew which side of that line she stood on. But duty had two faces, and that was the trap Amos had died inside of. Duty to the profession. Duty to the truth. Duty to the work of her own hands, which wanted, with an honesty that felt like betrayal, to build the line — because it *could* be built, and engineers were not permitted to pretend otherwise. And against all of it, the plain human fact of the water, and the town, and the boy who had once shared a blanket with her on the porch of a foreclosed farmhouse and sworn he'd take care of her, and who had spent fifteen years taking care of her in the only currency he had left.

"Eli," she said. "Look at me."

He looked. He looked terrible — hollowed, unshaven, sitting on the boulder like a man waiting for a verdict, and she realized with a lurch that he was. That he had been waiting since the canyon. Maybe since Meridian. Maybe since the day he'd signed the requisition for dynamite with his sister's name in the margin of the plan.

"If I refuse," she said, "they'll ruin me. You know that. Voss told me what happens to engineers who file adverse findings against Lockridge & Vale. There won't be another commission. There may not be another territory that'll license me. Everything I am goes into that refusal, and it buys a river I can't eat and a town that barely knows my name."

"I know."

"And if I sign, I save my career, and your medical debts if you take another bullet, and the firm makes me rich enough to buy back the farm twice over." Her voice stayed level. Only her hands shook, and she folded them. "And every child in Kestrel Falls drinks poison before they're grown, and Amos Craw's twenty years in the hills amount to nothing, and I spend the rest of my life knowing the sound this water makes."

"Mara—"

"I'm not asking you to argue," she said. "I'm telling you what the choice weighs. Because you never weighed it, Eli. You signed papers without weighing them, and that's the difference between us, and it's the only difference, and I need you to finally, finally understand it." She knelt beside the spring, and dipped her whole hand in, and lifted it, and let the water fall back into itself, silver in the last of the afternoon light. "I loved you through all of it. Through the funeral. Through the will. Through every year you didn't write. Loving you was never the hard part. The hard part is that I love you and I am still going to do the right thing, and you have to decide, right now, whether you can live in the same world as that decision — or whether you're going to run back down this mountain and tell them where I am and what I've found."

The wind moved through the cirque. Somewhere above, a patch of snow let go of the headwall and slid, soft as flour, into shadow.

Eli stood up slowly. He took the requisition copy out of his coat — the original, the one with the boundary coordinates and the staking schedule and, in the corner, in his own handwriting, the initials *M.L.* beside the notation *certifying engineer: secured*. He had carried it the whole way like a man carries a splinter too deep to dig out. He looked at it for a long moment.

Then he tore it in half, and in half again, and dropped the pieces into the spring, where the newborn river took them, turned them over once, and carried them away downstream toward Kestrel Falls.

"They'll come for the journal," he said hoarsely. "Vale keeps enforcers on retainer in Meridian — six men, maybe eight, and they'll have ridden the moment the staking crew signaled. They'll want the journal burned and the map destroyed and your finding unwritten, and they won't care what they have to do to get it. We have maybe two days' lead. Less, if they pushed hard."

Mara stared at the torn paper dissolving in the current — at her own initials going white and soft and gone — and felt something in her chest come loose that had been clenched since the depot at Kestrel Falls. Not forgiveness. Not yet. But the space where forgiveness might someday fit.

"Then we'd better get down the mountain," she said. "Get the case. We survey on the way — I take my readings at every bench and every grade, honest ones, and we file the finding ourselves before they can file anything at all."

Eli shouldered the pack. At the edge of the cirque he stopped and looked back once at the spring, small and bright and utterly indifferent to everything men wanted from it.

"Mara," he said. "If they catch us—"

"Then they catch us," she said, and started down the trail with the sun dropping red behind the headwall, "and they find out what this river's made of. Come on. We've got a town to save and about thirty hours to do it in — and Eli? Ride behind me where I can see your hands."

He almost smiled. Almost. And followed her down into the darkening valley, where, far below, lanterns were already moving along the river road — too many lanterns, and moving too fast.
