## 15. The Survey

The morning of the third day came up cold and clean off the snowfields, and Mara Lockridge woke in Amos Craw's cabin with the journal closed on her chest like a hand pressing her down into the bunk.

She had read it twice more after Eli slept. The later entries were terse — a surveyor's shorthand, elevations and bearings and then, increasingly, sentences that had nothing to do with either. *They want me to move the source. As if a river is a line on paper.* And near the end, in a hand gone shaky: *I will not sign it. Let them take my name. I have seen what the water does for this valley.*

Now she rose in the blue half-light, dressed by feel, and stood a long moment at the door with her instruments in their leather case, listening to the Silverthread talking to itself somewhere below the talus. It was a sound she had heard all her life in one form or another — under trestles, through culverts, in the drainage ditches beside every cut her father ever staked — but here, at its beginning, it sounded different. Smaller. Stubborner. A thing that had been deciding the shape of this valley since before there were men to argue about it.

Eli was still asleep, his bandaged arm cradled against him, his breathing shallow but even. She let him sleep. What she had to do today she would have to do alone.

* * *

The route the concession papers described began at the saddle below the eastern cirque and ran four miles along the river's north bank to the headwater basin itself. Four miles. On her old surveys she had covered twenty in a day without thinking about it. But those had been railroad miles, measured with an eye toward what could be made of them, and this morning Mara forbade herself that eye. That was the whole discipline of it. An honest survey was not a matter of arithmetic; it was a matter of refusing, over and over, the small flattering corrections that expertise offered like a hand extended in friendship. *You could shift the grade here. You could shave that shoulder. Nobody would know.* Everybody knew. That was how men like Amos Craw ended up dying in cabins above the tree line.

She started at first light with the transit set up on a granite shelf, and she worked the way her father had taught her before he taught her anything else: plumb, level, read, record, and only then think. The numbers went into the book in her own tight hand, no interpretation, no allowance. The canyon walls narrowed as she moved upstream until they were scarcely forty feet apart, the river compressed between them into a white rope of water that filled the gorge with noise. She shot the profiles. She measured the falls where the river dropped twelve feet through a slot you could jump across. She noted the scree slopes, the avalanche chutes streaked with old timber debris, the seeps weeping out of the rock face in silver threads that froze and thawed and would, in any season, be shrugging boulders onto whatever anyone chose to build beneath them.

By noon she had the shape of it, and the shape of it was bad. Not impossible — that was the trap, and she saw it now with terrible clarity, saw why the company had wanted *her* signature specifically, why Eli's firm had bankrolled a concession on ground their own engineers must have known was marginal. The line was buildable. That was the horror of it. With enough cuts, enough fills, enough timber trestle and blasted shoulder, a man could carry rails up this gorge. She could draw it. She could draw it beautifully. Every number she took today only confirmed what her hands already knew how to do: bend terrain to purpose, find the grade hiding inside the mountain, bring the iron through.

Any engineer worth her transit could certify this route.

She sat on a boulder above the slot falls and ate hardtack and looked downstream, the way the water went, and tried to make herself see it as the company saw it — a corridor, a right-of-way, a problem with solutions. And the solutions assembled themselves in her mind unbidden, neat and complete, because that was what twenty years of training did. Cut and fill through the narrows. Trestle at the falls. Grade holding at two percent if she swung the alignment wide around the cirque —

And stopped.

Because the alignment swung wide around the cirque directly across the mouth of the headwater basin. There was no other way. She had known it since yesterday, walking in, and had refused to know it, and now the transit had taken the refusal out of her hands. To bring a rail line to the ore body the concession claimed, the line had to cross the basin floor. And the basin floor was the source.

Not near the source. Not above it. The springs rose out of the gravel bar in the exact center of the basin — she counted seven of them, clear mouths breathing cold air, feeding a pool that spilled over a lip of stone and became, from that spill, the Silverthread. Any crossing meant fill. Fill in a floodplain of springs meant embankment. Embankment meant impoundment. And the river, blocked, would not stop being a river; it would simply begin to rise behind the ballast and the ties, backing up into the meadow, drowning the springs one by one, until the company did what companies always did when water stood between them and a schedule.

They would dam it. Formally, deliberately, with masonry and gates. Divert the headwaters through a flume to keep the construction dry, run the line across the dry bed, and leave the source of the Silverthread standing behind a company wall, metered, drawn down in summer, released when it suited the mill downstream at the mine they intended to sink into the very aquifer that fed it.

She got out Amos's map and laid it open on the rock, weighting the corners with stones, and compared it against her own field book entry by entry. His bearings matched hers within minutes of arc. His notes on the springs matched her count. And there, in the margin of his careful drafting, the annotation she had read in the journal and not understood until this moment, written small and furious: *They knew. They always knew. The ore report is real — the vein runs true — but it sits under the water table and the only way to work it is to take the river apart.*

Mara closed the field book and held it in both hands and felt the weight of it change.

It was the same weight it had always been — leather, board, paper, maybe two pounds — but it had become something else entirely, the way a tool becomes a weapon without changing shape. This was the document. This was what thirty days had been for. If she signed the certification — *surveyed, examined, and found feasible* — her name would travel down out of these mountains ahead of the snow and arrive in some territorial office as a sentence of death passed on a valley she had never met until a dying man put a map in her hands. The springs would go first. Then the meadow. Then, come the dry years that always came, the river itself, throttled at its throat so that a stamp mill could have water in August. Kestrel Falls would watch its river learn a new master. The town would not die quickly — towns like that didn't — it would die the way mining towns died, slowly, in the voice, in the leaving, one family at a time boarding out with everything they owned tied under a wagon canvas.

And she could sign it. That was the thing that sat in her chest like swallowed stone. She *could*. Her signature was good. It was trusted from here to the coast; men had built bridges on it. Everything she had spent twenty years becoming — the precision, the pride, the armor of competence she had worn through every lonely year on the road — all of it converged on this page and made her exactly, precisely, the person the company needed. They hadn't hired her despite her skill. They had hired her *for* it. Her expertise was the weapon; they had merely needed her finger on the trigger.

She thought of her father, dead in a washout on a line he had certified in a season too short, because the schedule mattered more than the spring thaw, and his signature had been good too.

"No," she said aloud, and the gorge took the word and threw it back at her from both walls, doubled and doubled again, until it sounded like a crowd.

* * *

She worked the rest of the day anyway.

That surprised her, how much it cost and how necessary it was — she could not refuse a route she had not fully surveyed, could not stand before Ada Voss and the town and whatever inspector eventually came with nothing but conviction. Conviction was what Amos had, and it had gotten him a cabin and a grave. She needed numbers. So she shot the basin crossings three times from three stations and got the same answer three times, and she measured the flow at the lip of the pool with a plank and a notch cut to a known width, timing the water with her watch, and computed the yield, and wrote it down: enough water in this source to feed every farm and faucet in Kestrel Falls four times over, in the driest August on record. Enough to make a town permanent. Enough, diverted, to make a mine rich.

Two figures. One river. It came down, as everything came down, to which column you served.

Toward late afternoon she climbed the moraine at the basin's edge and stood looking at the whole of it — the cirque holding its snow like a cupped hand, the meadow green as a promise, the seven springs steaming faintly in the cold, the pool so clear she could count the stones on its bottom at six feet down. Somewhere under that meadow lay the vein, real and rich, exactly where Amos said. The earth had put its treasure under its wellspring, and only a certain kind of creature would dig through the one to get at the other.

She opened the field book to the certification page. The form was printed there, carried all this way folded in oilcloth: the blanks for date, for route description, for the engineer's finding, and at the bottom the line for her name, waiting with the patience of all printed things.

She did not sign it. She turned past it instead and wrote, in the plain language she would need when she stood in front of people who were not engineers:

*Route traversable at 2% maximum grade by conventional cut, fill, and trestle construction. No alternative alignment exists that avoids crossing the headwater basin. Crossing necessitates impoundment or diversion of the primary source of the Silverthread River. Certification of feasibility is therefore inseparable from destruction of the watershed supplying the town of Kestrel Falls. The line can be built. Building it requires killing the river.*

She read it back twice. It was true, and it was hers, and it would cost her everything — the license men respected, the work she loved, the identity she had worn so long she sometimes mistook it for herself. She felt the grief of that arriving already, quiet and enormous, like weather coming over a ridge.

Then she heard the crunch of boots on scree behind her, and turned, and it was not Eli.

There were three of them coming up the moraine from the south trail, moving with the unhurried economy of men paid by the day, and the one in front wore a canvas coat with the collar up and carried a rifle across his back the way a carpenter carries a hammer — as a tool, not a threat, which was worse. Mara's hand went to the field book at her hip before she could stop it, and the man in front saw the gesture, and smiled.

"Evening, ma'am," he called, friendly as a neighbor. "You'd be Miss Lockridge. We've been looking forward to meeting you."

Behind him, far below in the gorge, the river kept on running, clear and loud and unaware, toward a town that had four days left.
