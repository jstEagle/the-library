## 16. Descent

The report weighed less than Eli expected. Fourteen pages of Mara's precise hand, the survey folded into oilcloth and bound with cord, riding against his chest in the inner pocket of his coat where it rose and fell with every breath like a second heart. He had carried ledgers heavier than this across three territories. He had carried contracts that moved towns, that erased valleys from maps before anyone thought to draw them. None of them had ever felt like this — like carrying a lit fuse through dry country.

They came down off the high shoulder of the divide at first light, the canyon of echoes falling away behind them, and the Silverthread appeared below as a thread of hammered silver unspooling through green country. Two days' hard travel to Kestrel Falls, if the weather held and their luck held, and Eli did not believe for a moment that either would. He knew the men who would be coming. He had signed the requisitions for their wages.

"You're favoring the left side." Mara's voice came from ahead of him, flat with the particular care she used when she was worried and refusing to show it. She hadn't looked back at him. She was reading the ground instead — the way she read everything, terrain and timetables and people, hunting for the flaw.

"It's a bruise."

"From a rockslide."

"From a rockslide," he agreed. "Which I walked away from. Which you dug me out of. I'm aware of the accounting, Mara."

She stopped then, half-turned on the trail, and the morning light caught her face — thinner than he remembered from Chicago, wind-scoured, her eyes holding something he'd spent the whole climb upstream trying not to name. Not forgiveness. Not yet. But the door to it, unlocked, standing open a crack.

"The accounting," she said, "is that you're going to tell Ada Voss everything. The claim jumpers. The dynamite. The firm's name on those crates. Before you set foot in that depot."

"I know what I'm going to do."

"Do you?" Something moved behind her eyes — not cruelty, but the old sisterly precision, the one that had found every lie he'd ever told since they were children stealing apples off the Hollis farm. "Because you've been quiet for two days, Eli, and you were never quiet. Even as a boy you narrated your own sins. So when you go silent, I've learned to wonder what you're rehearsing."

He wanted to laugh. It hurt too much to laugh. The rockslide had given him more than a bruise; there was a deep ache under his ribs where a slab had clipped him, and every mile downhill drove it deeper. He adjusted the pack strap and said, "I'm rehearsing how to say *I'm sorry* in a way you might actually hear. It's a hard translation. The words don't have good equivalents."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned and kept walking, and over her shoulder she said, "Try plain language. You always dressed everything up until it died."

The river ran beside them now, loud and cold and utterly indifferent, and Eli thought: *that's* the plain language. The water didn't dress anything up. It went where gravity told it, and it took the shape of whatever held it, and it never once pretended to be something it wasn't. He had spent forty years being something he wasn't. The last thirty days had been the unwinding of it, and the unwinding was not finished, and he suspected it would never be finished, and that maybe that was the point — that a man ought to spend what remained of his life becoming honest at a rate slightly faster than he'd become otherwise.

They made eight miles by noon, dropping out of the pine into aspen country where the light came down green-gold and the trail followed an old game path along the water. Mara called a halt at a bend where the current pooled slow and deep, and they drank, and ate hardtack and dried venison, and neither of them spoke about what waited downstream. There was no need. They both knew the arithmetic: if they reached Kestrel Falls tomorrow evening, they reached it with hours to spare. If they lost even half a day, the permits lapsed on their own and none of this mattered — no signature required, no refusal possible, just silence doing the work that courage should have done. And if the company reached them first—

Eli didn't finish the thought. He didn't have to. They both heard the same silence where the birds should have been.

"Mara." He said it quietly. "Upstream side. Behind the deadfall."

She froze with the canteen halfway to her lips. Then, without hurry, without any sign she'd heard him, she capped the canteen and began repacking the food with the deliberate slowness of a woman buying time. Her eyes flicked once — once only — toward the aspen stand above the trail, where between the white trunks something had shifted that was not wind. Wind did not hold its breath.

"How many?" she murmured.

"I counted three. Could be five." He drew his coat closed over the report. "The bend past the pool narrows. If we push through fast—"

"They're not trying to stop us here. Here they're watching." Her voice was very steady, and he recognized the steadiness for what it was: her armor, the competence she wore the way other women wore jewelry. "If they wanted us dead, we'd already be dead. They want to know what we're carrying. They want to see it destroyed in front of witnesses so there's no question later." A pause. "Or they want us to run, and run tired, and be caught somewhere with no witnesses at all."

"So we don't run."

"So we don't run." She stood, shouldered her pack, and gave him a look that across thirty years of sibling warfare meant approximately *stay alive, you idiot*. "Stay behind me on the narrow ground. Your ribs are bad and you're a terrible liar and both of those things make you slow."

"That's the nicest thing you've said to me since Chicago."

"Don't spend it all in one place."

* * *

The ambush came at the narrows, exactly where the land offered no choices, because men who planned ambushes were engineers of a kind too, and Eli understood their thinking the way he understood all thinking that reduced a landscape to its uses.

They stepped out of the trees with the unhurried confidence of hired violence — three of them visible, two more flanking through the brush, rifles carried low and easy. The one in front was broad and gray-templed, with the settled calm of a man who had done this often enough that it no longer qualified as an event. Eli knew the type. He had approved this type's expense accounts.

"Mr. Lockridge." The gray-templed man's voice was almost warm. "Mr. Renner sends his regards. Says you've been out of contact longer than expected. Says there's concern."

"There's concern," Eli repeated.

"There's concern about the survey. About the journal. About what gets filed tomorrow." The man's gaze moved to Mara, catalogued her, dismissed her, returned to Eli — and that dismissal was the single greatest mistake of the afternoon, though none of them yet knew it. "The firm's position is simple. Hand over the documents, walk away, everyone's whole. Mr. Renner is prepared to be generous. Pensions have been arranged. Positions. There's a life after this valley, Mr. Lockridge, and it can start today."

"And if we refuse?"

The man sighed, genuinely regretful, a craftsman confronted with unnecessary difficulty. "Then we take them. And what happens in the taking is up to you."

Eli felt Mara shift half a step behind him, and he understood suddenly — with the strange clarity that arrives in the last seconds before a thing goes wrong — exactly what she was going to do. She was going to talk. She was going to deploy facts and figures and the full weight of her certainty, because that was her weapon and she believed, as she had always believed, that precision could cut through anything. And these men were not precision's audience. These men had been paid to end a conversation.

"No," Eli said.

Mara glanced at him.

"No," he said again, louder, stepping forward, putting himself squarely between his sister and the rifles. "You can tell Renner from me — from *Eli Lockridge*, who signed his damn requisitions and sat in his damn boardroom — that the survey gets filed. That the journal gets read aloud. That every name in it gets spoken in front of the territorial inspector, starting with mine. That's the firm's position. That's *my* position. And the difference between us—" his voice cracked, and he let it crack, let them hear it, "—the difference between us is that I finally know which side of the ledger I'm standing on."

The gray-templed man's regret curdled into something older and simpler. "Pity," he said, and lifted the rifle.

Later — in the fevered days afterward, in the long slow convalescence that would follow — Eli would try to reconstruct those four seconds and find them mostly gone, worn smooth by shock, leaving only fragments. The crack of the shot arriving before the muzzle flash registered. The impact like a sledgehammer swung into his back, spinning him, the world tilting into green sky and white trunks. Mara screaming his name in a voice he had never heard from her, a voice stripped of all armor down to the raw wood underneath. And himself, falling, doing the only mathematics that had ever come naturally to him: *she is behind me, the bullet was for her, my body is between them, and that is the first true thing I have built in twenty years.*

He hit the ground on his side, and the pain arrived late and total, a white bloom spreading through his chest, and he discovered he could not draw a full breath. The report — God, the report — still pressed against him inside the coat, soaked now, warm.

Through the roaring in his ears he heard the fight he couldn't see: Mara moving like water finding its level, low and fast, using the deadfall, using the narrows' broken ground that she had read in three seconds while the rest of them saw only scenery. A rifle cracked and splintered bark above him. A man shouted. Something heavy went into the pool with a tremendous splash, and the gray-templed man's calm finally broke — Eli heard it break, heard the orders turn from command to plea — and then the two remaining riflemen were backing away into the aspens, dragging their dripping companion, and the gray-templed man fired one last wild shot and followed, and the forest swallowed them, and the birds slowly, cautiously, resumed.

Footsteps, running. Hands turning him over with a gentleness that shook worse than the bullet had.

"Eli. Eli, look at me. Look at *me*."

He looked at her. Her face was above his, ashen, streaked with dirt, and her hands pressed hard against his back where the blood came from, and she was talking — rapid, precise, clinical, listing his injuries like a survey, because the numbers were her armor and right now the armor was the only thing keeping her upright.

"Entry's high, left of the spine. No exit I can find. You're breathing, that's — keep breathing, shallow, don't try to fill it all the way—" Her voice snagged. Broke. Reassembled itself. "Why did you do that. Why did you *do* that."

"Plain language," he whispered. Blood came with the words, and he tasted copper. "You said. Try plain language."

"Don't you dare be clever. Don't you *dare*."

"Mara." He caught her wrist. His hand wouldn't close properly; the strength had gone somewhere else, poured out onto the ground beneath him. "Listen. The report. Inner pocket. It has to reach Ada. Tomorrow. Before sundown. Promise me you'll—"

"Stop it. Stop talking like that. You're going to carry it yourself, you stubborn ox, you're going to walk into that depot on your own feet and say it yourself, because it has to be *you*, do you understand? It has to be your mouth that says it or none of it counts—"

"It counts," he said. The white bloom was spreading, softening the edges of everything, the aspens going gold and blurred around her face. "It counts coming from you. It always counted. Coming from you. I just — I spent twenty years making sure nobody listened."

Her hands pressed harder. She was crying now, silently, furiously, hating every tear, and he watched his precise, armored, magnificent sister come apart above him and thought: *there it is. The plain language. Took us thirty years to find it.*

"The cabin," she was saying, forcing herself back into procedure, into lists, into the next hour and the hour after that, because that was how she survived and he loved her for it. "Amos's cabin is six miles back. I can't move you six miles with a chest wound, but I can't leave you here, and I can't—" She stopped. Drew one shaking breath. Made the decision he could see assembling itself behind her eyes, the engineer weighing load, distance, time, and choosing the impossible option because the others were worse. "I'm getting you to Kestrel Falls. Tonight. Both of us. The report and the journal and you. All of it arrives together or none of it does."

"Six miles," he managed. "Of trail. With me on your back."

"You weigh less than a transit instrument. I've carried worse uphill."

He laughed, and laughing was a mistake, fire all through his chest, and she hushed him with trembling hands and began tearing strips from her shirt hem, packing the wound, building a litter from pack frames and rope with the swift economy of a woman who had surveyed a thousand miles of impossible country and refused, absolutely refused, to let this be the line she could not build.

The shadows lengthened through the aspens. Somewhere downstream, twelve miles on, the lights of Kestrel Falls would be coming on, and Sheriff Ada Voss would be standing at her window watching the road, and the deadline would be closing like a hand.

"Hold on," Mara said — to him, to herself, to the whole unjust arithmetic of the day. "Hold on, Eli. We're almost home."

And Eli, drifting at the edge of consciousness with his sister's heartbeat hammering against his own through the litter poles, thought that he had spent his whole life drawing lines across other people's country, and that this — this rough litter, this desperate mile, this woman hauling him toward a town that owed him nothing — was the first line he had ever helped build that led anywhere worth going.

Behind them, far up the trail, a rider broke from the tree line and turned downriver at a gallop.

Toward Kestrel Falls. Toward the lawyer waiting with his papers. Toward tomorrow.

Toward the deadline.
