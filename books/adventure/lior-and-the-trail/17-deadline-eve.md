## 17. Deadline Eve

Ada Voss had learned to measure a day by the light on the water.

Kestrel Falls sat in a fold of mountains where the sun arrived late and left early, and in thirty years of wearing the tin star she had come to know every shade the river turned between dawn and dark — the pewter of first light, the hard blue of noon, the copper spill of evening when the falls caught fire for ten minutes and then went out. She stood at her office window now, watching that copper fade, and she thought: *one more hour, maybe less.*

The last day. The permits died at midnight unless an engineer's certification was filed with the territorial clerk, and the only engineer who could file it was somewhere upriver in country that had tried to kill her twice already this week.

Ada had done what she could do. She'd wired the territorial inspector at Silver Bend — twice, then a third time when no answer came back. She'd deputized four men she trusted and two she didn't, because desperate times made strange arithmetic. She'd watched the company's lawyer, a smooth-browed man named Corliss who wore his city suit like armor against the mud of Main Street, set up his folding table on the depot platform at noon and wait there all afternoon like a spider who had learned patience from a book.

And now the light was going, and there was nothing left to do but stand at the window and count the minutes.

"You'll wear a groove in those boards," said Ruth Calloway from the doorway. Ruth ran the boarding house, had buried a husband in the closed mines, and had appointed herself Ada's conscience sometime around the flood, whether Ada wanted one or not. "Come sit. Drink something. Waiting's a kind of work too, but it goes easier sitting down."

"I can't sit."

"You've been standing since Tuesday."

Ada almost smiled. Almost. Down the street, Corliss checked his pocket watch for perhaps the hundredth time, and beside his table two of the firm's men leaned against the depot wall with their hats low and their eyes moving. They hadn't been subtle about the rifles under their coats. They weren't trying to be. The message was the point: *we are here, we are armed, and midnight is coming.*

"They know she's coming down," Ada said. "That's why he's still out there. He wouldn't waste the theater otherwise."

"Or nobody's coming at all," Ruth said gently. "River's high. Canyon road washed out Tuesday. You said yourself—"

"I know what I said."

What Ada had said, three days ago, when she'd sent Tom Bracken up the valley trail with a mule and a prayer: *if Mara Lockridge is alive, she'll come down the river path, because Amos Craw came down it once with a map in his coat, and people like that don't take the long way around.* She hadn't known if it was faith or foolishness. She still didn't.

She picked up her hat.

* * *

The river path came into town through the willows below the millrace, and Ada walked it now with a lantern unlit in her hand, saving the oil, saving everything, hoarding small things the way a drowning man hoards breath. The water was loud tonight. Spring melt had the Silverthread running brown-shouldered and furious, and the spray off the falls hung in the air like a fog that never quite lifted, beading on her coat, silvering her hair.

She heard them before she saw them — not voices but the particular broken rhythm of someone carrying weight that fights back. A stumble. A low word. Then shapes resolving out of the dusk under the willows: a woman, mud to the hip, hair plastered flat, leading a horse that moved with the careful, favoring gait of an animal carrying something precious and hurt.

"Mara Lockridge," Ada said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt.

The woman looked up. Ada had met her twice before, briefly — a surveyor's eyes, a handshake like a level finding true. What came down the path now was something else. The same face, but scoured. There was blood on her sleeve that wasn't hers, and her right hand was wrapped in a filthy bandage, and when she spoke her voice was gravel dragged over rock.

"Sheriff Voss." She stopped, swayed slightly, corrected herself. "Eli's shot. Through the shoulder. It's through-and-through, I think, but he's lost blood and the fever started this morning. I need Doc Merriweather and I need him now."

"Doc's at my office. I sent for him an hour ago." Ada stepped forward, took the horse's bridle, felt it trembling under her hands — the animal as spent as its riders. "Who did the shooting?"

"Company men. Four of them, up past the gorge. They wanted the map and Amos Craw's journal." Mara's jaw worked. "They got neither."

Behind her, slung across the horse's back and half-supported by a rope harness Mara had clearly rigged herself, lay a man Ada recognized from the depot photographs in the firm's papers — Eli Lockridge, the railroad's own man, gray-faced, breathing in shallow hitches, one arm bound tight against his chest with a strip torn from somebody's shirt.

"He took a bullet meant for me," Mara said. Flatly, as if reciting a survey note. As if the words cost nothing, which Ada knew they cost everything. "I want that understood up front, whatever else happens tonight. Whatever he's done — and I know some of what he's done — he paid part of the bill on that canyon trail."

"We'll sort accounts later." Ada was already moving, leading the horse toward town, raising her voice to split the dusk. "Tom! Tom Bracken! Get Doc Merriweather and bring the buckboard!"

* * *

They got Eli onto Doc Merriweather's table by full dark, and Ada stood in the doorway of her own office while the lamp threw shadows and the doctor worked, and she watched Mara Lockridge refuse a chair.

That was the thing Ada would remember afterward, in the years when the story got told and retold in the Falls until it wore smooth as river stone: that Mara Lockridge, after nine days in the high country, after a rope bridge and a canyon and dynamite and bullets, stood upright against the wall with her ruined hand cradled against her chest and watched her brother bleed, and did not sit down, and did not look away, and answered every question put to her in a voice like a driven stake.

"The journal," Ada said. "You have it?"

Mara drew it from inside her coat — a squat, water-stained book bound in oilcloth, tied with a leather cord gone soft with age. She held it a moment before surrendering it, the way a person holds something they have carried so far it has become part of their skeleton.

"Amos Craw kept it from the day the firm hired him to the day they drove him out," she said. "It's all in there. The ore reports they told him to falsify. The letters. The plan to use a certified feasibility survey — mine, specifically — to legitimize claims that sit directly in the headwaters. Damming the source. Diverting the river." Her voice did not rise. That was the terrible part of it; it dropped instead, quieter with each sentence, like water cutting stone. "If I sign their papers tonight, Kestrel Falls drinks that mine's tailings within two years. Every well. Every field. The falls themselves."

"And your certification is the last piece they need."

"It's the only piece they need. The permits die at midnight without it." Mara looked at the journal in Ada's hands. "Read the entry dated the fourth of March. Read it aloud if you have to. He wrote it the night they fired him. He wrote—" and here, for the first time, the gravel broke, just a fracture, quickly sealed, "—he wrote that he hoped whoever found the map would be stubborn. His word. *Stubborn.*"

From the table, a sound. Eli Lockridge, half-conscious under the doctor's hands, murmuring something. Mara crossed to him in two strides and took his good hand, and Ada turned away to give them the small privacy of her own back.

* * *

Corliss came at half past eleven.

Ada heard the buckboard before she saw it, and she was waiting on the boardwalk when it rolled up, lanterns swinging, and the lawyer descended in his city boots with the folded papers already in his hand as though he'd rehearsed the dismount. Behind him, at a discreet distance, the two riflemen sat their horses like furniture.

"Sheriff." He tipped his hat. Polite as arsenic. "I understand the surveyor has returned."

"She's returned."

"Then I'll make this brief, because I'm given to understand the hour matters." He smiled with exactly the bottom half of his face. "Miss Lockridge files her certification of the Silverthread route, properly executed, before midnight. In exchange: the firm covers all medical expenses for her brother — and I'm told gunshot wounds run expensive — withdraws the trespass charges pending against him, restores her standing with the engineering registry, which I'm told her little stunt with the mountain spur reports has rather jeopardized, and offers her a position. Chief surveyor. Western division. A salary with another zero on it than anything she's seen."

He let it sit there, the whole glittering package of it, priced to the penny against a bleeding man on a doctor's table and a career already half in ruins.

"And if she doesn't sign?" Ada asked, though she knew.

Corliss spread his hands. "Then the permits lapse, the firm's investment is written off, and gentlemen write off investments very badly, Sheriff. But surely it won't come to that. A woman of her capabilities understands that a river is just water, and water is just a resource, and resources exist to serve progress. Whereas a brother—" he glanced toward the lit window of the doc's office, where a shadow moved, "—a brother is irreplaceable."

Inside, Ada knew, Mara had heard every word through the open door. She could see it in the way the woman stood when she came out onto the boardwalk — too straight, the way people stand when bending would break them.

"Say the medical part again," Mara said quietly.

"Miss Lockridge—"

"Say it again. All of it. Slowly."

And Corliss, mistaking hunger for weakness, said it again, slowly, laying each term out like coins on a counter, and Ada watched Mara Lockridge listen with her bandaged hand pressed flat against her thigh and her eyes fixed on the middle distance where, Ada suspected, she was seeing not the money or the title but a rope bridge over a gorge, a bullet's arc, a hand catching hers out of white water.

* * *

It was Ruth Calloway, God bless her, who broke the moment — coming down the boardwalk with a shawl over her nightdress and fury in her face, and behind her, drawn by lantern light and word-of-mouth, others. Tom Bracken. The Widow Prentiss. Old Soderman from the assay office, still in his suspenders. Miners' wives. The miller. A dozen, then two dozen, gathering along Main Street in their nightclothes and work coats, silent, watching, the way a town watches when it has decided to be present for its own history.

Mara looked at them. Then at the doc's window. Then at Corliss's papers, pale rectangles in the lamplight.

"There's a version of tonight," she said, and her voice carried down the street the way voices carry when a crowd goes utterly still, "where I sign those papers, and my brother lives comfortable, and I get my name back, and in two years the children of this town drink poison out of their own wells. I've been doing the arithmetic since the canyon, Mr. Corliss. I've had nothing to do up there but walk and bleed and add figures." She stepped forward, close enough that Ada saw her hands were shaking — and saw also that her feet were not. "Here's mine. You can keep your salary. Keep your charges. Keep your zero."

"Mara—" It was Eli, in the doorway, propped upright against the frame with fresh bandages blooming red at the shoulder, gray as ash and burning-eyed. "Don't you dare trade yourself for me. I heard the terms. I'm telling you — I'll pay my own bills. I always have. Don't you dare."

"Shut up, Eli," Mara said, without turning around, and something in it was so much their father's tone that several townsfolk laughed despite themselves, a short sharp bark of release.

Then she turned to Ada, and held out her bandaged hand, palm up.

"Sheriff Voss. As the nearest officer of the territory, I'm asking you to witness. And I'm asking for the journal back."

Ada put the oilcloth book into her palm.

Midnight was twenty minutes off. Somewhere east, over the black shoulders of the mountains, the first stars were out, and the river below the falls ran loud and brown and alive, and Corliss stood on the boardwalk with his useless papers beginning, very slightly, to tremble in his manicured hand.

"Read it," Mara said to the crowd, opening the journal to a marked page. "All of you. It's your river. It's time somebody read you what's been written about it."

And she began, in a voice scraped raw, to read Amos Craw's words aloud by lantern light while the whole town of Kestrel Falls leaned in to listen, and the clock above the depot climbed toward the stroke of twelve.
