## 18. The Refusal

The sun was going down behind the western ridge when Mara came down out of the hills with her brother bleeding in the back of a borrowed buckboard and Amos Craw's journal pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Kestrel Falls had changed since she'd left it eleven days ago. The spring melt had receded from the lower streets, leaving a brown tidemark on the storefronts like a bruise going yellow, but the town had not receded with it. Every window along Front Street glowed. Men stood in knots on the boardwalks — miners with their hands still black from the closed workings, shopkeepers in their aprons, women who had spent the day hauling mud out of their cellars and now stood with their sleeves rolled and their arms crossed, watching the depot as though it were a fire they expected to start at any moment.

And it might have. The company's men were already there.

Mara counted them from the seat of the buckboard as Eli groaned behind her: eight of them, maybe ten, standing in a loose crescent around the depot platform where a lawyer in a city suit held a leather portfolio under one arm like a hymnal. Lamplight caught the brass buttons of their coats. They did not look like miners or railmen. They looked like what they were — paid to stand somewhere until standing stopped being necessary.

"Easy," she said, though she didn't know if she said it to the horses or to herself.

Eli's blood had soaked through two bandages and the blanket beneath him. The bullet had gone through the meat of his shoulder, high, missing everything that mattered by inches that felt like miles on the trail down. He'd been conscious for most of it, which was worse somehow than if he'd slept. Conscious, and talking — apologizing in fragments, naming things he'd done, names of men he'd met, dates she hadn't wanted to hear. She'd told him twice to save his breath for breathing.

Now his eyes found hers, gray in the failing light. "Mara. Don't—"

"Don't what?" She reined in before the depot steps. "Don't do what you know I'm going to do?"

His mouth worked. Under the stubble and the fever-flush he looked young again, the boy who used to hold the lantern while their father checked the gauge glasses, back before the railroad took one Lockridge and the mountains took the other. "They'll ruin you."

"They'll try." She set the brake. "That's different."

* * *

Sheriff Ada Voss met her at the bottom of the depot steps, and Mara had never been so glad to see anyone's hard face in her life.

"You're late," Ada said. It was not an accusation. It was an accounting.

"The canyon cost us four days. Then the enforcers cost us more." Mara climbed down; her legs nearly folded under her, and she locked them at the knee and refused to let them see it. "Where's the territorial clerk?"

"Inside. Waiting on you." Ada's eyes went past her shoulder to the buckboard, to the shape under the blanket, and something moved through her face — quick, banked, like heat behind iron. "Is that your brother?"

"That's my brother. He needs Doc Halloran and he needs her tonight."

"He'll get her. Wren!" Ada called over her shoulder, and a boy peeled off from the crowd and ran for the doctor's house before she'd finished the name. Then Ada turned back, and her voice dropped to the register she must have used for twenty years of breaking up saloon fights and delivering bad news. "Before you go in there, you should know what they've got waiting. Lawyer named Corliss. Filing papers drawn up, signature lines blank, and an offer sweet enough to rot teeth. Full medical costs for your brother. Your old position back with the railroad, senior grade, your survey reinstated into the record. All of it contingent on one thing."

"One thing," Mara repeated. Her hand went to the journal inside her coat without her meaning it to.

Ada saw. Of course she saw. "You don't have to decide anything tonight except whether to walk up those stairs."

"Yes, I do." Mara looked past her, at the crowd gathering along the platform rails — faces she knew now, faces she'd eaten beside and argued with and sat silent among in the church the night they buried the last of the mine dead. Old Tam Verrity, whose hands shook too badly to hold a drill anymore but who'd taught her which way the wind turned in this valley. Mrs. Okonkwo from the dry goods store, who'd fed her for a week when she first came off the mountain with a dead man's map and no explanation. The Aldous boys, fourteen and sixteen, standing on a barrel to see over the crowd.

Thirty days ago she'd come here meaning to leave the map and go. Thirty days ago these people had been strangers with grief she didn't own.

"Mara," Ada said quietly. "Whatever you're about to do — do it knowing the whole valley is standing behind you. Not because we asked you to. Because you came back down off that mountain alive, and nobody comes back off that mountain alive unless they mean it."

Mara almost laughed. It came out as something rawer. "I mean it."

She went up the steps.

* * *

The depot's waiting room had been cleared of benches. A single table stood in the middle under the big hanging lamp, and behind it sat the lawyer, Corliss — a narrow man with a narrow face and hands folded like a man at prayer, though nothing about him suggested prayer. Two of the enforcers flanked the door. The territorial clerk, a stooped man named Featherstone with ink-stained fingers and the weary neutrality of a man who had watched a hundred transactions he could neither bless nor prevent, sat to one side with the concession ledger open before him.

"Miss Lockridge." Corliss rose. His voice was warm oil. "You've had a difficult journey. We're grateful — the firm is grateful — that you've arrived in time. Mr. Featherstone informs me the filing window closes at midnight."

"I know how long I've known," Mara said.

"Of course." He gestured to the papers laid square on the table. "Then let me be brief, because I suspect you're tired and your brother needs attention. The Silverthread Consolidated Mining and Development Company offers, upon your certification of the feasibility survey — which you are uniquely qualified to complete, having surveyed the upper route yourself — the following consideration." He touched each document in turn. "Full payment of all medical expenses incurred by Mr. Elijah Lockridge, including transport to Denver should specialist care be required. Reinstatement of your position with the Cascade & Western Railroad at senior engineer grade, with back pay for the period of your suspension. And withdrawal of the firm's pending action regarding the destruction of company property — the survey drafts, Miss Lockridge. Arson is still arson, even when the property is paper."

There it was. Priced exactly to her wounds. Her brother's blood on one line, her career on another, and her own small crime — the burning of her drafts on the riverbank, done in rage and grief after learning what her certification would buy — held up like a mirror.

She thought of Eli in the buckboard. She thought of the surgeon's bill that would come, the months of healing, the possibility — she would not let herself finish the thought — that the arm might never work right again.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

Corliss spread his hands, regretful as a undertaker. "Then the survey goes uncertified, the claims lapse at midnight, and the firm sustains a considerable loss. Losses make men unreasonable, Miss Lockridge. There are other engineers. There will be other filings, other routes, other seasons. This valley has ore in it, and ore does not lose patience the way permits do." He smiled thinly. "You cannot burn every draft in the territory."

"No," Mara agreed. "But you can't buy every witness either."

She reached inside her coat and brought out Amos Craw's journal.

It was a cheap thing — oilcloth cover gone soft as chamois, pages swollen and dried again with weather, the string binding replaced twice with wire. She had carried it through the gorge crossing and the canyon and the ambush, wrapped in oilskin against the river, and her hands knew its weight the way they knew the weight of a transit or a level rod. She set it on the table between the filing papers, and the sound it made was very small.

Corliss looked at it the way a man looks at a snake on a dinner table. "What is that?"

"A confession," Mara said. "Not mine."

She turned to Featherstone. "Mr. Clerk. Before any certification is filed tonight, I'm invoking my right as a licensed surveyor to enter a formal finding into the record of this proceeding. And I want it heard by everyone in this town, because it concerns everyone in this town." She raised her voice toward the doorway, where the crowd had pressed close despite the enforcers' shoulders. "So open the doors."

For a moment Corliss's composure flickered — just once, like a lamp in wind. "This is a private transaction—"

"It's a public filing," Ada Voss said from the doorway, "under territorial statute twelve, and I'll thank you to step aside from my depot door."

The doors opened. The lamplight from the room spilled out onto the platform, and the people of Kestrel Falls filled the doorway and the windows and the steps — miners and shopkeepers and children hoisted onto shoulders, a hundred faces turning gold in the lamplight. Somewhere in the back, Mara heard the buckboard arrive and knew Doc Halloran had gotten to Eli. That was one debt she could stop carrying for a few minutes. Only a few.

Featherstone wet his pen. "Proceed, Miss Lockridge."

* * *

She opened the journal to the marked page — she had read it so many times on the trail she could have recited it, but some things needed to be read aloud from the page itself, in the dead man's own hand, so no one could say she'd shaped the words.

"Amos Craw," she said, and her voice carried farther than she expected, steadier than she felt. "Company surveyor, Cascade & Western, assigned to the Silverthread assessment, eighteen eighty-nine. These are his words."

She read:

*"March 4th. Completed the traverse of the upper basin today. The ore body is real. That's the devil of it — I can't fudge it away, because there's nothing to fudge. It sits exactly where the maps say it sits, and where the maps say it sits is the source of the Silverthread.*

*"To work the claim by adit is impossible — the rock won't hold a tunnel at that grade, I've tested it three ways. The only engineering answer is the one the firm wants: dam the headwater lake, divert the source into a flume, run the mill on the dry bed. It would work. God forgive me, it would work beautifully. I drew it myself and it's the finest piece of engineering I ever did and I hope to God I die before I sign it.*

*"Because below that dam there's Kestrel Falls. Four hundred souls drinking from this water. Every farm in the valley floor. The falls themselves, which is the only reason half this town exists. One season of tailings in that creek and the river is dead from the source to the gorge, and dead rivers don't come back. I've seen one. It doesn't come back.*

*"March 9th. Told Hargrove I couldn't certify. He asked me what price I put on my signature. I told him it wasn't for sale. He laughed. He actually laughed, and said every man's is, I just hadn't met my number yet.*

*"March 11th. They've taken my credentials. Said I falsified the ore reports — me, the man who wouldn't touch them. I'm driven out. I'll go up-country, above the claims, where the ground is free. If I can't stop them with my name, I'll keep the truth in ink and wait for someone honest to carry it down."*

Mara lowered the journal. The room was absolutely silent. Even the river, far below the depot, seemed to have gone quiet to listen.

"There's more," she said. "Pages of it. Traverse notes, elevations, grades — the work of a careful man, done honestly, in the cold, alone, for years. He died in my survey camp with this book under his arm and one request: take it to Kestrel Falls before the snows. I'm late by a season, but I'm here."

She turned to Featherstone. "Mr. Clerk, enter this into the record. And then enter my finding."

He nodded, pen moving.

"I, Mara Lockridge, licensed surveyor, having personally traversed the upper Silverthread route from the gorge to the headwaters, do hereby file my formal finding as follows." She made herself say each word plainly, the way she'd have written it, because plain words were the ones that held weight. "A rail line to the Silverthread headwaters is technically feasible. I want that entered too — I won't hide behind a lie, because lies are what brought us here. The grades can be worked. The rock can be cut. But the route is buildable only by damming and diverting the source of the Silverthread River, and the working of the claimed ore body requires the same diversion. Therefore the line cannot be built without destroying the watershed on which the town of Kestrel Falls depends. I find the route unbuildable as proposed, and I decline to certify it. Let the record show that I decline freely, without coercion, and with full knowledge of the consequences."

"Consequences," Corliss said softly, "which include—"

"Which include," Mara said, turning to face him at last, "the end of my career. Yes. I know. You priced it carefully — my brother's arm, my name, my livelihood, all of it weighed to the ounce. You're good at that. But you made one mistake in your arithmetic, Mr. Corliss. You assumed I was selling what Amos Craw wouldn't sell, at any number. He wasn't for sale, and neither am I. Some things aren't inventory."

Corliss's face had gone the color of tallow. Behind him, the enforcers shifted, and Ada Voss's hand came to rest — casually, unmistakably — on the butt of her revolver, and the shifting stopped.

"You understand," Corliss said, and now the warmth was gone entirely, leaving only the metal underneath, "that the firm will contest this finding. That there will be other surveys, other engineers, other filings. That midnight is not the end of anything."

"Midnight is the end of *these* permits," Mara said. "And the next men who come up that canyon will find the town knows what's at the top of it, because I've just told them, and the record shows it, and Amos Craw's book shows it, and there isn't a jury in this territory that hasn't drunk from a river. Contest whatever you like. The truth is filed."

Featherstone blotted the page, signed beneath her name, and spoke the words she'd carried eleven days up a mountain and eleven days back down to hear: *"Entered into the record, this twenty-ninth day of April, at seven minutes past nine in the evening."*

Outside, someone began to cheer, and then the whole platform was cheering, and old Tam Verrity's cracked voice rose over it shouting her name, and Mrs. Okonkwo was crying openly, and none of it sounded like victory to Mara. It sounded like a door closing on one life and opening onto a country she couldn't see the far edge of. She had just testified, publicly and permanently, that the great work of her profession could not be done here — that the thing she loved best in the world, the iron road, the long silver line stitching mountains together, had no place in this valley. She had unmade herself in front of a hundred witnesses, and it had felt like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying.

Corliss gathered his papers with hands that were not quite steady. At the door he paused, and did not look at her.

"You've cost the firm a great deal, Miss Lockridge."

"No," Mara said. "Your firm cost the firm. I just declined to pay for it."

He went out into the dark, and the enforcers went with him, and the crowd parted around them the way water parts around a stone it intends to outlast.

* * *

Later — much later, when the depot had emptied and the lamps were guttering and Featherstone had packed his ledger away with the sigh of a man whose feet hurt — Mara climbed the outside stair to Doc Halloran's surgery and pushed open the door.

Eli lay pale against the pillows, his shoulder bound high and tight, his eyes fever-bright but clear. The smell of carbolic and woodsmoke filled the little room. He watched her come in, and his mouth twisted into something that wasn't quite a smile.

"I heard it from here," he said. "Half the street heard it. The other half will have it memorized by morning." He swallowed. "You burned it all down, Mara. The whole rotten structure. With me inside it."

"No." She pulled the chair up beside the bed and sat, and took his unwounded hand, and held it the way she hadn't held it in fifteen years. "I burned down the part that was rotten. What's left standing is what we build next." She glanced toward the window, where the last light was dying on the ridge above the falls, and her voice dropped. "But you should sleep with one eye open, Eli. Corliss didn't come all this way to lose gracefully. Midnight ends the permits — but men like him don't obey midnight."

Down in the street, faint through the glass, she could see the company men regrouping under the depot eaves, and Corliss speaking low and fast to the biggest of them, gesturing — not toward the road out of town.

Toward the sheriff's office. Where the record was kept.
