## 19. Enforcers at the Falls

Ada Voss had learned to read a town the way other people read weather. Kestrel Falls at dawn told her everything she needed to know: the smell of woodsmoke from the bakery chimney, the creak of the mill wheel turning slow and steady in the high water, the sound of Hattie Brandt's broom scraping the boardwalk in front of the dry goods store — three long strokes and a pause, three long strokes and a pause, the same rhythm she'd kept for eleven years. A town that kept its rhythms was a town that believed in tomorrow.

This morning the rhythm was wrong.

Ada stood on the sheriff's office porch with a tin cup of coffee going cold in her hand, and she counted what was missing. No smoke from the mine office chimney — that was expected; the Silverthread Consolidated men had cleared out their papers two days ago, loading them onto a wagon under escort of four hired guns who looked at the townsfolk the way wolves look at a fence line. But there was no smoke from the Ruthie May boarding house either, and old Tom Quill's mule wasn't tied at the farrier's, and the school bell hadn't rung.

Instead, people were gathering. Quietly. Without being called.

Down by the depot, miners who hadn't worked a shift together since the shafts closed were standing in loose clusters, talking low. She saw Deke Harrow with his hands jammed in his coat pockets, saw the Vasquez brothers, saw young Peter Loomis who was barely old enough to hold a pick. Shopkeepers came out of their stores without bothering to lock up behind them. Hattie Brandt leaned her broom against the wall and walked down Main Street like a woman going to church.

Ada set down her cup and went inside for her badge and her rifle.

* * *

The trouble had been building for six days, ever since Mara Lockridge stood up in front of half the town outside the assay office and refused to sign. Ada had been there, arms crossed, watching the engineer's hands shake as she read Amos Craw's journal aloud — page after page of a dead man's careful handwriting describing exactly how the company had asked him to lie, and how he'd chosen the hills over the lie, and how it had killed him by inches. When Mara finished, nobody cheered. That was the thing about Kestrel Falls Ada loved most: they didn't cheer. They just went home and started getting ready.

The lawyer, Corbett, had left the next morning on the eastbound stage, his filing papers unsigned, his face the color of tallow. But Ada had been a lawman long enough to know that men like Corbett didn't carry the anger. They carried the paperwork. The anger came later, wearing gunbelts.

It arrived on the ninth day, an hour past noon, in the form of nine riders coming down the river road.

Ada heard them before she saw them — the particular heavy cadence of shod horses moving at a walk in formation, not strung out the way travelers string out but bunched, deliberate. She stepped off the porch and stood in the middle of Main Street, and the town gathered around her the way water gathers around a stone.

"Nine," said Deke Harrow, coming to stand at her shoulder. He still wore his mining jacket, the one with the company patch torn off at the shoulder seam, the threads still visible where it used to be.

"I count nine," Ada said.

"Twelve if you count the ones holding back at the bend."

"I'm counting nine." She let that sit. "Deke, you remember what I said at the meeting."

"You said nobody throws the first punch."

"And?"

"And nobody throws the last one either, because there won't need to be one." He almost smiled. "I was listening."

The riders came up Main Street at that same unhurried walk, and Ada made herself study them instead of the fear climbing her spine. Nine men, hard-used, well-mounted, carrying Winchesters in saddle scabbards and sidearms worn low. Not cowboys. Not drifters. These were professionals, the kind the company kept on retainer in Denver and shipped west when paper failed. Their leader was a broad man with a gray-streaked beard and the flat eyes of someone who had done this work in more than one territory. He reined up twenty feet from her, close enough to talk, far enough to shoot, and Ada noted that he'd done the arithmetic too.

"Miz Voss." He didn't tip his hat. "Name's Rulon. I'm told you're the law here."

"I'm the law here."

"Then you'll want to know we're here lawful. Writ of replevin, territorial court, Judge Ames's signature." He drew a folded paper from his coat but didn't hand it over, just held it up like a preacher showing scripture. "Company property was unlawfully retained when your engineer refused to return survey materials. We're authorized to recover those materials — the Lockridge woman's field books, the Craw journal, any maps or drawings pertaining to the Silverthread concession. Peaceable recovery, Miz Voss. Nobody needs to get hurt."

"Peaceable," Ada repeated. "That why you brought twelve men and held three back at the river bend?"

Something flickered across Rulon's face — not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. "You've got good eyes."

"I've got a town full of good eyes. Every man jack of them knows these hills better than you ever will." She stepped forward, and she heard the town step forward with her — boots on boards, the soft scrape of maybe sixty pairs of feet, and behind her the click of Deke Harrow working the lever of a rifle he'd brought from somewhere. "Now let's talk about that writ. Replevin covers personal property in the possession of a named party. You name Mara Lockridge?"

"The Lockridge woman, yes—"

"She's lying upstairs at the Ruthie May with a fever off a bullet wound she took protecting that journal from men like you. Doctor says she doesn't see visitors. So unless you've got a writ against a sick woman's sickbed, you're standing in my street talking about stealing from a patient." Ada let her voice carry. It carried well; she'd spent twenty years making it carry over river noise and saloon noise and the noise of frightened men. "And the Craw journal isn't company property. Amos Craw wrote it in his own hand, in his own cabin, on his own time, after your company drove him into the hills to die. His sister-in-law holds his estate. She's deposed it to the territorial inspector arriving Thursday. So that paper in your pocket is worth about as much as the ink it's printed in."

Rulon sat his horse and looked at her, and then — this was the part Ada would remember all her life, the part she would tell and retell until it wore smooth — he looked past her. At the town.

At Hattie Brandt standing with a double-barreled shotgun broken open over her forearm like she was waiting for the bird to flush. At Tom Quill, seventy years old, leaning on the hitching rail with a pistol stuck in his belt that probably hadn't been fired since the war. At the Vasquez brothers flanking the alley beside the assay office, where anyone foolish enough to try the back way would find themselves in a dead-end canyon with two men who'd spent thirty years shooting rock. At Peter Loomis on the depot roof with a coil of rope, ready to drop freight crates onto the platform to block the doors. At the women lining the second-story windows of the boarding house with kettles of water — not boiling, Ada noticed, just present, just witnesses.

Sixty-some ordinary people. Bakers and blacksmiths and idle miners and a schoolteacher holding a school bell in one hand and something heavier in the other.

"We're nine armed men," Rulon said quietly. "You're a town."

"That's the size of it."

"You'd die over some papers."

"No," Ada said. "We'd live over them. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and your company has never once understood it." She took a breath. "Here's how today goes, Mr. Rulon. You turn around and ride back down the river road, and you tell whoever sent you that the permits expired at midnight Tuesday, that the claims lapsed, that the territorial inspector is already on the train out of Bannock Junction with the lapse order in his valise, and that the only thing your firm can still win is how stupid it looks when the newspapers get hold of this. Or you can try to take those records. And I will arrest every man of you for attempted robbery, and the ones I don't arrest, the town will deal with, and I won't ask them to be gentle, because I won't be able to stop them anyway. Those are your choices. I'd choose fast, because my patience is shorter than my temper."

For a long moment, nothing moved. The mill wheel turned. The river ran loud under the bridge. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Rulon's hand drifted toward his holster — just an inch, just a test — and forty rifles and shotguns came up along Main Street with a sound like a wave breaking, and Ada felt rather than heard the town lean in, and she understood in that instant that she had never once been afraid of the wrong thing. She'd been afraid of losing this town. She should have trusted it.

Rulon took his hand off the gun.

* * *

They might have ridden out clean. That was the version Ada told herself afterward, the version where courage is its own armor and the story ends with the bad men turning tail. But Rulon was a professional, and professionals don't leave without trying the cheap trick first.

"Ma'am," he said, and now his voice changed, went softer, went reasonable, which was worse. "Nobody wants blood over ore dirt. But my instructions are explicit, and the man who gives them doesn't accept failure. So let me make you an offer that costs this town nothing." He swung down from his horse, slow, hands wide, and walked toward her, and Ada hated how much of the street stepped back to give him room even now. "The company will pay every man in this valley what the mines owed them when they shut down. Back wages, severance, the whole ledger. Cash money, next week, no lawyers. All we want is the journal and the field books burned in front of witnesses, and everybody goes home whole. You can rebuild this town on what we'll pay. Hell, you can build a railroad of your own."

The silence that followed was the worst silence Ada had ever stood in, because it wasn't empty. It was full of arithmetic. She could see it happening behind the eyes of every man on that street — the winter coming, the seed corn running low, the children who needed shoes, the number a dead prospector's journal could bring versus the number a family needed. Sixty people doing sums they'd been doing for two years since the mines closed, and hating themselves for doing them.

It was Hattie Brandt who broke it. She walked out into the middle of the street, shotgun still broken over her arm, and planted herself between Ada and Rulon like a fence post.

"My husband worked the Silverthread eleven years," she said. "He died in the Number Two collapse because the company skimped on timber to pad the quarterly report. Amos Craw wrote that down in his journal. Page nineteen. I've read it." Her voice didn't rise at all. "So you can keep your back wages, Mr. Rulon, and you can tell the man who pays you that Hattie Brandt's price for her husband's life was never money, and it isn't for sale now, and neither is this town. Now get on your horse."

And the arithmetic stopped. Ada watched it stop — watched the sums close like books, one face at a time, Deke Harrow and Tom Quill and the Vasquez brothers and shy Peter Loomis on the roof, all of them putting away the numbers and picking up something older and heavier than numbers. Rulon looked at Hattie Brandt for a long five seconds, and whatever he was looking for, he didn't find it.

"Thursday," he said finally, remounting. "Your inspector comes Thursday. My instructions say the records don't reach Thursday." He gathered his reins. "I've got eight days of contract left and men who follow me because I don't waste them. Sleep light, Miz Voss."

"Mr. Rulon." Ada smiled at him, and it was not a kind smile. "You sleep lighter."

Nine riders went back down the river road at that same unhurried walk, and three more peeled away from the bend to join them, and the town stood in the street until the dust settled, and then — this was the part that undid her — they didn't cheer either. They just turned and went back to work. Hattie picked up her broom. Deke went to check on the depot barricades. Tom Quill led his mule to the farrier's like it was any other noon.

Ada stood alone in the middle of Main Street and let her knees shake now that nobody could see.

* * *

She spent the afternoon fortifying. The records went from the assay office safe into the cellar of the church, which had stone walls two feet thick and a congregation that would notice strangers. She wired Bannock Junction from the depot telegraph and got back a reply that made her laugh out loud for the first time in a week: INSPECTOR GRAVES ADVANCED SCHEDULE ARRIVING TOMORROW 3 P.M. TRAIN CANNOT BE STOPPED STOP TELL YOUR PEOPLE TO HOLD ONE MORE DAY.

One more day. Ada folded the telegram into her pocket and climbed the stairs of the Ruthie May, where the doctor let her sit ten minutes in the chair beside Mara Lockridge's bed. The engineer was gray-faced and burning, but her eyes found Ada's and stayed sharp.

"They came," Mara said. It wasn't a question.

"Came and went. Town held."

Mara's mouth twitched toward something that wanted to be a smile. "Hattie?"

"Hattie quoted the journal. Page nineteen."

That got a real breath of a laugh, which turned into a cough, which the doctor ended the visit over. But at the door Mara caught Ada's sleeve, and her voice dropped to a rasp. "Eli. Where's Eli?"

"Down the hall, sleeping. Took a ball in the shoulder, but he'll keep the arm. He's asking for you every hour, and the doctor won't allow it, so you two can argue about that when you're both upright." Ada paused. "He did right, Mara. Whatever else he did, at the end of it, he stood in front of a bullet meant for you. That buys a man a hearing in this town."

Mara closed her eyes. "It buys him a hearing with me too. Eventually." Then, quieter: "Ada. Rulon said eight days of contract. Men like that don't wait for Thursday."

"No," Ada agreed. "They come tonight, or they come at dawn, and they come mean, because they've got nothing left to lose but their fee." She patted the sleeve she was holding and stood. "But here's what they don't know, Mara. They think they're riding into a town. They're not. They're riding into sixty people who decided yesterday what they're worth, and there isn't a price on earth that un-decides a thing like that."

She went down the stairs and out into the blue evening, and the town was already at work — lanterns lit, men posted at the bridge and the bend and the church cellar door, Hattie carrying coffee along the picket lines like a quartermaster. Ada took her rifle and walked toward the depot, because if the night was going to break over somebody, it would break over the place where the trains came in, and she intended to be standing there when it did.

The 3 p.m. train tomorrow. One more day.

Down the valley, dark and patient, the river kept running toward the falls, and Ada Voss kept watch.
