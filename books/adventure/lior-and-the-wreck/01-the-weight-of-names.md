## 1. The Weight of Names

The town was called Ferrenholt, which meant nothing to anyone who hadn't been desperate enough to end up there. It sat in the fold between two mountain ranges like a coin dropped in a gutter — a border town with a customs house nobody used anymore, a rail spur that carried ore down and men up, and three taverns that all served the same watery beer at different prices.

Elodie Delacroix had been in Ferrenholt for eleven days. She knew this because she'd been counting the coins too.

She sat in the back corner of the Kestrel's Rest with her back to the wall — an old habit, one of the few her father's training had left her that still paid dividends — and watched the room the way she'd once watched a snow slope for the telltale shiver before it let go. The evening crowd was mostly miners off the night shift, their faces gray with rock dust, and a scattering of traders who smelled of wet wool and dishonesty. Nobody looked at her. That was the point of sitting where she sat, but tonight the not-looking had a texture to it, a deliberate quality, like the silence after someone says something unforgivable at a dinner table.

Word had gotten ahead of her. Word always got ahead of her now.

On the table in front of her: a tin cup of tea she'd stretched across two hours, a guide's license folded into quarters so soft the creases had gone to felt, and a letter she'd read so many times she could recite it with her eyes closed.

*The Alpine Guides' Registry hereby confirms the permanent revocation of certification held by E. Delacroix, following findings of the inquiry into the events of 14–19 September on the Delacroix Route, during which four clients and one assistant guide perished. This decision is final and is circulated to all member registries.*

*Final.* As if mountains were courts. As if the ice had testified.

She unfolded the license anyway, out of something between masochism and mourning. Her name was printed there in ink that had faded from black to a tired brown: *Elodie M. Delacroix, High Mountain Guide, Class One.* Eleven years she'd held that card. She'd earned it younger than anyone in her cohort, and she'd kept it clean through avalanches and rockfall and one whiteout on the Serac du Nord that even now she couldn't think about without her hands going cold.

Four clients. One assistant guide. Tomas. She set the license down flat, as if it might behave better lying still.

"You're the Delacroix woman."

The voice came from her left, where she hadn't been watching because the man had come in quiet. He was big through the shoulders in the way of men who'd hauled loads for a living, with a beard going iron-gray and eyes that did the arithmetic of strangers quickly. A packer, maybe. Or worse, a guide.

"I'm nobody," Elodie said.

"Sure." He pulled out the chair across from her without asking, which told her everything about how the conversation was going to go. "I saw you come in on the freight sled eleven days ago. One bag. Slept in the customs house the first two nights till they chased you off." He turned his cup around on the table, not drinking. "You're the one who walked five clients into the storm on the Delacroix Route."

"Is that what they're saying?"

"That's what everyone's saying."

"And what do you say?"

He looked at her then, really looked, and whatever he found made him lean back a little. "I say a Class One doesn't walk five clients into anything. I say either the story's wrong or the woman's changed." He shrugged. "But my saying it doesn't put bread in your hand."

"No," Elodie agreed. "It doesn't."

The barman chose that moment to drift over, wiping his hands on a rag that had stopped being white sometime in the previous decade. "We're full up," he said, though Elodie was the only person at the table besides the packer. "And we don't serve—"

"She's served," the packer said, without raising his voice. Something passed between the two men, some old ledger of favors, and the barman retreated with the particular stiffness of a man who'd lost a small battle and intended to remember it.

"Kessler," the packer said, offering a hand. "I run loads over the Verren in summer. You're Delacroix. The real one."

"The real one is dead," Elodie said. "They buried her in September."

Kessler didn't smile, but something around his eyes suggested he appreciated the shape of the sentence. "There's a man asking after guides in the front room. Been here two nights. Offering good money for someone to take a party over Verren Pass, starting tomorrow." He paused. "He's been told about you."

"Then he won't want me."

"He hasn't heard yet. Word travels slow to people who just arrived." Kessler stood, dropped a coin beside his untouched cup, and looked down at her with an expression she couldn't fully read — pity, maybe, or its harder cousin, respect. "For what it's worth, I climbed with your father once. Thirty years ago, the north face of Col de Vaire. He said a guide's reputation is like a rope. Everyone holds onto it. Almost nobody checks it."

Elodie almost smiled. "Did he check yours?"

"Every day." Kessler touched two fingers to his brow, half salute, half apology, and went out into the cold.

*

The man asking after guides was named Ostrer, and he conducted his business in the front room of the Kestrel's Rest with the door open, as if he wanted the whole town to witness his generosity. He was soft in the middle and hard in the eyes, wearing a coat that cost more than everything Elodie owned combined, and when she approached his table he looked her over the way a man looks over a horse he suspects is lame.

"You're the guide," he said. Not a question.

"I'm a guide."

"I heard otherwise." He tapped a folded newspaper on the table — a border-town rag, three weeks old, and there she was on the fourth page, DELACROIX DISASTER: REGISTRY REVOKES LICENSE OF MOUNTAIN GUIDE, above a photograph taken years ago at some award ceremony, her smiling like a person who had never watched anyone fall. "The article says—"

"The article says what someone paid it to say." She kept her voice level. She'd had practice; she'd given the same answer to registry clerks, to journalists, to a widow in a black veil who had spat at her feet outside the inquiry hall. "If you want the truth of the Delacroix Route, I'll give it to you free: the forecast lied, the window closed early, and I brought four of my six clients home alive through weather that killed a rescue party sent in behind us. The other two died doing exactly what I told them to do, which was stay put. They moved. That's the whole story. It just isn't the profitable one."

Ostrer studied her for a long moment. Outside, sleet ticked against the window glass like fingernails.

"The pass closes in nine days," he said finally. "Seasonal closure. After that, nothing crosses until spring thaw. I have clients who need to be over the Verren before it does. Four of them. I'll pay two hundred crowns a head, half up front, plus a bonus if we make the far side inside six days."

Two hundred a head. Eight hundred crowns. Elodie did the arithmetic against the debts pinned inside her coat lining — the outfitter in Vail-sur-Cime who'd extended her credit out of pity, the boarding house, the fine the registry had levied along with the revocation, as if taking her livelihood weren't punishment enough. Eight hundred crowns would clear most of it. Would buy her a month of not being hungry in a town where nobody would hire a disgraced guide even to shovel snow.

It would also mean leading strangers over a pass in late autumn, on a route she didn't know, with her name traveling ahead of her like a bad smell.

"Why not hire locally?" she asked. "Ferrenholt has guides."

"Ferrenholt has drunks with rope," Ostrer said. "I asked. Three of them refused outright — said the Verren's running ugly this year, early storms, unstable cornices. The fourth wanted double and I don't pay double." He leaned forward. "Which leaves a Class One who'll work cheap because no one else will touch her. I'm not a charitable man, Madame Delacroix, but I recognize a bargain."

There it was. He wasn't hiring her despite the blacklist. He was hiring her *because* of it. Somewhere under her ribs, humiliation and relief arrived at the same moment and shook hands.

"My conditions," Elodie said. "One: I make the calls on the mountain. All of them. If I say turn back, we turn back, and you eat the deposit. Two: I see the clients before I commit. If any of them can't walk ten kilometers with a loaded pack, the deal's off. Three—" she hesitated, because the third condition was pride dressed as procedure, "—you introduce me as the guide. Not the guide *despite*. Just the guide."

Ostrer's mouth twitched. "Done, done, and done. They're upstairs. Come meet your lambs."

*

There were four of them, gathered in a rented room that smelled of woodsmoke and expensive luggage, and Elodie took them in the way she took in any new party — fast, thorough, and without mercy.

A tall man by the fire, thirty-something, with an easy smile that arrived a half-second before his eyes caught up to it. He wore expedition gear that was brand new and already scuffed in ways that suggested he'd scuffed it himself, deliberately, to look experienced. His boots were top-of-the-line and completely unbroken. He introduced himself as Corvin, said he was "in publishing," and shook her hand like they were old friends at a party.

A broad, silent man sitting apart from the others, forty or fifty, hard to say, with forearms like dock rope and a face that gave away nothing except a scar through one eyebrow. Brase, Ostrer said. Former mining man. He nodded at Elodie once, the way you nod at weather.

A widow — Elodie guessed it from the ring worn on a chain rather than the finger, and from the particular stillness of her hands. Marta, mid-fifties, silver-haired, dressed better than the occasion required and carrying herself like someone who had decided grief was a private country she'd emigrated to alone. She looked at Elodie with an expression of careful, courteous assessment, the kind wealthy women use on staff.

And last, half-hidden behind the widow's chair, a boy. Sixteen, maybe seventeen, all elbows and defiance, with a knapsack that had been mended with wire and a jaw set at the angle of someone who expected to be sent home and had rehearsed refusing. No name offered. Ostrer called him "the boy," and the boy's glare suggested he'd been called worse.

"This is the party?" Elodie said.

"This is the party," Ostrer agreed.

"They're not climbers."

"They're walkers. The Verren is a walking crossing this time of year, lower route, no technical sections." He said it smoothly, and something in the smoothness snagged on Elodie's attention like a loose thread. She let it go. Broke guides couldn't afford threads.

She addressed the room instead. "Rules. We move when I say move, stop when I say stop, and turn around when I say turn around. You carry what I tell you to carry. If I give you a rope, you clip it, and if I tell you to walk in someone's footsteps, you walk in their footsteps. On the mountain I'm not your employee, I'm not your servant, and I'm certainly not your friend. I'm the reason any of you come home. Questions?"

Corvin raised his hand like a schoolboy, grinning. "Just one. What made you take this job? If you don't mind my asking."

The question was friendly. The question was also a knife, and Elodie saw, in the flicker of something watchful behind the grin, that he knew exactly what he was holding.

"Eight hundred crowns," she said. "What made you take it?"

"Same reason, more or less." He spread his hands, all innocence. "I follow stories. And the Verren has a good one — there was an expedition went missing up that way, oh, twenty years back now. Private plane, never recovered. Some people say it never existed." His grin widened a degree. "I like the ones people say never existed."

Something shifted in the room — subtle, a change of pressure rather than temperature. The miner, Brase, looked up from the floor for the first time, his gaze settling on Corvin with an expression Elodie couldn't parse and didn't like. The widow Marta's hands went still on her lap. Even the boy glanced over.

"There are no stories on my mountain," Elodie said. "Only weather, distance, and daylight. Leave your story at the trailhead."

"Oh, I always do," Corvin said pleasantly. "Right up until it follows me anyway."

*

She left the Kestrel's Rest after midnight and stood in the street letting the sleet hit her face, because it was cleaner than the air inside.

Below the eaves of the tavern, someone had chalked words on the stone. Fresh — the rain hadn't gotten to them yet. *MURDERESS.* Below it, smaller, in a different hand, as if even the vandal had flinched: *4 DEAD.*

Elodie stood looking at it for a long time. In Vail-sur-Cime they'd burned an effigy outside the inquiry hall. In Chastel they'd refused her a room with the bed visible through the window, already made up, warm. Here it was chalk, which was almost merciful — chalk could be rained on.

She thought about Tomas, who had stayed put because she told him to, whose body they'd found in spring still clipped to the anchor, still obedient. She thought about the widow in the black veil, and understood now — standing in front of chalk in a border town — that the woman had needed somewhere to put the weight of it, and Elodie had been the only surface available. Grief looks for a name. That was the whole cruelty of it. Grief will take any name it's handed.

Her father had a saying, one of the few she'd kept: *The mountain doesn't care what they call you. It cares whether you're paying attention.*

She crouched, spat on her glove, and scrubbed at the word *MURDERESS* until the chalk smeared into gray ghost-writing, illegible, deniable. Then she straightened, shouldered her pack, and went to find the outfitter who'd extended her credit, because eight hundred crowns or not, she'd need rope, and the Verren — if Ostrer's smoothness about "no technical sections" was even half a lie — would want every meter of it.

Behind her, the sleet began again, patient, erasing what was left of the chalk line by line.

Tomorrow they'd start up. Four strangers with four secrets, a broker with a smile like a locked drawer, and a route that three local guides had refused. Elodie told herself it was just money. Just a crossing. Just work.

But she lay awake that night in the rented bunk listening to the wind come down off the Verren — a sound she knew, a sound with a grammar to it, and tonight it was saying something she didn't like — and she admitted the truth she'd been stepping around since Corvin opened his mouth:

Nobody paid eight hundred crowns a head to walk a safe pass in late autumn.

Somebody wanted these people on that mountain. And come dawn, she was going to lead them straight into finding out who.
