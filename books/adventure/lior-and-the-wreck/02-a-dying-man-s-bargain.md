## 2. A Dying Man's Bargain

The storm came down off the Verren massif an hour after dark, and Elodie Delacroix did what she always did when the mountain spoke: she listened, and she made camp.

She'd found a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite where the wind broke into confused eddies, and she'd dug her vestibule into the drift with the flat of her snowshoe, working by feel because her headlamp battery was down to its last hour and she was rationing it like blood. The tent was old. The stove hissed on its last canister. Her food bag held three days of thin rations stretched to five, and in the morning she would descend to Verren Serrai with nothing in her pockets but the shame of a woman who had been turned away from every guiding office between here and the coast.

She was melting snow for tea she couldn't afford when she heard the sound.

At first she took it for the wind — the mountain made a hundred voices at night, groaning ice, sighing cornices, the long exhalation of spindrift off the ridgelines. But this voice had weight in it. A dragging, a stumbling, a wet animal laboring through snow that came up past the knee. Then a shape resolved against the blue-black of the glacier tongue below her camp, moving in the wrong direction for anyone sane: uphill, alone, no light.

Elodie killed the stove and put her hand on the axe lying beside her.

"Who's there." Not a question. She pitched it low and hard, the way you spoke to things on a mountain that might be men or might be trouble.

The shape stopped. Stood swaying. And then it said, in a voice scraped down to gravel, "Delacroix."

Her whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the altitude. Nobody called her that anymore except people who wanted something, or people who wanted her ruined, and often those were the same people.

"Come into the light where I can see you," she said, though there was almost no light to come into. She risked thirty seconds of headlamp, thumbed it on, and swept the beam downslope.

An old man stood in the snow with one hand pressed to his belly and the other holding a broken ski pole like a spear he'd forgotten how to use. His parka was expedition-grade once, twenty years ago, now patched with wire and tar. Ice had built up in his beard until his face seemed carved from dirty glass. And below the hand pressed to his stomach, black in the beam, the snow around his legs was not white.

"God," she said, before she could stop herself.

"No," the man said. "Just Lior."

* * *

Getting him into the tent was a fight against everything she knew about survival arithmetic. He weighed nothing, which frightened her more than the blood did; a man that light had been dying for days before he ever reached her hollow. She got him through the vestibule, stripped his frozen outer layers as gently as she could, and saw the wound by stove-light: a single entry low on the left side, ugly, days old, packed with moss and pine resin in the manner of someone who had treated himself with whatever the forest offered and then kept walking anyway.

"Who shot you?" she asked, pressing fresh dressing over it. She had a proper kit — guides always did — but she knew what she was looking at even as she worked. The ball had gone deep and sideways. There was no doctor within three days' travel who would see a prospector, and no road fast enough to matter.

"Man from Halvern's survey crew," Lior said. His eyes were clear, which was worse than delirium. Clear-eyed men knew what they were asking. "I found something I wasn't supposed to find. They followed me four days. Got me at the ford." He coughed, and the cough cost him something permanent. "You're going down tomorrow."

"I'm going down tomorrow," she agreed. "There's a stretcher lashed to my pack. If we start early—"

"Listen to me." His hand shot out and closed on her wrist with a strength that startled her, cold fingers like a trap sprung shut. "I'm not going down. You know that. You've seen enough bodies to know it too."

She had. She said nothing.

"I know your name, Elodie Delacroix." He said the whole thing slowly, tasting it. "Everyone on this side of the range knows your name. They say you killed your clients on the Delacroix route two winters back."

"They're wrong," she said, flat, automatic. She had said it so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones, meaning nothing.

"Maybe they are." He didn't argue. That unsettled her more than an accusation would have. "Doesn't matter to me either way. What matters is you're the only guide left on this mountain who'll still be up here tomorrow, and the only one Halvern isn't watching, because they think they've already buried you." A ghost of a smile moved under the ice in his beard. "Being dead has its uses."

Outside, the storm thickened. The tent fabric snapped and boomed. Elodie fed the stove another measured breath of fuel and waited, because she had learned long ago that dying men talked best to silence.

Lior's hand went inside his shirt and came out with a oilskin packet, soft-cornered with age, tied with waxed cord. He held it against his chest like a child.

"There's a plane," he said. "Went down in 'sixty-one. An expedition — survey team, they called themselves, but they were mapping more than rock. Nine souls aboard. The company story is they flew into the north face in bad weather and everyone burned." His breath whistled. "They didn't all burn. Two walked out of the wreckage. One died on the ice. One crawled south for eleven days and reached my father's village with frostbite to the wrists and a satchel he wouldn't let go of even when they cut the coat off him."

Elodie sat very still. The stove ticked. Somewhere above, a serac calved with a sound like a door slamming in an empty house.

"What was in the satchel?"

"A ledger." Lior's eyes had gone somewhere else — sixty years away, a smoky room, a dying stranger. "Every day of that expedition, written down. Where they went. What they surveyed. And what they *found*, Elodie. What the ground under the Kessarat valley was full of, besides ore." His voice dropped. "Photographs too. My father kept them his whole life and never showed a soul, because showing them was death. He gave them to me last spring, when his lungs went. Told me: take it home or die trying, but never let Halvern touch it."

"Home," she repeated. "Where's home?"

"South," he said, which was not an answer, and by the set of his mouth she understood it was the only answer she was getting tonight.

She looked at the packet. Oilskin, waxed cord, small enough to fit in a breast pocket. It looked like nothing. It felt, sitting there in the stove-light, like the heaviest object she had ever seen.

"Why bring it to me? You don't know me. By your own account I'm a killer."

"Because you're here." He said it simply. "And because a woman the world has already ruined has less to lose than anyone else on this mountain. And because—" the smile again, terrible and thin, "—because I watched you work the east couloir two seasons ago, before all of it. Before the blacklist. You read terrain like scripture, girl. I need someone who reads terrain. The wreck sits high on the southern wall, in country Halvern pretends doesn't exist, and the route to it will kill anyone who navigates by hope."

"The southern wall," she said slowly. "That's closed country. It's not on any permitted map."

"It's on mine." His hand trembled as he touched the packet. "Everything's on mine."

* * *

He faded near midnight and she thought she'd lost him, and some treacherous part of her — the part that counted calories and fuel grams and days of margin — noted with horror that she felt relief. Then he surfaced again, gray-faced, and gripped her wrist, and made her boil water and wet his lips though he couldn't swallow.

"Promise first," he rasped. "Before I give it to you. Promise me, or it goes in the crevasse with me and Halvern wins forever."

"You should rest—"

"*Promise.*"

The word cracked across the tent like a whip. Outside the storm screamed. Inside, a dying man held her gaze with the fixed ferocity of the last ember in a burnt-out fire, and Elodie Delacroix, who had spent two years learning exactly how much a promise could cost, heard herself speak.

"Tell me the promise."

"Two parts." He lifted one finger, and it shook. "Carry the ledger out of the mountains. All the way out. Don't leave it in a cache, don't mail it, don't trust it to a post office or a priest or a policeman — carry it yourself, in your own hands, to the people it belongs to. I'll tell you who they are when you swear."

"And the second part?"

His finger curled closed. "Tell no one what it found. Not the journalist who'll be sniffing after you — yes, I know about Corvin, everyone in Verren Serrai knows about Corvin, he buys drinks and asks questions like a man paying by the word. Not the widow with her husband's mining shares. Not the boy. No one. The truth comes out once, in one place, to one people, or it comes out never. If it goes to newspapers first, Halvern's lawyers will bury it under ten years of injunctions and the witnesses will die of convenient accidents, and my father's village will have bled for nothing." His chest hitched. "Swear it."

She wanted to ask questions. Who were the descendants, what exactly had the survey found, why her, why now, why not simply give it to the authorities in the lowlands — but she watched his face and understood that every question she asked was a question he might not live to answer, and that the packet in his hands was a fuse burning down to nothing.

"I swear," she said. "The ledger goes home, carried by me. And I tell no one what it found."

Something went out of his shoulders then — not tension, but purpose, the scaffold that had been holding him upright finally allowed to fall. He pushed the oilskin packet into her hands. It was warm from his body. That was the detail she would remember afterward, in the years that came: that it was warm, and that the warmth lasted only moments, and that she had held it tighter than she needed to, trying to keep it.

Inside, wrapped twice around a stiff leather ledger whose cover had been scorched brown, was a map. Hand-drawn, ink on treated linen, dense with contour lines in a draftsman's precise hand and annotations in two languages — the trade tongue and another script, older, flowing, that she didn't recognize. A route threaded the southern wall through country she had only ever seen as blank paper. Small symbols marked camps, caches, a bridge, a notch in a ridge shaped like a broken tooth. And near the center, circled twice, a tiny drawn airplane with wings folded, resting in a bowl of stone beneath a hanging glacier.

"Sixty years," Lior whispered. "It's sat there sixty years, watching the valley. Time somebody let it rest."

"Rest," she echoed. "Not burn?"

"Let it rest," he said, "with the truth beside it. That's all. That's everything."

He slept after that, really slept, and Elodie sat in the orange stutter of the stove and did the arithmetic she could not stop herself from doing. Three clients meeting her at the trailhead in two days — the journalist, the widow, the silent miner, the runaway boy, a party of liars she'd already begun cataloguing. A detour onto the southern wall that would add six days minimum through unpatrolled, unmapped, avalanche-fed country. A wound in her party's trust waiting to happen no matter which route she chose. And a promise, sworn on a dying man's breath, that forbade her from telling any of them anything.

Dread sat down beside her like a second guest and made itself comfortable.

Because she understood, with the clarity that comes only at altitude and in the small hours, what Lior had actually handed her. Not a map. A burden with a route printed on it. Whoever had shot him was still on the mountain, or would send others, and now the thing they wanted was sewn into her pack next to her spare socks. And she had sworn to walk toward them.

* * *

He woke once more, near dawn, when the storm had spent itself and the light through the nylon had turned the color of pewter. She was already packed, already dressed, stretching the stiffness out of her hands. He looked at her for a long moment without recognition, and then it returned, and with it a calm that frightened her more than the fever had.

"You're still here," he said.

"Where would I go?"

He smiled. Under the ice in his beard it was almost a young man's smile. "My father said the mountain would send someone. I told him the mountain sends weather, not people." His breathing had gone shallow and quick, the air whistling through him like wind through a broken window. "The people it belongs to — listen, listen — the Kessarat valley. South fork. Ask for the family that keeps the names. They'll know. They've been keeping the names sixty years." His hand found hers and squeezed, once, with everything left in him. "Tell them Lior came home the long way."

"Lior—" She leaned close. "What do I tell them you found?"

But his eyes had gone past her, up through the tent fabric, up through the storm-washed sky, to some country she couldn't see. His lips moved. The last word might have been a name, or might have been *home*, or might have been nothing at all.

Then the wind filled the silence, and the stove ticked, and Elodie Delacroix knelt alone in a tent on the shoulder of the Verren massif holding a dead man's warm map, with a promise on her neck like a yoke and a party of strangers waiting two valleys south.

She stayed long enough to do what could be done — a cairn would have to wait for softer ground, so she weighted him with stones from the hollow's lip and folded his hands over his chest, and she said the few words the mountain allows, which are mostly wind. Then she sewed the oilskin packet into the lining of her pack with dental floss and a bent needle, because a map in a pocket gets searched and a map in a seam does not, and she shouldered her load and turned south.

Below her, far down the white throat of the valley, smoke rose from the stoves of Verren Serrai, where four clients believed they had hired a disgraced guide for a simple crossing.

One of them, she would learn soon enough, had hired her for something else entirely.
