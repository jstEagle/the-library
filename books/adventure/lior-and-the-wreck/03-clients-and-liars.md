## 3. Clients and Liars

The tavern in Ferrenholt smelled of wet wool and burnt coffee, and by seven in the morning it had already decided what to think of me.

I'd taken the corner table with my back to the wall — an old habit, older than the blacklist — and spread the client dossiers across the scarred wood like a hand of cards I already suspected were marked. Four names. Four deposits, paid through a broker in Vell who asked no questions and charged handsomely for the discretion. The Verren Pass crossing, fourteen days, high season closing fast. It was more money than I'd seen in a year, and that alone should have frightened me.

Desperate people don't get to choose their clients. They get to choose whether they eat.

* * *

Corvin arrived first, which told me something before he'd said a word. Guides know clients the way sailors know weather, and the ones who show up early are the ones who've been rehearsing.

He was maybe thirty-five, lean in the way of men who burn everything they consume, with a smile that arrived half a second before his eyes caught up to it. He wore gear that was expensive but broken in — not bought for this trip, which meant he'd done this before, or wanted very badly to look like he had. He set a leather satchel on the table between us with the care of a man setting down something that mattered.

"Elodie Delacroix." He said my name like he was tasting it. "They said you'd be tall. They didn't say you'd look like the mountain already lost an argument with you."

"They say a lot of things about me." I didn't stand. "You're Corvin."

"Journalist. Features, mostly. Long-form." He slid into the chair opposite without waiting for an invitation, and I noted that too — the way he made himself at home in spaces that hadn't offered. "I want the pass at first light. The way the old survey teams saw it."

"Nobody sees the Verren Pass at first light. You see cloud, or you see your own breath."

"Then I'll write about the breath." That smile again, quick and warm as a struck match. "Look, I'll be honest with you—"

"No, you won't," I said, and watched the smile hold its shape while something behind it recalculated. "Clients never are. It's fine. I don't need honesty. I need you to do what I say when I say it, rope when I tell you to rope, and turn back when I tell you to turn back. Can you do that?"

"I can follow instructions beautifully." He leaned back, arms folding, studying me with the frank appetite of a man reading a document he intended to quote later. "You know, there's a story in you. I can always tell. People who've been through something carry it differently — shoulders, mostly. Yours are set like you're still carrying it."

"Your shoulders will be carrying forty pounds of rope and hardware by noon. Save your observations for the descent."

He laughed — genuinely, I thought, which unsettled me more than the flattery had. And then, as he rose to claim a bunk upstairs, he paused with one hand on the chair back.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I read the reports on the Delacroix expedition. All of them. The official ones and the others." His eyes held mine, mild and blue and entirely unreadable. "I don't believe everything I read."

He went up the stairs two at a time. I sat very still and listened to my own pulse.

He knew my name before the broker gave it to him. Everyone in Ferrenholt knew my name, but they knew it the way you know the name of a disease. Corvin had said it like a key trying three different locks.

* * *

Brase came at noon, exactly when the letter had said, and stood in the doorway of the tavern like a man checking an exit.

He was big without being heavy — the kind of size built by decades of labor rather than a gym — with a face weathered down to its essentials: eyes, mouth, a jaw that looked hewn rather than grown. He wore canvas and wool, all of it patched, none of it new, and a pack that sat on his shoulders like it had grown there. When the innkeeper pointed him toward my table, he crossed the room without looking at anything except the floor directly ahead of him, and sat where I indicated, and said nothing.

"You're Brase."

A nod.

"The listing says ex-miner."

Another nod. Then, after a silence long enough to make the innkeeper glance over: "Kettleman shafts. Eleven years."

"High altitude work?"

"Some."

That was all. I laid out the route map — the official route, the one I'd show any inspector — and walked him through it, and he listened with his eyes fixed on the contour lines and his hands flat on the table, and when I finished he said, "Fine," and that was the whole of our conversation.

But here is what eleven years of leading fools up mountains teaches you: you learn to read bodies the way other people read letters. And Brase's body was wrong. A tourist slouches. An amateur fidgets. Even Corvin, polished as he was, had performed his ease. Brase did none of these things. He simply occupied space the way stone occupies space, and when a serving girl bumped his chair he absorbed the impact without appearing to notice it had happened, and his eyes — this was the thing, this was the thing that put a cold finger on the back of my neck — his eyes never stopped moving. Not nervously. Systematically. Door, window, stairs, my face, door again.

Men who worked Kettleman shafts learned to watch for rockfall. Men who watched like this had learned it somewhere worse.

"Any medical conditions?" I asked, because the form required it.

"No."

"Allergies?"

"No."

"Reason for the crossing?"

For the first time he looked at me directly, and his eyes were pale gray, the color of glacier ice under cloud, and completely empty of anything he didn't choose to put there.

"Heard the Verren's beautiful this time of year."

It was snowing sideways outside. I wrote *no reason given* on the form, because it was true, and because writing things down sometimes shames the universe into explaining them later.

* * *

Marta Voss arrived in a hired car, which caused a small sensation on Ferrenholt's single street, and stepped out of it wearing boots that cost more than my annual kit and had clearly never touched anything steeper than a hotel staircase.

She was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, straight-backed, with the particular composure of women who have buried husbands and discovered they are excellent company. Her luggage included a lacquered case, a fur-lined coat, and a walking stick that was ornamental if it was anything. She surveyed the tavern, the mud street, the mountains chewing the horizon, with the expression of a woman confirming a rumor.

"Madame Delacroix." Her handshake was dry and firm. "My late husband climbed. I have his journals. I thought—" she paused, and for just a moment the composure flickered, showing something raw beneath, "—I thought it was time I saw what he saw."

"We're not climbing his mountains, madame. We're crossing a pass. There's a difference, and it's measured in how many people come home."

"How reassuring. Then no one will compare me to him." She smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and I felt myself almost liking her, and distrusted the feeling immediately. Wealthy widows who hire blacklisted guides through anonymous brokers are not looking for views.

"Your deposit came through a third party," I said. "Standard practice?"

"My accountant finds it simplifies things." She settled into a chair and accepted coffee she would not drink. "Does it trouble you?"

"Everything troubles me. It's why I'm still alive."

"Mm." She looked out the window at the weather, and her reflection looked back at her from the glass, and for a moment neither of them seemed to be listening to me at all. "My husband wrote about a plane once. In his journals. An expedition flight that went missing over the northern ranges, years ago. He wondered about it for the rest of his life — whether anyone had ever gone looking." She turned back to me, pleasant as Sunday. "Do you follow such stories, Madame Delacroix?"

"No," I said.

"A pity. He found them fascinating." She opened her lacquered case, took out a book — one of her husband's journals, cloth-bound, swollen with pressed flowers and marginalia — and began to read it as though I had left the room.

I hadn't. I stood there holding my forms against my chest like a shield, and understood that I had just been lied to twice in one sentence: once in the question, once in the answer she expected from me.

* * *

The last one didn't come to the tavern at all.

I found the kid at dusk behind the feed store, sitting on an overturned crate with a pack nearly as big as she was, eating an apple with the total concentration of someone who wasn't sure where the next one was coming from. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Hood up, hair chopped short with what looked like kitchen scissors, and the specific defiant exhaustion of a runaway three towns past caring.

"You're the minor," I said. "The booking says seventeen, accompanied by consent of guardian."

The apple stopped halfway to her mouth. "The booking says a lot of stuff."

"It does. That's why I read them." I crouched to her level, which she hated — I could see her hating it — and held out my hand. "Papers."

"There aren't any."

"Name?"

A long pause. Wind worried the loose tin on the feed store roof. "Tally," she said finally, in the tone of someone testing whether the word fit. Maybe it was hers. Maybe it was the third one she'd tried on this month.

"Tally. Why the Verren Pass?"

"Because it's far away." She said it flatly, without drama, which was somehow worse than crying would have been. "Because nobody comes back from it asking questions. Because the broker man said you don't ask any." Her chin came up, and underneath the grime and the bravado her eyes were bright and furious and young. "Are you going to send me back?"

Every rule I had said yes. Every regulation, every insurance clause, every line of the professional code that had already failed me once and spat me out. Send her back. Refuse the deposit. Report the broker.

Instead I heard myself say: "Can you cook?"

"...What?"

"On the mountain. Can you cook, or melt snow, or mend a torn pack? Everybody works. That's the only law I have."

Something shifted in her face — not trust, nothing so generous, but the recalibration of someone who has been braced for a blow and received terms instead. "I can cook," she said. "My mother ran a kitchen. Before."

Before. The word had a cliff edge in it. I didn't step off it.

"Dawn tomorrow. East gate. If you're late, we leave without you."

"I'm never late." She picked up the apple core, considered it, and ate the rest, down to the stem. "You're not what I expected," she added, watching me with those bright suspicious eyes. "The broker said you were desperate."

"The broker was right. Go sleep somewhere warm, Tally. Cold kills more climbers than falls do."

She shouldered the enormous pack and melted into the alley dark, and I stood there in the smell of hay and horse, doing arithmetic I didn't like.

* * *

That night I sat in my rented room with the lamp turned low and dealt the four of them out across the bed like cards, and tried to find the game.

A journalist who knew my history and quoted it back to me like a man laying bait. An ex-miner who watched doors and answered questions with stones. A widow whose dead husband had wondered aloud about a missing plane, hiring me through cutouts to cross the exact range where that plane had vanished. And a child running from something toward nothing, whose only crime was being young enough to believe distance was safety.

Four clients. Four reasons. None of them true, or none of them whole, which amounts to the same thing on a mountain.

And beneath it all, coiled tight as wire in my chest, the thing I hadn't let myself think since Lior died breathing his promise into my hands: *the wreck is up there, and I am not the only one who knows.*

Lior had been followed. He'd said so, or close enough — the words had come ragged and half-frozen, but I remembered the shape of them. *They'll come looking. Don't be on the mountain when they do.* I had told myself it was the fever talking. Dying men see pursuers in every shadow.

But dying men are also, sometimes, the only ones telling the truth.

I thought about the broker in Vell who'd charged me nothing for finding four clients in three days — nothing, when brokers like him bled guides white in a good season. I thought about how quickly the job had appeared, how precisely it fit, how the route ran within a day's march of coordinates Lior had scratched into wax paper with a dying hand.

I got up and stood at the window. Below, Ferrenholt slept under new snow, and beyond it the Verren Pass waited, white and silent and patient as a trap that has been set a long time.

Professional courtesy. That was all I could afford them, and all they could afford me. Smiles at breakfast, knots checked twice, everyone's lie carried politely in everyone's pack alongside the rope and the stove fuel. Fourteen days. Whatever they were, whatever they wanted, I'd deliver them over the pass alive and be paid, and then I would lose them in the low country and go find a wreck alone.

That was the plan.

At dawn, when I reached the east gate, all five of them were waiting — the four clients and, standing apart from them with his collar up and his face turned from the wind, a fifth man I had never hired, never met, and never seen in my life.

He tipped his hat to me like an old acquaintance.

"Morning, guide," he said. "Lovely day for a crossing."
