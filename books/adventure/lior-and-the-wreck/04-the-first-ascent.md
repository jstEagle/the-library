## 4. The First Ascent

Dawn came to the Verren Pass the way it always did — grudgingly, gray light seeping down through the col like water through a cracked ceiling, touching the glacier's upper tongue long before it reached the valley floor. Elodie stood at the edge of camp with her back to the fire, watching that light crawl, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since midnight.

Four clients. One guide. Eleven days of route, two of them above four thousand meters, and a storm window she could feel tightening somewhere over the western ranges like a fist closing slowly.

She turned and looked at them where they sat around the dying fire, hunched in their borrowed gear, and made herself see them not as people but as variables. It was easier that way. Kinder, eventually. You could grieve for people; you couldn't grieve for variables until the mountain was done with them.

Corvin was already awake, of course. He sat apart from the others on a flat stone, notebook open on his knee despite the cold, writing something with the quick, proprietary strokes of a man who believed the world existed to be recorded. He'd slept in his boots. She'd watched him do it last night, pretending not to watch, and had filed it away: either he was afraid someone would steal his pack in the night, or he expected to have to move fast without warning. Neither possibility comforted her.

Marta Voss was awake too, sitting very straight on her bedroll with her hands folded in her lap, silver-haired and immaculate even at altitude, as if grief were a kind of tailoring. The widow had paid double for this crossing and asked no questions about the route, which meant she wanted something from these mountains that had nothing to do with mountains. Elodie had guided enough mourners to know the type. They came up here to be closer to their dead, or farther from them, and they never said which.

The boy — Tam, he'd finally admitted, though Elodie suspected it wasn't his real name any more than his story was real — still slept, curled tight as a fist, one arm thrown over his face against a world that had disappointed him early. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. All elbows and stubbornness. He'd lied about his age so badly that lying had become its own confession.

And Brase.

Brase was standing at the far edge of camp, facing the glacier, motionless. He'd been there when Elodie woke, or perhaps he'd never lain down. Big man, heavy through the shoulders, with the flattened affect of someone who had spent years being spoken to rather than speaking. Ex-miner, he'd said when they'd met. Twenty years underground. His hands said it was true — scarred, thick-knuckled, nails permanently rimmed with shadow. But his hands also said other things, if you knew how to read them, and Elodie had spent the whole night not quite letting herself read them.

Today she would let herself.

"Rope check in ten minutes," she called, and her voice carried the flat authority she'd spent fifteen years building, the voice that brooked no negotiation before breakfast. "Eat now. Drink more than you want. Anyone who hasn't broken in their boots, tell me now, because after the moraine there is no turning back for three days."

Tam sat up as if scalded. Marta rose without complaint. Corvin closed his notebook with a small, satisfied snap, as though the morning were a scene he'd finished drafting.

Brase turned from the glacier and looked at her. Just looked. And in that look, brief as a match strike, Elodie saw something she couldn't name yet — an assessment running the other direction, her own professional scrutiny reflected back at her from behind those flat gray eyes.

*He's counting us too,* she thought. *Why is he counting us?*

She put the thought away with the rest of them, in the locked drawer at the back of her mind where she kept everything that didn't help her get clients over a pass alive. The drawer was getting full this trip. That worried her more than anything in it.

* * *

The first hour was moraine — a mile of loose rock dumped by the glacier's slow retreat, ankle-breaking, knee-grinding, the kind of ground that sorted climbers into categories faster than any interview. Elodie set a deliberate pace, not fast but relentless, and positioned herself mid-line so she could watch everyone at once.

Marta walked like a woman who had decided to walk, which was different from walking well but not without its own power. Her footing was mediocre; her will was not. Twice she stumbled on scree and twice she recovered without a sound, jaw set, refusing the hand Tam shyly offered. She would be a problem on ice, Elodie noted. Pride was heavier than any pack.

Tam surprised her. The boy moved with the low, economical stride of someone who had walked away from things before — towns, homes, names — and had learned to do it without wasting energy. He complained constantly, cheerfully, a running mutter of grievance that Elodie recognized as armor. Complaining boys didn't have time to panic. She'd take the complaints.

Corvin was competent. That was the word she settled on, and it annoyed her. Competent the way a journalist embedded with soldiers is competent — enough technique to keep up, acquired recently and deliberately, worn lightly. He stepped where she stepped, matched her rhythm within minutes, and never once asked why she chose the line she chose. Most clients asked. Clients who didn't ask had usually already read the answer somewhere.

And Brase —

Brase walked the moraine like it was a hallway in his own house.

It was nothing dramatic. That was what unsettled her. No show of strength, no swagger. But every placement of his boots was exact, weight rolled onto the uphill edge before the rock could shift, his stride shortening automatically where the slope steepened, his breathing a steady metronome that never varied even as the grade bit into the others. When a slab the size of a door tilted under Marta's foot, Brase's hand was on her elbow before Elodie could call the warning — not grabbing, just steadying, the touch of someone who had caught falling people before and knew exactly how much force a human frame tolerates.

Elodie stopped calling warnings after that. She watched instead.

Twenty years underground, he'd said. Mines had ladders and timbered galleries. Mines did not teach a man to read unstable ground with his feet, to distribute his weight like a dancer, to anticipate another body's fall half a second before physics committed to it.

Glacier schools taught that. Rescue teams taught that. Armies taught that.

She filed it in the drawer. The drawer groaned.

* * *

The glacier's tongue began where the moraine ended, a river of dirty ice flowing down out of the white silence above, crevassed near its edges, ridged and blue-veined in its depths. Here the real test would happen. Elodie called a halt at the snout, in the shelter of a boulder, and got out the rope.

"Rules," she said, and held up fingers. "One: nobody moves on ice unroped. Not one step. Two: spacing is twelve feet, no less — if I go in a hole, you don't come in with me. Three: when I say *down*, you drop and anchor, no questions, no looking around to see if I mean it. I always mean it."

"And if someone else goes in?" Corvin asked. His tone was light, curious, a man collecting information.

"Then you do exactly what I trained you to do five minutes from now, and you pray I'm heavy enough to hold." She looked around the circle, meeting each pair of eyes. Marta's were dry and distant. Tam's were wide. Corvin's were interested. Brase's were — she made herself hold them — calm. Too calm. The calm of a man hearing a briefing he'd heard before, in some other life, in some other uniform.

"We cross the lower tongue today," she went on. "It's crevassed but bridged — snow covers the worst of it, and my map marks the safe line. Tomorrow we climb to the saddle. Questions?"

"What's under the snow," Tam said, "when the bridge isn't a bridge?"

"A hundred meters of blue nothing," Elodie said. "That's why we rope."

The boy swallowed. Good. Fear, properly dosed, was the best equipment a client could carry.

She ran them through the mechanics: harness checks, the prusik loop, the ice axe arrest position, falling into self-arrest posture on a snow slope until her voice was hoarse with repetition. Marta learned slowly but perfectly, a woman who practiced things until they yielded. Tam learned fast and sloppily and then, corrected, fast and well. Corvin performed each drill with the fluency of recent instruction — she could almost see the instructor's hands adjusting his grip, some course, some winter, some purpose.

Then Brase took the rope, and Elodie stopped performing.

She gave him the same drills, watched with the same apparent boredom, and inside herself she opened the locked drawer all the way.

His self-arrest was textbook — no, better than textbook, because textbooks are written by people who learned it in courses, and Brase had clearly learned it in weather. He rolled into position without the half-second hesitation everyone has, the body's natural protest against throwing itself headfirst down a slope. His axe angle was exact. His boots, edged into the snow, dug trenches straight as survey lines. When she sent him across a short stretch of bare ice in crampons, he moved with a rolling, deliberate grace that made the spikes sing, and when she abruptly shouted *Down!* — a test, unfair, unannounced — he dropped and anchored before the echo died, body already braced to hold a fall, eyes finding hers with an expression that said, quite clearly: *I know what you're doing.*

She held his gaze a moment longer than she needed to.

"Good," she said aloud. "Very good."

"Used to climb," Brase said. First words he'd offered all morning. His voice was gravel poured slow. "Long time ago."

"Where?"

"Different mountains."

He said it without hostility, without evasion even — just finality, a door closing gently on a room he didn't wish to show her. Elodie smiled the guide's smile and moved down the line, and added *different mountains* to the drawer, where it lay glowing like a hot coal among the other coals.

* * *

They crossed the lower tongue in the early afternoon, roped in order: Elodie leading, then Tam, then Marta, then Corvin, then Brase anchoring the rear. She'd debated the order all through lunch and settled on it for reasons she didn't fully examine — the boy where she could reach him, the widow where Brase could catch her, the journalist where she could hear whatever he muttered, and the ex-miner at the back, where she could watch him without turning her head.

Because that was the truth she was circling: she didn't trust him at her six, and she trusted herself to watch him more than she trusted anyone else to.

The glacier was beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful, all blues and shadows, the snow bridges arcing over darkness like frozen breath. Elodie found her line from memory and instinct — she'd crossed this tongue twice before, years ago, in another life with another name attached to hers — and probed each bridge with her axe before committing her weight, then waved the others across the same steps.

Halfway across, the mountain tested them.

It was a small thing, as mountain things go. A snow bridge that had held her probe decided, with the perverse independence of snow, that it would not hold Tam. The boy's right leg punched through to the thigh with a sound like a giant biting a biscuit, and suddenly he was up to the hip in the glacier, arms windmilling, screaming a wordless note that echoed off the seracs.

Elodie had him before the scream finished — flat on the bridge, axe anchored, left hand locking his wrist, her whole body become a lever against the void. "Stop moving. *Stop.* Look at me. I've got you."

"I can't feel — oh God — I can't—"

"You can. Breathe. Slow." She turned her head. "Brase. Anchor point, three o'clock, that bulge. Everyone else — flat, spread the load, *now*."

And here was the thing she would turn over that night, and the next night, and for many nights after: she didn't need to say the rest. She felt the rope go taut behind her, a second anchor coming alive exactly where she'd have placed it, and knew without looking that Brase had driven his axe into the bulge, wrapped the rope, and thrown his weight backward with the smooth economy of a man doing a thing he had done many times, in worse places, with worse consequences hanging on it.

Between the two of them, Tam came out of the hole like a cork, gasping, frost-white to the waist, laughing shakily in the way people laugh when the alternative is weeping. Elodie checked him for injuries, found none, and stood there a moment with her heart slamming against her ribs, feeling the strange, unwelcome warmth of competence shared — the rare, animal pleasure of working with someone whose hands you don't have to doubt.

"There," she heard Brase say quietly behind her, to the boy. "You fell in a hole. Now you won't again. That's the whole lesson."

"That's the whole lesson?" Tam's voice cracked halfway up.

"That's the whole lesson."

Elodie got the line moving again. But something had shifted in her chest, some plate grinding against another plate, and she understood with reluctant clarity that the question had changed. An hour ago the question had been *what is Brase hiding*. Now it was *why does a man with his training need to hide it* — and underneath that, quieter, colder, the question she liked least:

*What was he trained for?*

* * *

They made the far moraine by late afternoon and camped in the lee of a granite spine, tents pitched on bare stone above the ice. The light went gold, then copper, then gone, and the temperature fell off the edge of the world the way it does at altitude, twenty degrees in an hour, the stars coming out hard and close.

Elodie cooked, checked knots, checked the boy's frost-nipped leg, checked the sky. The sky bothered her. There was a veil of cirrus sliding in from the west, thin as smoke, pretty and meaningless to anyone who hadn't been betrayed by weather before. To Elodie it read like handwriting on a wall. Two days, maybe three, and then the pass would make its own decisions about their schedule.

She was banking the stove when Brase came and sat across the fire from her. Uninvited. Close enough to talk low, far enough to be proper. He held his hands to the flames, and the firelight moved in the old scars there.

"You knew," he said.

"Knew what?"

"Back on the ice. That I'd place the anchor. You called the point without looking."

Elodie fed a stick to the fire and considered lying to him, then decided lies between professionals were a waste of good material. "You move like someone who's done this. Done all of it. Arrests, anchors, loads. Moraine doesn't teach that. Mines don't teach that." She looked up. "So where did you learn, Brase? Different mountains — whose?"

For a long moment he only watched the fire. Then he said, "You're good at reading people. That's a dangerous skill up here. Makes you see things that aren't threats."

"And makes me see things that are."

"Yes." He said it simply, without argument, and somehow that was worse than denial. He rose, dusted off his knees, and paused at the edge of the firelight. "Get your clients over the pass, Ms. Delacroix. That's the job. Some questions weigh more than the answer's worth."

He walked away toward his tent, big shoulders rolling slightly, footsteps silent on the stone — silent, she noted, absolutely silent, which no miner's walk ever was — and Elodie sat alone with the fire and the cirrus veil and the full, groaning drawer of things she knew.

Different mountains. Silent feet. Textbook blood. A man paid — she was suddenly sure of it, sure the way you're sure of weather — to be on this route, at this time, with this ragged little company of liars.

She should turn them around. She thought it plainly, watching the embers: turn them around, refund the widow, put the boy on a bus, and go find some smaller mountain where the secrets were only her own.

But Lior's map was folded against her breastbone, and a dead man's promise weighed more than a live man's evasions, and besides—

Besides, the pass was already closing. She could feel it in the west, in the smoke-thin clouds, in the pressure sitting on her back teeth like a thumb.

Tomorrow they'd climb for the saddle. And whatever waited for them up there — storm, wreck, or the thing Brase wouldn't name — it had been waiting a long time, and it was patient, and so, apparently, was everyone she'd hired to help her avoid it.

Elodie banked the fire, checked the boy's leg one last time, and lay down beneath stars that felt less like lights and more like eyes.

In the middle of the night, she woke to a sound she almost missed: boot soles on stone, soft as breath, moving past her tent toward the dark edge of camp. She lay still, heart ticking, and listened until the silence closed back over.

By dawn, whatever Brase had gone out into the dark to do or see, he was back in his bedroll, sleeping the sleep of the innocent.

Almost.
