## 5. What Lior Carried

Corvin Ash waited until the others slept before he opened his notebook.

He had a system for this — had refined it over eleven years and four continents, the small rituals of a man who made his living off other people's secrets. Wait for the fire to burn down to coals. Wait for the guide to make her last circuit of the camp, checking anchors, checking the sky. Wait for the widow's breathing to slow into its heavy, medicated rhythm, and for the boy to stop fidgeting in his bag. Then, by the light of a candle stub shielded inside his jacket, read.

The tent was a cheap nylon coffin he'd bought in Verrenstadt, and it smelled of paraffin and his own sweat. Outside, the glacier creaked and settled like an old ship, and somewhere above them the icefall groaned through its nightly argument with gravity. Corvin had heard that sound on three other mountains, and it had never once stopped making the skin of his arms prickle. He told himself it was the cold. He knew better. It was the feeling of being watched by something vast and indifferent, something that did not care whether he got his story or became part of someone else's.

He clicked his pen twice — an old habit, a trigger — and turned to the pages he'd labeled, in his neat draftsman's hand, *D.C.X.*

*Delacroix Crossing, Ten.* The expedition that had gone up the Verren massif seven years ago with ten clients and come down with four. The expedition that had ended Elodie Delacroix's career before Corvin had ever heard her name.

He'd been chasing it for two years. Not full-time — no one could afford to chase anything full-time anymore; the magazines wanted listicles and adventure-porn photo essays, not investigations — but persistently, the way water works at stone. He had interview notes from two of the survivors, both of whom had signed settlements with Halvern Mining's legal department and both of whom had suddenly remembered nothing useful. He had a coroner's report from the town of Sault-Brume that had been requested by three journalists before him and released to none. He had a photograph, purchased from a retired mountain police officer, of the recovery sleds coming down: four body bags, one of them child-sized, and standing apart from everyone else, a woman in a red guide's jacket with her face turned away from the camera as if she couldn't bear to look at what she'd brought down the mountain.

That woman was now fifty meters from his tent, asleep in a bivouac under an overhang she'd chosen without hesitation, on terrain she read like handwriting.

Corvin had recognized her at the tavern in Verrenstadt. Of course he had. He'd spent two years looking at that photograph. But recognition and certainty were different animals, and he was a professional, so he had done what professionals do: he had said nothing, taken the job, paid his fee, and let the mountain do the interrogating. Mountains were better interrogators than he would ever be. Give a person a rope length of exposure and a storm front, and every lie they carried came up out of them like groundwater.

Except she hadn't cracked. Three days on the ice and she hadn't put a foot wrong. She'd caught the boy's panic on the first crevasse field before the boy himself knew he was panicking. She'd read the cloud over the western col that morning and moved the crossing up two hours, and the storm had arrived exactly when she said it would. She'd tested them — he'd seen it for what it was, the long traverse where she'd spaced them out and watched how each of them moved, cataloguing their weaknesses with those flat grey eyes — and when it was his turn he'd given her his best performance: competent enough to be unremarkable, frightened enough to be believable, charming enough to keep her from looking too closely.

And she had smiled at him, and thanked him for keeping the widow steady, and moved on.

Which should have pleased him. Instead it had kept him awake.

He flipped back through the notebook to the beginning of the D.C.X. section. His own handwriting stared up at him, younger and more eager than he felt.

*Survivor accounts (inconsistent):*
*Mercier — "the guide pushed us past the turnback time. She wanted the summit for her own name." Signed settlement 4 months after.*
*Ilsa Voss — "Elodie saved my life. Whatever they say about her, I would be dead in that whiteout if not for her." Refused settlement. Has not spoken to press since.*

Two survivors, two stories, mirror opposites. That alone should have made him careful. In his experience, when the accounts split exactly down the middle, the truth was usually standing between them holding something neither witness had mentioned.

He turned the page. His notes from the Sault-Brume coroner's office — obtained, technically, through a Freedom of Information request that had been "lost" twice before a clerk with a gambling problem had found it for him.

*Cause of death, client R. Mercier Jr.: hypothermia compounded by fall injuries. Note appended: "injuries consistent with long slide over rock/ice, not protected arrest." Meaning: he wasn't roped. Or his rope failed. Or someone cut it.*

Below that:

*Client T. Anselm: cause of death listed as "exposure." No fall. Died warm-side of the storm line according to weather data. Question: why was a client above the shelter line during a forecast storm? Who ordered the push?*

And below that, underlined twice, the note that had started all of this — the thread he'd followed out of the disaster and into something much larger, though at the time he'd thought it was nothing:

*All four deceased clients held exploration permits filed through Halvern Mineral AG. All four had surveyed the same drainage: the Kessel valley, south aspect. Coincidence?*

It had not been coincidence. He knew that now. He'd known it for eight months, since the night in a Grenoble archive room when he'd cross-referenced the permit filings against Halvern's internal survey schedule — leaked to him, in a manila envelope with no return address, by someone inside the company who had either a conscience or a grudge — and found that the four dead clients weren't tourists who happened to hold mineral claims. They were prospectors. Working Halvern's interests. And the route their guide had led them on, the route that killed them, passed directly over the one valley in the entire massif that Halvern's own geologists had flagged, in a memo dated eleven years ago, with a single word: *Kessel — DO NOT SURVEY. SENSITIVE.*

Sensitive. Corporate language for *there is something here we do not want found*, and in Corvin's experience there were only three things companies meant by that: money, liability, or bodies.

He had built the whole theory on that foundation. *The Guide Who Led Them to Die* — that was the working title, and it was a good title, and he hated how good it was. The story wrote itself: ambitious young guide, hungry for a first ascent and a reputation, takes a fat fee from mining-company clients, pushes them past safety into a storm, four die, and then — here was the elegant part, the part that had made him sit back in the archive chair at two in the morning — the blacklist. Because Halvern needed the deaths to look like incompetence rather than corporate survey work, and a disgraced guide was the perfect scapegoat. They didn't just abandon her afterward. They had *built* her ruin, brick by brick, whisper by whisper, in every guide's association hall and insurance office and hiring desk from Chamonix to Kathmandu. She wasn't the villain of the story. She was the second victim.

He had proof of most of it. He had the memo. He had the permit trail. He had the settlement agreements and the lost coroner's file and the pattern of whispers he'd traced through three guide associations, each trace ending at the same Zurich PR firm that also, conveniently, handled Halvern's crisis communications.

What he did not have was Elodie Delacroix's account. And what he did not have, above everything, was the reason. Why that valley. What was in the Kessel drainage that Halvern had been willing to kill four men to survey quietly and one woman's reputation to bury loudly?

He'd had a hypothesis. He'd written it down six weeks ago, on the train out of Zurich, in handwriting pressed so hard into the page it had nearly torn:

*If the dead clients were surveying Kessel, they were looking for something. If Halvern sealed the whole disaster, they were protecting something bigger than a lawsuit. Follow the map. Find the thing. The thing is the story.*

And then Lior had walked into Elodie's camp with a bullet in his gut and a map in his coat, and Corvin — who had bribed a mule driver, befriended a cook, and eavesdropped shamelessly for three days to learn that much — had understood that the thing had just fallen into his lap wearing a guide's red jacket.

Because that was the real reason he was on this climb. Not the Verren crossing, not the fee, not the cover story about a mountaineering feature he'd pitched to a magazine that would never run it. He was here because Elodie Delacroix was going to lead them somewhere, and he intended to follow, and whatever she found at the end of it, he would be there with a camera and a notebook and a satellite transmitter wrapped in a wool sock at the bottom of his pack.

His transmitter. He touched the wool sock through the nylon of his duffel, the way a gambler touches a lucky chip. One ridge with line-of-sight to the relay tower on the Verrenstadt side, Elodie had told the clients on the first evening, pointing — casual, informative, a guide doing her job. *If anyone needs to send word home, that's our window.* She'd said it like a courtesy.

She'd said it like a woman who knew exactly which ridge had signal, and Corvin had wondered ever since why a blacklisted guide leading a routine commercial crossing cared about transmission windows at all.

He turned to a fresh page. At the top he wrote the date, and beneath it, in letters smaller than his usual hand:

*Known facts about E.D. as of tonight:*

*1. She took this job broke. Genuinely broke — I saw the state of her gear. A woman planning a comeback doesn't patch a harness with wire.
2. She knows this route better than any commercial guide should. She's been on the south aspects. Nobody guides the south aspects.
3. She asked me, on day two, what I was really writing. Casually. Over tea. I gave her the magazine pitch and she nodded and looked at me for exactly one second too long.
4. She is not running from her reputation. She is walking toward something. I don't know what yet. But Lior's map points at the Kessel, and she has that map, and she has not turned us around.*

He stopped writing. The pen hovered.

Here was the thing he had not written down, because writing it down would make it real, and he was not yet ready for it to be real: he liked her.

Not the shallow liking of a source managed well — he was good at that, he manufactured it by the yard, it cost him nothing. This was worse. This was the liking that had crept up on him across three days of shared danger, the liking that lived in specific moments: the way she'd knelt beside the widow Marta on the moraine and adjusted the woman's gaiters without asking, without being asked, without appearing to notice she'd done it. The way she'd talked the runaway boy down from the ice bridge, her voice low and even, telling him about the physics of snow bridges as if fear were just another load calculation. The way she stood the last watch every night and let the others sleep, and never once mentioned it.

Ambition was a simple machine. Corvin had maintained his for eleven years: fuel it with grievance, cool it with cynicism, and it ran forever. The story of Elodie Delacroix — the true story, the exoneration, the takedown of Halvern — would make his career. It would be the piece other journalists lied about having worked on. Front page, byline above the fold, maybe a book deal, certainly the end of scraping by on adventure fluff and corporate ghostwriting. He had imagined the moment of publication so many times it had worn smooth as river stone.

But the machine had developed a rattle. Because the woman in the story was not a character. She was a person who had given him extra tea when his stove sputtered, and who had checked his anchor knots on the fixed lines without comment — not because she distrusted him, he realized now, but because she trusted herself to catch what he missed, and that was somehow more intimate than distrust. And if he used her — if he rode behind her to the wreck and let her open whatever door Lior's map pointed to and then published the whole thing over her head, taking the story and the credit and leaving her exposed again, this time by the one person she might have believed was just a client —

No. He shut that thought down. Sentiment was how sources burned you. He'd seen it happen to better reporters than himself: they fell in love with the people in their notebooks and softened the copy, and the copy died, and the injustice survived, and the people they loved stayed victims anyway. Ruthlessness was a form of respect. If you truly believed a wrong needed righting, you owed it your sharpest self, not your kindest one.

Besides — and here he made himself look at it squarely, in the candle stub light, the way she would have looked at a cornice before crossing beneath it — she was using him too. Maybe. Probably. A guide who took a job this desperate, from clients this mismatched, onto a detour that bent toward a forbidden valley? Either she was the luckless instrument of Lior's dying wish, or she was running her own operation and the five of them were cover, porters, witnesses, alibi. He genuinely could not tell which. And the fact that he could not tell — after eleven years of reading people for a living — was itself information. It meant she was better at this than he was.

The candle guttered. Outside, the wind had shifted, coming around to the north with a dry, mineral smell that meant high cold air descending, and Corvin thought, with the automatic reflex of two years of obsession: *weather window closing. Whatever she's going to do, she'll do it soon.*

He looked down at his notebook, at the fresh page with its four numbered facts, and beneath them he wrote a fifth:

*5. Decision required. When we find it — whatever it is — do I transmit, or do I ask her first?*

He sat with that for a long time. The glacier ticked and groaned. Somewhere in the dark, an owl — impossibly, at this altitude — gave its flat call and got no answer.

Then he added a sixth line, and the handwriting was almost too small to read even by his own eyes:

*6. Note to self: she promised a dying man something. Find out what, before you decide what you owe her.*

He blew out the candle.

In the darkness he lay listening to the mountain breathe, and it occurred to him, finally, with the clarity that comes only at the edge of sleep, that he had framed the question wrong from the start. It was not *what did Lior carry*. He'd spent three days assuming the answer was a map, a secret, a scoop — cargo, something to be intercepted and weighed and sold.

But he had watched Elodie take the old man's hand that night at the edge of the firelight. He had watched her listen. And Lior had gripped her fingers and spoken too quietly for Corvin to hear, and whatever he'd said, it had changed the set of her shoulders — not the way grief changes them, but the way a yoke does.

Lior hadn't carried a map.

He'd carried a promise. And now she was carrying it, up the mountain, with five strangers — one of whom was paid to watch her, one of whom was lying about why she'd come, one of whom was a child, and one of whom was a journalist planning to steal the ending.

Corvin closed his eyes. Tomorrow they would break camp and move higher, toward the col, toward whatever the map bent toward, and the wind from the north was picking up, dry and cold and smelling faintly — or perhaps he imagined it — of smoke.

He was almost asleep when he heard it: footsteps outside his tent. Soft ones. Pausing, once, directly beside the nylon wall, close enough that he could hear the fabric shift in the displaced air. Then moving on, unhurried, toward the edge of camp where the fixed ropes began.

Elodie, on her rounds. Except her rounds had ended an hour ago. He'd counted her circuits, the way he counted everything.

He lay very still in the dark and did not breathe, and listened to her walk away into the wind, and understood that he had just learned the most important fact of the expedition:

She was counting too.
