## 6. The Pass Closes

The storm announced itself the way storms do in the Verren — not with thunder but with a smell. Elodie caught it at dawn on the fourth day out from the last settlement: a mineral tang riding the wind off the northern icefields, the scent of snow that had been lifted and dropped ten thousand times before it reached you. Old snow. Mean snow.

She stood outside her tent with the thermometer strip between two fingers and watched the light come up wrong. Not gold, not even grey — a bruised, greenish pallor over the eastern col, like something festering behind the mountains.

"Bad?" Corvin asked, zipping out of his tent with his hair flattened on one side and his camera already in hand. He slept with it, she'd noticed. Or pretended to.

"Barometer's fallen forty points since midnight."

"Is that bad?"

"It's the mountain exhaling," she said. "Before it inhales."

He laughed, delighted, and raised the camera, and she turned away so he wouldn't photograph her face doing whatever it was doing. Four days of watching him watch everything. He was good at it — better than he let on. The charm was real but it was also a tool, the way a piton is a tool, and she'd begun to notice how he deployed it: jokes for Brase, who didn't laugh; gentle questions for Marta, who answered too fully; an older-brother ease with Tam, the runaway boy, who had started orbiting Corvin like a cold moth around a lamp. Everyone trusted him within three days. That was either a gift or a profession.

Behind her, the camp woke in its usual order: Brase first, silent, already boiling water over the stove with the economy of a man who'd fed crews underground for twenty years. Then Marta, emerging immaculate from a tent no one could believe she carried, wool coat brushed, silver hair pinned as if for a luncheon in the city rather than four thousand metres of rock and ice. Tam last, always last, dragged up by hunger.

"Storm coming," Brase said. Not a question. He'd smelled it too.

"How long do we have?"

He looked north, squinting against the wind. "If it's what I think it is — half a day. Maybe less."

The high route to the Verren Pass crossed the Serracol Icefield, a broad white shoulder that in fair weather took six hours to traverse. In weather like this it became a maze of buried crevasses and wind-driven blindness, a place where parties died in groups, holding rope, walking politely into the void together. Every guide's manual said the same thing: *do not be on the icefield when the green light comes.*

And every guide's manual assumed you had somewhere else to go.

Elodie spread the map on the flat top of a supply crate and weighted the corners with stones. The high route ran clean and simple across the icefield and down the far side into the pass proper. Below it, to the east, the terrain folded into a labyrinth of ridgelines and hanging valleys — slower, lower, technically possible, the kind of route you took when the sky went bad and you were willing to trade two days for your life.

Except that Lior's map showed another option. She'd studied it every night since his death, by headlamp, alone, telling herself she was memorizing it for safety. A dotted line left the main route just below the icefield and swung through a narrow valley the survey maps marked only as *Kessel Draw* — unglaciated, sheltered, with a bench of solid ground partway along where a party could sit out a storm. It was almost suspiciously convenient. Almost as if someone had walked it recently and wanted it remembered.

She hadn't told the clients about Lior's map. She'd told them about Lior himself, briefly — a prospector she'd met, dead now, who'd shared route knowledge — and they'd accepted it the way clients accept everything, as scenery.

"We can't cross the icefield today," she told them now, gathered around the crate. "We'd be in the white by noon with no visibility and crevasses under us. So we drop east, into Kessel Draw, and wait out the front. It'll cost us a day."

"A day." Marta's voice was mild, but her eyes weren't. "Our window for the crossing—"

"The window closes if we're dead, Mrs. Voss."

A flicker of something — respect, or its cousin — crossed the widow's face. "Then Kessel Draw it is."

"I've been thinking," Corvin said, in the tone of a man who'd been thinking charmingly. "That draw. Any stories attached? Local color? My readers eat that up."

"There are no readers on this trip," Elodie said, rolling the map. "There are four people whose lives I'm responsible for."

"And yet you keep talking about this route like it has a history."

She met his eyes. They were warm brown and entirely unreadable, which she was beginning to understand was their most dangerous feature. "Everything on this mountain has a history. Walk."

* * *

They dropped east through the morning as the green light thickened overhead, descending scree slopes into the throat of Kessel Draw. The wind followed them down, funnelling, gaining voice — by midday it was moaning through the narrows like something grieving, and the first flakes came sideways, dry and stinging, harbingers of the tons of snow stacked up behind them.

Elodie had to admit Lior had known his ground. The draw was exactly as mapped: steep-walled, wind-shadowed, with the promised bench of gravel terraces above the streambed where they could pitch tents without fear of avalanche runout. She found herself almost grateful, and then immediately suspicious of her own gratitude. Nothing on this mountain had been convenient since the day a dying man bled into her camp.

They made camp fast, in the practiced rhythm of the fifth day — Brase anchoring tents, Tam hauling and stowing, Marta organizing the food stores with a precision that suggested boardrooms, Corvin photographing everything until Elodie told him to put the camera away and help.

By late afternoon the storm had arrived in earnest. The world shrank to the radius of a lantern: white walls, roaring dark, the tents snapping like sails. They crowded into the big dome with the stove, four bodies and their guide, steam rising off wet wool, and played cards by lamplight while the mountain tried to erase them.

It was Tam, inevitably, who broke the silence that mattered.

"So what's actually down this valley?"

Elodie looked up from her cards. "What do you mean?"

"You keep looking at the map. Like there's something on it." The boy shrugged, all teenage armor, but his eyes were sharp. "You did it twice during dinner."

"He's right," Marta said quietly. "You have been."

The stove ticked. Wind pressed at the fabric walls like hands.

"There's a wreck," Elodie said.

She hadn't planned to say it. But lying to four people in a tent the size of a coffin felt suddenly worse than the truth, and besides — some instinct told her the truth was safer than the questions they'd ask if she kept silent.

"An aircraft," she went on. "Old one. Went down in these ranges years back. The prospector I told you about — Lior — he found it. Marked the location. He gave me the map before he died."

Corvin had gone very still. Not dramatically still — he was too good for drama — but she saw his thumb stop moving on the edge of his cards, saw the small recalibration in his face. "A wreck," he repeated. "Of the Delacroix expedition plane?"

The name landed in the tent like a dropped stone.

"You know it," Elodie said.

"Everyone knows it. It's the great mystery of the range. Eleven people, vanished thirty years ago, no wreckage ever recovered. There are whole chapters of books about it." His smile was easy again, but late. Too late by half a second. "How far is it from here?"

"Why?"

"Curiosity."

"Your curiosity has a byline, Mr. Corvin. Mine doesn't. We're here to wait out a storm, and then we cross the pass, and that's the end of it."

But Tam was leaning forward, eyes bright with the particular hunger of the young for anything forbidden, and Marta was studying Elodie with the expression of a woman reading a difficult contract, and Brase — Brase was looking at the tent wall as though he could see through it, down-valley, toward something only he knew.

* * *

The storm held them for eighteen hours. When it finally thinned, near dawn of the second day, it left behind more than a metre of new snow and a silence so total it rang.

Elodie crawled out into a world remade. The draw's walls wore fresh white; the sky was rinsed blue and hard; the cold came off the snowfield like a blade laid flat against the skin. Beautiful. Deadly later, when the sun warmed the slopes and the avalanches began their daily arguments with gravity. For now, stable. For now, they could move.

She shouldered her pack and led them up-valley toward the point where Lior's detour rejoined the main route below the icefield. Two hours' walk. The plan was simple: regain the standard trail, cross the icefield in the clear window, put Kessel Draw behind them and everything it contained with it.

They were forty minutes up-valley when they reached the cache.

She'd placed it herself on the approach, five days ago — a standard guide's precaution, a sealed drum lashed under an overhang at the draw's junction, holding spare rope, two racks of protection, emergency rations, and the fixed lines she'd need for the icefield's bergschrund. She'd checked it that evening. Rope coiled tight, drum sealed, everything exactly where a careful guide leaves it.

Now the overhang stood bare.

The drum was there — she could see the glint of its lid under the new snow — but the lashings had been cut. Not frayed, not chewed by foxes, not snapped by rockfall. Cut, with a blade, clean through both straps, and the contents were gone. All of it. Rope, rack, rations, lines. Someone had emptied her cache sometime in the days since she'd packed it, and the fresh snowfall had erased whatever sign they'd left.

She stood in front of the empty overhang for a long time, feeling the cold come up through her boots, feeling something colder come up through everything else.

"Problem?" Corvin asked, coming up beside her. His breath smoked.

"Our rope cache is gone."

"What do you mean, gone?"

"Gone." She crouched, touched the cut strap ends. The cuts were days old — the nylon had oxidized slightly, gone chalky at the edges. Before the storm. Before they'd even entered the draw. "Someone took it. Days ago."

Brase arrived and looked, saying nothing, but his jaw worked once, twice, like a man chewing on words he refused to swallow.

Marta came next, then Tam, and the five of them stood in a half-circle around the stripped rock like mourners around an open grave.

"Cut," Brase said at last. His voice was flat gravel. "Both straps. One motion each."

"You sound sure," Corvin said.

"I am sure. Animals don't cut. Rockfall doesn't cut twice, clean, parallel." He turned and scanned the valley walls, the slopes, the white silence above them, and Elodie watched his eyes move the way a soldier's move — sector by sector, threat by threat. "Someone did this."

"When?" Tam's voice cracked upward. "Who's even *up here*?"

"That," Elodie said slowly, "is an excellent question."

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing. The cache had been cut before the storm — before any of them knew they'd be routed through Kessel Draw. Which meant whoever cut it had known the storm would close the high route. Had known, or had counted on it. Had known that an early-season front would seal the Serracol Icefield and force her party down into this valley, along this exact line, past this exact rock.

Or had simply known where she would go regardless — because the map in her pack, the dead man's dotted line, led here. Led past here. Led, if she read the contour lines correctly and she always read them correctly, up the eastern branch of Kessel Draw to a hanging basin the survey maps had never bothered to name.

To the wreck.

Her skin prickled beneath the layers. She thought of Lior bleeding out against her cook pot, pressing the map into her hands with fingers that had no strength left and all the urgency in the world. *Carry it out. Tell no one.* She'd thought she was receiving a dying man's burden. Now, standing over a sabotaged cache in a valley someone had prepared for her arrival, she wondered whether she'd received something else entirely.

Whether she'd been chosen. Whether the choosing had been visible to other eyes.

"Options," she said aloud, because guides talk, because silence spreads panic faster than wind spreads fire. "One: we go back down the draw and out the way we came. Three days, low ground, we lose the season and everyone loses their fees. Two: we continue as planned, regain the main route, cross the icefield tomorrow in the clear window. We don't need the cached rope — I carry backup, and the schrund crossing is short. It's tighter than I'd like. It's doable."

"And option three?" Corvin asked softly.

She looked at him. He was watching her with those warm, unreadable eyes, and she understood suddenly that he already knew there was an option three, had perhaps known since the word *wreck*, and was waiting to see whether she'd say it.

"There is no option three," she said.

The wind moved down the valley, lifting powder off the slopes in long white banners, and for a moment the light through the blowing snow turned the whole world the colour of bone.

They moved on. But Elodie fell to the rear, the place she never walked, and watched her four clients climb the trail ahead of her: the journalist glancing back too often, the ex-miner scanning the heights, the widow upright and unreadable, the boy stumbling and eager. And she made herself look at them the way she'd looked at the cut straps — closely, honestly, without mercy.

One of them might have done it. She had no proof, no logic that held — the cuts predated the storm, predated any knowledge of the detour, unless someone had known the forecast before she did, or known the map before she did. Unless someone had known about Lior. About the wreck. About her.

Unless someone had wanted her here.

Ahead, Brase paused at a bend in the trail and stood very still, facing up the eastern branch of the draw, where the unnamed basin waited under its metre of new snow. He stood there a moment too long. Then he turned and kept climbing, and said nothing at all.

Elodie followed, and the certainty settled over her like the weather itself, cold and vast and impossible to outrun:

They had not taken the detour. The detour had taken them.
