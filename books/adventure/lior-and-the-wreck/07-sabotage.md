## 7. Sabotage

Brase woke before the others, the way he always did, and lay still in the dark of his bivouac sack listening to the mountain breathe.

It was a sound most people never heard. Wind moving through the icefall two hundred meters below, a long slow exhalation. The tick and groan of seracs settling their weight. Somewhere higher, a cornice shedding frost in whispers. To Brase it was not silence but speech, and he had spent thirty years learning its grammar — first as a boy hauling ore carts at the Kessel pit, later in the deep workings where the rock talked back if you listened wrong.

He knew what mountains said when they were angry. He knew what they said when something on them was wrong.

This morning, they were saying it now.

* * *

He crawled out into the grey pre-dawn and stood among the tents, working blood back into his fingers. Four mounds of nylon under rime. Delacroix's tent sat apart from the clients', pitched with a precision that annoyed him — every guyline taut, every stake angled against the prevailing wind. A professional. That was the trouble with this whole arrangement. Professionals noticed things.

Brase had been hired to make sure nobody noticed anything.

The memory of it sat in his chest like swallowed coal. Six weeks ago, an office in Vellmar with leather chairs and a window that looked out on nothing but other offices. A man named Ostrevant — Halvern Mining Company, Office of External Affairs, which was a way of saying the part of the company that made problems disappear — sliding a photograph across the desk. A wrecked plane in a cirque below the Verren massif, half-swallowed by a glacier's slow crawl, tail fin still legible: *Delacroix Expedition, 1971.*

"You'll be guiding this season," Ostrevant had said. "Verren Pass route. There are people who come looking for that aircraft. Historians, journalists, relatives. Sentimentalists." He had smiled the way men smile when they've rehearsed it. "Your job is to be there first. Watch. Report. If anyone gets close—" a shrug, elegant and empty, "—the mountain handles most things itself. We'd just like to know about them beforehand."

And Brase had taken the money. Eight thousand up front, eight on completion, deposited to an account his daughter didn't know existed, earmarked for the surgery she kept postponing because she couldn't afford it. He told himself it was watching, only watching. Told himself the mountain handled most things itself, and that wasn't murder, it was weather.

He had been telling himself that for six weeks, and every morning the story weighed more.

* * *

The ropes were gone.

He found it when he went to sort the cache for the day's carry — the stash they'd buried yesterday at the moraine bend, wrapped in the orange tarp, marked by the cairn Delacroix insisted on building even though cairns could be read like signatures. He dug down through the scree with bare hands and came up with the tarp, light as a dead leaf.

Inside: the spare rack, the tent poles, the stove fuel. No ropes. All four dynamic lines, cut from their coils — not dropped, not lost. Cut. The ends were clean, sheared in single passes, no fraying, no sawing. Someone had knelt here in the dark with a good knife and all the time in the world.

Brase crouched over the empty tarp and felt the cold come up through his knees and into the place where his guilt lived, and the two colds met and became one thing.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not escape: he hadn't done it. And if he hadn't done it, then someone else had. Someone else was on this mountain, following this party, sleeping within earshot of their snores and their bad jokes and the widow Marta's careful coughing, and that someone had brought a knife and patience and, very likely, company instructions.

Ostrevant's voice, smooth as drill slurry: *We'd just like to know about them beforehand.*

Beforehand. Not instead. Not only.

* * *

"Problem?"

Elodie Delacroix stood ten feet away, already dressed, already watching him with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. She had the look of a woman who had been awake for hours, and Brase understood with a lurch that she might have seen him dig. Might have seen his face when he found the tarp empty.

"Cache is short," he said. Kept his voice level, the way you kept your voice level around unstable ground. "Ropes."

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. "Show me."

She knelt where he had knelt and examined the cut ends without touching them, turning them with a tent stake, and Brase watched her read the mountain's handwriting the way he did, and hated how much they had in common.

"One pass," she said quietly. "Sharp blade. They took their time." She looked up at him. "You heard nothing?"

"I sleep heavy."

"Do you?" she said, and it wasn't quite a question, and it stayed with him like a splinter as she rose and walked back toward the tents to wake the others, her voice pitched calm and competent: *change of plan, we rope-share, we double-check everything, nobody wanders.*

Nobody wanders. As if wandering were the danger.

Brase stood alone at the ruined cache and let himself, finally, do the thing he had refused to do for six weeks: remember it all, in order, without flinching.

* * *

He remembered the second meeting, three days before departure. Same office, different feel — Ostrevant less polished, more hurried, a man whose schedule had developed teeth.

"There's a guide named Delacroix leading a commercial party over the pass," Ostrevant said. "You may know the name."

Everyone knew the name. Everyone in the trade knew the name the way you knew the name of a shipwreck. *The guide who walked her clients into the storm. The woman who buried nine people under the Verren ice and walked away with her license.* Brase had believed it too, comfortably, the way you believe a story that requires nothing of you.

"I know it," he said.

"She's blacklisted. Broke. She'll take any work." Ostrevant studied his fingernails. "If she turns off the main route — if she goes anywhere near the south cirque — you'll signal us. There's a transmitter in your kit. Channel seven, dawn and dusk checks. You remember the protocol."

"And if she doesn't turn off?"

"Then you guide your little trip and collect your fee and go home to your daughter." The smile again. "Halvern rewards discretion, Herr Brase. It also remembers its friends."

He remembered lying to his daughter on the phone that night, telling her the season would be good, telling her not to worry, hearing the lie sit between them like a third person on the line.

He remembered checking in on channel seven, dawn and dusk, the ritual of it, the small dirty comfort of routine. *Party on schedule. Route normal.* Words that meant nothing and cost him nothing, or so he'd thought.

And he remembered — this was the part that curdled, this was the part that woke him now at four in the morning with his heart going like a stamp mill — the moment two days ago when Elodie had announced the detour. Storm closing the high route. Lior's marked alternative, the prospector's hand-drawn line bending south, toward the cirque, toward the wreck, and Brase standing there with the transmitter burning a hole in his pack while everyone else grumbled about the extra distance.

He had transmitted that night. *Route change. South approach. Possible objective unknown.* And the reply had come back in Ostrevant's flat cipher, and Brase had read it by the red glow of his headlamp shield, and the reply had been one word longer than any previous message:

*Understood. Stand by.*

Stand by. Not *acknowledged*, not *noted*. Stand by — the words you used with a demolition crew, with a firing detail, with men who needed to be ready to move when the order came. Brase had lain in his bag staring at the tent roof and understood, with the slow horrible clarity of a man watching an avalanche start above him, that he had not been hired to watch.

He had been hired to aim.

* * *

"Brase." Corvin, the journalist, dropping onto the rock beside him with that easy grin that Brase trusted exactly as far as he could throw the man uphill. "You look like a man doing arithmetic."

"Just checking gear."

"Mm." Corvin produced two cups of snow-melt tea and handed one over, uninvited, friendly as a trap. "Funny thing about last night. I sleep light — old habit, city nights. And I thought I heard movement. Downslope. Maybe an hour before dawn. More than one set of feet, I'd have said." He sipped. "Probably nothing. Wind does tricks up here."

"Wind does tricks," Brase agreed.

"But our guide seems rattled. Cache trouble, I hear." Corvin's eyes were pleasant and bottomless. "Ropes don't walk off by themselves, do they?"

"No," Brase said. "They don't."

He drank his tea and gave the man nothing else, and Corvin drifted off to interview the widow or needle the teenager or whatever it was he did to fill the hours, and Brase sat with the new information turning over in him like a stone in a drum.

More than one set of feet. Downslope. An hour before dawn.

They weren't following the party. They were ahead of it. Waiting at whatever the map bent toward, patient as ice, and the cut ropes weren't sabotage at all — they were herding. Pushing the party exactly where a herded party will go: forward, because backward means no ropes and no margin and slow death on the descent. Whoever held the knife wanted them to keep climbing.

Wanted them to reach the wreck.

Or wanted them reachable when they got there.

* * *

The party moved out at eight under a sky the color of dishwater. Elodie led, moving with a economy that Brase recognized and resented — she was better than her reputation, far better, which meant the reputation itself was manufactured, which meant the machinery that manufactured it was bigger and closer than he'd let himself believe. Corvin came next, cheerful, watchful. Then Marta Voss, the widow, steady as ballast, and young Tam bringing up the middle where Elodie had put him, and Brase took the rear position she'd assigned him and told himself it was trust.

He knew it wasn't. Rear position was where you put the person you suspected.

Fine. Let her suspect him. Suspicion was cleaner than what he deserved.

All morning the mountain spoke to him, and all morning he translated. The wind had shifted north-northwest — storm feed, twelve hours out, maybe less. The icefall below them ticked faster than yesterday; something big was settling in the lower seracs. And once, crossing a bare slab above the moraine, Brase stopped and crouched and touched a print in the frost-scoured grit.

A boot print. Fresh. Large, hobnailed, military pattern — not a brand sold in any shop Elodie's clients shopped at. It pointed upslope, toward the cirque, and beside it, half-erased, the print of another.

He covered it with his palm and then with scree, glancing back down the line. Nobody had seen. Nobody except, possibly, the mountain, which kept its own counsel.

But Elodie glanced back once, twenty minutes later, at nothing, at wind, at the empty grey world behind them — and Brase saw in her face that she had counted her party and found the number wrong. Five of them on the route. And somewhere behind or ahead, moving when they moved, stopping when they stopped:

the sixth.

* * *

Camp that night was a hollow beneath a leaning flake of gneiss, defensible, one way in. Elodie ran the evening like a drill: ropes coiled and clipped inside tents, kit inventoried twice, watches set in pairs. When she posted the first watch — herself and Brase, midnight to three — she said it plainly, no apology, and met his eyes while she said it, and the message was unmistakable: *I'm putting the wolf where I can see it.*

He took the watch. He sat with his back to the rock and the transmitter in his lap, channel seven, and the dusk check had come and gone unanswered — first time in six weeks the frequency had returned nothing but static, and static, tonight, sounded like held breath.

At 2 a.m., Elodie emerged from her tent and sat beside him without a word. For a long time neither spoke. The icefall ticked. The stars wheeled behind fast cloud.

"My last expedition," she said finally, to the darkness rather than to him, "I was told the weather window was guaranteed. Told by people who owned the mountain the way a man owns a dog. I believed them because believing was easier than asking why a mining company cared about my schedule." Her breath smoked. "Nine people are still in that ice, Brase. And every year somebody tells me it was my fault, and some years I almost believe them."

He should have said something. Confession was right there, warm in his mouth, eight thousand reasons to spit it out. Instead he heard himself say, carefully: "Who told you the window was guaranteed?"

"A fixer in Vellmar. Name of Ostrevant."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool.

"He works for Halvern," Brase said.

"I know," Elodie said. "I've known for eleven years. What I don't know is what he wants on my mountain now." She turned and looked at him, and her eyes in the starlight were the color of the glacier's deepest crevasses. "You checked in on a radio last night. Channel seven. I found the antenna wire in your kit this afternoon. So you have until sunrise to decide which side of this you're standing on — because someone cut our ropes, Brase, and it wasn't me, and it wasn't you, and whoever it was has boots I've been tracking since the moraine."

She had known. She had known and said nothing, watched him all day, given him a full day to choose — and that, more than any threat, broke something loose in his chest.

Below them, downslope in the dark, a light blinked twice. Paused. Blinked twice again. Deliberate. Answered, a moment later, by two blinks from somewhere above the camp — above, in the direction of the cirque, in the direction of the wreck.

Brase closed his fist around the transmitter until the casing cracked.

"Sunrise," he said hoarsely. "Ask me at sunrise."

But even as he said it, he was already answering — because the signals below and above had trapped their little camp in a pincer of light, and there was only one reason armed men signaled each other across a sleeping party, and it wasn't rescue.

The mountain breathed. The storm fed itself in the northwest. And Brase, watcher, traitor, father, began — at last — to plan how a man betrays the people who paid him.
