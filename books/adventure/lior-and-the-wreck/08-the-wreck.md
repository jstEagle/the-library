## 8. The Wreck

The detour had taken them two days off the main route, and by the second morning Elodie had stopped pretending to herself that she didn't know where they were going.

Lior's map was inked on the back of a flour sack, creased into softness from years of folding, and it showed a spur of rock above the Kestrel Icefield that no survey chart acknowledged — a shoulder of black stone the locals, if there had ever been locals up here, might have called something. Lior had called it nothing at all. He'd simply drawn a small, careful X and beside it, in a hand that shook only at the very end: *the bird.*

She hadn't shown the map to anyone. Not Corvin with his easy smile and his questions dressed as small talk. Not Brase, who watched her hands when she checked the route each morning as though memorizing them. Not Marta Voss, whose grief had hardened into something almost architectural, or the boy, Tam, who was too busy surviving to scheme. Elodie had folded the flour sack into her breast pocket every night and slept with it against her ribs like a second heart.

Now, on the third morning out from the cut rope cache, the mountain gave them the spur.

It rose out of the icefield's eastern edge like the rib of some drowned leviathan, black rock furred with rime, and where the rock met the glacier there was a scar — a long smear of displaced ice, old and healed over but still visible, the way a broken bone shows in an old man's hand.

Tam saw it first. "What's that?" He pointed with his chin, hands buried in his sleeves. The boy had stopped asking for things with his hands days ago; he'd learned that requests froze faster than fingers.

"Icefall debris," Elodie said.

"It's straight," he said. "Ice doesn't fall straight."

She looked at him. Fifteen years old, half-starved, running from something he wouldn't name, and sharp as a fresh edge. "No," she agreed. "It doesn't."

Corvin came up beside her, breath steaming, camera hanging under his coat where he thought she didn't know about it. He followed the line of the scar upward, and she watched his face do something complicated — the flicker of a man recognizing a shape he'd only ever seen described in documents.

"That's a debris field," he said lightly. "From a crash."

"Rockfall."

"Elodie." He said her name the way he said everything, warm and unhurried, and then ruined it by adding, "You've known since the pass closed, haven't you. This is where we were always going."

The wind came down off the col behind them, carrying grit and the smell of deep cold. Brase stood ten paces back, saying nothing, doing what he always did, which was stand exactly where he could see everyone's hands. Marta leaned on her pole, gray-faced but upright; whatever was wrong with her heart, she carried it the way she carried everything, privately and without complaint.

"We're making camp below the spur," Elodie said. "We go up tomorrow, first light, before the sun softens the slope. Nobody goes ahead. Nobody wanders." She met Corvin's eyes. "And nobody touches anything until I say."

"Touch anything," Corvin repeated softly. "That's an interesting instruction for a rockfall."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a serac wall, and Elodie did not sleep.

She sat up through the blue hours with the flour sack open on her knees, studying Lior's marks by the light of a candle stub shielded inside her parka. There was more on the map than she'd let herself see before. A second set of annotations, older, in different ink — distances, bearings, a note in a language she didn't recognize, written in a script that curved like water. And beneath the X, pressed so hard the sack had nearly torn: three words in Lior's hand.

*Tell them true.*

She'd assumed it meant tell the truth. Now, with the spur a black presence above their camp, she wondered if it meant something else entirely. Tell *them*. Whoever they were. Whoever Lior had been.

Dawn came up the color of pewter. She woke them one by one, roped them in pairs — Brase with Tam, because the boy trusted him and trust was worth more than skill on a slope like this — and started up the scar.

The climb itself was nothing. That was the terrible thing, she thought later. After the pass, after the storm, after the ropes someone had cut and the nights of short rations and shorter sleep, the final approach to the wreck was a walk. Forty minutes of moderate snow over stable ice, the spur shielding them from wind, the sun rising fat and orange behind them and throwing their shadows huge across the slope ahead — five shadows stretching toward whatever waited at the top.

Elodie went first. At the crest of the scar, where the ice smoothed into a shallow basin cupped against the rock, she stopped so abruptly that Tam walked into her back.

"Oh," said Marta, behind her. Just that. Just the smallest sound a person can make.

The plane lay in the basin like a thing that had died mid-struggle.

It was a twin-engine transport, military-built, civilian-painted, its fuselage silver under forty years of frost. It had come down nose-first and slightly left, and the impact had folded the forward third of it downward as if the mountain had pressed a thumb into its spine. One wing swept up and back, tip buried in ice. The other was simply gone, scattered somewhere down the debris field they'd climbed. The tail rose intact against the sky, and painted on it, faded but legible, was a name.

*Kestrel Survey Expedition.* Below it, smaller: *Halvern Mineral Development — Sponsored Flight.*

Corvin made a noise low in his throat. Elodie heard him fumbling under his coat for the camera and said, without turning, "Not yet."

"You can't be serious—"

"Not yet."

The silence that fell over the party was not empty. It was full — of breath, of the creak of settling ice, of the particular hush that hangs over graves. Because that was what this was. Elodie had seen wrecks before; mountain work collected them the way rivers collect drowned branches. But those had been machines that failed, weather that turned, human error written in bent aluminum. This was different. This plane had been found, tended, and left. Someone had stacked stones at the base of the fuselage — dozens of them, a low cairn running the length of the hull, each stone carried here and placed by hand. And wedged into the ice beside the shattered cockpit, upright, was a wooden marker with no writing on it at all. Just a shape carved into the grain: a spiral, worn shallow by weather.

Someone had come here. Someone had grieved here. And then someone had made sure the world forgot.

"The door," Brase said.

His voice was flat as ever, but Elodie caught the thing underneath it, the vibration of a plucked wire. He was staring not at the plane but at the cairn, counting the stones, his lips moving faintly. When he noticed her watching, he shut his mouth and looked away, and for the first time since the trailhead, Brase looked afraid.

The cabin door hung open on a hinge of ice. Of course it did. Whatever Lior had done here — and Elodie was certain now, certain down to her bones, that Lior had been here — he had gone in and come out. She broke the crust around the frame with her axe butt, tested the sill, and climbed inside.

* * *

Cold has a texture indoors that it never has outside. Outside, cold moves; it pushes and pulls and takes your heat by force. Inside the fuselage the cold was still, patient, archival — the cold of a vault. Her headlamp beam swung across rows of seats bolted to a floor tilted fifteen degrees, across frost-furred windows glowing gray-blue, across the instruments of the cockpit crumpled ahead of her like a snapped wrist.

And across the shapes.

There were six of them. Skeletons now, mostly, cloth rotted to lace, but arranged — arranged was the word her mind kept refusing and returning to. Two in the cockpit. Four in the cabin, laid out along the seats side by side, hands composed, someone having folded their coats over them like blankets. On the seat nearest the door, apart from the others, sat a fifth figure — smaller, alone, facing the window. Facing the valley.

Elodie stood in the aisle and could not move.

Her breath came out in small ghosts. The beam of her lamp trembled, and she hated it, hated her own shaking hand, and made herself hold it steady. Professional courtesy, she thought absurdly. These people deserved a steady light.

One of the seated figures wore a surveyor's harness, brass instruments still clipped to it. Another had a woman's boots, good leather, cracked now but once fine. The one by the window — she made herself look, made herself see — had been young. A ring still hung on the bone of one finger, and beside that hand, on the frosted sill, someone had scratched words into the metal with a knife or a nail. The frost had half-filled the grooves, but she brushed it clear with her glove, gently, the way you'd brush hair from a sleeping face.

*WE SAW WHAT THEY DID*

Nothing else. No date, no name. Just that, scratched in a dying person's hand, facing the direction of home.

Behind her, the cabin floor creaked. Corvin had followed her in, and his lamp was sweeping the seats, and he was breathing fast, and for once in his charming life he had nothing to say. Then his beam stopped on the luggage rack above the rear seats, where a steel document case sat wedged between a rotted pack and a coil of frozen rope. Stenciled on its side: *K.S.E. — RECORDS.*

"There," he whispered.

"I see it."

"Elodie, that's—"

"I know what it is."

She reached up and took it down. It weighed less than she expected; grief usually does. The latches were frozen, and she worked them loose with her thumbnail, and opened the case right there in the aisle because her arms would not wait, because some part of her already knew and needed to stop knowing.

Papers. Notebooks. A ledger bound in oilcloth, its pages swollen and buckled with forty years of freeze and thaw. And beneath the ledger, a packet of photographs, rubber-banded, the band disintegrated to powder at her touch.

She lifted the photographs out and fanned them, and the world went very quiet.

They were pictures of a village. Not a wreck, not a crash site — a village, in a green valley, with terraced fields and a stream and round stone houses with smoke rising from them. In the first photograph the smoke was ordinary, kitchen smoke. In the second, the smoke was black and the roofs were fire. In the third, the fire had moved to the treeline and there were figures in the fields, small with distance, some standing, some not. The fourth photograph was closer. She would not describe it, even to herself. She held it and her body understood it before her mind consented to, and something in her chest folded closed like a wing.

On the back of the fourth photograph, in pencil, in a surveyor's neat block capitals: *KORRIM VALLEY — 3RD INST. — HALVERN SURVEY CREW PRESENT — WE COUNTED.*

We counted.

The ledger fell open in her hands to a page of columns. Names. Dozens of names, written in several hands, some careful, some scrawling, added page after page — names with ages beside them, names with lines struck through and annotations in the margin. *Confirmed. Confirmed. Seen alive, north road. Unknown.* It was a census of the dead, compiled by the people in these seats, who had flown over a burning valley and then refused, all of them, all six of them, to keep flying. Who had landed — or tried to — or been brought down, and had spent whatever time they had left not trying to survive but trying to *record*, scratching names into notebooks by candlelight while the cold came for them, so that someone, someday, would count what Halvern wanted uncounted.

The last entry was in a shakier hand. It was not a name. It read: *If you are reading this, you are the messenger now. Take it to the valley. Tell them true.*

Lior's handwriting. Younger, steadier, but Lior's.

Elodie stood in the aisle of the frozen plane with the dead around her and the evidence of a massacre in her hands, and she did not cry, and she did not speak, because she had promised. A gut-shot man freezing to death against her side had made her promise, and the promise had a shape she was only now understanding: *carry it out, tell no one what it found.* She had thought it was a smuggler's caution, a treasure-keeper's paranoia. It wasn't. It was a seal. It was the difference between this becoming a story — Corvin's story, a headline, a sensation, a thing that happened to other people in a magazine — and this remaining what it was. A debt. Owed to specific people, in a specific valley, paid in person, or not paid at all.

"Elodie." Corvin's voice, very quiet. His lamp was on the photographs in her hand. He'd seen. "Christ. Christ almighty. That's—" His jaw worked. "I need to take these pages. You don't understand what this is worth, what this *does* — this ends Halvern, this ends everything, this is the biggest—"

"No."

"No?" He actually laughed, one syllable, brittle. "Elodie, there are skeletons in seats wearing survey harnesses and a ledger of a burned village in your hands, and you're going to say *no*?"

She closed the ledger. Held it against her ribs, where the map lived. Looked at him, and found that her voice, when it came, was level and cold and completely calm, and that this frightened her more than the storm had.

"I made a promise," she said. "To a dying man. That this goes to the people it belongs to, and that until it gets there, no one hears what it says. Not your editor. Not the world. Not yet." She stepped past him toward the door, toward the light. "You can hate that. You can write about it after. But you don't touch it, and you don't transmit it, and you don't say a word on this mountain. Swear it or stay here with them."

For a long moment Corvin just looked at her, and she watched ambition and conscience fight it out behind his eyes like two climbers on a bad rope. Then he exhaled, and his shoulders dropped a centimeter, and he said, "After the valley. Whatever happens — I get the story after."

"After," she agreed. "When they've had it first."

Outside, the others were waiting in the white glare. Marta had gone to the cairn and was laying something on the stones — a glove, hers, fur-lined and good. Tam stood with his hood back, staring at the tail and the faded sponsor's name, and even from twenty meters Elodie could see him mouthing the word *Halvern* like a curse he'd just learned. Brase stood apart, as always, looking not at the plane but at the mountainside beyond it, scanning the slopes with the systematic patience of a man reading a patrol report.

He caught her looking. For a heartbeat neither of them moved. Then Brase turned and walked to the head of the descent line and began checking knots, and Elodie told herself she'd imagined the warning in his face.

She was halfway down the scar, the ledger riding against her ribs, when Tam called out from the rear of the line.

"Elodie." His voice was thin. "There's someone on the ridge. Behind us. They've got — I think they've got rifles."

She turned. High on the western shoulder, five dark specks moved in single file along the skyline, unhurried, descending toward the basin they had just left.

Toward the wreck.
