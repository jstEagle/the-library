## 9. Ledger of the Dead

Marta Voss had buried a husband, a garden, and thirty-one years of marriage in that order of difficulty, and she had learned one thing from all three: grief does not announce itself. It waits in small objects. A coat still on its hook. A ledger, perhaps, frozen inside the belly of a dead airplane.

She was the last to leave the wreck.

The others had drifted out into the grey afternoon — Elodie standing at the fracture line where the fuselage met the ice, saying nothing, her face doing something complicated that Marta chose not to read; Corvin circling with his camera like a man afraid to touch what he'd found; the boy, Tam, pale and mute, pressing himself against the far wall as though the plane might scream again. Brase had simply walked away, downwind, and stood with his back to all of them like a man at a funeral he hadn't been invited to.

Marta stayed because someone had to stay. That had always been her role — at Aldric's deathbed, at the reading of his will, at every board meeting since where men twice her age spoke over her until she made them stop. Someone has to stay in the room when everyone else needs air.

The cabin was tilted maybe fifteen degrees, everything slid toward the starboard wall. Snow had come in through the shattered cockpit in slow annual installments, packing itself around the seats like a glacier's idea of upholstery. Frost furred the windows. The light through them was the color of old bone.

And there, wedged beneath the pilot's seat, half-crushed by decades of shifting cargo, a metal document case. Olive drab. Stenciled letters worn nearly smooth: *DELACROIX EXPEDITION — SURVEY RECORDS.*

Marta knew she shouldn't open it. She opened it anyway. She was sixty-two years old; she had stopped waiting for permission somewhere around the funeral.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were photographs first. She lifted them carefully, the way one lifts things from a coffin.

A village. Terraced fields climbing a valley wall, stone houses with smoke rising from chimneys, drying racks hung with fish or grain — she couldn't tell which. Children in the second photograph, a whole row of them on a low wall, squinting into whatever sun that valley got. A woman with a baby slung on her back, laughing at someone outside the frame.

Then the next photograph, and the next, and Marta's hands went cold in a way the altitude could not explain.

The same village burning. The terraces black. A house collapsed inward, roof beams jutting like ribs. The low wall where the children had sat — empty now, scorched, and beside it shapes that her mind refused for three full seconds to name, and then named anyway.

There were more. She made herself look at all of them. Eleven photographs. She counted, because counting was something to do instead of screaming, and Marta Voss did not scream. Not in front of the children in the pictures. Not in front of anyone.

Under the photographs, a ledger. Leather-bound, water-stained, swollen so the cover no longer closed. The expedition's survey record, presumably — mineral assays, coordinates, the dry arithmetic that had brought these people here and killed them here.

It was not that.

It was a list of names.

*Vessel: Halvern Survey Flight 7. Manifest discrepancy noted at Kessarin airstrip.* And then, in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the following pages, page after page after page:

*Ayalen, headman. Wife: Suri. Sons: Daven (12), Ilo (9).*

*Kesh, elder. Granddaughters: Mina, Tessa.*

*Ondra, weaver. Daughter: Pell.*

On and on. Whole families, entered household by household, with columns at the right edge of each page that Marta did not understand at first — dates, initials, notations in a shorthand she almost recognized —

And then she did recognize it.

Her breath left her body slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes in winter.

The shorthand was Aldric's. Or rather, it was the shorthand of Voss & Halvern correspondence, the internal cipher their clerks used, the one Aldric had taught her to read so she could help him with his letters during his last illness, when his eyes were failing and he'd dictate and she'd transcribe. Thirty years of business letters. Invoices. Contracts. Complaints from village councils about water rights. Letters she had typed herself, some of them, on the good typewriter in the study, while Aldric paced behind her with his pipe unlit because the doctor said.

The column headers resolved themselves: *date of contact. disposition. compensation authorized.*

Compensation.

She turned pages with fingers that no longer felt like hers. The names kept coming. Two hundred and six entries — she counted those too, because counting was all she had — and beside many of them, in that clerk's cipher, the same recurring notation. A symbol she knew. Three strokes and a crossbar. The mark Aldric's senior partners used to indicate a matter *closed to satisfaction.*

Closed to satisfaction. Two hundred and six households. Burned villages in photographs. Compensation authorized.

She thought: *this is not possible.*

She thought: *I typed some of these letters.*

She thought: *Aldric.*

---

Here is what Marta knew about her husband's business, in the way that wives of a certain generation knew such things: completely and not at all. She knew Voss & Halvern pulled ore out of mountains on three continents. She knew Aldric came home from inspection trips thinner and quieter each year, and that in his final decade he drank more than he admitted and slept less than he pretended. She knew he kept a locked drawer in his desk that she had never once opened, because theirs had been a marriage built on the elegant architecture of mutually agreed blind spots.

She knew he died holding her hand and saying her name, and that his last full sentence before that had been, "Some accounts can't be settled, Marta. Don't let them tell you otherwise." She had assumed it was morphine talking. She had chosen to assume it was morphine talking. There is a word for that choice and the word is mercy, and she had extended it to herself for eleven years.

Now she sat in the frozen gut of an airplane with two hundred and six dead families in her lap, and the mercy curdled.

Because she remembered. God help her, she remembered the letters. Autumn of a specific year — she could place it by the wallpaper they'd ordered that season, gold damask, hideously expensive — a run of correspondence marked urgent, from a regional office, regarding "the Kessarin situation." She had asked Aldric what the Kessarin situation was. He had looked at her across the study with an expression she had spent eleven years misfiling under *tiredness*, and he had said, "Mining matters. You don't want the details."

And she had believed him. Because believing him was easier than the alternative, and because she loved him, and because love, in her experience, was mostly the practice of not asking.

The cold crept up through her boots and into her knees. Somewhere outside, wind moved across the icefield with a sound like a long exhalation. Inside the wreck, Marta Voss held a ledger full of the dead and understood, with terrible clarity, that she was the only living person who could read certain parts of it — the cipher margins, the authorization codes, the routing marks that tied this burned valley to a specific office, a specific fiscal quarter, a specific signature.

Not Aldric's signature. She checked, twice, three times, hunting for it the way you hunt a wound for the source of pain. His initials appeared only twice in two hundred and six entries, both times in the *compensation authorized* column, both times followed by the closed-to-satisfaction mark.

Two times. Out of two hundred and six. Some cold, reasonable corner of her mind pointed out that this was not a massacre authored by her husband. It was a massacre *processed* by her husband's company, in which her husband had signed off on payments to silence — what? Survivors? Witnesses? Whose silence had cost how much?

But the other corner of her mind, the one that had sat with him while he died, heard his voice again: *Don't let them tell you otherwise.* He had known. He had known and he had paid and he had carried it home in his locked drawer and drunk himself quiet around it, and she had dusted that drawer every week for thirty years.

Marta discovered she was crying only when a tear froze against her knuckle. She laughed — a short, awful sound, alone in a graveyard of steel — wiped her face with a glove already stiff with frost, and did what she had done at every funeral of her long life.

She composed herself, and she got to work.

She photographed nothing — Corvin had the camera. Instead she memorized. Page markers, entry numbers, the routing codes. If this ledger was going home to anyone, Marta intended to be able to testify to exactly what it contained, in the language of the people who had written the margins. Let them try to call her a hysterical widow. She had sat through forty years of board meetings. She knew the grammar of ledgers better than any man alive who'd never had to type one.

She rewrapped the oilcloth, replaced the case beneath the seat — then stopped, took it back out, and tucked it inside her own pack instead. Let Elodie say what she liked about promises and order and who carried what. Marta had carried a husband's secrets for thirty years without knowing it. She would carry these knowingly or die on this mountain, and given the current weather forecast, the second option was not entirely out of the question.

Outside, the light was failing. She climbed down through the torn fuselage door onto the ice, and the wind hit her like a hand, and she saw Elodie apart from the others, checking anchors on the fixed line with mechanical precision, and Marta thought: *she knows. Whatever that promise was that Lior extracted from her — she knows what's in this ledger, and she has known since she walked into that cabin, and she said nothing.*

Which meant either the guide was party to the crime, or the guide was protecting something. Marta had spent a lifetime distinguishing between those two categories of silence. She was very good at it.

"Miss Delacroix," she called, pitching her voice under the wind. "A word. Alone."

Elodie looked up. Even at twenty meters, even in the flat dying light, Marta saw the flicker of it — the guilt, or the dread, or whatever lived behind that woman's eyes since the wreck. Good. Guilt was leverage. Dread was leverage. Marta had negotiated with men full of both.

They walked fifty paces from camp, to a rib of blue ice that broke the wind. Marta set her feet wide, the stance Aldric had teased her about on their one and only glacier holiday, and opened her pack, and drew out the ledger.

"I believe this belongs to me," she said. "Or rather — it belonged to my husband's company. Which makes me, as far as I can determine, the nearest thing to family it has."

Elodie went very still. Stillness, Marta noted, of a person recalculating everything. "You opened it."

"I did."

"You were told—"

"I was told nothing," Marta said, "which is precisely the problem. Nobody tells me anything. Not my husband for thirty years, not you since Lior's map, not Mr. Corvin with his camera and his careful questions. So I have taken the liberty of telling myself." She held up the ledger between them, the swollen cover dark with old water. "Two hundred and six entries, Miss Delacroix. Households. Headmen, elders, granddaughters. And in the margins, a cipher I happen to read fluently, because I typed my husband's correspondence for three decades. Do you know what the margins say?"

Elodie's jaw tightened. "No."

"Liar," Marta said, without heat. "You went white as the snowpack when you saw those photographs, and you've been silent as the grave ever since, and I have watched enough guilty men in boardrooms to know the difference between a person with nothing to say and a person keeping a promise. Lior made you promise something. Silence, I'd wager. Tell no one what it found." She watched the guide's composure crack, just at the edges, just enough. "I'm not going to pry your promise out of you. I'm going to tell you what I found, and then you're going to decide whether your promise was made to a dead man's peace or to the truth."

The wind filled the pause. Below them, Tam's voice rose faintly, arguing with Corvin about watch rotations — ordinary human noise, absurdly domestic, here at the top of the world.

"The village in those photographs," Marta said, and her voice did not shake, because she would not permit it to, "is Kessarin Valley. I know because I filed the correspondence. Twenty-two years ago — I can give you the quarter, if it matters — Voss & Halvern's regional office reported a 'situation' involving the local population and the survey claims. Land disputes, water rights, the usual. What the usual became, Miss Delacroix, is in these photographs. What it cost is in this ledger. Two hundred and six households, entered by name, with compensation authorized in the margins — hush money, blood money, whatever we're calling it today — and closed to satisfaction." Her breath fogged and tore away. "My husband's initials are on two of those authorizations. I have spent eleven years believing Aldric Voss was a hard man with a good heart. I am now prepared to learn he was something else, and I will not flinch from it, because flinching is how this happened in the first place. Everyone flinched. Everyone agreed not to look. Including me."

"Marta." Elodie's voice, when it came, was rough. "Stop."

"No."

"*Listen.*" The guide stepped closer, and up close Marta could see the exhaustion carved into her, days of it, years of it. "You think you're the first person to stand on this ice holding that book and wanting answers? Lior carried it for thirty years. He crawled half a mountain to put it in my hands, and the last thing he did with his breath was make me swear to bring it home and tell *no one*. Not the authorities. Not the press. Not—" her eyes cut briefly toward camp, toward Corvin, "—not anyone. Because the last time this ledger moved, everyone carrying it died, and the company that killed them is still operating, still powerful, and still watching these passes. You want to know why I said nothing? Because I gave my word to a dying man. That's the whole of it. That's all there is."

"And the descendants?" Marta asked quietly. "Does your promise include them?"

Something moved through Elodie's face then — fast, involuntary, like a fish under ice. Marta had spent a lifetime reading faces across negotiation tables, and she knew a hit when she landed one.

"There are descendants," Marta said. It wasn't a question. "You know there are. Lior told you where it goes — not to a court, not to a newspaper. Home. To the valley. To whoever's left." She hugged the ledger to her chest, feeling its weight settle against her sternum like a second heart. "Good. Then our promises may not be enemies after all. But hear me, Miss Delacroix, and hear me clearly, because I am too old and too high up and too grief-struck to play games: I am in this now. Not as a client. Not as a passenger. My husband's company burned a village off the face of the earth and my marriage helped pay for the matches, and I will carry this book to its people if I have to crawl the way Lior crawled. You do not get to manage me. You do not get to decide what I can bear. I buried a man I loved and I am only now learning what I buried him *with*, and the only thing worse than that knowledge would be dying on this ice having done nothing with it."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The storm-light thickened in the west, bruise-colored, and somewhere above the pass the wind changed its note — a higher, thinner sound that Marta didn't recognize but saw Elodie recognize, the guide's head lifting toward it like a deer's, her whole body going taut.

"That's turning," Elodie said. Almost to herself. "That's turning early." She looked back at Marta, and something in her expression shifted — the wariness giving way, grudgingly, to something that might, in a different life, have been respect. "Keep it in your pack. Keep it dry. And Marta — if I tell you to run, you run, and you don't argue, and you don't negotiate. Agreed?"

"I don't negotiate with people who lie to me," Marta said. "You've stopped lying. So yes. Agreed."

They turned back toward camp, and that was when they both saw it.

Corvin stood at the edge of the tents, not moving, not pretending anymore to fiddle with gear — just watching them, camera hanging forgotten at his side, his face lit by the last flat light with an expression Marta recognized from a hundred boardrooms: a man who has just realized the story has changed shape in his hands.

And beyond him, at the perimeter of camp, Brase was crouched over the snow where their tracks came up from the wreck — crouched the way a man crouches over something that frightens him.

Tracks, Marta realized. Other than their own.

Fresh ones.
