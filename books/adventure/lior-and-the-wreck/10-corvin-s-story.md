## 10. Corvin's Story

The storm had passed in the night, leaving the world scoured and blue-white, and Corvin woke to find Elodie sitting on his pack.

Not beside it. On it. Her boots planted on either side of his bedroll, her hands wrapped around a tin cup of snowmelt tea she had no intention of offering him, her face wearing the particular patience of a woman who had already decided how this conversation would go and was merely waiting for him to arrive at it.

"Good morning," he said, because he was a professional, and professionals reached for words the way drowning men reached for driftwood.

"Walk with me."

"I haven't had—"

"You'll want your legs under you for this." She stood, took her boot off his pack with a small deliberate pressure that told him exactly what she thought of his notes — *his notes*, which were supposed to be hidden in the false bottom of his camera case, wrapped in oilcloth, invisible — and walked out onto the shelf of rock above the tents without looking back to see if he followed.

He followed. Of course he followed. He'd been following her since Verren Pass, since the tavern where she'd stood with her back to the wall and let a room full of men remake her into whatever story suited them, and he had watched her do it with the flat, unblinking calm of someone who had heard it all before and found none of it worth dying over. He'd written three pages about her that night by candlelight. *The blacklisted guide. The disaster she may or may not have caused.* Good copy. Clean villainy. The kind of narrative that sold.

She stopped at the edge of the shelf, where the glacier fell away below them in a long blue curve, and handed him the oilcloth packet without turning around.

"Tell me what these are."

His own handwriting looked up at him from the top page. *Delacroix expedition, seven years ago. Six clients. Four confirmed dead. Guide survived. Company of record: Halvern Mining (logistics contract). Insurance settlement sealed. Guide's certification revoked by Northern Alpine Guild, citing 'gross negligence in route assessment.' Source: Guild tribunal minutes, partial. Source: Halvern internal memo, quoted secondhand, unverifiable.*

Below that, in his tighter hand, the list he'd been building for two years: every person who had ever spoken against Elodie Delacroix in print, in testimony, in the whispered economy of mountain towns. And beside each name, the thing he'd only recently allowed himself to see — the funding trail behind each voice. A guild official who'd retired to a Halvern pension. A newspaper editor whose paper ran Halvern advertisements. A rescue coordinator who'd been on Halvern's retainer for emergency response contracts. Eleven names. Eleven threads. All of them running back to the same spool.

"That's mine," Corvin said. It came out smaller than he wanted.

"It is." She finally turned. Her eyes were the color of old ice, and there was no anger in them, which was worse. Anger he could work with. "I went through your camera case last night while you slept. I'm telling you so you know I don't lie to you, even when you've lied to me. Now you're going to tell me why a journalist who writes travel pieces for provincial papers has been carrying a two-year investigation into my ruin across a pass he paid me to guide him over."

The wind came up the valley and pushed at them both, patient as an interrogator.

*Because,* he almost said, *you're the story. You've always been the story.* But that was the version he'd rehearsed, the version with himself as the dogged truth-teller, and standing here with her reading his own suspicions off his own pages, he understood that the rehearsal was over.

"Sit down, Ms. Delacroix," he said. "It's a longer story than yours, and less flattering."

*

They sat with their backs against a boulder that broke the wind, and he told it the way he'd promised himself he never would: from the beginning, with the money in it.

"There's a man named Aldous Veyn," he said. "You won't know the name. Nobody knows the name. That's rather the point of Aldous Veyn. He's not an executive — executives have titles and photographs and resignations when things go wrong. He's what the industry calls a reputation consultant, which means he finds stories before they become stories and buries them. I met him four years ago, when I was hungry and good and stupid enough to think being good mattered. He liked my work. He said I had a nose for what people didn't want printed, and he was right, and I hated that he was right, and I took his retainer anyway."

"What did he bury?"

"At first? Small things. A tailings pond leaking into a salmon river — we got it reframed as a permitting dispute. A foreman who'd falsified safety inspections before a collapse — we got the dead man's family a settlement and a confidentiality agreement, and the settlement bought more silence than the collapse had bought grief. You learn the arithmetic of it. Grief is loud for a year. Money is quiet forever." He turned his cup in his hands. "And then, two years ago, Veyn called me in and put a folder on the table, and the folder had your name on it."

Elodie said nothing. Below them, a serac calved somewhere deep in the glacier with a sound like a door closing in another house.

"He didn't ask me to attack you. That's the first thing you should understand — the elegant thing, the thing that took me a year to see. He asked me to *prepare*. There was going to be a survivor account, someday. Someone from the Delacroix expedition, or a rescuer, or a widow who wouldn't stay bought. When it surfaced, it would need to be discredited within a news cycle, before it could attach itself to anything. So I was to build the counter-narrative in advance. Find the inconsistencies in the official record — the ones that made *you* look bad, specifically. Establish you as reckless, ambitious, financially desperate. Get the blacklist language into circulation so thoroughly that when the real story came out, anyone telling it would sound like they were defending you, and everyone knew you were indefensible."

"And you did it."

"I did it." His voice cracked on the second word and he let it. "I wrote the piece that ran in the *Meridian Courier*. 'The Guide Who Led Them Into the Storm.' I interviewed three of the families — I told them I was writing a memorial profile. Two of them cried on the record. I used their grief as ballast for a structure Veyn had already drawn. I didn't invent a single fact, Ms. Delacroix. That was the trick of it, the thing I told myself at three in the morning. Every fact was true. I just chose which truths got to stand next to each other, and the arrangement killed you as surely as any lie would have."

"The guild tribunal," she said quietly. "My route assessment. They cited a report I'd never seen."

"Veyn wrote it. Or had it written. The guild reviewed it, and the guild's chair of standards was a man named Oskar Rehn, who now lives very comfortably outside Basel." He watched her absorb that — watched the seven years of it land, the tribunals and the town squares and the doors that closed, all of it traced back along a line she'd never been permitted to see. "You weren't punished for the disaster, Elodie. You were punished for being the one witness who might someday matter."

"Why?" The word came out of her like something pried loose. "A plane goes down in the Verren high country. An expedition dies in a storm. Why does a mining company spend years and money making sure the guide stays ruined? What is there to protect?"

And there it was. The question he'd been circling for two years, the question that had stopped being professional somewhere around the rope cache and started being survival. He looked down the valley, toward the detour Lior's map had forced them onto, toward whatever waited at the end of it, and made the choice he'd been walking toward since Verren Pass.

"Because of what the plane was carrying," he said. "And because I don't think it was carrying it where Halvern says it was."

*

He laid it out for her the way he'd laid it out in his head a hundred times, the facts arranged honestly this time, which felt like taking off a coat he'd forgotten he was wearing.

"The Delacroix expedition wasn't a climbing expedition. Not really. Six clients, yes — but look at the manifest I pulled from the port authority. A surveyor. A cartographer. Two men listed as 'geological consultants' whose names appear nowhere in any geological registry. A photographer whose equipment manifest included aerial survey cameras. And a radio operator whose license was issued by Halvern's own communications division." He ticked them off on his fingers. "That's not a party of climbers. That's a reconnaissance flight dressed as one, flying a route over terrain Halvern had been denied exploration rights to twice. The northern valleys. Indigenous land. Protected by treaty since before either of us was born."

"Halvern's been trying to get into those valleys for twenty years," Elodie said slowly. "Everyone knows that. The concessions keep failing."

"They kept failing because the surveys kept coming back honest. So eventually they stopped sending surveys and started sending an expedition that could look at the ground itself and come back with a report only Halvern would read." He paused. "The plane went down in weather that no pilot should have flown into. Which means either the pilot was ordered up, or the pilot was flying home in a hurry — carrying something he'd been told to destroy and couldn't make himself destroy."

"You think the survivors — if there were survivors—"

"I think," Corvin said, "that Veyn's file on you had a gap in it. One page missing. I saw the tab of it, once, when he wasn't looking. The label said *Verren cargo recovery — status unresolved*. Unresolved. Seven years later. Companies like Halvern don't leave things unresolved unless resolving them means finding something they can't afford found." He turned to face her fully. "And then a dying prospector walks into your camp with a map to a wreck nobody's supposed to know exists, and asks you to carry out a ledger and tell no one what it found. And then our rope cache gets cut by someone who isn't in this party, and we get funneled onto a detour that leads exactly where that map leads. Elodie — we aren't lost. We're being *herded*. Someone wants us to find that wreck, or wants to find it using us, and either way the story I was hired to bury and the story you were ruined to silence are the same story, and it's still alive down there in the ice."

For a long moment she didn't speak. The wind moved. Somewhere above them, a raven rode the thermal in slow circles, black against the white, watching the way ravens watch — for what falls.

"When you looked at me in that tavern," she said at last, "in Verren Pass. When you asked me questions about the disaster. What were you hoping I'd say?"

He considered lying, decided he'd done enough of it for one lifetime, and said: "I was hoping you'd be guilty. Guilt was the story I'd built. If you'd been guilty, everything fit, and I could stop digging, and Veyn would keep paying, and I could go on being a man who arranged true facts into comfortable shapes." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Then I watched you take a job leading strangers over a pass that terrifies you, for money you clearly need, and you checked every knot twice and rationed food fairly and carried the widow's pack when she thought no one saw. And guilty people don't check knots twice, Elodie. Guilty people stop looking at the mountain. You never stopped looking at it. Like you were waiting for it to tell you what actually happened."

"It was my storm," she said. "Whatever else is true — I led them into it. I chose the schedule over the sky. That part of your story stands."

"I know." He met her eyes. "And when we get down off this mountain, if we get down, I'll print that too. Every word of it. That's the deal I'm offering you, and I know what it's worth after what I've done, which is nothing, which is why I'm offering it anyway. Full truth. Yours and theirs and mine. Veyn's machinery, Halvern's money, the blacklist, the buried report — all of it, in one piece, in a paper Veyn doesn't own. I know a publisher in the capital. Old woman, owns her presses outright, has been waiting two years for me to bring her something worth ruining myself over." He held out his hand, palm open, the way you offer a hand across a crevasse. "I was sent to be the second knife, Elodie. I'd rather be the first witness."

She looked at his hand for a long time. Then past it, down the valley, toward the white silence where the wreck waited.

"If you betray this," she said, "there's no tribunal this time. No guild. Just me, and this mountain, and a very long way down."

"Understood."

"And Corvin — the ledger. Whatever Lior made me promise, whatever's in it—" Her jaw tightened. "There's a boy in that camp down there who's sixteen and lied about his age to be here, and a widow who lost her husband, and a man who moves like a soldier and won't say why. If the truth costs them, I decide what it costs. Not you. Not your publisher. Me."

"Agreed," he said, and meant it, and was surprised by how much he meant it.

She took his hand. Her grip was cold and hard and certain, and something in his chest that had been clenched for two years — some muscle he hadn't known he was using, holding the shape of the lie — finally let go.

They walked back to camp together, and he was composing the first honest paragraph of his life when they crested the rise and saw Brase standing at the edge of camp, facing downslope, utterly still.

Following his gaze, Corvin saw them too: tracks in the fresh snow below the shelf. Boot prints. Six sets, maybe eight, moving in disciplined single file.

Coming up the same detour. Coming, like them, toward the wreck.

Brase turned, and his face was gray as old ice, and he said only one word:

"Halvern."
