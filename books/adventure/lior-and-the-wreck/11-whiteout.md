## 11. Whiteout

The storm came down on them like a verdict.

Elodie had watched it build all afternoon — a gray wall stacking itself against the western peaks, swallowing the ridgelines one by one, the light going strange and yellow-green beneath it, the color of old bruises. She had called the halt early, against Corvin's grumbling and Marta's anxious questions about schedule, because she knew what that wall meant. Twenty years in the high country had taught her to read weather the way other people read faces, and this face said *kill*.

They were still a half-day short of the col when it hit. Not enough time to reach shelter, not enough time to descend. The best she could do was the crevasse field below the serac band — a shattered glacier where the ice had split into blue chasms deep enough to swallow a house. One of them, broad-shouldered with an overhanging lip on the windward side, would do.

"Rope up. Tight intervals. Nobody moves without my say."

The wind arrived before they finished rigging, a flat slap that knocked Tam sideways into the snow. Then the world simply erased itself. Sky became snow became sky. Elodie clipped herself to the fixed line and hauled the party forward by feel alone, counting paces, calling turns into a wind that tore her voice apart before it reached the second rope team. Somewhere behind her, Brase moved steady as ballast. Somewhere ahead, the boy was crying and pretending he wasn't.

She found the crevasse by falling into its wind shadow — a sudden pocket of dead air at the lip, like stepping through a doorway. Below, the chasm dropped forty feet to a floor of packed drift, curved and blue and lit by a ghostly glow filtering through the snow bridge above.

"Climb down," she ordered. "All of you. Now."

They went down the fixed line one at a time, packs first, then bodies, into the cold blue throat of the mountain. By the time the last of them — Brase, who checked the anchor twice before trusting his weight to it — reached the floor, the storm had fully arrived overhead. The snow bridge groaned like something alive. Fine spindrift poured through cracks in the ceiling in silver threads, accumulating in slow dunes around their boots.

It was, Elodie thought, looking around at the four of them — Corvin pale and jaw-set, Marta clutching her coat closed, Tam wide-eyed and trying to be brave about it, Brase already moving with quiet purpose to level a spot for shelter — the worst room she'd ever stood in. And for three days, it was going to be home.

* * *

The first day was almost manageable.

They dug in. Brase proved unexpectedly good at it, cutting blocks from the drift with the economy of a man who'd done hard physical labor his whole life, and together he and Elodie raised a windwall across the crevasse's narrow end and hollowed a chamber into the side wall, big enough for five people to sit shoulder to shoulder out of the spindrift. They rigged the stove in the middle, hung the tarp overhead to catch melt, and settled in to wait.

Elodie inventoried the food with the stove's small flame lighting her face from below. The number she arrived at made her stomach tighten.

"We have rations for two days," she announced. "Maybe two and a half if we're careful. If the storm holds longer than that, we have a problem."

"How long will it hold?" Marta asked.

"I don't know." Honesty was the only currency she had left to spend. "These systems can sit on a pass for a week."

"A week?" Tam's voice cracked upward. "We can't— we don't have—"

"The boy's right to be scared," Elodie said. "But scared is fine. Scared keeps you sharp. What kills you up here isn't being afraid; it's doing something stupid because you couldn't stand being afraid anymore." She looked each of them in the eye in turn. "So here are the rules. We eat twice a day, small portions. We drink melted snow even when it makes you gag. We take turns sleeping close to the stove. And nobody goes up the line alone. Nobody. Not for anything."

Corvin laughed — a short, ugly sound. "And what exactly happens if someone does?"

"They die," Elodie said flatly. "Or worse, they get someone else killed coming after them. That's how it works on glaciers. That's how it worked on—" She stopped herself.

The silence that followed had weight. Everyone in the crevasse knew what she'd almost said. Everyone had read the articles, heard the rumors, formed their private picture of Elodie Delacroix, the guide who walked clients into a whiteout and led six of them off a mountain feet-first.

"That's how it worked on your last expedition," Corvin finished for her, softly. It wasn't cruelty, exactly. It was curiosity wearing cruelty's clothes. He was watching her the way he'd been watching her since the trailhead, and she'd known for days what he was: a journalist didn't sign onto a guided crossing of Verren Pass out of wanderlust. "Isn't that right, Delacroix? Six dead. Guide survived."

"Corvin." Marta's voice, sharp with warning.

"No, I think we should talk about it." He hadn't raised his voice. That was the worst part — the reasonable tone, the interviewer's patience. "Three days in a hole in the ice with nothing to do but talk. Seems as good a time as any. Don't you think?"

Elodie met his eyes across the stove flame. There was something in his face beyond ambition — a genuine hunger, the look of a man who'd chased a story so long it had started chasing him back.

"You want to know what happened," she said quietly, "ask me again when we're off this mountain. Because right now, every ounce of energy in this crevasse needs to go toward keeping us alive, and a fight doesn't keep anyone warm."

He held her gaze a moment longer, then shrugged and looked away. But she felt the question settle over the group like another layer of snow — soft, silent, accumulating.

* * *

The second day, the storm found their weaknesses.

It started small. Tam complained of a headache, then nausea — altitude, dehydration, the thin air of the crevasse. Marta hovered over him with the fussy tenderness of a woman who had lost someone young and couldn't bear to lose anyone else. Elodie forced water into him, made him sip, monitored his pulse with two fingers on his wrist until she'd memorized its rhythm.

"He'll be all right," she told Marta. "He needs to drink more than he wants to and eat less than he thinks he should. Same as the rest of us."

"And you?" Marta asked. Her eyes were shrewd despite her exhaustion. "What do you need, Miss Delacroix?"

"Sleep," Elodie admitted. "I haven't slept properly since Lior died."

She hadn't meant to say the name aloud. It slipped out, raw, and she saw Marta register it — *Lior*, the prospector no one else in the party had ever heard mentioned — and file it away with the same quiet competence she applied to everything.

Marta said nothing. But that night, while the others slept in shifts, she moved beside Elodie at the edge of the firelight and spoke low, under the sound of the storm.

"My husband climbed mountains," she said. "Before his heart failed. He used to tell me that the mountains don't test your character. They reveal it. Whatever you brought up with you, that's what comes down." She paused. "Whatever it is you're carrying, Miss Delacroix — whatever name that man said, whatever promise — you don't have to carry it alone up here. That's all I wanted to say."

Elodie stared into the stove flame for a long time. When she finally answered, her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"What if what I'm carrying got people killed?"

"Then it's heavy," Marta said simply, "and you need help carrying it. That's all."

* * *

The third day, the rations ran to crumbs and the tempers ran to nothing.

It began with Corvin accusing Brase of hoarding. There was no evidence — there never is, in a crevasse — but fear doesn't require evidence, only a target. Brase took the accusation with a stony stillness that somehow made it worse, saying nothing, meeting Corvin's eyes with a flat gray stare until the journalist's voice cracked and faltered and finally died into embarrassed silence.

Then Tam snapped.

It happened fast, the way these things do. One moment the boy was sitting against the ice wall picking at the frayed cuff of his glove; the next he was on his feet, shouting — at Corvin, at the storm, at the ceiling of ice that groaned and sifted snow down on them like a taunt.

"I can't do this! Three days in a hole! I'm going up. I'm going up and I'm walking out of here—"

"Sit down, Tam."

"—you can't stop me, none of you can stop me, I'd rather die moving than sitting in this—"

"Tam." Elodie rose slowly, hands open and visible, the way you approach a spooked animal on a ledge. "Look at me. Just look at me."

But the boy wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the fixed line, at the climb back up into the screaming white, and Elodie saw with sick clarity what was happening behind his eyes: the claustrophobia, the hypoxia, the adolescent certainty that anything — *anything* — was better than one more minute in this blue grave. She'd seen it before. On the Delacroix expedition, a client named Oskar Vey had worn that exact expression in the hour before he unclipped from the rope and walked into the whiteout, and they had found what was left of him three days later, two hundred meters downslope.

Not this boy. Not again.

"Grab him," she said, and Brase moved.

The ex-miner crossed the crevasse floor in three strides and caught Tam around the chest just as the boy lunged for the line. Tam fought — wild, sobbing, surprisingly strong — and for ten horrible seconds the two of them staggered across the ice while Marta screamed and Corvin scrambled to help and Elodie shouted over all of it: *"You clip that line and the wind takes you off the lip and we cannot come after you, do you hear me, there is no coming after you—"*

Brase pinned the boy gently against the ice wall, one forearm across his chest, and held him there, murmuring something too low for anyone else to hear. Slowly, by degrees, the fight went out of Tam. He sagged against the miner's grip, then into it, shaking, his face buried in Brase's shoulder.

"I want to go home," the boy whispered. "I just want to go home."

"I know," Brase said. It was the longest sentence Elodie had heard him speak since the trailhead.

* * *

Afterward, no one slept.

They sat in a ring around the dying stove, drained past speech, the storm grinding overhead like a millstone. Tam had been dosed with the last of the tea and tucked between Marta and Brase, asleep at last from pure exhaustion. The silence stretched until Corvin broke it — quietly, without performance, all the interviewer's polish burned away.

"I wasn't hired to write a story," he said, to the flame rather than to any of them. "I was hired to kill one. Before it could be written."

Elodie turned her head. In the guttering light, his face looked older than it had at the trailhead, younger than it had an hour ago — stripped, like everything else in this crevasse, down to the load-bearing structure.

"There's a plane in these mountains," he went on. "Went down twenty-two years ago carrying documents. Photographs. A ledger. Evidence of something Halvern Mining did in a valley that doesn't appear on any map. My employers wanted any account of it discredited before it surfaced. And there was a guide, twenty-two years ago, who led an expedition through a storm because Halvern's schedule demanded it — and when it went wrong, when six people died, the machinery that paid me made sure she took the whole weight of it. Made sure no company, no town, no client list would touch her name again." He looked up, finally, and met Elodie's eyes. "That machinery put you on this mountain, Delacroix. You weren't blacklisted. You were buried."

The crevasse was very quiet. Above them, the storm howled on, indifferent.

"Why are you telling us this now?" Marta asked softly.

"Because the boy nearly walked into a hole today over a few days of hunger," Corvin said, "and I've spent three days watching all of you decide whether to trust each other, and I'm tired. Tired of holding the story like a knife. Tired of—" He stopped, exhaled. "Because survival strips you down to what's true. And the truth is I came up here to use all of you. And somewhere on the glacier that stopped being all I want."

Elodie sat with it. The betrayal she'd suspected since the trailhead, confirmed at last — and yet it landed not as a blow but as a strange, exhausted relief. At least the shape of the trap was visible now. At least she knew which walls were walls.

Outside, the wind changed.

It was subtle — a shift in pitch, the groan of the snow bridge easing, the spindrift through the ceiling cracks thinning from threads to occasional grains. Elodie lifted her head, listening the way a sailor listens to rigging. Ten minutes. Twenty. The pressure in her ears changed.

"It's breaking," she said. "The storm's breaking."

No one cheered. They were too far past cheering. But Brase began rolling the rope without being asked, and Marta woke Tam gently, and Corvin started breaking camp with the efficiency of a man trying to outrun his own confession.

Elodie was last up the fixed line, and when she pulled herself over the crevasse lip into the morning, the world had been remade — sculpted, gleaming, lethal. The storm had scoured the slopes down to blue ice and rebuilt the skyline in cornices that overhung the route like drawn blades. And there, far below and to the east, stitched across the fresh snow of the icefield in a neat diagonal line—

Tracks.

Boots. Many boots. Fresh, unweathered, cutting straight toward the col.

Someone else had climbed through the storm's edge. Someone else was on the mountain, and they were ahead of them.

Behind her, she heard Brase come up over the lip, follow her gaze, and go very still.

"Elodie," he said quietly. "Those aren't ours."
