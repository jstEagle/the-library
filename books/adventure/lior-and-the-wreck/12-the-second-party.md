## 12. The Second Party

Brase had spent thirty-one years learning to read snow the way other men read ledgers, and the tracks that crossed the col above their camp told him everything he needed to know in the space of one held breath.

Six sets of boots. Crampon marks, deep and even — men who knew ice but didn't love it, who moved because they were paid to move. A sledge trail behind them, narrow, the kind that carried rope and fuel and things heavier than rope and fuel. And at intervals, small dark punctures where someone had planted a pole and leaned on it while scanning the slope.

Scanning downhill. Scanning toward them.

He crouched at the edge of the moraine with his back against a boulder the size of a chapel, and he did what he had done every morning since the pass closed: he counted his sins and found them still growing.

The wind came off the glacier in long white breaths, carrying the smell of nothing, which was the mountain's mercy. Below him, tucked into a hollow beneath the moraine wall, the others were waking. He could hear the boy — Tam, though nobody had asked the boy's name out loud for two days, as if naming him might cost something — chipping ice into the pot. He could hear Delacroix's voice, low and even, giving the morning its shape: *we move by eight, we're off the icefield before the sun softens the bridges.* He could hear Marta breathing, that wet, careful sound she tried to hide, and Corvin not talking, which from Corvin was louder than shouting.

And somewhere up-slope, closing at perhaps three kilometers a day, six armed men were following Lior's map.

*His* map. The route Brase himself had confirmed for Halvern fourteen months ago, sitting in a warm office in Vellmark with a brandy in his hand, tracing the line with one blunt finger while a man named Ostrevan watched him with eyes like coin slots. *If anyone comes looking for the wreck,* Ostrevan had said, *this is how they'll come. Tell us if you see them.*

Brase had said yes. It had been easy to say yes. It had been eleven thousand crowns easy, paid in quarterly installments to an account under his dead brother's name, and it had seemed like the cheapest thing in the world — watching a mountain for ghosts. Nobody came looking for ghosts. That was why they were ghosts.

Then a dying prospector had crawled into Delacroix's camp with a map sewn into his coat, and the ghosts had started walking.

Brase pressed his bare palm flat against the boulder and felt the cold of it climb his arm like water. He thought about the rope cache at the Verren notch, cut clean and coiled away, and about the fact that it hadn't been him. For nine days he had carried that particular guilt like a stone in his boot, certain it was his own payment come due — that Halvern had decided a watcher who'd gone soft was worth more dead or stranded than employed. Then last night, on watch, he'd seen the light. A single point of green far up on the shoulder of the Kestrel Tooth, moving in the pattern of a signal lamp, patient as a lighthouse. Not his people's color. Not any survey team's color.

Someone else was on this mountain. Someone who wanted them exactly where they were going, or exactly where they'd already been.

He should have said something then. He was saying something now, in the only way left to him: he memorized the tracks, the spacing of the boots, the depth of the sledge trail, the direction of the wind, and he turned back down toward camp to decide, finally, after thirty-one years and eleven thousand crowns and one burned village he had never seen but had helped bury, whose side he was standing on when the shooting started.

* * *

They looked up when he came into the hollow, all four of them, and Brase understood suddenly how a man must feel walking into a room full of mirrors that show him worse than he is.

Delacroix first, always first. She sat with her back to the rock, boots on, pack half-laced, a woman built out of decisions. Her eyes went to his face and stayed there, reading him the way he read snow. She'd been doing it since the glacier crossing, he knew. She knew he moved wrong for a laborer, right for something else. He'd watched her decide not to say it, day after day, and he had never decided whether that was mercy or calculation.

Corvin lay propped on his pack with his notebook open on his knee, pen idle, wearing the pleasant blank face he wore when he was working. The journalist's face. Brase had known a dozen men with that face in the mining camps; it was the face of a man cataloguing you for later use.

Marta Voss sat nearest the stove-plate, gray around the mouth, her left side stiff where the fall on the serac field had bruised something deep. She nodded to him — courteous, always courteous, the widow's armor — and went back to mending a strap with numb-fingered patience.

Tam stood at the melt-pot with a ladle frozen mid-stir, watching Brase with the naked, hungry attention of the young, who have not yet learned to pretend they aren't afraid.

"Tracks," Brase said.

The word landed the way stones land. Delacroix was on her feet before it finished.

"How many. Where. How old."

"Six. Crossed the col above us sometime in the night — twelve hours, maybe less. Sledge. Armed." He let that sit. "Moving our line. Our exact line."

"Nobody knows our line," Corvin said, too quickly, too smoothly, and then caught himself, because everyone in the hollow knew exactly who else knew their line, and the silence that followed had Brase's name written all over it in letters of fire.

"I know our line," Brase said. His voice came out flat, the voice he'd used for shift reports, for accident inquiries, for the one time he'd had to tell a widow her husband's number instead of his name. "I've known it since before any of you signed on. I was hired to know it."

Tam set down the ladle very carefully, as if it might go off.

"Halvern," Delacroix said. Not a question.

"Halvern." Brase looked at each of them in turn, because a confession owed eye contact the way a debt owed interest. "Fourteen months ago a man from Halvern's security office found me in Vellmark. Bought me dinners, bought me drinks, bought me — " he made a short, ugly sound that was meant to be a laugh — "eleven thousand crowns over four quarters, to watch the approaches to the southern passes and report anyone asking after the 'sixty-one expedition. Survey work, they called it. Recovery of company property." He swallowed. "I gave them the route assessments. I told them how a party would come if it came. And I have taken their money every quarter since, including the one I drew the week before I joined this expedition."

Corvin's pen had stopped entirely. The boy had gone pale and very still, the way the young go still when the adult world shows them its underside.

"You're their man," Marta said quietly. "You've been theirs the whole time."

"I'm their man," Brase agreed. "I was sent to make sure no one reached the wreck, or that if they reached it, they didn't leave. Those were my instructions, dressed up in softer words." He took a breath that hurt going in. "I never cut your ropes. I want that understood. I've wanted it understood for days and couldn't say it without saying the rest. Someone else is on this mountain — I saw a signal lamp on the Kestrel Tooth two nights past, green light, and green isn't Halvern's recovery code. Somebody wants us funneled. Somebody cut that cache to push us onto Lior's detour, and I don't know who, and that frightens me more than the six men on the col."

"Why?" Tam's voice cracked on it, and he pushed through anyway, chin up, sixteen years old and furious with fear. "Why are you telling us now?"

Brase looked at the boy for a long moment. Because of the photographs, he almost said. Because I saw the village through the frost on that plane window and I have buried enough villages in my life, most of them with paperwork. Because I am fifty-three years old and I have been paid to look away since I was nineteen, and looking away is the only skill I have ever mastered, and I am so tired of being good at it.

"Because the money's spent," he said instead, which was true, and let the rest stay folded inside it where they could see its shape.

Delacroix hadn't moved through any of it. Now she came closer, close enough that he could see the windburn split across her cheekbone, and studied him the way she'd study a cornice before trusting it.

"If you're lying," she said, "if this is a play to walk us into them — "

"Then I'd be walking myself into them," Brase said. "I know what happens to watchers who stop watching. I wrote those protocols. Half of them, anyway." He held her gaze. "I'm not asking you to trust me, guide. I'm asking you to aim me. Point me at what needs doing and I'll do it until I can't."

Something shifted behind her eyes — not forgiveness; he hadn't earned the word — but assessment concluding. She turned to the others.

"They're a day behind us and gaining. They have a sledge, so they have shelter and hot food and fresh rope, and we have none of those things in surplus. If they catch us on open ice, we die, and it won't be quick, because these aren't rescue men." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, and somehow that was worse. "So we don't be on open ice. We take the eastern flank, under the Kestrel Tooth, through the broken ground. It costs us a day. It hides our track."

"It puts us under the lamp," Corvin said. "Whoever's signaling is on the Tooth."

"It does," Delacroix agreed. "Which means we move at night and hole up by day, and it means somebody watches the high ground while the rest sleep." Her eyes went around the hollow and stopped, deliberately, on Brase. "Somebody who knows what a watcher looks like."

* * *

They broke camp in twenty minutes, fast and silent, and Brase took point because taking point was the only apology he currently knew how to make.

The broken ground under the Kestrel Tooth was a chaos of fallen seracs and blue ice ribs, terrain that would break an ankle as soon as look at it, and Brase moved through it with the old certainty flooding back into his legs — the mine-workings certainty, the rubble-and-shadow certainty, thirty years of reading collapsed ground. Behind him the party strung out in Delacroix's order: the boy third, tied short; Marta fourth with Corvin on her rope; Delacroix sweeping. Nobody spoke. The mountain spoke enough for all of them, groaning and ticking as the afternoon cold locked into the ice.

Twice he found sign that stopped his blood. A cigarette end, crushed, half-buried — recent, within days. A tin ration case, Halvern issue, empty, wedged in a crevice like a man hiding evidence of a lunch stop. The recovery team had scouted this flank before. They knew it. Which meant the eastern route wasn't safety; it was just a different kind of gamble, and Brase filed that knowledge where he filed everything now, in the ledger of things he should have said sooner.

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a shattered ice block, and Delacroix posted him on the rise with the glasses and told him, in front of all of them, "Wake me at midnight. Whatever you see, wake me." It was a small sentence. It was also, he understood, a key turning in a lock he'd jammed himself. She was handing him the watch. She was letting him guard them.

He sat on the rise as the light died, the binoculars steady in his big hands, and let himself feel the whole weight of what he'd done and what he was doing, because there was no one up here to perform for and no performance left in him anyway.

He thought about the village. He'd never seen it — none of the watchers had; Halvern kept its contractors blind on principle — but he'd heard Ostrevan describe it once, in the flat administrative language of men discussing weather. *A settlement on the upper Sorel, noncompliant regarding mineral rights, resolved.* Resolved. Ninety-one people, the ledger said. He'd read the ledger for exactly as long as it took to understand what it was, and then he'd handed it back to Delacroix with hands that shook, and she had watched them shake and said nothing, and that nothing had been the kindest thing anyone had done for him in a decade.

Eleven thousand crowns. He tried to compute the exchange rate between eleven thousand crowns and ninety-one lives and found the arithmetic wouldn't hold. It had never held. That was the whole trick of it — the money was never meant to balance the books. It was meant to keep the bookkeeper from looking at the column at all.

Below him, the camp had gone quiet. Marta coughed twice, softly, and was still. The boy slept curled like a comma. Somewhere in the dark, Delacroix lay awake — he could tell by the absence of her breathing rhythm — keeping her own vigil over her own ledger.

At half past ten, the clouds tore open along the northern horizon, and the moonlight came down the glacier like spilled milk, and Brase raised the glasses one more time out of habit rather than hope.

The tracks were there. Six sets of boots, a sledge trail, moving along the very line they'd abandoned — and then stopping. Bunching. At the head of the column, a lantern swung in a slow arc, searching the broken ground, sweeping east, sweeping toward the flank, toward the Tooth, toward —

Toward them.

And below the lantern, catching the moonlight, the dull wet gleam of barrels. Not slung. Carried.

Brase lowered the glasses. His heart was going hard and slow, a stamping mill. Below, four people slept who had every reason to put a rope around his neck, and every reason in the world not to, and one of them was sixteen years old and had never done anything to anyone.

He was down the rise and shaking Delacroix's shoulder before he'd finished deciding, and the decision, he realized, had made itself days ago — on the glacier maybe, or in the plane, or fourteen months ago in a warm office when he'd said yes and some buried part of him had begun, quietly, all along, digging its way out.

"They've stopped," he whispered. "They've seen our turn-off. They're coming east."

Delacroix was already reaching for her boots. "How long?"

"At their pace, with that load — dawn. Maybe less if they drop the sledge."

"Then nobody sleeps." She was moving through the camp, waking them, low and calm, and Brase stood in the blue dark with his hands still trembling and understood that whatever happened on this mountain now, he had already chosen. There was no payment left to weigh it against. There was only the watching, and at last, after thirty-one years, something worth watching for.

Far up on the black shoulder of the Kestrel Tooth, the green lamp blinked once — twice — and went dark.
