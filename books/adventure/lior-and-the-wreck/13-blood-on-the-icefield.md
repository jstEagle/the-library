## 13. Blood on the Icefield

The icefield opened beneath us like a held breath.

We had come down out of the col at first light, six figures roped into a ragged line — me in front, Brase behind me, then Corvin, then Marta with the boy, Tam, clipped to her harness like something she'd decided she was no longer willing to lose. The ice stretched ahead of us for nearly two kilometers, a vast white table tilted gently toward the valley below, crevassed at its edges but clean across the middle. Clean was a word I used carefully on mountains. Clean meant visible. Visible meant fast.

Fast was what we needed. The tracks Brase had found yesterday — boot prints in a neat military cadence, four men minimum, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who knew where their prey would be — had gained on us through the night. Halvern's recovery team didn't have to hurry. They only had to follow.

"Keep your steps in my prints," I said over my shoulder. "If we move well, we're across by ten."

Marta's breathing had been bad since the whiteout. Three days in that crevasse camp had taken something out of her that food and rest weren't giving back — a wet rattle at the bottom of each exhale that I pretended not to hear because hearing it would mean deciding what to do about it, and there was nothing to do but descend. She caught me glancing back and gave me the ghost of her tavern smile, the one that said *don't you dare fuss over me, girl.*

I turned forward and moved.

The light came up hard off the ice, blue shadows pooling in every hollow, and for twenty minutes there was nothing in the world but the rhythm of it: step, plant, breathe; step, plant, breathe. Tam's crampons scraped behind Marta's. Corvin had stopped talking somewhere around dawn, which frightened me more than the tracks did. A silent journalist was a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

It was Brase who saw them first.

"Elodie." Just my name, low, the way he'd said *ropes are cut* a week ago. I followed his chin.

At the far edge of the icefield, where the glacier poured itself over a lip of rock into the serac field below, four figures stood in a loose skirmish line. Dark parkas. Rifles slung, not raised — not yet. They'd come up from the valley side while we were crossing, and now they simply stood between us and the way down, patient as weather.

I stopped the rope. The wind moved across the icefield and carried nothing with it, no sound at all, so that when one of the figures lifted a hand — not a weapon, just a hand, an open palm, the universal gesture of *let's talk* — it felt obscene in all that silence.

"How far to another route down?" Corvin asked quietly, coming up beside me.

"There isn't one. Not from here." I looked at the serac field below the lip — a chaos of leaning ice towers, blue and rotten, the kind of ground I'd spent twenty years teaching clients to fear — and then back at the four men. "They know that. That's why they're standing there drinking tea."

Brase unslung his pack slowly. "They'll want the ledger."

"They'll want everything," I said. "The ledger, the photographs, and then they'll want the four of us to have a tragic accident on the descent." I said it flatly, and watched Tam's face go white, and hated myself for saying it in front of him. But he was sixteen years old and on this mountain because of me, and lying to him now would be one more theft from a boy who'd already been robbed of everything.

"What do we do?" Marta asked. Her voice was steady. Her hands, wrapped around her ice axe, were not.

I looked at the distance. Eight hundred meters of open ice between us and them. No cover. No angle. If we ran left or right they'd simply walk across and intercept, fresh legs against our wrecked ones. If we went forward, we walked into rifles. If we stood still, the cold and the men behind us would finish the argument by afternoon.

"We talk," I said. "That's what they're offering. We give them talk until I find the thing they haven't thought of."

"And if there isn't one?" Corvin asked.

"Then we're dead on a nice day," I said, "and I'd rather die moving. Come on."

* * *

Their leader met us at the midpoint of the icefield, alone, rifle slung, hands visible. He was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with the sun-cured leather of a man who'd spent his career outdoors on someone else's payroll. He smiled at me like we were old acquaintances.

"Delacroix." He pronounced it correctly, which told me how long they'd been watching me. "Voss. Halvern Resource Recovery. I'd like to say this doesn't have to be unpleasant."

"Then don't say it," I said. "Say what you want."

"The documents you're carrying. All of them. And anything else the prospector gave you — maps, notes, personal effects." His eyes flicked past me to the rope, counting us. "In exchange, you walk south to the mining road. There's a truck. Everyone goes home. No report filed, no names in any file. You were never up here."

"And the blacklist?" I asked. "Does everyone go home to careers, or just home?"

Voss's smile thinned by a degree. "Ms. Delacroix. Whatever you think you found in that plane, I promise you it's less than it appears. Old wreckage. Old grief. Paper rots. Companies endure. Take the truck."

Behind me I heard Tam's breath going quick and shallow, and Marta murmuring something to him, low and rhythmic, a lullaby for a grown boy on a killing field. Brase hadn't moved. I could feel him standing slightly behind my right shoulder, the way he'd stood through the whole expedition — a man positioned, always, exactly where a bodyguard would stand, and never once acknowledged it.

"You were on the original recovery," I said to Voss. It wasn't a question. Something in his stillness answered it. "Twenty-two years ago. When the plane went down."

Something moved behind his eyes. "I was younger."

"Were there survivors?"

"Ms. Delacroix—"

"Were. There. Survivors."

He looked at me for a long moment, and the wind pulled at both our hoods, and somewhere behind him one of his men shifted his weight and the rifle sling creaked.

"There was a ledger," Voss said finally, and his voice had changed — flatter, wearier, the voice of a man reciting a catechism he'd been made to memorize. "And there were photographs. And the pilot had transmitted before he went down. The company has spent twenty-two years making sure those three facts never met in the same room. You are standing in the room, Ms. Delacroix. Please leave it."

"No," I said.

I hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way avalanches come out — after long accumulation, all at once, without negotiation. Somewhere inside me Lior's frozen face surfaced, and the promise I'd made him, and the other promise I'd made Marta two nights ago in the dark of the crevasse, holding her hand while she shook with fever: *we get down, and it gets delivered.* Two promises. One direction.

Voss sighed. Genuinely, it seemed. "I was afraid you'd say that."

He stepped back. Behind him, three rifles came off shoulders in a motion so practiced it was almost gentle.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do — each fragment complete and terrible and disconnected from the ones beside it.

Corvin shouting, throwing his arms wide, screaming something about cameras, about transmission, about *the whole world is already reading it* — a bluff, a beautiful magnificent bluff, and Voss's men hesitating for exactly one second because even hired guns fear the story more than the bullet—

Tam breaking right. Sixteen years old, panicked, running across open ice away from the rifles, which is the single worst thing a human being can do on a glacier and also the most human thing in the world—

One of the riflemen swinging his muzzle toward the movement, not aiming, tracking, the barrel wandering the way barrels wander when the man behind them is frightened—

Me lunging, useless, eight meters away, screaming the boy's name—

And Marta.

Marta Delaunay, sixty-one years old, widow, whose lungs had been failing since the crevasse, who had buried a husband and crossed a continent and read the names of murdered strangers in a frozen ledger and recognized her own complicity in every page — Marta moved faster than I have ever seen any human being move on ice. She covered the distance to Tam in four strides and folded herself around him, a wall of wool and grief, turning her back to the rifle just as the shot cracked off the ice walls and came back to us twice, three times, echoing, endless.

She made a sound I will hear until I die. Not a scream. Smaller than that. A sound of surprise, almost — the sound of a woman discovering, at the end, the exact weight of what one bullet costs.

Then the icefield was full of motion and I was in it.

They didn't fire again. Later I understood why — Voss screaming *hold, hold, you idiot,* because a widow's corpse on a glacier was a different animal entirely from a missing guide, because even Halvern's arithmetic couldn't absorb a photograph of this — and in that paralysis, in that half-second of institutional terror, Brase moved. He came past me like a landslide, put himself between our rope team and the riflemen with his axe in his fist and his head down, and bellowed, in a voice I'd never heard him use, a name — a name, a rank, a string of words in the flat argot of men who'd worked Halvern's high camps — and two of the riflemen actually stepped back, because Brase, it turned out, was known to them, and known in a way that made them uncertain.

"GO!" Brase roared at me, over his shoulder, not turning. "South lip! GO!"

I grabbed Tam — he was screaming under Marta, trying to hold her together with his hands, blood coming up between his fingers, too much, far too much — and Corvin took his other arm, and between us we peeled him away from her, and Marta looked up at me from the ice with her face gone gray and her eyes absolutely clear.

"Down," she said. "Take him down. That's the job, Elodie. That was always the job."

"Marta—"

"*Go.*"

I went. God forgive me, I went.

* * *

The serac field began at the southern lip and fell away below us like the ruins of a drowned cathedral — towers and slabs of blue ice leaning against each other, some the size of houses, riddled with melt channels, groaning faintly in the warming morning the way rotten timber groans. No guide alive takes clients into ground like this. No guide alive takes anyone into it. It is the kind of terrain you cross once, quickly, on luck, and never speak of again.

We went into it at a dead run, because behind us Brase was holding three armed men on an open icefield with an axe and a reputation, and because ahead of us was the only down.

Tam sobbed as we moved, Corvin hauling him by the harness, the boy's legs working on autopilot. "She — she jumped in front — she *jumped*—" 

"I know," Corvin said, and his voice cracked clean through. "I know, kid. Move."

The ice groaned louder as the sun climbed. I could feel it through my boots — a deep, structural complaint, the sound of a frozen waterfall deciding whether to remain architecture. I picked our line on instinct, threading the towers, refusing to look at the fractures webbing out from the bases, refusing to calculate the tonnage hanging above us. There is a mode I go into on mountains, the mode that kept me alive through the disaster that ruined me, and I fell into it now: the world narrows to slope and step and the next thirty seconds. Grief stays outside. Grief can wait at the top of the icefall with the rifles.

Halfway down, the mountain made its decision.

It started as a crack — not a sound but a feeling, a shudder through the soles of my feet — and then the tower to our left, a hundred-meter slab we'd passed not forty seconds earlier, leaned. Slowly, almost courteously, the way a drunk leans against a doorframe. Then it committed.

"RUN!"

We ran. The world behind us became noise — a roar like the mountain clearing its throat, ice the size of a church collapsing into ice the size of gravel, the whole slope below the lip letting go in a chain reaction as the first fall struck the next tower and the next. I shoved Tam into a crevasse mouth and dove in after him and Corvin piled in behind us as the air went white, a blizzard of pulverized ice blasting through the gap above our heads, filling my hood, my collar, my mouth, the world reduced to thunder and cold and the boy shaking against my chest.

And then, gradually, the way storms end, it stopped.

Silence. Dust of ice drifting down through the blue gloom like snow inside a bottle. Above the lip of our crevasse, the sky had changed shape — the towers that had framed it were gone, sheared off, and beyond them, far up the icefield, I could see nothing of the men, nothing of Brase, nothing but a new scar of rubble across the glacier where the route — and everything on it — had ceased to exist.

"Brase," Tam whispered. "Is Brase—"

I didn't answer. I couldn't. I lay in the dark of the crevasse with a dying woman's blood frozen on my jacket and a boy shaking in my arms, and I listened to the mountain settle, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade, the exact price of the ticket we'd punched. Not someday. Not abstractly. Here, itemized: Marta's blood on the ice above. Brase somewhere on the wrong side of that rubble, or under it. For a ledger. For names. For the truth about a village none of us had ever seen.

Corvin surfaced beside me, spitting ice, and looked up at the changed sky, and then at me, and his face was the face of a man who had spent weeks treating this as a story and had just, in the space of one avalanche, discovered it was a debt.

"Elodie," he said hoarsely. "The valley. Marta's valley — the descendants. How far?"

"Two days," I said. "If the south face lets us through."

Below us, out of the settling dust, came a sound that stopped my heart and restarted it sideways: voices. Human voices, calling up through the rubble — one voice, rough and unmistakable, cursing in the flat argot of Halvern's high camps.

Brase was alive. Brase was below us, on the far side of the collapse, alone in the wreckage with three armed men somewhere on the icefield above — and between us and the valley, the forbidden south face dropped away into the unknown, unroped, unmapped, and already, faintly, we could hear engines.

Halvern had trucks in the valley.
