## 14. What Elodie Did

The storm found them an hour after dark and did not let go.

Corvin had been in weather before — monsoon rain in the Cardamoms, a white squall off the Hebrides — but nothing like this. The wind didn't blow so much as *insist*, a physical pressure against the tent walls that made the nylon breathe in and out like something alive. Snow came not as flakes but as a horizontal white roar, erasing the world beyond three feet of headlamp glow. Somewhere below their ledge, the icefield they'd crossed at noon was already gone, swallowed whole.

They were trapped. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it.

The bivouac was a shallow overhang beneath a rib of black rock, barely deep enough for two tents pitched shoulder to shoulder and a cooking vestibule between them. Elodie had chosen it in ten seconds flat, driving her pickets with the economy of someone who'd done this a hundred times and expected to do it a hundred more. Now she sat with her back against the rock, arms around her knees, watching the storm the way other people watched a fire.

Brase was on first watch, though watch meant little tonight; nothing would come up the mountain in this, and nothing could leave it either. The boy — Tam, he'd finally given his name somewhere on the glacier, offered like contraband — slept in the far tent, curled against Marta's side. Marta's wound had been dressed and redressed, and she'd taken the last of the good painkillers without complaint, which frightened Corvin more than any complaint would have. Grief had company now, she'd said, back at the wreck. She hadn't known how literal that would become.

Corvin sat across from Elodie in the vestibule, close enough to share the stove's small heat, far enough to pretend he wasn't studying her.

He'd been studying her for weeks, of course. That was the job. That had always been the job. But the nature of the study had changed since the wreck, since the ledger, since the photographs of the burned village that Elodie had looked at for exactly four seconds before folding them away and saying nothing at all. Since Marta had read names out loud in a voice like breaking glass. Since Corvin had stood in the snow and admitted what he was — not a journalist, or not only one, but a hired hand, paid by Halvern's fixers to find survivor accounts and discredit them before they surfaced. Paid, in the beginning, to do the same to her.

She knew all of it now. She'd cornered him on the moraine two days ago and listened without blinking while he confessed, and then she'd said, *Good. Then you can be useful.* No fury. No forgiveness either. Just that flat mountain pragmatism that was somehow worse than both.

But there was a hole in the story, and Corvin had spent eleven years learning where holes lived. Hers was shaped like a word nobody used: *why*. Why her? Of all the guides in the northern ranges, why had Halvern's machinery ground itself down to destroy one woman's name?

"You're going to ask," Elodie said, without turning her head. "You've been circling it since the moraine."

"Ask what?"

"Why me." She turned then, and the stove light caught the scar through her left eyebrow — he'd never asked about that either. "Go ahead, Corvin. You've earned one question tonight. It's the only thing to do in a storm anyway. Talk, or think about dying. I've done enough thinking."

* * *

He chose the question carefully, because he suspected he'd only get one.

"The Delacroix expedition," he said. "Four dead. You were the guide. Every account I ever pulled — and I pulled all of them, Elodie, every deposition, every insurance filing, every interview the company lawyers didn't get sealed — every account says you pushed through a storm window to keep a schedule. That you were warned twice and went anyway. That's the version that ended your career. That's the version *I* was hired to reinforce." He paused. "It never fit. Guides don't survive that kind of failure. They don't survive the guilt, and they don't survive the industry. You survived both. So tell me the version that fits."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind trying to peel the mountain apart.

Then Elodie laughed — short, bitter, no humor in it, the laugh of someone hearing a joke at her own funeral.

"The version that fits," she repeated. "All right. But you should know what you're asking for. Once I say it out loud, you carry it too. That's how it works up here. That's how Lior worked it on me." Her jaw tightened at the name. "Fine. You want it chronologically?"

"I want it true."

"Chronologically, then." She fed another pinch of fuel to the stove, and the flame steadied, throwing her shadow huge against the rock behind her. "Seven years ago I was thirty-one years old and the best guide working the Verren approaches, and I believed that mattered. I had a reputation. Clean routes, clean record, clients who came back and sent their friends. And then Halvern Mining came to me — not directly, never directly, through a logistics firm called Ardent & Voss, which was Halvern wearing a suit — and they offered me a contract that was too good. Triple my rate. A private expedition, six clients, high route over the Verren Pass into territory Halvern held survey rights to. Officially it was a recreational trek. Unofficially—" she shrugged— "I didn't ask. That was my first sin, Corvin. Not asking. I told myself the money was for a lodge I wanted to build. I told myself a lot of things."

"What was the cargo?"

"Survey equipment. Sealed cases I wasn't allowed to open. And a schedule." She said the word like it tasted of copper. "A schedule built by people who had never been above four thousand meters in their lives. Ardent & Voss gave me a window — nine days, in and out — because the survey team needed the pass cleared before the autumn freeze, because a board meeting was scheduled in October, because money. None of those are reasons a mountain cares about. But I was thirty-one, and I thought I could beat the mountain to its own calendar. I'd done it before. Or I thought I had."

The stove hissed. In the other tent, Tam murmured in his sleep and went quiet again.

"Day five," Elodie said. "We summited the pass ridge on day five, right on schedule, and that's when the barometer started falling like a stone down a well. I'd seen the cloud buildups all afternoon — lenticulars stacking over the south col, the kind of sky that old guides call a warning and young guides call weather. My radio got a forecast update: a system coming in forty-eight hours early, bigger than anything the models had shown. Any sane guide turns around. Any sane guide takes the clients down the way they came, loses the summit fee, eats the penalty clause, and lives."

"But you didn't turn around."

"I called Ardent & Voss." Her voice had gone very flat now, the flatness of a surface over deep water. "And the man I spoke to — I never learned his real name, he was 'Mr. Renner' for the duration — told me that aborting carried penalties that would bankrupt me, and that completing on schedule carried a bonus that would build my lodge. And then he said something I've heard in my head every night for seven years. He said: *Miss Delacroix, the mountain has killed worse guides than you and forgotten their names by spring.*"

Corvin felt the cold reach through his jacket, though the stove was burning fine.

"He was right about the second part," Elodie said. "That's the joke. Go on. Ask me if I hesitated."

"Did you?"

"No. That's the truth, and I won't dress it up. I looked at that sky and I did the arithmetic — distance, time, margin — and the arithmetic said we could outrun it if nothing went wrong. And I wanted the bonus, and I wanted the record, and I wanted to prove that a woman alone in that industry could be *infallible*, and so I led six clients into a storm window to save Halvern's schedule. My decision. My signature on the route plan. Whatever else happened — whatever they did to me afterward — that part is mine, and I have never once tried to give it away."

"But people died."

"People died." She closed her eyes. "Not the way the reports said."

* * *

The storm screamed. Elodie spoke into it quietly, and Corvin leaned in to hear, and understood that he was the first person in seven years to hear it at all.

"We were on the descent, day seven, when the front hit us — twelve hours early, because of course it was. Whiteout inside twenty minutes. I got the rope teams anchored in a col below the east face and we sat it out for a day, burning schedule, and I radioed Ardent & Voss to tell them we'd be late. And Renner told me the survey team couldn't wait. Told me there was a secondary route down the north flank — steeper, unpatrolled, but fast — and that a local man had guided parties that way for years. He even gave me a name." She opened her eyes. "Lior."

Corvin went still. "Lior. The prospector."

"The prospector. The dying man who gave me a map and made me promise. Seven years ago he was the local guide Ardent & Voss hired to walk their dirty route, same as they hired me to walk their dirty schedule. Except Lior knew what was in those sealed cases, Corvin. He'd seen the survey photos. He knew what Halvern had done in the valley — what they'd burned, who they'd buried under the tailings. And he took the guiding job anyway, because they paid him, and because he thought maybe he could steer the evidence out over the pass himself, in our packs, past the checkpoints. Get it to journalists. Get it anywhere."

"And instead—"

"Instead the storm caught us on the north flank at fourteen thousand feet, exactly where a man who loved that mountain would never have taken anyone in October, and the slope let go." Her hands had curled into fists inside her sleeves. "Avalanche. Class three. It took the middle rope team — three clients — before anyone could shout. We dug for six hours. We found two of them alive under a meter of debris. We did not find the third until morning, and by then—" She stopped. Started again. "By then the cold had finished what the snow started."

"Three clients," Corvin said slowly. "The reports said four dead."

"The fourth was Lior." Her voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it. "When the slide hit, he wasn't roped in. He'd clipped himself loose to move ahead and probe the route — his choice, his craft, his mountain. The recovery teams found his body two days later, half a kilometer downslope. And Ardent & Voss saw their opening. A dead local guide, no family listed, no one to speak for him. So they wrote him out of the official account entirely — undocumented presence, they called him, an unauthorized individual — and they wrote the deaths onto *me*. All of them. Four souls, one negligent guide, case closed. The inquiry was run by a firm that shared two directors with Ardent & Voss. My license suspended pending review became revoked pending appeal became blacklisted, full stop. Every outfitter in three countries got a quiet letter. By winter I couldn't buy a job carrying daypacks for tourists."

"And Lior's name was erased."

"Lior's name was erased, and mine was nailed up in its place, and here is the part that kept me alive through seven years of hating myself: I couldn't make the math work. Three clients died on my rope, in my storm, on my decision — that debt is real and I pay it daily. But the *blacklist* never matched the crime. Guides have buried clients and worked again within the year. What was done to me was disproportionate, surgical, permanent. For years I thought it was cruelty. Then a gut-shot prospector crawled into my camp with a map to a wrecked plane, and I finally understood it wasn't cruelty at all." She looked at him, and her eyes in the stove light were the oldest thing on the mountain. "It was housekeeping. I was the last living witness to the north-flank route, to the sealed cases, to Renner's voice on the radio telling me to push through. I was a loose end with a reputation, and reputations are loud. They didn't blacklist me to punish me, Corvin. They blacklisted me to bury me where no one would ever dig — and then they waited seven years to see if I'd stay buried."

* * *

Corvin sat with it. Above them the storm hammered the rock like a fist on a door, and he thought: *this is why they hired me.* Not to find the wreck. To find *her*, the moment she moved toward it, and to be standing in the story when she arrived — the credible outsider, ready to confirm the official version one final time. He had been the last nail, purchased in advance.

"They'll do it again," he said. "To all of us. When this is over, whatever happens with the ledger — they'll write the account, and we'll be the villains in it. Marta will be a grieving widow manipulated by a disgraced guide. Brase will be a lone criminal. The boy will be a runaway who died of exposure, if it comes to that. And you—" he met her eyes— "you'll be the blacklisted guide who led innocents onto a forbidden route. Again. It writes itself. They've had practice."

"I know." Elodie fed the stove its last pinch of fuel, and the flame shrank to a blue bead. "Which is why you're going to write it first."

He blinked. "What?"

"You're a journalist. A real one, underneath the hire — I've watched you work, Corvin, you can't help yourself, you take notes in your sleep. So you're going to write the true account. All of it. The ledger, the village, the massacre, the north flank, Renner, the inquiry, me. Everything. And you're going to get it off this mountain to someone who'll publish it before Halvern's version exists to fight." She said it simply, the way she gave orders on rope: not a request, just gravity. "That's your penance. Mine is carrying the ledger down. Brase's is whatever he decides it is. We each carry what we broke."

"And if I refuse?"

"You won't." Almost — almost — she smiled. "Because you already wrote the false version once, and I've seen what it costs you to sit with that. Guilt's just grief with a job attached. Do the job."

Corvin looked at his hands. Eleven years of bylines, and the piece that mattered most was the one he'd have to smuggle out in pieces, off a mountain, past armed men, with a dying widow and a runaway boy depending on him. His notebook weighed four ounces. It had never weighed anything before.

"All right," he said. "I'll need everything. Dates, names, the radio logs if you kept them. Renner's voice — describe it, every call. Lior's route. Start at the beginning and don't skip."

Elodie nodded once, and for the first time since he'd known her, something in her shoulders actually came down — a centimeter, maybe less, but on her it was an avalanche of release. Seven years of solitary weight, shifting at last onto more than one pair of legs.

"Day zero," she said. "There was fog in the valley when the clients arrived..."

She talked for three hours. Corvin filled nineteen pages, writing blind by stove light, his fingers stiff, his handwriting degrading into shorthand only he could read. Outside, the storm raged on without opinion. Inside the overhang, a story that had been buried for seven years came up out of the ice piece by piece, and Corvin understood that he was doing what the recovery teams did with avalanche victims: digging, carefully, hoping to find warmth.

Near midnight, Tam's voice came small from the far tent. "Is she telling it? The real story?"

Elodie paused. "Go back to sleep, boy."

"Is it bad?"

"It's true," Elodie said. "Sleep."

Silence. Then, from the darkness, the boy's voice again — but different now, careful, and aimed not at Elodie but at the storm wall, as if he were reading something written on the snow:

"There's a light out there. Down-slope. Moving."

Corvin's pen stopped. Elodie was already on her feet, killing the stove, and in the sudden dark they all heard it together: far below through the roaring white, faint and steady and wrong for this weather at this hour — a lantern beam, sweeping the icefield, climbing.
