## 15. Marta's Vigil

Pain was a country, and Marta had crossed its border without papers.

She lay in the lee of a granite wall while the others argued in low voices about the descent, and she let the pain have her for a while, because fighting it cost more than she had left. The bullet had gone in below her ribs on the left side — she knew what that meant. She had sat with her husband through the last year of his cancer; she had learned the geography of dying the way other women learned their gardens. This was deeper ground than that. Colder.

The cold was the strange part. Not the mountain's cold — she'd stopped feeling that hours ago — but an interior cold, spreading outward from the wound like frost climbing a windowpane. Her hands were gray. When she pressed them to her belly they told her nothing at all.

*Not yet,* she told herself. *There is one thing left.*

"Marta." Elodie crouched beside her, and even now, even bleeding from a gash across her brow, the guide moved like water finding its level. "We're moving as soon as Brase scouts the serac field. Can you walk?"

They both knew the answer. Elodie asked anyway, because she was kind in the way that strong people are kind — by refusing to decide things for you until you made them impossible.

"I can be carried," Marta said. Her voice came out thin, papery, a stranger's voice. "For a while."

Elodie's jaw tightened. She was doing arithmetic too — weight, distance, altitude, the boy who couldn't carry, the journalist who shouldn't be trusted with a rope end. Marta watched the numbers fail behind her eyes and almost pitied her.

"Then we go slow," Elodie said.

"No." Marta caught her wrist. Her fingers wouldn't close properly; they skated on the sleeve of Elodie's parka like ice on iron. "Listen to me. I need you to listen before I stop being able to say it."

Above them the sky was doing something obscene — a hard blue so deep it looked personal, the sun striking off the icefield where Halvern's men had stood not six hours ago, where Marta had stepped in front of a boy and the world had gone white and then red and then this: a granite wall, a borrowed coat, a view of the glacier that would have been beautiful if beauty hadn't abandoned her somewhere around the second hour of bleeding.

"Corvin," Marta called, and the journalist came down the slope with his camera bag clutched to his chest like a child. He had changed since the icefield. Whatever he'd been hired to do, whatever machinery he'd served, the bullet had passed through him too, in its way. He knelt on her other side without being asked.

"The ledger," Marta said. "Where is it?"

Elodie touched the waterproof roll strapped inside her jacket. "Here."

"Give it to me."

"Marta—"

"*Give it to me.*" The strength of her own voice surprised them all, herself most of all. It came up from somewhere below the wound, below the cold, from a stratum of her that grief had been compacting for thirty years into something that could bear weight. "I read it first, Elodie. In the wreck. Before any of you. I earned the right to hold it once more."

Elodie hesitated — Marta saw her weighing the promise she'd made to a dead prospector against the request of a dying woman — and then worked the roll free and laid it across Marta's lap.

Her hands shook too much to untie the cord. Corvin did it for her, gently, and unrolled the pages flat on the granite, weighting the corners with stones.

There were the names again. Forty-one of them, written in a surveyor's careful hand, each with an age and a village designation. *Kesh-Valley, holding 7.* As though people could be holdings. As though a village could be reduced to a number in a mining company's column, and the number reduced to ash, and the ash reduced to a line item marked *resolved.*

Marta had known, reading it in the plane, that some of the names were familiar. Now, with the cold rising and time running out of her like sand through a split sack, she understood how familiar.

"Aldric," she said. "My husband. His firm handled Halvern's land acquisitions. Thirty years of contracts, and I used to file his correspondence because I liked the smell of his office." She laughed, and it hurt, and she laughed anyway. "I filed these names before I ever saw them here. *Resolved,* Aldric wrote me once, in a letter I thought meant a boundary dispute. *The Kesh matter is resolved.* I thought it was a fence line."

Elodie had gone very still.

"There's a name on the third page," Marta said. "Suri Kesh. Age nine. Listed as *unaccounted.* Do you understand what that means? It means the company couldn't confirm she burned. It means someone got her out. Someone carried a nine-year-old girl down a mountain in the dark, and the company spent thirty years making sure no one ever went looking for whether she lived."

"How do you know she lived?" Corvin asked quietly.

"Because Lior found this plane." Marta put her hand flat on the ledger, over the names, over the ash. "Because a man spent decades walking passes no one walks, looking for a wreck no one wanted found, and then gut-shot and freezing he gave the map to the one woman on this mountain with nothing left to lose. That is not chance. That is a son coming home. Suri Kesh would be my age now. And somewhere there is a valley where she raised children who were never told why their grandmother flinched at the smell of smoke."

The wind came down the col and turned the ledger's pages, and Marta watched the names flutter — alive, alive, alive, for one paper moment — before Corvin weighted them down again.

* * *

They tried to move her twice.

The first time, Brase lifted her with a tenderness that astonished everyone, this silent slab of a man who had confessed nothing and remembered everything, and they got perhaps two hundred meters along the moraine before the pain tore through whatever was holding her together and she heard herself screaming and hated the sound, hated frightening the boy, Toma, who hovered at the edge of her vision with his chapped face and his runaway's wariness finally burned away into something rawer.

The second time they didn't get fifty meters. The world tilted, went granular, and when it reassembled itself Marta was on her back again with Elodie's coat rolled under her head and four faces arranged above her like a jury that had already deliberated.

"We make her a camp," Brase said. First full sentence she'd ever heard him speak. "Storm shelter, both tarps. I'll stay."

"You'll do no such thing," Marta said. "You will take those three down the south face and you will keep them alive, because that is what you were paid to prevent and it is the only penance they will accept from you."

Brase didn't argue. She suspected he never argued; he simply absorbed verdicts the way rock absorbed weather.

"It's not a decision," Elodie said. "No one gets left on this mountain."

"Everyone gets left on this mountain," Marta said. "That is the entire lesson of mountains. You of all people know it. You've been paying for one leaving for ten years." She regretted it the instant she said it — saw the flinch move through Elodie like a tremor through a rope — but she was past the country of tact now, well into the interior, where only true things grew. "Leave me the stove, the tent, and the ledger, and I will die warm and useful. There are worse trades. I've seen the alternatives."

"Toma stays with you," Elodie said.

"No." Marta reached for the boy's hand and pulled him down into her failing field of vision. His fingers were freezing. Children were always freezing; no one ever checked. "Toma goes with you. If I am to be a grave, I will not also be an anchor. Look at me, boy. Look at me and hear me: I chose to step in front of that rifle. It is the first fully honest thing I have done since my wedding day. Don't you dare spend it on guilt. Spend it on getting down."

His chin was trembling. He was sixteen and trying not to cry in front of ex-miners and journalists, and Marta loved him with a sudden ferocity that felt like the last hot coal in her chest.

"When you're old," she told him, "and someone asks what you did in the Verren Pass, you will say you walked out carrying a book of names. Say it plainly. No embellishment. Plainness is the only currency that doesn't devalue."

He nodded, jaw clenched, tears cutting clean lines through the grime on his face.

* * *

Dusk came early, the way it does behind high walls of stone. They built her shelter with a competence that would have moved her if she'd had anything left to be moved with — tarps guyed against the wind, the little stove lit in the vestibule, a foam pad layered double beneath her. Brase melted snow and made tea she couldn't drink and set it near her hand anyway, because the warmth of the cup was a kindness separate from the drinking.

One by one they came to say goodbye, or rather they came to say things that were not goodbye, because none of them could afford the word.

Corvin sat with her longest. He told her, haltingly, about the contract — the retainer from Halvern's communications office, the brief to *manage the narrative around legacy assets*, the elegant phrase that meant bury survivors' accounts under doubt. "I told myself it was just spin," he said. "Everyone spins. But sitting here—" He gestured at the ledger, still open on the stones between them. "You can't unspin forty-one people. I'm going to publish everything. Every page, every name, every contract. Even the parts that hang me."

"Good," Marta said. "Hang yourself thoroughly. It lends credibility."

He laughed, wet-eyed, and pressed her hand, and crawled out into the dark.

Then it was Elodie.

Marta had saved her for last, though she'd never admit to planning it. She watched the guide bank the stove, check the tarp lines twice, arrange the ledger within reach of Marta's stiffening fingers — fussing, the way the competent fuss when competence has run out of uses.

"Elodie."

The woman looked up. In the stove light her face was all planes and exhaustion, ten years of blacklist carved into it, and Marta thought: *there she is. The one Lior chose.*

"Come here. Closer. My voice doesn't travel anymore."

Elodie lay down beside her, shoulder to shoulder, the way women sit with the dying in hospital corridors when chairs are scarce and honesty is abundant.

"I'm going to give you a name," Marta said. "And a valley. And I need you to swear — not to me. To him. To Lior, wherever he is, and to every name in that book."

"Suri Kesh," Marta said. "Third page. Unaccounted. She'd have come south when the valley burned — the Kesh family had summer grazing rights in the Ansel valley, on the far side of the Verren massif. Aldric's files mentioned an Ansel claim dispute, years back. A woman named Suri Kesh-Ansel who wouldn't sell. Stubborn as bedrock, the lawyers complained. Still won't sell, I'd wager. People like that don't." Her breath caught, held, released. "Find the Ansel valley. Find her, or her children, or her children's children. Put that book in their hands. Tell them a stranger read their names aloud on a mountain so they wouldn't be numbers anymore. Tell them—" The cold reached her throat then, briefly, and she had to wait it out, jaw shaking. "Tell them the world kept a copy. All these years. The world kept a copy."

"I swear," Elodie said. Her voice cracked down the middle. "By Lior. By every name in the book. The ledger reaches the descendants of Kesh-Valley, or I don't come off this mountain at all."

"That's too much," Marta murmured. "Come off the mountain. Just don't come off empty-handed." She smiled, and felt the smile reach her face, which surprised her; she'd assumed the muscles were past it. "You remind me of no one, Elodie Delacroix. That is the highest compliment I own. Everyone else in my life reminded me of someone — my husband reminded me of ambition, this mountain reminds me of money, Halvern's men remind me of everything I closed my eyes to for thirty comfortable years. You remind me of nothing. You are entirely your own weather. Now — help me sit up. I want to watch you go."

"You should lie flat—"

"*Help me sit up.*" A flicker of the boardroom voice, the widow who had signed off on foundations and endowments and, God forgive her, a Halvern charity gala. "I intend to conduct my own vigil, and I will do it upright, facing east, like a person and not a parcel."

Elodie propped her against the pack, wrapped every layer they could spare around her, tucked the ledger into the crook of her arm where her hands could rest on it. Then she paused at the mouth of the shelter, silhouetted against the last violet band of dusk.

"Marta. About your husband's letters. About what you knew and didn't know—"

"I know exactly what I didn't know," Marta said. "And I know what I declined to ask. Both of those are mine to carry, and I'll carry them the last few miles myself, thank you. Go. Take the boy down. Come back for the story if you must — I'll leave a good death lying around somewhere."

Elodie's laugh broke in half. She gripped Marta's hand once, hard, and was gone into the blue dark, and Marta heard the small sounds of the party assembling — rope coils, buckles, Toma's sniffed-back tears — and then, fainter and fainter, boots on scree, going down, going toward the valley, going toward the name.

* * *

The night was enormous.

Marta sat with the ledger open on her knees and read the names aloud into the wind, all forty-one, slowly, giving each one its full weight, because a name spoken alone on a mountain at night is a small bell rung in a very large cathedral and she intended to ring every bell. Her voice lasted through the twenties. After that she mouthed them, tracing each line with a fingertip gone numb, and the wind took over the speaking, worrying the tarp edges, keening down the col, and she chose to believe it was pronouncing them properly.

The stars came out with the terrible clarity of altitude. Somewhere down there, in the dark, a stubborn old woman who wouldn't sell was sleeping in a valley that smelled of pine instead of smoke, and in the morning — or the day after, or however long the south face took — Elodie Delacroix would come walking into her life carrying forty-one bells.

Marta found she was not afraid. She catalogued this with mild professional interest, the way she'd once reviewed quarterly reports: *fear, absent; regret, present but manageable; purpose, fully subscribed.* The cold had climbed past her waist now, past her ribs, a tide with excellent manners, taking its time.

Near dawn — she thought it was dawn; the east went the color of a healing bruise — she heard something that was not wind.

Boots. More than one pair. Above her, on the moraine, moving with the deliberate spacing of men who had been trained to search.

Halvern's recovery team had doubled back. Of course they had. A witness left alive on the mountain was a loose page, and Halvern burned loose pages — it was practically the company motto.

Marta closed the ledger. She held it against her chest with both arms, the way you hold the one thing, and she turned her face east toward the bruise-colored light and the invisible valley beyond it, and she began, in a voice that had almost nothing left, to sing — some half-remembered hymn from her grandmother, tuneless and defiant, a small bell rung against the dark.

The boots stopped.

Then they started again, coming down.
