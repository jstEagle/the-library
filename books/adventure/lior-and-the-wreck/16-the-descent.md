## 16. The Descent

The south face of the Verren massif did not appear on any map Elodie had ever owned, and she had owned most of them. The cartographers had left it blank — a white smear below the summit ridge, annotated only with the surveyor's old warning: *unstable. no route.* That was a lie in the way most warnings were lies; there was always a route. What the blank space really meant was that no one had ever come back to draw one.

She stood at the lip of it now, wind tearing at her hood, and looked down into three thousand feet of broken ice and shadowed rock.

Behind her, the others waited. Corvin with his camera bag cinched tight against his spine like a hump. The boy — Tam, though he'd only given them the name two nights ago, in the dark of the crevasse camp, as if truth were something you surrendered in installments — clutching Marta's spare mittens because she'd told him his hands were useless without them. And Brase, standing slightly apart, the way he always did, reading the face the way she was.

Marta they had left at the high camp, wrapped in both tents and every scrap of insulation they could spare, propped against her own pack so the wound wouldn't drown her. She'd refused to say goodbye. "I'll be here when you come back," she said, which was either courage or arithmetic, and none of them had asked which.

"We can't go down this," Tam said. His voice cracked on *this*. He was sixteen and he'd already watched a woman take a bullet meant for him; Elodie decided not to hold the crack against him.

"We can't go up," she said. "Halvern's team holds the col. They'll have the north route by now, and the east glacier is a shooting gallery." She pointed with her axe at the white smear below them. "So we go where they think no one would be stupid enough to go."

"And if they're right?" Corvin said. There was no mockery in it. Somewhere between the icefield and the serac collapse, he had stopped performing for her.

"Then we're stupid together." She swung over the lip before anyone could argue, because arguments on exposed ground killed faster than falls.

* * *

The first hour was the worst kind of climbing — not technically hard, but demanding a total, continuous attention that left nothing over for fear. Elodie led. She placed protection where the mountain offered it and accepted bare friction where it didn't, and behind her came four people moving in her rhythm: Corvin surprisingly steady, Tam terrified but obedient, and Brase — Brase moving like water finding its level, as he had since the first glacier crossing, in a way that had stopped surprising her and started feeling like luck she hadn't earned.

They descended through a band of rotten rock where the mountain shed stones in whispers, then onto blue ice veined with meltwater, then into a couloir so steep they climbed down it facing the slope, axes buried to the head. Twice the ice groaned beneath them, a long tectonic complaint that Elodie felt in her teeth. Twice she called a halt and listened until the mountain finished its sentence.

It was during the second halt, wedged into a niche barely wide enough for two, that Brase spoke without being asked.

"Patrol change is at eighteen hundred."

Elodie turned her head. Below them the couloir dropped away into cloud; above, the rim they'd fled was a bright line against a bruised sky.

"Halvern runs two teams on the Verren approaches," Brase went on, eyes fixed on the ice. "Four men each. One works the north routes out of the Kessel hut, one covers the eastern glacier from a snowcat track. They don't cover the south face because—" he paused, and something moved through his heavy face that might have been shame— "because Halvern's own surveys said it couldn't be done. I filed that report. Eleven years ago."

"You surveyed this face?"

"I surveyed all of it. Every approach, every cache, every patrol line. It was my job." He finally looked at her. "I knew these mountains better than anyone alive, once. For them."

The wind filled the pause. Elodie thought about the rope cache, cut clean at the anchors. About how precisely the recovery team had found them on the icefield, as if following a script someone had written years in advance. About how much of what had hunted them had been drawn, in effect, by the silent man now hanging beside her on borrowed protection.

"Eighteen hundred," she said. "That's when they rotate?"

"That's when they're furthest from anywhere. If we cross the lower icefield between six and seven, we pass their blind spot while they're walking away from it." He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that almost. "They taught me that too."

"Then we move when I say, and we trust your clock."

"My clock's been right for eleven years," Brase said. "It's the only thing about me that has been."

* * *

They crossed the lower icefield at 6:40 in the evening, in light the color of old bone, strung out on one rope with Tam in the middle where Elodie could reach him fastest. The crevasses here were old and half-bridged, sagging tents of snow over black throats, and they moved fast and light exactly the way Brase described the patrol doing the opposite — four men trudging back toward the Kessel hut somewhere beyond the shoulder of the mountain, tired, cold, thinking about dinner.

Elodie felt them anyway. Felt the mountain's whole geography reorganize itself around armed men, the way it had ever since the icefield. Every shadow was a scope. Every wind sound was radio static.

Halfway across, the snow bridge ahead of Tam sighed and settled an inch, and the boy froze with the particular rigid stillness of prey.

"Breathe," Elodie said quietly. "Weight on your feet, not your fear. Two more steps and you're on solid ice."

He took the steps. On the far side he didn't celebrate or collapse; he just looked back at the bridge, then at her, and nodded once — a small, grim transaction between people who had agreed to keep each other alive. Something in Elodie's chest loosened a degree. Somewhere in the last week the boy had stopped being cargo and started being crew, and she suspected neither of them had marked the moment.

Corvin brought up the rear, and when they reached the moraine he crouched beside her, breathing hard, and said, "You know what's strange? Six days ago I'd have sold this route to my editor for a byline. Now I keep checking the skyline for snipers." He wiped his nose on his sleeve. "I'm trying to decide if that's growth or just terror."

"Both usually arrive together," Elodie said.

* * *

They made the tree line at dusk, dropping out of the world of ice into a steep forest of black spruce where the darkness came up to meet them like water. Here, for the first time in two days, they could risk fire — a fist-sized flame in a pit dug against a fallen trunk, screened by rock on three sides. They melted snow, ate the last of the cheese and half the remaining biscuits, and let the heat work into their hands.

Tam fell asleep mid-sentence, sitting up, his head tipping against Corvin's shoulder. Corvin went very still, as if afraid of breaking something, and after a moment shifted the boy's weight to a more comfortable angle and kept talking in a whisper.

"Marta made me promise to look after him," he said. "Before we left her. She grabbed my wrist with her good hand and said, *the journalist looks after the boy, that's the trade.*" He shook his head slowly. "I never agreed to anything in my life less formally. I've never kept a promise more seriously."

"She has a talent for that," Elodie said. "Making trades you don't remember accepting."

Brase sat apart, as always, but closer than before — within the firelight rather than at its edge. He was cleaning his boots with a stick, methodically, and Elodie realized after watching him for a while that it wasn't the boots he was working on. It was himself. Scraping the mountain off, or trying to.

"The valley's two days south," he said, without looking up. "If we follow the river through the gorge, we stay under the patrol lines the whole way. But there's a bridge."

"I saw it on Lior's map. The old footbridge above the gorge narrows."

"It's the only crossing for thirty kilometers. Halvern knows that too. If they guess where we're headed, that's where they'll wait." He set the stick down. "There's also a man named Ostrick who runs their logistics out of the mill town. He signs the pay orders. Mine included." He said it flatly, a fact among facts. "If anyone knows we're coming, it'll be because Ostrick told them."

Elodie fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. "Why are you telling us this now?"

"Because tomorrow I stop being useful and start being a liability, and you should decide what to do about that while you still have options." He raised his eyes to hers at last. In the firelight they were the color of wet slate. "You should know I've already decided what I'll do about it."

"Which is?"

"Whatever keeps the ledger moving south." He picked up the stick again. "Eleven years I got paid to make sure it stayed buried. Consider this the refund."

Across the fire, Corvin caught her eye over the sleeping boy's head, and neither of them said anything, because some things said aloud become smaller.

* * *

They walked the next day through the gorge itself, on a shelf of stone above a river gone violent with melt, the walls rising sheer on either side until the sky was a ribbon overhead. It was the safest and most claustrophobic terrain Elodie had ever led anyone through — no patrols could see them, and no helicopter could follow, but if the shelf collapsed they would simply cease to exist, unrecorded, inside the mountain's closed fist.

They talked, because there was nothing else to do with the hours. Not about important things — about food. Corvin described a meal he'd eaten in Lisbon in such pornographic detail that Tam walked the entire next kilometer with his eyes closed. Brase, prompted by nothing, admitted that the best thing he'd ever tasted was canned peaches eaten in a tent at five thousand meters, and refused to elaborate. Elodie told them about her mother's kitchen in Chastel, the bread oven, the way the whole house smelled on Saturdays, and heard her own voice go soft and strange with homesickness for a place that had stopped answering her letters after the blacklist.

Somewhere in the afternoon, without ceremony, Tam began walking beside her instead of behind her. Matching her pace. Pointing out a loose block before she'd seen it. When they stopped for water he handed her the bottle first, automatically, the way you hand things to someone whose judgment you've stopped questioning.

Fugitives, she thought, watching the four of them move along the shelf in single file — the disgraced guide, the bought journalist, the company man, the runaway boy, carrying a dead prospector's ledger and a dying widow's promise between them. None of them had chosen the others. All of them had chosen this.

It wasn't a family. Families didn't form at gunpoint on forbidden faces. But it was whatever families grew from — the shared weight, the automatic handing-over of the bottle, the way Brase had begun, without noticing, to walk at the rear where a guard walks, facing back toward the danger he understood best.

At dusk they climbed out of the gorge, and the trees opened, and Elodie saw it.

The bridge.

It spanned the river narrows a quarter mile downstream: a skeleton of old timber and rusted cable, sagging in the middle like a tired animal, half-eaten by forty years of weather. Beyond it the land rose into forested foothills, and beyond those, invisible, the valley where names were waiting to be read aloud.

And on the near bank, planted in the mud of the trail like a flag, a fresh wooden stake with a strip of orange survey tape tied to it — fluttering in the evening wind, bright as a wound.

Someone had marked the crossing. Recently.

Brase stopped beside her, and she felt more than saw him go still, the stillness of a man hearing a clock he trusted begin to tick wrong.

"That tape isn't ours," he said quietly. "And it isn't old."

Downstream, faint on the wind, carried across the water — the sound of an engine starting.
