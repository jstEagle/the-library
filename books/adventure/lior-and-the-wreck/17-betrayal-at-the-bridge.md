## 17. Betrayal at the Bridge

The river found them before the bridge did.

Wren heard it first — a low grinding under the wind, like the mountain clearing its throat — and then the trees thinned and there it was: meltwater gone violent with spring, gray-green and muscular, shouldering past boulders the size of cottages. Spray hung in the air like breath. Across the chasm, maybe forty meters of nothing, the bridge sagged between two stone pylons, a rope-and-plank thing that looked less built than abandoned, its cables furred with ice.

"Old survey bridge," Brase said. He'd said almost nothing in six days of descent, but he always spoke when it mattered, short and flat as a hammer strike. "Halvern built it to haul ore. They'll know it's here."

"They'll also know we have no other way across," Elodie said. She stood at the lip of the gorge with the wind pulling at her hood, and Wren watched her do the thing she did — that stillness, that total attention, like she could read the mountain's next sentence before it finished speaking. "So we cross fast and we cut it behind us."

Behind them, somewhere up the valley they'd descended through the night, were men with rifles and a tracker who moved like weather. Behind them was Marta, carried on a litter of ski poles and pack straps, feverish, her breathing wet and shallow. Behind them was everything.

Wren's hands wouldn't stop shaking. She shoved them into her pockets where nobody could see.

* * *

They had picked up the pursuit two days ago, tracks stitched along their own like a second heartbeat. Elodie had made them travel through the dark after that, which was its own kind of torture — headlamps bouncing off ice, the south face dropping away beneath their boots into blackness that had no bottom anyone would admit to. Corvin had fallen twice. The second time, Wren had grabbed his wrist without thinking, and he'd looked at her in the lamplight with an expression she couldn't name, and said, "Thanks, kid," and she'd hated how much she liked hearing it.

She hadn't told anyone yet. That was the thing sitting in her chest like swallowed glass.

Three nights ago, camped in the lee of a serac with the storm screaming itself out above them, a voice had come out of the white — not shouting, just close, conversational, the way you'd talk to someone across a table. A man's voice, patient and reasonable.

*Girl. We know you're with them. We know you're not one of them.*

She'd frozen with her back against the ice, heart slamming.

*You're sixteen. You've got no stake in this. Halvern doesn't want you. Walk down to the river, walk to the bridge, light the lamp we left in the cairn there, and you go home free. Amnesty. Paper. Your mother stops worrying.*

Her mother. He'd said *your mother*, and something in Wren had cracked open like lake ice, because her mother was the whole reason she'd run — or half the reason, or a reason she'd stopped examining around the time the mountains started making sense in a way kitchens never had.

*Or stay with them,* the voice had said, almost kindly. *And be buried with them.*

The voice had gone. She'd lain awake until dawn doing arithmetic with her own life, and every sum came out wrong no matter how she arranged the numbers.

Now the bridge hung in front of her, and the cairn stood ten paces from the near pylon — she saw it immediately, a man-made pile of stones among God-made ones, too neat, with a gap in its crown exactly lamp-sized. It had been waiting for her for three days. It would wait forever if she let it.

* * *

Elodie sent Brase across first with the rope, because he was the heaviest and the surest, and if the bridge held him it would hold anyone. He went out onto the planks the way he did everything — no wasted motion, eyes forward, boots finding the sound boards by some instinct older than thought. Halfway across, a plank gave way under him and dropped into the gorge, and Wren flinched at the crack of it, but Brase didn't even slow. He reached the far side, tied off, and signaled.

Marta went next, carried, moaning softly at each sway. Then Corvin, who crossed badly, arms out, swearing under his breath in a way that was somehow meant to be encouraging. Then the packs, hauled across on the rope hand over hand.

Wren stood last on the near bank with Elodie.

"You're quiet," Elodie said. She was coiling rope, not looking at her, and that was the worst part — Elodie noticed everything by pretending to notice nothing.

"I'm tired."

"Mm." Elodie bit the end of a knot tight. "When we're down, there's a town called Sorrel Ford. Hot springs. Terrible food. You can sleep for a week." She glanced up then, and her eyes were the color of the glacier pools, and they went through Wren like water through cloth. "Whatever's weighing on you, kid — set it down before the summit does it for you. Mountains don't negotiate."

Wren's throat closed. She nodded, or made a motion like nodding, and Elodie turned to cross, and Wren stood alone on the near bank with the cairn at her shoulder and the lamp inside it cold and waiting, and the choice sat on her chest like a boulder.

If she lit it, the men would come to the bridge. But the others would already be across. They'd cut the ropes. The men would be stuck on this side — no, no, that was wrong, that was the lie she kept telling herself, because the men had ropes and gear and time, and what lighting the lamp actually bought her was a place at their fire tonight, and a ride down, and paper with her mother's address on it, and freedom from the sound of Marta's wet breathing and the weight of a secret that got heavier every hour she didn't set it down.

If she didn't light it, she stayed a fugitive. Sixteen years old, wanted by nobody officially, protected by nobody officially, attached to people whose enemies shot widows on icefields.

Her hand was already moving toward the cairn before she decided anything. That was the horror of it, what she'd understand later — there was no decision, just her body obeying three days of fear, kneeling at the stones, lifting the top ones aside, taking out the little storm lantern with its wick trimmed and its reservoir full. Someone had prepared this. Someone had thought about her, planned for her, valued her enough to leave a light burning in the wilderness.

Nobody had ever planned for her before. Not even her mother, really. Not in the way that counted.

The match caught on the second strike. The wick took the flame with a soft sigh, and Wren set the lamp in the gap in the stones, and the light was so small, so absurdly small against all that gray morning, that for one moment she thought — good, it won't work, they won't see it, I haven't done anything —

Then she stood up, walked fast to the bridge, and stepped onto the planks, and did not look back.

* * *

Halfway across, the shooting started.

It came from upriver — not at them, not yet, just into the air, three cracks that rolled down the gorge like the mountain applauding. Wren froze on the planks. Below her feet the river roared through its slot, close enough to touch, close enough to swallow.

"Move!" Elodie's voice, from the far bank, stripped of everything soft. "Wren — *move!*"

She moved. She ran the last of the bridge on legs made of wire and terror, and hands pulled her off the planks — Corvin's hands, then Brase's, hauling her behind the pylon as bullets began to sing off the stone, sharp insect sounds followed by the flat crack of the shots arriving late.

"Rope!" Elodie shouted. "Cut the anchor — Corvin, knife!"

Corvin sawed at the near-side anchor line while rounds chewed the pylon above their heads, stone dust snowing down. But the bridge wasn't anchored only where they stood; Wren saw it in one flashing glance — the far cables ran to iron rings bolted into the opposite pylon, and those rings were old, rusted, and the men coming down the near bank were already at the bridgehead, four of them, five, a sixth kneeling with a rifle.

"They'll repair it," Brase said. His voice was calm in the way of a man reading a weather report. "Those bolts are soft. Give them an hour, they'll have a line across."

"Then we take the hour and run—"

"We don't have an hour." Brase was looking at the bridge, at the sag of it, at the men swarming onto the near end. Wren watched him do the same math Elodie did, the terrible fluent math of mountains and margins, and arrive somewhere different. "Planks are soaked. Cable's tarred hemp on the main span." He shrugged off his pack, and from the side pocket he drew the coil of fuse cord he'd been carrying since the icefield, since the recovery team, since a decision he'd apparently been walking toward for days. "Somebody has to hold the far end while it catches."

"No," Elodie said.

"Somebody light and somebody hold," Brase said, over her, gentle and immovable. "I'm the only one who knows how long tarred hemp takes. And I'm the slowest of us now anyway." He glanced down at his leg — wrenched in the serac collapse, lamed since chapter upon chapter of descent, though he'd never once complained. "You all knew this math too. Don't insult me by pretending otherwise."

"You are not—" Elodie started.

A bullet spanged off the iron ring beside her head. From the bridgehead, a bullhorn voice: *"Delacroix! Step away! Last offer — the girl walks, the rest of you freeze where you are!"*

*The girl walks.* Wren's stomach turned over. They were talking about her. She was a term in their equation, a variable to be extracted, and the lamp in the cairn — her lamp, her match, her trembling hand — had been the extraction point. She looked at Brase and he was already looking at her, and his face did something she'd never seen it do. It softened.

"It's all right," he said. Just that. As if he knew — as if he'd known for days, the way he knew rope and ice and load-bearing weight, the way he knew everything about what holds and what fails. Maybe he'd seen her at the cairn. Maybe he'd seen the lamp. Maybe it didn't matter, because whatever he saw when he looked at her, he wasn't holding it against her. "Sixteen is too young to carry a thing like this. Put it down. I've got it."

"I'm sorry," Wren said, and her voice came out tiny, a child's voice, drowned instantly by the river. "I lit it, I'm sorry, I didn't—"

"I know." He said it simply, without cruelty, the way you'd confirm a bearing. "I know, girl. Go on now."

He stepped out onto the bridge.

* * *

What happened next, Wren would carry the rest of her life, and it would never get lighter, only more precisely remembered.

Brase walked onto the span with the fuse cord unspooling from his fist and a striker in his other hand, limping, unhurried, straight toward the men advancing from the far end. They stopped. Whatever they'd expected — surrender, panic, a hostage — it wasn't a lame ex-miner strolling toward them with fire in his hand, and for three full seconds nobody shot.

He struck the fuse at the midpoint of the bridge. Flame ran both directions along the cord, twin sparks hurrying toward the soaked, tarred, ancient cable at either end, and Brase sat down on the planks, straddling the span like a boy on a fence rail, and took out his pipe.

He'd never smoked it the whole trip. Wren realized she'd been watching it in his pocket for days, wondering.

*"BRASE!"* Elodie's scream tore across the gorge.

He raised one hand — *stay back* — and tamped the pipe, and lit it off the burning fuse, and the first of the men on the bridgehead seemed to understand all at once what was happening and started shooting, and Brase rocked with the impacts but did not fall, did not spill the flame, held the cord down against the cable with his palm as if pinning a snake.

The main span caught with a whump of blue fire. Tarred hemp burns like a grudge. Flame raced along the cables in both directions, and the bridge groaned, and the planks began to drop away section by section into the roar below, and Brase sat in the middle of it smoking his pipe, and through the smoke and the noise he turned his head and looked back at them once — at Elodie, at Corvin, at Wren — and Wren would swear until her dying day that he smiled.

Then the cables parted, and the bridge folded, and Brase went down with it into the gray-green violence of the river, and the mountain took him the way it took everything, completely and without comment.

* * *

Afterward there was running. Wren's body did it without her — Elodie's fist knotted in her collar, hauling her up the far slope while the recovery team shouted uselessly on the broken bank, their bridge gone, their quarry dissolving into the trees. Somewhere behind them Corvin was laughing or sobbing or both. The rifle fire dwindled, then stopped. There was nothing left to shoot at but distance.

They ran until the gorge was a wound behind them and the trees closed overhead, and then Elodie stopped, and set Marta's litter down with unbearable gentleness, and stood with her back to all of them, shoulders shaking once, twice, and then still.

Wren sat down in the pine needles and put her face in her hands.

"He knew," she said. Her voice came out shredded. "At the bridge. He knew what I did. He knew and he — why would he—"

"Because that's what he was for." Elodie turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, and her voice was level in the way of ice under load — holding, but you could hear the strain singing through it. "He spent years being paid to betray people. I think he'd decided this trip was where he paid it back. All of it. At once." She crouched in front of Wren, and Wren braced for the verdict, the fury, the banishment she deserved.

Instead Elodie took her chin in one rough hand and lifted it, and looked at her the way she looked at cornices — assessing whether the thing could bear weight.

"You lit the lamp," Elodie said.

"Yes."

"And you crossed anyway. Toward us. You could have stayed on that bank and walked to them."

Wren hadn't thought of it that way. She'd crossed because crossing was the direction everyone else was going, because standing alone on the wrong side of a gorge was unthinkable, because — she understood it suddenly, fully, the way you understand a fall only after landing — because some part of her had already chosen, days ago, on the ice, when she'd grabbed Corvin's wrist in the dark. The lamp had been fear. The crossing had been the truth.

"I'm sorry," she said again, and meant it down to the marrow, and knew it changed nothing, and said it anyway.

"Yeah." Elodie released her chin. "Me too. About all of it." She stood, and looked back the way they'd come, toward the broken bridge and the river and the man in it, and when she spoke again her voice had that flattened quality Wren had learned meant grief being converted, efficiently and at cost, into fuel. "He bought us the crossing. We don't waste it. Up. Both of you. Marta needs a valley and a doctor, and we've got maybe six hours before they find the ford downstream."

They moved. Wren fell in at the rear, the traditional place of shame, and Corvin dropped back beside her without a word and matched her stride.

"For what it's worth," he said quietly, after a kilometer of silence, "the men who offered you amnesty were going to bury you in a file marked *loose end*. I've read their files. I used to write adjacent ones." He glanced sideways at her. "Brase knew that better than any of us. That's why he didn't hesitate."

Ahead of them, Elodie crested a rise and stopped dead, one arm thrown up in the universal gesture of halt. Wren's stomach dropped — tracks, ambush, the end of everything — but when she scrambled up beside her, Elodie was staring not backward but forward, down into the next valley, where the trees opened onto a sight that made no sense this deep in forbidden country: a thread of woodsmoke, rising thin and domestic and unmistakable from a fold of land below.

Smoke meant people. People meant either salvation or Halvern.

"There's no village on any map of this valley," Elodie said slowly. "Which means it's either a patrol camp..." She paused, and Wren saw her hand tighten on her ice axe. "...or it's the valley. Lior's valley. The one Marta named."

The smoke rose, patient as a question. Elodie started down toward it.
