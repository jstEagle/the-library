## 18. The Valley of Names

The river told them the way.

It had been telling them for two days, since the bridge burned behind them and Brase with it — a grey-green thread running south through country no map admitted to, and every kilometer it descended, the air grew warmer and thicker with the smell of living things. Pine resin. Wet stone. Somewhere below, woodsmoke that was not their own.

Elodie stopped at the lip of the gorge and let the others catch up. Her legs had stopped being legs somewhere around noon; they were instruments that did what she asked and sent back complaints she ignored. Across her chest, wrapped in oilcloth inside her pack, the ledger rode against her heartbeat like a second heart.

"There." Corvin came up beside her, breathing hard, and pointed with his chin because his hands were too raw to gesture with. "Smoke."

Wren scrambled past them both, dropped to her belly at the edge, and hung half over it with the fearlessness of the young and recently bereaved. "Roofs," she said. "I can see roofs."

Elodie looked. Below them, where the river widened into a valley floor, terraces stepped down the slopes like the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal. Fields, dark with late planting. A cluster of low buildings with sod roofs, and beyond them — her breath caught — a stand of fruit trees in blossom, absurd and white against all that green, as if spring had been waiting here all along for someone to arrive.

A village. The village. She knew it before she knew it: the curve of the river, the shoulder of the mountain behind it, the shape of the terraces. She had seen it in photographs taken forty years ago from the open door of a plane, in images that ended in fire.

"Marta's valley," she said quietly.

No one answered. There was nothing to answer. Marta's grave was three days behind them, a cairn of river stones under a sky that hadn't stopped snowing until this morning, and the name she'd given them with her last strength — *Ashkenu, the Ashken people, tell them Anselm kept faith* — was the only inheritance she'd left besides a bullet wound and a widow's stubborn love.

"Elodie." Wren had come back to her side. The girl's face was smudged with soot from the bridge fire, and there was a new stillness in her that hadn't been there a month ago, when she'd been a runaway with stolen boots and a mouth full of defiance. "What if they don't believe us?"

"Then we show them the ledger," Elodie said.

"What if they don't want it?"

Elodie had been asking herself the same question since the icefield, since the blood, since the night in the crevasse when survival had stripped them all down to wire and truth. What do you do with a thing like this? A book of names. Forty years old. Every page a person who had been erased so thoroughly that even the mountain had been paid to forget them.

"Then we ask them what they want done with it," she said. "That's the whole point, Wren. It was never ours."

She led them down.

* * *

They were seen long before they arrived. Of course they were. Three ragged figures descending out of the forbidden north — filthy, frostbitten, one of them limping, one of them carrying a camera he'd stopped pretending was for scenery — would have been spotted by any child on the terraces.

But no one came out to meet them. That was the first strange thing. Elodie had expected runners, spears, dogs, barricades — the welcome you got in border country when you came uninvited. Instead the fields emptied slowly and silently, the way water drains from a sink, and by the time they reached the first terrace wall the valley had gone quiet in a way that made the hair rise on her arms.

Not hostile silence. Waiting silence.

An old woman stood at the gate of the largest house. She was small and straight-backed, wrapped in a shawl the color of rust, and her hair was white as the glacier they'd crossed a lifetime ago. Behind her, filling doorways and windows and the spaces between the fruit trees, stood perhaps sixty people. Old men. Mothers with children on their hips. Teenagers with their grandparents' eyes.

None of them spoke.

Elodie stopped ten paces away. Her body wanted to kneel — exhaustion, maybe, or something older than exhaustion — but she stayed standing, because Marta had told her something about these people once, in a whisper, in the dark of a crevasse camp: *they are not a broken people, whatever Halvern wanted. They are a patient one.*

"We've come from the high passes," Elodie said. Her voice cracked on the altitude of the moment. "From the wreck of an expedition plane. We were sent by a dying woman named Marta Voss, whose husband—" she faltered, steadied, "—whose husband traded with your people, years ago. And we were sent by a man called Lior."

At the name, the silence changed.

It was the old woman who moved. She came forward slowly, leaning on a stick of black wood, and stopped an arm's length away, and looked up into Elodie's face with eyes gone pale as winter ice but not yet blind.

"Lior," she repeated. The word came out shaped differently than Elodie had ever heard it — softer, rounder, a name spoken in a mother tongue before being handed over to the world. "You knew him?"

"He found me in the mountains. He was dying." Elodie's throat closed. She forced the words through. "He'd been shot. He gave me a map and made me promise to bring something home. I didn't know what home meant. Not then."

The old woman turned her head. From the crowd behind her, a man stepped forward — middle-aged, heavyset, with Lior's same broad hands and none of his weathering. He was crying without any noise at all, tears simply falling off his jaw, and Elodie understood before anyone said it.

Her brother.

"My brother went up that mountain thirty-one years ago," the man said. His voice was steady the way a rope is steady under load. "Our father went before him, twenty years before that, looking for the plane. My uncle after him. The company's men watched the passes. Anyone who went looking didn't come back, or came back wrong." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, almost angrily. "Lior said he'd found another way. A guide's route. He said there was a woman the company couldn't buy because they'd already ruined her."

The words landed in Elodie's chest like a pick driven into ice.

*They'd already ruined her.* Her blacklist. Her ruin. The disaster that wasn't hers alone, the hearings, the whispered accusations, the towns that closed their doors — she had spent six years believing it was punishment for failure. It had been preparation. They had broken her in advance, the way you break a trail, so that when the moment came, the one person desperate enough to take a stranger's map would be a person no one would believe.

"They used me," she said aloud. Not a question.

"They chose you," the old woman corrected gently. "There is a difference. A chosen thing can choose back."

Elodie slid the pack off her shoulders. Her fingers had gone clumsy with cold and feeling, and it took her three tries to work the buckles, and nobody helped her, because everyone understood that this had to be done alone.

The oilcloth came away. Beneath it, the ledger.

It was smaller than she remembered — a surveyor's field book, canvas-bound, its corners worn soft as cloth. The expedition's stamp was still legible on the cover, half obliterated by burn scars. She had carried it over a glacier, through a whiteout, down a burning bridge, past a man's death and a woman's, and now it weighed nothing at all in her hands.

"The pilot kept it," she said. "And photographs. Records of what the expedition saw before—" She stopped. Before the company burned your village. Before the massacre. Before forty years of silence bought with mining money and enforced by men like the ones chasing us right now. "Before everything. Marta said to tell you: Anselm kept faith."

The old woman took the ledger the way you take a newborn. With both hands. With her breath held.

She opened it there in the gateway, standing in the light, and read.

Elodie watched her face and understood that she was watching forty years happen to a single human being in the space of minutes. Grief first — the old woman's lips moving over names, and around her the crowd pressing closer, and here and there a voice breaking loose: *that's my grandmother. That's my father's brother. I was named for her.* Then rage, rising through the crowd like heat through a floor. Then, strangest of all, a kind of terrible relief — the sound of a people who had been told for four decades that they were crazy, that the fire was an accident, that the village that vanished had never held as many souls as their own elders remembered, finally hearing the paper say: *you were right. You were always right. Here are the names. Here is the proof. You are not crazy. You are survivors.*

The old woman closed the ledger and pressed it to her sternum.

"Forty years," she said, to no one and everyone. "We planted every spring. We buried our dead with their true names so the mountain would remember them even if the world forgot. And we waited." She looked up at Elodie, and her pale eyes were dry and blazing. "My granddaughter asked me last winter why we keep waiting. I told her: because someday someone will come down out of the snow carrying the truth, and it will be heavy, and they will be nearly dead, and we will know them by that."

She reached out and took Elodie's frost-burned hand in both of hers.

"You are known," she said. "All of you. Come in. Eat. Rest. The dead can wait one more night — they have waited long enough to be good at it."

* * *

They fed them trout from the river and flatbread baked in stones, and tea that tasted of pine and honey, and Wren ate until she fell asleep sitting upright against a wall, mid-sentence, with a piece of bread still in her fist. Corvin sat apart with the village's headman, showing him the transmitted pages on his battered satellite terminal, explaining in careful words what he'd already sent up the ridge and what it meant: that the story was live, that it could not be recalled, that Halvern's lawyers were at this very moment discovering what it felt like to be outrun.

Elodie barely ate. She sat near the fire in the longhouse while the village gathered around the old woman — whose name, she learned, was Sefeya, and who had been eight years old the night the planes came — and listened to the ledger read aloud, name by name, in the Ashken tongue and then in the trade language, each name followed by a murmur from the crowd, a relationship, a memory, a correction. *He had a limp. She sang better than the river. They were married only a season.*

Somewhere past midnight, Sefeya settled beside her with two cups of tea and pressed one into Elodie's hands.

"You knew him," Sefeya said. "Lior. At the end."

"For an hour. Maybe less." Elodie stared into the fire. "He was shot and freezing and he talked like a man spending his last coin carefully. He made me swear two things. Bring the ledger out. Tell no one what it found." She laughed, and it came out broken. "I kept the first. I broke the second. People died helping me carry it, and I decided the dead deserved better than a secret."

Sefeya sipped her tea, unhurried. "And which promise do you think he meant more?"

Elodie opened her mouth. Closed it.

"He was my grandson's age when he left," Sefeya said. "Stubborn. Clever with ropes. He used to say the mountain keeps secrets the way a river keeps fish — you can hold one in your hands, but you cannot hold the whole river." She turned her pale eyes on Elodie. "He did not send you here to bury the truth in our grief, child. He sent it here so it could grow. A seed in a locked box rots. A seed in the ground becomes a forest. Whatever Halvern does next — and they will do something, men like that always come down the mountain angry — this village will not be silent again. That is what he bought with his life. That is what Marta bought with hers. Do not mourn that you broke the small promise. Mourn only the ones who paid for the large one."

Elodie looked across the fire at Wren, asleep. At Corvin, hunched over his terminal, sending one last transmission with the concentration of a man defusing something. At the crowd of faces lit gold by the flames, holding names like lanterns.

Grief transfigured. She had heard the phrase before, in books, and thought it pretty nonsense. Sitting here, she understood it at last: not the end of sorrow, but sorrow put to work, sorrow given a job, sorrow hauling stone alongside you until the wall stands and the wind stops getting through.

"I'll stay," she heard herself say. "As long as you need. I know the northern approaches. I know how Halvern's recovery teams move — I've spent the last month being hunted by one. If they come down into this valley, they come down against someone who knows the ground."

Sefeya smiled, slow and old. "We hoped you'd say that. Though I think," she added, nodding toward the dark doorway, toward the north, "the decision may be closer than any of us would like."

Elodie followed her gaze outside, into the cold clear night.

High on the shoulder of the mountain, where the burned bridge had been, where nothing should have been moving at all — lights. A chain of them, descending switchback by switchback, patient as a glacier, coming down the valley road.

Halvern had found the river.
