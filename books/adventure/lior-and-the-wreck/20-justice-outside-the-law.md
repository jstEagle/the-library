## 20. Justice Outside the Law

The valley did not sleep that night, and neither did I.

Word of Corvin's transmission had come down from the ridge like weather — first a boy running with a scrap of paper, then an old woman reading it aloud in the square by firelight, then the whole village talking at once in their liquid, consonant-heavy tongue that I was only beginning to pick apart. The ledger pages were live. Halvern's name was in the open, tied to the burned village, tied to the photographs I had carried out of a frozen plane, tied to me.

By morning there were journalists at the valley mouth. Not many — three, maybe four, the ones close enough and hungry enough to move fast on Corvin's signal — but they came with satellite phones and cameras and the particular gleam of people who can smell a story that will define them. The villagers let them in the way you'd let rain into a house whose roof you were already tearing off: deliberately, because it served.

I stood at the edge of the square watching Marta's blanket-wrapped body lie in state under the great carved post, and I understood something with the clarity of altitude: no court would touch this. Not quickly. Halvern had lawyers the way glaciers have ice — massed, ancient, indifferent to anything that struck them. A filing would take years. An inquiry would take decades. The men who ordered the massacre would die comfortable before a subpoena reached them.

But there are other kinds of law. Older ones. The kind that lives in valleys and graveyards and the faces of survivors.

* * *

It was Sef who said it first — the village headman's daughter, the one who had waited thirty years for her grandfather's name to come home. She found me at dawn on the third day, standing where I always stood now, near Marta's cairn-in-progress of stacked river stones.

"You carried them out," she said. Her English was accented but precise; she'd worked a season in a coastal hotel before coming home. "The names. You kept your promise."

"Half of it."

"All of it that mattered." She looked at me the way mountain people look at weather — assessing what I could bear. "The director of Halvern is coming."

I turned. "Coming here?"

"He has been seen at the mine road. Two trucks, men with guns. He thinks he is coming to bury this." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "We think he should come further. To the graves. To the cameras. To us."

"It won't work," I said, though even as I said it I felt the idea take root the way Lior's map had taken root in me — unwanted, undeniable. "Men like that don't break. They buy their way out. They—"

"He broke once already," Sef said. "Thirty years ago, when he signed the order. Men who break once will break again if you put them in the right place." She nodded toward the high valley above the village, where the old graves lay under cairns no one had been permitted to visit since the burning. "You know mountains, Elodie Delacroix. You know what happens to a man when the ground itself testifies against him."

I knew. I had watched it happen to clients a hundred times — the moment the landscape stripped away everything they'd built themselves out of, and they stood revealed as whatever small frightened animal lived underneath. Some of them wept. Some of them raged. All of them changed.

"We'll need him to walk," I said slowly. "Not ride. If he comes up in a truck he stays armored. He has to climb. He has to hurt. He has to stand where they died and feel how far it is from anywhere that would help him."

Sef studied me. "You would guide him?"

"I would guide him," I said, and heard in my own voice the thing I'd been afraid I'd lost — the flat certainty of a woman who knows exactly where she's going and why. "Straight up. No shortcuts."

* * *

Halvern's convoy reached the valley mouth at midday, and the whole village turned out to watch it come — not hostilely, not loudly. They simply stood along the track in silence, hundreds of them, old and young, and let the trucks roll past a corridor of faces. It was, I thought, more terrifying than any mob. You cannot disperse grief that doesn't shout.

I met the trucks at the foot of the trail with Corvin at my shoulder, camera rolling, and Brase's replacement — one of the village hunters, a quiet man called Odan — standing behind me with a rifle slung the way farmers sling them, casual as a scythe.

Aldous Vane-Halvern climbed down from the lead truck looking exactly like his company's letterhead: silver-haired, immaculate even after two days' drive, a man whose wrinkles had been pressed in rather than earned. His security detail fanned out — six of them, armed, nervous. Their eyes kept sliding to the silent crowd, to the ridge lines, to me.

"Miss Delacroix." He knew my name. Of course he did. "I'm told you have something of my company's."

"I have nothing of yours," I said. "Everything here belongs to them." I tipped my chin at the village. "You're not going to find a ledger to burn, Mr. Vane-Halvern. It's already on every screen in the world. What's left is for you to decide what kind of man arrives at the end of this."

His jaw tightened. Behind him, one of his security men murmured something about the road back, about extraction windows, and I watched the director do the arithmetic — the story live, the evidence public, the only move left being damage control or flight. Flight would confirm everything. Even he could see it.

"What do you want?" he asked.

"To show you something." I turned toward the trail. "It's a four-hour climb. You'll go on foot. Your men can carry your pack."

"And if I refuse?"

Corvin lifted his camera slightly, so the lens caught the light. "Then tomorrow's headline writes itself," he said pleasantly. "*Halvern Director Flees Survivors' Valley.* I've got the shot framed already. You've got a good profile for it."

Vane-Halvern looked at the mountain. Then at the crowd of faces. Then, briefly, at me — and I saw him recognize me properly for the first time, the blacklisted guide, the loose end he'd helped snip off years ago, now standing between him and the only exit.

"Four hours," he said.

* * *

He lasted ninety minutes before the mountain started working on him.

I set a deliberate pace — not cruel, but honest. The trail to the high graves was one the villagers still walked, worn smooth in places, steep in others, crossing two snowfields that required care. I gave him a pole. I gave him water. I did not give him sympathy.

His security came too, all six, and discovered within the hour that rifles are heavy at altitude and guilt is heavier. One of them, a broad-faced man who'd been a soldier once, began helping the older villagers carry their bundles without being asked. I marked him and said nothing.

Vane-Halvern talked for the first hour. That was his armor — words, framing, the executive's reflex of narrating himself into the hero of any room. He spoke of difficult eras, of operational contexts, of regrettable excesses committed by regional personnel. Corvin filmed all of it, saying nothing, letting the camera be the witness that never blinks.

At the second snowfield, the talking stopped.

It stopped because Sef was waiting at the top of the pitch, and she did not speak either. She simply stepped aside and gestured upward, and Vane-Halvern climbed past her, and she fell in behind him, and for the next forty minutes the only sounds were boots and breath and the small wet collapse of spring snow.

The graves sit in a cirque below the old village site — the original village, the burned one, its charred foundation stones still visible through the grass after thirty years. There are perhaps sixty cairns. Some are just stones. Some, the newer ones, hold bones that descendants carried back from wherever the dead had fallen. At the center stands a post taller than a man, carved top to bottom with names in the village script, and beneath each name a small notch for each year the name went unspoken.

Sixty cairns. And the ledger in Corvin's pack held more names than there were cairns.

We arranged them without discussion — it happened the way good rope teams move, everyone finding their place. The villagers formed a crescent facing the graves. The cameras faced Vane-Halvern. I stood beside the director, close enough to catch him if he fell, far enough to let him fall alone.

"Look at them," Sef said quietly. "Say their names. You signed the order. You can read."

For a long moment he didn't move. The wind moved instead, lifting the prayer flags strung between cairns, snapping them like small applause. Then Aldous Vane-Halvern stepped forward, one step, onto the ground where the dead lay, and I watched the last of the letterhead fall away from his face.

What was underneath was an old man. Just an old man, breathing hard, standing in grass over sixty holes in the world.

"They were going to file suit," he said. His voice came out strange, thinned by altitude or something else. "The village elders. Thirty years ago. They'd retained a lawyer in the provincial capital. The mine was eighteen months from insolvency — the whole company was. If the claim had gone forward—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "There was a survey flight. The expedition. They were gathering testimony. Photographs. I authorized the recovery team to intercept the evidence. I was told the flight went down in a storm. I was told there were no survivors." His eyes went to me. "I was told the guide had caused it. That she'd been paid."

"I wasn't," I said. "But you knew that. You signed the blacklist yourself, didn't you? My name crossed your desk."

He didn't answer, which was an answer.

"The pilot survived," Sef said. "Lior. He crawled out of that wreck and walked two hundred kilometers home to us, and he spent thirty years trying to get back to the plane, and when his body finally failed him he found someone stubborn enough to finish it." She gestured at me without ceremony. "You could not kill the truth. You only delayed it. And delay has a price, and the price is standing in front of you."

Vane-Halvern turned to the carved post. He read the names. I watched his lips move through the first few, the foreign syllables clumsy in his mouth, and then stop moving altogether, because somewhere in the list he must have found the ones he knew — the elders' names from the old legal correspondence, the ones from his own company's suppressed files. Names he had known as obstacles. Names that belonged to bodies under stones.

His knees went.

I caught him — professional habit, hands under his arms before I'd decided to move — and held him while he shook. He was crying without sound, which is somehow worse than the other kind, and around us the crescent of villagers neither advanced nor retreated. They simply witnessed. That was the sentence, I understood. Not prison, not yet, maybe not ever. Witness. Being seen, completely, by the people he had tried to erase, on the ground where he had tried to erase them.

"I didn't pull a trigger," he whispered.

"No," Sef agreed. "You signed a piece of paper, and men pulled triggers, and planes fell out of the sky, and a woman lost everything she had because she was convenient. The paper was yours." She crouched to his level, so their eyes met. "Now say it to the camera. All of it. Or spend the rest of your life as the man who wouldn't, and let the world imagine something worse — because the world will, Mr. Director. We will make sure of it."

* * *

He talked for eleven minutes.

Corvin filmed every second. I stood off-frame with my arms folded against the cold and listened to a powerful man dismantle himself sentence by sentence — the authorization, the cover-up, the blacklist, the recovery teams, the bounty on anyone seeking the wreck. He named names. Regional managers. The security chief who'd run the interception. The fixer who'd hired journalists to discredit survivors — Corvin flinched almost invisibly at that part, and I pretended not to see. When it was done, when the camera light finally blinked off, Vane-Halvern sat on a cairn like any exhausted climber and asked, in a voice scraped raw:

"What happens to me now?"

"That's not ours to say," Sef told him. "That's for courts, eventually. Maybe. But first—" she nodded at the post, at the cairns, at the living crescent of her people "—first you listen. Every name. All sixty-one. Out loud. Until you can say them without stumbling. That's the toll for this ground. Everyone pays it."

So he did. It took until the light went long and gold across the cirque. The villagers corrected his pronunciation without cruelty, patiently, again and again, until each name came out true. By the end his voice was wrecked and the old women were nodding, not in forgiveness — I don't think anyone in that cirque mistook it for forgiveness — but in acknowledgment that something had been said that could not be unsaid.

We descended in the dark, torches strung along the line like a procession. Halfway down, the broad-faced security man — the ex-soldier — fell into step beside me.

"I was on the recovery team," he said quietly. "Eight years ago. We burned a cache of documents outside the mine office. I've thought about it every day since." He looked ahead at the torches. "When this goes to trial, whatever happens — I'll testify. Others will too. He opened the door. People are walking through it."

Below us, the valley lights came on one by one, and somewhere among them Corvin was already uploading, and somewhere beyond the mountains the world was waking up to a story it could no longer pretend it hadn't heard.

I stood a moment on the trail and let myself feel it — not triumph, nothing so clean as that, but a settling, like snow finding its angle of rest. Justice hadn't arrived the way the brochures promise, with gavels and verdicts. It had arrived the way weather arrives in high country: inevitable, unstoppable, indifferent to what it destroyed on the way through.

And it wasn't finished. Because in the morning, Sef took me aside and pressed a folded paper into my hand — a page torn from a notebook, in a shaky hand I recognized from the margin notes on Lior's map.

"There is something else he wanted brought home," she said. "Something he couldn't fit in the ledger. And it isn't in the valley."
