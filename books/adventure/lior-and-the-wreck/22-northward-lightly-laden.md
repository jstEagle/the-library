## 22. Northward, Lightly Laden

The letter came in spring, riding the first mail sledge of the season into Thornevale with a smell of woodsmoke and something green clinging to the paper — a smell that did not belong to mountains at all.

Elodie was on the porch of the guide house when it arrived, mending a rope with a fid and a pot of resin, her hands moving through the old patterns without thought. The boy — Tam, though he'd finally given her his real name in February, whispered over a stove: *Tomasz* — came out barefoot in the snow-melt to see who'd come, and stood beside her chair while she turned the envelope over.

No return address. Just a single line of ink on the front, in a hand she knew now as well as her own: *For the woman who carried them home.*

"You could open it slower," Tomasz said.

"I could," she agreed, and didn't.

Inside, folded twice around a pressed sprig of some flowering thing she couldn't name, was a page written by three different hands. The first, careful and formal, in the script of a man who had learned letters late:

*Sister Elodie. The valley is green again where they said nothing would grow for a hundred years. We have built the school your friend Corvin promised, and the children learn both our names and yours. My grandmother says you must stop apologizing in every letter or she will come down the mountain herself and scold you in front of everyone.*

The second hand was younger, looser, almost laughing off the page:

*And we are holding you to it. You said you'd come back when the pass opened. The pass is open. The river is low. There is no excuse left, and my mother has already started arguing about which roof you'll sleep under, so really you have no choice.*

The third hand was old, trembling, each character set down like a stone placed on a cairn:

*You are welcome here always. This is not politeness. This is law older than any company's law. Come home.*

Elodie read it three times. On the third reading her eyes stopped working properly, and Tomasz pretended very hard to be interested in the far end of the valley until she cleared her throat and handed him the page.

He read it standing, then sitting, then read it aloud just to hear it, his voice cracking on the last line the way it still cracked on everything that mattered. He was sixteen now by his own best guess, taller than her by half a head, and he had filled out through the shoulders that winter hauling loads for the supply trains. But when he looked up from the letter his face was still the face of the kid who had nearly walked into the void on the Verren, who had signed his name to a signal fire at a bridge because he was terrified, who had spent a whole summer learning how to live with what he'd done.

"We should go," he said.

"The season starts in three weeks."

"After the season, then."

"After the season," she said, and took the letter back, and pressed the sprig between the pages of the ledger — no, not the ledger; the ledger lived in the valley now, in a case of glass and cedar in the new school, where children touched its pages every day so the names would stay warm. She pressed the sprig into her own battered route book instead, the one with Lior's map still ghosted faintly against the inside cover even after the burning, ash-shadowed lines like a scar that had decided to become a map of itself.

* * *

She guided again now. Legally. Quietly.

The word *quietly* had taken some doing. When the Halvern story broke — Corvin's dispatches running in eleven papers across two countries, the photographs reproduced until even people who had never seen a mountain knew the shape of that burned village — her name had been dragged up out of the mud along with everything else. The blacklist hadn't been formally lifted so much as it had simply ceased to exist, dissolved in scandal the way ice dissolves in a warm river: not all at once, but thoroughly, and without apology. One morning there was simply no list anymore, and the licensing board in Thornevale issued her papers with the flat bureaucratic courtesy of people who had never blacklisted anyone in their lives, certainly not her, and would prefer it if she didn't make a scene.

She hadn't made a scene. She'd taken the papers, walked out into the street, and stood in the sun for a long time doing absolutely nothing, which was its own kind of scene, if you knew how to look.

Her clients these days were few and chosen. She ran small parties — never more than four — on routes she picked herself, and she had a rule she explained on the first evening of every trip, sitting by the stove with her hands wrapped around a tin cup: *I will tell you the truth about this mountain, including the parts that frighten me. If you want a guide who pretends the mountain is safe, hire someone else.* Some of them hired someone else. The ones who stayed were the ones worth guiding.

They asked about the Verren sometimes. Everyone did. She gave them the short version — storm, wreck, ledger, descent — the way you'd tell a story about other people, other lives. And in a way it was true. The woman who had come down the south face carrying Marta on her back and a dead man's promise in her pocket was not entirely the woman who sat on this porch mending ropes. That woman had been made of dread and debt and the grinding fear of being unwanted everywhere. This one was made of something else. Lighter. Not unscarred — the nights when the wind came off the glacier with a certain frequency still put her back in the crevasse camp, listening to Marta breathe shallower and shallower — but the scars had changed jobs. They used to be walls. Now they were windows.

Marta's cairn stood above the tree line on the shoulder of the Verren, where the party had carried her stones on the way down — all of them, even Tomasz, even Brase's share carried double because Brase wasn't there to carry his own. They'd carved her name deep, and beneath it the words Elodie had chosen after a long night of thinking: *SHE READ THE NAMES ALOUD.* Because that was what Marta had done, in the end, with the last of her strength — not died quietly, but made Elodie swear, made her repeat the descendants' names back syllable by syllable until she got them right, turning her own death into a delivery instruction.

Every June, before the season began, Elodie climbed up alone and rebuilt the cairn where winter had knocked it loose. It was the one ascent she made without clients, without purpose, without anything except the going-up. She talked to Marta while she worked. Told her about the season ahead, the clients, the boy. Last June she'd told her about the letter from the valley, and the wind had come up off the ice in a long sighing rush exactly then, and she had chosen to believe that was an answer.

Brase she visited differently. There was no cairn for Brase — the river took him, and the bridge burned with him, and the recovery team's report listed him as an unidentified casualty of the collapse, which was, Elodie thought, the only lie Halvern ever told that she was grateful for. He'd wanted to disappear into the mountain that had employed him. Instead he'd disappeared defending the people he'd been paid to betray, and if that wasn't a good death then Elodie didn't know what the phrase was for. She kept his ice axe in the guide house, hung on the wall by the door where every client saw it and most asked. *A friend's,* she said. *He held a bridge.* Nobody ever needed more than that.

Corvin had gone east, to the cities, where his byline now opened doors instead of closing them. He wrote twice a month, long letters full of other people's scandals, and always ended the same way: *Still waiting for you to let me write your version. Still taking no for an answer. — C.* She suspected she'd keep saying no forever, and that he'd keep asking forever, and that this arrangement suited them both. Some stories belonged to the people who'd bled for them. Hers was one.

* * *

The season opened mild. Her first party was a pair of schoolteachers from the lowlands, wide-eyed and game, and Tomasz led them through the moraine field on the second day with such crisp competence that Elodie hung back and let him, walking sweep, watching him check their footing and call the hazards in the exact cadence she used — her own voice coming back at her from a sixteen-year-old's chest, strange and moving, like hearing a song you wrote sung by someone who'd survive you.

On the fourth day, high on the shoulder below the old cairn, one of the teachers — the younger one, a woman named Ilsa with sunburned cheeks and a laugh like breaking crockery — fell into step beside her during a rest halt.

"Can I ask you something improper?"

"Those are usually the best questions."

"Why do you do this? You could write a book. You could sit on panels in the capital and be famous and rich. People would pay just to hear you talk." She gestured at the trail, the load, the thin cold air. "Instead you carry strangers' packs up a hill for coin."

Elodie considered the question honestly, which was her policy with improper questions. Below them Tomasz was showing the other teacher how to read a cornice, arms wide, patient as a saint.

"When I was blacklisted," she said, "the worst part wasn't losing the work. It was that the mountain stopped belonging to me. Every route I'd ever climbed, every camp, every dawn — all of it was taken away and given a story that wasn't true. For two years I couldn't look at snow without flinching." She turned her cup in her hands. "Guiding is how I take it back. Not all at once. One client at a time. One honest day at a time. The mountain doesn't owe me anything, and I don't owe it anything either. We're just... on speaking terms again."

Ilsa looked at her for a moment. "That's the most improper answer I've ever gotten," she said, delighted. "Thank you."

That evening, camped under a sky rinsed clean by wind, Elodie sat outside her tent long after the clients slept, watching stars wheel over the Verren's dark shoulder. Somewhere up there was the wreck. The recovery teams had gone in after the scandal broke and stripped it — records, remains, the sad cargo of a cover-up thirty years old — and then, in a rare fit of conscience or embarrassment, had blasted the approach route closed and struck it from every chart. The mountain had swallowed the site whole. Good, she thought. Let it keep things for once, instead of giving them up.

Lior. She thought of him less often now, and less painfully — the way you think of a wound that has healed into a mark. A dying man in the snow trading a map for a promise, betting everything on a stranger with a ruined name because ruined names were the only ones he could trust. He'd gambled right. She'd carried the ledger home. She'd broken half the promise — told everyone, eventually, everything — and kept the truer half underneath it: the ledger reached the descendants, the village learned what happened to its people, the names were read aloud. She liked to think he'd have forgiven the arithmetic. She liked to think the whole valley had already ruled on it, in that letter, in that last trembling line: *This is not politeness. This is law.*

*Come home,* they'd written. And the word *home* had landed in her chest like a piton driven into sound rock — that deep, resonant certainty of purchase. She had spent so many years thinking home was a place you lost. Thornevale had never been it. The border towns, the cheap rooms, the guide houses where she was tolerated — none of it. Home, it turned out, was a valley full of strangers who had decided she was theirs, and a boy asleep ten feet away who called her *boss* when he was joking and *Elodie* when he meant it, and a cairn on a shoulder of stone with a brave woman's name cut deep, and a letter pressed in a route book smelling faintly, impossibly, of green growing things.

Survival, she had learned, was a skill. Living was a choice you made daily, in small increments — a repaired rope, an honest answer, a letter answered. She went inside, found her pen by feel, and by the light of a stub of candle wrote four lines back to the valley:

*After the season. I swear it on the cairn. Tell your grandmother to pick which roof, and tell the children I am bringing someone who wants to learn their names too.*

She signed it, sealed it, and set it by the door for the morning post. Then she lay back and listened to the wind move through the pass — the same wind that had screamed through the wreck, that had tried to kill them in the whiteout, that had carried the sound of Brase's last stand down the river gorge — and heard none of that. Just weather. Just the mountain breathing, on speaking terms with her at last, keeping its side of the bargain the way it always had: indifferent, immense, and, if you learned its language, unexpectedly kind.

In the morning they would climb north, lightly laden, toward the light coming up over the ice. She was already looking forward to it. That, more than the license, more than the cleared name, more than anything the newspapers had written — that was the thing she had crossed the whole terrible length of the Verren to find.

It felt, finally, like living.
