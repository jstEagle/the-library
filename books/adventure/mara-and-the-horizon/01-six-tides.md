## 1. Six Tides

The river had been lying about itself all week.

Mara Coldharbour noticed it first at the fuel dock in Sankofa Bend, where the water sat brown and patient as a dog pretending not to beg. It had risen four inches overnight — no rain to account for it, no wind, just the slow green swell of a flood coming down from country she'd never seen and never wanted to. Old men on the dock read the water the way other people read scripture, and they didn't like what it said. *High water brings back what it takes,* one of them told her, spitting sunflower shells into the current. *That's the trouble with it.*

She'd laughed. She was still laughing when she loaded the last crate of penicillin into the Otter's belly and cinched the straps down over it, because laughing at old men's superstitions was cheaper than arguing with them, and Mara had built fifteen years of flying on doing the cheap thing first.

The med-run was routine the way her runs were always routine: three settlements upriver, vaccines that would spoil if she dawdled, gossip that wouldn't spoil at all but traveled better by air than by boat. She took off into a sky the color of hammered tin, wheels leaving the strip with a sound like a held breath released, and for forty minutes the world narrowed to instruments and riverbend and the particular silence of a woman alone at altitude, which was the only kind of silence Mara had ever trusted.

The flood found her anyway.

It came down the Vessany Reach — nobody called it that anymore except the charts, which kept dead names the way museums keep dead languages — a broad brown tongue pushing against the current, carrying whole trees, a drowned outhouse, half a fisherman's roof with its thatch still smoking wet. She saw it from two thousand feet: a seam in the river where clear water met dirty, running parallel for miles like the border of a country that existed only in catastrophe.

By the time she came back down at dusk, the flood had reached her landing strip.

Her strip wasn't much — a shelf of packed clay and river grass above the waterline, wide enough for the Otter and narrow enough that she landed it by feel after all these years, the way you'd find your own bed in the dark. The flood hadn't taken it. It had done something worse. It had *given* her something.

A chest sat in the middle of her runway.

Not washed up at the edge, not tangled in the reeds where flotsam goes to die. It sat square in the center of the strip, upright, as if it had been set there by hands. River mud clung to it in ropes and sheets, and around it the clay was scoured clean in a fan shape, as though the water had delivered it and then stepped back to watch what she'd do.

Mara stood at the edge of her own runway for a long time, engine ticking cool behind her, and did not approach it.

Fifteen years on this river teaches you things that can't be taught, only absorbed, the way wood absorbs damp. One of them is that nothing arrives without a reason, and the reasons are rarely yours. Another is that the river keeps secrets the way a banker keeps money — carefully, and always expecting interest.

But it was getting dark, and the chest was between her and her tie-downs, and fear is a luxury you have to schedule around work. So she walked out to it across the scoured clay, boots printing the mud, and looked down at the thing the flood had chosen for her.

It was iron, or had been once; the rust had gone through it like rot through fruit, orange blooms spreading under a skin of black pitch. Straps of some dark leather crossed the lid, and the whole of it was maybe the size of a sea chest, heavy enough that the mud beneath it had been pressed into a bowl. On the lid, stamped deep enough to survive the rust, survived the river, survived whatever years it had spent drowning, was a seal.

Mara knew seals. She'd carried government mail, mission correspondence, a wedding contract once, sealed in wax the size of her fist. This was none of those. This was a crest: a tower rising out of water, and around the tower's base, a ring of small figures rendered so finely they might have been swimmers, or mourners, or both.

Vessany.

The name arrived in her head before she permitted it, uninvited, the way certain words do — words you've heard your whole life in the lowered voices adults use for shameful things. Vessany, the drowned nation. Vessany, whose capital had gone under the Reach sixty years ago and stayed there, whole streets intact below the silt, cathedrals and customs houses and whatever else a country keeps when it expects to come back. Vessany, whose name her grandfather had refused to say at the dinner table, going quiet in a way that made the quiet worse than any story.

There were no stories. That was the point. The Coldharbours did not tell stories about Vessany, and a child learns quickly that the holes in a family's talk are load-bearing.

Mara crouched beside the chest. The light was going blue and then gray, moths beginning their stuttering work around her headlamp, and the river behind her kept up its low conversation with the bank. She put her hand flat on the lid. The iron was cold in a way metal shouldn't be after a day of floodwater — cold like it had been somewhere deeper.

The straps were knotted, not locked. Whoever had closed this chest had expected it to be opened by someone who already had permission. She worked the knots loose with fingers gone clumsy from the evening chill, and the leather came apart with a smell of old tallow and riverbottom, and the lid groaned up on hinges that had waited a long time to complain.

Inside, on a bed of tarred canvas gone stiff as bark: paper.

Half a chart, torn along a ragged vertical line, showing the lower Reach — the drowned country's approaches, soundings marked in a neat antique hand, depths in fathoms where now there was nothing but flood and legend. And folded inside the chart, a single sheet of heavier stock: a tide-table. Six days out, the columns ran, each day annotated in faded ink, and on the sixth day someone had drawn a line and written a single word beside it.

*Slack.*

Mara sat back on her heels. Her heart was doing something she didn't authorize — a quick, shallow flutter, curiosity wearing fear's clothing. Six days. Whatever this chest had been waiting for, it was waiting for six days from now, at slack water, at the bottom of the Reach, at a place marked on half a chart that someone had torn deliberately, keeping the other half.

She lifted the chart toward her headlamp to see the soundings better, and that was when she saw the margin.

There was writing in the margin. Pencil, mostly worn, pressed hard enough in places to groove the paper. A note in a hand she knew — knew the way you know a voice on a bad telephone line, instantly and against your will, because you heard it every day of your childhood until the day you stopped hearing it at all.

*E. Coldharbour.* Her grandfather, Elias. The same slanted capitals. The same way he crossed his sevens, hard and flat, like a man slamming a door. She used to copy that seven when she was learning her numbers, sitting on the floor of the boathouse while he worked over charts exactly like this one, and he'd caught her at it and laughed — the only time she could remember him laughing about anything connected to paper.

The marginal note read: *If the water gives it back, it was never ours to keep.*

Below that, smaller, almost erased: *Burn the manifest. Let them rest.*

Mara did not move for a long time. A moth landed on the chart's torn edge and sat there, opening and closing its wings like a tiny book being read too fast.

The river talked. The moths worked. Somewhere up the bank a nightjar started its engine-whir call, and the world went on being an ordinary world in which she had a vaccine cooler to unload and a plane to tie down and a kettle to put on, and in which her grandfather — thirty years dead, buried upcountry under a stone she visited exactly once — had apparently written instructions to whoever found a chest he must have known the river would someday surrender.

*If the water gives it back, it was never ours to keep.*

Which meant it had been theirs to keep once. Which meant Elias Coldharbour had known what was in this chest, had handled it, had written on it, and had let it sink — or sunk it himself — and had spent the rest of his life not saying the word Vessany at dinner.

Mara stood. Her knees cracked. She looked down the Reach, where the floodwater lay flat and gleaming under the first stars, hiding everything the way it had hidden everything for sixty years, and felt the wary curiosity in her chest finish its slow hardening into something with weight and edges.

Dread, she thought. That's the word for it. Dread is curiosity that's learned your name.

She repacked the chart and the tide-table exactly as she'd found them, closed the lid, and dragged the chest — it weighed far less than it should have, which was somehow worse — to the shadowed side of her supply shed, and threw a tarp over it, and weighted the tarp with fuel drums, and stood back looking at the shape of it like a woman who has just agreed to hide a body.

Six days. Slack water. Half a chart.

She went inside, lit the lamp, put the kettle on, and did not drink the tea. She sat instead with her hands wrapped around the hot mug, staring at the wall where a photograph hung that she'd stopped seeing years ago: her grandfather at the boathouse door, squinting into the sun, one hand resting on the shoulder of a girl who became a woman who became a pilot who flew away from that boathouse and never once looked back at it.

*Let them rest,* he'd written. But the river had other ideas, and the river, tonight, had made her the messenger.

Out past the strip, far down the black length of the Reach, a light appeared. Then another. Running lights, riding low on the water, moving upriver against the current with the unhurried confidence of something that knows exactly where it's going.

Mara turned off the lamp and watched them come.

* * *

They weren't headed for her strip. She told herself that twice, standing at the window with the mug going cold in her hands. Boats ran the Reach at night all the time — smugglers, late barges, fools. The lights slid past the mouth of her channel, a mile out, maybe more, and kept going north toward the drowned country, and she let out a breath she'd been holding since the chest came open.

Then the lead light stopped.

It hung there on the black water, motionless, a fixed point in a moving world, and after a while a second light joined it, and a third, arranging themselves with a patience that felt like attention. Like eyes adjusting.

Mara set the mug down. In six days the tide would go slack over Vessany, and half a chart said so, and her grandfather's handwriting said so, and now three lights on the night river said that she was not the only one who'd been waiting for the water to give something back.

She began, quietly, to pack.
