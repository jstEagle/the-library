## 2. The Broker's Wake

The river told her first.

Mara had learned to read water the way other people read faces — the small tell-tale tremors, the lies, the sudden silences. So when the current below the delta began running wrong on the third morning after the chest came ashore, she knew something heavy had entered it. Something displacing more than its share of the world.

She was loading ampoules of penicillin into the Otter's cargo bay when she heard it: not an engine exactly, but a pressure, a low industrial heartbeat rolling up through the silt and into the soles of her boots. The fishing skiffs at Perch Landing had all pulled in tight against the pilings, their owners standing in them like herons gone still, every face turned downriver.

She turned too.

The ship came around the last bend of the main channel like a piece of night that had decided to travel. Black hull, black superstructure, no name painted anywhere she could see from this distance — which was itself an answer, because honest ships wore their names like faces. It sat low in the water despite being empty, ballasted for work, and along its deck she could make out the shapes of cranes and winches, coiled cable thick as anacondas. A salvage vessel. But salvage fleets worked wrecks, and there were no wrecks on this stretch of river worth a ship that size.

Unless you counted the ones that had been underwater for sixty years.

Mara closed the Otter's cargo door with a care that cost her something. Her hands wanted to slam it. Her hands wanted to be anywhere but steady.

* * *

She told herself she wouldn't go down to the landing. She told herself the smart move was to fuel up, lift off, and put two hundred kilometres between herself and that black hull before anyone aboard so much as finished their morning coffee. She'd spent fifteen years learning when to leave; leaving was the one inheritance she'd actually claimed from her family, and she'd claimed it early and often.

But the chest was sitting in the back of her hangar under a tarp, and the chart inside it was folded into her flight jacket, pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and the tide-table said six days, and five of them were already burning away like fog in sun.

So she went down to the landing, because apparently she was her grandfather's granddaughter after all — curious past the point of sense, drawn to water that should have been left alone.

The broker was waiting at the end of the dock before she'd even tied off her skiff.

He stood with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the river with the patience of a man who had never once needed to hurry. Tall, broad through the shoulders, dressed in a charcoal suit that had no business surviving a delta morning — the fabric somehow shedding both humidity and river-spray as though it had been engineered rather than tailored. His skin was deep brown, close-cropped hair silvering at the temples, and his eyes, when he finally turned them on her, were the colour of the river in flood: brown carrying gold, calm over enormous depth.

"Marguerite Coldharbour," he said. Not a question. He pronounced all four syllables of Marguerite as though tasting each one.

"Folks here call me Mara." She kept her voice level, kept her hands loose at her sides. "And folks here don't generally know I'm coming."

"Adeyemi Voss." He didn't offer a hand; he offered a slight inclination of his head instead, formal as a treaty. "Broker for the Meridian Salvage Consortium. We're surveying the delta." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "For wreckage."

"This is a big delta," Mara said. "Full of wreckage. Boats sink here every season. You'll be busy."

"We're looking for one wreck in particular." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm even, the kind that made men sign contracts they couldn't afford. "A national one, you might say."

Behind him, the black ship idled at anchor in the channel, and Mara could feel it the way you feel a storm cell building past the horizon — pressure behind the ears, animals going quiet. Two crewmen had come down to the dock's far end and were pretending to inspect a mooring cleat. They weren't pretending well.

"What can I do for you, Mr. Voss?" she asked. "I'm a pilot. I fly medicine and mail. If your consortium needs a charter—"

"I'd like to buy a chest."

The words dropped into the space between them and sank without a ripple, and Mara understood, with a cold clarity that started in her spine and spread outward, that everything before this moment had been courtesy. The survey story, the polite approach, the suit — all of it scaffolding around this single sentence, which he had carried upriver like a blade wrapped in silk.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No?" He tilted his head, studying her with what looked almost like kindness. "Floodwater gave up a sealed chest three days ago, on or near a private landing strip south of here. Cedar, iron-banded, lead-lined. Stamped—" he paused, savouring it, "—with the seal of Vessany."

"There's no such place."

"There hasn't been since 1961," Voss agreed pleasantly. "Which makes anything bearing its seal either a forgery or a miracle. The Consortium pays very well for miracles, Ms. Coldharbour. Sight unseen. Name your figure."

* * *

Later, lying awake in the hangar office with the chart hidden inside a spare tire cover, Mara would replay the conversation a hundred times and catalogue every mistake she made, the way pilots do after a bad approach. But standing on the dock with the river breathing beneath her feet, she could only watch herself lie badly and know it.

"I think you've got bad information," she said. "I haven't seen any chest."

"Mm." He let the silence stretch until it creaked. Somewhere out in the reeds a bittern boomed, once, like a door closing. "You know what interests me, Ms. Coldharbour? Not whether you've seen it. Whether you've opened it."

"Why would that interest you?"

"Because a sealed chest is salvage. An opened chest is a choice." He stepped closer, and up close she could smell him — cedar and machine oil and something faintly mineral, like wet stone in a cave. "Whoever opened it has read whatever's inside. Whoever has read it knows what it points to. And whoever knows what it points to is, as of that moment, the most important person on this river." He smiled again, softer now, almost gentle. "I'm trying to find that person before someone less polite does. My competitors are not brokers, Ms. Coldharbour. They are scavengers. There is a difference, and the difference is blood."

"You're describing a treasure hunt."

"I'm describing a debt," Voss said, and for just a moment — half a second, maybe less — the broker's polish slipped and something else looked out through those floodwater eyes. Something old. Something grieving. "Sixty years old. My family's, as it happens. But we can discuss genealogy another time." The polish slid back into place, seamless. "The offer stands. The chest, sight unseen, cash or transfer, and my ship leaves your delta by tomorrow's tide and never troubles you again."

"And if I keep saying I don't have it?"

"Then I'll keep believing you," he said, "the way one believes a river won't rise." He unclasped his hands at last and produced a card — heavy stock, black ink, no logo, just a name and a frequency and a number. He held it out, and she took it because refusing would have been its own confession. "Every settlement from the headwaters to the sea will hear from me within the week, Ms. Coldharbour. Every dock, every mission, every doctor. Someone saw where the flood left that chest. Rivers have a thousand mouths, and I am very good at listening to them." He inclined his head again, that treaty-bow. "I'd rather hear it from you."

He walked back up the dock toward his launch without waiting for an answer, and the two crewmen fell in behind him like punctuation, and Mara stood alone at the end of the planks with a card in her hand and her pulse hammering in her throat.

* * *

She burned the card that evening, in the hangar's dented stove, and watched the black ink curl and go grey. It changed nothing. She knew that even as she fed the flames. Burning a man's name doesn't erase it; ask any Coldharbour.

The truth was simpler and much worse: he hadn't come to the delta because he suspected. He'd come because he knew. Knew the chest existed, knew what day the flood surrendered it, knew — or strongly believed — whose strip it had landed on. Which meant somewhere upstream of Adeyemi Voss there was information moving faster than the river itself, and Mara Coldharbour, who had spent fifteen years making herself small and mobile and forgettable, was suddenly the fixed point on everyone's chart.

She pulled the half-chart from the tire cover and spread it under the hangar's single bulb. Those familiar coordinates marching down the margin in her grandfather's hand — the same slanted capitals, the same impatient crossbars on the t's — and beside them, in different ink, different decades, a column of figures she'd taken for tide corrections.

They weren't tide corrections. She'd stared at them long enough now to see the pattern. They were payments. Dates and sums, running from 1961 to 1974, thirteen years of entries, each one initialed twice — once with initials she recognized with a sickness rising in her throat, and once with a mark she didn't: a small, precise *V* with a line struck through it.

Someone had been paid to keep a secret for thirteen years. Or paid to keep it buried. And now the descendant of one signature was anchored in the channel asking to buy the chest sight unseen, and the descendant of the other signature was her.

Outside, the river ran dark and full, carrying its secrets seaward as it always had, patient as arithmetic. Somewhere down that black water the salvage ship rode at anchor, lights burning late, and Mara understood that the quiet she had bought with fifteen years of motion was over. Silence had been a country she lived in, and it had just sunk beneath her.

There were six days on the tide-table. Five remained. And every mouth on this river was about to start talking.

She folded the chart, zipped it into the jacket she no longer took off, and went to check the Otter's fuel.

If she was going to run, she'd need to outrun a wake.
