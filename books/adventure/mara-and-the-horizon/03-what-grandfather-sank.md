## 3. What Grandfather Sank

The houseboat sat where it had always sat, moored in the crook of the river like a tooth that refused to fall out, and Mara hated how her hands remembered the way to it. Fifteen years of flying everywhere but here, and still her fingers knew the three knocks — two high, one low — that meant *family* in a language she'd sworn to forget.

Aunt Iyabo opened the door before the third knock landed.

She was smaller than memory made her. Same iron-grey braid coiled at the nape, same reading spectacles pushed up into her hair, but the woman behind them had been thinned by years the river takes from everyone who stays. Her eyes went from Mara's face to the oilskin bundle under her arm and back again, and something in them closed like a shutter.

"So," she said. "It found you."

"You knew."

"I made tea an hour ago." Iyabo stepped back from the doorway. "I've been waiting since the water rose. Come in before the mosquitoes do."

Inside smelled of kerosene and dried bitterleaf and the particular dust of books nobody had opened in decades. The lamp was already lit. That was the detail that undid Mara a little — the lamp, lit and waiting, as if her aunt had been sitting in this room for hours rehearsing a conversation forty years overdue.

Mara set the chest on the table between them. The Vessany seal caught the lamplight: the drowned crown over three waves, worn but legible, the way old sins stay legible.

"Open it," Iyabo said.

"I did. There's half a chart. A tide-table." Mara's voice came out flatter than she intended. "And handwriting in the margin that I'd know anywhere, because I spent my childhood copying it onto flight logs. Grandfather's."

Her aunt lowered herself into the chair across the table with the care of someone whose joints kept their own ledger. She didn't touch the chest.

"Do you know what Vessany was?" she asked.

"A country. Before the flood. Before—" Mara stopped.

"Before our family helped drown it." Iyabo said it the way you'd read a weather report. "Sit down, Marguerite. You'll want to be sitting."

Mara didn't sit. She stood with her knuckles white on the table's edge while her aunt reached beneath the seat cushion and drew out an envelope gone soft and brown as an autumn leaf. On its face, in that same handwriting — the handwriting Mara had fled across six hundred miles of river to escape — was written: *For whoever the chart finds. God forgive us all.*

"He wrote it the year I was born," Iyabo said. "He told me to burn it. I never could. Read it aloud. Some things need air."

Mara broke the seal. The paper inside crackled like something waking up.

---

*Kilometre Zero Mission, October 1961*

*My brother-in-law says I should write nothing down. He is probably right. But if I am to be damned I would rather be damned honestly, and paper is the only honesty left to me.*

*Today we sank a nation.*

*Not with guns. With charts and tide-tables and a barge load of treasury ledgers, and the quiet agreement of men who said it was the only way to end the war. The Vessany government was finished — everyone knew it, the capital was burning, and the brokers were circling like gulls over a gutted fish. If the treasury fell into their hands, the money would buy ten more years of killing. So a group of us — pilots, boatmen, one very frightened priest — took it all down to the deep channel and put it where no broker's dredge would ever reach.*

*I guided them. My hands are on every bearing. I chose the anchorage off the cathedral point because I knew the water there better than any man alive, and because I believed — God help me, I still believe — that some things belong at the bottom of the world.*

*But here is what I will not write in any official report, and what I ask Iyabo, when she is grown, to understand: we did not sink only gold. We sank names. The ledgers record every man who sold his neighbour to the brokers, every official who emptied the orphanages' accounts, every name that bought its safety with another name's drowning. The treasury was not wealth. It was evidence. And evidence, buried, keeps its power forever.*

*Which is why I have done the unforgivable thing. I kept one chart. Half of one. The other half stays with the mission at Kilometre Zero, sealed, until the last keeper dies or the last debt is paid. As long as our family holds our half, no one can ever assemble the whole — and no one can ever use the whole against us. They call this insurance. I call it blackmail, and I have signed my name to it, and my children's children will inherit the signature.*

*If you are reading this, then the river has given back what we put away, which means the world has decided our silence is no longer worth keeping. I am sorry. I am sorry for the country we ended and the truth we hoarded and the fear I am passing to you like a lamp passed hand to hand down a dark corridor.*

*Do not sell it. Do not spend it. Do not let anyone die for it, least of all yourself.*

*— E.A. Coldharbour*

---

Mara read it twice. The second time her lips moved without sound, the way you reread a sentence when the first reading felt like a hallucination.

"He blackmailed a dead country," she said finally. "That's the inheritance. That's what he left us. Not money. Leverage."

"He left us a wound that never closed." Iyabo poured tea neither of them drank. "Your grandfather spent his last thirty years watching the river, Marguerite. Every flood season he'd stand on the bank like a man waiting for a summons. When he died, your father couldn't bear the house anymore. That's why we scattered, why you grew up thinking the Coldharbour name was just a name."

"It wasn't just a name."

"No. It was a lock. And now the chest has washed up on your landing strip, which means the lock is breaking, which means—" Iyabo's hands trembled around the cup, and Mara saw, for the first time, that her aunt was afraid. Genuinely, bone-deep afraid. "Who else knows?"

"A salvage fleet came through yesterday. Black hulls. Their broker offered to buy the chest sight unseen."

"Sight unseen." Iyabo laughed, and there was no humour in it, only the sound of a woman hearing a death sentence pronounced politely. "Then they know exactly what it is. Adeyemi Voss?"

"You know him?"

"I know the name. His grandfather captained the barge that carried the ledgers down." She set the cup down with a click that sounded final. "There were two families at the bottom of that channel, child. Ours held the chart. His held the shame. And shame, given enough years underwater, turns into hunger."

Outside, the river moved past the windows, brown and patient, carrying whatever it carried. Mara thought of the chest spitting up onto her strip after fifteen quiet years, right when a salvage fleet happened to be working the delta. Coincidence was a word pilots used when they hadn't checked the wind.

"The other half of the chart," she said slowly. "Grandfather's letter says it's at Kilometre Zero. The mission."

"The mission burned out forty years ago. If anything's left, it's under the water by now, or in the hands of whatever priest stayed to keep the ghosts company." Iyabo looked at her niece across the table, across the chest, across sixty years of silence, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "You're going anyway. I can see it in your face. You fly the way he walked toward things — straight at them, like distance was a kind of courage."

"Is that wrong?"

"I don't know. He never found out either." Her aunt reached across and closed Mara's fingers around the letter. "But listen to me. Whatever you find down there — the men who sold those names, the families they ruined, the war they ended or didn't end — remember that you didn't choose this. You inherited it. There's a difference, and the difference is the only clean thing you own."

Mara tucked the letter inside her jacket, over her heart, where it sat like a coal.

At the door, Iyabo caught her wrist. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested.

"One more thing. The broker — Voss — if he comes asking here, I'll tell him nothing. But he'll come. Men like him always find the family eventually." She paused. "And Marguerite — the deckhand who runs medicine upriver for me, Tunde's boy, he mentioned a pilot asking about you at the last settlement. Asking whether you flew alone."

The river slapped against the hull, patient as a clock.

"They're not following the chest," Mara said quietly. Understanding settling over her like cold water. "They're following me."

"They were always going to follow the blood," Iyabo said. "Fly careful, child. And come back — not because the chart needs you. Because I do."

Mara walked out into the grey morning with her aunt's fear clinging to her like river fog, and the letter burning its small hole against her ribs, and one thought repeating with every step toward the plane:

*Half a chart points down. The other half waits at Kilometre Zero. And somewhere behind her, a black-hulled fleet was already turning upriver.*
