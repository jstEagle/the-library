## 4. Medicine for Lies

The wound came upriver before the man did.

Tunde heard it first as a rumor in the water — a boat running too fast for the current, engine note ragged, the sound of someone fleeing rather than traveling. He was elbow-deep in his morning's first patient, a fisherman's boy with a hook through the web of his hand, when the sound crossed the water and made him lift his head the way an animal does. Fifteen years on this river had taught his ears to sort engines the way other men sorted voices. This one was wrong. This one was afraid.

He finished the boy, wrapped the hand, sent him home with his mother and a lecture about barbed trebles, and was still wiping down the table when the skiff scraped against his pilings. The man who climbed out did it badly, one arm clamped to his side, leaving a dark smear on the gunwale that told Tunde everything the man's gray face could not.

"Doctor." The word came out ground between teeth. "They said — the mission at Bend Three said you don't ask questions."

"I don't ask questions I don't want answers to," Tunde said. "Inside."

The man was young, maybe twenty-five, with the rope-scarred hands of a deckhand and the salt-cracked lips of someone who'd been drinking bilge water to stay quiet. Tunde got him onto the table and cut away the shirt, and there it was: a graze along the ribs, ugly but honest, the kind of wound that bled theatrically and killed almost no one. Below it, older, a burn scar in the shape of a cable whip. Deck work. Salvage deck work — he knew that pattern from men who'd washed out of the fleet and into his surgery over the years, always with the same look, the look of dogs that had bitten the hand and were still deciding whether they regretted it.

"Knife or bullet?"

"Bullet. One of Voss's own security boys." The deckhand laughed, a dry cracked sound with no humor in it. "Slipped on the ladder rail, he says. Point-blank, and slipped."

Tunde cleaned the track with iodine while the man hissed and gripped the table edge. "Name?"

"Doesn't matter."

"It matters if you die on my table and I have to write something on the report."

A pause. "Okonkwo. Sami Okonkwo."

"Well, Sami Okonkwo, you're lucky. Half a centimeter deeper and we'd be having a very different conversation." He threaded the needle. "Hold still. This is the part where you tell me why a salvage fleet's own deckhand is running from it with a hole in his side, and I pretend I'm not listening, because listening would make me curious, and curiosity on this river has been getting people shot lately."

Sami looked at him then — really looked, the way wounded men do when the pain has burned off everything except the need to know if the person holding the needle can be trusted. "You know Mara Coldharbour."

It wasn't a question, so Tunde didn't answer it. He tied off the first stitch.

"The broker," Sami went on, words coming faster now, as if the wound had loosened something in him that fear had been holding shut. "Voss. Adeyemi Voss. He's got six ships in the delta now, maybe seven. They're working every landing, every fuel dock, every church that rings a bell. Cash for word of a woman pilot. Not her name — they never say her name — just *a woman pilot, alone, mid-forties, flies an old de Havilland*. Five hundred at the small settlements. A thousand at the bigger ones. I watched a man at Kalaba sell his own cousin's description for eight hundred and a case of diesel."

The needle paused, just for a beat, and Tunde hated himself for the pause because he knew Sami saw it.

"And how much are they paying inside the fleet?" Tunde asked quietly. "For loyalty?"

"Nothing. That's the joke." Sami's laugh again, worse than before. "Voss doesn't pay for loyalty. He pays for results and docks wages for failures. Two weeks ago a chest came up in the floodwater — sealed, old, stamped with some dead country's seal — and a pilot ferried it out of a landing strip before our survey crew got there. Voss has been eating himself alive over it since. He had the whole lower river dragged by divers. Nothing. So now he's buying eyes instead." His breath caught as Tunde tied another stitch. "I carried crates for that man three years. Crates marked MEDICAL that weren't medical. Crates marked SALVAGE GEAR that went down into the hold at night and never came up. I kept my mouth shut because the wages were good and my mother's lungs needed medicine that only fleet scrip could buy. But yesterday—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Yesterday I saw what happens to a man who asks what's in the crates."

"So you ran."

"So I ran." He flexed the hand Tunde wasn't stitching. "And everyone on this river knows the woman pilot drinks coffee at your clinic like it's a second liver. You want to know why I came here? Because this is the first place they'll look, and I figured anywhere they look first is somewhere they haven't gotten to yet."

Tunde sat back on his stool and looked at the boy — because that's what he was, whatever the scars said, a boy who'd run from one set of wolves straight toward the den of the only person the wolves wanted more. Outside, the river moved past his windows, brown and patient, carrying its secrets the way it always had. Somewhere north of here, Mara was doing whatever Mara did when she wasn't in his clinic stealing his coffee: flying, mostly. Outrunning, always.

"You'll stay in the back room," he said finally. "Two days, until that track closes. Then I'm putting you on the mission boat at Bend Three and you'll disappear so thoroughly even God will have to check his ledger twice."

Sami's shoulders dropped an inch. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank whoever taught you to run zigzag."

* * *

She came at dusk, the way she always did, the Otter settling onto the water below his clinic with that particular grace she had — half apology, half challenge, as if the river should be grateful she'd bothered to land on it. By the time she climbed his steps he had two cups poured and the good chair pushed out for her, because fifteen years of friendship meant he'd learned which battles to lose in advance.

She looked tired. That was the first thing. Not river-tired, not long-haul tired — tired underneath, in the place where sleep doesn't reach. She took the coffee without tasting it and stood at his window looking out at the darkening water, and Tunde understood that she already knew. Of course she knew. News traveled faster than boats on this river; it just traveled quieter.

"A deckhand jumped ship," she said. "Fleet colors. Wounded."

"Sami Okonkwo. Graze along the ribs. He'll live."

"He talk?"

"He talked. I listened. There's a difference, and on this river it's the difference that keeps people alive." Tunde set his cup down. "Mara. They're offering five hundred dollars at settlements that don't see five hundred dollars in a year. A thousand at the towns. And it's not just money — Voss's people are leaving medicine behind too. Quinine, antibiotics. Real medicine, the kind I have to beg off church boats. They're buying more than information. They're buying the idea that helping you costs something."

She turned from the window. In the last light her face was all planes and shadow, and he could see her doing the arithmetic she'd been doing since the chest came out of the floodwater — weighing herself against the river, against the settlements, against him.

"Then I fly higher," she said. "Different strips. No landings after dark."

"For how long? Six days? Sixty? That tide-table in that chest has a date on it, and Voss knows the date too. You can't outrun a calendar, Mara. You've been trying for fifteen years and look where it got you — hunted on your own water."

"My water," she repeated softly. Something flickered across her face. "That's the thing, Tunde. Everyone keeps saying *give it to them*, like it's mine to give. Like I picked it up off a dock." Her hand went to her jacket pocket, an unconscious gesture he'd seen a dozen times since the flood, the touch-check of a woman confirming a weight was still there. "My grandfather's handwriting is on that chart. My grandfather, who I buried believing he was a bush pilot and a decent man. Whatever's in that chest, it isn't treasure. It's a confession. And confessions aren't currency — they're debts."

"Then pay the debt and be done with it."

"To Voss?" Her voice didn't rise, but the temperature of it dropped, the way it did when she was angriest. "You met him?"

"No. But I patched a man today whose ribs he put a bullet in for asking a question. That's a reference." Tunde rose and stood across from her, close enough that he could see the exhaustion living in the corners of her eyes. "Mara, listen to me. I've known you since we were children stealing mangoes off Father Anselm's tree. I have never once asked you what you're running from, because you always came back, and coming back was enough. But this time you're not just running. You're dragging the whole river behind you. Every settlement that takes fleet money stops trusting its neighbors. Every dockhand starts calculating what a woman pilot's description is worth against his own family's medicine. You think you're carrying that chest alone? You're not. It's in every handshake on this water now, and it's poisoning them."

The silence stretched. Down at the mooring, the Otter rocked gently against its lines, patient as a dog waiting to be leashed.

"So what are you saying?" she asked, and her voice was very quiet now, and very careful, and that frightened him more than shouting would have. "Say it plainly, Tunde. You've never had trouble saying things plainly."

He made himself say it. "Give Voss the chest. Let the dead bury their dead. Let the river go back to being a river."

Something closed behind her eyes. He watched it happen — watched the door swing shut on whatever room of herself she'd briefly opened, watched her become the Mara the river knew, the one who landed light and left lighter and never let anything hold.

"You don't know what's in it," she said.

"Neither do you."

"I know enough." She set the untouched coffee on his table with a precision that hurt worse than throwing it would have. "You want me to hand my grandfather's sins to a man who shoots his own deckhands, and trust him to file them under *closed*? Voss doesn't want the chest, Tunde. He wants what the chest points to. And whatever that is, my family helped put it at the bottom of this river, which means it's not his to take and it's not mine to surrender. It's mine to answer for."

"That's pride talking."

"That's inheritance talking." She smiled, and it was the worst smile he'd ever seen on her, thin and bright and full of distance. "You of all people should understand. You stayed. You built the clinic your father started and you patch whoever crawls up your steps, and you've never once left, because leaving would mean the work dies. Well — this is my work now. I didn't choose it. It chose me, the same way the river chose you."

"The river never shot at me."

"Not yet."

She turned for the door, and Tunde felt the friendship strain between them like a mooring line in flood season — not snapping, not yet, but singing with tension, fibers letting go one by one somewhere beneath the sound.

"Mara." She stopped, hand on the frame, back to him. "Whatever you're flying toward — come back from it. That's all. Don't do what your grandfather did and make the river keep your secrets for you. Rivers give things up eventually. Everything comes up in the floodwater sooner or later."

She was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone soft and far away, already airborne.

"Keep Sami hidden. Keep your head down. If anyone asks, I came for coffee and argued about the price of avgas." A pause. "And Tunde — thank you for the stitches you gave my friend. Even if you charged him with a lecture."

Then she was down the steps and across the dark water, and the Otter lifted off the river with that same half-apology, half-challenge grace, climbing north into the last violet light until the sound of her engine dissolved into the evening, and Tunde stood alone in his doorway with two cold cups of coffee and the terrible, growing certainty that the next time he saw her, blood would be involved.

Behind him, in the back room, Sami Okonkwo slept the shallow sleep of the hunted, and murmured a name into his fever:

"*Kilometre Zero.*"

Tunde went still. He'd heard that name before — decades ago, as a child, in his father's voice, low and frightened, spoken of the abandoned mission far upriver. The place on no chart. The place the old people wouldn't name.

The place, he realized with a coldness that had nothing to do with the night air, that Mara was already flying toward.
