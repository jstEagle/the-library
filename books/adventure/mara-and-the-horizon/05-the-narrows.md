## 5. The Narrows

The fog came off the river at dawn like a held breath finally released, and Mara flew into it on purpose.

She'd left the mission strip before first light, chest lashed into the cargo bay behind her seat, the half-chart folded against her ribs where a second skin might be. Tunde's words from two nights ago still sat in her chest like a swallowed stone — *hand it over, Mara, hand it over and live* — but she had kissed his forehead, lied that she would think about it, and gone. Some inheritances you could refuse. This one, she was beginning to understand, refused back.

Below her, the delta unspooled in silver ribbons, channels braiding and rebraiding the way they had since before Vessany drowned, before Coldharbours drew breath. The fog lay over it all in long white shelves, and she dropped the Otter's nose and slid beneath the lowest one, down into the corridor between water and cloud where the world went soft and directionless.

For eleven minutes, there was only the engine's drone, the smell of avgas and old canvas, the compass card trembling like something alive.

Then the radio crackled.

"—aircraft on the river channel, this is *Kestrel One*, salvage survey flight out of Port Amity. We're tracking a twin otter without flight plan. Request you identify—"

Mara switched the radio off.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Fifteen years of flying medicine to settlements that paid in fish and gratitude had taught her hands what her mind still argued about: fear was fuel, and panic was just fuel burned wrong.

She banked south, toward the Narrows.

Every pilot on the river knew the Narrows and every pilot avoided them. Forty kilometres of gorge where the old river had cut itself a throat through black basalt, walls rising six hundred metres on either side, the channel below narrowing until in places the water ran white and the spray hung in the air like static. In clear weather it was a dare. In fog it was a coffin with an open lid. The current funneled up the gorge and tore the mist into rags, so that flying it meant threading gaps that opened and closed like breathing.

It also meant no seaplane with a broker's money aboard would follow her in.

*No sane pilot,* she corrected herself, and laughed — a short, ugly sound the cockpit swallowed.

The gorge mouth appeared out of the murk all at once, a black wound in the white world. She flared, bled speed, and went in.

* * *

Inside, the fog did not behave like fog. It came in layers, strata of grey and pearl, and the river below flashed between them like a knife being drawn and sheathed. The walls rose sheer on both sides, wet stone streaked with mineral green, close enough some mornings to read the lichen. She flew by memory and by the sound of the water — the pitch of the rapids told her where the channel bent before her eyes confirmed it.

Behind her, faint as a conscience, she heard it: a different engine note. Higher, sleeker, wrong for this place.

Voss's seaplane. A converted amphibian, all glass nose and hungry lines, the kind of aircraft built by people who believed money solved physics.

"He wouldn't," she said aloud.

He would. She heard him commit — the throttle push, the note climbing — and felt something twist in her stomach that wasn't entirely fear. It was almost admiration. Almost.

The first turn nearly killed them both.

The channel hooked hard left around a spur of rock the charts called the Deacon's Finger, and the fog closed to nothing as she banked, wingtip swinging toward stone she could hear but not see. She rolled out on instinct and prayer, and the wall slid past her window close enough that the propwash flattened the moss on it. Behind, the amphibian's engine stuttered — its pilot flinching, pulling up, losing the line.

Mara grinned, wild and breathless, and for one bright moment this was what flying had always been supposed to feel like: the old joy, uncomplicated, the sky a country that belonged to no nation and sank none.

Then the fog ahead turned solid, and the moment curdled.

* * *

The trouble with trading distance for altitude is that you spend altitude like currency, and the gorge does not extend credit.

She had been descending since the mouth — each gap lower than the last, each turn tighter, buying metres of lead with metres of sky. Now the channel dropped toward the gorge's deepest cut, where the river fell through a slot barely wider than her wingspan, and the ceiling of fog came down to meet her like a lid closing on a box.

Two hundred feet of air between stone above and stone below. Then a hundred and fifty. Then less.

She could climb. Climb meant breaking the fog line, meant the amphibian — circling somewhere behind, patient as debt — sliding onto her tail with its radio and its reach and whatever Voss kept in its hold besides rope and questions.

She could turn back. Turnback meant the same thing, slower.

Or she could go under, thread the slot, and come out the far side of the Narrows into country where the fog broke apart and the river widened and she could put thirty kilometres between herself and the sound of that engine before the murk lifted.

"Under," she said, and pushed the yoke forward.

The slot took her. There was no other word for it — the rock rose on both sides and the water roared below and the plane became a needle being drawn through cloth, spray hammering the floats, the fog strobing past the windshield in fists of white. Something in the airframe began to speak — a shudder through the airframe itself, deep and structural, the plane telling her in the only language it had that it was being asked to be more than it was.

She gave it more anyway.

The last bend came blind. She felt it before she saw it — the current's voice changing key — and hauled the yoke left with both hands, and the world swung, and the right main gear struck something.

Not the wall. A deadfall — a tree the floods had wedged into a shelf of rock, trunk jutting out over the channel like an accusation. The strut took the impact along its length and transmitted it up through the airframe as a crack she heard with her teeth, a sound like ice giving way on a March river.

The Otter staggered, wallowed, held.

And then the gorge opened its throat and spat her out into light.

* * *

The far side of the Narrows was another country. The fog shredded into streamers, the valley widened into terraced hills gone gold with dry grass, and the river spread itself flat and calm as if nothing had happened anywhere, ever. Sunlight came through the broken cloud in slanted columns, and for a full minute Mara simply flew in it, shaking, listening to her airplane.

The strut was cracked. She didn't need to land to know it; she could feel it in the way the right side of the aircraft answered the controls a half-beat late, loose, uncertain, like a friend who'd stopped trusting her. Every bump in the air drove the fracture a little deeper. The gear would hold for a gentle landing, maybe. On smooth water, into wind, flown perfectly.

There was no smooth water within range that wasn't a settlement, and no settlement that hadn't already been offered Voss's money for word of a woman pilot.

Except one.

She'd seen it years ago from cruise altitude and marked it without thinking, the way pilots mark things — a roofless chapel on a bluff above an oxbow, forty kilometres north, a clearing grown half over with brush. An abandoned mission from the first wave of river churches, the kind the maps stopped updating when the last priest died or left. Kilometre Zero, the old charts called the stretch of river below it, though nobody remembered why.

She turned north.

The strut sang its cracked song all the way, a vibration she felt in the soles of her feet, and she talked to the plane the whole way because there was no one else to talk to. "Hold together. Hold together, old girl. I know. I know. I've been asking too much for fifteen years and paying in someone else's coin." The chest shifted in the cargo bay behind her, knocking against the frame, patient as a passenger.

The oxbow appeared exactly as memory had filed it: a horseshoe of brown water, the bluff above it crowned with the chapel's broken gable, and beside it a long low building with its roof mostly intact, walls of river stone going soft with moss. A clearing where the brush had been cut back recently — recently enough that she frowned at it even as she set up her approach.

Into wind. Gentle as a mother. Full stall over the grass, holding the Otter off the ground until it ran out of patience and settled.

The right gear collapsed on touchdown.

The wing dipped, dug, and the plane slewed around in a grinding half-circle, propeller chewing turf, tail swinging, and then it was over — stillness, dust, the tick of hot metal, and Mara sitting in the wreckage of her approach with her heart going like a trapped bird and the smell of mown grass and hydraulic fluid filling the cockpit.

She climbed out on the left side, because the right side was the ground now, and stood looking at her airplane.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of that aircraft being the shape her freedom made in the world — the means of leaving, always the means of leaving, every horizon a runway. The right float strut was cracked clean through, the gear folded under the wing like a broken knee. The prop was notched. The wing spar, maybe, if the impact had traveled. She walked the length of her and put her palm flat on the cold cowling, the way you'd touch a horse.

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it more than she'd meant anything in years.

Behind her, gravel crunched.

She spun, hand going to the flare pistol in her belt, and found herself looking at a man standing at the treeline — tall, gaunt, in a cassock gone grey as ash, a crowbar over one shoulder and sawdust on his sleeves. He looked at the wrecked Otter, then at her, and his face held neither surprise nor greed nor any of the expressions the last six days had taught her to expect.

"You'll be the pilot," he said. His voice was quiet, worn smooth like the stones below the bluff. "The river said someone was coming. It's been saying it for sixty years."

Mara's hand stayed on the pistol. "Who are you?"

"Brother Elias." He nodded past her, at the chest visible through the sprung cargo door, at the seal stamped on its lid catching the afternoon light. "And you'd better come inside, daughter. Because the men who follow that box are already asking after you in the villages downstream — and what your grandfather helped drown isn't treasure."

His eyes were very old and very clear.

"It's a list."
