## 6. The Mission at Kilometre Zero

The strut cracked the way old men break — not all at once, but with a sound like a confession starting.

Mara heard it over the engine's drone, a high singing note in the aluminum that hadn't been there when she'd lifted off from the fog-choked narrows. She had traded altitude for distance, trading like a woman gambling with her own hands, and now the bill was coming due. The left main gear shuddered. A hairline of bright metal showed through the paint where the strut's skin had split, and behind it, she knew without looking, the whole assembly was folding itself toward failure with the patience of water working on stone.

Below her, the river unspooled grey and indifferent. And there — a smudge against the far bank that she had passed a hundred times and never once landed at: the mission. Kilometre Zero, the old charts called it, though no chart said why. A chapel roof gone green with moss, a bell tower leaning like a drunk against his friend, a scatter of whitewashed buildings surrendering one wall per decade to the jungle. A grass strip beside it, long abandoned, long enough if she was gentle.

She was not gentle. She was alive, which was different.

The Otter came down hard on the right gear first, then the left gave its crack a final syllable and folded, and the world became noise and earth-smell and the terrible intimacy of your own aircraft trying to kill you. She rode it out on the prop tips and the belly until the plane lay still in the tall grass, steaming, ticking, her heart going like a sewing machine in a house with no other light.

For a long time she didn't move. She listened for the seaplane that had shadowed her through the narrows — Voss's black-winged ghost — but the sky held only cloud and the thin complaint of birds resettling after her arrival. Fog was climbing back up the gorge downstream, filling the cuts like milk poured into glass. Whatever followed her would have to wait for weather, or for luck, and she intended to be gone before either arrived.

* * *

The strut was worse than she'd hoped and better than she'd feared. The lower housing had split along a weld seam some mechanic thirty years dead should never have trusted. She could not fix it properly. But the mission, when she limped around it on foot, turned out to be a graveyard of good intentions, and graveyards have salvage.

Behind the chapel, half-swallowed by bougainvillea, stood a workshop — a mission workshop, because missions always had workshops; faith travels on repaired engines as surely as on scripture. Inside: rusted lathes, a workbench furred with dust, and hanging from nails in the beam above, lengths of steel stock, angle iron, a broken plough, and — she laughed out loud, alone, the sound startling her — a crate of salvaged aircraft parts stamped with markings forty years out of date. Some pilot before her had made this same arithmetic of desperation and paid in parts.

She worked through the afternoon with the door open to the light, cutting and fitting, welding with a torch whose tanks were nearly empty, swearing at the metal in three languages. Sweat ran into her eyes. Splinters of steel found the soft skin between her fingers. It was honest work, the kind her grandfather had taught her at the boathouse before everything else he taught her, and she hated how much her hands remembered, how much they loved it.

She did not hear him come.

"You are setting the gusset plate backwards," said a voice, mild as morning. "It will hold longer the other way."

Mara spun, wrench raised, and found an old man standing in the doorway with a tray of tea.

He was small and brown and neat as a pinned moth, wearing a cassock faded to the color of weak coffee, and he regarded her and her raised wrench with the unhurried patience of someone who had been threatened by better weapons than a spanner. His eyes were extraordinary — pale amber, almost gold, the color the river went at dusk.

"I heard you land," he said. "Or rather, I heard you stop landing. There is a difference." He set the tray on the workbench among her chaos. "Tea. You will want it. Welding makes the blood sour."

"Who are you?"

"Brother Elias." He said it the way other men say their own names — without ceremony, as if it were merely a handle and not a history. "Keeper of this place, such as it is. Keeper of less than that, if we are honest. Sit. The strut will keep. Metal is patient. People are not."

* * *

They drank tea on the chapel steps while the light went long and copper across the water. The bell tower, she saw now, held no bell — just an empty yoke of timber, and below it a plaque so weathered the letters had become suggestions.

"Why kilometre zero?" she asked.

Elias smiled, and the smile was old grief worn smooth. "Because everything began here. When Vessany drowned, the ones who got out — the fishermen, the clerks, the children carried in grain sacks — they came upriver until they could come no further, and this is where they stopped. Kilometre zero of a country that no longer existed. They built the chapel first. Always the chapel. Then the school, then the houses, then the graves." He nodded at the treeline, where she now noticed stones, dozens of them, leaning at all the angles of forgetting. "Forty families. Sixty years. My grandmother was four years old when she walked here. She died two hundred metres from where she arrived, and she never once went back downriver."

Mara's tea had gone cold in her hands. "You know who I am."

"I know the airplane," Elias said. "I know the Coldharbour stride — you walk like a woman reading a map even when she's standing still. And I know why you're repairing a strut instead of flying home." He turned those amber eyes on her fully then, and she felt seen the way X-rays see. "There is a chest, isn't there. With the seal on it."

Her hand moved before her mind agreed to it — toward the map case slung across her body, the half-chart and tide-table she had not let farther than arm's reach since the floodwater surrendered them. Elias watched the motion and nodded slowly, as if she'd answered a question he'd been asking for sixty years.

"My grandfather," Mara said carefully, "had nothing to do with—"

"Don't." It was not harsh. It was tired. "Don't lie to me in my own church, child. The Coldharbours guided the scuttling. Everyone upriver knows it in the way everyone knows the river floods — quietly, every generation, without saying it aloud. Your family kept what they took." He rose, joints cracking like small green wood. "Come. If you carry the chest's cargo, there is something you must see before you decide what you are carrying."

* * *

He led her past the graves to the last building, low and windowless, its door locked with a padlock gone to lace-rust. Inside, the air was dry and paper-sweet. Shelves. Hundreds of them. Not treasure — documents. Ledgers swollen with damp and dried again, photograph albums, bundles of letters tied with fishing line, school registers, shipping manifests, a wedding book with pressed flowers flattened between its pages like butterflies.

"The refugees saved everything," Elias said. "When you lose a country, paper is the only soil it grows back from. For sixty years they wrote down what happened. Who collaborated. Who warned whom. Which officials sold exit papers and which refused and which drowned for refusing. Every name." He drew out one ledger and opened it with both hands, gently, the way you touch a wound. "And at the center of it, the thing they built the whole archive around. The list of the betrayed."

Mara looked at the page. Columns of names. Dates. Small annotations in a hand she recognized with a sickness that started in her teeth — the same slanted, confident script that crawled across the margin of her half-chart. Her grandfather's handwriting, cataloguing the people his own family had helped drown.

"They think the chest holds gold," Elias said. "Voss thinks it. The fleet thinks it. Perhaps your grandfather wanted them to think it — fear keeps divers away better than depth does. But the treasury of Vessany was never coin. It was this." He tapped the ledger. "A nation's memory, written by the people it betrayed and the people it lost. Names, child. The chest holds names. And names are heavier than gold, and worth more, and infinitely more dangerous — because gold can be spent and forgotten, but a name read aloud cannot be unheard."

The chapel bell tower creaked outside in the wind off the water. Mara stood very still among the shelves of the dead, and something shifted in her that she would spend the rest of her life describing and never quite finish.

All day she had been planning escape. Fuel, route, the fog's schedule, the hours she'd bought with a cracked strut. She had been running the way she'd run for fifteen years — treating the chart as cargo, the chest as contraband, herself as courier to nobody. But standing in the archive at kilometre zero, smelling the paper and the dust and the faint river-damp in the walls, she understood that you cannot outrun a list of names. You can only decide whether to read it or burn it. Those were the only two doors, and both of them opened inward.

"They'll come here," she said finally. "Voss. When he finds my trail. He'll burn this place to get to me."

"He may," Elias agreed placidly, reshelving the ledger. "We have been burned before. We write it down again." He looked at her over his shoulder, and the amber eyes were kind and absolutely without pity. "But you should know something else, since you've come all this way. The chest's tide-table. Six days out. That is not a delivery date, child. That is slack water at the wreck — the only six hours a year the flooded cathedral-hold can be dived. Your grandfather's handwriting isn't a warning on that margin." 

He closed the shelf cabinet and locked it.

"It's an invitation. Someone in your family meant for the ledger to be found. The question that should be keeping you awake tonight is: meant for *whom*?"

Far downriver, so faint it might have been thunder deciding whether to become weather, Mara heard an engine — a heavy one, a marine engine, running against the current with purpose.

Elias tilted his head, listening, and his face did not change at all.

"Well," he said. "Perhaps you won't need to wonder about the fog after all."
