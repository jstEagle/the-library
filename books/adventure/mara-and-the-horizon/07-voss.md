## 7. Voss

The black hull of the *Meridian's Wake* cut the delta water like a blade through silk, and Adeyemi Voss stood at the rail watching the river refuse to ripple behind it. That was the thing about this country — it forgave nothing, forgot nothing, and gave back only what it chose. He had learned that lesson at seven years old, standing on a dock in another delta entirely, listening to his grandmother say his grandfather's name the way you'd say a prayer for someone who wasn't coming back.

*Emile Voss. Drowned with Vessany.*

Not *died*. Not *lost at sea*. The old women of the family had always used the word *with*, as if the nation itself were a ship that went down and took its people with it — which, in every way that mattered, it had.

He turned from the rail. Behind him, the chart room smelled of diesel and old paper, and his men were going through the day's takings: three settlements questioned, two bribes accepted, one lie told so badly it might as well have been a confession. A woman pilot. Medicine run. Fifteen years on the river. The details assembled themselves into a shape he recognized, because he had spent eleven years and most of his inheritance learning to recognize shapes.

"Kolawole," he said. "The doctor's settlement. Show me again."

His second spread the river chart on the table and weighted its corners with brass shells. The settlement sat where the river bent around a drowned forest, a scatter of stilt houses and one long building with a red cross painted on its roof — faded now to the color of an old wound. Tunde Kolawole. River doctor. Oldest friend of Marguerite Coldharbour, if the barman at the last fuel stop could be believed, and Voss had paid the man enough that he would have believed anything.

"She'll go to him," Kolawole said. "Sooner or later. People like her always do."

"People like her." Voss smiled without warmth. "You make her sound like a species."

"Aren't we all, sir?"

He didn't answer that. He looked instead at the photograph pinned above the chart table — not of Coldharbour, but of a young man in a naval uniform, 1961, standing beside a barge loaded under tarpaulins. His grandfather. Emile Voss had been the salvage master's engineer, the man who kept the pumps running when the order came down to open the sea-cocks, and he had gone down with the treasury rather than surface as a traitor. Or so the family story went. Voss had long ago stopped believing family stories and started believing documents, and the documents said something simpler: Emile Voss had been ordered to drown, and he had obeyed, and the men who gave the order — men named Coldharbour — had lived long, comfortable lives on the proceeds of everyone else's silence.

The ledger was the only thing that could change what those documents said. Names. Dates. Signatures. Proof of who had betrayed whom, and in what order, and for how much. With it, the Voss name stopped being a stain and became a wound — visible, honorable, bleeding in the right direction. Without it, his grandfather stayed a footnote: the engineer who drowned, cause unknown, file closed.

Voss had built a fleet on that absence. Six ships, two seaplanes, a broker's license in four jurisdictions. Every year the fleet grew and the absence grew with it, and he understood now — the way you understand things only after you've paid too much for them — that power was just grief with better logistics.

"We take the doctor tonight," he said. "No guns unless they're needed. I want him comfortable. I want him *grateful*."

Kolawole raised an eyebrow. "Grateful?"

"When a man understands that his life depends entirely on my good temper, he develops a certain appreciation for my good temper. It's remarkable how quickly gratitude follows fear, if you give it time to breathe." Voss rolled up the chart. "And when Coldharbour comes for him — and she will come — I want her to find him fed, treated well, and utterly dependent on my continuing generosity. Fear makes people stupid. Obligation makes them reasonable."

"And if she doesn't come?"

He looked out the porthole at the darkening water. Somewhere upriver, a woman was flying medicine and lies through fog-choked gorges with half a chart and her grandfather's handwriting burning a hole in her pocket. She was running, but she was running along a line, and lines have two ends.

"She'll come," he said. "She's her grandfather's granddaughter. And Coldharbours always come back to what they've sunk."

* * *

They took Tunde Kolawole at dusk, on the water, in his own boat — which was the cleanest way, Voss had found. No doors kicked in, no neighbors screaming. Just a black hull sliding alongside a wooden skiff, a hand on the gunwale, and a voice saying, quietly, "Doctor. We need your professional opinion aboard."

Tunde was braver than Voss expected, and stupider. He swung an oar. One of the deckhands took the butt of it across the shoulder, and then there were three men in the water and one doctor hauled over the rail by his collar, coughing river water and fury in equal measure.

"You're making a mistake," Tunde said, dripping on the deck. He was a big man, broad through the chest, with the calm hands of someone who had stitched wounds by lantern light for twenty years. "Whatever you think she has—"

"I don't think," Voss said. "I know. Sit down, Doctor. You're wet, and I'd hate for you to catch something before your patients need you."

They put him in the aft cabin — not a cell; Voss had been explicit about that. A bunk, a lamp, a basin, a plate of rice and smoked fish that went untouched for six hours and then, inevitably, eaten. Voss checked on him twice. The first time, Tunde sat with his back against the bulkhead, arms crossed, radiating the particular silence of a man conserving his anger. The second time, near midnight, Voss brought coffee, and Tunde surprised him by speaking first.

"You're Voss," the doctor said. "The broker. They talk about you at the fuel stops. They say you can buy anything."

"They say a great many flattering things about me."

"They say you bought a whole village's silence once. Downriver. Over a drowned dredge crew." Tunde's eyes were steady, unafraid, which Voss noted with something close to respect. "Did you? Buy their silence?"

"Yes." Voss set the coffee down within the man's reach. "And I paid for the widows' roofs afterward, which no one ever mentions. Grief is expensive, Doctor. Someone has to fund it."

"And if the grief is yours to begin with?" Tunde picked up the coffee. He drank it like a man taking medicine — deliberately, without pleasure. "That's what this is, isn't it. This isn't business. Businessmen don't burn six tides chasing one pilot up a river."

Voss stood in the doorway a moment longer than he meant to. The lamp threw his shadow long across the cabin floor, and for a moment he saw himself as the doctor must see him: a tall man in a salt-white coat, silver at the temples, hands soft from ledgers instead of rope. A man whose grandfather had drowned and whose entire life since had been an attempt to dive down after him.

"My grandfather was on the Vessany treasury barge," Voss said. "When it went down. He was the engineer. He opened nothing and closed everything, depending on which liar you believe, and the men who actually sank it wrote the history and got rich." He paused. "There's a ledger. Names. The truth about who betrayed the nation and how. I'm going to have it, and I'm going to read it aloud in front of whatever courts still remember Vessany existed, and my family's name will mean something other than *drowned*."

Tunde was quiet for a while. Then: "And Mara? What does she get, in your version?"

"What she gets," Voss said, "is to stop running. Fifteen years, Doctor. Fifteen years she's spent her life outrunning a name, exactly like me. The difference between us is that I turned toward mine and she turned away. When this is done, neither of us will have anywhere left to hide. Some people find that freeing."

"Some people find that drowning," Tunde said. "You'd know all about that, I suppose. Your family specialty."

The words landed harder than any oar could have. Voss kept his face still, but something behind it shifted — the same shift he felt whenever he stood over charts of the old coastline, tracing the place where a nation used to be. He closed the door gently. That was the discipline: gentleness, always gentleness, until gentleness stopped working.

* * *

At dawn he sent the message upriver, the way you send a message to a hawk — written where it could be seen from the air.

They anchored off the doctor's settlement, the *Wake* riding high and conspicuous, and Voss had Tunde brought up to the foredeck in clean clothes, unhurt, visibly fed, and seated in plain view with a cup of coffee he was permitted to drink. Then Voss had the chest — an empty chest, bought in a market two hundred miles south, aged with salt water and deliberate care — lashed to the rail beside him.

Below it, painted on a hatch cover in letters legible from five hundred feet:

**THE DOCTOR DRINKS FREE. THE CHEST COMES HOME. SIX DAYS.**

Then he waited, the way he had learned to wait — not patiently, never patiently, but with the terrible focus of a man who has bet everything on a single current. Through the binoculars he watched the stilt houses, the red-crossed roof, the small figures on the docks who watched him back and said nothing, because he had already bought their silence once and they knew he honored his purchases.

Kolawole joined him at the rail. "If she doesn't take the bait?"

"She takes it." Voss lowered the binoculars. "But not for the chest. She's smarter than that — she'll know it's empty, or she won't care. She comes for him." He nodded down at Tunde, who sat drinking his coffee with the composure of a man performing calm for an audience of one pilot somewhere in the sky. "Look at him. He knows it too. That's why he's sitting there instead of trying to swim. He's not my hostage, Kolawole. He's my letter, and he's already written himself."

"And when she comes? Six days, mid-river, on your terms?"

Voss watched the fog lift off the water in slow rags, revealing the drowned forest below the anchorage — black branches reaching up through thirty feet of clear, cold current, the way his grandmother had described the masts of Vessany reaching up through the sea. Everything in this country came back eventually. Floodwater surrendered chests. Rivers gave up their dead. He had spent his whole life believing that, needing it to be true, because the alternative was that some things stayed down forever, and his grandfather was one of them.

"When she comes," Voss said, "I'll offer her a trade she can't refuse. Her grandfather's crimes for my grandfather's honor. The ledger for the doctor. Everyone walks away with exactly what they always wanted."

"And if she refuses?"

He thought of the letter in his cabin — his grandfather's last letter, recovered from a dead man's locker forty years later, water-stained, the ink blooming like frost: *Tell my son the truth costs more than silence, but silence costs everything.* He had built an empire on the second half of that sentence. He intended to end his life on the first.

"Then the river gets another Voss," he said quietly. "But it will not be me."

Far upriver, faint as a scratch on glass, he heard it — or imagined he heard it — the thin drone of a single engine, coming down the current line toward them, carrying a woman, a chart, and fifteen years of someone else's sins.

Six days, the paint promised.

Voss smiled at the empty sky and began, in his head, composing the terms.
