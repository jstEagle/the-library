## 8. Trade of Blood

The shoal had a name on no chart: the Teeth, because at low water it rose out of the river like something jawed and hungry, and at high water it only pretended to be gone. Mara had threaded it a hundred times in the dark, reading the water by feel — the way the current combed its fingers through the gravel bars, the way the light lay flat or broke. She knew it better than Voss ever could.

That was the arithmetic she was running as she came down out of the morning haze with the chest lashed into the second seat and Tunde's last message folded against her heart like a splinter she couldn't dig out.

*Mid-river shoal, slack tide, come alone.* His handwriting had been steady. That steadiness frightened her more than a tremor would have. Tunde's hands shook when he lied; they hadn't shaken.

The river ran pewter-grey under a sky the color of old gauze. Mist stood off the banks in columns, slow as smoke from doused fires. Somewhere below her, hidden in that grey, were boats she couldn't see, men she couldn't count, and Adeyemi Voss, who had spent six tides teaching her that every silence has a price and every price gets paid eventually.

She brought the plane down long and shallow, floats kissing the water a hundred meters short of the exposed gravel spine of the shoal, and taxied up to it with the engine idling hot behind her. The chest sat where she could see it. The flare pistol sat where she could reach it. Both of those decisions had cost her a sleepless night, and neither of them felt like enough.

Voss's launch came out of the mist exactly on slack water, black hull riding low, and behind it — her stomach dropped before her mind caught up — a fuel barge, squat and rust-streaked, towed like a leashed animal. Two hundred drums of aviation gasoline if it was one. Enough to run his whole salvage fleet for a season. He'd brought his own heartbeat to the trade.

And beside him at the rail, hands bound with wire, a shirt gone dark across one shoulder —

Tunde.

Mara killed the engine. The silence after was enormous, filled only by water slapping the floats and the creak of the barge's towline.

"You brought him." Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. Inside, everything was screaming.

"I brought your friend," Voss said. He stood at the bow with his hands clasped behind him, a tall man dressed for a funeral he intended to enjoy. "You brought my inheritance. I'd call us even."

"He's a doctor. He's never done anything to you."

"Neither did my grandfather," Voss said, "and the river drowned him anyway. Get out of the plane, Ms. Coldharbour. Bring the chest. Walk it to me, and Dr. Okonkwo goes home to his patients."

She climbed out onto the float, boots wet instantly, the river's cold climbing through the leather. The gravel crunched under her as she stepped onto the shoal, dragging the chest by its handle. It was heavier than names should be. Fifty meters of shining wet stone between them. The mist moved in slow walls on either side, and she counted what she could see of Voss's crew — four on the launch, two on the barge, one she couldn't find, and the one she couldn't find was the one who would kill her.

Tunde lifted his head. One eye was swollen nearly shut. He shook his head, small and deliberate: *don't*. Then, because he was Tunde, because fifteen years of friendship had taught her that he would bleed before he'd let her carry something alone, he opened his mouth and shouted across the water:

"Mara! The margin! Read the margin!"

"The margin is *mine*," Voss snapped, and struck him across the face with the back of his hand, casual as swatting a fly, and something in Mara's chest went from cold to molten in the space of a single breath.

She set the chest down.

"Here's how this happens," she called, walking forward anyway, hands open, voice level. "I put it down halfway. You send him to me. We both walk away rich in whatever it is we're actually trading."

"Halfway," Voss agreed, and gestured, and two of his crew waded Tunde off the launch onto the gravel.

They met in the middle, the way such meetings always do — everyone moving at the pace of people pretending not to be afraid. Ten meters. Five. She could see the wire biting Tunde's wrists, see the blood dried black at his collar, see past his shoulder the crewman whose rifle had drifted, casually, terribly, to track her instead of the chest.

"Tunde," she said quietly. "On three, you drop and roll left."

"Mara—"

"One."

"Don't you dare—"

"Two."

The rifleman's finger tightened. She saw it happen the way you see lightning — already too late, already decided — and she threw herself sideways as the shot cracked across the shoal and the world went white and loud.

But she wasn't the one who fell.

Tunde had moved first. Fifteen years of patching bodies had taught him exactly what bullets do, and he'd put himself between her and the muzzle without hesitation, without calculation, the way he did everything — and the round took him high in the back, and he went down on the gravel with a sound she would hear for the rest of her life, a sound like all the air leaving a room.

She was screaming before she knew it. She was on her knees beside him before she knew that either, hands pressing the wound while blood pushed up between her fingers, hot and shocking, his breath coming in ragged hitches under her palms.

"No, no, no — stay with me, stay with me, you don't get to do this, you *stupid*, beautiful man—"

"Marguerite." Voss's voice, above her, distant as weather. "The chest. Now, or I let him finish bleeding here, and you can explain to the river why."

She looked up at him through the haze of spray and gunsmoke, and what she felt then had no name she'd been given. It was older than fear. It was the thing her grandfather must have felt, standing on a deck sixty years ago, watching a nation go under — the moment when a person decides what the water will remember.

Her hand found the flare pistol in her belt.

Not pointed at Voss. Pointed past him — at the barge, riding low with its two hundred drums of gasoline, its towline taut, its paint blistered by years of sun.

"You want an inheritance," she said. "Come and inherit this."

She fired.

The flare arced over Voss's shoulder trailing red, and hit the barge's deck amid a spill of fuel where a drum seam had been weeping since Kilometre Zero, and the world answered her with fire.

It went up with a sound like the sky tearing — a bloom of orange that swallowed the stern, heat slamming across the shoal in a wall that drove Voss's crew screaming off their feet, that turned the mist to gold for half a mile in every direction. The towline parted. The barge, burning, began to drift with the turning tide, away from the launch, away from the shoal, a floating pyre carrying two hundred drums toward the deep channel where they would burn themselves out over open water and drown nothing but fish.

Voss stood in the firelight with his coat smoking and his face lit orange, and he did not scream. That was the worst part. He looked at her across the burning water with an expression she'd need years to understand — grief, and recognition, and something almost like respect — and then the smoke took him.

Mara dragged Tunde to the plane. She didn't remember doing it. Her hands remembered: under his arms, boots skidding on gravel, the weight of him enormous and alive, still alive, still breathing in wet hitching pulls. She got him over the float and into the seat and lashed him in with cargo straps because there was nothing else, and her hands were red to the wrist and she left prints on the strut, on the stick, on his forehead where she pushed his hair back to check his eyes.

"Stay," she told him. "You've spent fifteen years telling me to sit still. Take your own advice."

His good eye found hers. "Fuel," he rasped. "You burned... his fuel."

"Yeah."

"That's..." A breath, wet and terrible. "That's going to make him... unreasonable."

"It's going to make him honest," she said, and started the engine.

The plane lifted off the burning water into air that tasted of copper and kerosene, and below her the Teeth fell away, and the pyre drifted downriver trailing black smoke like a signature, like a promise. Behind her: a broker with nothing left to bargain with except violence, and a doctor bleeding in her passenger seat, and a chest full of names sitting in the back where anyone could take it, and the tide beginning to turn.

She flew north with her hands shaking so badly she had to lock her elbows to hold her heading, flying not toward safety but toward the only place left — the mission at Kilometre Zero, Brother Elias, a roof and a table and someone who knew how to keep secrets older than either of them.

Tunde's breathing stayed. She made herself count it, in and out, in and out, a metronome against the roar of the engine.

"Hang on," she said, to him, to herself, to the river sliding away beneath her. "I'm taking you home."

But home, she thought, watching the smoke thin astern, had never once meant safe. And somewhere behind her, in the gold-lit mist, Adeyemi Voss was standing in the wreckage of his leverage, learning the same lesson her family had paid sixty years to learn:

That the river keeps what it's owed.

And it always sends a bill.
