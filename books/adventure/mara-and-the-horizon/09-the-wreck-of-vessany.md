## 9. The Wreck of Vessany

The sixth tide came in grey and breathless, the way the river did when it had made up its mind about something.

Mara anchored the *Kestrel* over the drowned cathedral at first light, when the water lay flat as poured pewter and the current forgot itself for an hour. Slack water. The old pilots called it the river's confession — the one moment a day it stopped lying about where everything was. Below her keel, forty feet down, the hull of the *Vessany* waited with its cargo of silence, exactly where her grandfather's half-chart said it would be, exactly where it had waited since 1961.

She suited up on the float, hands steady enough to surprise her. The wetsuit smelled of neoprene and old rubber and something faintly like church — incense, maybe, or just the idea of it, clinging to her from Brother Elias's stories. She checked her gauge twice, then a third time, because dying down there would hand Voss everything he wanted without him firing a shot.

The water took her like a held breath released.

Cold first, then pressure, then the green-gold murk of the river proper, where silt hung suspended like the dust motes in Elias's chapel. She descended along the anchor line hand over hand, and the wreck rose to meet her out of the dark the way cathedrals rise out of fog — not all at once but in pieces, a rail here, a davit there, then the great black shoulder of the hull itself, furred with sixty years of river weed.

Her grandfather had drawn the hold entrance on the chart margin in that cramped, slanted hand she'd known since childhood — the same hand that had written *MARGUERITE* on her fifth-birthday card, the same hand that had signed the scuttling order. *Aft companionway. Third frame past the coal seam. Mind the plate.*

Mind the plate. Sixty years dead and he was still telling her what to do.

The companionway gaped like a mouth. She finned through, her lamp beam carving a tunnel through the dark, and the cathedral opened around her.

Elias had been right to call it that. The hold had been built to carry refugees once, before it carried anything else — she could see it in the iron ribs arched overhead like vaulting, in the brass fittings gone green as verdigris saints. Silt lay across the deck plates in soft dunes, undisturbed, holding the shapes of things long dissolved: a boot here, the ghost of a crate there, small hollows where smaller lives had set their burdens down and never picked them up again.

Her bubbles rose in silver strings toward the shadowed ceiling. Somewhere above, the river went on carrying its gossip to the sea.

The vault door was where the chart promised — a steel rectangle bolted into the aft bulkhead, its wheel fused with rust but not, miraculously, seized. She braced her fins against the frame and hauled, and the wheel gave with a groan she felt in her teeth more than heard, a sound like the ship clearing its throat after six decades.

Inside: nothing.

Not gold. Not the treasury that had ended a war, not the ransom-weight of coins the salvage fleet had chased across six tides and three graves. The vault was a steel room full of river-dark and empty shelving, and in the center of the floor, weighted under a brass bar gone black with age, lay a single oilskin packet.

Mara's heart hammered against the regulator. She pulled the packet free, tucked it inside her suit against her chest, and for one long moment she hung there in the flooded cathedral, lamp beam trembling on the far wall, breathing borrowed air in a dead nation's belly.

Then she saw the second thing.

Scratched into the vault's inner door, deep enough to survive the water, someone had cut two lines. Her lamp found them and her blood went cold and hot all at once, because she knew the hand. Of course she knew the hand.

*What we buried was never here.*
*It sleeps where the Coldharbours sleep.*

Below the words, a bearing and a distance, and a name she'd grown up hearing spat like a curse by every pilot on the river:

*Kilometre Zero. The boathouse. Home.*

The boathouse. Her family's boathouse — the sagging, spider-haunted shed on the Coldharbour property upriver, the one her father had forbidden her to enter, the one her grandfather had kept locked his whole life and which Mara, at nine years old, had pressed her ear against and heard... something. A drip. A hum. She'd told herself it was the river under the floorboards.

Sixty years, and the treasure had been sitting twenty miles from where she'd learned to walk.

She laughed into her regulator, a wild, barking sound that scattered a school of pale fish from the vault doorway. All this. The chest, the chart, Voss's fleet crawling the delta like flies over a wound, Tunde bleeding on a mission cot — all of it for a locked shed she'd been forbidden since childhood. The treasure was always home. It had been home the whole time, sleeping under her own family's roof while she spent fifteen years flying away from it.

She kicked for the surface, and the cathedral let her go without ceremony.

* * *

She broke the water beside the *Kestrel*'s floats into sunlight so clean it felt like an accusation, and the first thing she registered was the smell.

Diesel. Fresh diesel, riding the morning air off the water, and beneath it the flat metallic note of a cold engine recently run.

Voss's launch sat fifty yards off her starboard bow, black hull, black canopy, patient as a heron. Adeyemi Voss stood in the bow with a rifle broken open over his forearm, loading it with the unhurried economy of a man who had done this ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. He wasn't pointing it at her. He didn't need to yet.

"You dive well," he called across the water. His voice carried the way voices do over still water, flat and close no matter the distance. "My grandfather dove too. Off the *Vessany*, the last night. They say he went back down for the ledger and the sea kept him." He worked the rifle closed with a click. "I've spent my whole life being kept from the same water."

Mara hauled herself onto the float, dripping, the oilskin packet hard against her sternum. "Your grandfather drowned with them?"

"He chose to drown with them. There's a difference." Voss stepped down into his launch's cockpit and let it drift closer, idling, the wake lapping at her floats in small insolent pushes. "He was Vessany's harbor master. When your family scuttled the ship, he went below to save the passenger manifest — the names. The true ledger. The war ended, everyone got their peace treaty and their monuments, and my grandmother got a telegram and an empty grave." His eyes were the color of the water at depth. "I don't want the gold, Coldharbour. I've never wanted the gold. I want the names. I want the country that was erased to have existed somewhere other than in my grandmother's kitchen."

"Then you should know," Mara said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, "the vault is empty."

Something moved behind Voss's face — not disappointment. Confirmation. As if she'd told him the sky was blue and he'd finally been given permission to believe it.

"I know," he said quietly. "I've always known. My grandfather's last letter said the ledger never sailed. *They moved it before the tide.*" He raised the rifle now, casually, resting it across his arm, muzzle angled at the water between them rather than at her — a courtesy, or a message. "Which means someone moved it ashore. Which means someone kept it. And every road I've followed for thirty years leads to one family." He nodded at the packet bulging inside her suit. "That's the second half of the chart, isn't it. Give it to me."

"And then what? You burn the boathouse with me in it?"

"And then," Voss said, "I read the names aloud. Every one. In the cathedral square in Vessany City, if there's still a square. On the radio, if there isn't. I will make a nation out of paper if that's all that's left to make it from." The rifle came up another degree. "You think I'm the villain of this river, Coldharbour. But your grandfather helped drown four hundred people to end a war, and your family kept the proof as leverage for sixty years, and you flew medicine over their graves for fifteen without once asking what was under you. I'm not the villain. I'm the receipt."

The words went into her like the cold water had, all at once, everywhere. Because he wasn't wrong. That was the obscenity of it — he wasn't wrong about any of it.

"The names are mine to read," she said. "Mine to carry. You want to trade blood for paper, Voss. I'm done trading blood."

"Then give me the chart," he said, "and no one else bleeds. Tunde is still breathing. For now. My men are watching the mission." He let that sit between them like a stone dropped in still water. "Last offer, pilot. The tide's turning. In twenty minutes this water starts moving, and I'd rather not be floating over a graveyard when it does."

Mara looked at him across the shrinking gap between the boats — at the grief wearing the mask of method, at the boy who'd grown up on an empty grave — and she understood, with terrible clarity, that she could not outrun him, could not outfly him, could not out-dive him. He had one thing she didn't: nothing left to lose.

But she had something he didn't either.

A home. And a key she'd never needed, because the door had only ever been locked against strangers.

"The chart stays with me," she said, already reaching for the mooring cleat. "Follow me if you want, Voss. But follow me *upriver*. To Kilometre Zero. To the boathouse." She met his eyes and held them. "Where your grandfather's names have been sleeping the whole time — under my family's floor."

For the first time since the delta, Adeyemi Voss looked genuinely unmoored. The rifle dipped an inch.

"The treasure was never in the wreck," Mara said, hauling up her anchor, the *Kestrel* swinging her nose toward home. "It was always home. Come see."

The sixth tide turned behind them, and the river began to move.
