## 10. Years, Not Miles

The boathouse smelled of creosote and old rain, the way it had smelled when Mara was nine and her grandfather still pretended to be a man who merely kept boats. She stood in the doorway with the river at her back and the dark ahead of her, and for a long moment she could not make herself go in.

Behind her, Voss's footsteps crunched on the shingle. He had not tied up his launch. She noticed that. A man who expected to leave quickly.

"You're certain it's here," he said. Not a question. A man rationing his questions like water.

"The chart says so." She touched the oilskin packet in her jacket, the second half of the chart she'd pulled from the flooded vault of Vessany two tides ago, salt-stiff and soft as old skin. "My grandfather drew a false bearing on the half everyone hunted. The real one points home. It always pointed home. He was laughing at us from the grave."

"Then let's not keep him waiting."

She went in.

The boathouse was a single long room, boat-shaped, built by hands that believed in straight lines and honest timber. Her grandfather's skiff still hung from the beams in its slings, hull down, keel toward heaven like something waiting to be read its rights. Dust lay on everything in a fine grey felt. There was a workbench with clamps gone orange with rust. There was a stove that had not been lit since before her father left. And there, against the far wall where the floor met stone, a hatch — iron-ringed, painted over so many times it looked like part of the boards.

Mara knelt. Her knife found the seam. Paint came away in curls, decades of it, colors stacked like sediment: green over blue over red over the original grey of grief. The ring came up stiff, then all at once, and cold air breathed out of the dark beneath, smelling of river clay and paper and time.

A ladder. Twelve rungs. She counted them because counting was what pilots did when their hands wanted to shake.

At the bottom, a vault no bigger than a chapel closet. Dry — impossibly dry, her grandfather had been an engineer before he was anything else — and lined in cedar. On a shelf cut into the stone sat a tin box, army-green, unmarked except for a number stenciled in fading white: the same number that appeared on the salvage chest the flood had given her, six tides and one lifetime ago.

She carried it up into the light.

Voss stood at the workbench, and when she set the box down between them he did not reach for it. That frightened her more than reaching would have. His hands were flat on the wood, and they were trembling, very slightly, the tremor of a man holding himself to a mooring that might not hold.

"Open it," he said quietly.

She did.

Inside, wrapped in waxed linen gone amber with age, lay a ledger. Not gold. Never gold. A book bound in grey hide, its spine cracked, its pages dense with columns of names — names in ink, names in pencil, names pressed so hard into the paper they'd torn through. Beside each name: a village, a date, a debt. What the Coldharbours had been paid. What the Coldharbours had betrayed. Every family that had trusted the treasury ships. Every soul aboard them when the seacocks opened.

The drowned nation's true ledger of the betrayed. Brother Elias had told her true.

And there, in the front matter, in handwriting she knew better than her own heartbeat — the strong downstrokes, the little hooks on the *f* — her grandfather's attestation. *I, Aldous Coldharbour, do set down what was done, that it may be answered for.*

Mara lifted the book. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than her plane.

* * *

They came up onto the shingle because she asked for open sky. Voss agreed too easily; she understood later that he thought she was surrendering it. The evening was going copper along the water, the tide low and slack, the whole delta holding its breath between breaths.

Tunde sat propped against a fuel drum near her aunt's houseboat, his shoulder bandaged, his color bad but his eyes clear. He'd insisted on coming, riding in Voss's own launch under the broker's reluctant guard, because — as he put it, wincing — someone had to witness whatever foolishness she was about to commit. The bullet had missed everything that mattered by the width of a prayer. Tunde said the width of a prayer was exactly how he lived.

Mara stood at the waterline with the ledger in both hands. Voss faced her across ten feet of wet gravel, and behind him the black hull of his salvage ship rode at anchor, patient as a debt.

"Adeyemi Voss," she said. "Your grandfather's name is in this book."

His jaw moved. "I know."

"He wasn't a traitor. He was a passenger. Third page. *Oluwaseun Voss, deck steward, refused the order to close the hatches.* They shot him on his own deck and sank him with the rest." She watched the words land on him like weather. "Your family didn't drown in disgrace. Your family died trying to save people. The only shame in that book belongs to mine."

"Then give it to me." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated it, and she saw him hate it. "Give me the ledger and I will take it to every capital that will listen. I'll buy the hearings. I'll buy the historians. I'll make Vessany a nation again if I have to build it out of paper. My grandfather will have his name back. Give it to me, Mara, and I'll call off my fleet tonight. You'll never see another black hull on this river."

The offer hung there, enormous, glittering. Fifteen years of running, ended with a signature. Peace, purchased.

Mara looked at the ledger. Then she held it out — sideways, spine toward him, pages loose.

"Take it," she said.

He blinked. "What?"

"It's yours. It was always yours — yours more than mine. Read it. Copy it. Photograph every page. Take the names to whoever you want." She turned the book fully into his hands and let go. "I'm not selling memory, Adeyemi. Not to you, not to anyone. But I'm not hoarding it either. That's what my family did — kept it locked in a hole in the floor and called it leverage. Look what it bought them. Look what it made me."

Voss stared down at the ledger in his hands like a man handed fire he'd spent his life believing was buried.

"This is a trick."

"It's a gift. Those are different things. One of them you can't steal."

For a long moment the only sound was the tide ticking against the shingle and, far off, a heron complaining about the evening. Voss opened the cover. He read the first page. His lips moved around his grandfather's name, and his eyes went bright and terrible, and Mara understood that she was watching a man receive exactly what he had burned six tides and three lives to seize — and discovering it did not fit the shape of the wanting.

Because he had wanted to *take* it. Taking would have meant the world was the kind of place where taking worked, where his methods were justified, where the drowning had simply been economics. Given freely, the ledger accused him instead. Grace was a mirror, and he could not look into it.

He closed the book slowly.

"I'll have copies made," he said. His voice was level again, broker-flat, but something underneath it had broken or been born; she couldn't tell which. "You'll never see me again."

"That's your choice," Mara said. "But the offer stands. If you ever want to read the names aloud somewhere — properly, with witnesses — come find me. I'm not going anywhere."

He almost smiled. It didn't survive the journey. He tucked the ledger inside his coat, walked to his launch, and pushed it off the shingle without looking back. The engine caught on the second try. The black hull grew small against the copper water, then smaller, then was only a wake, then only a rumor, then nothing at all.

Tunde limped over and stood beside her, watching the empty river.

"You gave it away," he said.

"I lent it."

"To him? Mara, that man put a bullet in me."

"And he's carrying the only proof that his grandfather was a hero. That's heavier than any bullet. He'll either grow around it or it'll crush him. Either way—" she exhaled, and it came out ragged, fifteen years long, "—either way it's not mine anymore. None of it. The chest, the chart, the name. I'm just the woman who carried it the last mile."

Tunde was quiet a while. The first stars were coming out over the narrows, the ones he'd taught her to navigate by when they were children stealing his father's canoe.

"So what does the woman do now," he said, "when she isn't running?"

Mara looked at the boathouse. At the skiff hanging keel-up in the dark. At the hatch standing open, breathing its cold breath into the warm evening. At the strip beyond, where her plane sat with its mended strut and its empty med-lockers, fueled and ready and going nowhere unless she made it go.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I've never stayed long enough to find out what happens next."

* * *

Spring came up the river the way forgiveness comes — slowly, then all at once.

Mara flew the med-run still, but shorter legs now, home every night, the plane's new strut singing a slightly different note on final approach that she had stopped noticing and started loving. She ferried Tunde's vaccines and the mission's school supplies and, once, a wedding party so drunk they tried to baptize the co-pilot's seat. She fixed the boathouse roof. She lit the stove. She took the ledger's copied pages — Voss had sent them, unasked, in a plain parcel with no letter — and read them aloud on the first Sunday of every month at the mission at Kilometre Zero, Brother Elias nodding along, the refugees' grandchildren learning names they had only ever known as silence.

Vessany stayed dead. That was the choice, and she made it again each month, reading: some nations are not meant to rise again, only to be remembered honestly. The names went into living mouths instead of vaults. It seemed a fairer resurrection.

On an evening in the wet season, rain drumming the new roof, Tunde sat at her table eating her cooking and complaining about it with the enthusiasm of a man whose shoulder ached only when the weather changed, which was to say, always, gloriously, alive.

"You know what the strange thing is?" he said. "I keep waiting to feel like leaving. Fifteen years I've watched you leave. I figured it was in the blood."

"It was," Mara said. She looked out the window at the strip, silver with rain, at the plane tied down snug under its tarps, at the river rising fat and secret past the banks. "Turns out blood can learn."

Outside, the floodwater was climbing again, higher than any spring in memory, lapping at the shingle, at the boathouse door, whispering against the timbers the way water does when it has something to give up — or something to ask. Mara listened to it a long time, her hand resting easy on the table, and when the knock finally came at her door, deep and deliberate as a tide, she was not afraid.

She got up, and she answered it.
