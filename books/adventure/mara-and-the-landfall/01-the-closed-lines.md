## 1. The Closed Lines

The morning fog had not yet burned off the Aldmere valley when Theo Kavanagh stepped off the rusted maintenance ladder and onto the bed of the old Corven Spur, her boots finding the ties by memory rather than sight. She had walked this line eleven times in six weeks, and she could have done it blindfolded, which was nearly what she was doing now, the mist so thick it turned the world into a watercolor of gray and darker gray.

She carried her instruments in a canvas roll slung across her back: the transit, the level, the steel tape coiled like something sleeping. In her coat pocket, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, sat the letter she had read four times already and would read again before noon, because reading it changed nothing and she needed to be reminded that nothing had changed.

*Final notice. The Harrow Syndicate, assignee of record for the estate debts of one T. Kavanagh (deceased), hereby—*

Her father's debts. Her father's name on the paper, hers now in every way that mattered to men who counted money instead of counting grief.

Theo set up the transit at chainage forty-one hundred, where the spur curved along the bluff above the river, and leveled it with the small, precise movements of hands that had once set grades for main lines carrying two hundred tons of freight at speed. Now she surveyed a dead branch line for a salvage company that wanted to know how much usable rail they could tear up and sell south. Eleven miles of track laid by men who believed the north would bloom. It hadn't bloomed. The rails would go to smelters, the ties to someone's stove, and the grade would slump back into the hillside as if no one had ever dreamed here at all.

She sighted down the curve, fog swallowing the far end, and felt the familiar ache settle into her chest—not sharp, never sharp anymore, but constant, like a stone worn smooth from being carried too long.

There had been a time when Theo Kavanagh could look at a stretch of country and see the road through it. Not metaphorically—actually *see* it, the cut through the ridge, the trestle over the gorge, the way the grade should breathe and ease so the engines wouldn't labor. Her father used to say she had been born with compasses in her blood. He'd said it the year she passed her certification exams at nineteen, youngest in the province's history, and he'd said it again, quieter, the last time she saw him, when his own certification meant nothing and his debts meant everything and the syndicate's lawyers were circling like gulls over something dying.

*They'll take the tools first,* he'd told her. *Guard the tools.*

So she guarded the tools. The transit, the level, the tape, the drafting kit with its brass fittings gone dull from use. Everything else—the house in Corven, her mother's furniture, the little brass clock that had chimed the hours of her childhood—had gone to auction two winters ago, itemized in a broadsheet she'd made herself not read.

Through the transit's lens, the dead rail gleamed wetly, running straight until the fog took it. Straight lines were honest things. They went where you pointed them or they didn't exist. Theo had built her whole reputation on that honesty, and it had bought her exactly this: solo contracts on closed spurs, because she worked cheap, worked alone, asked no questions, and produced surveys so clean that even the salvage clerks who couldn't read a profile sheet filed them without complaint.

Invisibility was a skill like any other. She had practiced it the way she'd once practiced curve computation—daily, deliberately, until it became reflex. Don't linger at the customs house. Don't drink where collectors drank. Pay the ferryman in exact coin. When Ambrose Pell's name surfaced in conversation—and it surfaced often enough, in the low voices of men who owed money—look at your boots and walk on. Everyone in the valley knew Pell's face the way farmers know weather coming. Nobody wanted to be the field it rained on.

She broke down the transit at forty-two hundred and moved on, counting ties under her breath out of habit. Sixty-one, sixty-two. At sixty-three there was a gap where scavengers had pried one up, and she noted it automatically: *one tie missing, ch. 42+63, likely removed within past month, edges fresh.* Her pencil moved in short, certain strokes. Even for a line that would never carry anything again, she recorded everything. It was the only form of faithfulness left to her.

The fog began to thin near midmorning, tearing into rags that drifted up the bluff and dissolved. Below her, the Aldmere showed itself in patches—brown water moving fast after last week's storm, carrying branches and foam and, occasionally, things harder to name. A door, once. A crate of bottles. The river had been high all spring, chewing at its banks, rearranging its shallows, and Theo had watched it work with the professional respect one moving force pays another.

At the end of the spur, where the rails simply stopped—no buffer, no terminus building, just rail ends pointing at the water like fingers that had forgotten what they were reaching for—she ate her lunch on a tie and let the wind come up the valley. Cold still, this far north. The landfall was three days' hard travel downstream, though she had no reason to go there. Nobody did. The town at the landfall had emptied when the money moved on, the way towns do, leaving their bones to the gulls.

The letter came out of her pocket then, because it always did around noon, the way a tooth finds the tongue.

She read it standing, wind pulling at the corners.

*FINAL NOTICE OF ASSIGNMENT AND INTENT TO SEIZE.*

*The Harrow Syndicate, holding assignment of the outstanding obligations of the late Tomas Kavanagh (principal, interest, and accrued charges totaling 1,840 crowns), hereby notifies the guarantor, THEO M. KAVANAGH, that absent payment in full or approved restructuring within fourteen (14) days of this notice, collection agents are authorized to seize assets of equivalent value, including but not limited to instruments of trade, and to refer the matter for warrant of attachment against future earnings.*

Fourteen days. Fourteen days minus the five since the letter had reached the Corven post office, which meant nine, and the post had been slow, so call it eight. Eight days before Pell—or whoever the syndicate sent, though it was always Pell eventually, Pell was thorough the way rot is thorough—came up the valley with his inventory book and his two quiet men and took the transit, the level, the tape, the drafting kit. After that she would be a woman who had once been an engineer, doing whatever work remained for such women. There wasn't much. There was less every year.

Eighteen hundred forty crowns. She earned eleven a week on salvage surveys when she could get them. The arithmetic was not difficult. The arithmetic had never been difficult; that was the cruelty of it. Debts were the one engineering problem with no elegant solution, only the slow grinding failure of insufficient input.

She folded the letter along its existing creases and put it away.

What stung—not the debt itself, she had made her peace with the debt, or something adjacent to peace—was the waste. That was the honest word for it, and Theo permitted herself honesty in the privacy of her own skull the way other people permitted themselves drink. The waste of it. There were bridges in this province she had helped calculate, standing and carrying loads, and culverts she had designed that had turned back floods, and here she was measuring up scrap rail for eleven crowns a week while the part of her mind that could see roads through country sat idle like a great engine left cold in a shed, seizing slowly, growing useless from disuse.

Talent wasted on small jobs. Worse than no talent at all, some days. No talent at least spared you the daily evidence.

A bird startled out of the gorse beside the track—a thrush, ragged from the wet spring—and Theo watched it go and thought, not for the first time, about leaving. Just walking south along the dead line until it met the live main line, hopping a freight, vanishing into a city where nobody knew the name Kavanagh or the figure 1,840 or the shape of Ambrose Pell's smile. People did it. The papers called them debt-fleers and printed their descriptions, but the papers forgot, and cities were kind to the anonymous.

But they'd attach her father's grave plot. That was in the fine print somewhere; she'd checked. And beyond that—she didn't fully understand her own refusal, except that it felt like the rails felt, running true or not at all. If she ran, the running would be the truest thing about her ever after, and she could not bear that. So she stayed, and paid what she could, and kept her head down, and waited for a miracle she didn't believe in.

The afternoon survey went quickly. She shot levels down the last mile, noted three more missing ties, a washout at the culvert where the stream had undermined the embankment, and a length of rail near the stopping point that someone had struck hard enough to kink—old damage, years old, a derailment maybe, back when trains still ran here and something had gone wrong in the fog. She stood over the kinked rail longer than the job required, reading it the way other people read letters. Metal remembered. Somewhere in that bend was a bad night, a brakeman's shout, perhaps worse. The line had been abandoned within the year after; she'd seen the dates. Whatever happened here had helped kill it.

Or the money had killed it, and the wreck was just the symptom. With the northern lines it was always worth asking which.

By the time the light went long and gold across the water, she had her profile drawn and her notes squared, and she packed the instruments with the care of a woman handling the last of something. The transit went into its case, the case into the canvas roll, the roll onto her shoulder. She allowed herself one look down the dead rails toward the sea she couldn't see, the way you look at a door you've decided not to open.

Then she walked back along the line toward Corven, invisible as ever, counting ties.

* * *

The post office in Corven occupied the front room of a house that had once been the stationmaster's, back when Corven had a station worth master-ing. Now it held pigeonholes, a counter scarred by decades of parcels, and Mrs. Aldery, who had watched Theo grow up and had the tact, rare in small towns, to say nothing about it.

"Two for you," Mrs. Aldery said, and handed them over without ceremony, which Theo appreciated. Pity was heavier than any parcel.

The first was the survey payment—nine crowns, docked two for the delay, with a note in the clerk's crabbed hand about *budgetary constraints.* Nine. Against eighteen hundred forty. She put it in her inside pocket where it made no difference whatsoever to its weight.

The second envelope was cheap paper, unsealed, addressed in a hand she didn't know. She opened it leaning against the counter, and read it twice, because the first time she assumed she'd misread.

It was from a man named Doss, described himself as a wrecker working the lower Aldmere shallows, said the spring floods had shifted the riverbed below Harrow Bend and "brought up things that have been down thirty year." Said he'd seen wreckage—timber, iron, a section of hull with lettering on it he couldn't make out, sitting in two fathoms of water where last month there'd been a sandbar. Said he'd heard she was the sort who knew old ships and might pay for a look, or might know someone who'd pay.

Said, in the last line, almost as an afterthought: *Lettering looked foreign. V-something. Thought of you because of your da.*

Theo stood very still at the counter, the letter in her hand, and felt something turn over in her chest that she hadn't felt in years and did not immediately recognize. It took her a moment to name it, and when she named it she was almost embarrassed.

Interest.

Vessany. The nation that had been struck from the maps twenty years ago, its grants dissolved, its railways pulled up or abandoned, its very name sanded out of atlases by treaty. Every schoolchild learned the date the way they learned the flood years. And a hull with Vessany lettering, raised by a flood out of the Aldmere shallows, thirty years down—

Ships didn't lie about their names. Rails didn't either. Things that had been sunk stayed sunk unless something moved them, and rivers only moved what rivers touched, and the Aldmere did not touch Vessany, because Vessany was nowhere anymore.

Mrs. Aldery was watching her with the careful blankness of a woman pretending not to watch. Outside, evening was coming down blue and cold over the dead spur, the dead station, the whole dead north, and somewhere in it Ambrose Pell was presumably doing his arithmetic too, nine days minus however many it took him.

Theo folded both letters together into her coat, thanked Mrs. Aldery in a voice that came out steadier than she expected, and stepped out into the dusk already calculating—tide, current, distance, cost. Two fathoms. She'd dived in worse for less.

She told herself it was curiosity. She told herself it was nine crowns' worth of distraction from a debt she couldn't pay anyway.

She did not quite believe herself, and the river, waiting down in the dark with whatever it had kept all these years, did not believe her either.
