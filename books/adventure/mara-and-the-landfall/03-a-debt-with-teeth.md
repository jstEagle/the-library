## 3. A Debt with Teeth

The chest had not left the floor of her cellar in three days.

Theo told herself this was prudence. A thing that valuable deserved to be studied slowly, catalogued properly, understood before it was moved or shown or—her mind shied from the word—*spent*. But she knew the truth of it as well as she knew the gauge of any rail: she was afraid to touch it again. Every time she descended the stairs and lit the lamp beside it, the Vessany seal seemed to look back at her, that embossed sunburst over crossed keys, patient as a debt collector's knock.

She had spent those three days doing what she always did when a problem would not resolve itself: measuring something else. The spur line out to the old Harrow loading yards needed a grade survey before the county would sign off on her invoice, and so she walked its two miles twice with her level and her chain, recording elevations to the sixteenth of an inch for a railway that would never carry another car. The work was precise and pointless, and it steadied her hands, which was most of what precision had ever been for.

It was on the second pass, near the collapsed culvert where blackberry canes had swallowed the ballast, that she saw the carriage.

It stood on the county road above the cut, a dark lacquered thing with syndicate grey on the door panels, too fine for these backlands by half. It had not been there on her first walk. A man leaned against its fender with his hat tipped back, watching her work with the unhurried attention of someone who has already decided how the afternoon will go and is merely waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

Theo finished her sight. Wrote the figure. Capped her pencil. Then she climbed the embankment with her instrument case swinging at her side and her heart going like a piston under full steam.

"Miss Kavanagh." He straightened as she approached, and offered a hand she did not take. Up close he was younger than his posture suggested—thirty perhaps, maybe thirty-five—with fair hair combed flat and a face built for pleasantness, all soft planes and mild blue eyes. Only the stillness of him betrayed anything. Men who worked for their living fidgeted. This man did not fidget. "Ambrose Pell. Harrow Syndicate, collections division."

"I know what a collector looks like," Theo said.

Something flickered behind the pleasantness—not offense, but interest, the way a man might look twice at a lock that turned out to be better made than expected. "Then you'll know I'm not here to threaten you. I'm here about your account." He produced a ledger book from inside his coat, thin, bound in grey leather, and opened it without consulting her permission. "Principal outstanding, four hundred eleven pounds, fourteen shillings. Interest accruing since your father's estate entered administration. The Syndicate has been patient, Miss Kavanagh. Patience is a virtue we're known for." He smiled. "But virtues are expensive, and the board has asked me to begin recovering assets."

"My tools," Theo said. It wasn't a question. The letter had said as much, three weeks ago, in language so courteous it took a full reading to understand it was a death warrant for her trade. *Repossession of productive equipment in satisfaction of arrears.*

"The transit level, the chains, the theodolite if you have one. The drafting instruments. The Syndicate considers them collateral against—" he glanced at the ledger as though checking, though she'd have bet her last shilling he knew the figures by heart "—against a debt you have no realistic prospect of servicing. You survey closed lines, Miss Kavanagh. There's no future in it. Even you must see that."

"I see a man from a company that owns every mile of closed line in the valley telling me there's no future in them."

Pell laughed—a real laugh, brief and unguarded, and somehow that was worse than the smile. "You're your father's daughter. Everyone says so." He closed the ledger. "I met him once, you know. Years ago. At the Vessany hearings. He gave testimony for four hours and never once raised his voice. They tore his grants apart in front of him and he just kept saying, *the figures are the figures.*" Pell shook his head, almost fondly. "He was wrong, of course. Or right in a way that didn't matter. Same thing, usually."

Theo kept her face still. Somewhere below her ribs, older grief shifted like ballast in a hold. "You came for my tools."

"I came for your tools." He gestured toward the cut, the dead rails running down into the bramble. "Walk me through what you're doing here first. Humor me. I like to understand what I'm taking away."

So she did—because refusing would cost more than complying, and because explaining was a kind of armor. She talked about grades and drainage and the frost-heave that had buckled the culvert, and Pell listened the way few laymen listened, asking one question about load ratings that showed he'd done his reading. All the while some cold compartment of her mind ran a separate calculation: *he came himself*. Collectors did not come themselves for four hundred pounds. Collectors sent clerks. Ambrose Pell had driven two days into hill country to stand in the rain-rotted bracken and watch a disgraced woman measure a dead railway, and that meant the four hundred pounds was not why he had come.

*Salvage.* The word arrived in her head unbidden and sat there, glowing like a signal lamp.

The storm three nights past had been the worst in a season. Half the river towns had reported wreckage; the customs house at Aldmere Landing logged flotsam reports by the dozen after any hard blow. And salvage reports were public record. She had filed hers herself, because not filing was its own kind of flag—and now she understood that the record was a lamp she had lit in a dark window, and moths were coming.

"You've heard," Pell said, when her explanation wound down, "about the wreck they pulled out of the shallows below Corry's Bend."

"I've heard the river moved a lot of things."

"A steamer, they think. Old. Pre-Annexation hull, by the timbers that washed up." He was watching her with those mild eyes, and his voice stayed conversational, almost idle, a man discussing weather. "Customs keeps a list of claimants. Your name's on it."

"Half the landing's name is on it. A storm like that, everyone walks the banks looking for free timber."

"Mm." He turned a slow circle, taking in the derelict spur, the hills going blue with evening beyond it. "Here's my difficulty, Miss Kavanagh. The Syndicate has interests along this river. Historical interests. And when things wash out of that river—things from before the Annexation—those interests occasionally become... complicated. So when I hear that a woman with debts to us has filed a salvage claim on pre-Annexation wreckage, I don't think about four hundred pounds." He looked back at her. "I think about what else might be in the water."

"Timber," Theo said. "Rope. A ship's bell if she's lucky. Salvage is mud work, Mr. Pell. If there were anything valuable in that wreck, the river would have kept it, and the river doesn't file reports."

The lie came out smooth and level, pitched exactly between boredom and candor, and she watched it land. Pell studied her for a long moment. Under his gaze she felt the chest sitting thirty feet below the water table in her cellar, wrapped in oilcloth under a false floor she'd built for hiding things from her father's creditors, and she willed her pulse to slow, willed her hands to hang easy, and thought: *the figures are the figures.*

"You're good at this," Pell said at last, and it was not clear whether he meant the surveying or the lying, and she suspected he meant both. "All right. Here's what I'll do, because I'm feeling generous and because the board prefers recovery to ruin. One week. In seven days I return, and either the debt is settled or the tools come with me—and if I learn, in the meantime, that there was more in that wreck than timber..." He let the sentence die, which was its own kind of threat, cleaner than finishing it. "Well. Debts are simple, Miss Kavanagh. It's the secrets that accrue interest."

"I don't keep secrets."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Nobody does. That's rather the point of my profession." He tipped his hat. "Seven days."

* * *

She watched the carriage until it topped the ridge and dropped from sight, and then she stood a long while in the failing light with the wind coming up the valley smelling of rain and iron, and let herself feel the fear properly—one long, controlled breath of it, the way she'd once vented pressure from a boiler, slow enough not to rupture anything.

Then she went home and got to work.

Hiding was no longer possible. She saw that plainly now, and strange to say the seeing steadied her. For three days she had crouched over the chest like a woman guarding a grave, and crouching was a posture for prey. Pell had given her seven days, and seven days was not a sentence—it was a schedule. Theo Kavanagh had spent her whole life turning other people's deadlines into working drawings.

By lamplight she made her lists. What the documents were worth. Who might pay for them—or worse, who might kill for them. What it would take to move them somewhere Pell could not find them, which meant off the roads, which meant the river, which meant a boat and a crew and money she did not have. Her eye fell on the key from the chest, small and heavy, brass gone dark with age, stamped with the same crossed keys as the seal. Whatever it opened, it opened somewhere, and things that had locks had owners, and owners had enemies.

Her father had died chasing exactly this. She had spent ten years telling herself he'd died for nothing—for pride, for a lost cause, for figures that were right in a way that didn't matter. Pell had said it casually, kindly even, and it had landed like a spur driven home. But Pell had also driven two days into the hills over rumors of timber. Men did not do that for nothing.

She spread the freshest grant across her drafting table, weighted its corners, and read the signatures again by the lamp's small circle. Fresh ink on twenty-year-old parchment. Names she recognized from newspaper front pages—men who had signed the Annexation, men who had mourned Vessany publicly and profited privately. The figures were the figures. Her father had been right. He had simply been alone, and early, and dead for it.

"Not this time," she said aloud, to the empty room, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out.

She worked until midnight. By the end of it she had a plan with the shape of a plan: sell what couldn't be carried, hire what couldn't be bought, and get the chest downriver to whatever that key unlocked before Ambrose Pell's seven days ran out. It was a bad plan in three places and a desperate one in five, but it had a load path, and every structure she'd ever trusted had started the same way.

At the bottom of the page she wrote a single line and stared at it a long moment:

*Sefa Ilo — Kestrel — runs the lower Aldmere, cash or shares.*

She'd seen the steamer twice from the survey cuts, patched and smoke-scarred, piloting water that honest boats wouldn't touch. Its captain had a reputation for asking no questions and answering fewer.

Seven days. Theo blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the river talking to itself in the distance, patient as everything else that meant to have its way.

Tomorrow she would go and find out what a share of the truth cost.
