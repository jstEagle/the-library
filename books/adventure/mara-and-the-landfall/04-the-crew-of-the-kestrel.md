## 4. The Crew of the Kestrel

The Kestrel sat in the mud like a patient animal that had stopped expecting kindness.

Theo found her at the third slip down from the fish market, where the harbor silted shallow and the shipwrights had given up pretending the berths were worth rent. She'd walked past twice before she let herself stop, because a woman who looked too long at one boat was a woman asking questions, and Theo Kavanagh had spent three days learning how to be invisible in her own city—collar up, eyes down, taking the long way around streets where Pell's collectors drank their midday coffee.

The steamer was forty feet of stubbornness and rust. Her paddle housings had been patched with plate steel of at least three different vintages; her funnel leaned a degree or two off true, not from damage but from someone's careless welding years ago; and her name had been repainted so many times the letters stood out in relief, ghosting through each layer like old scars through new skin. *Kestrel*. A small hawk that hunted things bigger than itself.

A woman was working on the deck, on her back beneath the housing of the paddle wheel, with her boots braced against a stanchion and a wrench turning in slow, deliberate arcs. She did not look up when Theo stepped onto the gangplank. The plank announced her—the whole hull shifted under the weight—but the woman kept turning the wrench.

"Slip's spoken for," she said.

"I'm not here about the slip."

"Everyone's here about the slip." The wrench stopped. "Rent, or salvage rights, or they want to know if I'll run cargo for them and then they want to know what kind. You've been past twice. Third time means you're deciding whether I'm desperate enough to say yes to something stupid."

Theo considered lying. She found she didn't have the appetite for it this early in the morning.

"Are you?"

The woman slid out from under the housing and sat up, and Theo got her first real look at Sefa Ilo. She was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders in the way of people who had spent their lives hauling lines, with skin weathered to the color of teak and a white scar running from her left ear down into her collar. Her eyes were the flat gray-green of the Aldmere in February, and they took Theo apart with one unhurried pass: the surveyor's coat too good for her current circumstances, the ink stains on her right hand, the way she stood with her weight balanced as if the deck might move under her.

"Desperate enough for money," Sefa said. "Not stupid enough for most offers. Which are you carrying?"

Theo crouched, laid her hand flat on the deck planking. Warm already from the sun, dry, sound. Whoever maintained this boat maintained her out of love or obsession, and both were useful.

"Shares," she said. "One passage upriver, no questions about cargo beyond what you need to know to keep us alive. A fifth share of what we find at the end of it."

"Find." Sefa laughed once, without humor. "Salvage. That's what the last three men who stood where you're standing said, and two of them owed me money by the end and the third owed me an apology I'm still waiting on."

"What happened to the third?"

"He ran cargo for people who thought a river-runner was disposable." She flexed her right hand, and Theo saw the fingers didn't close all the way. "I don't run for those people anymore. So before you waste your morning: who's after your cargo? Because there's always someone after the cargo. Collectors, customs men, worse. I can smell it on you. You've been looking over your shoulder since you stepped on my plank."

Theo held her gaze. There was no point building anything on sand, and something told her Sefa Ilo could taste a lie the way catfish tasted blood in muddy water.

"A syndicate collector," she said. "Harrow. He thinks I have something I shouldn't. He's right."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere behind them the fish market was waking up—gulls screaming over the gutting tables, the slap of ice, a boy crying the price of eels. The river moved against the hull with its patient, indifferent sound.

"Harrow," Sefa said at last. "You know I used to work for them."

Theo hadn't known. She let it show on her face rather than pretend otherwise.

"Eleven years. Pilot, then master. Ran their grain, their ore, their quiet little crates they didn't put on manifests." Sefa stood, joints cracking, and looked downriver toward the open water as if she could see something in it. "Then I carried a load I wasn't told the contents of, and when it went wrong, they decided the wrong was mine. Took my license. Took eleven years of wages in 'fees.' Left me this." She patted the rail of the Kestrel the way another woman might pat a horse's neck. "I bought her back piece by piece doing work I'm not proud of. So. Harrow Syndicate wants what you have, and you want to take it somewhere they can't reach, and you're offering me a cut of revenge with a stranger's arithmetic attached."

"Yes."

"Why should I believe your fifth is worth anything?"

"Because I'll show you enough to judge. Not everything. Enough."

Sefa studied her for a long time. Then she jerked her chin toward the cabin door. "Coffee's on the stove. Come tell me which stretch of river you mean, and I'll tell you if you're going to die."

* * *

They talked for two hours while the tide turned. Theo gave her the lower Aldmere, the derelict locks, the drowned country above the old Vessany line—and watched Sefa's face change when she named the reaches above the junction station. Not fear exactly. Recognition.

"No one runs that water," Sefa said. "Flood season's coming early this year. The channel shifts every week."

"That's why I need you and not some harbor captain with a clean boat."

"Flattery."

"Accuracy."

Sefa snorted, but Theo saw the corner of her mouth move. "I'll need an engineer. My last one drank himself into the current in March, and I won't run those locks with a boiler I don't trust. I know a man. Bram Oduya. He'll cost you."

"Everyone costs me. That's the arrangement."

"Bram doesn't cost money." Sefa poured the last of the coffee and looked at Theo over the rim of her cup. "He costs answers. He was an examiner for the railways, before. Before your father's line closed, if I remember right. He asks questions until he understands a thing completely, and then he decides whether he likes it. If you're hiding something ugly, he'll find it, and then you'll have to live with what he does next."

Theo thought of the ledgers in their oilcloth wrapping, sewn into the lining of her coat these three nights, heavy against her ribs like a second heart.

"Then I'd better decide what I'm willing to live with," she said, "before he asks."

* * *

Bram Oduya worked out of a shed behind a chandlery that smelled of tar and hot iron, and he received them standing in his doorway with the particular stillness of a man who had already noticed everything worth noticing.

He was younger than Theo expected—forty, maybe less, with gray coming in early at his temples and hands so stained with machine oil they looked cast in bronze. Behind him, visible through the doorway, a disassembled boiler gauge lay across his bench in ordered rows, each part cleaned, each part labeled in a small precise hand.

"Sefa says you need an engineer for the upper reaches," he said. His voice was quiet and carried anyway. "She also says you're paying in shares of something unnamed, that a Harrow collector is circling you, and that you flinched—just slightly—when Sefa mentioned railway examiners." He stepped aside to let them in. "I notice things. It made me unpopular in the service. Sit, if you like. There's one chair and it's mine, so most people stand."

Theo stood. She appreciated a man who told you the rules of his room upfront.

"You examined the northern line closures," she said. "Fourteen years ago."

"I wrote the minority report." He said it without emphasis, but the words had been polished smooth by repetition, the way stones get smooth in a current. "The majority found structural failure justified the shutdown. I found the structural failure was real but secondary, and that the primary cause was a financing collapse nobody could adequately explain. My report was filed. Then it was unfiled. Then I was reassigned to inspecting culverts in the southern provinces until I resigned." He folded his arms. "So. You're going upriver along a dead line built on grants from a dead nation, pursued by the syndicate that helped kill it, carrying something you won't name, and you want me to keep your boiler alive while you do whatever it is you're actually doing."

"Yes."

"And my share?"

"A fifth. Same as Sefa's."

"Of what?"

"The truth," Theo said, "eventually. And whatever it's worth."

Something flickered in Bram's face—not hunger, exactly. More like thirst finally offered water it didn't trust. He crossed to his bench, picked up one small brass fitting, turned it over in his stained fingers.

"I spent fourteen years being told the numbers didn't mean what they plainly meant," he said. "I'd like, this once, to be on the side of the numbers." He set the fitting down. "I'll come. But understand me, Miss Kavanagh—I will read everything on that boat. Every paper, every manifest, every mark on every crate. Not because I'm your enemy. Because if I'm going to die in floodwater on a dead man's railway, I intend to know precisely why."

"That," Theo said, and meant it, "is the most reassuring thing anyone has said to me in weeks."

* * *

Nils came last, and came cheap, and Theo would remember later that this should have warned her.

Sefa found him at the dockside cookhouse where he worked the morning shift and slept in the loft above it, a lean, sandy-haired northerner of maybe twenty-five with a burn scar on his forearm and a laugh that arrived half a second before he did. He signed on as cook and deckhand for a sixth share and seemed genuinely delighted about it, which itself was strange—a sixth share of nothing was nothing, and everyone else had at least pretended to negotiate.

"I don't care what we're hauling," Nils said, wiping his hands on his apron and grinning at all three of them around the galley table of the Kestrel that evening. "I've been cooking dock food for two years. Dock food is a crime against God. Give me a stove that doesn't rock and a crew that isn't fishermen, and I'd have done it for free."

"Don't say that in front of him," Sefa muttered, jerking a thumb at Bram. "He'll hold you to it."

But Theo watched him. She couldn't help it; watching people was the one habit her father had taught her that she'd never managed to break. And Nils was quick—quicker than a cook needed to be. When Sefa mentioned the derelict locks, his eyes went to the chart on the wall and found the upper reaches before she finished pointing. When Bram talked boiler pressure, Nils asked one question, a good one, about feed-water quality in silty water, and then covered it with a joke about tea. He listened the way a man listens when he's memorizing.

And once—only once—when Theo mentioned the Harrow collector by name, Ambrose Pell, Nils's knife stopped moving on the onion board. Just for a beat. Then it resumed, and he made a joke about syndicate men and their tailored coats, and everyone laughed, and the moment sank away into the clatter of the galley.

Later, alone in the narrow cabin she'd claimed, Theo sat on the bunk with the chest's key in her palm and turned it over and over, catching the lamplight on its worn brass. Four of them now. A pilot with a grudge, an engineer with a wound, a cook with a secret, and herself with a chest full of proof that the world was more rotten than any of them yet knew. Shares instead of questions—that had been her design, her clean little architecture. But questions leaked in anyway. They always did. Trust wasn't a wall you built; it was a leak you bailed, and you bailed it every day, and you hoped the sea stayed smaller than your bucket.

She heard the others above her—Sefa's low voice running through the pre-departure list, Bram's quieter one answering, Nils laughing at something—and felt the strange, unwelcome warmth of it. Crew. She hadn't belonged to a crew since her father died, and she had promised herself she never would again, because crews were just more people to lose.

The warmth stayed anyway.

They were to cast off at first light on the morning tide, ahead of the flood, ahead of Pell. Theo blew out the lamp and lay back and let the river rock her, and was nearly asleep when she heard the galley door open softly below, and footsteps on the wet deck, and the scratch of a match.

Through the cabin's salt-fogged porthole she saw, briefly, a glow at the stern rail—someone smoking where they shouldn't, facing downriver, toward the harbor mouth and the dark water beyond it. Standing very still. The way a man stands when he's waiting for an answer.

By the time she reached the deck, the cigarette was gone, the rail was empty, and Nils was coming up from below with an armful of kindling, whistling, asking if anyone wanted coffee before bed.

Theo smiled and said yes.

And in the morning, as the Kestrel backed slowly out of her slip into the gray-gold light of the rising tide, a customs launch cut across the harbor toward them, white ensign snapping, and a woman in a magistrate's coat stood at its bow with a folder in her hand, watching them come.
