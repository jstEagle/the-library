## 5. Magistrate Wren

The customs house at Corren's Landing had been built to impress, back when the river trade was worth impressing, and now it mostly impressed the pigeons. Theo climbed its salt-stained steps at half past eight in the morning with a salvage declaration folded in her coat pocket and a lie rehearsed so thoroughly she could almost believe it.

The hall inside smelled of tar, old paper, and the particular staleness of rooms where men stamped things for a living. A clerk dozed behind a counter scarred by decades of cargo hooks. Theo set her hat on the counter deliberately — a small noise, an honest noise — and waited for him to surface.

"Declaration of salvage," she said, when he blinked awake. "Wreckage recovered from the Aldmere shallows below Harrow Bend. Personal effects, ship's fittings, nothing of value."

The clerk took the paper without reading it. That was the beauty of small lies: they were shaped exactly like the truth nobody wanted to hear. Wreckage on the Aldmere was weather, not news. He stamped it, slid it into a drawer thick with its brothers, and went back to sleep standing up.

Theo turned for the door and stopped.

A woman stood in the entrance, blocking most of the morning light. She wore a magistrate's grey coat, travel-dusted, cut plainly but well, and she carried herself the way surveyors carry their instruments — carefully, because everything depended on them. Her hair was dark going iron at the temples, pinned back with the severity of someone who had decided long ago that vanity was a tax she refused to pay. Her eyes found Theo across the hall with the unhurried certainty of a compass needle.

"Theo Kavanagh," the woman said. Not a question.

"I'm expected nowhere."

"No," the woman agreed. "That's rather the point." She crossed the hall, boots ringing on stone worn hollow by forty years of freight clerks. "Elara Wren. Circuit magistrate, third district. You filed a salvage report four days ago through the Harrow Bend waystation. I've read it."

"It's stamped. You can see it's stamped."

"I can see it's stamped *thin*." Wren's voice was level, almost gentle, which made it worse. "'Personal effects, ship's fittings, nothing of value.' Filed by a licensed engineer who was, according to the same report, surveying a closed spur eleven miles from any navigable water. Explain that to me as if I were stupid, Miss Kavanagh. It will go faster."

* * *

Wren watched the engineer consider her options, and found herself doing something she rarely permitted: admiring the opposition.

It was in the hands first. Most people lied with their mouths and their eyes gave them away within a sentence. This woman lied with her whole body evenly distributed — the tired set of her shoulders, the honest dust on her boots, the faint impatience of someone with better things to do. Only her hands were wrong. They hung too still. Liars fidgeted; this one had trained stillness into herself like a discipline, and it told Wren more than any confession would have.

"You've been misinformed, Magistrate," Kavanagh said. "I surveyed the spur. The storm flooded the low fields. On my way back along the levee road I saw wreckage in the shallows and did what anyone would — pulled out what was reachable, reported it, kept what was mine by right of salvage. There's a chest of rotted linens and a ship's bell with no name on it sitting in my shed if you'd like to see it."

"There is," Wren said. "I'd like that very much. But first—" she let the pause stretch until the clerk behind the counter pretended very hard to be asleep— "let's talk about the Vespera."

She watched the name land. Not a flinch — Kavanagh was far too good for a flinch — but something behind the eyes recalibrated, a surveyor's instrument being re-leveled after a tremor.

"Don't know it."

"A coastal packet out of Vessany. Lost with all hands twenty-two years ago, in waters she had no business sailing. Her loss was entered into the record the same season the Vessany grants were voided and the Harrow Syndicate absorbed two hundred thousand acres of northern territory at bankruptcy prices." Wren recited it the way she recited charges: flatly, precisely, letting each fact bear its own weight. "Three weeks ago, a fisherman downriver reports a storm has shifted the shoals below Harrow Bend and uncovered old hull timbers stamped with brass fittings no Aldmere trader ever used. Two days ago, a licensed railroad engineer files a salvage report claiming she happened past. And yesterday, Ambrose Pell — whom you may know as the Syndicate's collector, though I suspect you know him better than you'd like — paid cash for a fast launch at Corren's Landing and asked questions about you by name."

The hall was quiet except for the pigeons shifting in the rafters and the river muttering against the pilings outside.

"So," Wren said. "You've been misinformed, or I have. One of us is about to be embarrassed. I've learned to survive embarrassment. Can you?"

* * *

Theo felt the trap close around her with the slow, hydraulic patience of good engineering. No sudden force. Just load applied correctly, at the correct angle, until the structure had no choice.

Pell had bought a launch. Pell had asked about her by name. The week she'd bought from him was already spent, then — or worse, he'd never intended to honor it, and the visit to her workshop had been reconnaissance while his real work happened elsewhere.

And this magistrate knew about the Vespera. Which meant the fisherman had talked, or the waystation clerk had, or Wren had simply done what Theo did — followed the load paths, found where the weight actually rested.

"I know who Ambrose Pell is," Theo said carefully. "He holds paper on my tools. He came to repossess them and I talked him into a week. If he's asking about me now, it's because he thinks I found something worth more than my debt. I haven't."

"That's the second time you've said some version of *nothing of value*, and both times your hands didn't move." Wren glanced down at them, then up. "You keep them still when you lie. It's a good habit. It nearly worked."

Heat crawled up Theo's neck. She thought of the chest under the loose floorboards of her rented room, wrapped in oilcloth, cold as a held breath. The ledgers with their columns of figures in a dead nation's hand. The grants bearing signatures dated *after* Vessany had ceased to exist. The key.

She thought of her father, and put the thought away the way she always did — quickly, before it could bleed.

"What is it you want, Magistrate?"

"To help you." Wren said it simply, and the terrible thing was that Theo believed she believed it. "You're standing between a corrupt syndicate and whatever you pulled out of that river, and you're doing it alone, with debt collectors and — if I'm right about the Vespera — much worse coming. I can give you protection. Protective custody for the salvaged materials, a stay on your debts pending investigation, my personal warrant over anything Pell tries. In exchange, the chest comes to the district court. Sealed, catalogued, entered into evidence. For the court, Miss Kavanagh. Not for me. I don't want what's in it. I want it to matter."

"And if the court decides it doesn't matter?"

Something moved behind Wren's eyes then — quick, quickly suppressed, like a fish turning under ice.

"Then it goes through proper channels and you have my word it won't be buried."

"Your word." Theo kept her voice mild. "Forgive me, Magistrate, but your word travels through exactly the institutions the Harrow Syndicate owns. Judges. Registrars. The men who signed off on the Vessany collapse are the men your appeals would climb toward, rung by rung. You'd be handing a lantern to people whose business is darkness and asking them to carry it carefully."

"You'd rather trust Ambrose Pell?"

"I'd rather trust no one. It's cheaper."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Outside, a barge horn sounded, long and low, and the pigeons exploded upward through the rafters in a clatter of wings and dust.

* * *

Wren had heard every variety of distrust in eleven years on the circuit. Cynicism from the guilty, paranoia from the hunted, contempt from the rich. But this was none of those. This was arithmetic. Kavanagh distrusted the courts the way an engineer distrusts a bridge — not out of malice, but from having calculated the loads and found them wanting.

And the worst of it, the part Wren would not say aloud even to herself, was that the calculation wasn't wrong.

She had spent her career believing the law was a machine that ground slowly but ground true, and she had spent the last three years watching sand get into the gears. A land dispute in Ostermere settled suspiciously fast. An arson case that died in chambers. A promotion she'd been promised twice, dangled and withdrawn, always with a smile that suggested the price of it was something she hadn't yet been asked to pay. She had told herself these were friction, not failure. Standing in the customs house looking at a woman who had clearly decided otherwise, Wren felt the distinction wobble under her feet like deck plating.

"You think I'm naive," she said.

"I think you're honest," Theo said. "Which in your position amounts to the same thing, and is more dangerous."

Wren laughed — short, surprised out of her. "You know, most people flatter me."

"I don't flatter. It wastes time and it bends measurements."

"Then measure this." Wren stepped closer, and lowered her voice beneath the clerk's hearing. "Twenty-two years ago, my predecessor in this district opened an inquiry into the Vessany bankruptcy. He was reassigned within the month. To a posting so remote they had to build the courthouse after he arrived. His files were sealed, and the seal was countersigned by an appellate judge whose name you'll find on the charter renewal of the Harrow Syndicate. I found that file when I inherited the office. I've been carrying it for six years, waiting for a second document to corroborate it. Whatever you pulled out of the Aldmere — I believe it's that document. So understand me: I am not offering to take your evidence into a machine I fully trust. I am offering to stand between you and the machine while we find out together whether it still works."

There it was. Wren heard herself say it and felt the strange vertigo of a conviction spoken aloud for the first time — how saying a thing changed its weight, made it real, made it refutable.

Kavanagh studied her for a long moment. Those grey eyes went through Wren like a lamp through fog, taking soundings.

"Why tell me that?" the engineer asked quietly. "You've just handed a stranger leverage over you. That's not procedure."

"No. It isn't."

"Then why?"

"Because you're going to make your decision based on whether you trust *me*, not my office. And I'd rather you decide with accurate figures." Wren straightened her coat, restoring the uniformity she'd briefly let slip. "So. The chest, sealed into evidence, with my warrant and my name attached. Protection, a stay on your debts, and my personal guarantee that it sees daylight. Or you walk out of here, and I follow you at a legal distance, and we find out which of us the river treats more kindly."

* * *

Theo looked at the magistrate and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that she was looking at what she might have been. Another life, another set of chances — a woman who had taken her precision and her stubbornness and pointed them at institutions instead of rivers. Someone who still believed the rules were a machine worth repairing rather than a wreck worth scavenging.

And that was exactly why she couldn't say yes.

Not because Wren was false. Because Wren was true, and true things got broken here. Theo's father had been true. He had carried his own chest of documents to the registrar at Northgate twenty years ago, certain the system would hold, and the system had held him instead — held him until the inquest called it an accident on unlit track, held his daughter's inheritance until the Syndicate bought it at auction, held his name in a file somewhere that Elara Wren would probably have loved to read.

Rules were load-bearing structures. Theo believed that. But you didn't hand your only beam to a structure that was already cracking. You shored yourself first.

"Magistrate," she said, "I'm going to do something discourteous."

"You're going to stall."

"I'm going to stall. I need three days. There are — matters to settle. Papers to gather. If I'm going to enter evidence, I intend to enter all of it, complete, in order, properly documented. Half a truth helps no one. You of all people should appreciate that."

Wren's jaw tightened. Three days was an eternity and they both knew it; three days was enough time to run, to sink, to burn. But there was nothing in the offer Theo had made that a court could compel, and they both knew that too.

"Three days," Wren said at last. "At noon on the third day, I come to you. Where?"

"The Kestrel," Theo said, before she could stop herself, and cursed inwardly. "A steamer. River-runner. I'm — hiring passage north. Surveying work."

Something flickered across Wren's face — not suspicion exactly, but the look of a woman filing a fact into a drawer she'd open later. "The Kestrel. Sefa Ilo's boat."

"You know her."

"I know her file." Wren stepped back, and the morning light came in around her again, ordinary and indifferent. "Noon, third day, aboard the Kestrel. If you're there with the chest, Miss Kavanagh, you'll have no stronger friend in this district than me. If you're not—" she paused at the door, silhouetted against the bright water beyond, "—then I'll grieve, and then I'll find you. I'm very good at finding people. It's the only part of the job they've never managed to take from me."

She was gone. Theo stood in the emptying hall, listening to the footsteps fade on the stone, feeling the shape of the next three days close around her like water around a sinking thing.

Behind the counter, the clerk slept on. The stamp lay beside his hand, and Theo looked at it for a long moment — the dull brass face of it, the official seal worn smooth by ten thousand small approvals — and thought: *that little stamp has buried more truth than any shovel.*

Then she picked up her hat and went to hire a crew, because three days was not a deadline anymore.

It was a head start.
