## 6. Downriver

Bram Oduya had spent eleven years listening to engines, and he could tell you a ship's health by the sound of her before he ever laid a hand on her plating. The Kestrel spoke with a lisp—a slight irregularity in the starboard cylinder's rhythm, a hitch like a stammer in an otherwise steady sentence—but she spoke honestly, and after three days on the lower Aldmere, Bram had begun to think of that stutter as affection rather than defect.

The fog came off the water at dusk and did not lift again until midmorning, if it lifted at all. It lay over the river in banks and shelves, thick as wool, and Sefa Ilo steered through it the way a blind woman walks her own hallway: by memory, by the feel of the current against the hull, by small sounds no one else could name. She stood at the wheel with one hand resting on the brass rail and the other holding a tin cup of tea gone cold hours ago, and she never once raised her voice.

"Lock ahead," she said, in the tone another pilot might use to remark on the weather. "Number nine. Gates'll be rotted through on the left leaf. We go in slow, port side first, and Nils drops the fender line."

Nils dropped the fender line.

The derelict locks of the old Vessany navigation works came at them out of the white like the ribs of drowned animals. Twenty years of neglect had not destroyed them—Vessany had built to last, built for a nation that its own engineers had believed would outlive empires—and that was somehow worse than ruin. The stone was sound. The ironwork, though furred with rust, still held true in its sockets. The machinery of the place had been abandoned mid-life, not worn out, and standing in the lock chamber while the Kestrel's boiler breathed and the water rose around them, Bram felt like a surgeon operating in a hospital where every instrument was clean and every bed empty.

"Chamber's tight," Theo said beside him. She had come down from the bow without his noticing, which was itself remarkable; most people announced themselves into a space. Theo Kavanagh arrived the way fog did. "Whoever poured this concrete knew their business."

"Vessany knew their business," Bram said. "That's rather the point of everything in that chest you won't let me finish reading."

She gave him a look—not hostile, but measuring, the look of a woman deciding how much rope to pay out—and said nothing, and went forward again, and Bram climbed back into the engine room feeling the weight of the ledgers in the strongbox under his bunk like a second heartbeat.

* * *

He worked on them at night, when the Kestrel tied up or drifted at anchor in the slack water behind some shingle bar, and the others slept, and the only light was a shaded lamp and the only sound the tick of cooling metal. He was, by training and temperament, a man of figures. He had kept books for a sugar concern in Corvane until the concern collapsed and took his savings with it; he had audited freight manifests for a shipping line until the line was bought by men who wanted the audits to say what they said. Numbers did not lie, but they could be made to stand in a lineup wearing someone else's coat, and Bram had built a career on recognizing the coat.

The Vessany ledgers were beautiful. That was the first thing that unsettled him. They were bound in gray cloth, stamped with the seal he had now seen often enough to know in his sleep—the crowned bridge, the two rivers meeting—and the hand that filled them was precise, disciplined, almost loving. Column after column of disbursements: payments to surveyors, to magistrates' clerks, to a newspaper called the *Aldmere Argus*, to a man identified only as H— whose sums dwarfed all the rest.

The second thing that unsettled him was the arithmetic.

He found it on the fourth night, running the columns against each other the way he'd run pressure readings against gauge tolerances, looking for the discrepancy that would tell him where the fraud was. There was no discrepancy. That was the discovery. Every figure reconciled. Every payment matched a receipt, every receipt matched an authorization, every authorization traced back through the chain of command to a signature at the top—and the signature at the top belonged to Harrow Syndicate officers who had sworn, in open court, twenty years ago, that they had lost everything in the collapse. Bankrupt. Ruined. Their grants escheated to the crown, their assets scattered to creditors.

And here were their books, balanced to the penny, covering the exact years of their supposed ruin.

Bram sat back from the lamp and looked at the fog pressing against the porthole glass, and let the shape of it assemble itself in his mind the way a machine assembles in the dark when you finally find the one gear you've been missing.

They hadn't lost the grants. They had *manufactured* the collapse. The bankruptcy was a transaction—an entry in these very ledgers, itemized. Payments to judges to accelerate the escheatment. Payments to clerks to misfile the appeals. Payments, God help him, to the men who had then been appointed to administer the "abandoned" lands, so that the syndicate's own officers, wearing new hats, leased the territory back from the crown at rates set by their own valuations. The nation of Vessany had not fallen. It had been harvested. And the harvest had never stopped. The fresh signatures on the land grants in Theo's chest were continuations of a ledger that ran unbroken from the fall to the present day—to this year. To this season.

To the debt collectors currently sniffing up and down this river.

Bram closed the ledger and sat very still, and heard, distantly, the Kestrel's anchor chain shift against the shingle, and thought: *I have been hired hands my whole life. I have kept honest numbers for dishonest men and called it survival.*

* * *

In the morning the fog thinned to gauze and Pell's launch appeared.

Sefa saw it first—or rather, she saw the absence of it first, the wrongness in the pattern of the morning mist downstream, a patch where the fog moved against the current instead of with it. She said nothing for a quarter hour. Then she turned the wheel two spokes and altered their course toward the far bank, casual as a change of mind, and watched the mirror-image alteration bloom in the fog behind them.

"There," she said.

Theo came up from below with the chart case under her arm. Bram followed. Through the thinning haze they could see it clearly now: a long black launch, twin stacks, low freeboard, riding the middle channel a half mile back with the patience of something that fed on time. No flag. No name visible. Just the steady beat of her screws carrying across the water like a pulse counted through a wall.

"He's not closing," Theo said. "He's herding."

"Herding where?" Nils asked, and there was something in his voice Bram noted and did not yet understand—a fraction too much eagerness, a question asked a half-beat too fast.

"Downstream," Sefa answered for all of them. "There's only one place worth herding us to, if you know the river. The Narrows." She spat over the rail, an old habit, unselfconscious. "But the Narrows flood late in the season, and we're early, and he knows the channels less well than he thinks. Let him follow. Fog's my country."

It was true, mostly. But Bram had spent the night reading the syndicate's handwriting, and he understood now that a man like Ambrose Pell did not need to know the channels. He needed only to know where the ship was going, and wait there, and be paid either way. The launch held its distance all day, a black stitch in the seam of the fog, and the knowledge of what Bram had read sat in him like coal in a firebox, building heat.

* * *

It was Nils who broke the silence first, that evening, in the way that Nils broke silences—sideways, through work. He was splicing a mooring line on the foredeck, and Bram came out to smoke the pipe he allowed himself once a day, and the two of them ended up sitting against the deckhouse in the last gray light while the black launch hung in the distance like a promise neither of them liked.

"My brother's inside on syndicate testimony," Nils said, to the rope, not to Bram. "Did you know that?"

"I guessed it was something like that."

"Eight years. For a theft he didn't do, in a yard he wasn't working. A man testified against him who'd never seen him before in his life, and the court ate it whole because the court was paid to eat it whole." Nils pulled the splice tight. His hands were enormous and gentle with the rope, the hands of a man who cooked for a living and could have crushed walnuts idly. "I signed onto this ship for shares. That's what I told myself. Shares, and a new start, and to hell with the past."

"And now?"

"Now I've been watching you come up from below every night with your eyes full of something, and I've been thinking about what a man does when he finds out the whole river is poisoned upstream." He looked at Bram directly then. "What's in those books, engineer? Tell me plain. I'm not clever, but I can carry heavy things."

So Bram told him plain. Not everything—he kept the names, the specific names, the ones that would burn a hole through any pocket they rode in—but the shape of it. The manufactured collapse. The balanced books of a bankrupt empire. The leases signed by the same hands that had signed away the land. He watched Nils's face as he spoke, and what he saw there was not surprise. It was recognition. It was the look of a man hearing, for the first time spoken aloud, the explanation for a wound he'd carried so long he'd stopped asking how he got it.

"They never lost anything," Nils said slowly. "My brother's inside because losing things has to look real. Somebody has to go to prison so the bankruptcy reads true." He laughed, short and terrible. "Eight years. My brother is eight years of somebody else's bookkeeping."

"That's what it is," Bram said. "All of it. Every ruin on this river, every closed line, every dead town—the whole landscape is entries in a ledger. We've been sailing through somebody's arithmetic."

* * *

Theo joined them at the rail an hour later, drawn, Bram suspected, by the sound of voices where there should have been none. She listened while Nils repeated it back to her in his own words, blunt and unadorned, and Bram watched her take the weight of it. She had known the chest was dangerous. She had known it since the night she pulled it dripping from the shallows. But knowing a thing is dangerous and understanding *why* are different cargoes, and Bram could see the second one settling on her shoulders now, changing her stance the way extra ballast changes a ship's roll.

"The ledgers prove intent," she said at last. "Not just corruption. Intent. Premeditation, across twenty years, documented by their own hands." Her voice was level, but her knuckles were white on the rail. "My father spent six years trying to convince a court of half of this. He had fragments. Invoices. A witness who recanted. They buried him in paperwork and then, when that wasn't enough—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He died believing he'd failed. Believing the failure was his. That he'd been too slow, too poor, too alone."

"He wasn't wrong about being alone," Bram said. "One man with fragments is a crank. That's how they win—not the courts, not the guns. They make truth look like madness by rationing it."

"Then we don't ration it," Theo said.

It was not a plan. It was not even a decision, quite. But Bram felt the deck change under his feet all the same, some subtle shift in the ship's soul, the way a crew changes when a voyage stops being a job. He had seen it before, in the sugar boats and the coastal steamers—in the moment when men and women stop hauling cargo and start hauling meaning. It happened in the body before it happened in the mind. Sefa at the wheel stood a little straighter. Nils coiled his lines with a new economy, nothing wasted. Even the Kestrel's lopsided engine note seemed, in Bram's imagination at least, to steady toward purpose.

They were not hired hands anymore. He could not have said the hour it happened. Perhaps it had happened in the fog, or in the dead locks, or somewhere in the gray arithmetic of the night watches. But it had happened, and everyone aboard knew it, and nobody said it aloud, because saying such things aloud invites the river to test them.

* * *

Near midnight, Bram went below to return the ledgers to the strongbox and found Theo already there, kneeling before the open chest in the lamplight, the key on its ribbon around her neck. She looked up at him, and for a moment neither spoke.

"You've read them all," she said. It was not a question.

"All but the last volume. The recent one. I saved it." He hesitated. "I suppose I was afraid of it. The old crimes are history. The recent ones are weather—we're sailing in them."

"Read the last volume," Theo said. She closed the chest and locked it, and pressed the key briefly against her sternum before tucking it back beneath her collar, a gesture so private Bram looked away. "Read it tonight. Because Pell's launch hasn't fallen back all day, and a man doesn't chase this hard for history."

Bram carried the final volume to the engine room, where the lamp burned and the gauges glowed and the great heart of the Kestrel beat its patient, lisping beat, and he opened it across his knees.

The first pages were more of the same—payments, authorizations, the familiar gray cloth of routine corruption. Then, near the middle, a letter copied into the record in full, addressed to the syndicate's board, initialled at the bottom with letters that made the fog outside seem warm by comparison. It concerned the salvage of a wreck called the *Vespera*. It concerned a sealed chest. It contained, in the flat administrative language of men who discuss murder the way they discuss freight rates, a complete accounting of what should be done if the chest were ever recovered—and by whom, and at what cost, and with what disposal of witnesses.

And at the very top of the page, a routing instruction in a different hand:

*If recovered en route to the northern landfall, close the narrows.*

Bram read it twice. Then he blew out the lamp, because a man thinking clearly needs darkness sometimes, and sat in the engine room's red glow with the sound of the river going by.

Pell was not following them.

Pell was *awaiting* them—and he already knew their destination.
