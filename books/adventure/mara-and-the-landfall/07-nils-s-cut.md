## 7. Nils's Cut

The Kestrel slept at anchor off Garrow's Bend, and Nils could not.

He lay in the fo'c'sle with his boots on, listening to the river work at the hull—the soft, patient knocking of water against iron, like a fist testing a door. Above him, through the deck planking, he could hear Sefa turning over in her bunk, Bram's pencil scratching even now, even at this hour, against ledger paper. The ship breathed around him. It was a good ship, patched and scarred and honest, and he had been aboard her eleven days, and every one of those days he had lied to her.

Nils was not a liar by nature. That was the thing nobody understood about what he'd done, or what he was about to do again. Liars enjoyed it. They liked the shape of a falsehood in the mouth, the little power of it. Nils hated lying the way some men hated deep water—hated it and did it anyway, because the alternative was drowning.

His brother was in Coldmarsh Prison, four hundred miles east, behind walls the color of old teeth. Torvald. Twenty-six years old and built like their father, all shoulders and stubbornness, with a laugh that could be heard through two closed doors. He had been there three years now, sentenced on testimony bought and paid for by the Harrow Syndicate—two witnesses who swore they'd seen Torvald fire the shot that killed a customs inspector at Vell's Landing. Nils knew, because Torvald had told him the night before the arrest, weeping into his beer, that he hadn't done it. And Nils knew something else too: the witnesses had never seen anything at all. One of them had been paid out of an account Nils had later traced, clumsily, obsessively, to a syndicate holding company with a name so bland it might have been chosen as a joke.

That was how Pell had found him. Not through cleverness—through grief. A grieving man asks questions, and questions leave tracks, and Ambrose Pell read tracks the way Sefa read water.

*Your brother is innocent,* Pell had said, in a riverside tavern eleven months ago, sliding onto the stool beside Nils as though they were old friends. *And innocent men rot while guilty ones eat well. I can change the first part. You can help me with the second.*

All Nils had to do was watch. Report. Small things—cargo manifests, crew rosters, who came aboard the ships he cooked for, who paid them, where they went. He had told himself it was nothing. Information about shipping, not people. He had told himself Torvald would be home by spring.

Spring had come twice since then. Torvald was still behind the walls, and the reports had gotten bigger, and last month Pell had asked him to do something that made his hands shake just remembering it: find out whether a surveyor named Theo Kavanagh had salvaged anything from the wreck of the Vespera, and if she had, what.

Now he sat up in the dark, swung his feet to the cold deck plates, and took the signal lamp from its bracket.

* * *

They were anchored below Garrow's Bend because Sefa didn't trust the current above it after dark, and because the derelict lock-keeper's house on the eastern bank gave them a landmark for the morning run. The house was a black tooth against the lesser black of the sky. Beyond it, up the slope, stood a signal mast left over from the days when this stretch of the Aldmere had carried barges in convoy—a tall bare pole with a rusted crossarm, meant once for flags and lamps, long dead.

Long dead, but standing. And standing was enough.

Nils went up the ladder to the aft deck quietly, though quiet hardly mattered; Bram slept like a man drugged and Sefa like a woman keeping one ear open for mutiny, which amounted to the same thing. The night was thick with river fog coming off the water in slow ropes, and the air tasted of silt and cold iron. He checked the lamp's shutter slides with his thumb. Green to port, red to starboard, clear in the middle. Three colors, three messages, if you knew the code—and Pell knew the code, because Pell had taught it to him, or rather had handed him a card with it printed on and watched until he'd memorized it and burned the card.

One flash of green: *nothing found.* Two flashes of red: *she has it.* Three flashes of white: *danger, come.*

Eleven days ago, when they'd pulled the chest dripping from the shallows of the Aldmere, Nils had stood at the rail with his heart going like a hammer and thought: *this is it, this is the moment I sell her.* And then Theo Kavanagh had knelt on the wet deck with the seawater running out of her hair and opened that chest, and the look on her face—

It wasn't triumph. Nils had seen triumph; he'd seen men win hands of cards and contracts and lawsuits, and it always looked the same, a kind of greedy brightening. What was on Theo's face was closer to grief. She'd looked at those ledgers the way you look at a grave you've just discovered belongs to someone you loved.

He hadn't flashed the lamp that night. Or the next. Or any night since, and every morning he woke telling himself *tonight*, and every night he stood at the rail with the lamp in his hand and couldn't make his thumb move the shutter, and then dawn came and he was still a coward, and Torvald was still in Coldmarsh, and the lie got one day heavier.

Tonight he made himself climb the mast.

There was a ratline ladder bolted to the signal mast itself—he'd noticed it the first evening, the way a man notices exits—and he went up it hand over hand with the lamp slung across his back, the rust flaking under his palms, until he stood on the crossarm with the whole gray world swaying gently beneath him. The river below was a moving darkness full of whispers. The Kestrel rode at anchor, small and lit at one porthole—Bram, still working, poor mad brilliant Bram, who'd barely slept since he'd started reading those ledgers.

Nils unshuttered the lamp. His fingers were numb. He told himself it was the cold.

Two flashes of red. *She has it.*

The light leapt out across the water, brief and obscene, two red eyes blinking at the empty shore. He counted five seconds, shut the slide, counted five more, flashed again. Twice more after that. Then he killed the lamp entirely and hung there on the crossarm, shaking, waiting for an answer that would come—if it came—from far downriver, from a launch with a muffled engine, a single white flash in return: *received.*

Nothing. Fog everywhere, fog like wet wool, fog that swallowed the banks and the stars and his own courage. Maybe Pell wasn't close enough to see. Maybe the fog was too thick. Maybe—

Maybe he should climb down and never speak of this again and let the whole rotten business sink with the Vespera, and write to Torvald and tell him to be patient, justice was coming, justice moved slowly but it moved—

"Nils."

Theo's voice came from directly beneath him, out of the fog, quiet as the water.

He nearly fell. His foot slipped off the crossarm and he caught the pole and hung there, breath gone, staring down at the small figure standing at the base of the mast with a lantern held low, so its light made a pool at her feet and nothing else. She was dressed. Fully dressed, boots and coat, hair tied back. She had been awake. She had been watching.

"How long," he said. His voice came out cracked.

"Since you started up." She lifted the lantern slightly, and the light climbed the mast and found him clinging there like something washed ashore. "You go up the same way every time. Left foot first, right hand high. I cook with you, Nils. I know how you move."

* * *

She made him come down. He expected shouting—Sefa woken, a knife, the plank, whatever river-runners did with traitors—but Theo said nothing at all until they were in the galley with the door shut and the single burner lit, the little room glowing orange and smelling of coffee she'd already brewed, as though she'd known he'd need it. She poured two cups and pushed one across the table at him and sat down opposite, and waited.

"I can explain," Nils said.

"You can," Theo agreed. "But let me save us both the trouble." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table, precise as everything she did. "Three years ago your brother was convicted of killing a customs inspector at Vell's Landing. The conviction rested on two witnesses. One of them, a man named Corrick Hale, was paid four hundred crowns through a shell company called Meridian Holdings, which is a syndicate account—I know because Bram found Meridian in the ledgers, in a column labeled *contingencies, legal*. Your brother's name isn't in these books. But the shape of him is. The price of a false witness hasn't changed much in three years."

The galley seemed to tilt. Nils put his hand flat on the table to steady himself, and the table was warm from the burner, and that small warmth was somehow the worst thing, the domestic ordinary horror of it.

"How—" His voice failed. He tried again. "How long have you known?"

"Since the second night. You asked me at dinner whether I believed courts could be fair to a man without money. You asked it casually. Nobody asks that casually." She drank her coffee. "I've spent two years being asked casual questions by people who wanted to know what I knew. I've gotten good at hearing the weight under them."

"Why didn't you say something? Why didn't you throw me off at the first port?"

"Because a man who betrays for love is worth ten who stay loyal for wages." She said it flatly, without sentiment, an engineer stating a load tolerance. "Because I needed to know who you reported to and how. Because—" and here something moved behind her eyes, something older and more tired than the rest of her, "—because I've been where you're sitting. On the wrong side of a debt, doing ugly arithmetic with someone you love as the collateral. My father taught me that math. I flunked the course. I wanted to see if you would too."

"And if I'd flashed the lamp tonight and gotten away clean?"

"You wouldn't have." She set down the cup. "Pell doesn't pay off debts, Nils. He collects them. The moment you're no longer useful, your brother's pardon evaporates and your name goes on a list next to mine. You've seen what he does to lists."

Nils looked at his hands. They were a cook's hands, burned and nicked, and they were trembling, and he hated them for it. "Torvald didn't kill anyone," he said. "I have to say it out loud. To somebody. He didn't. He was drunk and stupid and he was *there*, and that's all he was. And they gave him fifteen years."

"I believe you."

"They'll never reopen it. I wrote petitions. I wrote to advocates, to newspapers, to the magistrate's office in Corvane. Nothing. You don't fight the syndicate with paper. Paper is what they own."

"Yes," Theo said. "It is." She reached into her coat and laid something on the table between them: a single page, photostat-crisp, covered in Bram's cramped annotations. "This is from the ledgers. Dated eight months before the Vessany collapse. It's a disbursement order authorizing payments to *secure favorable testimony and judicial discretion in matters arising*, signed by a syndicate director whose initials appear forty times in these books. Your brother's case is one thread of a rope, Nils. The rope runs through judges and ministers and half the magistracy of two nations. Which means your brother won't be freed by a petition. He'll be freed by exactly what's in that chest—or he'll die waiting."

The fog pressed against the galley's little porthole. Somewhere out on the water a bird cried, once, and was silent.

"So what happens to me now," Nils said. "Sefa throws me to the river?"

"Sefa doesn't know. Bram doesn't know. As of this moment, it stays that way." Theo leaned forward, and in the burner-light her face was all planes and shadow, and Nils thought, not for the first time, that she had the kind of face that got carved on monuments by people who hadn't actually met her. "Here's what happens instead. Pell is expecting a report. So you'll send one."

Nils's stomach dropped. "You want me to keep working for him."

"I want you to work for *us* while he thinks you're working for him. Tonight you signaled *she has it*. Good—that's true, and true reports build trust, and trust makes him careless. From here on, everything you tell him, I approve first. When he gives you instructions, I hear them within the hour. When the time comes, you'll feed him what we choose, and he'll swallow it, because he believes you're a frightened little man who'd sell his own mother for his brother's skin." She paused. "Are you? A frightened little man?"

"No," Nils said. It surprised him, how steady his voice came out. "I'm a big man who's been frightened for three years. There's a difference."

Something that was almost a smile touched the corner of her mouth. "Then you understand the arrangement. You spy for Pell until I tell you to stop. In exchange, when this ends—however it ends—your brother's case goes to Magistrate Wren with the Meridian payments attached, along with my sworn statement and Bram's analysis. Not a petition. Evidence. The kind that can't be buried, because it comes up out of the ground with the rest of them."

"And if it doesn't work. If we lose."

"Then we lose together, and Pell learns what you did, and I'd advise you both to be very fast swimmers." She said it without flinching, and that was the thing about Theo Kavanagh that Nils had been slowly realizing for eleven days: she never promised safety. She only promised company. Somehow that was worth more.

He looked down at the ledger page between them, at the dry columns of numbers that were really a list of ruined lives, his brother's among them, priced at four hundred crowns. Guilt sat in his chest like swallowed ballast—guilt for the lamp, guilt for the eleven months of small betrayals before it, guilt for every casual question he'd asked Theo over dinner while learning how she moved, what she feared, what she loved. But underneath the ballast, for the first time in three years, something else was stirring. Lighter. Hotter. It took him a moment to recognize it, it had been so long.

Purpose.

"One condition," Nils said.

Theo raised an eyebrow.

"When it's finished—when Torvald walks out of Coldmarsh—I want to be the one who tells him. Face to face. And I want to tell him his sister-in-law-to-be—" he stumbled, corrected, "—his brother's stupid coward of a brother is the one who carried the evidence out. I want him to hear my name in the same sentence as the word *free*."

Theo studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she nodded once, slow, the nod of a surveyor confirming a bearing.

"Done," she said. "Now listen carefully, because here's what you'll tell Pell tomorrow..."

* * *

She talked for an hour, low and precise, sketching the architecture of the lie in the same way she'd sketch a bridge—loads and tolerances, what would bear weight and what would collapse. The report Nils would send: the chest existed, the crew was small and divided, the surveyor was heading north toward the old line. All true. Truth, Theo said, was the best mortar for a lie; you built the falsehood out of real stones and it held under inspection. What he would omit: Wren. The vault key. Bram's decipherments. The fact that the crew had stopped being hired hands somewhere in the last hundred miles and become something harder to buy.

Near the end, her voice dropped further.

"There's one more thing. If Pell contacts you—if he changes the plan, if he orders you to do anything to the ship or anyone aboard—you come to me before you act. Even if it's the middle of the night. *Especially* if it's the middle of the night." Her eyes held his. "Because I don't think he means to trade, Nils. Men like Pell don't trade. They tidy. And when the tidying starts, I want to know before the tide does."

Nils nodded. He understood. He had understood, in some cellar of himself, since the night he'd first taken Pell's coin: that the man on the other end of the signal lamp was not a negotiator but an eraser, and that erasers worked fastest on people who believed they were still useful.

He climbed back up the mast once more before dawn—not to signal, but to stand where he'd stood, to feel the sway and the cold and take the height into himself, the way you take a dose of medicine. Below him the Kestrel lay black and sure on the moving water, Bram's light finally out, Sefa's silhouette motionless at the wheelhouse glass even now, because Sefa slept the way cats sleep. His ship. His crew. His crime and his penance, all riding the same hull.

Far downriver, deep in the fog where the channel bent toward the sea, a single white light blinked. Once. Twice. *Received.*

Nils stood on the crossarm and watched it fade, and felt the fear in him change its shape—not leaving, never leaving, but hardening into something he could carry, the way ore hardens into steel.

Then he climbed down, and went to bake the morning bread, and began, carefully, to write his report.
