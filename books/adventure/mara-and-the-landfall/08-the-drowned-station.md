## 8. The Drowned Station

The river changed its mind about them on the third morning.

Theo felt it before she saw it—a difference in the way the *Kestrel* moved beneath her feet, the hull's old shudder smoothing out as Sefa took them off the main channel and into water that ran slower, deeper, darker. The Aldmere here had swallowed its banks whole. Willows stood to their shoulders in flood, their crowns still green, their trunks gone the color of drowned bone. Mist lay on the surface in long unbroken sheets, and the paddle wheel turned through it with a sound like slow breathing.

"Junction water," Sefa said from the wheelhouse, not looking back. She said it the way other people named a grave.

Theo climbed the ladder to stand beside her. Ahead, where the mist thinned, she could make out shapes that did not belong to any living landscape: the black ribs of signal gantries rising from the flood, a signal arm still hanging at caution over water forty years deep. And beyond them, low and vast, a roofline.

The station.

It had been built, Bram's ledgers said, as the meeting point of two lines—the northern trunk out of Vessany and the coastal spur the Harrow Syndicate had financed with money that was supposed to build something else. In the maps of Theo's childhood it was called Merrow Junction. In the maps now it was nothing at all, because the maps had been redrawn when Vessany was struck from them, and whoever redrew them had not bothered to keep the small places. Only the big lies survived cartography.

The flood had taken the lower floor entirely. What remained above the waterline was one story of soot-black brick, windows like empty eye sockets, a slate roof sagging in the middle where a tree had come down on it decades ago and been allowed to stay. A clock tower rose from the eastern end, its face long since fallen, its hands with it. Time had resigned from this place.

"How deep?" Theo asked.

Sefa spat over the rail, a habit Theo had stopped finding crude and started finding navigational. "Over the platform, easy. Maybe twelve feet at the door. Water's cold enough to kill a strong swimmer in ten minutes, and there'll be glass and iron down there you can't see." She looked at Theo sideways. "You're going in anyway."

"I'm going in anyway."

"Then we go in at slack tide, midday, when the current stops arguing. And I'm putting Nils on the winch with a line tied to you, because I don't fish dead engineers out of my river. Bad for the ship's reputation."

*My river.* Theo had noticed that Sefa only ever claimed ownership of the water, never the land. She wondered what the syndicate had done to make a woman love one and refuse the other.

* * *

They anchored in the lee of what had once been the goods shed, its corrugated roof half-collapsed into the flood, and waited for the turn. Theo spent the hours on deck with the chest open across her knees, though by now she hardly needed to look inside. She knew its contents the way she knew her own hands: three ledgers in Vessany's cramped clerical script; a brass key wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a sin; and the grants themselves—land grants bearing the seal of a nation that officially no longer existed, signed and countersigned with dates that ran *forward*, past the collapse, past the bankruptcy, past the year her father died. Fresh ink. Living signatures. A country erased on paper and very much alive in contract.

Bram sat opposite her with the third ledger balanced on his knee, cross-referencing in a pocket notebook, his lips moving. He was a broad, quiet man who treated machinery and mysteries with the same method: take it apart, name every piece, put it back together better than you found it.

"You've read that page four times," he said without looking up.

"I'm trying to find the flaw in it. There has to be a flaw. People don't sign contracts for property in a country that got deleted."

"People sign contracts for exactly that," Bram said. "That's the entire point of deleting the country. You can't sue for land in a nation that isn't on the map. The grants become ghosts, and ghosts can't contest title." He turned the ledger toward her, tapping an entry. "Except these were never voided. Look at the witnessing clause. Every grant names a sitting magistrate of *our* courts as arbiter. They kept one foot inside the law the whole time. That's not fraud, Theo. That's architecture."

She closed the chest and latched it, and the small sound of the clasp seemed very loud against the dripping silence of the flooded junction.

Nils came aft with coffee that tasted of river and tin, and for a while none of them spoke. That was the thing about the crew Sefa had gathered—the silences were comfortable, which Theo distrusted on principle, having learned that comfort was how people talked themselves into trusting each other right up until the moment it cost them everything. But Nils hummed while he worked, some tuneless sailor's drone, and Bram's pencil scratched, and the mist lifted off the water in rags, and Theo let herself have ten minutes of almost-peace.

Then the tide turned, and peace was over.

* * *

They went in at noon, as promised: Theo in the water, Nils on the winch line, Sefa at the rail with a boathook and a face that suggested she was reconsidering several life choices. Bram held the lantern, useless in daylight but ready for the interior, where the sun would not follow.

The water was colder than she'd braced for—not the sharp cold of mountain melt but a flat, patient cold, the temperature of things that had stopped hurrying. She swam the thirty yards to the station's loading arch with the line trailing from her waist, and pulled herself up onto the lip of the platform, which broke the surface like the back of a whale, furred with weed. From there she could walk, bent double, along the top of the old platform canopy supports toward the stairwell that had once descended to the booking hall. It was dry below the waterline only in the sense that a tomb is dry.

"Talk to me, Kavanagh," Nils called, taking in the slack.

"I'm fine. Stairwell's ahead. There's—" She stopped.

There was light.

Not sunlight. Lamplight, or the memory of it—a faint chemical gleam on the wet brick below, down in the flooded hall, where no lamp should be burning in a station abandoned before Theo was born. She went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the water.

"Nils. Get Sefa. Someone's been here."

* * *

The booking hall was a cathedral of black water. Theo waded down the last steps until she stood thigh-deep among the drowned furniture—ticket counters, benches, a luggage trolley standing on its nose—and held the lantern high. The air smelled of rot and lamp oil and, underneath both, something sweeter and worse.

The searchers had been thorough. Drawers hung out of the ticket counter like tongues. A wall of pigeonholes had been systematically emptied, each cubby's contents either taken or floated in a gray pulp across the water's skin. Someone had pried up floorboards along the entire eastern wall, working methodically left to right, and someone else—or the same someone, hurried—had given up halfway and simply smashed the rest.

"They were looking for a vault," Bram said behind her. He had come down despite the line, despite Sefa's opinion of it, and stood at her shoulder with his notebook wrapped in oilcloth. "Same as us. The key opens a strongroom—that's what the ledgers say—but the ledgers don't say *where*. We thought the junction might tell us."

"It told someone else first." Theo raised the lantern.

Against the far wall, half-submerged where the floor dipped, lay a man.

He had been dead for days—not weeks; the river kept its dead too well for weeks, but this one the water had barely begun to work on, cold slowing everything the way cold slows a clock. He wore a good coat ruined by water, city boots, and a canvas satchel still strapped across his chest. His face had been turned to the wall, whether by him or by someone else Theo couldn't say, and she was grateful for the small mercy of not knowing.

"Sefa," she called up the stairwell, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Bring the boat hook. And don't let Nils come down."

But Nils came down anyway, because Nils was Nils, and together they brought the body up to the platform where the light was honest. Sefa crouched over him with the detached efficiency of a woman who had seen rivers produce worse, and went through the coat pockets with two fingers, quick and impersonal.

"No papers. No identification. Somebody stripped him." She turned back the coat lapel, and stopped. "Well. Almost somebody."

Pinned inside the lapel, hidden by the fold, was a small enameled badge: a blue circle, a white star, and around the rim a motto in Vessany's old script. Theo knew it. Everyone of her father's generation knew it. It was the badge of the Vessany railway police—the men who had guarded the grants, guarded the lines, guarded the truth, right up until the day the truth stopped mattering to anyone with power.

"He wasn't syndicate," Bram said quietly. "Or he wasn't only syndicate. This is a loyalist's badge. Someone who kept faith with a dead country."

"And someone killed him for it." Theo looked back down the stairwell, at the black water and the wrecked drawers and the lamplight smell. "Look at the search pattern again. Left to right, careful, then rushed. He was searching with someone. Or someone was searching after him. Either way—" she made herself say it "—they're ahead of us, they know about the strongroom, and they didn't get what they came for, or they wouldn't have torn the place apart."

"Could be Pell," Nils offered. His voice was carefully casual. Theo noted it, filed it, said nothing. "Fixer like that, he'd have men out ahead of the ship."

"Pell travels with hired muscle and expense accounts," Sefa said. "This—" she nodded at the body "—this is someone's own blood on the line. Syndicate doesn't bleed for Vessany. Syndicate *made* Vessany bleed."

The wind shifted then, coming downriver, and with it came a sound that stopped all four of them where they stood: the flat, carrying report of an engine bell. Not the *Kestrel*'s. Something larger, upstream, moving with the unhurried confidence of a vessel that owned whatever water it entered.

Through the broken windows, across the flooded plain where rails once ran, Theo saw it: a launch, white-hulled, flying no colors at all, which was itself a kind of colors. Behind it, further off, a second shape—bigger, slower, riding low.

Two vessels. Two parties. Pell ahead of them, yes.

And Pell, it appeared, was not working alone.

"Back to the ship," Sefa said, already moving. "Now. Quietly. And Kavanagh—" she paused at the stairwell, and for the first time since Crowsfoot Landing, Theo saw something in the pilot's face that was not competence or irony but plain, human fear. "Whatever your father found out about this water, I'm starting to think we're not the first crew he sent looking."
