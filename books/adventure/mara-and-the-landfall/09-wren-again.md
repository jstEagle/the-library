## 9. Wren Again

The river port of Saltmere had been built for a prosperity that never quite arrived, and now wore its ambitions like a coat too large—broad stone quays meant for grain barges that came only in ones and twos, a customs house with columns flaking salt-bloom down their faces, warehouses standing half-empty with their doors rolled open to the gulls. Elara Wren stepped off the mail packet at midmorning into air that smelled of brine, tar, and old rope, and felt the warrant in her breast pocket the way one feels a coin sewn into a lining: always there, always weighing.

She had been traveling for six days on a trail of paper. The salvage report filed at Crowsfoot Landing—a chest, stamped with a seal no clerk could identify, recovered from the wreck of the *Vespera*—had crossed her desk in Corvenna like a fish dropped on clean linen. She had read it twice. Then she had pulled the old admiralty registries, found the *Vespera* listed as lost with all hands nineteen years past, bound for a port that no longer appeared on any chart, and understood that either the report was a fraud or the world was stranger than her training had prepared her for. She had requested a warrant for the detention of all salvage materials pending inquiry, and the clerk of seals had signed it without reading it, because clerks of seals did not read things; they sealed them.

That was the first hairline crack, though she did not yet know to call it that. She would remember it later—the ease of it, the blankness of the official eye—and she would wonder how many other warrants had passed under that same unseeing hand, and toward what ends.

Saltmere's harbormaster was a woman named Quist, broad as a bollard, who received Wren's credentials with the weary courtesy of someone long accustomed to officials arriving after the interesting part was over.

"The *Kestrel*," Quist said, tapping her ledger. "Sefa Ilo, master. Berthed two nights, paid through tomorrow noon. Steam packet out of Crowsfoot, patched hull, crew of four." She looked up. "You'll want to know they took on coal and fresh water like they mean to go far."

"Where does far begin?"

Quist shrugged, a gesture that moved her whole shoulders like a hill shifting. "Downriver, past the derelict locks. Nobody goes down there but smugglers and fools, and Ilo's been both."

"And the passenger? A Theo Kavanagh."

"Surveyor, by her papers." Something flickered in Quist's face—not suspicion exactly, but the particular wariness of a woman who had watched too many people pass through her port carrying more than their papers said. "Quiet one. Stood on the quay an hour the first evening, just looking at the water. Like she was listening to it."

Wren wrote nothing down. She had already memorized what mattered.

* * *

She found the customs house annex at the end of the quay—a squat granite building where the empire's paperwork went to be forgotten—and established herself in its one furnished room with the warrant, her seal, and a view of the harbor through windows filmed with salt. It was, she reflected, a poor kind of ambush: announcing herself to the harbormaster, sitting in plain sight behind government glass. But she was not trying to trap Theo Kavanagh. She was trying to offer her something, and offers required daylight.

She spent the waiting hours reviewing what she knew, which was less than she pretended. Kavanagh: daughter of Edric Kavanagh, the line engineer whose name still surfaced in the older case files she'd inherited when she took the magistracy—files about land grants along the northern corridor, petitions dismissed, an inquest ruled accidental. Drowned, the record said, though the river in question was barely knee-deep in autumn. Wren had read that file at twenty-six and felt the shape of wrongness in it without having the tools to name it. At thirty-four she had better tools and fewer illusions, and the file still sat in her memory like a splinter worked too deep to draw.

The syndicate: Harrow Consolidated, chartered sixty years ago to build the lines, dissolved eleven years ago amid the general collapse that had also swallowed Vessany—the nation that wasn't, the country struck from the atlases in a single administrative season while the chancelleries argued over tariffs. Wren had been a junior examiner then, stamping orders she hadn't written, and she remembered the strange quiet of those weeks, the way the maps were corrected so smoothly, so completely, as if Vessany had been a smudge rather than a place with rail stations and school registers and graves.

A chest from a drowned ship out of a deleted nation, salvaged by the daughter of a silenced man, carried downriver on a patched steamer by a pilot who used to run cargo for the very interests now dissolved. Wren laid these facts out in her mind like cards and did not like the hand. It was not a legal matter. It was becoming a historical one, and history, in her experience, was where institutions went to lose.

Near dusk, the *Kestrel* came in.

Wren saw her from the customs house window before she saw anything else: a low black hull with steam standing off her stacks in the cold evening air, moving with the unhurried authority of a vessel that knew every rock between here and the sea. She tied up at the commercial quay with a minimum of fuss, and figures moved on her deck—two men, one heavyset, one lean; a woman at the rail with a pilot's stillness; and a fourth figure, slighter, who stood looking up at the customs house columns for a long moment before coming ashore.

Theo Kavanagh walked the quay like a woman counting her own footsteps. Wren watched her pause at the harbormaster's office, speak briefly, glance once—deliberately, Wren was almost certain—at the lit window of the customs annex. Then she turned and walked toward it.

Wren opened the door before the knock came.

"Magistrate," Theo Kavanagh said. Her voice was level, her eyes taking in the room, the warrant box on the table, the lamp, the exits, in perhaps two seconds. "I wondered when you'd catch up. You're faster than Pell's people gave you credit for."

The name landed like a stone in still water. Wren kept her face still. "You know Ambrose Pell."

"I know his handwriting on collection notices. I know the sound of his launch." Kavanagh stepped inside, uninvited, and stood with her back to neither door nor window—an old habit, Wren thought, of someone who had learned to be hunted young. "You have a warrant. I assume for the salvage."

"For all materials recovered from the wreck of the *Vespera*, pending formal inquiry." Wren held her eyes. "You filed honestly at Crowsfoot. That was either carelessness or conscience. I've come to find out which."

"Has it occurred to you that it might be strategy?"

"It has. I don't think it is."

Something moved behind Kavanagh's eyes—surprise, quickly banked. "Why not?"

"Because careless people hide. Conscientious people file reports and then run anyway, because they've learned the report won't protect them." Wren gestured to the chair across the table. "Sit. Please. I'm not arresting anyone tonight."

Kavanagh did not sit. "Then what are you doing?"

"Offering protection." Wren laid the warrant on the table, face up, and set her hand flat beside it—not on it, beside it, a distinction she hoped would be legible. "The chest comes into court custody. Sealed, inventoried, witnessed. In exchange, you and your crew receive protective writs. No collector touches your tools. No fixer burns your berth. Whatever the Harrow Syndicate's ghosts want from you, they'd have to come through a magistrate's order to get it."

"And if the ghosts own the magistrate's order?"

The room seemed to contract around the question. Outside, a gull cried once and thought better of it. Wren heard herself answer before she had fully decided to.

"Then we find out together, and I burn my career finding out. Is that reassurance enough?"

"No," Kavanagh said quietly. "But it's honest, which is rarer." She finally sat, perched on the edge of the chair like a bird deciding whether the branch would hold. "You traced me from the salvage report. Who else reads those reports, Magistrate? Before they reach your desk?"

Wren opened her mouth to say *no one*, and found the word wouldn't come. Reports crossed three desks between Crowsfoot Landing and her chambers. Three clerks. Three chances. And Pell had known about the salvage within days—had been ahead of them at the drowned station, had left a body there. She had told herself he had his own informants on the water. She had not asked herself the simpler question: who else profits from knowing early?

"You don't know," Kavanagh said, watching her face. "You can't know. That's the trouble with your offer. It isn't worth less than you think it is. It's worth exactly what you think it is, and you think it's worth everything, and that's why it frightens me."

* * *

They talked for two hours while the tide turned and the lamps of Saltmere came up one by one along the quay. Wren laid out the law: salvage jurisdiction, evidentiary custody, the protections of formal inquiry. Kavanagh listened with the attention of an engineer hearing a structural argument—weighing load paths, testing joints—and dismantled none of it. That was the unsettling thing. She didn't argue the law was inadequate. She assumed it, the way one assumes weather.

At some point Wren realized she had stopped making her case and started asking questions, and that Kavanagh had noticed, and was answering carefully, giving away nothing whole.

"Your father," Wren said at last. "Edric Kavanagh. He petitioned the colonial office nine times about the northern grants. I've read the files."

Something closed in the other woman's face, gentle and total, like a door drawn shut against weather. "Have you."

"He drowned in eight inches of water. Ruled accidental. The survey notes he'd been collecting were never entered into evidence—they were logged as lost, though the inquest transcript says they were 'recovered and retained' by the syndicate's counsel." Wren leaned forward. "I was young when I read that. I thought: mistake, oversight, the machine grinding imperfectly. I'm not young anymore. Tell me what happened to him."

"Why?" Kavanagh's voice was soft. "So you can reopen an eleven-year-old inquest before a court system that lost the evidence the first time? So his death can be re-ruled, correctly this time, in a judgment nobody will ever enforce?" She shook her head slowly. "You'd get a cleaner lie, Magistrate. That's all the law can give us here. A well-documented lie."

"That's not all the law is."

"No," Kavanagh agreed. "It's not. But it's all the law is *here*. There's a difference, and I think you feel it, or you wouldn't have come alone. You wouldn't have told me you'd burn your career. A confident institution doesn't send one woman with a warrant and a promise. It sends a seizure order and four men."

Wren wanted to argue. She catalogued, rapidly, all the ways the analogy failed, all the safeguards, all the good faith—and underneath the catalogue ran a colder current she could not dam: *the clerk of seals signed my warrant without reading it.* How many signatures had Edric Kavanagh's death passed under? How many blank official eyes?

"I need to see something," Kavanagh said.

"If you're asking me to board the *Kestrel*—"

"I'm asking you to look at one page. Here. Now. Not the chest—one page, brought to you, in this room, under your seal." Kavanagh's eyes were steady. "You show me what a magistrate does with it, and maybe I show you the rest. Maybe. One page, Wren. Consider it a load test."

It was irregular. It was precisely the sort of extra-judicial accommodation she had been trained to refuse, and she heard her own training recite its objections—chain of custody, contamination, precedent—and heard, beneath them, the quieter voice that said: *you will learn nothing tonight otherwise, and she knows it, and she is offering you the only door she has.*

"One page," Wren said. "Witnessed. I'll note it in my journal."

Kavanagh went to the door, spoke low to someone in the dark—Wren caught the lean silhouette of the engineer, Oduya, holding a wrapped bundle—and returned with a single sheet of heavy paper, folded once. She laid it on the table and unfolded it with both hands, carefully, the way one handles a wound.

It was a land grant. Wren knew the form instantly—she had processed dozens, the standard colonial instrument, the ornate border, the clause of conveyance. This one conveyed title to twelve thousand acres along the northern corridor, the very corridor Edric Kavanagh had died examining. The grant was dated eleven years ago. The date of the syndicate's dissolution.

And beneath it, in the execution block, were signatures. Fresh ink. Recent. Wren bent close. The registrar's signature belonged to a man currently serving as Deputy Minister of Lands in Corvenna—a man whose appointment had been ratified the year she took her post, whose office had, eighteen months ago, denied her request for records relating to the old Vessany corridor on grounds of *administrative closure*.

"This is impossible," she said, and heard how the sentence wavered. "This grant was executed after the charter was voided. After the assets reverted. Legally, this document cannot exist."

"No," Kavanagh said. "It can't. There are forty more like it in the chest. And ledgers showing payments against each one—payments that continued straight through the collapse, through the strike-from-the-maps, through every year since. The syndicate didn't die, Magistrate. It went underground and kept collecting rent on a country everyone agreed to stop seeing." She tapped the deputy minister's signature, gently, with one finger. "That man administers the courts' funding. Your salary passes through his ministry. So does the pension you'll draw, if you last long enough to earn one."

Wren stood very still. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere out on the water, a bell buoy counted the swell, patient and idiotic, marking time for a world that had apparently ended some time ago without informing her.

She thought: *forgery.* She examined the ink, the paper stock, the embossed seal—she was competent at document examination, it was part of the trade—and found no forgery. She thought: *elaborate fabrication,* and tested that too, and found the fabrication would cost more to produce than any salvage fraud could hope to recover, and would serve no purpose but to destroy the forger. She thought, finally, with the clarity of cold water: *it's real.*

The law she served was built on top of this. Funded by this. Her warrant, her seal, her chambers—all of it stood on ground that this single page proved to be stolen.

"Say something," Kavanagh said quietly. "You've gone somewhere. I'd like to know where."

"I'm trying to find the floor," Wren said. "It moved."

For a moment—just a moment—something passed between them that was not adversary and not alliance but the rawer thing underneath both: two women standing in the same collapsed room, looking at the same broken joists. Kavanagh's hand rested on the table near the page, and Wren was aware of it the way one is aware of warmth from a fire in a cold room, and she put the awareness away firmly, in the drawer where she kept things that were true and untimely.

"What will you do?" Kavanagh asked.

"My duty," Wren said. It came out steadier than she felt. "Which begins with telling you the truth: if I take that chest into custody tonight, by morning it will be in Corvenna, and by winter it will be gone. Lost. Retained by counsel. Logged and misfiled. I know the machinery, Kavanagh. I am the machinery." She drew a breath. "And I cannot let you simply sail away with stolen evidence either, because that's not justice, that's just a different thief with better motives."

"So we're at an impasse."

"We're at a beginning." Wren took out her journal and wrote, in full view: *One page examined, voluntarily produced, this date. Custody declined pending further inquiry. Subject cooperative. Inquiry continuing.* She signed it, dated it, and showed Kavanagh the entry. "As far as my record shows, I am still investigating. As far as your record shows, you are still cooperating. Neither of us has lied yet." She met the other woman's eyes. "Keep it that way. Send me proof when you have it—copies, testimony, anything that can survive being buried. Don't send me the chest. If I hold it, it dies. If you hold it, you might live long enough to make it matter."

Kavanagh studied her for a long moment. "You realize what you're describing. You're asking me to build a case outside your courts and hand it to you to detonate inside them."

"I'm asking you to give me something worth resigning over," Wren said. "If it comes to that."

"And if I decide the courts can't be saved at all? If I decide the truth needs daylight, not procedure? Publication, not prosecution?"

"Then we're rivals," Wren said, "and I'll spend the rest of my career chasing you. But I'll chase you honestly, and I'll know why you ran, and some small part of me will be running alongside." She paused. "That's not an endorsement. It's a confession. Log it accordingly."

Something almost like a smile touched Kavanagh's mouth—brief, tired, real. "You're not what I expected, Magistrate."

"Neither is that page." Wren folded it along its original crease and slid it back across the table. "Go. Before the night watch changes and someone asks questions I'd have to answer badly. And Kavanagh—" she hesitated, hating the hesitation, hating more the reason for it. "Pell's launch was seen at the fuel depot yesterday, buying for a gunboat's consumption, not a launch's. He's not following you anymore. He's positioning. Whatever you're sailing toward, he means to be there first, with more guns than a parley requires."

Kavanagh went still. "You're warning me. Off the record. Against your own interest—if I escape, your inquiry fails."

"If you die, my inquiry becomes an obituary." Wren closed her journal. "I've written enough of those."

* * *

From the customs house window, Wren watched the *Kestrel* cast off two hours after midnight, riding the ebb with her lights doused, a black shape gathering itself against blacker water. Steam stood off her stacks and shredded in the wind. At the rail, briefly, a figure raised one hand—not a wave, she decided, an acknowledgment. A signal between professionals. She chose to read it that way, and knew she was choosing, and let the knowledge sit there, uncomfortable, like grit in a bearing.

She walked back to the annex and sat a long time without lighting the lamp. The warrant lay on the table, unsigned in effect, unspent. She thought about the deputy minister's signature, the smooth ink of it, the ordinary competence of the hand—how corruption always looked, up close, like good penmanship. She thought about her oath, and discovered it had split cleanly down the middle sometime during the last three hours, and that both halves were still true, and that they pointed in different directions.

*To uphold the law.* Whose law? Funded by whom? Sealed by clerks who did not read?

*To do justice.* By what means, when the instruments of justice were themselves the evidence?

Outside, the bell buoy counted on. Wren took out a fresh sheet of paper, and after much deliberation wrote a letter of resignation, complete, dated, and unsigned, and folded it into the back of her journal—not sent, not yet, but written, the way one writes a will before a dangerous crossing. It was, she understood, a door she had built for herself so that she would know it was there. Whether she walked through it depended entirely on what came next downriver.

She slept two hours in the chair, the warrant under her hand, and dreamed of rails running into the sea, and woke before dawn with the taste of salt and iron in her mouth, and knew, with the certainty that arrives without argument, that she would follow.

By the first ferry she was at the harbormaster's office, buying passage downriver on a coal barge—no flag, no escort, one magistrate traveling light, carrying a warrant she might never serve and a resignation she might never send, toward a landfall where all of it would come due.
