## 10. Pell's Offer

The parley flag went up an hour after dawn, and Theo knew it before anyone shouted.

She had been awake since the grey hour, sitting on the Kestrel's rail with a cup of chicory going cold in her hands, watching the mist peel off the Aldmere in long ribbons. The river at Port Callow ran wide and slow there, brown as old tea, and the warehouses along the quay stood shoulder to shoulder like tired men waiting for work that never came. She had learned to read water the way other people read faces—Sefa's doing, three weeks of correction and mockery—and so when the current changed its mind, when it stuttered and flattened in a long lane downstream, she set down her cup and said, quietly, to no one: "He's here."

Pell's launch came upriver against the current with the insolent ease of something that had never once been told no. It was white-hulled, brass-railed, too clean for this stretch of the Aldmere, and from its mast flew a square of pale green cloth—the parley flag, older than any of the nations still using it, a courtesy so ancient even the syndicates pretended to honor it. Under its protection a man could step aboard an enemy's deck unarmed and unmolested, speak his piece, and walk away whole. It was one of the few laws of the river that everyone kept, because everyone needed it eventually.

"He'll want coffee," Nils said, coming up beside her with a pan in his hand. His face was carefully, elaborately normal. Since the night she'd caught him signaling, he had become almost aggressively useful—up first, last to sleep, scrubbing decks that were already clean. Guilt, worn like a borrowed coat that didn't quite fit. "It's what they do. The collectors. They sit down in your own galley and drink your coffee and make you feel like the beggar."

"Then we'll give him river water," Sefa said, swinging down from the wheelhouse. Her arms were crossed; her knuckles were white on her own elbows. "Let him taste where he is."

Bram said nothing at all. He stood at the gangway with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth under his coat, and his eyes followed the launch the way a man watches a snake cross a path—not afraid, exactly, but unwilling to look away in case it changed direction.

Theo made her decision the way she made all of them now: quickly, precisely, with the feeling of a gauge needle settling. "We let him board. We hear him out. Nobody speaks about the chest, nobody shows him anything, and nobody—" she looked at each of them in turn, and let her gaze rest on Nils half a heartbeat longer than the rest— "nobody is alone with him. Not for a moment."

They nodded. Even Nils. Especially Nils.

* * *

Ambrose Pell came up the gangway like a man visiting property he already owned.

He was smaller than Theo remembered from the collector's office in Harrow City—memory had inflated him—but he had lost none of that terrible tidiness. Grey suit without a wrinkle. Grey gloves. A grey hat held at the correct angle to shed rain that wasn't falling. He looked around the Kestrel's deck with the mild, cataloguing interest of an appraiser, and Theo watched him take inventory: the patched plating, the rusted winch, the crew arranged in a loose crescent that was absolutely not a defensive formation except in every way that mattered.

"Miss Kavanagh." He inclined his head. "You'll forgive the early hour. Rivers are best traveled before the wind wakes up."

"You're traveling under parley," Theo said. "Say what you've come to say."

"All in good time." He smiled—it was a well-made smile, machined like everything else about him—and gestured at the deck. "Might I?"

She wanted to refuse him the courtesy of standing on their boards. But parley was parley, and Sefa had drilled into all of them that you did not chip at the old laws, not ever, because the day you needed them they would be full of your own chisel marks. Theo stepped aside.

Pell removed his gloves finger by finger, folded them, tucked them into his breast pocket, and looked out over the brown water with something that might have been genuine appreciation. "I grew up on a river," he said. "Not this one. A smaller one, further south, dead now—silted in when the syndicate rerouted the trade. My father poled barges until there were no barges to pole. Do you know what he said about rivers?" He turned back to her. "He said a river never forgives, but it always forgets. Everything sinks or floats away, given time. That's why men like me exist, Miss Kavanagh. To help things sink."

"I know why you're here," Theo said. "The chest."

"Ah." He said it softly, tasting the word. "So there is one."

She kept her face still. Somewhere behind her, Bram's breathing changed by a degree no one but an engineer would have caught.

"Mr. Pell," she said, "you boarded under a flag of truce to fish. That's not parley. That's just rudeness with better manners."

"Fair." He laughed—a short, dry sound, genuinely amused, which was somehow worse than menace. "Then let me be plain, since you value it. Three weeks ago a salvage report was filed at the Callow customs house describing wreckage recovered from the Aldmere shallows. The report was vague. Deliberately, I think—you're a careful woman. But the wreck it described matched a vessel that appears in none of the registries, because the vessel it matched was scuttled twenty-two years ago by men who were paid to make it disappear. The *Vespera*, Miss Kavanagh. Vessany mail packet. And whatever she carried when she went down, my employers have spent two decades and a very great deal of money believing it stayed down." He paused. "You pulled something out of that river. I don't need to know what. I only need you to give it back."

"And if I say there's nothing?"

"Then I'll be disappointed," Pell said pleasantly, "and my employers will be thorough, and neither of those outcomes will go well for the people standing on this deck." He glanced—at Sefa, at Bram, lingering just long enough on Nils to make Theo's blood cool—before returning to her. "But I don't think it will come to that. Because I'm not here to threaten you. I'm here to make you rich."

* * *

He laid it out over the next quarter hour with the smoothness of a man who had rehearsed it against mirrors, and perhaps he had. Full forgiveness of her debt—the entire crushing weight of it, the Harrow survey bonds, the interest compounding like rot, gone, struck from the books, the creditor's letters that had haunted her for six years rendered into blank paper. Beyond that: a position. Chief survey engineer for the syndicate's northern division. Her own office. Her name restored—*rehabilitated*, he actually used the word, as though her disgrace were a building needing renovation rather than a thing they themselves had built.

"You're the best line engineer this side of the mountains," Pell said. "Everyone knows it. Your father knew it—that's how you got the bond terms you got, incidentally. Talent attracts investment. The syndicate invests in talent. We simply require loyalty, and loyalty requires that certain items of historical paperwork be returned to their rightful custodians." He spread his hands. "A chest, Miss Kavanagh. Old paper. Salt-ruined, most likely. Worth nothing to anyone except as a matter of tidiness. And in exchange: your life back. All of it. Every year they took."

Theo listened the way she read blueprints—looking for the load-bearing lie, the point where the whole structure would come down if you pressed it.

She found it immediately, because it was the foundation itself.

"The land grants," she said.

Pell's smile did not move, but something behind it did—a flicker, a hairline crack across machined steel. "I'm sorry?"

"The Vessany grants." She said it clearly, letting the word carry across the deck, letting Sefa and Bram and Nils hear it spoken aloud for the first time in the open air. "That's what's in the chest. That's what your employers have spent twenty years pretending burned with a nation. They didn't lose the grants when Vessany fell. They *wrote* the fall. They manufactured the bankruptcy, starved the colony, scuttled the packet that carried the appeal to the capital—and then they kept the land. Every mile of it. Every town built on it since. Every family paying rent on ground their grandparents owned outright." She stepped closer, and was distantly surprised to find her voice level, almost gentle. "You didn't come to buy back old paper, Mr. Pell. You came to buy the only proof that the ground under three hundred thousand people was stolen. And you can't afford it. No one can. That's the joke of it. You finally found something the syndicate's money can't price."

The silence that followed had weight. Downstream a gull cried once and thought better of it.

Pell put his hat back on. He did it slowly, and when he looked up at her again the appraiser was gone, and what looked out through those grey eyes was the thing underneath—the thing that policed silted rivers and helped things sink.

"My father," he said, conversationally, "drowned drunk in a ditch when I was nine. The syndicate paid for my schooling. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am what gratitude looks like, Miss Kavanagh. I am what happens to a man who learns early that the world is owned, and decides to be on the owning side of it." He replaced his gloves, one finger at a time. "You have until the end of the week. After that, I stop making offers and start making examples. And I should tell you—I'm very good at examples. Ask anyone on this river."

He walked down the gangway without waiting to be dismissed. The launch pulled away against the current, white and clean and wrong, and the parley flag came down, and Theo stood at the rail until it was a smudge and then a rumor and then nothing at all.

Behind her, Nils was the first to speak. His voice cracked in the middle of it. "End of the week. Theo—he knows the river. He has gunboats. What do we—"

"We leave tonight," Theo said.

Sefa shook her head. "Flood season's coming early this year. Main channel's already running high past Corven's Bend. Another four days and the lower Aldmere closes entirely—we'd be trapped in port with him."

"Then we don't take the main channel."

Sefa stared at her. Then, slowly, something moved through her face that Theo had never seen there—not fear exactly, and not eagerness either, but the memory of both. "You don't know what you're asking."

"I know you do," Theo said. "Whatever route you left the syndicate's service over. Whatever water you swore you'd never run again." She held the pilot's eyes. "We need it, Sefa. All of it."

* * *

Pell did not wait for the end of the week to send his answer.

It came that night, an hour after full dark, while the Kestrel sat loading coal by lamplight at the Callow fueling dock. Theo heard it before she understood it—a low whump rolling up the river, felt in the soles of her feet before it reached her ears, the sound of a great deal of air being displaced very suddenly by fire.

She was on deck in time to see the glow.

Two piers down, the old Merrow warehouse—one of the derelict grain stores the port authority had been meaning to demolish for a decade, empty these five years, worth nothing to anyone—stood with flame already punching through its roof. The fire climbed fast, faster than a cold building should burn, and Theo understood within seconds that it had been helped: oil, probably, laid in advance, a lesson prepared the way Pell prepared everything. The light of it turned the whole river orange. It lit the faces of the dockworkers who came stumbling out of the shadows to watch, and it lit the Kestrel's deck, and it lit the words some of the watchers were saying to each other in low voices: *the Kestrel's people. The salvagers. That's who it's meant for.*

Nobody had been inside. Of course nobody had been inside. Pell was tidy. The message required witnesses, not corpses—not yet. The warehouse burned with a great show of violence and destroyed nothing but itself, and that was the whole of the sentence: *this is what I can do whenever I decide to. Next time it won't be empty.*

Theo stood at the rail and watched it burn, and discovered that she was not afraid.

That was the strange part. She catalogued it later, the way she'd catalogue an anomaly in a survey reading: heat on her face, smoke souring the river air, Bram's hand heavy on her shoulder, Sefa swearing steadily and creatively at the far end of the deck, Nils white-faced and gripping the rail as though the fire might jump the water for him specifically. And herself, calm. Colder than the night. Something in her had finished moving during the parley that afternoon—a needle settling, a decision seating itself home—and what Pell's fire did now was only confirm it.

Her father had spent eleven years gathering proof like what lay wrapped in oilcloth below her feet. He had believed in courts, in filings, in the slow machinery of law, and he had died on a flooded spur of track with his evidence scattered in the mud and his name entered in the record as a trespasser. Theo had inherited his patience and his debts and his grave, and for six years she had told herself the machine worked, it was only slow, justice was a freight line and freight lines ran late.

Pell had just shown her the truth of the freight line. The syndicate didn't fear the courts. The syndicate *commuted* on them.

"They want us frightened," Bram said quietly beside her. Embers drifted overhead like slow red snow. "This is theater."

"I know."

"It changes nothing. The ledgers are real. The vault is real. What's in that strongroom doesn't stop being true because a man in a grey suit likes fire."

"I know," Theo said again. And then, because he deserved more than repetition, because the crew deserved it, because maybe she needed to say it aloud to make it solid: "He offered me my life back today. Six years of debt, gone. A career. A name. Everything I've been drowning for since my father died." She watched the roof of the warehouse sag, fold, collapse inward in a geyser of sparks. "And all I could think, standing there listening to him, was that he was offering me a ticket on a line I've already seen the end of. There's no station down that track where I get to be clean. There's just a bigger office and a better view of everything burning."

Bram was silent a moment. "So what do we do?"

"What he's most afraid of," Theo said. "We run his river. Tonight. Through whatever water Sefa can find. And we carry every page of that chest to the one place the syndicate can't buy, burn, or bury it." She turned from the fire at last. "Wake the crew. Coal's loaded? Then we cast off within the hour. Flood season won't wait, and neither will he."

Below the burning pier, black and patient, the Aldmere slid past in the dark—high, swollen, already rising—and somewhere upstream in the hills the rains that would close the channel were falling on ground that had been stolen twice over.

Sefa appeared at Theo's elbow, coiled and grim, a pilot dressed for war with the river. "You're sure," she said. It wasn't a question, but it waited for an answer anyway.

"No," Theo said honestly. "But I'm done being careful with my life. It hasn't gotten me anywhere worth keeping."

For the first time since Theo had known her, Sefa Ilo smiled. "Then you'd better pray I remember the way," she said, and went forward to take her ship into the flood.
