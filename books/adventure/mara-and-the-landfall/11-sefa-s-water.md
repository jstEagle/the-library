## 11. Sefa's Water

The river told Sefa Ilo the truth before any of them could.

She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* in the grey hour before dawn, watching the main channel of the Aldmere swallow itself. The water had come up two feet overnight—she could read it in the way the current dragged at the mooring lines, in the color of it, brown gone to the yellow of churned clay, in the debris riding past: whole trees, a fence gate, half a roof with its shingles still attached, spinning slowly like a drowned man turning over. Flood season had arrived not as a season but as a decision, sudden and total, and the Aldmere had decided to close its front door.

Behind her, Nils was frying something that smelled like onions and forgiveness. Bram sat on an overturned crate with the ledgers spread across his knees, pencil behind his ear, muttering figures to himself the way other men prayed. And Theo Kavanagh stood at the rail with her arms crossed, watching the floodwater carry away their route north.

"The channel's done," Theo said. It wasn't a question. She'd spent enough years reading grades and gradients to know when a river said no.

"Done for anyone who doesn't know better," Sefa said.

Theo turned. "There's a better?"

"There's always a better." Sefa spat over the rail, a habit she'd never broken from her syndicate days and didn't intend to break now, since it annoyed Pell's kind of people. "There's the old cut. The Vessany pilots used it before your maps were drawn. It runs through the marsh country west of Corren's Bluff—narrow, shallow in spots, and the flood's turned half of it into rapids. But it connects to the northern reach above the locks, and it's got one virtue."

"Which is?"

"Pell doesn't know it exists." She let that sit. "His gunboat draws too much water anyway. He'd have to take the long way around through the locks, and the locks are closed for flood stage. We'd have two days on him. Maybe three."

Bram looked up from his ledger. "You've run it?"

"I grew up on it." Something moved across Sefa's face then, quick as a fish under the surface, there and gone. "My mother ran grain barges through it when I was small enough to stand on the deck and hold the sounding pole. Before the syndicate bought everything." She turned back to the wheel. "I'll need everyone working. Theo on the lead line. Bram on the boiler—she'll run hot and I'll need every pound of steam you can wring out of her without blowing us all to Vessany. Nils on the fenders and the poles."

"And you?" Theo asked.

Sefa smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile. It was the smile of a woman about to do something dangerous because it was the only thing she was good at. "I'll drive."

* * *

They cast off into the flood at first light, and the river tried to kill them before they'd made a mile.

The *Kestrel* was forty tons of patched steel and stubbornness, and the current took her stern the moment Sefa eased her off the bank, swinging her broadside like a door in the wind. Sefa countered with full ahead starboard wheel, felt the old engine hesitate—that hesitation was new, that was the boiler running sour—and then the paddles bit and the bow came around and they were into it, running with the flood instead of against it, which was the only way to survive it.

"Lead line!" she called.

Theo stood on the foredeck with the coil in her hands, swinging the weighted line in a flat arc, letting it fly. The call came back in the old cadence, the one every pilot learned at their mother's knee or didn't learn at all: *"Mark twain! Mark twain! Quarter less—"*

"Quarter less twain," Theo called, and Sefa did the arithmetic without thinking: four and a half feet. Not enough for comfort. Enough for the *Kestrel*, if the bottom was mud and not rock, if the channel hadn't shifted since the last flood rearranged it, if a hundred things held true that no one could promise.

"Steady," she said, mostly to herself.

The marsh country opened around them by midmorning—a drowned world of reeds and dead trees, water standing in the fields where cattle should have been, farmhouses on their knolls turned into islands with families watching them pass from upstairs windows. Somewhere under all that water were fences and roads and the graves of the old Vessany settlements, and Sefa felt them pass beneath the hull like names being read aloud.

The entrance to the old cut was invisible unless you knew it: a gap in a wall of willows where the water slid sideways instead of down, a seam in the flood. Her mother had taught her to find it by the leaning tree on the east bank, struck by lightning thirty years ago and still refusing to fall, and there it was—black and bare and bent, exactly where memory had put it.

Sefa took a breath she didn't know she'd been holding.

"Hold on," she said, and drove the *Kestrel* into the seam.

* * *

The cut was everything she'd promised and worse.

It narrowed until the willows brushed both rails, until Bram had to duck under branches at the boiler hatch, until the sound of the river changed—the deep throat-roar of the main channel giving way to a higher, faster voice, water hissing over submerged fences and singing through the drowned culverts of the old Vessany line. Twice they passed trestle bridges, black timber rising out of the flood, and once the rusted hulk of a barge lay across half the channel with its cargo of stone spilled out beneath it like a cairn.

"Depth," Sefa called, and Theo answered, and between the calls they built the channel together, word by word, the way her mother had taught her: the pilot drives, but the boat is steered by a hundred voices, and trust is the only chart that matters.

It was near noon, in the worst of it—a straightaway where the flood had found an old drainage canal and turned it into a chute, water standing in standing waves, the banks too close to turn in—that the *Kestrel* hit the snag.

None of them saw it. A cottonwood, dead and waterlogged, riding just under the surface with its root ball down like a fist. It caught the hull on the port side forward of the paddle box and the whole ship shuddered, a groan coming up through the deck plates that Sefa felt in her teeth, and the wheel went light in her hands.

"Report!" she shouted.

Nils was already over the side rail with a pole, sounding along the planking. "We're taking water! Forward, port side—slow, but it's coming!"

"Stuff it," Sefa said. "Rags, canvas, anything. Bram—I need steam, whatever you've got left, we're going to ground on that bar ahead before we sink in deep water."

Ahead, maybe two hundred yards, a sandbar showed its spine through the flood, a long pale curl of it, and beyond the bar the channel bent hard to starboard around a bluff. It was the only ground in a mile that wasn't trying to drown them.

The *Kestrel* limped toward it, listing, the pumps clacking, Nils and Theo stuffing canvas into the breach with their hands while the river pushed cold fingers through the seams. Sefa held her on the line she wanted with the wheel trembling under her palms and the boiler pressure falling and the current shoving her stern around, and for one long moment—one breath, one heartbeat—it seemed the river would simply take her, roll her over onto the bar and fold her under like it had folded a hundred prouder ships.

Then the keel touched.

The jolt threw them all against the rails. The *Kestrel* settled onto the sandbar with a sigh, listing hard to port, and the flood roared past her on either side, cheated.

For a while nobody spoke. Steam hissed somewhere aft. Water dripped. On the far bank, a heron stood in the shallows and watched them with the indifference of a creature that had seen ships die here before.

"Well," Bram said finally, wiping soot from his face with a rag, leaving it blacker. "That could have gone worse."

"How?" Nils demanded.

"We could all be dead."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon with the ship aground and the water dropping—slowly, grudgingly, the flood cresting and beginning its long retreat. They stuffed the breach properly with oakum and a patch of boiler plate Bram riveted over it, sweating over the iron while the river hissed past. They shifted cargo to ease the list. They ate Nils's onions and beans sitting on the deck plates with their boots dangling over the side, watching the sun go down over the drowned marshes, and it was there, in that orange light, with the work done and nothing left between them and each other, that Sefa finally talked.

"You want to know why I left the syndicate," she said. It wasn't a question either. She'd seen the question in Theo's face for weeks, patient and unasked, and she supposed she owed the answer to people who'd shoved canvas into a hole in the hull with their bare hands rather than let her ship die.

Nobody said anything. Nobody had to.

"I was a pilot for their packet service. Six years. Best routes, best pay, best ship on the river—the *Coriander*, if you've heard of her." She turned her cup in her hands. "And in my sixth year they gave me a contract run. Ordinary freight, they said. Sealed crates, armed escort, don't ask. So I didn't ask. I was young and the pay was good and asking questions is how you end up poor."

She stopped. Out on the water, the heron lifted and flapped away downstream.

"It was people," she said. "Debtors. Families the syndicate had ruined—farmers, shopkeepers, a couple of river men who'd defaulted on loans they never should have signed. Being shipped upriver to the work camps at Karst Hollow. My mother's route. My mother's water." Her voice stayed level, the way it does when a person has practiced a thing a long time. "Forty-one souls in the hold, and my orders were to run the river at night and log them as machinery."

Theo had gone very still. Bram had set down his cup.

"I logged them as machinery," Sefa said. "I ran the river at night. And when we docked, I walked off the *Coriander* and I never went back, and I've been running this patched-up scow on honest freight ever since, telling myself that was enough. That staying clean was the same as being good." She looked up at them, and the sunset was in her eyes, and she didn't blink. "Then you three came aboard with a chest full of paper, and I watched Bram read those ledgers, and I understood that the same hands that loaded my hold are the ones that wrote those grants. So no—we don't sell it, we don't ransom it, we don't trade it for our cuts. We finish the run. All the way to the landfall. Whatever it costs."

The silence after that was long and comfortable, the kind that only happens among people who have stopped being strangers. Nils reached over and refilled her cup without being asked. Theo nodded once, slow, the nod of an engineer signing off on a load rating.

"Whatever it costs," Theo agreed.

* * *

They floated free on the midnight tide, when the dropping river lifted the *Kestrel* off the bar like a hand opening. Sefa took her out into the dark channel alone at first, testing the depth with the lead line herself, feeling the ship answer the wheel the way she used to before the snag, before the years, whole again in the ways that mattered.

By dawn they were through the cut and into the northern reach, running clean water at last, the derelict locks falling astern. The crew slept in shifts. Bram worked his ledgers. Nils cooked. Theo stood at the rail for hours, watching the water, and Sefa watched her watch it, and thought: *there's a woman carrying something heavier than any chest.*

It was near midday, with the bluffs of the upper river beginning to rise gold and green on either side, that Nils came up from below decks looking wrong. Sefa saw it before he said anything—the set of his shoulders, the way he wouldn't meet anyone's eyes. He crossed to Theo and spoke low, and though Sefa couldn't hear the words, she saw Theo's face change, saw the blood leave it, saw her look once at Nils and once downriver, toward the bend where the current carried them.

Toward whatever waited there.

Sefa put both hands on the wheel and held the *Kestrel* steady, and waited to be told what the river had in store for them next.
