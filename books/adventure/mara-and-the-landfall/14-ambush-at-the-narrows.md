## 14. Ambush at the Narrows

Nils had learned to read water before he could read words. His mother taught him on a fishing skiff off the Vessany coast, back when Vessany still existed on maps and his brother was just a boy who laughed too loud for their small house. She'd hold his hand flat above the wake and say, *Feel that? The river tells you everything, if you stop talking long enough to listen.*

He was listening now. That was the problem.

The Kestrel came down the last reach of open channel in the grey hour before dawn, her patched boiler breathing unevenly, her decks still scarred from Sefa's flood-route gamble two days past. Nils stood at the rail with a mug of chicory he wasn't drinking, watching the narrows ahead—the place where the Aldmere pinched itself between bluffs of black stone and ran fast and narrow as a held breath. Everyone aboard knew what the narrows meant. One way through. No room to turn. If Pell's gunboat was going to catch them, it would be there.

What nobody aboard knew—what Nils had kept folded up inside him like a letter he couldn't stop touching—was that Pell already knew they were coming.

Because Nils had told him.

* * *

It had started three nights ago at the coal depot, with a signal lamp and a codebook he'd memorized in another life, when he'd been a syndicate harbor clerk and not a cook-deckhand on a stolen cause. Three short flashes, two long. *Position confirmed. Narrows transit, dawn.* He'd sent it because of Anders.

His brother had been inside Halloway Prison for six years on testimony bought and paid for by the Harrow Syndicate—a dockworker's brawl twisted into manslaughter, a witness who recanted twice and then un-recanted when men visited his family. Nils had spent those six years learning exactly how far a man could bend before he broke. Apparently further than this. Further than cooking for people who trusted him, further than eating at their table, further than watching Theo Kavanagh stand in the lamplight with her father's ghost all over her face and tell them why the chest mattered.

Pell had promised him Anders. A pardon, signed, sealed. All Nils had to do was keep feeding coordinates.

And then Theo had caught him at the lamp—had stood behind him in the dark with the light dying between them—and instead of putting him off the ship at the next lock, she'd made him something stranger than a traitor. A double agent. *Feed them true things that cost us nothing,* she'd said, *and lies where it counts. You'll buy your brother with their own coin.* He'd agreed because agreeing was easier than drowning, and then, somewhere in the days since, agreeing had turned into something else. Something that felt disgracefully like belonging.

Which was why, standing at the rail with his undrunk chicory, Nils felt terror and resolve braided together so tightly he couldn't tell which strand was which. Because last night, at the fueling stop, he'd sent one more message. And this one was a lie.

*Transit delayed. Midday passage, full steam.*

The truth was they'd run the narrows at first light, cold boiler, half power, hugging the eastern shelf where the current ran slack. The lie would put Pell's gunboat—the *Corbie*, sixty feet of riveted arrogance with a bow chaser and eight crew—waiting at the narrows mouth at noon, watching an empty channel, while the Kestrel slipped through behind her at dawn like a thief through a sleeping house.

If Pell believed it.

If Pell hadn't already guessed that his cook had turned.

If Nils's brother wasn't already dead in a cell because of it.

* * *

Sefa Ilo came up beside him without sound, the way she did everything, a compact woman whose hands looked like they'd been carved from mooring line. She didn't look at him. She looked at the narrows.

"Fog's burning off slow," she said. "We'll have maybe twenty minutes of grey to work with. Eastern shelf, third of speed, no smoke showing. Bram's got the fire banked low."

"Twenty minutes," Nils said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him.

"You've been quiet." Still she didn't look at him. "Quieter than usual, even."

"I'm thinking about my brother."

That was true, and it wasn't, and Sefa—who had left the syndicate's service years ago and never said why, who understood better than anyone aboard what it cost to carry two loyalties—just nodded once, slowly, like a woman accepting a payment she knew was short.

"Theo wants you on the wheel with me," she said. "Says your eyes are good in fog."

His eyes were good in fog. His hands, though—his hands had shaken all night, and he'd gripped the edge of his bunk until his knuckles ached, listening to the engine tick as it cooled, doing arithmetic with other people's lives. Eight crew on the *Corbie*. Five aboard the Kestrel, counting himself. One brother in Halloway. One fixer named Ambrose Pell who smiled like a ledger balancing.

"Then I'll be on the wheel," Nils said.

* * *

They entered the narrows into a world the color of unwashed wool.

Fog lay on the water in ropes and shelves, thick enough that the bluffs existed only as a darker absence on either side, and the sound changed—the river's voice compressed between stone walls until it seemed to come from everywhere, a low continuous roar under the hull like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Nils stood at the wheel with Sefa close at his shoulder, calling depths from memory and instinct both: *shelf coming, ease starboard, hold, hold.* The Kestrel crept. Her boiler breathed shallow. Every plank in her old hull creaked its own opinion.

Theo stood at the bow rail, wrapped in an oilskin too big for her, watching the fog the way other people watched fires. Bram Oduya crouched near the engine hatch with a wrench across his knees, listening to his machinery the way a priest listens to confession. Nobody spoke above a murmur. The whole ship moved like a held breath.

Nils counted minutes. Twenty for the fog. Thirty to clear the narrows proper. Forty to the bend at Carrick Point, where the channel widened and a boat could run if it had to.

At minute eleven, he heard it.

Not a sound—a silence. A hole in the river's roar, upstream and to port, where an engine idling against the current would make its own pocket of quiet. He knew that silence. He'd stood watch on boats that made it.

"Sefa," he said, very quietly. "Port side. Upstream. Do you hear—"

She raised a hand. Stopped him. Listened with her whole body, head tilted, eyes closed.

Then the fog tore.

It didn't lift—it ripped, a single seam opening down the middle of the channel as though someone had taken a knife to the morning, and through the seam came the *Corbie*, black-hulled, bow-on, close enough that Nils could see the rivets, could see a man at the bow chaser racking the breech, could see—God help him—that the gunboat was already moving to block the channel. Not searching. Waiting. Positioned exactly where a boat would come through if it came through at midday, except it was here now, at dawn, which meant—

Which meant Pell had known. Had known the delay message was a lie, or had never believed it, or had simply hedged his bets with the patience of a man who owned judges and could afford to wait out a river.

"They knew," Sefa said. Flat. Terrible. "Full astern. Now."

Bram slammed the reversing gear before she finished the sentence, and the Kestrel shuddered like a struck animal, her paddles chewing backward against the current, but the narrows gave them nowhere to go—bluff to port, bluff to starboard, and sixty tons of gunboat closing the throat ahead. The bow chaser fired. The first round went high, shrieking off the western bluff in a gout of stone-dust and echo, and the sound of it went through Nils's chest like a fist.

"Hard aport!" Sefa shouted. "Behind the point—there's a scour pool behind the eastern spur, deep water, we can swing!"

"There's no room to—" Bram started.

"There's room if I say there's room!" Sefa had the wheel now, hauling it over, and the Kestrel heeled sickeningly, her deck running with spray, and for three seconds Nils was certain they were going to die against black stone—and then the stern swung, the scour pool caught them, and they were around, running back downstream with the *Corbie* coming about behind them with the ponderous certainty of something that knows its prey is tired.

Second shot. This one low. It took the Kestrel aft, just above the waterline on the port quarter, and the sound was wrong—not the crack of impact but a deep wooden groan, like a rib breaking. Bram was below before the echo faded. When his voice came up the speaking tube, it was worse than shouting would have been.

"We're holed. Slow leak, but she's taking water. I need ten minutes and calm water, and I can give you neither."

* * *

They ran.

There was nothing else. Sefa drove the wounded Kestrel downstream through the thinning fog with the *Corbie* grinding after them, gaining, always gaining, because a holed steamer carrying extra weight of river water through her planks cannot outrun a gunboat with a clean bottom. Nils stayed at the wheel because Sefa ordered him to and because if he stopped steering he would have to think, and thinking was a country he couldn't afford to visit right now.

*He knew,* Nils thought, over and over, the words wearing a groove in him. *He knew the delay was a lie. Which means he knows I turned. Which means Anders—*

No. He wouldn't finish the thought. He put it down the way you put down something hot, knowing you'd have to pick it up again later with burned hands.

Theo came aft along the tilting deck, oilskin streaming, and grabbed the wheel housing beside him. Water was sheeting across the boards now, green-grey and cold, finding every seam in the old ship.

"How much does Pell know?" she asked. No accusation in it. Just the question, direct as a surveyor's line.

"He knew we'd run at dawn," Nils said. "I sent him a false delay. It didn't take—or he never trusted me at all." His throat worked. "Theo, if he's burned me, my brother—"

"Your brother is alive," Theo said, "because Pell doesn't spend hostages he might still need. Listen to me." She leaned close, fog-wet hair plastered to her cheek, and her eyes were the coldest, clearest thing in that whole grey morning. "You're the only man on this river he thinks is his. That's not a wound anymore, Nils. That's a weapon. We're going to use it."

"How? He's not answering my signals. I tried at the fueling stop—"

"He answered," Theo said. "By being here. That IS an answer—he's telling you the game's over, that he doesn't need your messages anymore." A grim flicker crossed her face, almost a smile, entirely without warmth. "But he's wrong about one thing. He thinks you're finished. Spent. A burned asset runs or dies. So do something a burned asset wouldn't do."

"What?"

"Signal him again. Right now. In the open, where we can all see it. Tell him the Kestrel will ground herself at Carrick Bend and surrender the chest—tell him you're delivering it yourself to save your own skin. Make it pathetic, Nils. Make it desperate. A man who believes he's won stops shooting."

"And when we don't ground? When he finds out it's another lie?"

Theo looked at him, and for a moment the weariness in her face fell away and he saw the thing underneath—the thing her father must have had, the thing that had gotten him killed and that Theo carried like an inheritance she never asked for.

"Then he learns," she said, "that the only person on this river who's lied to him more than you is me. Get the lamp."

* * *

Nils sent the message from the aft rail with the *Corbie* half a mile back and closing, his hands steadier than they had any right to be, the shuttered lamp clicking out its treachery in long and short: *Kestrel disabled, grounding at Carrick Bend. I have the chest. Come alone to the bend and take delivery. I want my brother. This is the last time I ask.*

He meant the last part. God knew he meant it.

For a long minute there was nothing but the chase, the groaning hull, the pump clanking in Bram's engine room like a dying man's heart. Then the *Corbie*'s lamp blinked back: *Acknowledged. Hold position. Any deception and the cook dies first.*

Nils lowered the lamp. His legs felt strange, distant, like they belonged to someone reporting news from far away.

"He says any deception and I die first."

"Good," said Sefa, who had come up silently and heard everything. "Men who make specific threats are men who believe they're in control. Pell thinks he's in control." She took the wheel back from him, gentle about it, the way you take something from someone whose hands are done. "Go forward. Tell Theo the bend is four miles. Tell her we have four miles to invent a miracle."

* * *

The miracle, such as it was, came from Bram Oduya and from the river itself.

Carrick Bend was not a beach. It was a graveyard of the old Vessany line—a mile of drowned pilings and rotted trestle-work where the northern railway had once crossed a slough, abandoned so long ago that the pilings had become a maze, a wooden reef just under the surface, invisible at any speed. No pilot in his right mind would take a ship in there.

Sefa Ilo was not in her right mind. Sefa Ilo had been running this river since she was nine years old, and she knew the maze the way you know the rooms of a house you grew up in—which channels were silted shut, which gaps between pilings would pass a hull of the Kestrel's beam with six feet to spare, and where, in the deepest pocket of the slough, behind a screen of dead trestle, a holed steamer could hide while her engineer stopped her wounds with canvas, tar, and prayer.

So they led the *Corbie* on a four-mile dance—running, falling behind, running again, Nils signaling false distress all the while, playing the panicked traitor with a sincerity that required no acting at all—and then, at the bend, with the gunboat's bow chaser reloaded and its searchlight sweeping the fog ahead, the Kestrel turned hard into the pilings and vanished.

The *Corbie* slowed at the mouth of the slough. Nils, crouched behind dead trestle-work with the others, watched her searchlight wander the maze, touching piling after piling, finding nothing but rot and water. Pell would not bring his gunboat into that wooden reef. Too shallow, too blind, too many ways to hole a hull that couldn't afford holes. He'd send a boat. Maybe two.

Below decks, Bram worked by lantern light with his sleeves rolled and blood and bilge to his elbows, driving oiled canvas into the wound in the Kestrel's quarter while Nils pumped and Theo held the lantern and the river pressed patiently at the broken wood, wanting in.

"Hold it there," Bram muttered. "Hold—there. Good. Good girl." Whether he meant the ship or the canvas or Theo's steady hand, nobody asked.

Above, faint through the planking, came the sound of the *Corbie*'s launch—oars muffled, a whispered order—and then voices in the maze, close, frustrated, circling. Searching for a ship that wasn't there. Searching for a traitor who had, for the first time in his life, told the exact truth about where he was and let the river do the lying for him.

Nils pumped. His arms burned. Terror was still there—it never left, it lived in him now like a second heartbeat—but threaded through it, inextricable, was something harder. Resolve, maybe. Or the beginning of it. The feeling of a man who has finally picked a side and found that the side holds him up instead of weighing him down.

*Hold, Anders,* he thought, pumping, pumping, water singing through the pipe. *Hold the way I'm holding. I'm coming. I'm coming with the whole truth in my hands, and this time it's worth something.*

* * *

They slipped out of the slough an hour after dusk, dark-shipped, no lights, the Kestrel limping but whole, her patched quarter weeping a little at the seams the way old soldiers do. Behind them the *Corbie* sat at the bend with her searchlight sweeping empty water, hunting a ghost, and Ambrose Pell—Nils could feel him out there in the dark, patient as arithmetic—was learning the lesson that would define the rest of it:

The cook was gone. Truly gone. And a man like Pell did not forgive that, did not negotiate with it, did not spend hostages on it.

He ended it.

Far downstream, at the landfall where the old rails ran into the sea, a message was already moving toward them faster than any boat—carried by telegraph, by rider, by the syndicate's long corrupt arms—to set the final board in place. Nils didn't know its contents yet. But he knew Pell, and when the Kestrel rounded Carrick Point into the open dark, and Theo stood at the bow looking south toward the landfall with the fog finally, fully gone, Nils saw her expression change.

"What is it?" he asked.

She didn't answer at first. Then, quietly, almost to herself:

"He's not chasing us anymore."
