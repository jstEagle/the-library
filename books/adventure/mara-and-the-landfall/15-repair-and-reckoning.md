## 15. Repair and Reckoning

The Kestrel lay on her side in the mud like a whale that had given up arguing with the world.

Theo stood on the shingle at dawn and looked at her the way she imagined a doctor looks at a patient he has grown fond of and does not entirely expect to save. The steamer had been dragged up onto a shelf of grey gravel where the Aldmere bent wide and slow, a backwater Sefa knew from years of running contraband past men who thought themselves clever. Her hull showed a ragged star of damage below the waterline where Pell's gunboat had kissed them—holed twice, plating peeled back like the lid of a tin, one paddle wheel hanging by its shaft bearings. Smoke still seeped, thin and apologetic, from the seam where the fire had been beaten down with wet canvas and Nils screaming.

The river moved past them without opinion. Mist came off it in long ribbons. Somewhere upstream, a heron complained.

"She'll float again," Bram said behind her. He had not slept. Nobody had slept. His hands were black to the wrist with rivet grime and there was soot in the creases of his neck like a tattoo gone wrong. "She'll float again if I can get plate off the old lock gates downstream, and if the shaft bearing isn't cracked, and if the weather holds four days."

"And if none of those things happen?"

"Then we walk." He said it mildly, the way he said everything, and Theo found she almost believed him. Bram Oduya was the kind of man whose calm was not optimism but arithmetic—he had counted what was true and refused to count anything else.

Four days. She turned the number over and felt how thin it was, how much could arrive inside it.

* * *

By midmorning the beach had become a shipyard, which is to say a chaos with a purpose barely visible in it. Sefa waded the shallows marking the hull's wounds with chalk, calling depths to Bram in the flat voice she used for everything except water. Nils hauled timber. The chest sat above the tideline under an oilcloth weighted with stones, and Theo caught herself—three times in the first hour—looking at it the way you look at a sleeping animal you are not sure is tame.

They stopped work when the sun stood high enough to make the gravel steam. Sefa built a fire out of driftwood and broken dunnage, and Nils produced coffee that tasted of char and river, and for a while nobody spoke, because exhaustion has its own etiquette.

It was Sefa who broke it, because Sefa did not believe in waiting for things.

"So," she said, cradling her tin cup. "What is it for?"

Theo looked at her. "The chest."

"The chest." Sefa nodded at the oilcloth mound as if it were a person they'd all been politely ignoring. "We've bled for it. Nils nearly burned for it. That boat—" she tipped her chin at the Kestrel lying wounded in the mud, "—is going to cost me every coin I have and probably my name in three ports. So before we go any further up this river, I want to know what we're carrying it toward. Ransom? Leverage? Or do we burn the whole rotten edifice down and stand in the ashes feeling righteous?" She drank. "I've done all three in my life. They pay differently."

Bram set his cup down carefully, the way he did when something mattered. "Exposure," he said. "Publish it. All of it. Every grant, every payoff, every signature."

"To whom?" Sefa asked. "The courts? Wren's own judges are in those pages, Bram. We read them at the vault. You read them aloud. Half the magistracy of two provinces is bought and paid for, and the other half is scared. You hand that ledger to a court, it goes into a drawer, and six months later the drawer is burned and we're dead in ditches with our deaths ruled accidents."

"Then hand it to newspapers."

"Newspapers have owners." Sefa smiled without warmth. "I've met some of them. They dine with the same people Pell dines with."

"Then hand it to everyone." Bram's voice stayed level but something underneath it had begun to burn. "Every clerk, every dockhand, every farmer along every line the syndicate stole. Print ten thousand copies. Nail it to church doors if you have to. A secret that everyone knows stops being leverage and becomes a fire."

"A fire burns whoever's standing closest," Nils said quietly.

They all looked at him. He was sitting slightly apart, knees drawn up, turning his cup in circles. Since the narrows—since the false coordinates, since Pell's shells had come down on empty water instead of on them—Nils had been different. Smaller and larger at once. He had bought their lives with his cover and he knew what that made him now: a man with no side to run to.

"You're thinking of your brother," Theo said.

Nils flinched. "I'm thinking that leverage keeps people alive. Ransom buys boats and lawyers and quiet passages out of countries. Exposure gets people hanged—the wrong people, sometimes. The right ones too, maybe, but after." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "My brother is in Harrowgate prison on testimony the syndicate wrote. If we publish, they have nothing left to trade for him. If we sell, maybe I buy him out. That's my vote. Sell it. I know it's ugly. It's mine anyway."

Silence, then. The river filled it, sliding past with its endless patience.

Sefa stretched her legs out and studied her boots. "Leverage," she said at last, "is just exposure you're saving for later. And ransom is exposure you're selling to the devil himself. I ran guns for the syndicate for six years, and I'll tell you what I learned about holding things over powerful men." She looked at Theo. "They don't negotiate. They wait until they can take it back, and then they kill everyone who held it. Pell isn't going to let us auction his masters' sins. He's going to keep coming until the chest is ash or we are."

"Then what would you do?" Theo asked.

"Me?" Sefa shrugged. "I'd have sold it in Vessany waters to the first buyer with a gunboat and been drinking on a beach in the south by now. But I didn't, and neither did any of you, and I've been wondering why." Her eyes came around the circle and settled last on Theo, and stayed there. "Why, Captain?"

* * *

Theo got up because it was easier to say standing, facing the river, than sitting in the middle of their attention.

She walked a few paces down the shingle. The mist had burned away and the far bank showed itself—willows, a rotted fence post, the green indifferent country that had once been Vessany and was now a name on no map. Behind her the fire crackled. Nobody followed. Nobody spoke. They were giving her the silence the way you give someone room at a graveside, and she understood that they already knew, somehow, that what she was about to say had been waiting in her a long time.

"My father was a surveyor," she said. "You knew that much. Everyone knows that much—it's on the license I lost, it's in the file they keep at the syndicate offices, it's the polite version." She picked up a flat stone and turned it in her fingers without looking at it. "The truth is he wasn't surveying closed lines for the province. Not at the end. He was surveying for himself. For a case."

The stone was cool and smooth. She kept her eyes on the water.

"He grew up in Vessany. Before the collapse. He was nine when the banks failed, eleven when the grants were voided and the land changed hands, thirteen when the nation stopped existing on paper. He used to tell me about it like other fathers tell fairy tales—the harbor at Solenne, the trains that ran on time, the bell at the customs house that rang when a ship came home. He never believed it fell honestly. He said a country doesn't die of bad luck. It dies of murder, and murder leaves papers."

Bram had gone very still. Sefa had stopped pretending to drink.

"For twenty years he collected. Quietly. A deed here, a letter there, a payroll record from a clerk who owed him a favor. He mapped it the way he mapped rail lines—every connection, every junction, every place the money turned a corner. He called it the northern line, jokingly, because that's what he was tracing: the line of the theft, running north from the coast to wherever it ended." Her throat tightened. She made herself go on. "He got close. Close enough that someone noticed. In the spring of the year I turned nineteen, he went out on a solo survey of the old spur near Corren Falls and never came back."

The river said nothing. It had heard worse, probably. It kept all of it.

"They found his instrument case downstream, three weeks later. Just the case. The coroner wrote *misadventure*—slipped crossing a flooded cut, body carried off. The syndicate sent a condolence letter and settled his debts against his estate, which is how I inherited what I owe Ambrose Pell. They were very tidy about it." She laughed, briefly, and hated the sound. "Tidy. That's the word. Everything the syndicate touches is tidy. My father's death was the tidiest thing they ever did."

"The ledgers," Bram said softly. "In the chest. The handwriting on the early pages—"

"Is his." Theo finally turned around. Their faces were lit unevenly by the fire, and she made herself look at each of them. "You've been reading his work for two hundred miles and didn't know it. Those first ledgers, the ones in the faded ink, the ones with the marginal notes in shorthand—that's his shorthand. I learned my letters out of it. The chest in the Vespera is the end of the line he started. Someone collected his proof after he died—or part of it—and shipped it, and the river took the ship, and the river kept it for twenty years until it gave it to me." She opened her hand; the flat stone dropped onto the shingle. "So when you ask what it's for, understand that I've asked myself that since the night I broke the seal. And here is my answer, as honest as I can make it."

She breathed.

"I wanted to sell it. At first. I'll not pretend otherwise. I wanted to clear the debt and buy back my licenses and disappear somewhere the name Kavanagh meant nothing. Then I wanted leverage—I wanted Pell to flinch, I wanted the men who wrote my father's death warrant to feel a cold wind on their necks. And every one of those wants felt reasonable, right up until the warehouse burned. Right up until I watched a man torch forty tons of another man's livelihood just to send me a message, and understood that he'd do the same to a town. To a family. To any of you." Her voice roughened. "Power like theirs doesn't negotiate. Sefa's right. It waits, and it takes, and it tidies."

"Then exposure," Bram said. "Publication. All of it."

"All of it," Theo agreed. "Unredacted. Unrationed. Not because the courts will be fair—they won't, not most of them, not at first. Not because the newspapers will be brave—they'll be terrified, and then they'll be hungry, and hunger will do. But because a secret can be killed, and a truth that ten thousand people carry cannot. My father tried to move his proof through channels. Through proper hands. Through the law." She shook her head slowly. "The law was the destination of the bribes. He never saw it. He died believing the line ended at a courthouse, when it ended at a judge's desk, and the judge cashed the check."

Nils had put his face in his hands. When he lowered them, his eyes were wet and furious. "And if publishing gets my brother killed?"

"Then we get him out first," Theo said. "Before a single page goes public. That's the order of operations, and I'll stake the whole expedition on it. You didn't betray us, Nils—you bargained with the only currency you had, and then you spent it buying our lives instead of your brother's freedom. I don't forget debts like that. None of us should."

Sefa snorted softly, but it was a warm sound. "Listen to her. Two weeks ago she wouldn't tell us her middle name."

"I still won't," Theo said, and something loosened in the circle—a breath, a half-laugh, the small human sound of a crew becoming something more than a contract.

Bram stood, brushing gravel from his knees. "Four days of repairs," he said. "I'll draft the publication plan tonight. Printing houses, foreign presses, couriers—we'll need three routes out of the country or they'll strangle one. And we'll need—" He stopped.

They all heard it at the same moment: the low, rhythmic thud of an engine on the river, still distant, still hidden by the bend, but coming.

Sefa was on her feet first, glass already at her eye, scanning the mist-thinned channel downstream. For a long moment she said nothing. Then she lowered the glass, and her face had gone the color of the gravel.

"It's a launch," she said. "Small. Running light and fast." She paused, and Theo watched the words assemble themselves reluctantly behind her eyes. "And there's a man standing in the bow with his hands tied in front of him."

Theo took the glass. The image swam, steadied.

Nils.

Walking the deck of Pell's launch with his shoulders squared and his head high, a prisoner being brought back to the place where prisoners were traded—and even at this distance, even through heat-shimmer and river haze, Theo could see that he was not struggling. He was pointing. Deliberately, repeatedly, gesturing toward the shore, toward the beached and broken Kestrel, toward them.

Leading Pell straight in—or leading him somewhere else entirely.

"Get to cover," Theo said quietly. "All of you. Now."
