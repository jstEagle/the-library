## 16. The Traitor's Price

They found the message nailed to the Kestrel's mast at dawn, and Theo knew before she read it that Nils was gone.

The paper was heavy stock, syndicate stock, the kind used for dividend notices and funeral invitations, and it had been folded with a precision no river-runner would bother with. Sefa took it down with a boat hook rather than touch it bare-handed, and Bram read it first because his hands were steadiest, and then he went very quiet in the way that was worse than shouting.

*The cook is our guest,* it said, in a copperplate hand Theo recognized from a hundred collection notices. *He sends his regards and his apologies. Bring the chest to the landfall dock, two days hence, noon. Come armed and you will not need to be. Come without it and we will return him to you by installments.*

Below the words, pressed into the wax of the seal, was the Harrow Syndicate's mark: a plow breaking ground, rendered in black.

Theo stood on the wet deck with the fog coming off the Aldmere in slow gray ropes and felt something in her chest go very still and very cold. Not fear—not exactly. Fear had been her companion for weeks now, a low animal hum beneath everything, and she knew its weather. This was different. This was the silence after a surveyor's line snaps taut. This was the moment when all the wandering stopped and there was only the bearing ahead.

"He signaled Pell," Sefa said flatly. "Back at Marrow Bend. I told you—"

"You told me," Theo said. "And I made him a double agent instead of putting him ashore. That was my choice, not his betrayal." She heard how it sounded—the loyalty of a woman defending her own judgment—and did not take it back. "Nils didn't know where the landfall dock was. He couldn't have given them coordinates for a place he'd never seen on any chart. Which means they didn't need him to tell them where we were going."

Bram looked up from the note. His spectacles had fogged and he hadn't wiped them. "They knew already."

"They always knew." Theo turned the paper over. Blank on the reverse, but the fold lines told her it had been carried folded small, in a pocket, against a body. Nils's body? Or the messenger's? "Pell has known our heading since the narrows. Maybe since the drowned station. He let us run because running kept us moving toward the one place he needed us to be—the terminus, where the old northern line meets the sea, where the grants say what they say. He doesn't want the chest destroyed. He wants it delivered. To him. At the scene, with witnesses, so that whatever we found can be buried under a tidy story about thieves dying in a shootout at an abandoned dock."

Sefa spat over the rail. "Then why take the boy?"

"Insurance," Bram said quietly. "And bait. And—" he hesitated, and Theo watched him arrive at the same arithmetic she had already finished, "—and a witness removed. Nils has seen the ledgers. He's heard us talk. If Pell means to end this cleanly—"

"He means to end *everything* cleanly," Theo said. "Every witness. Every copy. Every hand that touched the chest." She looked up at the mast, at the pale sky beyond it where gulls wheeled and screamed their idiot hunger. "There is no trade. There never was. A man who wanted a trade wouldn't nail his terms to my mast where anyone could read them. He wants me to come believing it's a trade, because a woman coming to trade comes careful, comes alone-ish, comes with the chest in her arms and her guard half-lowered out of hope. Hope is the weapon. He's going to hold it to my throat and pull."

* * *

They beached the Kestrel in a reed-choked inlet four miles short of the landfall, hull groaning against the mud, and held a council in the wheelhouse with the door shut and the stove lit against the morning damp. On the chart table Theo spread everything they had: the ledgers, the grants, the key, Bram's transcriptions in his small exact engineer's hand, and the single page she had shown Wren at Corren's Landing, which Wren had returned to her with a magistrate's neatness and a look Theo still hadn't finished deciphering.

"Walk it through," Sefa said. She sat on the wheelhouse bench with her boots up and a knife turning over and over in her fingers, not fidgeting—thinking. It was how she thought. "Pell takes Nils. Pell names the place. Pell says noon, two days. What does he actually have?"

"Men," Bram said. "A gunboat, or what's left of it after the narrows. And the dock itself. The landfall terminus is a natural kill-ground—one approach by water, one by the old rail bed, sightlines open along the quay for three hundred yards. He'll have it covered like a parade ground."

"And what does he not have?"

Theo answered before she'd fully decided to. "The truth about what happens after he kills us."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a heron lifted off the shallows with a sound like sails filling.

"Pell is a fixer," Theo went on, and now she was pacing, three steps each way between the chart table and the wheel, the old surveyor's habit of measuring a room by walking it. "Fixers are not soldiers. They're accountants with pistols. Everything Pell does—every ambush, every burned warehouse, every ultimatum—is designed to leave paperwork that reads one way. Clean. Deniable. A salvage crew lost to river pirates. A tragic fire. An accident at an abandoned dock. He cannot afford mess. Mess attracts inquiry, and inquiry is the one thing the syndicate bought twenty years of judges specifically to avoid." She stopped. "So we give him mess."

"How?" Sefa's knife had gone still.

"We don't come to trade. We don't even come to fight—not first. We come to *publish*." She laid her palm flat on the ledger. "Two days is enough time for copies. Bram, can your transcription be duplicated?"

"In triplicate by tomorrow night, if I can get ink and a press. There's a mission station at Halfway Slough with a hand press—the brothers there owe me for a boiler repair."

"Take the launch. Take everything except one full set. Sefa, how long to reach the landfall by the back channels, the ones only you know?"

Sefa's eyes narrowed. "With the Kestrel limping? Eighteen hours, if the tide cooperates and the reeds haven't silted the Cut since I ran it last. Why the back channels?"

"Because Pell expects us on the water, from downriver, at noon, flying some kind of parley rag. So that's what he gets—a boat, visible, slow, approaching the dock on his schedule. With someone aboard who looks like me from three hundred yards." She met Sefa's gaze. "But the chest won't be on it. The chest goes overland, tonight, through the marsh, on foot, with me."

Silence. Then Bram: "And when Pell searches the decoy boat and finds no chest?"

"Then he learns the trade was false from his side too, and he has a hostage he suddenly needs more than he planned, and a dock full of men pointed at nothing." Theo felt the plan assembling itself the way she used to feel a bridge assemble in her head—load paths finding their way down through the structure to solid ground. "Meanwhile the real cargo is already behind him. Because here's what Pell has forgotten, standing on his stolen dock: the landfall terminus isn't just a dock. It's a *railway*. The old northern line runs inland from that quay forty miles to Vessany Junction, and the rails are still there—rotted ties, rusted iron, but rails. And rails carry weight. Rails carry *witnesses*. There are villages along that line, whole towns that were strangled when the syndicate collapsed the grants—people who've spent twenty years being told the documents proving their claim to their own land burned with the Vespera. People who will come running when a stranger walks into their square carrying proof otherwise."

"You want to start a riot," Sefa said. There was something in her voice that wasn't disapproval.

"I want to start a *quorum*," Theo said. "I want two hundred landless farmers standing on that dock at noon in two days' time, holding copied pages, when Pell gives whatever order he intends to give. Let him explain to a crowd why the cook dies. Let him try to burn evidence in front of the people it was stolen from. Fixers die in daylight, Sefa. That's the whole trick of them. They only work in the dark, and we are going to bring the sun."

* * *

She wrote to Wren that afternoon, and hated every word of it, and wrote them anyway.

Not a plea—Wren would spit at a plea. A set of facts, in surveyor's language, coordinates and times and load-bearing estimates, as if she were submitting a report to a chief engineer. *The landfall dock, such-and-such bearing. Hostage: Nils Ferro, age 24, cook, guilty of nothing but a brother's name. Expected strength: twelve to twenty armed. One gunboat, damaged. My party: four, one vessel unseaworthy for combat. I intend to be present regardless. You may use this letter however serves.*

She read it over twice and added one line at the bottom, crossed it out, and then, because she was done lying to herself if she was done lying to everyone else, wrote it again:

*I would rather you came as Elara than as the Commission. But I'll take either.*

She sealed it and gave it to a reed-cutter's boy with a skiff and too much curiosity, paid him double to run it upriver to the port at Corren's Landing, and watched until the sail shrank to a white fleck and vanished into the haze. Then she stood a long time looking north, toward the landfall, where the old rails would be running out of the marsh grass and into the gray water, ending nowhere, built on theft, abandoned when the money moved on. Her father had drawn those terminal plans. She had them memorized—had, in fact, grown up tracing them in the margins of his letters, the elegant fan of sidings, the coal staithes, the great crane that had once loaded Vessany timber onto ships bound for ports that no longer admitted Vessany existed.

He had died collecting this same proof. She understood that now, fully, in the marrow way. Not died *for* it—died *of* it, of the slow poison of knowing and having no one to tell, of riding a closed line year after year with the truth locked in his chest like ballast. She would not die of it. She would spend it. All of it, at once, in public, in daylight, with her name attached to every page—even the parts that would cost her, the parts about the bribed surveyors whose signatures sat next to her father's on the falsified grant maps, the parts that would make certain boards of examiners ask certain questions about the Kavanagh name. Let them ask. She had spent six years being invisible, and invisibility had bought her nothing but smaller rooms.

* * *

Night came down over the inlet like a lid. They loaded the chest into Bram's borrowed punt at slack water—Theo, Sefa, and the chest itself, while Bram made ready to run for the press and Nils's cousin's boy, who had joined at Halfway Slough and could be trusted exactly as far as a share agreement reached, agreed to pole the decoy barge at dawn wearing Theo's oilskin coat and hat.

"The coat smells like you," Sefa said, helping her cinch the waterproof wrap around the chest. "That's the point, I suppose."

"That's the point."

"And the rest of it? Walking eight miles of marsh at night with the thing Pell would burn a warehouse for strapped to your back?" Sefa looked at her across the wrapped chest, firelight from the stove catching the old scar that split her eyebrow. "You've thought about what happens if we're seen."

"I've thought about what happens if we're not," Theo said. "If we deliver the chest to that dock on Pell's terms, we die politely. If we don't arrive at all, Nils dies and Pell wins by default and hunts us forever. The only road out is the one he hasn't priced in: the chest arriving *surrounded*, arriving *known*, arriving in front of two hundred people whose land it describes. He can shoot a courier. He can't shoot a congregation."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then she laughed, low and rough, and clapped Theo on the shoulder hard enough to stagger her. "Eighteen years on this river and I finally meet a woman who fights like floodwater. Fine. I'll take the decoy barge myself—I'm the better liar, and if they board, they'll find a captain with a syndicate past and a story about mutiny. You walk the marsh. Bram runs the press. And Theo—" she paused at the gangplank, silhouetted against the lantern, "—bring our boy home. Even the stupid ones. *Especially* the stupid ones."

* * *

The marsh at night was a country of whispers. Theo moved along the old surveyor's cuts—her father's cuts, cut into the peat sixty years ago to carry his instrument lines, still holding water in their straight scars—and the chest rode her shoulders like a sleeping animal, warm under the oilcloth where her body heat soaked through. Twice she froze in the reeds as lantern light swept the distant water: Pell's pickets, walking the shorelines, watching the river approaches. Watching everywhere except inland. Watching everywhere except the dead railway.

Near midnight she crested the last shingle bar and saw it for the first time in twenty years: the landfall. The quay running out into the black sea like a broken sentence. The crane arm skeletal against the stars. And the rails—she knelt and put her palm flat on the nearest one, and the iron was cold and real and humming faintly with the tide, and she thought: *you end here, but not everything ends here.*

Behind her, far down the dark coast, a light blinked twice from the decoy barge getting underway. Ahead, on the dock, watch fires burned where Pell's men waited for a noon that was thirty hours away.

Thirty hours to bring the sun.

Theo shifted the chest higher on her back and started down toward the sleeping villages of the old northern line, knocking, one by one, on twenty years of closed doors.
