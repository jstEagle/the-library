## 17. The Landfall

The rails ran out of the world.

Theo saw them first from the Kestrel's wheelhouse, a dark lattice emerging from the morning haze where the river widened into the sea—five lines of iron marching side by side out of the dunes and straight into the water, as if the engineers had simply refused to stop and the ocean had refused to argue. The tide had been at them for twenty years. Rust had eaten the fishplates to lace. Gulls stood on the rails in long patient rows, like mourners who had forgotten whose funeral it was.

"Vessany Terminus," Sefa said, and spat over the rail, an old habit Theo had never asked her to explain. "My father called it the Mouth. Said everything the north ever stole went down that throat."

Beyond the rails, the landfall itself: a crescent of gray shingle and black timber, the bones of a town built in five years and abandoned in one. A customs house with its roof caved in. A crane lying on its side like a felled animal, its cable trailing into the surf. And at the end of a rotting pier, half-collapsed already, the great loading shed where Vessany ore had once been weighed and stamped and shipped south to build cities for men who had never seen a mine.

Theo stood at the glass and felt her father's absence the way she felt weather—in her knees, in the old break in her left wrist. He had drawn this place. She had found his survey folio after he died: *Northern Line, Terminus Approach, T. Kavanagh, Senior Engineer*, every contour inked in that careful hand she'd inherited nothing of but the habit of. He had stood on this shingle and believed the grants were honest. Then he'd gone looking, and the looking had killed him, and now his daughter was sailing his terminus with a chest full of proof and two days to spend it.

"Where do you want her?" Sefa asked.

"Not at the dock." Theo traced the shoreline on the chart Nils had pinned flat. "There's a cut behind the dune line—my father's folio shows a service channel for the stone barges. It'll be shallow but it's there. Put us in behind the hill, out of sight of the water."

"And if Pell's gunboat comes up the channel behind us?"

"He won't. Not yet. He wants us landed, cornered, all our pieces on the board." She looked at the chart until the lines stopped swimming. "He's spent three weeks chasing us. Men like Ambrose Pell don't chase things they intend to sink from a distance. They want to watch you understand."

Sefa gave her a long look. "You've been reading him too close, Theo."

"Somebody has to."

* * *

They came through the service channel at slack water with Bram sounding the depth off the bow with a weighted line and Nils at the helm, because Sefa wanted her hands free and Nils, since the narrows, had not asked to be trusted with anything except work. The Kestrel scraped sand twice and freed herself twice, and then the dunes closed around her and the sea disappeared and there was only the smell of salt and rusted iron and the enormous quiet of a place built by money and left by it.

Theo went ashore first, boots sinking in shingle, and climbed the low bluff above the loading shed. From there she could see the whole terminus laid out below: the pier, the shed, the five dead rails running into the green water, and beyond them the open sea, flat and pewter-colored under a high thin sky. Two days. Pell's ultimatum had named the hour—dusk tomorrow—and the place—the dock—and the price—the chest for the boy.

Nils stood beside her. He had barely spoken since Pell's men had taken him and let him go again, a message delivered in a bruise across his jaw: *we know where your brother sleeps*. His hands were steady now. Theo had watched them shake for a day and stop.

"You think he'll bring Aris," Nils said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I think he'll bring something that looks like Aris from thirty yards."

"And if it is him?"

Theo didn't answer immediately. Below them, Wren was walking the length of the pier with a notebook, measuring distances by eye, marking positions with chalk stubs she'd bought in the last river port. A magistrate without a commission, without a warrant, without so much as a constable—and still she walked the ground like the law walked with her, because in Wren's case it always had.

"Then we trade," Theo said finally. "And we make sure the trade costs him more than it costs us."

* * *

Wren counted the pier's pilings twice, because counting was how she prayed.

Forty-one pilings, and eleven of them burned through at the waterline, and the deck planking soft enough in places that she could press a thumb through the grain. Pell would know this. Pell had chosen this dock not because it was defensible but because it was fragile—a stage built to collapse, a place where a chest could go into the harbor and a woman could go after it and neither would need to be fished out quickly. Everything about the man was paperwork, even his murders. He planned them like contracts, with clauses and contingencies, and the contingency here, Wren was nearly certain, was fire.

She wrote *oil?* in the margin of her notebook and underlined it.

"Theo." She didn't raise her voice; she didn't have to. Theo came down the shingle slope with that economy of motion Wren had learned to read like a timetable—every step placed, nothing wasted, a woman who had made herself small for so many years that now, given a reason to take up space, she did it carefully, as though the space might be repossessed.

"You've been at the shed," Theo said.

"There's a second entrance. Loading ramp on the seaward side, door off its track. If Pell puts men inside, we can come at them from behind." Wren held out the notebook. "I want your father's survey. The one you keep in the oilskin packet. I want to know where the culverts run under the yard."

Theo hesitated. Wren watched her hesitate—watched the flinch pass through her, the reflex of a woman who had survived by showing no one her maps—and then watched her decide, and unsling the packet, and hand it over. Their fingers touched on the oilskin. Neither of them remarked on it. There would be time, or there wouldn't.

"My father marked the culverts himself," Theo said. "Red ink. He surveyed this yard when he was younger than I am now." Her voice went flat in the particular way Wren had learned meant grief being set down like a heavy crate. "He thought he was building something honest."

"They usually do," Wren said. "That's what makes the dishonest ones necessary."

"That's a cynical thing for a magistrate to say."

"I'm not a magistrate. I resigned, remember. I'm currently, legally speaking, a busybody with a notebook." She opened the folio against her knee. The red ink was faded to the color of dried blood. Culvert one, under the crane apron. Culvert two, running beneath the loading shed itself—big enough for a man to crawl, dry at low tide. Wren felt the plan assemble itself in her head the way a case used to assemble, evidence clicking into position, and marveled briefly at how little had changed. Remove the office and the method remained. Perhaps the method had been the office all along.

"Pell will bring twelve men or fewer," she said. "A gunboat carries a crew of eight, and he'll leave four aboard. He'll stage the exchange at the pier head—open ground, his marksmen covering from the shed and the dune crest. He'll put the boy—" she corrected herself, gently, "—he'll put whoever he brings at the center, because he knows Nils will move toward him and he knows you'll move toward Nils."

"So we don't move toward him."

"So we're already moving before he finishes talking." Wren tapped the red-inked culvert. "Bram goes under the shed tonight with the lantern oil from the galley—not to burn it, to soak the seaward ramp and rig it. If Pell's men stage inside, we seal the door and take them from the culvert. Sefa holds the Kestrel behind the dunes with steam up. Nils—"

"Nils stands where Pell can see him," Theo said quietly. "Because if he doesn't, Pell burns whatever he brought instead of a hostage. And because Nils has earned the right to be the one standing there when it breaks."

Wren looked up. The wind came off the sea and moved the hair back from Theo's face, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the gulls cried over the dead rails, and Wren understood that she was no longer planning an arrest. There was no charge sheet for any of this. No court above them clean enough to receive it. What they were building on this broken dock was older than courts—an argument made in person, at close range, with the whole rotten ledger as its text.

"If this goes wrong," Wren said, "there's no institution to fall back on. No appeal. Just us and the water."

"That was true three weeks ago," Theo said. "We just hadn't read far enough in the ledger to know it."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the light went long and gold, and the landfall showed them what it had been.

Bram pried boards off the loading shed and found the weighmaster's office intact behind a jammed door: a desk, a stool, a wall of pigeonholes still holding waybills, hundreds of them, each stamped with the Vessany crown and countersigned in the syndicate's cipher. He stood among the paper with dust sifting down through the broken roof beams, reading names, and came out at dusk looking ten years older.

"It's all here," he said to the crew, gathered around a driftwood fire behind the dunes. "Every shipment. Every signature. This wasn't an office, it was an archive nobody bothered to burn because nobody thought anyone would ever come this far north again." He held up a single waybill. "Dated six months after the 'bankruptcy.' Ore moving under syndicate seal. The collapse was theater, and they couldn't resist keeping the playbill."

"Tomorrow night it stops being theirs," Sefa said. She was sharpening a boathook blade with a whetstone, slow rhythmic strokes, her eyes on the fire. "Twenty years my family carried their cargo and swallowed their insults. Tomorrow the paper talks."

Nils sat apart, as he had since the narrows, turning a coin over his knuckles—his brother's coin, Theo knew, sent from the prison at Coldharbour years ago. When the others slept he came and sat beside her at the fire's edge.

"If it isn't Aris tomorrow," he said, "if Pell brings a stranger or a coat on a frame—I'll still walk out there. You should know that going in. Don't spend the plan trying to protect me."

"I don't spend plans on protection," Theo said. "I spend them on outcomes."

"That's what worries me." He flipped the coin, caught it. "My brother used to say the law was a river. Slow, but it gets everywhere eventually. Out here I've learned rivers have floods and droughts too." He looked at her across the firelight. "Which are you tomorrow? The flood or the drought?"

Theo fed a stick to the flames and watched it catch. "I'm the surveyor," she said. "I just measure where the water's going to go."

* * *

Wren did not sleep. She told herself it was strategy—last watch, final review—but past midnight she climbed the dune alone and stood looking at the five rails running into the black water, moonlit, silvered, impossibly patient, and admitted the truth: she was grieving.

Not for the commission. She had written her resignation in nine minutes and meant every word of it. She grieved for the version of herself who had boarded the Kestrel's wake three weeks ago certain that somewhere above the rot there was sound timber—that the courts were compromised but the ministry wasn't, that the ministry was dirty but the bench wasn't, and so on upward, some clean room at the top of the tower where justice kept its lamp lit. She had spent her whole life climbing toward that room. The vault had shown her the tower was hollow to the top, and the lamp had been out for years, and everyone she'd ever saluted had known.

And yet.

She took out her notebook, this small square of authority she still carried, and by moonlight wrote the disposition of tomorrow: positions, signals, contingencies, in her precise clerk's hand. It calmed her. Law, she had come to understand, was never really the buildings or the benches or the seals. It was this—someone writing down what was true, and what would be done about it, and signing their name. Everything else was furniture.

Footsteps in the shingle. Theo came and stood beside her, wrapped in a coat against the sea wind, and for a while they watched the rails together.

"My father drew those," Theo said. "Straight into the sea. Everyone told him a terminus should curve along the shore. He said no—the line goes where the ore goes, and the ore goes to the ships, and the ships go to the world." A pause. "He believed the world was worth sending things to."

"Do you?"

"I believe the world should give receipts." Theo turned to her, and the moon caught the scar through her eyebrow, and her expression was the grimmest calm Wren had ever seen on a living face. "At dusk tomorrow, Pell walks onto that dock expecting debtors. He expects a frightened engineer, a turncoat cook, a disgraced magistrate hiding behind other people's guns. By the time he understands what he's actually standing in front of, the culvert will be behind him and the ledger will be in his face and there will be nowhere in three nations he can spend another bribe."

"And if it goes wrong?"

"Then the chest goes into the harbor, and I go after it, and you make sure someone writes down what happened." Theo's hand found hers in the dark—briefly, firmly, a contract signed. "Whatever else breaks tomorrow, the record survives. That's the whole design. That's the trap. Not Pell. Forgetting."

Wren closed her hand around the warmth and let it go. "Then get some sleep, Engineer. We build at dawn."

Below them the tide crept up the dead rails, touching iron, retreating, touching again—like a hand checking whether the world was still there. Far out on the black water, so faint it might have been a star falling wrong, a light blinked twice and went dark.

Pell's gunboat, taking up its station.

Theo saw it too. Neither of them said a word. The calm had done its work; what remained was readiness, laid out on the broken dock like instruments before surgery, and less than a day of it left to hold.
