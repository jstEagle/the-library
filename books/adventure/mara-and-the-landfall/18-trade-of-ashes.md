## 18. Trade of Ashes

The landfall dock had been built to receive empire and now received only gulls.

Theo stood at the end of it with the chest at her feet and the sea running gray-green beneath the pilings, and she thought: *my father walked this wood.* The rails came out of the dune grass behind her, six tracks converging on the terminus, rusted to a uniform brown, and then they simply stopped—ran off the edge of the loading platform and into nothing, as if the engineers of twenty years ago had believed the sea might one day consent to carry freight. Weeds grew up between the ties in silver-green tufts. A crane arm leaned over the water like a man peering down a well.

The morning was cold enough to see her breath. She counted the things she could control, because counting was what she did when fear came calling: the chest, sealed and heavy; Wren somewhere behind the derelict signal house with a pistol she'd sworn she knew how to use; Sefa and Bram aboard the *Kestrel*, anchored just past the breakwater where Pell's glass couldn't miss her but couldn't reach her either; Nils—Nils in Pell's hands, if he still breathed, which Theo had decided to believe until the world proved otherwise.

And Nils's lies, seeded through two weeks of false reports, growing now toward their harvest. Pell believed the northern channel dredged and passable. Pell believed the *Kestrel* low on coal. Pell believed his gunboat's secondary launch could cover the cliff road—and so it would be there, on the cliff road, while his men came at the dock from the only direction that mattered less.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said, materializing beside her out of the signal house's shadow. She wore no magistrate's sash today, no seal of office. Just a gray coat and a pistol grip showing at her belt like an admission. "Where you count things."

"It works."

"It works until it doesn't." Wren looked out at the water. Her hands were steady; her jaw wasn't. "I've stood at a hundred exchanges, Kavanagh. Warrants served, prisoners transferred. There is always a moment when the paper stops mattering and the men with guns remember they have them. When that moment comes—" she turned, and her eyes were very clear, "—you take Nils and go. The chest is mine to lose."

"The chest is mine."

"Was yours. Now it's evidence." But she said it without heat, almost gently, and Theo understood that Wren had already surrendered the argument somewhere in the night and was only saying the words to keep her shape. Two weeks ago Elara Wren had been the law entire. Today she was a woman in a gray coat standing on a rotten pier, and the law was nowhere to be found.

Fog lay along the water in ropes. Through it, at half past nine by Theo's watch, came the sound of engines—not the deep throb of the gunboat but the lighter slap-slap of a work launch, and then the fog tore and Pell's boat slid into view, riding low with men.

Ambrose Pell stood at the bow with Nils in front of him like a shield made of guilt. They had tied the boy's wrists and put a coat over his shoulders against the cold, which was somehow worse than brutality—the courtesy of a man who intended everything to end tidily. Nils's face was bloodied at the temple but his eyes found Theo's across the water and held them, and in them she read not terror but arithmetic. He was counting too. Counting Pell's men.

*Seven,* he mouthed, and then, barely: *cliff road empty.*

So the misdirection had held. Theo let one breath out slow.

Pell's launch bumped the dock fenders. Four men leapt ashore, rifles slung, spreading to the corners of the platform with the bored competence of hired violence. Pell himself stepped over the rail last, one hand on Nils's collar, and smiled at Theo the way he had smiled in her kitchen a lifetime ago, when he'd come to repossess her father's tools and she had lied to him about a drowned chest.

"Engineer Kavanagh." His voice carried flat across the water. "You look well. Prison agrees with some people; exile agrees with others."

"Nils first," Theo said. "He walks to me before the chest moves an inch."

"Of course." Pell pushed Nils forward. The boy stumbled up the dock planks toward her, and Theo kept her eyes on Pell because looking at Nils would make her run to him and running was death in an exchange like this. Nils reached her side. His shoulder was shaking. She put one hand briefly on his arm—*alive, alive*—and then stepped forward alone, dragging the chest by its handle, its weight singing through her wrist and shoulder.

"Ten feet," Pell said. "Set it down and step back."

She set it down. The iron bands rang on the planks. Somewhere under the harbor's smell of salt and rot she caught another smell, faint and wrong, and her stomach tightened before her mind named it: lamp oil. One of Pell's men near the dock's root had been liberal with a can. These people burned messages. She had seen the warehouse.

Pell walked forward. Up close his composure showed its seams—a twitch at the eye, sweat despite the cold. He crouched over the chest, produced a key of his own, tested the lock. And Theo watched his face do something complicated when the key turned and the lid rose and he saw that the ledgers inside were real, all of them, the whole drowned history of Vessany's murder laid out in ink.

"There it is," he said softly. "Twenty years of grief in a box." He closed the lid. "You could have been rich, you know. I offered."

"You offered me a leash."

"I offered you survival. It's the same thing, most places." He straightened, snapped his fingers, and two of his men came forward to lift the chest. "Now. The part where I'm honest with you, because you've earned honesty: there is no trade. There never was. The boy lives or dies depending on how boring the next five minutes are, and you—" his gaze moved past her, to Wren, to the signal house, cataloguing, "—you were always going to die knowing too much. Nothing personal. You're simply what happens to people who pick up things that fell out of my river."

Theo had known. Knowing and hearing it were different animals. Hearing it, her fear burned off all at once, the way fog burns off when the sun finally commits, and underneath it was something cold and clean and very old—the thing that had carried her through flooded wrecks and closed lines and every small indignity of a wasted career. Not courage exactly. Precision. The certainty that a problem has a solution if you measure correctly.

*Nine feet of dock between Pell and the oil can,* she thought. *Wren's pistol. Sefa waiting on the whistle of the *Kestrel*'s horn.*

The horn sounded.

It rolled out of the fog off the breakwater, long and brazen, and it was the signal and it was also a lie, because the *Kestrel* wasn't coming anywhere—it was making noise so that Pell's men on the dock would turn their heads, and they did, all four of them, a half-second of divided attention.

Wren fired first. Her shot took the oil-can man through the shoulder and he went down screaming into the weeds below the platform. Then the world tore open.

Rifles answered from the launch. Wood exploded off the pilings around Theo; she grabbed Nils's collar and hauled him flat behind the chest itself, iron-banded oak, the only armor on the dock. Bullets shrieked off the bands above their heads. Across the platform Pell had dropped behind a mooring bollard, shouting orders that the wind shredded, and Wren had gone to ground behind the crane's footing, returning fire with the methodical calm of a woman who had, after all, spent years around armed men and their paperwork.

"We have to move!" Nils shouted in her ear. "They'll flank the seaward stairs!"

"They won't." Theo pressed herself against the chest. "Cliff road's empty. He sent them to the wrong door. That's the whole game, Nils—that's your game, your reports, and it worked." She risked a look. One of Pell's men was down, the other three pinned between Wren's fire and the launch's own crossfire—the gunboat crew shooting badly in fog, hitting their own. Pell saw it too. She watched him arrive at the arithmetic every coward arrives at eventually: cut losses, save the asset.

"Get it to the boat!" Pell screamed. "Burn the rest! Burn *everything!*"

The oil man, bleeding, dragging his ruined shoulder, got a knee under himself and threw something—a lantern, lit, an arc of yellow flame across the gray morning. It shattered at the dock's root where the fuel had pooled, and fire stood up out of the planks like a thing summoned, roaring instantly in the wind, cutting the platform in two. Between Theo and the shore: a wall of flame. Behind her: the sea.

And the chest—two of Pell's men had it, staggering under its weight toward the launch, and the launch was backing water, oars and engine churning, because the pilot had seen the fire and wanted no part of a burning dock.

"No—" Nils said.

"They've got it," Theo said, and heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "They'll sink it or burn it or bury it in some syndicate vault and every name in those ledgers stays bought forever. My father died for those pages. Wren gave up her commission for them. Nils—" she looked at him, at his split temple, his shaking hands, "—you fed them lies for two weeks for them."

"What are you doing. Theo. *What are you doing.*"

What she was doing was measuring. Ten feet of burning dock. Water below, gray and cold, the kind of cold that stopped hearts. The launch thirty yards out and pulling away, the chest going over the side into it even now—one of Pell's men heaving it up onto the gunwale, the other bracing it—

If it went into the launch, it was gone. If it stayed on the dock, it burned. There was a third option and it was insane and it was the only one, and later she would understand that she had decided before she finished thinking, the way good engineers commit to a bridge design in their bones before the calculations catch up.

"Theo—"

"Tell Wren," Theo said, "the truth cannot be rationed."

Then she ran.

Not away from the fire—through it, along the seaward edge where the planks were wettest, flames licking at her left sleeve, heat slamming her face like an opened oven, the roar filling her skull. Someone fired at her and missed because no one sane fires at a woman running into fire. At the dock's broken end she didn't slow, didn't hesitate, didn't give the animal in her spine time to veto her. She dove.

The harbor took her like a fist.

Cold went through her body in a white shock, squeezing her chest, screaming *breathe* into lungs that must not breathe. She surfaced gasping, hair plastered to her face, and looked up in time to see the chest go over the launch's gunwale—Pell's man shoving it, done with its weight, letting the harbor do what fire hadn't.

It hit the water eight feet from her and sank, and she dove again.

Down into green murk and cold that ground against her bones. The chest fell ahead of her, bubbles streaming from its seams, sinking with the patient speed of iron-banded oak. She caught the handle. Her boots found nothing—here the harbor dropped away into black, the old dredged channel, forty feet of dark below—and the chest's momentum dragged her down with it, her ears singing, her chest cramping, the surface receding above into a ceiling of hammered silver.

*Let go,* said the animal in her spine. *Let go and live.*

Her father had let go. That was the thing she had never said aloud, the thing she had carried since she was nineteen: he had been in the water too, in a different river, with these same papers or their brothers, and he had let go—or been made to—and the syndicate had called it drowning and the courts had called it accident and Theo had called it nothing, for fifteen years, because calling it anything hurt too much.

She kicked.

Her legs answered the way they answer a person who has spent a life climbing embankments and hauling survey chains—badly, painfully, but they answered. The chest slowed. Stopped. Hung in the dark water, and Theo pulled herself up its length hand over hand, wrapped both arms through the handle, and began to kick for the light.

Her vision narrowed. The cold had passed through cold into something beyond it, a warm feeling that she knew from manuals was the last lie a drowning body tells. Above her the surface shimmered, impossibly far. She kicked. Kicked. Thought of nothing, measured nothing, was only a creature with one purpose, rising through the green dark with a dead nation's testimony locked in her arms.

Light. Air. She broke the surface coughing, and hands were on her—Sefa's hands, Sefa in the *Kestrel*'s tender, having come around the breakwater through fog on pure instinct, with Bram hauling on the painter and shouting words that weren't words yet.

They got her over the transom. They got the chest after her, streaming, saved. Theo lay on the deck planks with the harbor coming out of her in great racking coughs and someone put a blanket on her and someone else—Nils, wet-eyed, having crossed from the dock in God knew what chaos—held her head while she shook.

Behind them, the landfall dock burned, black smoke standing up into the gray sky like a pillar marking the place where Ambrose Pell's careful world had begun to come apart. Through the fog came the sound of Wren's pistol, still firing, still methodical, covering the retreat of men who no longer believed in their own plan.

"He's still on the dock," Nils said hoarsely. "Pell. The launch left him. He's cornered at the end of the pier with the fire behind him and Wren in front, and he's—he's offering her something. I could hear him shouting. Promotion. Everything they ever promised her."

Theo pushed herself up on her elbows, salt water running from her hair, her whole body one long ache, and looked back at the burning pier where the law's last honest officer stood facing the syndicate's last honest liar.

"Wren," she said. "Go. *Both of you go.* Whatever she decides—" her voice cracked, and she let it, "—she shouldn't decide it alone."

* * *

They rowed her back through falling ash.

The fire had begun to eat toward the pier's seaward end, planks glowing and dropping into the water like embers off a cigar, and at the very tip of it, framed between flame and open sea, two figures faced each other. Theo gripped the tender's gunwale and watched, helpless, as distance reduced Elara Wren and Ambrose Pell to silhouettes—to a conversation, to a gesture, to whatever bargain a desperate man offers a woman with nothing left to gain and everything left to prove.

The chest sat between Theo's feet, soaked, dented, its Vessany seal scarred but legible. Inside it: the names. All of them. Judges, ministers, the architecture of twenty stolen years—all of it hers now, hers and Wren's and the crew's, rescued from fire and harbor at the cost of nearly everything except the thing itself.

The tender grounded on the shingle below the signal house. Theo stood, swayed, stood anyway.

On the burning pier, Wren raised her arm—and it was not a pistol she held aloft.

It was handcuffs.

Theo let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for twenty years, and then Pell moved, lunging for the pier's edge, for the water, for escape—and the burning structure groaned, shifted, and began to come down beneath them both.
