## 20. Reading the Names

Bram Oduya had spent thirty years listening to machines, and he had learned that a machine in trouble always told you before it failed. A bearing sang a half-tone flat. A boiler developed a cough. The Kestrel, moored at the landfall dock with her hull patched and her superstructure scorched black along the starboard rail, was telling him everything at once.

He sat on an upturned crate in the shadow of the old terminus shed with the strongroom ledgers spread across his knees, and he listened to the ship complain, and he listened to the paper speak, and both of them said the same thing: *we are past the point where small repairs will do.*

The morning came up gray and clean over the northern sea. Beyond the end of the pier, the rails of the old Vessany line ran straight out into the water and vanished — iron disappearing into green, mile after mile of it visible at low tide, the drowned causeway like a spine beneath the skin of the harbor. Theo had stood out there at dawn and not come back for an hour. Wren had stood on the dock with her magistrate's commission folded in her pocket like a letter she couldn't decide to burn. And Pell sat in the customs lockup two miles inland, handcuffed to a pipe, guarded by three constables who kept looking at each other as if none of them were sure who had actually arrested whom.

Bram turned a page. His hands were steady. They were always steady; it was the rest of him that shook, quietly, in places no one could see.

* * *

They gathered in the terminus shed because it was the only roof left standing tall enough. Sefa had hung a lantern from a hook meant for signal lamps. Nils sat closest to the door — he always would now, Bram thought; the boy had learned what doors were for — with his bandaged wrist cradled against his chest and a mug of Nils's own terrible coffee going cold between his boots. Theo took the crate at the center without ceremony. Wren stood apart, near the great freight window, where the gray light came through and made her look like something carved rather than drawn.

On the floor between them, stacked in four careful piles, lay the evidence.

Bram had organized it himself, in the way he organized everything: by function. Deeds. Payoff schedules. Correspondence. And the fourth pile, the one he could not stop touching, the letters naming names. Ministers. Harbor commissioners. Judges. Two judges of the High Circuit — the court above Elara Wren, the court she had served for eleven years with the devotion other people gave to religion.

"You've all read them," Theo said. It wasn't a question. She looked tired in a way that went deeper than the bruise on her jaw or the salt-stiff state of her hair. "So you know what we're holding."

"Two hundred and forty-one pages," Bram said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, the number having lived in his head since the vault, ticking like a counter. "Fourteen years of continuous record. The syndicate never stopped writing things down. That's the thing I can't get past." He picked up the top sheet of the fourth pile. "They bribed a nation into dying and then they *filed it*. Cross-referenced. Indexed. There's a man — Harrow's own comptroller, initials E.V. — who initialed every payment like a proud father. They believed no one would ever read this room. That's why it's complete."

"That's why it's dangerous," Sefa said. She leaned against the wall with her arms crossed, and the lantern threw her shadow long across the deeds. "You're not holding proof, engineer. You're holding a map of every hand that ever fed at that trough. Half the governments on this coast are in there somewhere. You don't publish a thing like that. You *aim* it."

"And hit what?" Wren asked. Her voice was quiet, but it carried in the big empty shed the way a rivet gun carries through a hull. "That's the question you should be asking, Captain. Aim it at whom? Through what mechanism? Because I'll tell you what happens if we do this the proper way." She pulled a folded page from her coat — a copy, Bram knew, of the ledger sheet Theo had shown her at Corren's Landing, back when Wren still believed the courts might save them. "I have spent six days drafting filings in my head. Complaints, exhibits, chains of custody. I could write the most beautiful case in the history of coastal jurisprudence. And it would go to the High Circuit. Where Judge Amaranthe Voss sits — page ninety-one, initial E.V., eleven payments across nine years. Where Judge Corliss Hale sits — page one-thirty. The men who would rule on whether this evidence is admissible are *in* it. We would be handing the fire to the arsonists and asking them to investigate the smoke."

Nils shifted. "So we bury it again," he said. "Somewhere new. Somewhere they don't know about. Wait for—"

"Wait for what?" Theo asked, not unkindly. "A better decade?"

Nils looked down at his coffee. His brother was still in Halvatt Prison on testimony bought with syndicate money — page sixty-four, Bram remembered, a witness named Dorn paid forty crowns to swear away a smuggler's freedom. Every person in this shed was written into those pages somewhere, as victim or target or price. That was the horror of it, Bram thought. The ledgers didn't just name the guilty. They named everyone the guilty had ever touched, which was everyone.

"I've been thinking about furnaces," Bram said.

They all looked at him.

"It's what I know," he went on, and found, to his mild surprise, that the words came steadily. "Boilers. Fireboxes. You learn early that heat is honest. Put the right fuel in, open the draft, and the fire does exactly what the physics says it will do — every time, everywhere, for everyone. No favorites. No jurisdiction." He gestured at the piles on the floor. "This is fuel. Fourteen years of it, dry as tinder. The only question is the draft. Do we open it a crack — leak a page here, a name there, feed it to whichever newspaper or magistrate we judge safest — or do we open it all the way?"

"A crack is how they win," Wren said.

Everyone turned to her. She hadn't moved from the window. The light behind her had strengthened slightly; somewhere beyond the fog the sun was trying.

"I've been sitting with this since the vault," she said. "Since I stood in that strongroom and read my own superiors' names in a dead syndicate's handwriting. And I want to tell you what every instinct of my training says, so you can hear how insane it sounds coming out of me." She drew a breath. "It says: control the release. Sequence it. Give the uncorrupted courts what they can act on, protect the sources, redact the operational details, build the case brick by brick so it can't be dismissed. That is what I have believed my entire adult life. Procedure is the wall between justice and the mob. Procedure is why I—" She stopped. Her jaw worked once. "Procedure is what I *was*."

"And now?" Theo asked softly.

"Now I've read page ninety-one." Wren pushed off from the window frame and crossed to the piles, and she crouched down among the papers like a woman kneeling at a grave. "Now I know that the wall was built by the same hands it was supposed to stop. If we ration this truth — if we decide which pieces the world gets first, based on our judgment of what it can bear — then we become the next comptroller. Initials E.V. Filing the collapse of nations in triplicate." She looked up at Theo, and Bram saw something pass between them that he had been watching take shape since the narrows: not agreement exactly, but recognition. Two people arriving at the same coordinates by different rivers. "Publish everything. All of it. At once. Unredacted. Every name, every payment, every page — including the ones that embarrass us, including the ones that hurt people who don't deserve it, including whatever's in there about any of us. Print it in three languages and nail it to every customs post from here to Vessany's old border. Let the chaos come."

"That's not procedure," Sefa said. "That's a flood."

"Yes," Wren said. "I'm a magistrate proposing a flood. I'm aware." She almost smiled, and it was the saddest thing Bram had seen in weeks, and he had watched a boy get taken off this ship at gunpoint. "But think about what rationing assumes. It assumes we're smarter than the fire. It assumes we can predict which judge cracks first, which minister runs, which newspaper holds. It assumes the enemy stays passive while we meter out the damage." She shook her head. "Pell is in a cell, but Pell is one hand. The body is still attached. The moment they suspect we hold this, they move — buy the printers, disappear the witnesses, and we spend the rest of our lives leaking pages into a wind that blows them all back at us. The only defense against an institution is *simultaneity*. Hit every point of it at once, so there's nothing left standing to absorb the blow and adapt."

Bram felt it land in his chest like a hammer seating a rivet. Simultaneity. Of course. He'd have called it the same thing in engine-room terms: you didn't patch a blown seam under pressure one plate at a time. You blew the whole boiler, vented everything, and rebuilt from the bare steel.

"Theo," Sefa said. "Tell me you're not considering this. You know what a flood does. It doesn't ask permission. It takes out the good bridges along with the bad. There are people in these pages who cooperated under threat — informants, clerks, whole towns that signed what they were told to sign. Unredacted means *them too*. Their names in every gutter sheet on the coast. Some of them won't survive being named."

"I know," Theo said.

And Bram heard it — the thing underneath her voice that he had been hearing since the night on the beached hull, when she'd finally told them about her father. The man who had collected this same proof and died with it hidden, because he'd been waiting, always waiting, for the safe way, the legal way, the way that wouldn't burn anything down. Waiting had gotten him a shallow grave beside a closed line and twenty years of silence.

Theo stood. She walked to the edge of the paper piles and looked down at them for a long time, and the lantern flame moved in the draft from the freight window, and outside, faintly, the tide was turning — Bram could hear it, the long suck and shift of the water pulling back from the drowned rails.

"My father believed in the slow way," she said. "Not because he was a coward. Because he was careful, and care is a virtue right up until it's a grave. He had all of this — less than this, a fraction of this — and he sat on it for two years trying to find the court that wasn't bought, the official who wasn't fed, the channel that was clean. And every month he waited, the syndicate wrote another page. Bought another man. Another month, another entry." She crouched and picked up one sheet — a payoff schedule, Bram knew, from the weight of the paper — and held it as though she could feel the months in it. "The last entry in his notes is dated nine days before he died. It says: *found another clean one. Meeting Thursday.* There was no clean one. There never was. There was just the next man in the sequence, and the sequence was designed to outlast anyone patient enough to walk it."

She let the page fall back onto its pile.

"Wren's right. Not because chaos is good. Chaos isn't good. People will be hurt who shouldn't be, and I'll carry that, and so will all of us. But the alternative is arithmetic. We ration the truth, they ration their survival, and they have more money, more time, and more patience than grief does. The truth cannot be rationed. It's not a commodity. It's a pressure, and pressures find the weak seam eventually — the only choice we get is whether it finds it while we're still alive to stand in the doorway."

The shed was quiet. Somewhere out on the dock, gulls were working the pilings, screaming about nothing.

"So that's it," Nils said at last. His voice was rough. "We just — put it all out. Everything. My brother's file too. Page sixty-four. Everyone will know what was sworn against him and what it cost." He laughed, short and cracked. "You know what's funny? That's the first time in eight years anyone's published the truth about my family. Even if it ruins us, it's the true version."

"That's the whole argument," Wren said quietly. "In the end, it's the only one there is."

Sefa uncrossed her arms slowly. She looked at the papers, at Wren, at Theo, and Bram watched her run the pilot's calculation she ran before every hard water — depth, current, what the boat can take, what she can't. "If we do this," she said, "there's no river back. The syndicate's dead men won't care about warrants or parley flags. Every one of us becomes a name on somebody's list. You understand that, girl? You're trading your invisibility for a bonfire."

"I've been invisible my whole life," Theo said. "It kept me alive. It didn't keep anything else."

Bram closed the ledger on his knees. His hands, he noticed, were not steady anymore, and he let them not be. Thirty years he had spent keeping machines running inside other people's systems — syndicate freight lines, syndicate timetables, hauling the wealth of stolen grants over rails laid on stolen ground, and telling himself the engineering was neutral, the fire was honest, the politics belonged to someone else. Page after page in these ledgers had his old employers' initials in the margins. He had helped build the machine whose confession they now held.

"Well," he said, and his voice came out low and even, the voice he used when the gauge climbed past the red line and there was nothing left to do but hold the throttle open. "Then we'd best do it properly. If we're blowing the boiler, we blow it clean. Three printing houses, minimum — one in each nation, so no single government can suppress the plates. Copies of the full set sealed and hidden before publication, in case they burn the first press. And Wren writes the cover affidavit — her name, her former commission, her testimony that this is authentic. Not because the courts deserve it. Because a flood needs a witness."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, like a woman accepting a sentence.

Theo knelt and put her hand flat on the topmost pile — all two hundred and forty-one pages of it, fourteen years of a nation's murder, filed in triplicate by proud men who had never imagined an audience.

"Tonight," she said. "Before they learn Pell's talking. We start tonight."

Outside, the tide had fallen far enough that the old rails of the Vessany line stood bare above the water, running out toward the horizon, gleaming wet in the first real sunlight in days — pointing, as they had always pointed, at everything that came next.
