## 22. Mara's Compass

The train smelled of iron and cut grass, the way trains had smelled when Theo was small enough to ride in her father's coat pocket, and she had not known until this moment how much she had missed it.

The Vessany line ran north out of Harrowgate through country that had spent twenty years pretending it was wilderness—meadows gone to seed over old ballast, orchards grown wild and sour-sweet, a stone viaduct where swallows had colonized the arches like tenants who never signed a lease. Now the rails were new-laid on the old bed, polished bright by three months of traffic, and the stations that had stood shuttered since the fall of Vessany were opening their doors one by one, their paint still bearing the ghost-letters of a nation that no longer appeared on maps but apparently had never entirely stopped existing in the hearts of the people who lived along its spine.

Theo sat by the window with her boots up on the opposite seat, which would have scandalized every conductor of her acquaintance, and watched the land unspool.

Beside her, Elara Wren read a dispatch with the particular frown she reserved for news that was good but inconvenient.

"You're doing the thing," Theo said.

"What thing."

"The frown where you're pleased and annoyed about being pleased."

Wren folded the paper. "The Vessany provisional assembly has voted to reopen the northern terminus at Landfall permanently. They've also voted to name the station after your father."

Theo looked out the window for a while. The light came slantwise through the glass, gilding the dust motes, warming her hands where they lay in her lap. "Kavanagh Station," she said, trying the weight of it.

"Kavanagh Junction," Wren corrected. "They're very insistent about the junction. Something about honoring the man who understood that lines are only as honest as the places they connect."

"He'd have hated it," Theo said. "He'd have stood under the sign with his hat in his hands and told them to spend the money on ties and gravel." She smiled despite herself. "And then he'd have gone home and written my mother about it in his smallest, proudest handwriting."

Wren reached over and laced her fingers through Theo's, unhurried, as though they had been doing it for years instead of months—as though the whole journey from the burning pier at Landfall to this quiet morning had been nothing more than the ordinary distance between two people deciding. Her hand was warm. There was ink on her thumb; some things did not change with resignation.

"They also want you," Wren said. "For the commission overseeing the line's reconstruction. It's a real post. Salary. Title. Your name spoken in rooms where names get things done."

"No."

"I haven't asked yet."

"You were going to. You had the face on."

Wren laughed—a sound Theo was still collecting, still cataloguing, still surprised by each time, like a bird returning to a window it had once only struck against. "You can't survey other people's railways forever, Theo."

"I can survey whatever I like," Theo said. "That's rather the point of having burned down my entire life. I have nothing left to protect and therefore everything is permitted." She turned her palm up so their hands fit better. "Besides. I'm not finished with this one."

* * *

They changed engines at Drowned Station—the junction half-submerged in Theo's memory now raised on new pilings, dry-decked, lit, alive with porters and pigeons and a woman selling honey cakes off a tray. The water had been pumped from the lower galleries. The vault in the bluff had been sealed with a government stamp and a plaque so new the letters still shone: *Here the record of a stolen nation was recovered.* Theo had argued against the plaque. She had lost, gracefully.

While the engine took on water, they walked the platform, and Theo found herself stopping at the spot where, a lifetime ago, they had found the body of the man Pell had sent ahead of them. There was nothing there now but a flower seller and the smell of rain coming. A boy of perhaps ten was crouched at the platform edge, poking a stick into the gap between rail and timber, and when he saw Theo looking he held something up.

"Found this," he said. "Is it worth anything?"

It was an old track spike, rusted nearly to lace, the head stamped with the Vessany crown. Theo turned it over in her fingers. Once, a spike like this had been driven by men whose names were struck from the rolls before the mortar dried. Once, her father had died holding papers that proved what those spikes had cost.

"It's worth remembering," she said, and gave it back to him, and the boy looked at her as if she were slightly mad, which she supposed was fair.

On the way back to the carriage, Wren said quietly, "You could have kept it."

"So could he." Theo shrugged. "Things belong to the ground they're found in, mostly. I've learned to be careful about what I take out of the world." She glanced sideways. "Mostly."

"The chest?"

"The chest was different. The chest was owed."

* * *

They rode the last stretch north as the sun went low and gold, and somewhere past the third tunnel Theo fell asleep against Wren's shoulder for the first time in her life without meaning to, without bracing, without the old habit of keeping one ear cocked for footsteps behind her. She woke as the sea smell came through the open window—salt and kelp and creosote—and found Wren awake beneath her, still as a fencepost, watching the coast come into view with an expression Theo decided not to tease her about.

Because there it was. The landfall.

The old northern terminus rose out of the evening haze exactly as it always had: the great arched shed built for a traffic that never came, the turntable green with salt-spray moss, and beyond it the rails themselves, running straight and true into the surf, ending nowhere, ending in the sea. For twenty years it had been a monument to theft abandoned. Now cranes worked along the quay. Scaffolding climbed the shed walls. A ferry was tying up at the new pier, and even at this distance Theo could hear the hammers, the shouts, the particular music of a place being brought back from the dead.

And at the berth nearest the breakwater, patched and repainted and somehow still looking like a barge that had lost an argument with a cathedral, sat the *Kestrel*.

Sefa Ilo stood at her rail with her arms crossed, watching the train come in, and even across the water Theo could see her grinning.

* * *

Nils met them at the gangway with his brother beside him—taller than Nils, quieter, with the careful way of moving that men learn in prisons and never fully unlearn. He shook Theo's hand with both of his and said nothing, which was its own kind of speech. Nils talked enough for both of them, mostly about the ship, partly about the food, occasionally about a girl in the Vessany customs office whom he described in terms that made Sefa throw a rag at his head.

Bram Oduya arrived last, down the gangway with a leather satchel and ink on all ten fingers, and embraced Theo like a sister and Wren like a colleague and then immediately began talking about the printing.

"Three nations," he said, walking them toward the deckhouse, gesturing with the satchel. "Do you understand what happened? We published everything at once, unredacted, exactly as Wren proposed, and the world did not end. It *bent*. Ministers resigned in four capitals. Two judges fled. The Vessany assembly seated delegates from villages that hadn't been represented since before you were born. And it started here—" he tapped the deckhouse table, where the compass sat in its case among charts and tea mugs—"with a chest pulled out of a river by a woman everyone had already counted out."

"We were lucky," Theo said.

"We were *right*," Bram said. "Luck is what happens afterward, when people decide you were right."

The compass sat where it had sat for months now, ever since the salvage, ever since the harbor, ever since the night Theo had carried it dripping up the beach at Landfall with the chest's contents wrapped in oilskin against her heart. It was a beautiful instrument—Vessany work, brass and mother-of-pearl, the cardinal points chased in a script no living scholar had fully translated. And it did not point north. It never had. It swung lazily, settled, drifted again, as if following some current beneath the world, and Bram had given up on it and Sefa had named it Mara—for the drowned girl in the old ballad who walked the seafloor pointing sailors home—and Theo had privately begun to suspect it was simply broken in the most interesting possible way.

She opened the case now, out of habit, and watched the needle swing.

Southwest. Toward the open water. Toward the *Kestrel*'s idle engines and the long blue distance beyond the breakwater.

"Huh," she said.

"What?" Wren leaned in close, her shoulder warm against Theo's.

"It's pointing at the ship."

"It points at everything eventually. It pointed at the pie cart in Harrowgate for two days."

"That was Nils standing near the pie cart."

"Nils stands near most things," Wren conceded.

But Theo kept looking at the needle, and the needle kept looking southwest, steady now, patient, like a dog waiting at a door.

* * *

They ate on deck—Nils's cooking, which had improved from survival rations to something approaching cuisine, though he still salted everything as though the sea might take offense otherwise. The sun went down behind the terminus shed and turned the crane cables to harp strings. Somewhere in the town a band was playing for the reopening, and the music came across the water thin and bright.

Sefa poured tea that was mostly rum and said, "So. Charter season opens next week. I've had offers. Coastal mail run. Salvage survey off the shoals. Some fool wants to go look for the Vessany treasure fleet, which does not exist, and will pay handsomely to not find it." She looked around the circle of faces. "I need to know who's aboard."

"I'm aboard," Nils said instantly. "Both of us." His brother nodded.

"Bram?"

"I have a press to run," Bram said. "But presses travel badly. And there are stories out there that haven't been printed yet." He grinned. "Aboard."

All eyes came around to Theo and Wren.

Theo looked at Wren. Wren looked back, and in the lamplight her face was open in a way it had never been in the customs house, or the river port, or the vault, or the collapsing pier—all those places where duty had stood between her and the world like glass. The glass was gone now. What remained was just a woman on a ship's deck, waiting to hear an answer she already knew.

"I resigned a magistracy," Wren said mildly, "for this crew. It would be embarrassing to resign it for nothing."

"You didn't resign it for us," Theo said. "You resigned it because the law you served had stopped deserving you. That's different."

"Yes," Wren agreed. "And then I stayed anyway. Which tells you what the difference amounts to."

Theo laughed, and the laugh felt strange and easy in her chest, unarmored. She thought of her father's small proud handwriting. She thought of the spike in the boy's hands at Drowned Station. She thought of the chest sinking into the harbor and herself diving after it, the cold closing over her, and the single fierce clarity of that moment: *this matters, I matter, get it done.*

She had spent her whole life believing her talent was a debt she owed to people who owned her. She had spent these last months learning it was simply hers—like her hands, like her name, like the woman sitting beside her.

"Aboard," Theo said.

Sefa raised her mug. "Then it's settled. First charter leaves with the morning tide."

"And where are we chartered to?" Nils asked, mouth full.

Sefa smiled slowly and nodded at the compass in its case, where the needle had not moved all evening, holding its line to the southwest with the patience of something that knew exactly where it was going.

"Wherever that says," she said. "It hasn't been wrong yet."

* * *

Late, when the others had gone below and the deck was silvered with moonrise, Theo stood at the stern rail with the compass open in her hands. The lights of Landfall glittered astern. Ahead lay black water and the first faint stars reflected in it, and the needle trembling between them, faithful to neither, faithful to something else entirely.

Wren came to stand beside her, and Theo tilted the instrument so they could both see.

"Do you think it actually works?" Wren asked. "Or do we just keep finding things because we go looking?"

"Does it matter?" Theo said. "My father used to say a compass doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you whether you're still yourself while you're getting there." She closed the lid gently, the way you close a book you intend to open again tomorrow. "North is just a direction. This—" she touched the brass, warm from her hands, "—this is a promise."

The tide turned. The *Kestrel* shifted at her moorings, restless, ready, and far out past the breakwater the open sea waited, wide and dark and full of everything that needed finding.

Theo took Wren's hand, and together they watched the needle settle, and settle true.
