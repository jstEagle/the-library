## 1. The Debt Collector's Umbrella

The rain in Port Sable didn't fall so much as arrive, like it had somewhere better to be and was only passing through. Zephyrine Winterbourne stood under the rusted awning of the fuel depot and watched it hammer the harbor into gray lace, and he thought—not for the first time—that a man could drown standing still if the water came at him sideways enough.

He'd been counting bolts on the dock rail when the black car pulled up.

You learned to know them by their stillness. Everyone else's vehicle in Port Sable had a rattle, a lean, a personality worn into it like a scar. The Syndicate's cars were silent and level as held breath. This one glided to the curb without disturbing a puddle, and the wipers kept time like a metronome in an empty church.

Zephyrine didn't run. Running from the Meridian Syndicate was a category error, like running from weather. You could only choose where you were standing when it arrived.

The man who stepped out opened a long black umbrella before his shoes touched the wet pavement—opened it with a flick of the wrist, smooth as a card trick—and crossed the lot at an unhurried walk. He was dry when he reached the awning. That was the whole point of him, Zephyrine supposed. A man who never got wet no matter how hard it rained.

"Mr. Winterbourne." The enforcer's name was Corvane. Zephyrine had never learned his first name. He suspected Corvane had paid money to have it removed from records, the way you'd pay to have a tooth pulled. "You're difficult to find."

"I'm easy to find," Zephyrine said. "I'm just expensive to visit."

Corvane smiled the way a ledger balances—precisely, without warmth. He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, with the soft hands of a man who had never hauled a line or turned a wrench but knew exactly what both cost. His suit was charcoal. His umbrella was black silk over steel ribs. Everything about him said: *I am the polite part of something that is not polite.*

"You've been driving," Corvane observed, tilting his head toward the lot, where Zephyrine's truck sat under its tarp—a nineteen-year-old flatbed with a winch that worked on prayer and a cab that smelled of diesel and old coffee. "Fourteen thousand kilometers since spring, by our estimate. You move around."

"I work around."

"Mm." Corvane looked out at the harbor. Trawlers knocked against each other in the swell. Somewhere down the quay a crane groaned through its arc, lifting crates that nobody would admit contained anything interesting. "Do you know what I admire about you, Mr. Winterbourne?"

"I can't imagine."

"Your arithmetic." Corvane turned back to him. "Most debtors live in denial. They round down. They tell themselves next season, next contract, next lucky break. But you—" he tapped the umbrella's handle once, a small precise gesture, "—you keep perfect books. You know to the kilometer what you owe us. It's almost a virtue."

It wasn't a compliment. Zephyrine understood that. In eleven years of owing the Syndicate money, he had learned that everything Corvane said was a tool laid neatly on a table, waiting to be picked up and used.

The debt had started, as these things do, with a catastrophe wearing the mask of an opportunity. A salvage contract on a flooded freighter, financed by Meridian credit because no honest bank would touch a job that far upriver in storm season. The freighter had been there, all right. So had the flood. Zephyrine had come back with a damaged boat, two dead crewmates, and a contract that converted his loss into principal and his grief into interest. Eleven years later, the number had grown the way mold grows in a wet wall—quietly, structurally, everywhere at once.

He owed them one hundred and ninety thousand. He knew it to the digit. Corvane was right about that much. It was the only thing Zephyrine had ever finished.

"I'm not here to collect today," Corvane said.

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "You're here to remind me you *could*."

"That too." Corvane produced a folded paper from inside his coat—dry, of course, everything about him dry—and held it out. Zephyrine took it. It was a statement, printed on heavy stock, the Syndicate's seal embossed at the top like a wax stamp on a tomb. The number sat in the middle of the page, patient as a stone.

"Thirty days," Corvane said. "Full settlement or full collateral. Those are the terms now. The board has stopped finding your... mobility charming."

"Collateral meaning the boat."

"Collateral meaning everything with your name attached to it. The boat. The truck. The salvage licenses. And should those prove insufficient"—Corvane's smile deepened by a millimeter, which on his face was the equivalent of another man's open laughter—"meaning you. There are contracts available for men with your skills, Mr. Winterbourne. Upriver work. Long hours. Short life expectancy. The Syndicate is generous with the dead. We forgive their debts entirely."

The rain thickened against the awning, drumming like fingers on a desk. Down the quay, a gull screamed at nothing and got nothing back.

Zephyrine folded the statement once and put it in his jacket, inside the waterproof liner, because some documents you keep even when they're knives. Especially then.

"Thirty days," he said.

"Twenty-nine, technically. Today counts." Corvane checked a watch that probably cost more than Zephyrine's boat engine, then seemed to remember himself and offered a small courteous nod. "I'll confess something, off the record. I've read your file. All of it. The Kalyani contract. The flood twelve years ago. Two names on the casualty list." He paused, letting the silence do its work, the way water finds the crack in a wall. "Mara Voss. Dev Anand."

Zephyrine's hands went very still in his pockets. Outside, the rain kept falling on a city that had learned not to notice.

"You say their names like you own them," Zephyrine said quietly.

"I apologize. That wasn't my intent." And the terrible thing was that Corvane sounded sincere—he always did; sincerity was just another tool in the kit, kept oiled. "My intent was this: men who carry dead crewmates tend to make irrational decisions near rivers. The board worries. I told them you were rational. I told them you'd pay, or you'd sign, or you'd disappear somewhere we'd never bother looking." He opened the umbrella again, unhurried. "Prove me right, Mr. Winterbourne. It's the last favor I can afford you."

He walked back to the car through the downpour without a drop touching him, and the car slid away into the gray, and the harbor went on knocking its boats together like slow applause.

* * *

That night Zephyrine sat in the cab of his truck above the breakwater and did what he always did, which was nothing, magnificently.

He had a flask of coffee gone cold and a map book open on the passenger seat, though he wasn't reading it. He was doing the other thing, the private thing, the thing he'd never said aloud to anyone: counting miles.

Eleven years. Four hundred and six thousand kilometers, give or take, since the flood took Mara and Dev and the river took everything else. He knew because he'd kept the logbooks, every one, stacked in a locker on the boat like a man keeping receipts for a war. He measured his life in kilometers driven and liters burned and loads hauled, because miles couldn't die. Miles didn't have laugh lines or a way of humming when she thought no one was listening. Miles didn't stand on the bow in the rain with her arms out like she was greeting an old friend.

*Twelve years of miles,* he thought, and corrected himself—eleven years of debt, twelve years of miles, the numbers overlapping like tide lines on a piling—and drank the cold coffee anyway, because waste was for people with futures.

Port Sable glittered below him, all sodium lights and wet stone. Somewhere out in the dark, past the breakwater, the sea was deciding what kind of season it intended to have. The radio on the seat beside him murmured its late-night litany: shipping advisories, a fishing fleet overdue, and then—the item he'd half expected, the way you half expect thunder after heat—the words *monsoon formation* spoken in a calm announcer's voice, four hundred kilometers offshore, organizing itself.

Landfall projections: three weeks, possibly less.

Zephyrine turned the radio off. Three weeks. Thirty days. The two clocks were going to race, and he already knew neither of them cared whether he won.

He was reaching to start the engine when the headlights found him.

Not the Syndicate's stillness this time. These lights swung wild across the breakwater gravel, belonging to a mud-splattered sedan that took the turn too fast and braked too late, and a woman got out of it before it fully stopped. She crossed the gravel in long strides, coat collar up, hair dark with rain, and even before she stepped into the dome light Zephyrine knew the walk. He'd spent his whole career losing races to that walk.

Odile Vasseur stopped beside the truck window and looked in at him, rain running off her chin, an expression on her face that was equal parts apology and ambush.

"Hello, Zephyrine," she said. "I hear you're free in thirty days."

She held up a folded contract between two fingers, dry under her coat, and smiled like a woman holding the only match in a drowning room.

"I need a river pilot," she said. "And I need you to drive me somewhere everyone told you never to go again."
