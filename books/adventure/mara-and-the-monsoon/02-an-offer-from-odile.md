## 2. An Offer from Odile

The rain hadn't stopped in nine days, and Zephyrine Winterbourne had stopped pretending it would.

He sat in the back booth of the Salt Lantern, a dockside bar that smelled of diesel and fried fish and the particular despair of men who measured their lives in fuel receipts. His notebook lay open on the table—thirty columns, one for each day the enforcer had given him. He'd filled in eleven. In each column, a number: miles he could drive, miles he could run, miles of river he could cover if he sold everything he owned and still came up short.

It was arithmetic as prayer. It never added up to salvation.

The bar door opened at nine, letting in a slab of wet lamplight and a woman who walked through it like she owned the deed to the building and was considering foreclosure. Zephyrine knew her before he saw her face. He knew her by the stride, by the way the room's noise bent around her, by the old reflex in his chest that said *rival* before it said anything else.

Odile Vasseur. Twelve years since he'd seen her, and she looked exactly the way she had on the last day he'd wanted to look at her: dry when everyone else was drowning, composed when everyone else was screaming, and carrying something under her arm that she believed mattered more than anyone else's life.

She crossed the room without hesitation, found his booth by instinct or by research—he suspected research—and sat down across from him without asking. She set a leather document case on the table between them, square and heavy as a headstone.

"Zephyrine." His name in her mouth sounded like an invoice.

"Odile." Hers in his sounded like a warning he'd ignored once already.

"You look terrible."

"I've been told I'm aging into my debts." He closed the notebook. "Whatever you're selling, I can't afford it. That's not self-pity. It's accounting."

"I'm not selling." She signaled the barman with two raised fingers, ordered tea neither of them would drink, and waited until the man retreated. Then she leaned forward, and for the first time Zephyrine saw something underneath the polish—not softness, nothing so useful as softness, but urgency worn thin enough to show its weave. "I'm hiring."

He laughed. It came out wrong, rusty. "You know what I owe. You know who holds the paper. If the Meridian Syndicate found out I took a contract they didn't sanction, they'd call it flight. They'd be right."

"The Syndicate knows I'm here." She said it flatly, watching him. "They let me walk into this room. Ask yourself why."

He did ask himself, and he didn't like any of the answers, so he said, "Why?"

"Because they think you're broken. A man with thirty days and no options doesn't frighten anyone." She tapped the document case once, lightly, the way you'd tap a sleeping animal. "I intend to prove them wrong about both of us."

* * *

Here is what Zephyrine remembered about Odile Vasseur, from the years when remembering was still something he allowed himself:

He remembered her arriving at the Kalyani yards with a surveyor's license and a reputation, twenty-four years old and certain the river was a problem to be solved. He remembered arguing with her over channel markers while Mara laughed at them both from the wheelhouse, saying they fought like siblings and sulked like lovers and neither of them would ever admit which was worse. He remembered Odile's maps—beautiful, precise, arrogant—and how Dev used to trace the upper river on them with one finger and say, *she draws it like she owns it.*

And he remembered the last time he saw her. Three days after the flood. Three days after the water took Mara and Dev and the town and the twelve years of Zephyrine's life that had come before it. Odile had stood on the levee with rain streaming off her chin, and she'd said, "This wasn't weather," and Zephyrine had told her that if she said another word he would put her in the river himself, and she had looked at him with something he'd spent twelve years refusing to name, and then she'd gone away.

He had built a whole life out of that going-away. Miles and miles of it.

* * *

"Say the job," he said finally. "Just say it. Don't dress it up."

She didn't dress it up. That was the thing about Odile; whatever else she was, she had never once lied to him about the shape of a thing, only about its weight.

"Upriver," she said. "All the way. To the source of the Kalyani."

The words landed in the booth between them and sat there like a third passenger.

"No," Zephyrine said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"There are no terms. The upper Kalyani is closed. Not Syndicate-closed—river-closed. The settlements above Serpent Bend don't take traffic. The gorges flood without warning. And past all of it—" He stopped. Swallowed the rest, which tasted like silt and monsoon.

"Past all of it is where your crew swore off," Odile finished quietly. "Yes. I know. I was there when they swore it, or near enough. I also know that oath has kept you alive for twelve years, and it has kept you small for twelve years, and I am offering you the first chance you'll ever have to be paid for breaking it."

The tea arrived. Neither of them touched it. The rain worked at the windows like it was being paid by the hour.

"How far up?" he asked, hating himself for asking.

"To the basin. The source gauges at the old station."

"That station's sealed. Has been since construction. The Syndicate keeps it locked tighter than their vaults, and their private security patrols the reservoir road year-round. You'd need more than a boat. You'd need a miracle with a hull."

"I need a pilot who knows the river better than the people trying to stop us." She slid the document case across the table. "Open it."

He didn't move. "What's in it?"

"A contract. Payment sufficient to clear your debt twice over, with the balance held against completion. A crew manifest—I've hired three so far, you'd bring your own besides. Equipment lists. Charts." A pause, fractional, the only seam in her composure. "And a letter of intent explaining what we're doing up there, which I will show you when I trust you, and not one hour before."

"So I'd be flying blind into forbidden water for a woman who won't tell me why."

"Yes."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I hire someone worse than you, and you spend your last thirty days running, and the Syndicate spends them catching you." She picked up her tea, drank it like it was a formality she was humoring, set it down. "I'd rather it was you, Zephyrine. That's the truth. Not sentiment—truth. There are maybe four pilots alive who could run the upper Kalyani before landfall, and three of them are dead or bought. You're the fourth, and you're the only one I've never been able to predict, which makes you the only one worth having."

He looked at the case. It sat there, patient, heavier than leather and paper had any right to be.

"What's the deadline?"

"Landfall. The monsoon reaches the coast in nineteen days, hits the high valley four days after that. We go up ahead of the water or we don't go at all."

"Nineteen days." He almost smiled. "You always did schedule grief tightly."

"I schedule everything tightly. Grief included." Her eyes flicked to his notebook, to the thirty columns of doomed arithmetic, and back. "How many days do you have left, Zephyrine? Nineteen? Fewer?"

He said nothing. The silence answered for him, and they both heard it.

* * *

He made her leave the case and go, because he needed to think and he couldn't think with her watching him do it. She went without argument—which unsettled him more than argument would have—and paused at the door long enough to say, over her shoulder:

"For what it's worth, I never believed it was weather either. I just had the sense not to say it to your face three days after." Then the door swallowed her, and the rain took her, and Zephyrine sat alone with a contract he hadn't signed and a memory he'd never managed to drown.

He opened the case.

The contract was clean, professional, brutal in its clarity. Payment figures that made his hands go unsteady on the paper—enough to erase the debt, enough to start over, enough to stop measuring his life in miles. The manifest listed names he half-knew. The charts were Odile's own work, updated, annotated in her narrow hand, and he caught himself tracing the upper river the way Dev used to, one finger, slow, past Serpent Bend, past the drowned channels, up into the gorge country where the river narrowed and the bells hung and the water ran white over stone that had killed before.

Where it had killed *her*.

Mara died eleven miles below the source. He knew the number the way he knew his own pulse. He had sworn, on a levee in the rain, with Dev's brother holding his shoulder and the town burning its dead behind them, that he would never run that water again. And the crew had sworn it with him—all of them, every hand that survived, a vow passed around like a lantern: *never above the Reach, never again.* The ones who kept the oath scattered. The ones who broke it weren't around to say what it cost.

And now here was Odile Vasseur, twelve years later, offering him money to break it, and the worst part—the part that made him press his palms flat against the table like a man steadying a deck in rough water—was that some sealed room inside him had already said yes.

Not for the money. He examined that honestly, because lying to yourself was the first thing the river taught you not to do. Not entirely for the money. Underneath the debt and the deadline and the arithmetic, there was a simpler engine turning: *she said this wasn't weather.* Odile had said it on the levee, and he had threatened her for it, and he had spent twelve years driving away from the sentence instead of toward it. Because if it wasn't weather—if the flood that took Mara and Dev and his hometown had a cause, and the cause had a name—then his grief had an address. And a man can't run from an address forever. Sooner or later he has to knock.

He thought about the enforcer's umbrella, tapping the wet pavement. Thirty days. Nineteen now, effectively, if Odile's timeline held. The Syndicate thought he was broken. Maybe he was. But broken things float, if you seal the right seams, and the Kalyani had been trying to teach him that lesson his entire life.

He took out his pen. He signed the contract on the line, in the rain-noise and the fish-smell and the ghost of Mara laughing in a wheelhouse twelve years gone, and the moment the ink dried he felt the strange double sensation he'd felt only twice before in his life: terror and relief, arriving together, indistinguishable, like twin engines.

Then he turned to the crew manifest, looking for names he could trust with his life and hers, and found the page nearly empty—three strangers and a blank space where the rest should be—and understood that the hardest part of this job wouldn't be the river at all.

It would be finding out who, given the choice, would choose to come along.

He had nineteen days, a signed contract, and a boat with a cracked manifold sitting in a flooded slip across town.

Time to find a crew.
