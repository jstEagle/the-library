## 3. Crew of Strays

Tomas Reyes had a rule about harbors: never sleep anywhere you can't see the water. He'd broken it exactly once in his life, in a boarding house above the fish market at Puerto Sombra, and he'd woken to the smell of smoke and lost everything he owned except the charts he'd carried out the window under one arm. So when Zephyrine Winterbourne came looking for him, Tomas was where Tomas always was—on the deck of his own boat, a forty-year-old survey skiff named *Half Measure*, with a kettle on a camp stove and the tide tables spread out like scripture.

"You look terrible," Zephyrine said from the dock.

"I've been told I have a face for radio."

"Your radio's been dead since the storm season." Zephyrine crouched, forearms on knees, that way he had of making himself smaller than he was, which was a trick, because Zephyrine Winterbourne was not a small man and had never once made himself small for anyone's comfort. "I need a navigator."

"No."

"I haven't said where yet."

"You don't have to." Tomas poured a second cup of tea and held it up without looking at him. "You've got that look. The one you had before the Kalyani run. Before—" He stopped himself. Twelve years of practice stopping himself. "You've got the look."

Zephyrine took the cup anyway. He drank it like it was an argument he'd already won.

"The upper Kalyani," he said. "Source to sea. Before landfall."

Tomas set down the kettle very carefully, because his hands wanted to do something else with it. "The upper Kalyani is a graveyard with a current."

"It's a river."

"It's a river that ate Mara and Dev, and you know it, and I know it, and every pilot between here and the headwaters knows it. They call the gorge past Serpent Bend the Widow's Teeth. There are settlements up there that shoot first and ask questions with the second shot. And you want to run it before monsoon landfall, which means running it *into* monsoon landfall." He finally looked up. "Who's paying?"

"Odile Vasseur."

Tomas laughed, short and ugly. "Of course she is. The only person alive who ever beat you to a story and never let you forget it. What does she want up there?"

"She didn't say."

"And you signed on anyway."

Zephyrine said nothing, which was answer enough. That was the thing about Zephyrine that Tomas had never decided whether to admire or mourn: he didn't need reasons, he needed momentum. Give him a heading and he'd fill in the why later, or never. It made him the best river man Tomas had ever crewed with and the worst kind of friend, because friends needed whys.

"I owe you," Tomas said. "You know I owe you. When my license got pulled after the inquiry, you paid the reinstatement fee out of your own pocket and never mentioned it once in six years, which is its own kind of debt, honestly, because at least if you'd mentioned it I could've resented you properly and moved on." He rubbed his face. "But the upper Kalyani—"

"Mara's Reach is on the route," Zephyrine said quietly. "I'm not going around it this time."

The kettle hissed. Somewhere across the harbor a cargo crane groaned through its morning shift, and the water slapped the hull of the *Half Measure* in that patient rhythm that had been the only constant sound of Tomas's adult life.

"You're going back for her," Tomas said.

"I'm going back for the money. Whatever else happens happens." Zephyrine stood, joints cracking, and looked out at the gray water like it owed him something. "Contract pays enough to clear me and enough to clear you. Odile's good for it—I checked. For once in her life, she's actually good for it."

"How much?"

"Enough."

"That's not a number, Zeph."

"It's the only number that matters." He turned back, and for a half second—the length of a wave against a hull—Tomas saw what was underneath all that routine dread. Not greed. Fear. The specific fear of a man who has calculated exactly how many days he has left and found the arithmetic wanting. "Thirty days, Tomas. I've got thirty days before people I don't want to talk about come collect things I don't want to describe. I need a navigator who knows the Kalyani better than the Syndicate's own charts. There's exactly one of those, and he's sitting on a boat drinking tea and pretending he isn't already saying yes."

Tomas looked at his tide tables. He looked at his dead radio. He looked at the chart tube in the corner, the one he hadn't opened in twelve years, the one with the hand-annotated surveys of the upper river that no living pilot had bettered, because the last two men who'd tried to better them were dead.

"One condition," Tomas said. "We run Mara's Reach in daylight. Full sun, full visibility, or we tie off and wait. I don't care what the schedule says."

"Done."

"And you tell me everything Odile's carrying before we pass Serpent Bend. Everything. I'm not navigating blind into a toll post for a woman who won't show me her manifest."

Zephyrine smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from before, crooked and unrepentant. "Knew you'd say yes."

"I said yes to the conditions. The yes is provisional."

"Provisional yeses are still yeses." Zephyrine drained the tea and set the cup down on the tide tables, directly over the numbers for next Tuesday, as if to claim them. "Boat launch is the *Corvina*, berth fourteen. Priya Anand's already aboard. We sail when she says the engines are ready, and you know what that means."

"It means we sail late," Tomas said.

"It means we sail right."

* * *

Priya Anand was under the engine room floor when Tomas found her, which was where Priya Anand always was, physically and spiritually. Only her boots stuck out from beneath the port main, and there was a wrench in one hand moving with the slow confidence of someone who talked to machines and expected answers.

"Tomas," she said, without emerging. She'd recognized his footsteps. Of course she had. Priya recognized everyone by their footsteps; she claimed it was a habit from working engine rooms, but Tomas suspected it went back further than that, back to whatever she'd left behind in the delta country that she never talked about and that nobody asked about, because some exiles carry their histories the way other people carry knives.

"Priya."

"He offered you triple rates and a sob story, didn't he."

"He offered me tea and guilt. The rates were implied."

She slid out from under the engine, sat up, wiped her hands on a rag that had stopped being a rag sometime last year. Her hair was tied back with electrical wire, actual stripped electrical wire, and there was grease on her cheekbone like war paint. "Triple rates," she repeated. "And the route?"

"Upper Kalyani. Source to sea. Pre-landfall."

Priya was quiet for a moment. Around them the *Corvina*'s engine room ticked and settled, that great iron heartbeat, and Tomas watched her listen to it the way other people listened to music.

"They'll say no," she said finally. "Every engineer on this coast will say no. The fuel systems alone—the upper river runs hot silt in flood season, it eats impellers, it clogs intakes, and there's no yard past Kilometer Two Hundred that can machine a replacement. You break down up there, you're building parts out of scrap and prayer."

"So you're saying no."

"I'm saying I already said yes." She stood, joints popping, and looked at him with those flat dark eyes that missed nothing. "I've been saying yes since yesterday. I just wanted to hear how he pitched it to you so I'd know how bad it really is."

"How bad is it?"

"Worse than he told you." She tossed him the rag. "There's a Syndicate survey launch been sitting off the breakwater since dawn. Same hull as the ones that followed me out of the delta three years ago. Somebody's watching this berth, Tomas. Either they're watching Odile Vasseur, or they're watching us, and I genuinely don't know which option I hate more."

Tomas felt the old cold settle into his stomach, the one that lived there permanently now, the navigator's cold, the knowledge that every line you drew on a chart was a promise you might not keep. "Did you tell Zeph?"

"He told me not to. Said if they're watching, let them watch; he wants them to think we're slow." She shrugged. "It's his funeral. Ours too, technically, but he's paying triple, so."

They climbed up into the daylight together. The *Corvina* was a converted patrol boat, sixty feet of scarred steel with a shallow draft and an absurdly reinforced bow, the kind of vessel that looked like it had opinions about other boats. On the foredeck, coiling lines with the frantic energy of someone who'd been awake since four, was a kid.

"That's Wren," Priya said. "Deckhand. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Found him scrubbing barnacles off a charter hull at the fish quay for food money. Zeph hired him on the spot."

"Why?"

"Because the kid quoted him the draft of this boat from memory before Zeph told him what it was." Priya's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Also because he's got nowhere else to be. Like the rest of us."

Wren saw them coming and straightened up fast, trying to look professional, which mostly succeeded in making him look like a heron that had learned to coil rope. Up close he was all elbows and salt-cracked knuckles, hair chopped short with what appeared to have been a knife, eyes the color of shallow water over sand.

"You're the navigator," he said. It wasn't a question. "Tomas Reyes. You surveyed the Kalyani gorge system twice. Your charts are the ones everybody copies, except the copies are wrong because you corrected them after and never published."

Tomas blinked. "Where'd you read that?"

"Harbor archive. I read everything they'll let me in for free." The kid shifted, suddenly defensive, braced for mockery the way some people brace for weather. "Is it true? About the corrections?"

"Some of it." Tomas studied him. Underneath the bravado and the hunger there was something Tomas recognized with uncomfortable clarity: the particular loneliness of a person who had taught themselves the world because the world hadn't volunteered to teach them. "You want to see real charts sometime, come find me. Not copies. The originals."

Wren's whole face changed, and then, visibly, he forced it to change back, because sixteen-year-olds would rather drown than look eager. "Sure," he said, too casually. "If I've got time."

"You'll have time. You're crew."

"Provisional crew," Priya said. "Everyone's provisional until we pass Serpent Bend."

"Everything's provisional," Tomas agreed. "That's seamanship."

* * *

They signed the articles that evening in the *Corvina*'s cramped saloon, four names on a single page, while rain started against the portholes—the first real rain of the season, soft and tentative, like the sky clearing its throat. Odile Vasseur watched from the corner with a glass of wine she wasn't drinking, and Tomas watched her watch them, cataloguing. She had the stillness of a woman used to being underestimated, and the eyes of a woman who did the underestimating herself, privately, constantly, and was rarely wrong.

Zephyrine read the contract terms aloud. Triple standard rates, payment on return, hazard clauses that were frankly insulting given the route, and a confidentiality provision broad enough to hide a body in.

"What's the cargo?" Tomas asked, pen hovering.

"Survey equipment," Odile said.

"For what survey?"

"Hydrological." She smiled without warmth. "The source gauges at the Kalyani headwaters haven't been read in eleven years. I have a professional interest in reading them."

"Nobody has a professional interest in the source," Tomas said. "The source is a legend and a warning sign. What's really in the hold?"

For a moment—one held breath of a moment—he thought she wouldn't answer. Then Zephyrine said, mildly, "Tomas's condition. Everything before Serpent Bend. This counts as before."

Odile looked at Zephyrine. Something passed between them, old and complicated, the residue of whatever history they shared, and Tomas understood that these two had hurt each other in ways that predated all of them.

"Documents," Odile said. "Sealed cases. You'll know more when it's time for you to know more, and I give you my word that time comes before Serpent Bend. Beyond that—" she lifted the wine at last, a small toast to the room, "—you have my word that no one on this crew is being paid to do anything they'd be ashamed of. Whether you believe my word is your business."

"It's a terrible contract," Priya said. "The hazard clauses are insulting."

"They're negotiable," Odile said.

"Then we negotiate tomorrow. Tonight I'm signing, because if I sleep on it I'll wake up smart." Priya signed with a slash of the pen that nearly tore the page.

Wren signed his name in careful block letters, pressing so hard the paper dented, like he was carving it rather than writing it. Then he looked up. "Can I ask something? Nobody's said it yet, so somebody has to." His voice cracked slightly and he pushed through it. "The upper Kalyani. Everyone in the harbor talks about it like it's cursed. Is it true? About what happened twelve years ago? Because I've heard six versions and none of them match."

The saloon went very quiet. Rain filled the silence, ticking against the glass.

"Yes," Zephyrine said. "It's true. All of it, probably, depending which version you heard." He rolled up the contract, businesslike, but his voice had gone somewhere flat and far away. "Two of my crew died in the flood of the Reach. Mara Okonjo and Dev Raghunathan. Good people. Better than good. The river took them in under four minutes and gave back one of them three days later, thirty miles downstream, and kept the other." He met Wren's eyes, and didn't soften it, because Zephyrine Winterbourne had never once softened anything in his life. "You should know that before you sign. Not the rumor. The fact. We're sailing into the place where it happened, and I can't promise you it won't happen again."

Wren swallowed. Tomas watched the kid do the math—not the money math, the other math, the one where you weigh your life against your options and find the options thin. Every stray on this boat had done that same sum. That was what a crew of strays was: people whose alternatives had all expired.

"Okay," Wren said, and signed again, harder, as if the first signature hadn't taken.

* * *

Tomas couldn't sleep. He ended up in the wheelhouse at midnight with a lamp and the chart tube, and he opened it for the first time in twelve years.

The maps smelled like mildew and old ink. His own handwriting marched up the river's spine in careful annotations: depths, hazards, the shifting gravel bars, the places where the banks had collapsed into the channel. And there, at the bend marked in red—MARA'S REACH, in his own hand, underlined twice—a note he'd written the week after the funerals and never crossed out:

*Not a navigation error. Not pilot error. The river changed and we didn't know. Nothing we could have done.*

He'd written it to survive. He'd never quite believed it.

Footsteps on the companionway. Zephyrine, filling the doorway, looking at the open chart like a man looking at an old wound he'd convinced himself had healed.

"You kept them," Zephyrine said.

"Kept everything. Threw out nothing. It's a disease." Tomas smoothed the map flat. "Zeph. If we're doing this—if we're really going back up there—I need to re-survey as we go. Twelve years of floods, the whole channel's moved. My charts are a ghost map. Half of what killed Mara and Dev was that the river wasn't where our charts said it was."

"So we make new charts."

"So we make new charts. Slow, careful, sounding lines every kilometer. Which means we leave early and we push hard, because landfall isn't waiting for us to be thorough." He looked up. "And there's one more thing. Priya says there's a Syndicate launch on the breakwater. Has been since dawn."

Something flickered across Zephyrine's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "I know."

"You *know*?"

"I've known for two days." Zephyrine leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, and outside the rain thickened against the wheelhouse glass. "They're not following Odile, Tomas. They're following me. Or they think they are. And I'd rather have them where I can see them than guessing in the dark." He paused. "There's also a fourth crew member joining us. Deckhand. Quiet type. You'll meet him at the fuel dock."

"We're a skeleton crew taking on strangers the night before we sail, with the Syndicate watching, up a river that killed our people." Tomas laughed despite himself, a tired sound. "This is either the best contract we've ever taken or the last one."

"Statistically," Zephyrine said, pushing off the doorframe, "it's both."

He went below. Tomas stayed in the wheelhouse a while longer, lamp low, watching the rain come down on the black harbor and the distant shape of the Syndicate launch riding its anchor light like a patient shark. Then he took out a fresh sheet of chart paper, clipped it beside the old one, and began drawing the upper Kalyani from memory—every bend, every bell rock, every grave he knew of along its banks.

By the time he finished, the sky was graying toward dawn, and the rain had stopped pretending to be gentle.

At the fuel dock, a stranger was waiting.
