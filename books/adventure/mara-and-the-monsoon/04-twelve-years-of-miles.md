## 4. Twelve Years of Miles

Zephyrine didn't sleep the night before the contract signing. He told himself it was the rain, which came down on the tin roof of his berth above the chandlery like a man trying to talk his way through a locked door. But it wasn't the rain. It was the route Odile had unrolled across his table, her finger tracing the Kalyani up through the gorges, past Serpent Bend, past the drowned towns, all the way to the source—and his finger had stopped, without asking his permission, at a bend in the river marked on no chart he owned.

Mara's Reach.

He'd poured himself two fingers of cheap arrack and sat with the map until the lamp burned low, and the past came for him the way it always did—not like a wave but like water finding the crack in a foundation. Slow. Patient. Certain.

* * *

Twelve years ago, he had been thirty-one years old and rich in the only currency that mattered to him then: miles behind the helm. The *Kestrel* was younger then, or maybe he was, and her engine turned over on the second try instead of the fifth, and his crew was nine people who trusted him more than they should have.

Mara Iyer ran his bow. Twenty-six, quick as river light, born on the Kalyani's middle reaches to a family of ferrymen who'd plied that water for four generations. She could read the river the way other people read faces—every boil and seam, every lie the current told. Dev Okonkwo was his engineer, a big quiet man from the coast who'd come inland chasing a woman and stayed after she left because, he said, the river needed him more. He kept the *Kestrel* alive with baling wire and blasphemy in three languages.

They were running charter that season—survey work for a hydrology firm, mapping depths before the Syndicate's dam project went upstream. Zephyrine remembered thinking, in the way you think things you'll spend twelve years regretting, that the money was good and the season was early and the monsoon was still a rumor on the horizon.

The forecast said landfall in six days. It made landfall in two.

He learned later—years later, in fragments, from meteorologists and river people and once, drunk, from a retired gauge-keeper—that the storm had accelerated off the basin in a way the models hadn't caught. That happens. Storms do that. Rivers do that. The world is full of accidents, and most of them are nobody's fault, and for twelve years he had built his whole life on that sentence like a man building a house on a sandbar.

* * *

Here is what happened, though he rarely let himself tell it whole:

They were caught in the lower gorge when the front hit. Not Mara's Reach—the Reach came after, when they were already broken and running for home. The first day was survivable, barely: whiteout rain, the *Kestrel* lashed to a rock shelf while the river rose four feet in an afternoon. They waited it out in the boats' lee, cold and wet and joking, because that's what crews do. Dev made tea on a camp stove under an oilskin. Mara taught Wren's-age card games to the youngest deckhand and cheated cheerfully.

On the second day the river dropped just enough to look merciful, and Zephyrine made the call to run for the lower take-out before the next pulse arrived. Every pilot since the flood has told him it was the right call. Every pilot except the one who matters, which is himself.

They were in Mara's Reach when the second pulse came down—a wall of brown water carrying half a hillside, trees turning end over end like things drowning. Mara was on the bow line, setting a marker buoy so the boats behind could find the channel. Zephyrine saw her turn toward him and shout something—the wind took it—and then the flood arrived and the world went to noise.

He never saw her go. That was the mercy and the wound of it both: no last moment he could replay, no gesture he could have answered. One instant she was a figure in yellow against brown water; the next, the river had simply edited her out.

Dev went differently. Dev they saw. The *Kestrel*'s sister boat shipped water in the tail of the surge and rolled, and Dev went into the engine bay to free a trapped deckhand, and he got the boy out—he got the boy out, that was the thing, the boy lived, married, had children, sent Zephyrine a letter every year on the anniversary that Zephyrine had never once managed to answer—and the river took Dev for the trouble. They found him three days later, forty miles down, caught in a fish weir, his hands still curled around a wrench he'd pulled from his pocket rather than drop.

His hands wouldn't open. They had to bury the wrench with him.

* * *

After, the crew scattered the way crews do when the glue between them fails. Two quit the river entirely—one sold insurance in the capital, of all things, and seemed almost happy. The boy Dev saved drifted downstream and out of Zephyrine's life. The old hands found other boats, other captains, and Zephyrine understood why. A crew is a body of shared belief, and what they'd shared belief in had failed them on a Tuesday afternoon in ordinary weather, and there was no fixing that with talk.

He tried staying, once. After the funerals—two funerals, one closed casket—he rented a room in the port town where the *Kestrel* was moored and told himself he'd take a season off, refit the boat, grieve properly like people were always telling him to. He lasted eleven days. On the twelfth he woke at dawn with his heart going like a runaway engine and his bag already half-packed, and by noon he'd taken a cargo contract two hundred miles south, and the relief he felt pulling away from that dock frightened him more than the flood had.

That was the discovery that organized the rest of his life: staying hurt worse than leaving. Grief, he learned, was not a weight you carried. It was a current. And if you stopped moving—if you tied off, if you let yourself be still—it caught you and held you under and showed you everything, every detail, every what-if, in perfect clarity, forever. Motion blurred it. Miles blurred it. A man doing ninety down a flooded channel with his hands full of wheel doesn't have time to remember the sound Mara made laughing, or the exact angle of Dev's shoulders in the engine hatch, or the way the yellow of her slicker looked against the water.

So he drove. Twelve years of miles. Contracts up and down the coast, charters into rivers whose names he forgot by the next season, a bed that changed weekly, a life measured not in years but in nautical miles logged, in fuel receipts, in the slow arithmetic of debt accumulating against a boat that was itself a memorial he couldn't afford to maintain. He kept Mara's buoy knife in the wheelbox and Dev's wrench—no. He'd given the wrench back to Dev's mother, who'd held it in both hands and said nothing, which was worse than weeping. He kept the knife. He never used it. Using it would have meant deciding it was a tool again instead of a relic.

People called him restless. Rivals called him unreliable. Debt collectors called him a flight risk, which was the closest anyone ever came to the truth. Zephyrine Winterbourne wasn't running from the Meridian Syndicate, not really. You can't run from a number. He was running from a bend in a river, and the terrible joke of it—the joke he only allowed himself to see now, sitting in a lamplit berth with Odile Vasseur's contract on the table—was that the number and the bend had finally conspired to point him in the same direction.

* * *

Because here was the arithmetic Odile had left him with, and it was brutal in its simplicity.

Thirty days. That was what the enforcer's umbrella had bought him, thirty days before the Syndicate started taking things—first the *Kestrel*, then whatever else they decided he was worth, which by their ledgers wasn't much. Against that: Odile's contract. Triple his outstanding balance, paid on delivery, plus hazard rates through the gorges. Enough to buy the *Kestrel* free and clear. Enough to stop running.

Against that: the route. Up the Kalyani. Past Serpent Bend. Through the drowned towns where the reservoir had swallowed villages whole so the Syndicate's dam could hold its pretty lake. And through Mara's Reach, which he had avoided for twelve years the way you avoid a street where someone died, taking the long coastal runs, paying the extra fuel, telling himself it was about currents.

And beyond the Reach, the source itself. Forbidden. His old crew had sworn off the upper river after the flood—not formally, nothing so tidy, but in the way grief makes law. Nobody said *we don't go up there anymore*. Everybody knew. The upper Kalyani had taken enough.

He thought about Dev's hands around the wrench. He thought about the letter he'd never answered. He thought about the enforcer's polite voice saying *thirty days, Captain*, and the small courteous horror of a man who destroys lives the way rain falls on a roof—without malice, without effort, as a matter of course.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, a question he'd been dodging since Odile spread the map: *Why does she want the source? What's up there that's worth this?*

Odile Vasseur had never spent money she couldn't recover. He'd known her fifteen years, raced her down three coasts, watched her walk away from two fortunes and into a third. She didn't hire a washed-up rival at triple rates out of sentiment. Whatever she was carrying in those sealed crates she wouldn't let him inventory, it wasn't cargo. It was a reason.

He could refuse. He could sell the *Kestrel*, settle a third of the debt, and vanish down some river without a name, and the miles would keep blurring the water, and in another twelve years he'd be exactly where he was now, only older and poorer and no closer to the bottom of anything.

Or he could go back. Up the river. Into the reach. Toward the thing that broke him, at the speed of a loaded boat, with a stranger's money and a stranger's purpose.

Zephyrine poured out the last of the arrack and didn't drink it. Outside, the rain kept working at the roof, patient as water finding a crack.

He signed the contract at first light.

Then he went looking for a crew—which, on the Kalyani's waterfront, meant going looking for the people who owed him, and the people he owed, and hoping the difference between those two lists was wide enough to float a boat.
