## 5. Departure at Low Water

Priya found the first shadow at dawn, three days before they were supposed to launch.

She was under the *Kestrel's* engine housing with a torque wrench in her teeth and grease to her elbows, doing the kind of slow, loving inspection she only did when she was nervous, when she heard footsteps on the dock that didn't match the rhythm of anyone she knew. Not Zephyrine's long stride. Not Tomas's careful, counting walk. Not Wren, who moved like the deck might be a trap.

She slid out on her back and looked up through the gap between hull and dock boards.

Two men. Grey rain jackets, grey faces, standing at the head of the pier like they were admiring the harbor. One of them held a paper cup of tea he never drank from. The other had a phone out, angled toward the boat — not obviously photographing, just holding it, the way a man holds a phone when he wants you to know he could be photographing.

Priya had grown up in a country where men like that stood outside schools. She knew the species. They didn't need to do anything. Their whole job was to be seen doing nothing.

She rolled back under the engine and finished the torque check, because the work was real whether or not the world was ending, and because her hands were steadier with a wrench in them than without.

By the time she surfaced properly, Wren was crouched at the gunwale, watching the two men with open teenage hostility.

"They've been there since before light," Wren said. "I counted. Forty minutes. The tea guy hasn't taken one sip."

"You counted his sips?"

"I count everything." Wren shrugged. "It's not like there's anything else to do on this dock."

Priya wiped her hands on a rag and considered the pair. "Go find Zephyrine."

"He's arguing with the harbormaster about mooring fees."

"Then go interrupt him. This is more important than fees."

Wren went, quick as a cat down the dock. Priya watched the two grey men track the kid's movement — tracked, then dismissed. That told her something too. They were cataloguing assets, and a teenage deckhand didn't register as one yet. It would have been funny if it weren't so useful.

Zephyrine came back along the pier at his usual pace, which was to say like a man who had never once hurried for anyone, though Priya had learned by now that the pace was armor. He stopped beside her, looked at the two men, and said nothing for a long moment.

"Meridian?" he asked quietly.

"Who else wears that much grey?"

"Syndicate doesn't usually post shadows this early." His jaw worked. "Unless someone told them our schedule."

"Or unless they always intended to watch us and we were fools to think they'd wait until we were interesting."

"We're already interesting. We're carrying Odile Vasseur upriver into territory the Syndicate pretends doesn't exist." He turned the thought over the way Tomas turned a chart over. "We launch today."

"Today? The water's still low. Lower Kalyani runs shallow until the pre-monsoon pulse comes through — Tomas said—"

"Tomas can say it again while we're loading." Zephyrine was already moving toward the cabin. "Low water is a problem. Shadows are a bigger one. Every day we sit at this dock is a day they decide whether we're worth stopping here instead of out there, where nobody would ever find the wreckage."

Priya looked at the engine housing, half-open, her tools laid out in their careful order. Three days of checks remained on her list. Fuel lines hadn't been pressure-tested. The bilge pump's backup wasn't mounted.

"Give me four hours," she called after him.

He paused. "Three."

"Four, or we float downriver on a boat I don't trust."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. "Four," he conceded, and went to break the news to the others.

* * *

They cast off at 14:20, an hour before low water bottomed out, which Tomas informed everyone was either brilliant or suicidal depending on how the next six kilometers went.

Priya stood in the engine bay with the hatch open behind her so she could hear the river and feel the hull at the same time. The *Kestrel* was forty years old, a shallow-draft river freighter some previous owner had converted for expedition work: cargo hold amidships, crew quarters aft, a wheelhouse Zephyrine had refitted with instruments older than Wren. Priya had rebuilt her engines twice in the week since signing on, and she trusted them the way you trust anything you've personally taken apart down to the bearings — completely, and with the knowledge that trust is not the same as certainty.

The lower river was wide and brown-green, braided around sandbars the color of old bone. On both banks, terraced fields stepped down to the waterline, and beyond them the foothills of the middle gorge stood up blue-grey in the haze. Somewhere up there, twelve years ago, the river had eaten two people Priya had never met and remade the man at the helm into whatever he was now.

"Sandbar, ten degrees starboard," Tomas called from the chart table, where he'd spread hand-drawn soundings of the lower reach — his own surveys, done years ago, annotated in pencil in a cramped, patient hand. "Depth should hold at one-point-two meters off the bar's edge. Should."

"Should is doing a lot of work in that sentence," Zephyrine said, easing the wheel over.

"That's navigation. Everything is 'should.' If you want 'will,' get a different river."

Wren hung off the bow rail with a sounding pole, calling depths in a voice that cracked between registers and didn't care. Priya found herself listening to the kid's numbers against the feel of the engines — the pitch dropping slightly as the propeller bit shallower water — and building a picture of the channel from all of it at once. This was the part of the work she loved, the part she'd crossed an ocean and burned a life to keep: a machine and a crew and a moving river, all of it talking to her at once, all of it honest.

Odile Vasseur sat in the wheelhouse doorway with a leather case across her knees and watched everything.

Priya had spent a week trying to read the woman and gotten nowhere. Odile was polite in a way that used no warmth, competent in a way that advertised nothing. When they'd loaded the boat, she'd carried her own gear aboard — four cases, heavy for their size, which she stowed in the hold herself and roped down personally, declining Wren's help with a courtesy so smooth it functioned as a wall.

Now, as the *Kestrel* threaded the sandbar channel, Odile rose and went aft to the hold. Priya, checking the shaft seal through the access panel, saw her kneel by the cases and check the ropes. Not untie them. Just touch them, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal to confirm it was still breathing.

One of the cases had come loose an inch in the chop. Odile re-secured it, and as she leaned over it, her jacket fell open, and Priya saw what was clipped inside the breast pocket: a slim camera, lens cap off.

Not a tourist's camera. A document camera, the flat kind archivists used.

Priya put the panel back on and said nothing, and filed it away in the place where she kept things she wasn't ready to know yet.

* * *

The first rapids found them at dusk, where the valley pinched shut and the whole river funneled through a gap maybe sixty meters wide.

Tomas had warned them about it — the Ladder, he called it, three steps of broken water where the bed dropped a meter at a time through shattered rock. In high water it was runnable. In low water, with the rocks bare and the current compressed, it was a technical run that punished imprecision.

"Line is left of center," Tomas said, pencil tapping the chart. "There's a tongue of green water coming off the mid-channel bar. Stay on it. Right side has a hole the size of this boat. Left side has rocks just under the surface that will open us like a tin."

"And if we miss the tongue?" Wren asked.

"Then we find out what the hole does to a forty-year-old hull," Priya said from below, "and I find out how good my patch kit is."

"Nobody's missing the tongue," Zephyrine said, and there was something in his voice that ended the conversation.

Priya climbed up to the wheelhouse for this one. She told herself it was to watch the temperature gauges, which was true, but it was also because she wanted to see him work. She'd signed onto this trip for reasons she hadn't fully admitted even to herself — debts of her own, exile of her own, a name she'd left behind in a country that had stopped being safe for her — and one of those reasons was the reputation Zephyrine Winterbourne carried among people who ran rivers. She wanted to verify it.

He brought the boat in slow, throttle barely above idle, letting the current do the steering the way a good helmsman lets a horse find its own footing. The roar built. Spray came over the bow in cold sheets. Wren clung to the rail, grinning like this was the best day of a short life.

"There," Tomas said quietly. "Green water. Now."

Zephyrine gave it throttle — a measured, exact amount — and the *Kestrel* leapt onto the tongue of clean water and dropped.

The first step hit like a fist under the keel. Priya felt it through her boots, heard the engines surge as the props briefly left load, corrected the fuel mix by ear without touching anything. The second step came faster, the river accelerating as the gorge narrowed, and the boat slewed hard to port — a rock shelf, invisible, its wash deflecting the current — and for one long second they were sliding sideways toward the left-hand rocks.

Zephyrine didn't fight it. He turned *into* the slide, wheel hard over, using the pivot to swing the bow back across the current, and the *Kestrel* came around with two meters to spare and dropped down the third step straight and true, into the calm water below, throwing a curtain of spray across the whole deck.

Silence, except for the engines and the river and Wren whooping.

"Noted," Zephyrine said mildly, as if someone had complimented his parking.

But Priya had seen it — the half-second at the second drop where his hands had locked on the wheel, where his eyes had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere twelve years and a hundred miles upstream. The maneuver had been flawless. The man performing it had flinched.

She said nothing about that either. Her file of unsaid things was getting thick, and they weren't even out of the lowlands.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar in the lee of the gorge wall, running lines to shore and banking a cook fire against the stone. The sky had gone the deep bruised purple of the pre-monsoon weeks, heat lightning flickering silently over the far ridgeline like a rumor of what was coming.

Priya took the first watch, which suited her. She liked the river at night, the way it kept talking after everyone else went quiet.

Odile couldn't sleep either. She came and sat on an upturned crate near the fire, close enough for company, far enough for privacy, and opened the leather case.

Priya hadn't meant to look. But the firelight was generous, and Odile lifted out a folder of papers — photocopies, by their edges, dozens of them — and began reading them with a small penlight, making marks in a notebook.

"What are they?" Priya asked, before she could stop herself.

Odile didn't startle. She closed the folder with deliberate calm and regarded Priya across the fire. "Cargo documentation."

"Cargo documentation you read every night and photograph page by page?"

For a moment Priya thought the woman would deny it. Then something shifted — not warmth, exactly, but a recalculation, the way a lock recalculates when it decides a key might fit.

"You're the engineer," Odile said. "You understand structures. Loads. Failure points."

"I understand engines. Structures are cousins."

"The dam at the source basin," Odile said, "is the largest structure on this river. It generates power for three provinces and controls the water for fifty more. Twelve years ago, during the monsoon, its auxiliary spillway failed catastrophically. The official finding was sedimentation overload — unusual, tragic, unpreventable." She turned a page in her mind Priya couldn't see. "I believe the failure was scheduled."

Priya stared at her. "Scheduled."

"Engineered. Ordered. The flood that broke the spillway also destroyed every settlement in the middle valley — settlements whose land claims were the last obstacle to the Syndicate's upstream concessions. The flood solved a land problem." Odile's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "I helped build the financial architecture that made it possible. I didn't know what I was helping build. That's not absolution. It's just the shape of my guilt."

Downstream, twelve years ago. A flood. Two names Zephyrine wouldn't say and a man who measured his life in miles because staying anywhere meant remembering.

"You're going to tell him this," Priya said. It wasn't a question, but Odile treated it like one.

"When he can hear it. Not before." She tucked the folder away. "A man who learns his grief was manufactured — on a boat, surrounded by strangers, with the Syndicate behind us — will make choices with that grief. I need him to make them with evidence instead. So the evidence comes first."

"And your shadows on the dock?"

"Are proof they're frightened. Meridian doesn't spend watchers on ghosts." Odile fed a stick to the fire. "Get some sleep, engineer. Tomorrow the water gets harder."

Priya didn't sleep. She sat her watch and listened to the river and turned the woman's words over like stones, looking at what lived underneath each one.

Near midnight, she heard a second set of footsteps soft on the gravel — Zephyrine, unable to sleep too, coming to relieve her early. He settled onto the crate Odile had vacated, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"The rapids," Priya said finally. "Second drop. You froze. Half a second."

His profile didn't move. "Did I get us through?"

"Yes."

"Then the half-second is mine to carry." He said it without sharpness, which was worse than sharpness. "Anything else, engineer?"

She thought of the folder, the penlight, the word *scheduled*. She thought of Odile saying *the evidence comes first*, and of the two grey men drinking tea they never touched, and of a valley full of the drowned whose deaths had been someone's line item.

"No," Priya said. "Nothing else."

But she lay awake in her bunk afterward, listening to the current pull at the anchor lines, and understood that she had just become a person keeping a secret on a small boat — and that small boats have a way of surfacing everything eventually.

Upstream, past the dark bend of the gorge, the river ran on toward its forbidden source, and somewhere along it, she suspected, the truth was waiting for them with the patience of water.
