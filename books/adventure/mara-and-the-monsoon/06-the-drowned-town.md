## 6. The Drowned Town

Wren had never seen a town drown before.

She'd seen houses flood—flash floods in the low country, water through a doorway and gone again by morning, leaving mud lines like the rings on an old man's wrist. But this was different. This was a whole settlement sitting in brown water to its second stories, rooftops poking up like the backs of sleeping animals, and it had been that way long enough for the trees to die standing. Long enough for birds to nest in the gutters of submerged homes.

The *Kestrel* came around the bend at mid-morning, and Wren was on the foredeck with the mooring line coiled over her shoulder when she saw them: chimneys. Dozens of them, marching off into the reservoir in crooked rows, marking streets that were now channels. A church spire leaned at an angle no builder intended. A power line still stretched between two poles, sagging into the water, and something bright—a child's kite, maybe, or a shirt—hung snagged on it, faded almost white by years of sun.

"Old Sarn," Priya said from the wheelhouse behind her. "Reservoir took it six, seven years back."

"Took it?" Wren said. "Water doesn't take towns. Floods pass."

"Floods pass." Priya's voice was flat as slate. "Dams stay."

Wren looked back at the drowned street. Somewhere under all that brown water there was a kitchen where somebody had burned toast. There was a bedroom with posters peeling off the wall. She thought about how the water didn't look heavy from here, how it just lay there like it belonged, like it had always been there, patient as anything.

"How deep is it?"

"Forty feet at the old square." Priya adjusted the throttle, easing them past a half-submerged truck whose cab windows had gone green with algae. "Deeper at the mill."

The fuel depot sat on a concrete shelf above the waterline—the only part of Old Sarn that hadn't gone under, because it had been built high, on the bluff, for exactly this reason. Somebody had known. Somebody had drawn the line and built the depot above it and let everything below the line go dark.

That was the thing Wren kept turning over as they tied off: somebody had decided this. Water didn't decide anything. Water just did what gravity told it. But a line on a map—that was a person. That was a hand with a pencil.

* * *

The depot man was called Anselm, and he was maybe sixty, with hands like tree roots and a radio playing tinny music behind the counter. He sold them diesel without much talk, pumping it barrel by barrel while Tomas haggled the price down and Odile stood apart, watching the water the way she'd been watching it since dawn—not like scenery, but like a document.

Wren carried the empty barrels back to the rack, and that was when she saw the families.

They lived on the bluff now, above the depot and beyond it—a scatter of shelters made from boat hulls split lengthwise, tarpaulins, shipping pallets, corrugated tin weighed down with stones. Maybe thirty structures. Laundry lines strung between dead trees. Kids running barefoot along a dirt track worn shiny by feet. And below them, all around three sides, the water that had eaten their town.

One of the kids—a girl, eight or nine, hair braided tight against the damp—was standing at the edge of the bluff throwing stones into the reservoir. Not skipping them. Just throwing, hard, one after another, with a fury too big for her body. Each stone hit the water with a flat slap and sank without ceremony.

Wren drifted over, the way you drift toward a fire.

"Hey," she said.

The girl threw another stone. It went farther than the others.

"You live up here?"

"We live on top of our house," the girl said. "Technically."

Wren frowned. The girl pointed with her chin at the water.

"Our street's right there. Third chimney past the leaning one. You can see ours 'cause we painted the roof blue before—" She stopped. Started over. "Before they said we had to move up. My room's under about ten meters now. I left my books."

"They flooded your house while you were still—"

"They gave us notice." The girl said it in a sing-song way, like reciting something adults had told her so many times it had worn smooth. "Compensation schedules. Relocation allotments. My dad says those are fancy words for *less than it was worth*. He says the men who measured our house wrote down the smallest numbers they could get away with, and when he argued, they showed him a paper with a stamp on it, and the stamp ended the argument. Stamps end arguments here."

"What company?"

The girl looked at her like she was slow. "The same one. The only one. The one that owns the river." She picked up another stone, considered it, put it back down. "You're going upstream, aren't you? Toward the dam."

Wren glanced back at the boat. Odile had moved; she was standing at the rail now, binoculars up, aimed not at the drowned town but at the far shore, at the road that followed the reservoir's eastern arm. Zephyrine was beside her, arms crossed, saying nothing.

"Yeah," Wren said.

"Nobody goes upstream," the girl said. "Fuel boats come this far and turn around. My uncle used to run cargo past Serpent Bend and even he stopped." She kicked at the dirt. "There's a bell tree down the shore. You'll see it. River people hung it. It means *turn back* in their language. Or it means *they will hear you coming*. Depends who you ask."

"Who do you ask?"

The girl shrugged, and for a second she looked much older than nine, older than Wren, older than anybody. "Doesn't matter. Nobody turns back anyway. They just stop asking questions instead." She held out her hand, suddenly businesslike. "I'm Ilsa. If you've got anything from downstream—candy, batteries, magazines—I trade fair."

* * *

Wren traded two battery cells and a dog-eared adventure serial for a fishing hook the size of her thumb and a piece of local gossip she didn't yet know the value of: that a Syndicate survey team had passed through three weeks ago, going upstream, armed, riding two black-hulled boats with engines too quiet for their size.

She carried that gossip back down the bluff and found the crew gathered near the fuel rack, and she meant to lead with the boats, she really did, but what came out of her mouth instead was:

"Why is there a town underwater?"

Tomas looked up from the fuel ledger. "Old Sarn. Reservoir project."

"I know it's a reservoir. I mean why does the reservoir have a town in it. Why didn't they move the whole town first?"

"They did move it," Priya said. "Most of it. New Sarn's twenty klicks south."

"But the people are still *here*." Wren gestured up the bluff. "They're living on top of their own street. In boat halves. If they got moved, why are they—"

"Because compensation runs out," Priya said. Her voice had that flat quality again, but underneath it Wren heard something else, something with edges. "Because the new town has rents, and the compensation was calculated by men with stamps. Because some people wouldn't leave until the water was at the door, and by then the trucks were gone." She closed the ledger. "It's a common story on this river, Wren. There are four more like it between here and the gorges."

"Four more drowned towns?"

"Four more displaced settlements. Only Old Sarn drowned completely." Priya shouldered a fuel can. "Ask your questions on your own time. We cast off in twenty minutes."

But Wren had learned from Zephyrine that questions asked on someone else's time got answered faster, so she turned to him. He was coiling the bow line with the particular economy of motion he brought to everything, like he was rationing himself.

"Did you know about this?" she asked. "About the dam?"

He didn't stop coiling. "Everybody on the Kalyani knows about the dam."

"That's not what I asked."

The coil tightened. Finished. He set it down and looked at her, and his face did the thing it did sometimes—went smooth and distant, like a door closing in a house she couldn't see inside of.

"The dam's forty years old," he said. "It makes the power for half the valley. It holds back the monsoon so the low country doesn't wash away every year. It's a fact of the river, same as rain." He picked up the next line. "Facts don't need reasons."

"That's not true," Wren said. "Everything has a reason. Ilsa says the men who measured the houses wrote small numbers on purpose. She says the stamp ends arguments. Whose stamp is it?"

For a moment—one breath, maybe two—Zephyrine looked past her, upriver, toward the water and whatever lay at the end of it. His jaw worked once.

"Zephyrine," Odile called from the wheelhouse, mild as milk. "Charts."

And he went, grateful or pretending to be, and Wren stood alone on the concrete with the drowned town at her back and the smell of diesel and wet earth in her nose, holding a fishhook worth two batteries and an answer worth nothing at all.

* * *

They cast off at noon. The reservoir widened as they ran north, and the drowned things kept coming: fence lines vanishing into green water, a barn roof with its ridgepole exposed like a whale's back, an orchard of dead apple trees standing in rows to their shoulders, fruitless, patient. Wren sat on the foredeck and watched and counted chimneys and lost count at sixty.

Priya came forward eventually, maybe out of guilt, maybe just to check the anchor locker. She crouched next to Wren without looking at the water.

"My family farmed the Terai flats," she said, apropos of nothing. "Before the second reservoir. We got relocation allotments too." She tested the anchor chain link by link, feeling for corrosion. "I was twelve. Older than your friend Ilsa. Old enough to remember what the land was worth and young enough to have no say in any of it."

"What happened to your family?"

"Some went east. Some stayed and farm the new flats, which are worse soil and farther from everything." A pause. The chain clinked. "Some are still angry in ways that cost them. My brother, mostly." She clipped the chain down. "You want to know why nobody talks about it on this boat? It's not because we don't know. It's because everybody aboard already carries their own version of this story, and versions don't help each other carry better. They just get heavier."

"That's the saddest thing anyone's ever told me," Wren said.

Priya almost smiled. "You're sixteen. Give it time."

She went aft, and Wren stayed on the foredeck, and the reservoir kept unspooling ahead of them, wide and calm and full of secrets sitting on the bottom in neat little rows.

Near evening the channel narrowed, the water went from brown to green-black, and the current found them again—the river resuming its own shape after being held hostage for a stretch. On the western shore, half-hidden in reeds, Wren saw it: a great tree hung with dozens of bells, bronze and iron and scrap-metal, motionless in the windless air. Waiting.

Ilsa's bell tree. *Turn back*, or *they will hear you coming*.

As they slid past, the wake touched the bank, and one bell—just one, the smallest, at the lowest branch—swayed and rang, a single soft note that crossed the water and followed them for longer than it should have.

Wren didn't tell the others. But that night, anchored in a side slough with the crew asleep around her, she got out the big fishhook and turned it over in her fingers, and she thought about stamps that end arguments, and men with pencils drawing lines, and a roof painted blue under ten meters of patient water.

And she thought about the two black-hulled boats with the quiet engines, three weeks ahead of them, going where nobody goes.

Upstream, toward the dam. Toward whatever it was Odile watched through binoculars and Zephyrine wouldn't name.

Wren was sixteen, and she knew she was the youngest aboard and the least owed and the least owing. But she also knew the difference between a flood and a drowning. Floods pass.

Whatever had happened at Old Sarn—and whatever was waiting for them at the source—hadn't passed. It was just lying there, patient as water, waiting for somebody to finally ask the question out loud.

She resolved, lying in the dark with the bell's note still ringing somewhere behind her ribs, that she would be that somebody—even if she had to ask every single person on this boat, starting tomorrow, starting with the ones who least wanted to answer.
