## 7. What Odile Carries

The rain found us at Anchorline Bend, a fuel stop so small it didn't appear on charts Tomas trusted and appeared on the ones he didn't. A concrete apron, two rusted tanks, a shack with a hand-painted sign—FUEL, ICE, LIES—and a proprietor who charged triple for all three.

I let Priya handle the fueling because Priya haggled like she was born to it, which she probably was somewhere that made her the way she was. I took Wren to stretch rope and check the mooring lines, because Wren had energy that needed burning off before it burned through my deck. And Odile Vasseur said she'd be in her cabin going over route notes, which was the first lie of the evening, though I wouldn't know it for another hour.

Twelve years of running rivers teaches you things. It teaches you the sound water makes before it turns ugly. It teaches you which passengers sleep light and which sleep like the dead. And it teaches you that when someone tells you exactly where they'll be, you should want to know why they told you.

I didn't think about any of that yet. I was thinking about ice, actually, and how much of it we could afford, and whether the ice at Anchorline Bend was more river than ice this time of year. Small thoughts. Comfortable thoughts. The kind you think when your body is doing one thing and your mind is hiding from another.

Then I came back aboard early for the ice chest, and the cabin door was open six inches, and there was lamplight where there shouldn't have been lamplight, and Odile Vasseur was standing over my chart table photographing documents.

* * *

She didn't startle. That's what I noticed first. Most people, caught like that—the lamp swinging, the camera half-raised, the papers fanned out under their hands—most people flinch. They make noise. They perform being innocent, badly.

Odile just looked up at me the way she used to look across regatta starting lines twenty years ago, when we were young and stupid and competing over everything: races, contracts, the affections of people who deserved better than either of us. That level, measuring stare. Sizing up the current.

"You're back early," she said.

"You're holding my cargo manifest."

"I'm holding *a* cargo manifest." She set the camera down on the table, deliberately, the way you'd set down a card you wanted me to know she had. "Yours is in the wheelhouse safe. This one came with my equipment crates. You never looked inside them."

She was right. I hadn't. When a client pays half up front and tells you not to ask about the cargo, you develop a professional blindness. It keeps you employed. It also, apparently, keeps you ignorant of what's sleeping in your own hold.

"Those are Syndicate shipping ledgers," I said. I could read the letterhead from the door. Meridian Syndicate, Regional Operations, Kalyani Basin Division. I'd seen enough of that letterhead in my life. It arrived with bills.

"Photostats," she corrected. "The originals are somewhere I can't reach anymore. These came out with me."

"Out of where?"

"Out of a life." She straightened the pages, squared their edges, an archivist's habit even mid-confrontation. "Sit down, Zephyrine. Or stand in the doorway looking like a man deciding whether to throw me overboard. Either way, you're going to hear this."

I stood in the doorway. Old habits. Doorways give you options.

* * *

Here's what I knew about Odile Vasseur before that night: she was better funded than me, better connected than me, and had beaten me to three salvage contracts in the years before everything went wrong. Here's what I knew after: nothing I'd known was the useful part.

"The dam," she said. "Kalyani Station One. You've seen it on the charts."

"Everyone's seen it on the charts. Nobody's seen it in person. The whole upper basin's closed. Permits, checkpoints, armed patrols." I shrugged. "Syndicate property. They don't like visitors."

"They don't like witnesses." She turned one of the photostats toward me. Columns of figures, dense as rain. "This is a materials requisition log. Dated eleven years ago, four months before the collapse. Look at the spillway concrete batch numbers."

I looked. I'm not an engineer. But Priya is, and I'd heard her mutter about batch certifications often enough to know that concrete has a paper trail like everything else in the world.

"What am I looking at?"

"Batch numbers for concrete ordered at structural grade. Followed by delivery records showing the same batches diverted and sold downstream to a canal project. And then—" she flipped a page, "—replacement orders. Cheaper mix. Unrated aggregate. Signed off by a regional director whose initials are right here, and whose name you might recognize, because he's now on the Syndicate's central board."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the rain kept up its work on the roof, patient as interest accruing.

"So they cut corners," I said. "Corporations cut corners. Concrete fails. People drown. It's a tragedy, not a conspiracy."

"It would be a tragedy if it stopped there." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. Odile angry was loud; Odile certain was quiet. "It doesn't stop there. Two weeks before the failure, this same office filed a flood-insurance claim schedule against every settlement in the middle valley. Every town downstream of the weakened section. Pre-registered. Before the water ever moved."

I felt something shift under my feet, and we were tied to a dock.

"That's coincidence. Insurance companies file schedules constantly. It's paperwork."

"Zephyrine." She said my name the way you'd say the name of a man standing on train tracks. "They built the dam knowing the spillway would fail. They scheduled the claims knowing when. Then they bought the drowned land at auction for a fraction of its worth, and twelve years later the entire middle valley belongs to them, and the towns that used to be there belong to a reservoir. That's not cutting corners. That's a business plan."

* * *

I want to tell you I argued well. I didn't. I argued the way a drowning man argues with the river—with conviction and no leverage.

Because here's the thing she didn't know she was stepping on, or maybe she knew exactly, maybe that's why she waited until day nine of the journey instead of telling me in the port city where I could have walked away. My hometown is in the middle valley. Was. Ashford Landing, population four hundred, one cannery, one schoolhouse, one long street that flooded knee-deep every spring and everyone called character. Twelve years ago, in the last week of the monsoon, the water didn't stop at knee-deep.

Mara died there. Dev died there. Not in the flood itself—we lost them on the river during the evacuation run, which is its own wound and not tonight's—but the flood is why we were running boats at all, hauling people out through water that ate bridges. And afterward, when the water went down, Ashford Landing didn't. The Syndicate bought the basin. There's a buoy chain where my street was.

So when Odile Vasseur stood in my cabin and told me the Syndicate engineered a dam collapse in the middle valley, she wasn't telling me a story about strangers. She was telling me the water that took my town was scheduled. Invoiced. Initialled.

And I did what any sane man does when the ground opens under him. I denied the ground existed.

"No," I said.

Odile waited. She was always better at waiting than me.

"No," I said again, and heard how it sounded, and hated it. "You're telling me a regional office murdered four hundred people for real estate. That's not business. That's not even evil, that's—nobody's that—"

"Goebbels?" she offered, too dry. "No. Think smaller. Think of a man with quarterly targets and a bonus structure and a belief that the valley was doomed anyway, the rains were getting worse every year, those towns were finished regardless. He didn't push a button. He signed a form. He let the river do what the river was always going to do, a little sooner, a little more completely, and he profited from the timing." She tapped the photostat. "That's how it works now. Atrocity as administration."

"And you know this how? Really?" I finally stepped into the cabin and shut the door behind me, because some conversations shouldn't be visible from the dock. "You were Syndicate. Don't look surprised that I know. Tomas pulled your registry history before we signed anything. You held a regional logistics post for six years. You weren't investigating them, Odile. You were *them*."

Something crossed her face then—not guilt exactly. Guilt is simple. This was older and worse. The look of someone who has already served a sentence no court knows about.

"Yes," she said. "I was. I processed freight manifests for the basin division. Which means I processed the requisitions. I saw the batch diversions go through my own desk and I flagged nothing, because flagging things gets you reassigned to a place with no river in it, and I liked my life." She picked up the camera, turned it over in her hands. "Three years ago a woman named Sefa Okonkwo came to my office asking questions about the reservoir boundaries. She was writing a history of the valley. Six weeks later she drowned in a channel with no current. The report said alcohol. Sefa didn't drink. I knew she didn't drink, because I'd bought her tea twice a week for a month while she asked her questions, and I answered none of them, and I told myself staying silent was survival."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere on deck, Wren laughed at something Priya said, quick and bright, and the sound came through the hull like it came from another world.

"When she died," Odile went on, "I started copying everything. Slowly. Photostats hidden in equipment orders, my own signature authorizing the shipments. It took me two years to assemble enough to matter, and eight months to find a way out without disappearing the way she did. And it took me a year after that to find the missing piece." She looked up. "The source gauges. Kalyani Station One keeps the original flow-rate telemetry from the year of the collapse. Hard copy, sealed archive, on-site. The photostats show intent. The gauges show execution—they'll prove the spillway gates were cycled against protocol in the final week, manually, from the station. Someone opened the throat on purpose. That record is the difference between an accusation and a verdict. And it sits upstream, behind the checkpoints, at the end of the only navigable route in the basin."

"Which is why you need me."

"Which is why I need you." She spread her hands. "You're the only captain alive who knows the upper Kalyani and isn't on their payroll. Your old crew ran this water before the closure. You know the gorges, the bends, the settlements that don't appear on Syndicate charts. I could hire a Syndicate pilot, but a Syndicate pilot reports to Syndicate. I could hire an outsider, but an outsider dies at Serpent Bend. I need a ghost with a boat, Zephyrine. You're the closest thing this river has."

* * *

I should have thrown her off right there. Contract fraud, at minimum—she'd hired me under false pretenses, or at least incomplete ones, and every sailor knows that's how crews get killed. Instead I stood there with my jaw tight and my hands flat on the chart table, staring at her photostats, and what I felt wasn't anger.

It was the feeling of a knot you've been carrying for twelve years suddenly showing you a loose thread.

Because there had been things. Things I'd folded away and sat on, the way you sit on a hatch cover to keep a storm out. The speed of the water that week—faster than any monsoon I'd crewed through, faster than the gauge readings the radio broadcast, readings that came from upstream, from Syndicate instruments nobody could verify. The way the evacuation order came late and then the relief money came fast, Syndicate checks handed out on the dock before the dead were even counted, buying silence wrapped in gratitude. The way nobody downstream ever got answers, and nobody upstream ever got asked.

I'd called it bad luck. Bad luck is a load-bearing wall in a man's grief. You put everything against it.

"You're wrong," I said, and my voice came out lower than I intended. "About my town. The collapse that hit Ashford Landing—that was a different structure, a levee breach, weather. What you're describing is upstream. Higher basin. It's not—it doesn't connect."

"Doesn't it?" Odile asked softly. "Same division. Same director. Same insurance schedule—I can show you the line item. Ashford Landing, parcel 114 through 131, pre-registered eleven days before the breach." She slid one page across the table and turned it to face me, and there it was, in bureaucratic type, the name of my town sitting in a column between two other names of other towns, all of them dated, all of them priced.

Eleven days before the water came.

I looked at that line item for a long time. Long enough for the lamp to gutter once. Long enough to hear my own pulse in my ears, slow and enormous, like surf.

Denial is a strange country. You can live there your whole life and call it home. And then someone lays a single sheet of paper on a table in a fuel-stop cabin on a night of rain, and the whole country floods, and you learn it was never solid ground at all.

"I need air," I said, which was a lie, but a traditional one.

* * *

Outside, the rain had thinned to a drifting mist, and the dock lights of Anchorline Bend smeared themselves across the black water like spilled paint. Priya had the tanks nearly full. Wren was coiling line with exaggerated care, pretending not to watch me come down the gangway, failing completely.

"Captain?" Wren said. "You look like you saw a ghost."

"Worse," I said. "Paperwork."

Priya glanced up from the fuel gauge, and something in my face must have translated, because she shut the valve and said, quietly, "How much do we want to know?"

"Not yet." I looked upriver, into the dark where the gorge narrowed and the current ran cold off the high country, toward the checkpoint at Serpent Bend, toward the closed basin, toward a sealed station full of gauges and a valley full of graves with invoices attached. Toward whatever Odile Vasseur was really carrying, because a woman who spent five years smuggling evidence out of the Syndicate one photostat at a time does not tell you everything on the first night. She'd shown me the thread. She hadn't shown me what it was tied to.

"Tomas," I called toward the wheelhouse. "Get the charts out. All of them, including the ones you don't trust."

He leaned out, eyebrows up. "We're off-schedule?"

"We're off-everything." I climbed aboard and cast off the stern line myself. "New heading. We run the upper Kalyani ahead of the season, ahead of the patrols, ahead of whatever's following us—and Tomás, if anyone asks, we're a survey charter."

"A survey of what?"

I looked at the dark water sliding past the hull, all of it running down from a place I'd sworn off twelve years ago, carrying answers I'd refused to drink.

"Of what the Syndicate drowned," I said.
