## 8. Checkpoint at Serpent Bend

Tomas saw the checkpoint before anyone else did, because Tomas always saw the things nobody wanted to see.

He was in the wheelhouse with the chart spread across the table, running his finger along the gorge ahead, when he noticed the discrepancy. The Kalyani narrowed at Serpent Bend—that was on every chart, commercial and otherwise. What wasn't on the charts was the chain of orange buoys strung bank to bank a half-mile downstream of the narrows, or the steel gantry rising out of the water behind them like a jaw full of teeth, or the flat-roofed concrete building crouched on the left bank with an antenna mast bristling off its roof.

"Zeph," he said quietly. "You're going to want to look at this."

Zephyrine came forward from the engine hatch, wiping grease from his hands with a rag that only spread it around. Priya followed, and behind her Wren pressed against the doorframe, all elbows and curiosity.

Tomas laid two fingers on the chart. "Serpent Bend. Toll post. Syndicate colors on the building, Syndicate buoys in the water, and—" he tapped again, on a small square he'd drawn himself in pencil, "—a scanner mast. They'll have our transponder before we can see their faces."

"How far?" Zephyrine asked.

"At this current? Twenty minutes. Maybe fifteen if the gorge funnels us like I think it will."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The *Kestrel's* old diesel muttered beneath them, patient as ever, and above the wheelhouse the monsoon clouds had organized themselves into something with intent—low, bruised, moving against the wind the way storm clouds do when they've stopped being weather and become a decision.

"We could run it," Priya said. She said it the way she said most things: flat, technical, already calculating. "The narrows are fast but they're straight. If we kill the transponder and hug the right bank past the buoys—"

"They'd still log us," Tomas said. "The mast reads hull signatures, not just transponders. And if we run, we confirm everything they might otherwise doubt. A boat that runs is a boat with something to hide."

"And we don't have anything to hide?" Wren said.

Everyone looked at Odile Vasseur, who had appeared at the rear of the wheelhouse without making a sound, which was her particular talent. She was drinking tea from a tin cup, unhurried, her gray-streaked hair tied back and her eyes doing that thing they did—reading the room like a ledger.

"We have cargo manifests," she said. "Legitimate ones. We are a survey vessel contracted to take hydrological readings for an agricultural consortium. That consortium exists. Those contracts exist. I wrote them myself, three weeks ago, in a hotel room in Port Amara, and they will survive scrutiny."

"Forged papers," Wren said.

"*Prepared* papers," Odile corrected. "The difference matters legally and morally, though I grant it may not matter practically." She set down her cup. "The question isn't whether the papers are good. The question is whether the man reading them wants them to be good. Papers open doors; people close them. So the real question is—who's posted at Serpent Bend?"

"I can answer that," Tomas said, and hated that he could.

They all turned to him. He kept his eyes on the chart because looking at their faces would make it worse.

"The enforcer from Port Amara. The one with the umbrella. His name is Corvus Dain—he signs his toll logs with a stylized C that looks like a bird's foot, and I've seen those logs before, years ago, back when I ran charts for a different kind of operation. Dain doesn't do field work anymore unless it matters. If he's sitting at a river checkpoint in the middle of nowhere, it's because someone told him exactly which boat to sit there waiting for."

"You're sure it's him?" Zephyrine asked.

"He requested the posting," Tomas said. "I made some calls last night when we anchored. An old contact in the toll administration owes me a favor and sold me the duty roster. Serpent Bend station, this week: one supervising enforcer, four toll officers, six hired security. Supervising enforcer, C. Dain." He finally looked up. "He asked for this post, Zeph. That means he knows we're coming. Which means either Priya's right and we've got a leak, or he's simply thorough, and I honestly don't know which possibility frightens me more."

Zephyrine stood very still. Tomas watched him do the thing he always did—the arithmetic of other people's safety, the silent tallying of risk against debt. It was a strange way to live, Tomas thought. Measuring your life in what you owed instead of what you had.

"Then we go through," Zephyrine said at last. "Slow, polite, bored. A survey boat with nothing interesting aboard. Odile handles the paperwork. I handle the conversation. Tomas, you're my navigator—nervous, competent, eager to be somewhere else. That part shouldn't be hard."

"Thanks," Tomas said dryly.

"Wren stays below. Priya keeps the engine warm. If I give the signal—two short blasts of the horn—we come about and run the narrows and accept whatever happens after."

"And if they board us?" Priya asked.

"Then they find hydrological equipment, agricultural contracts, and a hold full of fuel drums, and Odile's papers earn their keep." Zephyrine looked at her. "It does earn its keep, doesn't it?"

Odile smiled the thin smile she used when she was about to be honest. "It earns its keep until someone checks the second page. After that, we improvise."

"Wonderful," said Tomas, who had survived twelve years on this river by knowing exactly how wonderful things were not.

* * *

The gorge took them the way Tomas had predicted—funneling the river between walls of black stone streaked white with bird droppings, the current doubling, trebling, the *Kestrel* yawing and correcting, yawing and correcting. Wren appeared beside him at the chart table despite orders, holding a sounding line like a talisman, and Tomas didn't have the heart to send the kid below. Everyone coped differently. Wren coped by being present for things.

"Those buoys," Wren whispered. "What happens if you hit one?"

"Tear out your hull on the anchor chain. Or get snagged, dragged, and wrapped around the gantry. Or get shot, depending on the mood of the day." Tomas checked the depth. "So we don't hit one."

Ahead, the checkpoint resolved into detail. The gantry spanned the river on concrete pylons, hung with floodlights now dark in the daylight, and beneath it the water slowed in a long artificial pool where two gray patrol skiffs sat moored. On the bank, the station building squatted behind razor wire, and figures moved along its porch—four, five, six of them, and one figure standing apart at the edge of the dock, under an umbrella, although the rain hadn't started yet.

Tomas's stomach dropped through the deck plates. The umbrella. Of course. A man who carried an umbrella into a drought was a man who wanted you to know the rain was coming whether you liked it or not.

Zephyrine brought the *Kestrel* in toward the inspection pool at a crawl, hands easy on the wheel, face arranged into the mild blankness of a working skipper at the end of a long contract. Tomas stood at his shoulder with the manifest folder. Through the wheelhouse glass he watched the umbrella figure raise one hand—not waving, beckoning—and the patrol skiffs peeled away from their moorings to flank them, port and starboard, close enough that Tomas could see the mounted machine guns and the young men behind them trying to look older.

A voice crackled over the marine band, pleasant and precise:

"*Survey vessel, this is Meridian toll authority, Serpent Bend station. Cut your engine and prepare for inspection. Welcome to the Kalyani Gorge.*"

Welcome. As if they'd been expected. Because they had been.

* * *

They tied up at the inspection dock under the eyes of six hired security men with slung carbines, and Corvus Dain came down the station steps at a stroll, umbrella furled now, held like a walking cane. Up close he was exactly as Tomas remembered from the stories: neat, silver-templed, wearing a gray suit that the humidity would ruin and clearly not caring, because ruining suits was probably a tax deduction. He smiled at Zephyrine with genuine warmth, which was the worst thing about him.

"Master Winterbourne." Dain's voice carried easily across the water. "What a coincidence. And yet—not, I suspect, much of one. May I come aboard?"

"It's a toll station," Zephyrine said. "I assumed you'd inspect whether I liked it or not."

"Oh, I like you, Master Winterbourne. I've always liked you. You pay what you owe, eventually, and you never make a scene." Dain stepped onto the deck, and two of the security men followed at a respectful distance while the others remained ashore, weapons loose but visible. "Now. Let's see what the river is carrying today."

Odile emerged from the cabin with the folder, and Tomas watched Dain's attention slide to her and stick there for half a second too long. Recognition. Not surprise—recognition. They knew each other. Of course they did. Tomas filed that away in the growing drawer of things he would think about later, when there was time, when thinking wouldn't cost lives.

Dain read the manifest page by page, unhurried, while the pool lapped at the pilings and the gorge wind funneled cold air down on all of them. "Hydrological survey. Agricultural consortium. Gauges, flow meters, sediment cores." He glanced up. "Rather a lot of fuel drums for a survey boat."

"We're going upriver," Zephyrine said. "There's no fuel to buy upriver."

"There isn't much of anything upriver," Dain agreed pleasantly. "That's rather the point of it." He turned a page. "Your route takes you past the dam, then?"

"Past it. We stop well short. Our gauges are for the tributaries."

"Mm." Dain closed the folder and handed it back, and for one suspended moment Tomas thought—hoped—they were through. Then Dain smiled again, wider, and produced a small leather ledger from inside his jacket, along with a fountain pen. "But of course, procedure is procedure. Every vessel transiting Serpent Bend must be logged. Name of vessel, name of master, point of origin, declared destination, date and time." He uncapped the pen. "Declared destination, Master Winterbourne?"

"The tributary confluence," Zephyrine said. "Mile one-nineteen."

Dain wrote it down in beautiful script. Tomas watched each letter land on the page and understood, with a cold clarity, that the words didn't matter. What mattered was the entry itself: *Kestrel, E. Winterbourne, master, transit logged, 14:40.* Somewhere in a Syndicate archive, that line would sit in a file next to a photograph of a boat, and when the story of this summer was finally told—whatever story it turned out to be—this page would be exhibit A.

"There," Dain said, capping the pen. "You're official. Oh—one small thing." He reached into the ledger and tore out a carbon copy, pressing it into Zephyrine's hand. "Your receipt. Keep it. You'll want proof you passed through properly, in case anyone downstream asks questions." His eyes flicked, briefly, to Odile. "People do ask questions about boats that go up the Kalyani and don't come back."

"We plan to come back," Zephyrine said.

"Most people plan to." Dain descended the gangway, then paused, one foot on the dock, and looked back with the expression of a man sharing a confidence. "Master Winterbourne—a word of professional courtesy. The monsoon has accelerated. Landfall estimates have moved up. Whatever your consortium is paying you, I'd wager it isn't worth drowning for." He opened the umbrella—it still hadn't rained, and Tomas realized with a chill that it never needed to; the umbrella was a message, not a tool. "Do give my regards to the upper river. It remembers visitors."

The patrol skiffs backed off. The buoys were swung aside on their chains, groaning, and the channel beyond the gantry opened like a throat.

* * *

They ran the narrows in silence, all hands, the *Kestrel* bucking through the gorge's throat while Wren worked the sounding line and Priya nursed the engine and Tomas called depths off the chart from memory because his hands weren't steady enough to trust to paper. Only when the walls fell away and the river widened into the long brown reach beyond did Zephyrine ring the engine back to cruising speed and let the silence break.

"He logged us," Priya said from the engine hatch, soot on her cheek. "Every mile we make from here, he knows."

"He knew before we got here," Tomas said. He sat down heavily on the chart bench and rubbed his eyes. "That's what I keep trying to tell everyone. The roster, the posting, the welcome—he didn't build a net today. He built it weeks ago. Today he just showed us the knots."

"Then why let us through?" Wren asked. "If he knows, why not just take us?"

Tomas looked at Zephyrine, and Zephyrine looked at Odile, and Odile gazed out at the brown water with the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't enjoy.

"Because a toll log is worth more than a sunken boat," she said finally. "If we vanish upriver, we're a rumor. But if we pass his checkpoint, declare a destination, and then turn up somewhere we shouldn't be—" she tapped the carbon copy in Zephyrine's hand, "—that's evidence. Documented intent. Something he can put in front of a tribunal, or use to justify whatever he does to us next. Dain doesn't want to stop us. He wants to *build a case*. We just handed him the first page."

"So every mile we go," Wren said slowly, "we're writing his report for him."

Nobody answered that. The engine muttered. Somewhere ahead, thunder rolled down the gorge behind them—not the monsoon yet, just the mountains clearing their throats—but everyone on deck heard it the same way, as a countdown starting early.

Zephyrine folded the carbon copy once, twice, and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where a man might keep a debt or a dare.

"Then we make sure the rest of his report is boring," he said. "Tomas—new heading. We run dark from here. No transponder, no radio traffic except false position reports every six hours. Priya, I want the backup antenna rigged tonight. Odile—" he hesitated, and Tomas watched him choose the question carefully, "—how much further to your gauges?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. "Four days," she said. "Three, if we push."

"And landfall?"

"Moved up," Tomas said quietly. "Dain wasn't bluffing about that. My contact mentioned it too. The estimates shifted yesterday. We may have less time than even Odile thinks."

Zephyrine nodded slowly, looking upriver at the gathering clouds, and Tomas saw something settle in his face—the look of a man who has stopped weighing options and started accepting costs.

"Then we push," Zephyrine said.

That night, anchored in a side channel with no lights showing, Tomas lay awake in the chart room listening to the rain finally arrive—soft at first, then steady, drumming on the deck above him. He thought about the ledger entry. *Transit logged, 14:40.* Somewhere downstream, a silver-haired man in a ruined suit was reading that same line by lamplight, adding it to a file, drawing the net one knot tighter.

And somewhere upstream, in the dark, the river ran on toward the source, indifferent to all of them, carrying their names toward a place none of them were supposed to reach.

Tomas slept badly, and dreamed of bells.
