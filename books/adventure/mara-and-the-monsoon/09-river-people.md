## 9. River People

Priya smelled the settlement before she saw it—woodsmoke and river fish and something green, some crushed herb she hadn't encountered since her grandmother's kitchen in a country that had stopped existing on maps. She closed her eyes at the engine housing, let the smell move through her, and hated how much it undid her.

"Smoke means people," Wren said beside her. The kid had developed a habit of narrating observations like a junior officer reporting to a captain who hadn't asked. "People mean fuel, maybe. Or trouble."

"Both," Priya said. "Usually both."

They'd been running the middle Kalyani for six days since Serpent Bend, and the river had changed character the way rivers do when they stop caring whether you survive them. The gorge walls had fallen back into terraced hills, but the water stayed fast, braided around gravel bars that shifted with every rain squall. Tomas navigated by instinct and by the battered survey charts Odile had produced from somewhere, charts marked in a hand Priya didn't recognize, with annotations in three languages.

Now, as the channel bent north, the banks opened into a wide floodplain where the trees grew in deliberate rows—not wild growth but planted, tended. And among the trees, structures: stilt houses with steep thatched roofs, walkways connecting them like a nervous system, drying racks hung with fish, nets folded in careful bundles. A whole town built to be drowned and rebuilt, drowned and rebuilt, generation after generation.

"No flag," Zephyrine said from the helm behind her. She heard him rather than saw him; she kept her eyes on the water. "No Syndicate post. No toll."

"That's because there's no law here," Odile said. She'd come forward to stand at the rail, her camera case strapped tight across her body the way she always wore it, like an organ she couldn't afford to have removed. "The upper settlements were never surveyed properly. The Syndicate wrote them off as unprofitable sixty years ago. They govern themselves."

"Govern themselves how?" Wren asked.

"You'll see," Priya said.

Because she knew. Not this settlement specifically—but places like it. She'd grown up in one, before the war took the valley and the valley's people scattered her across three borders and two oceans of paperwork. Places outside the law didn't become lawless. They became something older than law. Something with teeth.

* * *

The mooring was a forest of pilings driven into the mud at the river's edge, and by the time they idled in, a dozen people stood on the walkways watching them with the flat patience of people who had seen every kind of boat arrive and knew exactly which kinds brought trouble.

A woman stepped forward onto the end platform. She was perhaps sixty, broad-shouldered, gray hair cut short and practical, wearing a rubber apron over clothes that had been mended so many times the original fabric was mostly memory. In her right hand she carried a long-handled gaff hook, held not threateningly but the way a magistrate holds a seal of office.

Zephyrine killed the engine. The silence after was enormous.

"Travelers," the woman called. Her accent was old-river, vowels rounded, consonants soft. "You come at the turn of the season. State your business and your names, and know that whatever you say will be weighed when the water speaks."

Priya felt the hairs rise on her arms. *When the water speaks.* She hadn't heard that phrase in thirty years.

"We're running upriver," Zephyrine answered. He had his merchant voice on, the easy one, but Priya could hear the effort under it. "We need fuel, provisions if you'll sell them, and shelter for two nights while we repair our steering linkage. We pay fair."

"Fair is decided here, not offered." The woman's eyes moved across each of them in turn—Zephyrine, Odile, Tomas, Wren, and last, longest, Priya herself. Something flickered in her face. "You." She pointed the gaff hook, gently, at Priya. "You've stood in a circle before."

It wasn't a question. Priya's mouth went dry. "Yes," she said. "Long ago. In the Vellankanni reaches, before the war."

The woman lowered the hook. Around the walkways, the watchers' posture changed—a loosening, a settling, like a pack deciding the strangers might be wolves but not mad ones. "Then you know our ways. You'll vouch for your crew?"

"I'll vouch," Priya said, and heard her own voice steady in a way that surprised her. "They're hard people and honest ones. Mostly honest," she amended, glancing at Odile, and got a dry look back that acknowledged the hit.

"Mostly honest will do. I am Sefora. This is Tern Landing. You'll tie at the third piling. You'll eat what we eat and work while you stay—the steering linkage can be fixed in our shed, and you'll help with the nets besides. And tonight—" Sefora paused, and the pause had weight, ritual weight, "—tonight there is a hearing. A dispute between two families over a drowned field. Strangers may attend. Strangers may not speak unless the circle asks them. Do you accept these terms?"

"We accept," Zephyrine said.

"Good." Sefora turned away, then stopped. "One more thing. There are men asking about a boat like yours. Three days ago, downriver. They wore no colors, but they paid in new scrip, and they photographed our landing with a machine." Her eyes came back to Zephyrine and held there. "We told them nothing. We tell everyone nothing. But you should know the river has ears lately, and the ears have money."

* * *

The shed smelled of tar and hot metal, and Priya lost herself in the work gladly, because work was the one language that never lied to her. The steering linkage was worse than Zephyrine had claimed—one of the universal joints had worn to a knife's edge of metal, and if it had failed in the gorge instead of here, they'd all be a story the river told itself. She stripped it, reamed it, and began fitting the replacement bushings Wren fetched for her, the kid moving with a competence that still startled her sometimes. Two months ago Wren hadn't known a spanner from a shackle. Now the kid anticipated her hand like a journeyman.

"Tomas says the hearing tonight is a big deal," Wren said, passing her a cotter pin. "He says they settle everything this way. Fights, debts, even killings."

"They do." Priya peened the pin and tested the joint. It swung smooth as silk. "Where I come from—came from—it was the same. No police came past the second rapids. No courts. So when two people had a grievance, the whole settlement sat in a circle and heard it out, and the elders ruled, and the ruling stuck because everyone had watched it get made. You can't bribe two hundred witnesses."

"What happens if someone doesn't accept the ruling?"

Priya set down the hammer. "That's the part outsiders never understand. The punishment isn't jail. It's the circle turning its back. No one trades with you. No one shelters you when the flood comes. No one buries your dead." She looked at Wren, who had gone very still. "Out here, exile is death with extra steps. So people accept the rulings. Even the hard ones. Especially the hard ones."

Wren was quiet a moment, turning a spare bolt over in small fingers. "Is that why you left? Your place?"

"The war left. I just followed it out." Priya picked the hammer back up. "Different thing. Same result."

But it wasn't the same thing, and she knew it, and the knowing sat in her chest all through the evening meal—fish stew with river greens and a dark grain bread, eaten at long tables under a roof open to the air, surrounded by people who laughed easily and watched carefully. Children ran between the tables and were caught and kissed and released. An old man played a stringed instrument with only four strings left and made them sound like forty. Odile sat at the far end, saying little, eating little, her eyes doing their constant inventory of exits and valuables. Tomas had found the musicians and was already half in love with a woman who repaired nets and laughed at his jokes in the wrong places. Zephyrine ate mechanically, his gaze returning again and again to the darkening river, and Priya recognized the look. He was counting miles in his head even while sitting still. The man measured his life in distance traveled, and she'd known men like that before, and none of them had ever arrived anywhere.

And Priya herself—Priya sat in the warmth and the noise and felt something in her crack open that she'd welded shut decades ago.

This was the smell of her childhood. Firelight on wet wood. The particular cadence of a community talking itself into being, night after night, argument after argument, meal after shared meal. She had spent thirty years telling herself she'd escaped something. Sitting here, she understood she'd been starving ever since.

* * *

The hearing convened at full dark, in a clearing ringed by lanterns, with the whole settlement standing in a great loose circle and the disputants in the center—an older man named Coram and a younger woman named Ilsa, whose families had farmed adjacent fields on the floodplain for two generations. The dispute, as Sefora laid it out with judicial dryness, concerned a levee Coram had raised along his field's edge after last year's flood. The levee had redirected the spring water into Ilsa's field, drowning her planted rows, and now both fields would yield nothing, and both families were hungry, and hunger made people say things that required a circle to untangle.

What struck Priya was not the dispute but the process. Anyone could speak. A boy of ten stood up and described, with devastating precision, exactly how the water had moved through the fields—he'd been fishing on the levee the day it breached. An ancient woman testified about where the river had run fifty years ago and where it would want to run again. Coram argued his levee protected the whole settlement's eastern flank. Ilsa argued she'd warned him, twice, in front of witnesses. The circle listened without interruption, which was more discipline than any parliament Priya had ever read about, and when the testimony thinned, Sefora conferred with three other elders—two men, one woman, all old enough to remember previous floods—and delivered the ruling.

Coram would breach his own levee, by his own hands, within three days. The settlement would help Ilsa replant from common stores. And Coram would owe Ilsa's family a share of his next harvest, not as punishment but as acknowledgment, spoken aloud, that his protection had cost her her crop.

Coram's face went gray. For a moment Priya thought he'd refuse—and she saw the circle register the same possibility, saw two hundred people lean almost imperceptibly toward the center, ready. Then Coram nodded, once, and said, "I'll breach it tomorrow at first light," and the circle exhaled, and someone started the instrument again, and the hearing dissolved into an ordinary evening as smoothly as water closing over a stone.

Zephyrine found her afterward at the edge of the lantern light. He looked shaken in a way Priya suspected he'd deny.

"He just agreed," Zephyrine said quietly. "To tear down his own work. To admit fault in front of everyone."

"That's the price of belonging here. The truth costs, but it's paid in public, so nobody carries it alone." She watched him absorb that. "You could learn from it."

"I'm not staying."

"I know." She said it gently. "That's what worries me."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she decided to say the rest of it, because the circle had loosened something in her and because he needed to hear it before the river got any higher.

"Zeph. I vouched for this crew today. In front of people whose judgment doesn't bend. Understand what that meant to me—I haven't belonged anywhere in thirty years, and I spent my first hour here spending that belonging on all of you." She held his eyes. "So hear my warning the way they'd give it. Whatever deal you made with Odile, whatever debt is chasing us upriver—when the bill comes due, and it will come due, don't try to pay it twice. Some prices, once paid, buy you free. Others just buy the seller another chance at you. Know which kind you're carrying."

"And which kind are you carrying?" he asked.

She smiled, and it hurt. "Mine already cleared. That's the only reason I can afford to warn you."

* * *

She couldn't sleep. Past midnight she walked the walkways, barefoot, listening to the settlement breathe—the creak of stilts, the lap of water beneath everything, a baby crying and being hushed. Tern Landing was built to be drowned. Every house could be taken apart in a day and rebuilt on higher ground, and every person here carried the knowledge that the river would take it all eventually and that they would build it again anyway. That was the faith the place ran on. Not defiance. Continuance.

She reached the end of the main walkway and stopped.

Odile was there, at the far rail, in the dark, with a small light shielded under her coat. Not photographing the settlement. Photographing papers—documents spread on the rail planks, shot page by page with quick, practiced movements. And even at this distance, even in that sliver of light, Priya caught the letterhead on the topmost page, and her stomach dropped through the walkway boards into the black water below.

She knew that crest. Everyone from the drowned valleys knew that crest. The Meridian Syndicate's seal, and beneath it, printed in faded ink on what looked like engineering correspondence, a date twelve years old.

Twelve years. The age of the flood that had ended Zephyrine's old crew. The age of the disaster that had scattered half the river towns and filled the Syndicate's ledgers with seized land.

Odile sensed her and snapped the light off, but too late. They stood in the dark regarding each other, two women who trusted no one, and Priya understood suddenly that she was holding something explosive—and that the informant's messages she'd been forced to send downriver, the position reports squeezed out of her by threats against her brother's family, had never mentioned any of this. Couldn't have. She hadn't known.

"You saw nothing," Odile said softly.

"I saw everything," Priya answered. "That's the problem."

Downriver, faint as a heartbeat, thunder rolled—the monsoon announcing itself, still distant, still patient. But closer than yesterday. Closer every day.
