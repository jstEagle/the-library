## 10. Mara's Reach

Tomas came forward at dawn without being asked and stood beside the helm, saying nothing for a long time. That was how Zephyrine knew they'd arrived.

He'd been watching the banks change all morning—the jungle thinning to red clay, the cliffs closing in, the water going from brown to a strange glassy green that gave back the sky like a mirror. He'd known this stretch was coming since Serpent Bend. He'd charted around it in his head three times, looking for an alternate channel, and three times the charts had come back the same: there was no other way through. The Kalyani didn't offer detours here. It offered one door, and you walked through it or you turned around.

"Water's high," Tomas said finally. "Faster than the survey says."

"Faster is fine."

"Faster is faster." Tomas adjusted his glasses. "You want me on the wheel?"

"I'm fine."

"You said that at breakfast, when you put salt in your coffee."

Zephyrine looked down at the mug in the cup holder. Salt in his coffee. He couldn't even remember doing it. Twelve years of driving through the night and he'd never once lost the thread of his own hands, and now—

"I'm fine," he said again, and Tomas had the good grace not to argue.

Behind them, Odile sat on the cargo hatch with her maps spread across her knees, weighting the corners with engine parts. She'd been quiet since the gorge. Quiet since the bells. Wren was up in the bow on watch, one hand on the rail, scanning the water ahead with the seriousness of someone who'd been promoted from deckhand to something else and hadn't been told what yet. Priya was below with the engines, which were running rougher than she liked, which meant they were running exactly as rough as Zephyrine expected.

The river bent left. And then it bent right. And then Zephyrine saw the rock.

It was still there. Of course it was still there. Rivers don't move their bones for anyone's grief. A granite fin rising out of the green water like the dorsal ridge of some drowned animal, split down the middle by twelve years of current, and he knew—knew before Tomas said it, knew the way you know your own name—that the locals called it the Split Tooth now, but twelve years ago it had just been a rock, unnamed, unremarkable, and Mara had hit it at full throttle in the dark because the storm had knocked out every light on the boat and Dev had been shouting bearings and nobody could hear anything over the rain.

"Mara's Reach," Tomas said quietly. "That's what the river people call it. I asked last night, at the mooring. They have a name for everything on this river. This stretch—" He stopped. Started again. "They say the water runs honest here. No lies in the current. It goes where it goes."

"That's a kind way of putting it."

"It's the only way they put it."

The gorge narrowed. The walls rose on either side, sheer and dripping, hung with vines that trailed in the water like rigging. The sound changed—the whole river's voice deepening as the channel squeezed, a low throat-note that Zephyrine felt in the deck plates under his boots. Ahead, white water stitched across the green where the current found submerged rock, and beyond that, half-hidden in spray, the first real rapid: a long descending chute between two walls no wider than the boat and a half.

His hands were on the wheel. He was sure his hands were on the wheel. But somewhere between seeing the Split Tooth and seeing the chute, the connection between his eyes and his arms had gone out like a breaker tripped, and he was standing at the helm of a boat moving fifteen knots toward stone, and he was not steering.

*Twelve years,* he thought, absurdly. *Twelve years of miles.*

"Wren," Tomas said. Not loud. Just clear, the way you'd call a name across a quiet room.

Wren came back along the rail fast, no questions, and Zephyrine heard the kid's footsteps and thought distantly, *good crew, that, comes when called, doesn't ask why*—and then small strong hands closed over his on the wheel and eased them off, and Wren stepped into the space he'd occupied and became the helm.

"Talk to me, navigator," Wren said.

"Chute's two hundred meters," Tomas said. "Enter left of center. There's a hole mid-channel—see the boil?—hold right of it, then the current will push you left into the eddy behind the Split Tooth. Don't fight the push. Let it set you up for the drop."

"Don't fight the push," Wren repeated. "Let it set me up."

"Throttle's yours. Keep her at fourteen until the entry. Then idle down and let the river do the work."

"The river does the work," Wren said. "I just hold the wheel straight."

And then they were in it.

Zephyrine stood at the back of the cockpit with his hands empty and watched a seventeen-year-old run the reach where Mara died, and it was the strangest hour of his life, because part of him was screaming and part of him was taking notes. *Good line,* the note-taking part observed, as Wren threaded the entry gap with maybe a meter to spare on each side. *Kid reads water like it's large print.* The screaming part had nothing useful to add. It just said Mara's name over and over in a voice that wasn't a voice, the way it had for twelve years, usually at three in the morning in whatever rented room or truck cab he'd parked himself in that month.

The boil came up fast—a rolling cauldron of collapsed water, big as the boat—and Wren held right of it, calm, throttling down exactly when Tomas said, letting the current swing the stern left, using the river's own strength to line up the drop. Spray came over the bow. The gorge roared. And then they were through the chute and dropping, the whole hull lifting and slamming, once, twice, and settling into the wide green eddy behind the Split Tooth, rocking gently, the roar falling away behind them like a door closing.

Nobody spoke. The engine idled. Water dripped off the rails.

Wren let go of the wheel and stepped back, and looked at Zephyrine—not triumphant, not scared, just looking, the way you look at a locked door you've just realized you can open—and said, "She's yours," and went forward again to the bow, and that was all.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar in the lee of the gorge wall, a shelf of pale stone above the flood line where the river people had stacked firewood years ago and the wood was still dry under its tarp of leaves. Priya got a fire going. Odile retreated to her maps, which was her way of giving privacy without admitting she was giving it. Tomas cooked rice with too much chili, which was his way of saying *I know, and we're not talking about it.*

Zephyrine found Wren sitting at the edge of the gravel, feet in the shallows, skipping stones with more enthusiasm than skill.

"River people say the water runs honest here," Zephyrine said, sitting down. "Tomas tell you that?"

"He told me. I don't know if I believe it. Seems like a lot of water to trust."

"It's not the water you trust. It's yourself in it." He picked up a flat stone, weighed it. "You did good today."

"You froze."

The word landed clean, no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. Zephyrine nodded. "I froze."

"Why?"

He skipped the stone. Three hops, then the green water took it. Out on the current, the last light was dying off the Split Tooth, turning the granite the color of old blood.

"Her name was Mara," he said. "Mara Okonjo. She was our pilot. Best I ever saw—better than me, and I don't say that about many people, and I especially didn't say it about her while she was alive, because we were both too stupid and too proud, and that's the first thing you should know about her. That we wasted years being rivals when we could have been friends, and by the time we figured it out, we'd figured it out too late to do anything with it except be friends quietly, on a boat, on this river."

Wren had gone still. Kids always did that when an adult started talking like this—they sensed the weight coming and braced for it. But Wren didn't leave. That was the thing about Wren. Everyone else on this crew had signed on out of debt or need or some bargain struck in the dark. Wren had signed on because Wren wanted to be on the water, and wanting was rarer than Zephyrine had remembered.

"There were six of us then," he went on. "Me, Mara, Dev—he was our engineer, Priya would have liked him, same way of talking to machines like they're stubborn relatives—and four others who scattered after and I've never found again, or never looked hard enough. We ran cargo up and down the Kalyani. Anything legal, most things barely legal, nothing that hurt anybody. It was a good life. It was the only life I ever had that fit."

The fire crackled behind them. Somewhere up the gorge, water dripped in slow rhythm off the cliffs.

"Monsoon season, twelve years ago. We got caught in the lower reach—you passed through it your second week aboard, the wide part with the sandbars. Forecast said landfall was two days out. It was wrong by thirty hours. Storms do that sometimes. The river rises faster than any forecast, and if you're on it when it does—" He shrugged. "We tried to make the takeout at Kalipur. Didn't make it. Night came down early under the storm cloud, black as a pocket, every instrument on the boat shorted out, and Mara was driving by feel and memory, and she almost did it. She almost did it, Wren. She had us in the channel. I was on the bow with a torch trying to read the water, and I saw the Split Tooth maybe two seconds before we hit it, and I screamed a bearing, and she heard me, and she turned us, and it wasn't enough. The current was running eight knots against us. You can't outrun eight knots of river in a loaded boat."

"What happened to her?"

"She went into the water when the hull opened. Dev went in after her. That's the whole story. That's the story I've told myself a thousand times, anyway—she went in, he went in after her, the river took them both, and the rest of us climbed the bank and stood in the rain and watched the boat break apart, and in the morning the storm had moved on and the sun came out like nothing happened, and we buried what we could find and swore off the upper river and scattered like spilled coins." He turned the next stone over in his fingers. "I kept moving after that. Never stayed anywhere longer than a season. Told myself it was the work, the contracts, the Syndicate sniffing around. Truth is I was running from this exact gravel bar. Because if I stopped anywhere—if I built anything, if I let a place become mine—then it could be taken the way she was taken, and I decided twelve years ago that nothing was going to be mine ever again. Cheaper that way. Safer."

"Is it?"

"No." He laughed, short and surprised at himself. "No, it's the most expensive thing I've ever done. It cost me every friend I had. It nearly cost me this crew, because strays can smell a man who won't stay, and they don't sign on with him unless they're running from something worse." He looked sideways at Wren. "What are you running from?"

"Nothing. I told you. I wanted the work."

"You wanted the work on *this* boat, going *up* this river, with a captain everyone in the port bars calls cursed. Nobody wants that work, Wren."

Wren picked up a stone and held it, turning it, not throwing it. For a long moment the kid said nothing, and Zephyrine thought, *fair enough, I asked a question I had no right to,* and was preparing to let it go when Wren said, quietly:

"My mother worked the reservoir towns. Before they flooded them. She used to say the water took her house and the company took her claim and nobody ever paid for either. So when I heard a crew was going upriver to the source—to where the dam is—I—" The stone turned over once more in those small hands. "I wanted to see it. What's at the top of all this water. What it's for."

Zephyrine stared out at the darkening river. *Where the dam is.* The words sat in his chest like a swallowed coal. Odile's dam. Odile's documents, photographed in secret. Odile's questions he'd refused to answer for himself.

"Well," he said. "Maybe we'll both get our answer."

He threw the stone. One skip. The river took it, honest water, going where it goes.

* * *

He woke past midnight to the sound of the fire being fed and found Odile crouched at the coals, feeding them sticks one at a time with the precision of a woman defusing something.

"Couldn't sleep?" she said, without looking up.

"No."

"Me neither." She poked the coals. "Tomas told me where we were today. Before we entered the reach. I want you to know that. I knew, and I brought us through it anyway, because there's no other channel, and I've decided I'm done lying to you by omission. It's a bad habit from my old life. I'm trying to quit."

"You're doing poorly at it."

"I'm doing *better* at it. There's a difference." She looked up at him then, firelight in her eyes. "Zephyrine. When we get to the source—when you see what's up there—I need you to keep an open mind. Whatever you think I am, whatever you think this is about. Promise me that much."

"You've made me promise stranger things."

"I haven't made you promise anything. That's the problem. You've followed me up a forbidden river on a handshake and a number with commas in it, and you haven't asked me for one guarantee." She stood, brushing ash off her knees. "Ask me one. Tonight. While it's still cheap."

Zephyrine looked at her across the coals, this woman who had appeared out of his past with a contract like a knife wrapped in silk, and heard himself ask the only question that mattered anymore, the one that had been forming since the drowned town, since the photographs, since Wren said *where the dam is*:

"When we find what you're looking for—does it bring them back? Mara and Dev. Does any of it give them back?"

Odile held his gaze, and for once she didn't perform, didn't charm, didn't deflect. "No," she said. "Nothing gives them back. But it can make sure it never happens to anyone else's crew. That's all I have. It's not nothing."

Upstream, far up the black gorge, thunder rolled—or something that sounded like thunder—and the river rose a finger's width against the gravel bar, patient and honest, carrying something toward them neither of them could name yet.
