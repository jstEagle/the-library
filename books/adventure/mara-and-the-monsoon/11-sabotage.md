## 11. Sabotage

Wren woke to the smell of diesel.

Not the ordinary smell—the boat always smelled faintly of diesel, of engine grease and river water and old rubber, a smell that had become, over three weeks on the Kalyani, something like home. This was different. This was sharp and wrong, the smell of fuel where fuel should not be, pooling where it should not pool.

She lay in her bunk in the dark for a moment, listening. The *Kestrel* was tied up below the second set of rapids above Serpent Bend, in a narrow cut of jungle the crew had named the Pocket because it fit them like one—steep walls on both sides, a shelf of gravel barely wide enough for the hull, water sliding past black and fast even at night. Tomas had wanted to push farther before dark. Zephyrine had said no. Zephyrine said no more often now, Wren had noticed. He was watching the banks like a man counting enemies.

The smell got stronger. She swung out of her bunk barefoot, pulled on the canvas jacket she slept with because the river nights were cold even this close to the equator, and went aft.

The engine hatch was open.

Wren stopped. She knew she hadn't left it open. Priya closed the hatch every night like a ritual—checked the lashings, wiped the dipstick, latched the cover, patted the housing twice the way you'd pat a horse's neck. Priya's rituals were the most reliable thing aboard. The hatch stood open now by six inches, and beneath it, in the bilge shadow, something gleamed.

Diesel. A slow silver thread of it, running along the frame seam toward the drain.

Wren crouched. She didn't touch anything—Priya had taught her that first week: *if something's wrong on my engine, look, don't fix, come get me.* But looking told her plenty. The primary fuel line's coupling nut sat loose. Not broken, not stripped—loose, backed off maybe two full turns, enough to weep under pressure and then let go entirely when the engine hammered through rapids. And there was something else. A rag stuffed into the drip tray, dry on top, soaked underneath. Someone had been here long enough to be careful about it.

Her mouth went dry. She looked up at the deck, at the four sleeping shapes—Zephyrine in the bow under the tarp he never fully zipped, Odile's cabin shut, Tomas rolled in his blanket near the winch, Priya's hammock empty because Priya had taken first watch on the bank.

Four people aboard. One of them had done this.

* * *

She woke Priya first. It wasn't a decision so much as a reflex; Priya was the engine, and the engine was bleeding.

Priya came down the ladder without a word, knelt, put two fingers into the wet tray, and held them up to the moonlight. Her face did something complicated and quick, like weather crossing a valley.

"When?" she said.

"Maybe an hour ago? Maybe less. The rag's still damp underneath."

Priya looked at the rag a long time. Then she looked at Wren, and what was in her face made Wren step back half an inch.

"You're sure nobody came aboard."

"Nobody came aboard." Wren heard her own voice go flat and certain. "The gravel's soft on both sides. I'd have seen tracks in the morning anyway. Nobody came aboard from outside."

"Then—" Priya stopped herself. Wren watched her stop herself, watched her swallow whatever the next word was, and understood that the next word had been a name.

They woke Zephyrine together.

He listened standing at the hatch with his arms crossed, and Wren watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done, watched it land on him in stages: the loose nut, the rag, the open hatch, the four sleepers. His jaw worked once. When he spoke, his voice was quiet in the way that scared her more than shouting would have.

"Wake the others. Both of them. Here, not on deck."

* * *

It was ugly from the first minute.

Tomas arrived with his blanket still around his shoulders like a cloak, blinking, and Odile arrived dressed, boots laced, hair braided—which struck Wren as strange until she remembered that Odile Vasseur probably slept ready for exactly this kind of moment. Zephyrine laid it out flat: fuel line loosened, sometime after midnight, no sign anyone came aboard from shore.

Silence. The river filled it, sliding past the hull with its endless low voice.

Then Odile said, "Where were you, Tomas?"

And everything cracked.

"I was asleep," Tomas said. "Same as everyone."

"Everyone except whoever wasn't." Odile's voice was level, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "You had the last watch rotation. You were on deck until midnight."

"I was on deck until midnight, yes, and then I woke Zephyrine's relief—except there is no relief, because we're four people doing six jobs, so I went to my blanket like a sensible man and slept."

"The coupling was backed off clean. Two turns, no tools dropped, no scratches on the nut. That's someone who knows engines."

"And you'd know how that looks," Tomas shot back, "because you've been down in the engine compartment twice this week. I've seen you. Asking Priya questions about the fuel system, the range, the reserves—"

"Because I'm paying for this expedition and I'd like to know if we can outrun trouble! Which, incidentally, we now cannot, since our reserve will be leaking into the bilge by morning!"

"That's convenient," Tomas said. "That's very convenient. Sabotage discovered the same week you start asking how much fuel we carry."

Wren saw Odile go still. Not guilty-still. Something colder than that—the stillness of a person deciding how much of their real self to show. "Careful," Odile said softly.

"No, you be careful." Tomas's blanket had fallen off; he didn't seem to notice. He was pointing now, finger shaking slightly, and Wren realized with a small cold shock that Tomas was frightened—not of Odile, but of the situation, of the idea itself. "Twelve years ago I ran this river with a crew I trusted with my life. Two of them are dead. Now I'm on a boat where somebody opens the engine in the night, and the only person who benefits from us being stranded in Syndicate country is the person who led the Syndicate straight to Serpent Bend—"

"That toll post would have flagged any boat," Odile snapped. "I got us through it."

"Or you logged us through it. Or your friends logged us. How do we know what those papers really said, Odile? How do we know anything you—"

"Enough." Zephyrine's voice didn't rise. It just cut, like a knife laid across a rope. The silence afterward was total. Even the river seemed to hush.

He crouched at the hatch, picked up the rag between two fingers, and turned it over. Wren watched his face in the lamplight and saw nothing there at all, which was its own kind of frightening. He'd built that blankness, she thought. He wore it like armor worn smooth.

"Tomas. Did you touch this line?"

"No."

"Odile. Did you?"

"No."

"Do either of you believe each other?"

Neither answered. That was answer enough.

Zephyrine stood. He looked at each of them in turn—Tomas flushed and trembling, Odile carved out of ice, Priya with her arms wrapped around herself, Wren trying to make herself small against the bulkhead—and Wren watched him arrive somewhere none of them had yet.

"It wasn't any of you," he said.

* * *

They gathered on deck because no one could breathe below. Dawn was still an hour off; the gorge walls were black cutouts against a sky going grey-green, and mist moved on the water like something alive.

"It wasn't any of you," Zephyrine said again, "because it doesn't need to be. Think about what actually happened tonight. Somebody opened a hatch, backed off a nut, stuffed a rag so the leak wouldn't show until we were mid-rapid, and closed everything behind them. Quiet work. Careful work. Work done by someone who knew our watch schedule, knew Priya's habits, knew we'd be moored in the Pocket tonight."

"So it's one of us," Tomas said.

"No." Zephyrine's eyes moved across the water, not looking at any of them. "It's someone who *talks* to one of us. Or listens. Think back. The checkpoint at Serpent Bend—that enforcer read our papers and waved us through, and I told myself we'd beaten him. We hadn't. He logged us. He knew our heading, our draft, our schedule. And since Serpent Bend, who else has known where we'd moor each night?"

Nobody spoke. Wren felt the answer forming in her own chest before anyone said it, a cold stone dropping through her.

"The radio," Priya whispered.

"The nightly position call," Zephyrine said. "Every evening, same time, coded coordinates, like good professional runners. Except codes only work if both ends are honest." He finally looked at them. "Somebody aboard has been feeding our positions downstream. And tonight somebody decided we were getting too close to the source to leave running. So they gave us a fuel line that fails in white water. Clean. Deniable. If we'd gone down in the Mara's Reach rapids tomorrow, it would have been pilot error. River takes another boat. Nobody investigates a drowned crew."

Wren's skin crawled. She thought of the rag, damp under her fingers' memory, and of the fact that she had slept thirty feet from the person who'd placed it.

"But that means—" she started, and couldn't finish.

"It means," Zephyrine said quietly, "that the Syndicate has ears on this boat. Has had them since port, I'd guess. Bought or coerced—I've known them to use both. Debts. Family. Threats." He paused, and something crossed his face too fast for Wren to name. "So here's what happens now. Nobody accuses anybody. Nobody searches anybody's things. As far as all of you are concerned, this was a loose fitting, Priya found it, she'll re-torque the line and double-lash the spares, and we run tomorrow like nothing happened."

"And the informant?" Odile asked.

Zephyrine smiled, and it was the coldest thing Wren had ever seen him do. "The informant keeps informing. Right up until we decide what they inform."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone back to their bunks—or pretended to—Wren found Priya sitting on the engine housing with the hatch open, a lamp clipped to the frame, hands moving over the fuel line with a wrench and a torque gauge, checking and rechecking.

Wren sat down beside her on the cold metal. For a while neither of them said anything. Below them the river ran on, patient as ever, keeping all its secrets.

"Priya. Do you think it's true? What Zeph said?"

Priya didn't look up. "I think he's right that it wasn't one of us. I checked every fitting twice. Whoever did it knew exactly which nut and exactly how many turns." Her hands kept working, steady, precise. "I also think he's wrong about one thing."

"What?"

"He thinks mercy is strategy." Priya set down the wrench and finally looked at her, and in the lamplight her face was older than Wren had ever seen it. "I grew up around people like the Syndicate, Wren. People who buy other people. Sometimes they don't buy them with money. Sometimes they buy them with a sick mother, or a brother in a cell, or a village that burns if you don't cooperate. And the terrible thing—the thing nobody tells you—is that the person they buy can still love you. Can still mean every kind thing they ever said to you. Can still save your life tomorrow." She picked the wrench back up. "That's what makes them useful. And that's what makes them dangerous."

Wren hugged her knees. "What do we do?"

"We do what Zeph says. We run tomorrow like nothing happened." Priya tightened the coupling a final quarter-turn, then rested her palm flat on the engine housing, the way she did, the little ritual, the pat-pat Wren had never thought about until tonight. "But you keep your eyes open, girl. You sleep light. And if you ever see someone standing somewhere they shouldn't be—at night, alone, holding something—you don't confront them. You don't say a word to anyone but me or Zeph." She met Wren's eyes. "Because the moment that person knows we know, this boat becomes a much more dangerous place to be."

Wren nodded. She lay awake a long time after that, listening to the river and to the small sounds of the boat, cataloguing every creak, every footstep, every sigh of canvas. Somewhere aboard, one of the people she'd started to think of as hers had opened a hatch in the dark and tried to kill them all without ever touching a weapon.

Near dawn she finally drifted off, and dreamed of the flooded town they'd passed weeks ago, rooftops under green water, and doors opening underwater, and people she knew stepping out of them, smiling, waving her closer.

* * *

They cast off at first light into a river the color of gunmetal. The sky upstream had gone the bruised yellow-grey that meant weather stacking up over the high country, and the air had a pressure to it, a held-breath feeling Wren was learning to read.

Zephyrine took the helm himself. He ran the first rapids hard and clean, reading the water like print, and when they came through into the long straight reach below, he called back to Priya, casual as anything:

"How's the engine treating us?"

"Running sweet," Priya called back. "Fixed a loose fitting last night. Nothing serious."

"Good," Zephyrine said. "Keep her sweet."

And then, quieter, to no one—to the wheel, to the river, to himself, though Wren, coiling line ten feet away, caught every word:

"Let's see what you tell them tonight."

Ahead, the gorge narrowed, and the first far-off sound reached them across the water—a thin, ringing, metallic chime, repeated, many-voiced, like a thousand small bells hung in the wind.

Tomas went pale at the bow. "That's the Gorge of Bells," he said. "We shouldn't be hearing that yet. We're a day early."

Zephyrine's hands didn't move on the wheel. "No," he said. "We're exactly on time. They're the ones who are early."

The bells rang on, getting louder.
