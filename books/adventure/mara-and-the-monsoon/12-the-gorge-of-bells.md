## 12. The Gorge of Bells

Tomas counted the bells before he counted the dangers.

That was the navigator in him. Give him a chart and he'd find the flaw in it, the sandbar nobody marked, the channel that shifted with every flood. So when the gorge opened ahead of them—narrow as a throat, walls rising sheer and wet on either side—he didn't look at the water first. He looked at the cliffs.

Bells. Hundreds of them. Strung on rusted wire and braided rope, hung from ledges and dead trees wedged into crevices, from iron stakes driven into stone by hands long gone. Copper bells, brass bells, bells cut from fuel drums and shell casings and the flattened hoods of cars. Some no bigger than a thimble. Some the size of a man's chest. They turned slowly in the wind that funneled up the gorge, and they did not ring so much as *breathe*—a low, restless shimmer of sound, like a crowd murmuring before something happens.

"The river people," Priya said at his shoulder. She'd gone quiet since the settlement, two days back, and when she spoke now her voice had a weight it hadn't carried before. "They hang them after a drowning. One bell for each life the river took."

"How many drownings does that make?" Wren asked.

Tomas followed her gaze up the cliff face and did some arithmetic he didn't like.

"More than we want to think about," he said.

The *Kestrel* sat low in the water at the mouth of the gorge, engine idling against the current. Ahead, the river narrowed to maybe thirty meters of churning white between walls that would shear their hull open like a tin can if Tomas got the line wrong. And there was no getting the line right—not at this water level, not with the monsoon pushing the river up half a foot a day. Every rapid below them had been runnable. This one was a meat grinder.

"Portage," Zephyrine said from the helm. He said it the way other men said *surgery*. "There's an old haul road on the left bank. River people used it to drag boats around the falls. Odile's charts show it."

"Odile's charts," Priya muttered.

Tomas understood the sentiment. Six days upriver and their employer was still a locked box with excellent posture. But she'd also stood the night watches without complaint, and two days ago in the shallows she'd waded chest-deep to help free the propeller without being asked, so Tomas kept his suspicions fed but on a short leash.

Zephyrine brought the boat alongside the left bank where a shelf of gravel offered footing. The wind through the gorge picked up, and for one clear moment all the bells moved together—a wave rolling up the cliff face—and the sound they made was almost like language.

Wren shivered. "It's like they're talking about us."

"They're not talking about us," Tomas said. "They're just old."

He wasn't sure he believed it either.

* * *

The haul road was a scar cut into the left bank, switchbacking up and over the worst of the falls. In places it was genuine road, graded stone wide enough for a cart. In places it was a goat track with a drop on one side that went down and down into white water. The boat came apart into sections—hull, cabin, engine cradle—and eight trips' worth of gear besides. They'd done portages before. None like this.

By midmorning the sun found the bottom of the gorge and turned it into a kiln. Sweat ran into Tomas's eyes as he muscled the engine cradle around a bend, and the bells overhead caught the light and threw it back in fragments, so the whole gorge glittered like it was full of broken glass.

"Left side's undercut," Wren called from ahead. She'd taken point without anyone assigning it, picking her way along the road with the sureness of someone who'd grown up climbing things she shouldn't have. "Big slab's cracked. Stay right of the cairn."

"Right of the cairn," Tomas relayed, and watched Zephyrine adjust his path without looking up. The captain hadn't been right since Mara's Reach. He worked like a man underwater—competent, tireless, somewhere else. Tomas had seen it before, twelve years ago, in the weeks after the flood. He'd hoped never to see it again.

Odile came up the trail behind him with a crate of instruments on her back, breathing hard but steady. "How far to the put-in?"

"Two kilometers of this. Then the road drops back to the water above the falls."

"And the Syndicate?"

Tomas had been asking himself the same question since Serpent Bend. The enforcer—the polite one, the one with the umbrella who'd logged their passage like a man entering figures in a ledger—knew exactly where they were. The question was what he intended to do about it, and when.

"Behind us," Tomas said. "But rivers only run one direction. So is everything behind us."

She nodded like that was the answer she'd expected, which told Tomas she knew more about being chased than she'd admitted.

They were halfway up the second switchback when Wren stopped walking.

Tomas saw it in her shoulders first—the sudden stillness of her, head cocked, the way game animals go quiet. Then he heard it too, under the murmur of the bells: a sound that didn't belong. A metallic click, rhythmic, deliberate. Somewhere above them.

"Captain," he said, low.

Zephyrine set down his load without a word. Twenty years of running rivers had taught them both the same vocabulary of stillness.

"Everyone off the trail," Zephyrine breathed. "Into the rocks. Now."

They scattered. Tomas pulled Wren down behind a slab of granite as the first shot cracked off the cliff wall above them and a bell somewhere overhead rang—one clean, bright note, nothing like its usual murmur—and then the gorge woke up.

* * *

Later, Tomas would try to reconstruct those ten minutes and fail. Memory under fire came home in pieces.

There were six of them, maybe seven—Syndicate hired guns in gray rain shells, positioned on the high ground where the haul road topped out, where any portaging crew would have to come to them. Professional enough to hold fire until the crew was strung out along the narrowest section. Not professional enough to account for Wren, who'd ranged ahead off-trail and now had the only angle on their flank.

"Contact left!" someone shouted, and the shooting went ragged.

Tomas pressed himself flat as rounds chewed the granite above his head, stone splinters stinging his cheek. Beside him, Priya was returning fire with the crew's single rifle, disciplined three-round bursts, her face blank with concentration. He'd known she could shoot. He'd never asked why. Some questions you saved for calmer rivers.

"Fuel drums!" Odile shouted from cover near the first load. "They're shooting the drums!"

And they were. Tomas understood a heartbeat later, with a lurch: the guns weren't trying to kill them. Killing them meant bodies, inquiries, questions on a river where the Syndicate's authority was already fraying. No—they were destroying cargo. Fuel, food, the spare propeller, the medical kit. Cripple the boat and let the river do the rest. It was a colder arithmetic than murder, and it was working. He could smell diesel already, sharp and wrong, mixing with cordite and hot stone.

"We have to move the loads or lose them," Zephyrine said. He'd appeared at Tomas's elbow, flat on his stomach behind the same slab, and there was something in his voice Tomas hadn't heard in days—presence, the captain fully arrived in his own body. "Tomas. The road bends at the cairn. If you and I can get the last two crates around that bend, they're below their sightline."

"It's forty meters of open trail."

"I know what it is."

Wren's rifle cracked twice from the rocks above—she'd gotten hold of Priya's spare—and one of the gray shapes up top stumbled backward out of sight. "Go!" her voice came down, thin and fierce. "I've got them pinned!"

"She's fourteen," Tomas said.

"She's covering fire," Zephyrine said, and went.

Tomas went with him. Forty meters of open trail with bullets skipping off stone around them, hauling crates that weighed more than sin, and Tomas laughing—actually laughing, the mad kind, the kind that comes out when the body knows it might be buying its last minutes and wants to spend them loudly. They got the first crate around the bend. On the second trip, Zephyrine stumbled, and a round sparked off the rock an arm's length from his head, and Tomas grabbed his collar and dragged him the last five meters, and then they were both down behind stone, gasping, alive, the crates safe.

Then he heard Wren scream.

Not a battle cry. The other kind.

* * *

She'd pushed too far forward chasing her angle—that was how Tomas pieced it together afterward, from the position of things, the way you read a flood by its debris line. A gunner had flanked along the rim, gotten above her, and fired not to kill but to corner. Her footing went on the cracked slab she herself had warned them about, and she slid, catching the edge of the haul road with both hands, hanging above the drop to the falls.

Tomas reached her first. He dropped flat, stretched out over the broken stone, and gripped her wrist. Her boots kicked over empty air. Below her, the white water thundered, and the bells on the nearest ledge swung in the wash of the gunshot echoes, ringing and ringing.

"I've got you." His voice came out steadier than his hands. "I've got you, kid. Don't look down. Look at me."

Her face was white, streaked with dust, sixteen years of stubbornness suddenly very young. "I can't—I can't pull—"

"You don't have to. That's my job." He braced his feet against a boulder and hauled, inch by inch, his shoulder screaming. Priya was suddenly there beside him, gripping his belt, anchoring him, and together they drew Wren up over the lip and onto solid stone, all three of them collapsed in a heap, breathing like landed fish.

Above them, the firing stopped. Tomas lifted his head in time to see the gray shapes withdrawing along the rim, unhurried, mission accomplished. They'd gotten what they came for. Through the haze of diesel vapor drifting up from the shattered drums, he could see the slick rainbow stain spreading across the gravel where most of their reserve fuel had bled out into the ground.

"Anyone hit?" Zephyrine's voice, hoarse.

"Wren, nearly," Priya said. "Everything else, definitely."

They took inventory in the shadow of the cairn while the bells murmured overhead. Two fuel drums destroyed. Half the dry rations shredded. The medical kit holed—Priya salvaged maybe a third of it. The spare propeller was intact only because Zephyrine had moved it an hour earlier on a hunch no one had questioned. The engine cradle, thank God, sat below the sightline with the hull.

Nobody spoke for a while. Wren sat with her knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, watching the falls. When Tomas sat down next to her, she didn't look at him.

"I froze," she said. "At the end. My hands just—stopped."

"Your hands held on," Tomas said. "That's the part that counts. Everything else, we teach you."

She looked at him then, and something in her face settled, like a load properly lashed. "You came over that edge for me."

"That's the job too."

* * *

They finished the portage in silence broken only by work. It took until late afternoon. By the time the *Kestrel* was reassembled and relaunched in the calm water above the falls, the light had gone bronze and the gorge behind them was filling with shadow, the bells fading to a whisper at their backs.

They made camp on a gravel bar and ate cold food because cooking fuel was now a thing they measured in spoonfuls. Zephyrine stood apart, studying the river downstream, and Tomas joined him because that was also the job.

"They'll report we're crippled," Tomas said. "Low fuel, short supplies. The enforcer will know we either turn back or push on half-rations. He'll be waiting at whatever chokepoint comes next."

"He will." Zephyrine didn't take his eyes off the water. "So we don't give him a chokepoint. We run nights. Sleep in daylight, hidden. Priya can nurse the fuel—we'll make it."

"To the source?"

"To the truth," Zephyrine said, and the word came out of him with a weight that made Tomas finally turn and look at him properly. Something had changed on Mara's Reach, and more of it had changed today. The man standing at the rail wasn't running anymore. "Whatever it costs."

Tomas looked back upstream, toward the darkening gorge and its thousand small voices. Twelve years ago he'd sworn off this river, and grief had been the price of admission, and he'd paid it and walked away. Now he was standing on a gravel bar with half a tank of fuel and a crew of strays, heading straight back into the thing that had broken all of them.

"Fear's a funny current," he said. "You can fight it or you can steer by it."

Zephyrine almost smiled. "Since when are you a philosopher?"

"Since I started navigating for a man who steers by worse."

Below them, the river ran black and quick toward the sea, carrying everything it carried. Above, the last light caught the highest bells, and for a moment the whole gorge rang softly, like something being decided.

Tomas slept that night with the chart case under his head and the rifle within reach, and dreamed not of falling but of holding on—which was new, and which felt, in its battered way, like progress.

Two days later, at dawn, Priya came out of the radio shack pale as paper and said the words that ended the easy part of the journey:

"Landfall in four days. And the convoy's crossed Serpent Bend."
