## 13. Half-Truths at the Fire

Odile Vasseur had told lies for a living once, and she still knew the anatomy of one. A good lie had a spine—something true at its center, load-bearing, so that when you pressed on it, it held. Everything else was flesh draped over bone.

She had been draping flesh over this particular bone since the port city. *I'm an independent surveyor. I'm documenting hydrology. The gauges are a matter of professional interest.* Each statement true in isolation, each one a lie in composition, and every mile upriver the flesh had grown thinner while the bone showed through.

Tonight, sitting at the fire on the gravel bar below the gorge they'd have to portage tomorrow, watching Zephyrine Winterbourne clean his boots with the methodical patience of a man who measured his whole life in small controllable tasks, she decided the flesh was done.

The others had turned in. Wren was curled in the boat's shadow like a comma, one hand still wrapped around the bailer handle even in sleep—a kid who'd learned early that rivers took things and didn't itemize what they kept. Tomas snored with the dignity of a man who believed sleep was a contract he honored scrupulously. Priya's hammock swayed empty; Odile had seen her walk upriver along the bank twenty minutes ago, alone, the way she did most nights, as if checking whether the water remembered her.

That left the fire, the rain just beginning again in fine needles against the tarp, and Zephyrine.

"Your fuel lines," Odile said. "You've been thinking about them all day."

"I've been thinking about a lot of things all day." He didn't look up from the boot. "Sabotage. Toll posts. Why a woman with money enough to hire any guide on the coast hired the one man whose crew swore off this river."

"You came anyway."

"You paid enough that I could have come anyway."

"That's not why you came."

He set the boot down then, finally, and looked at her across the fire. Twelve years of miles lived in that face—not age exactly but accumulation, sediment laid down year over year until the original contours of him were buried somewhere underneath. She'd known him when those contours were still visible: young, arrogant, faster on the water than anyone she'd ever raced, certain the river was a thing to be beaten rather than survived. She'd hated him a little. She'd trusted him completely. Both facts had outlasted the rivalry.

"No," he said. "It isn't. But you don't get to audit my reasons, Vasseur. Not when yours are sealed in crates you photograph at two in the morning."

So she'd been caught. Of course she had. She'd known it the moment his shadow crossed the cargo hold three nights ago and she'd chosen to keep shooting rather than hide the camera—because hiding would have confirmed everything, and continuing had been a kind of dare. *See me. Ask me.* He hadn't asked. Until now.

She reached into her jacket—the movement made his hand drift toward the knife on his belt, and she noted that too, filed it away—and drew out a slim folio, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with cord. She set it on the gravel between them, equidistant from the fire and from him.

"What's that?"

"The truth. Or the closest thing to it I can carry without it burning a hole through the hull."

He didn't move. "You've had it the whole time."

"I've had pieces of it the whole time. The rest is upstream." She nodded toward the black wall of the gorge, where the river ran narrow and loud, and beyond it, two days' hard travel, the source basin where the Kalyani began its life under the shadow of the great dam. "Zephyrine. Before you open that, I need you to hear how it starts. Because if you open it angry, you'll do something stupid with it, and I need you not stupid. I need you the way you were at Serpent Bend—cold, precise, terrifying. That man got us past a toll post with forged papers and a straight face. That man I can work with."

"And the other man?"

"The other man is going to want to throw me in the river."

"Promising," he said dryly. But he shifted forward, elbows on knees, and waited. It was the waiting that undid her a little—he'd always been able to wait, when it mattered. Impatient about everything except the things that counted.

"I was Syndicate," she said.

The fire popped. Somewhere up the bank, water dripped from a branch onto stone, keeping its own slow time.

"Meridian Syndicate, Infrastructure Division, Records and Compliance. Eleven years." She kept her voice level, the way she'd been trained to deliver numbers that ruined people. "You know what Records and Compliance does? We don't build anything. We build the paper that says what was built, what it cost, who approved it, and who gets blamed if it fails. I was very good at it. I could make a catastrophic engineering failure read like weather."

His face didn't change. That was the worst part. She'd braced for rage and gotten stillness instead, and stillness in Zephyrine Winterbourne was a loaded thing.

"Go on," he said.

"Twelve years ago there was a project. You'll know it by another name—the flood, the monsoon, the act of God. Officially, the Harrow Bend levee failed because of rainfall exceeding design parameters. Hundred-year event. Tragic, unforeseeable, condolences to the families, here's a settlement agreement with a confidentiality clause, sign here."

Harrow Bend. His hometown. She watched the name land in him like a stone dropped into deep water—the surface staying smooth while everything beneath rearranged itself.

"I wrote the compliance summary," she said. "My name is on the document that closed the investigation. I certified that the failure was consistent with natural causes." She untied the cord on the folio, slowly, giving him time to stop her. He didn't. "Three years ago I found out I was wrong. Or rather—I found out I'd been right about everything except the part that mattered. The rainfall data was real. The storm was real. But the levee didn't fail because of the storm. It failed because it was allowed to fail. Maintenance deferrals logged and approved. Emergency gate protocols overridden. And the reservoir above it—" she tapped the folio— "drawn down and refilled on a schedule that had nothing to do with safety and everything to do with making the valley downstream cheap. Uninhabitable. Buyable."

"Why?" His voice came out flat. "Why would anyone—"

"Because dead towns don't litigate. Because the Syndicate wanted the valley—the water rights, the corridor for the upper river, the land. A disaster is cheaper than negotiation. They'd done it before, smaller scale, twice that I found. Harrow Bend was the largest. The dam at the source—the one your route passes tomorrow—it wasn't built to protect the valley. It was built to control it. And twelve years ago, someone in the Compliance chain used that control to arrange a tragedy and file it under weather."

The fire burned between them. He hadn't moved. She wished he would move—shout, stand, anything—but he sat with his hands loose between his knees and asked the only question that mattered:

"You knew. When you hired me. You knew what happened to my town, and you knew I grew up downstream of that dam, and you hired me anyway and said nothing."

"Yes."

"For three weeks."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I needed you to say yes before you understood what you were carrying." She held his stare. "Because if I'd told you at the dock, you'd have refused—not from cowardice, from grief. Grief makes people refuse. And because—" and here the last of the flesh came off the bone, and she let it— "because I'm a coward, Zephyrine. Eleven years building beautiful lies, and when I tried to tell the truth I discovered I only know how to do it by proxy. Through other people's hands. I needed a man with a boat and a grudge and nothing left to lose, and I went looking for exactly you, and I found you, and I used you. That's the confession. All of it. There's no version where I come out clean."

For a long moment the only sound was the river and the rain and Wren turning over in their sleep.

Then Zephyrine reached out and picked up the folio.

* * *

He read by firelight, page by page, and Odile watched him the way she'd once watched instruments during a pressure test—waiting for the needle to move, knowing it would, dreading the reading.

The folio held copies. The originals—the engineering ledgers, the maintenance logs, the gate-override authorizations signed in initials she'd memorized until they haunted her—were locked in the records vault at the source station, where all master documents lived, because the Syndicate trusted no server and no subordinate, only steel doors and its own indifference. Her copies proved the pattern existed. Only the originals, retrieved from the station itself, with their seals and registration marks intact, could prove it in a way that survived lawyers.

"Source gauges," he said finally, tapping a page. "You keep saying gauges. These are ledgers."

"The gauges are the key to the ledgers." She moved to sit beside him, close enough that the pages caught shared light. "Every master document in a Syndicate vault is registered against a physical reference—pressure readings, flow rates, timestamps pulled from the station's own instruments at the moment of filing. It's an anti-forgery system. The originals are only authenticatable against the live gauge record at the source. If we bring out the ledgers without the gauge data—or worse, if the Syndicate alters the gauges after we leave—the documents die in court. So we take both. Ledgers and gauge record, together, same trip, or not at all."

"And the station will be guarded."

"It will be awake. Alarms, yes. Possibly worse." She turned a page for him—the gate override order, dated eleven days before the flood, initialed *V.K.* "This is the man who gave the order. Vikram Kessler, then Director of Valley Operations. He sits on the Meridian board now. This signature is worth more than every debt on this river combined."

Zephyrine stared at the initials for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had gone somewhere she recognized from years ago—from the day his old crew lost people to this same stretch of water, though he'd never told her the details and she'd never asked. It was the voice of a man standing in a room where something irreversible had happened.

"My crew died in that flood," he said. "Two of them. Mara and Dev. Everyone thinks the river took them. The river did take them. But you're telling me someone opened a door first."

"I'm telling you someone scheduled it."

He closed the folio. His hands were steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have. "The toll post at Serpent Bend. The men in the gorge tomorrow—they weren't random bandits, were they. They knew our route."

"They know your route because someone is feeding it to them." She hesitated, then spent the coin: "And because I've been followed since the port city. I assumed I'd lose them on the water. I was wrong. I'm usually right, and I was wrong, and people may die of it. Add that to the ledger too."

He laughed then—one short, hard syllable with no humor in it, a sound like something snapping. "You realize what you're describing. You're asking me to break into the place that killed my town, steal the proof, and survive long enough to use it. With a crew of four and a teenager."

"With a crew of five," Odile said. "You keep forgetting yourself."

"And what happens to me in your version, Vasseur? After? Does the truth set me free, or does the Syndicate just add me to the list of loose ends? Because I've met their list. I owe them enough to be on it twice."

She had prepared answers for this. She had three of them, layered, each one calibrated. She heard herself abandon all three.

"I don't know," she said. "I genuinely don't. I know that if we succeed, the Syndicate's legitimacy cracks—there are investigators, journalists, river communities who've been waiting twelve years for exactly these papers. I know that warrants become possible. I know your debt dies with their authority, because debts issued by criminals are just violence with paperwork. And I know that none of that protects you on the night we do it. I won't insult you with promises. I've spent eleven years writing promises for other people to break. I'm done manufacturing certainty."

The rain thickened against the tarp. Down the bank, faintly, Priya's footsteps came back along the gravel—she stopped when she saw the firelight change, saw the two of them bent over the folio, and Odile watched the engineer's face do something complicated and afraid before she turned and walked to her hammock without a word.

*Interesting,* Odile thought, and put it away for daylight.

"So the gauges," Zephyrine said at last. "Live record, pulled at the station, matched to the filing stamps. And if the Syndicate reaches the gauges first—if they scrub the record before we get there—"

"Then we carry out paper ghosts. Worthless. Which means the real race isn't us against the convoy behind us. It's us against whoever reaches that station and touches the instruments." She met his eyes. "They'll scrub it the moment they confirm what I am and what I'm carrying. Every hour matters now."

He was quiet for a while. Then he fed a stick into the fire and said, without looking at her:

"Mara used to say the river keeps honest books. Every debit, every credit, eventually balanced. I thought that was poetry. Turns out it was accounting."

"Zephyrine—"

"Don't apologize. Apologies are flesh." He stood, brushing gravel from his knees, and looked down at her with an expression she couldn't fully read—anger and something adjacent to it, something that might, in a better-lit world, be called respect. "Tomorrow we portage the gorge. Day after, the basin. You'll tell the crew the truth tonight—all of it, the way you told me, no spine left covered. They sign on for a survey, they deserve to know they signed on for a war. And Odile—" he paused at the edge of the firelight— "if there's a third thing you haven't told me, the time to say it is now. Because after tonight, I stop asking."

She searched herself honestly, the way she'd once audited other people's lies. There was one more thing. Small, maybe. Maybe not.

"There's a name on the override order besides Kessler's," she said quietly. "A countersignature. Compliance verification. Mine. Dated twelve years ago. I verified the schedule that killed your town, Zephyrine. I didn't know what I was verifying. That's not innocence. That's just failure with better lighting."

He absorbed that. She watched him decide—watched the calculation run behind his eyes, the same cold precision that had talked them through Serpent Bend—and when he finally spoke, his voice was almost gentle, which was somehow the cruelest option available to him.

"Then we'll balance your books too. Tomorrow, the gorge. Sleep if you can."

He walked toward the boat. Odile stayed by the fire a while longer, alone with the truth she'd carried upriver like ballast, feeling lighter and more exposed than she had in eleven years.

Above the gorge, far off, thunder rolled—not the monsoon yet, just its advance scouts—and she found herself counting the hours backward from landfall, and wondering, with the first honest fear she'd felt since the port city, whether the convoy behind them already knew exactly where the fire was burning.
