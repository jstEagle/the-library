## 14. The Informant

Zephyrine didn't sleep. He told the crew it was the current—the Gorge of Bells had left them running night stretches to make up lost time—but that was only half of it. The other half sat in the engine room with grease on her hands and a debt she'd never mentioned.

He'd been turning it over since the fuel lines. Sabotage wasn't a stranger's crime. It took access, timing, and knowledge of where Priya kept the spare couplings. It took someone aboard.

He lay in his bunk with the deck lamp off and ran the crew through his head like a manifest.

Wren: sixteen, terrified of him half the time, had nearly died in the gorge holding a bell line while Syndicate rounds chewed the rock above. No.

Tomas: twenty-two years navigating rivers, could have sold them out at any checkpoint for less trouble than cutting a fuel line. If Tomas were the leak, they'd never have made Serpent Bend at all. No.

Odile: she *wanted* the Syndicate broken. Burning their own mission made no sense—unless it did, unless this was all an elaborate play to get him killed and take the ledgers herself. He turned that over twice and set it down. She'd had three chances to let the river do it for free. She hadn't taken one.

Which left Priya.

He hated how clean it fit. Her family in Vellankanni—she talked about them the way people talk about a place they're afraid to return to. Her sister's school fees. Her mother's clinic bills. And once, outside the drowned town, when Wren asked why anyone would build a reservoir over living houses, Priya had said, "Because someone signed a paper," in a voice flat as slate, and then gone below without her supper.

And there was the thing he'd noticed two nights ago and filed away without deciding what it meant: the radio. Priya serviced the long-range set every third day whether it needed it or not. Checked the grounding wire. Replaced a gasket that wasn't worn. Nobody gaskets a radio out of love.

* * *

At dawn he found Tomas on the bow, drinking tea that was more sugar than leaf, watching the river braid itself through the shallows.

"Question," Zephyrine said. "If you wanted to pass our position to someone downstream, how would you do it?"

Tomas didn't blink. "Short-band burst, encrypted, under the weather report traffic. Everyone listens to weather." He sipped. "Or you don't use the radio at all. You use a beacon. Fishing transponder, reprogrammed. Pings on a schedule. Looks like every other boat on the water."

"Could you find one? If there was one?"

Tomas looked at him then, properly, and whatever he saw in Zephyrine's face made him set the cup down. "You think it's Priya."

"I think I want to be wrong."

"Vellankanni," Tomas said quietly. "Her family's district went into receivership four years ago. Meridian bought the paper on half the fishing fleet there. Whole towns working off loans their grandfathers never signed." He rubbed his jaw. "I'm not saying it's her. I'm saying if it is her, it isn't a choice she got to make."

Zephyrine watched the water slide past, brown and patient. "That's what makes it worse."

* * *

He didn't confront her. That was the old Zephyrine—the one who'd run crews on fear and mileage, who'd have dragged the truth out over the rail by nightfall. Twelve years and two graves had taught him something slower.

Instead he waited for the third-day maintenance cycle.

Midmorning, mist still burning off the water, he came down the engine room ladder quiet as he knew how and stood at the foot of it. Priya knelt at the radio housing, back to him, a small soldering iron in her hand. On the deck plate beside her knee: not a gasket. A transponder module the size of a cigarette case, its casing pried open, a fine antenna wire running out through the hull gland where the depth sounder used to sit before she'd "repaired" it at Serpent Bend.

She heard him—of course she did; the ladder screamed—and went very still. Then she set down the iron, turned, and looked up at him with the expression of a woman who has already done the arithmetic and found no remainder.

"How long?" she said.

"Since the fuel lines. Certain since last night." He crouched to her level, forearms on his knees. "The schedule. Every six hours?"

"Four." Her voice didn't shake. He gave her credit for that. "It carries a grid code. They triangulate off two pings."

"Who holds your paper?"

Something moved behind her eyes—the flinch of a nerve touched directly. "My father borrowed against the boat license in '19. When he died, the debt didn't. Meridian refinanced it twice. My mother co-signed the second time because the alternative was the work camps." She laughed, short and airless. "You know what the interest runs. You of all people know."

"I know exactly what it runs."

"They have my sister's name in a file, Zeph. Not a threat. Just—a file. Names are enough." She wiped her hands on a rag that only spread the grease thinner. "They approached me in port, before we launched. A man with an umbrella. Polite. Said I'd ping our position and nothing would happen to anyone. Said if I didn't, they'd know that too, and files have a way of growing."

Zephyrine thought of another polite man with an umbrella, thirty days written in a ledger in a hand so neat it looked kind. The same hand, probably. The same pen.

"You cut the fuel lines," he said. Not a question.

"The lines or the brakes on the next rapid—I got to choose which. That was the message. They wanted the boat stopped somewhere they could board us without a fight." Her jaw tightened. "So I chose the lines. In the night, at anchor, where nobody would drown doing it. I told myself that was mercy. Cutting lines instead of people." She finally let her shoulders drop. "I've been telling myself a lot of things."

"Why tell me now? You could lie."

"Because you're standing there not hitting me, and that means you've already thought of something better than throwing me overboard. And because—" she stopped, started again, quieter. "Because Odile showed me what's in those ledgers. What they did to your valley. To mine, probably, if I read the annexes right. And I've been feeding them our heading every four hours like a good little instrument, and I can't—" Her breath caught. "I can't keep being their instrument. Even if it kills my family. Even. I can't."

The engine ticked around them. Somewhere above, Wren shouted something about a log jam and Tomas answered, ordinary voices, ordinary morning, the whole fragile machine of them humming along unaware—or maybe not unaware. Maybe Tomas had known too. Maybe they all carried their umbrellas.

Zephyrine stayed crouched a moment longer, looking at the open transponder, at the neat antenna wire, at the small ugly machinery of survival. He thought about Mara, who had believed people were their worst hour plus everything after. He thought about Dev, who'd believed the opposite. Both of them dead either way, and him still here, still having to decide.

Then he reached out and closed the transponder's casing with two fingers, gentle, like shutting a book.

"Here's what happens now," he said.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and by evening the plan had a shape.

"We don't cut her off," Zephyrine told Tomas and Odile in the wheelhouse, door shut, Wren deliberately sent upriver in the skiff to check a trapline that didn't exist. "We feed her."

Odile leaned against the chart table, arms crossed. "You want to leave the channel open."

"I want to leave the channel open and start lying through it. Priya keeps pinging on schedule—we don't touch the hardware, in case they've got a second way of knowing it's alive. But the grid codes she sends come from me now." He tapped the chart. "They think we're here." His finger sat on a stretch of the lower channel, wide and slow, a day behind them. "Tomorrow they'll think we're here"—he slid it west, into the old logging cut, a route Tomas called the Widow's Sluice because it ate barges—"and by landfall they'll be convinced we're running for the coast instead of climbing toward the source."

"They'll figure it out eventually," Tomas said.

"Eventually is the whole game. We need forty-eight hours of their belief. After that it doesn't matter what they know, because the monsoon will own the river and their convoy will be bailing water like everyone else." He looked at Odile. "How far behind are they?"

"A day. Maybe less since the gorge cost us." She frowned. "You understand what you're asking her to do. If they catch the deception—if they ever trace it—they'll go straight to Vellankanni. Her family becomes the lesson they teach the next informant."

"I know what I'm asking."

"Do you?" Odile's voice was soft, which from her meant dangerous. "Because there's a version of this where you're not showing mercy. There's a version where you're just spending her the way the Syndicate spends her, and calling it strategy so you can sleep."

The words landed where they were aimed. Zephyrine held them a moment, tested their weight, found most of it true.

"Both versions are real," he said. "I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But here's the difference I can offer." He turned to the doorway, where Priya stood—had been standing, he realized, long enough to hear all of it, grease still on her hands, face unreadable. "Tell her yourself."

Priya came in slowly. "Tell me what?"

"That you stop being an instrument tonight," Zephyrine said. "Not because I forgive you—you'll have to do that work yourself, with Wren, when you're ready, because she trusted you and trust is the only cargo on this boat I actually care about. But because starting with the next ping, you're not reporting to them anymore. You're reporting *for* us. Same wire. Different hands on it."

"And my family?"

"Odile's ledger names the loan portfolios. Every receivered district, every asset, every signature. When this breaks—and it breaks at the source or it doesn't break at all—your mother's paper burns with the rest of theirs. That's the trade. Your risk now buys their freedom later. I won't dress it up prettier than that."

Priya looked at the chart, at the false positions, at the long brown thread of the river running north toward everything that had ruined them both. When she spoke, her voice had gone steady in the way of someone stepping onto a rope bridge and choosing not to look down.

"The next ping is at nineteen hundred. Tell me what to send."

* * *

At 18:58, Zephyrine stood beside her in the engine room while she keyed the grid code he'd chosen: a position twelve miles south of their actual track, drifting east, lazy as a boat losing heart. The transponder clicked. Somewhere downstream, a man with an umbrella wrote it in a neat hand, and the net drew tight around empty water.

"There," Priya said, and exhaled like she'd been underwater for eleven days.

"There," Zephyrine agreed.

Above them, the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the wind that came down the valley smelled different than it had that morning—heavier, greener, charged. Tomas appeared at the hatch with the satellite printout folded in his fist, and Zephyrine saw the news in his face before he said a word.

"Storm advisory," Tomas said. "Landfall revised. Three days. Maybe two."

Zephyrine looked north, up the darkening gorge, toward the high valley where the river began and everything ended.

"Then we stop lying about our speed," he said. "Wake Wren. We run all night."
