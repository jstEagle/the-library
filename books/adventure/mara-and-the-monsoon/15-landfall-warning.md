## 15. Landfall Warning

Priya felt the storm before anyone told her about it.

It was in the way the air sat on her skin at dawn—thick, warm, patient, like a hand resting on the back of her neck. It was in the river itself, running a hand's width higher than it had been the night before, though no rain had fallen upstream of them that she knew of. The Kalyani was already drinking from the mountains. Somewhere above them, in country they hadn't reached yet, the monsoon had begun to arrive ahead of its own name.

She stood on the foredeck of the *Vesper* with her coffee going cold and watched the water climb the pilings of a drowned jetty they'd tied off to, and she did the arithmetic she always did when the air went soft like this. Barometric pressure falling for two days straight. Cloud deck lowering from cirrus to altostratus to something gray and bruised and low. Swell coming downriver against the current, which meant wind out of the northeast, which meant the system was already organized. She'd spent six years as an engineer on coastal survey vessels before the Syndicate took her family's mill and her family's name and put her on this river, and some part of her brain never stopped reading the sky the way other people read a room.

The sky was saying: *soon.*

The satellite uplink was Tomas's toy—a battered receiver he'd salvaged off a wrecked weather buoy two seasons ago and bolted to the wheelhouse roof with more faith than engineering. Every morning at first light he climbed up there, fiddled with the alignment, and pulled down whatever fragments the atmosphere let through. Most mornings it was shipping notices and half-decoded synoptic charts. This morning, when Priya came below to check the fuel lines—checking them twice now, every day, since Wren had found them loosened—Tomas was standing over the printout with his lips moving, counting.

"Tomas." She set her coffee down. "Say it."

He didn't look up. "You do the math. You're better at it."

That was how she knew it was bad. Tomas would joke through anything—through the checkpoint at Serpent Bend, through the ambush in the Gorge of Bells, through three days of cold rations and one of no rations at all. He joked the way she checked fuel lines: because the alternative was standing still and feeling the fear all the way down. If he wasn't joking now—

She took the printout from his hands.

The chart was degraded, half the contour lines dropped out, but the numbers were legible. Depression center tracking west-northwest, intensifying. Forecast landfall cone shaded across the upper Kalyani basin. And beneath it, in the clipped language of meteorological bulletins, the sentence that rearranged everything:

**LANDFALL EXPECTED WITHIN 96 HOURS. CONFIDENCE HIGH.**

"Four days," Priya said.

"Four days," Tomas agreed. "We planned for six."

"We planned for six *to the source*. We haven't even cleared the middle gorge."

"I know."

"And the convoy?"

Tomas finally looked up. He had the particular stillness of a man who has been holding bad news for an hour, waiting for someone else to arrive so he wouldn't have to carry it alone. "Zephyrine's topside talking to Odile. I figured you'd want to hear it with everyone."

Priya picked her coffee back up and drained it, cold as it was. "Then let's not make him wait."

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse, which was really just the largest excuse for a room aboard—the helm, a chart table, four chairs of four different eras, and Odile Vasseur's steel case chained to the bulkhead like a sleeping animal nobody wanted to wake. Zephyrine stood at the window with his arms folded, watching the water. Odile sat with her hands flat on the chart table, the way she did when she was making herself be calm. Wren perched on the engine housing by the door, seventeen years old and trying very hard to look like someone who belonged in a war council.

Priya spread the printout on the table and weighted the corners with a mug, a wrench, a coil of line, and Odile's pen.

"Landfall in ninety-six hours," she said. "Give or take. High confidence. The system intensified overnight—it's feeding on warmer water than the models assumed. Everything downstream of us is going to get hit hard, and everything upstream is going to get hit harder, because the basin funnels. When this rain lands in the high valley, the whole river becomes a weapon pointing at us."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, the current dragged past the hull with a low continuous sound, like breathing.

"The source station," Odile said at last. Her voice was level, but Priya had learned to hear the seams in it. "How long at best speed?"

"Thirty hours if nothing breaks," Priya said. "Forty if something does. Something usually does."

"And after we're inside?"

"That's not my department." Priya looked at Zephyrine. "My department is getting us there before the river closes. After that, the river doesn't negotiate."

Zephyrine turned from the window. He looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep—tired the way stone is tired, worn down along old lines. Twelve years of miles, Wren called it, behind his back, without quite knowing what it meant. Priya knew. Everyone who'd been on the river long enough knew about Mara and Dev, even if nobody said their names in front of him.

"Then we go now," Zephyrine said. "Full burn. No more stops."

"There's a problem with full burn." Priya tapped the fuel gauge through the console glass. "After the sabotage, I've been running the numbers. We have enough for thirty hours at cruising speed. At full throttle, twenty-two. And there's no fuel between here and the source—nothing legal, nothing safe. The last depot was the drowned town."

"So we cruise."

"We cruise, and we pray the gorge doesn't make us fight the current. Because fighting the current costs fuel, and the current is about to become a different animal entirely."

Odile's jaw tightened. "The convoy," she said. "Where are they?"

Tomas answered, because he was the one who'd been watching the traffic bands. "Two days behind us at Serpent Bend as of yesterday evening. That was before the forecast shifted. They'll have gotten the same bulletin we did." He paused. "If they know the window closed, they won't wait for the river either. They'll push. And they don't have to worry about fuel—they refueled at Serpent Bend, we saw the barges."

"So they're closing," Odile said.

"They're closing," Tomas confirmed. "And here's the ugly part. Our informant knows our speed, our route, and now they'll know our new timeline. Whatever Priya's been feeding them through her channel—" he glanced at her, quick and careful; only the three of them knew what her channel had become, a pipe carrying poison in both directions— "the Syndicate can do this math too. They know exactly where we'll be when the sky opens."

The wheelhouse felt smaller than it had a minute ago. Wren spoke from the engine housing, voice carefully casual. "So we're racing a storm and a convoy, and one of those things wants to kill us and the other one just will."

"That's about the size of it," Zephyrine said.

"Good." Wren nodded once, briskly, and Priya had to look away to keep from smiling. The kid had come aboard afraid of rapids. Now they treated the apocalypse as a scheduling matter. There was something in that that frightened Priya more than the forecast did.

* * *

They made way within the hour.

Priya ran the engine room alone—there wasn't room for two, and there wasn't time to teach—and she lost herself in the work the way she always did, in the rhythm of gauges and temperatures and the deep animal hum of the diesel. Work was her chapel. When her brother had called her three months ago, his voice thin with fear, telling her what the Syndicate's people wanted her to report and what would happen to the mill if she didn't, it was the work that had kept her from shattering. And when Zephyrine had found out—had looked at her across the chart table with those gray eyes and said, not unkindly, *then we use it*—it was the work that had saved her. Feeding false positions up the channel, lying to the men who owned her family, turning her coercion into a weapon. Some prices couldn't be paid twice. She'd told him that at the river settlement, warning him away from a bargain he hadn't yet understood he was considering. But a price paid once, deliberately, on your own terms—that was different. That was almost freedom.

She checked the fuel lines a third time. Clean. Welded, actually—she'd welded them herself after the sabotage, and she caught herself running her thumb along the bead of metal like a talisman.

The river narrowed as the morning wore on. The banks rose into walls of wet black rock hung with vines, and the current strengthened beneath them, and Priya stood in the engine room doorway watching the bow water change character—from a smooth green slide to a ridged, muscular chop—and thought about clocks.

Her grandmother had kept time by the flood season. Not dates, not calendars: the river's moods. *Early water*, she'd say, *is hungry water.* Priya hadn't understood it as a child. She understood it now. The monsoon wasn't arriving in four days. It was already here, in the headwaters, in the rising gauge lines, in the pressure dropping like a held breath being slowly released. The landfall was just the moment the storm admitted what it had been doing all along.

And somewhere behind them, the convoy was doing the same arithmetic, with better fuel and worse intentions.

* * *

At midday the sky closed.

There was no drama to it—one hour the clouds were high and broken, the next they were a single low ceiling pressing down on the gorge, gray-green, sourceless, raining lightly everywhere at once. Not the real rain. Not yet. A rehearsal. The drops were fat and warm and fell without wind, straight down, as if the sky were leaking rather than storming.

Zephyrine brought them into the lee of a rock shelf to wait out the worst of the visibility, and Priya climbed to the wheelhouse to give her report. What she found was an argument already in progress.

"—can't stop every time it drizzles," Odile was saying. "Every hour we lose is an hour the convoy gains."

"It's not drizzle," Zephyrine said. "Look at the water."

Everyone looked at the water. The river had come up another foot since morning. Debris was moving in it now—branches, a whole tree, a section of roofing that might have come from a settlement fifty miles upstream. The Kalyani was carrying messages from the high country, and every one of them said the same thing.

"If we push into the middle gorge in this," Priya said, "and the current turns against us, we burn fuel we don't have fighting it. We could end up dead in the water in the worst stretch of river on this continent, in the leading edge of a cyclone, with the Syndicate an hour behind us." She laid her hands on the chart table. "Or we sit here two hours, let the front's first band pass, and run the gorge on its terms instead of ours."

"Two hours," Odile repeated. It wasn't agreement. It was the sound of a woman measuring two hours against everything she'd spent twelve years and an unforgivable career to obtain.

"Two hours," Priya said. "I know what you're carrying. I know what it cost you. But a dead courier delivers nothing."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes—recognition, maybe, or the memory of having once been the person who needed to hear exactly that. She looked down at the chart, at the penciled line marking their position, and nodded once.

"Two hours," she said. "Then we run it on its terms."

* * *

Priya used the two hours the way she used all time: working.

She serviced the raw-water strainers, because the rising river was bringing silt, and silt in the strainers meant an overheated engine at the worst possible moment. She lashed the spare fuel drums harder. She went forward in the light rain and checked the anchor gear and found Wren there, coiling line with the automatic competence the kid had developed somewhere between the drowned town and the Gorge of Bells, as if competence were something the river doled out to those who survived it.

"Hey," Wren said. Not looking up. "Is it true? About the four days?"

"True enough."

Wren coiled in silence for a while. Then: "When it hits. The real one. What's it like?"

Priya leaned against the rail and considered the question. She could lie—make it small, the way adults made storms small for children. But Wren had stopped being a child somewhere on this river, probably around the time they'd pulled the kid out of the flooding tunnel at the bell gorge, or maybe earlier, maybe the day they signed aboard a boat headed for a place that had killed people Wren had never met. Lying would be a kind of theft.

"Loud," Priya said. "Loud in a way you feel in your chest. The rain comes sideways and it doesn't stop, and after a while you forget there was ever any other weather. And the river—" she nodded at the brown water sliding past, already swollen, already carrying the mountains' news— "the river stops being a place and becomes a force. You can't steer it. You can only be in the right part of it."

"Zephyrine's been in it before," Wren said quietly. "Haven't you. In this. On this river."

It wasn't a question, and Priya didn't answer it. They both knew the shape of what had happened twelve years ago, the way you know the shape of a scar on someone else's body—aware of it, careful around it, never asking how it got there.

"He'll get us through," Priya said. "But listen to me, Wren. When the time comes, and he freezes—you saw it at Mara's Reach, you know what I'm talking about—someone else has to hold the wheel. Without being asked. Without hesitating. Can you do that?"

Wren's hands stopped on the line. For a moment the kid looked seventeen again, scared and young, and then the moment passed and the face hardened into something older.

"I already did it once," Wren said.

"Yes," Priya said. "I know. Do it again."

* * *

They ran the middle gorge that afternoon, and it was a fight.

Not with rocks—with water. The current had turned, the way Priya had feared, pouring down the gorge against them in long brown rollers, and the *Vesper* clawed upstream at four knots that felt like none at all. Priya stood in the engine room with her hand on the manifold gauge and watched the temperature needle creep and begged it silently to hold, hold, hold, while the hull slammed and shuddered and somewhere overhead Zephyrine's voice called depths and bearings in a tone stripped of everything but function. Two hours of it. Then the gorge opened into a wider valley reach and the current relented and the temperature needle drifted back toward sanity, and Priya allowed herself to breathe.

They had burned eleven hours of fuel in eight hours of running. The math was getting worse. She wrote the new figures in her logbook in her small precise hand, and underlined them once, and did not show anyone yet, because there was nothing anyone could do about them tonight, and because a crew could survive a storm but not a captain who'd stopped believing the numbers worked.

Night came early under the low cloud. They anchored in a side slough, bow upstream, riding the swell that was arriving now in earnest—long, slow, rhythmic pulses rolling down from the high country, the storm's heartbeat transmitted through a hundred miles of water. Priya stood her watch from midnight to four, alone in the wheelhouse with the radar's green sweep and the rain ticking on the glass.

At 0300 the satellite receiver chirped.

Tomas had left it running on its cycle, and the printout curled out of the slot like a slow gray tongue. Priya tore it free and read it under the red lamp, and the clock in her chest—the one that had been ticking since dawn—changed tempo.

Two items.

The first was the storm bulletin, updated. Landfall revised again: **72 HOURS**, the cone centered almost perfectly on the upper basin. The system had accelerated. Three days now. Maybe less, once it made up its mind.

The second item was not weather. It was a traffic advisory, faint and fragmentary, decoded from a commercial band: a convoy of five vessels, flagged Meridian, logged passing Serpent Bend at dusk. Five vessels. Not two days behind anymore.

Priya did the arithmetic with a pencil stub, there in the red lamplight, while the rain thickened against the windows and the river swelled beneath the hull. The convoy's speed. Their own remaining fuel. The distance to the source. The hours until landfall. Three columns of numbers converging on a single point, and the point was a high valley where a river began, and the numbers said they would all arrive together—crew, convoy, and sky.

She carried the printout to Zephyrine's cabin door and knocked, and when he opened it—awake, of course, dressed, of course, a man who slept like a sentry—she handed it to him without a word.

He read it. She watched his face go through it: the storm first, the jaw tightening; then the convoy, and something else, something colder and calmer settling over him, the look of a man who has finally been given permission to stop hoping for an easy way out.

"Three days," he said.

"At most. And they're a day behind us, running heavy, and they don't stop for weather." She paused. "Zeph. Even if we beat them there—even if we beat everything—we'll be coming back down *with* the monsoon at our backs. There may not be a river left to come down."

He folded the printout once, precisely, and looked past her through the porthole at the black rushing water, and when he spoke his voice was quiet and absolutely certain, which was somehow worse than fear.

"Then we'd better make sure it's worth it."
