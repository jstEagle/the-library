## 16. The Forbidden Source

The river narrowed for three days until it was barely a river at all—more a rumor of water threading through stone—and then on the fourth morning it simply stopped being narrow and became something else entirely.

Zephyrine felt it before he saw it. A change in the air pressure, the way the wind quit fighting them and started pulling. Priya came up from the engine well with her head cocked like a bird's. "Hear that?"

He didn't hear anything. That was the thing. Twelve days of engine noise and gorge echo and the constant argument of water against hull, and now there was a silence so complete it had a sound of its own, a ringing absence.

They rounded the last bend at dawn and the Kalyani ended.

Not ended—began. That was the whole lie of maps, he thought later. Every map he'd ever owned drew the river as a line running downhill to the sea, as if it were fleeing something. But standing in the bow with the engine cut and the current gone slack around his boots, Zephyrine understood that rivers don't run away from anything. They run toward. This whole time, every mile of his life downstream, he had been moving in the wrong direction without knowing it.

The source basin opened below them like a held breath.

A high valley, walled in by ridgelines still white-shouldered with old snow, and in its floor a lake so still it looked poured rather than gathered—the color of gunmetal and sky mixed together, deep enough to swallow light. Mist stood on it in columns where the cold water met the warming air, slow-moving pillars that made the whole basin feel inhabited, though nothing moved. No boats. No nets. No smoke. The river people had names for this place and none of them would say it aloud, Tomas had told him back at Serpent Bend, and now Zephyrine understood why. It wasn't fear. It was manners. You didn't speak carelessly in a room like this.

And above it all, astride the valley's only outlet, stood the dam.

It was bigger than anything had any right to be this far from anywhere—a gray curve of concrete half a kilometer across, rising out of the lake on one side and dropping two hundred meters into the gorge they'd just climbed through on the other. Seamed and stained with decades of weather, crowned with transmission towers whose lines ran off over the ridge toward cities Zephyrine had spent his life drinking this water without once thinking about it. Set into the dam's abutment, built right into the living rock, was the station: a blocky complex of concrete and steel with no windows facing the lake, its loading dock sealed behind doors tall enough to drive a truck through. A Meridian Syndicate flag hung motionless beside the entry gate. Even the wind wouldn't touch it.

"There it is," Odile said quietly, beside him at the rail. She'd been up since before dark. She was always up since before dark. "The Kalyani Gauging Station. Original construction, sixty-one years old. The ledgers will be inside."

Zephyrine didn't answer. He was looking at the water.

Because here was the thing he hadn't let himself think about since chapter one of his own life, since he was a boy: he knew this water. Not this lake—he'd never been here, no one from downstream ever came here—but the water itself. The mineral bite of it, the particular cold that didn't quite belong to the latitude, the way it smelled faintly of stone and snowmelt even in high summer. His hometown had stood on the lower Kalyani, and every glass of water he'd drunk between the ages of four and nineteen had begun its life in this basin. He had swum in this river. He had been baptized in it, in the loose way river towns did such things, dunked under by his grandmother while the congregation shouted. When the flood took the town, it had taken it with water born *here*, gathered here, held back here by that wall of concrete until the day someone decided it shouldn't be held anymore.

The beauty of the place undid him. That was the trap of it. He stood at the rail with his hands gripping cold metal and felt his chest crack open along a seam he'd welded shut twelve years ago, and what came pouring out wasn't grief exactly, or not only grief. It was rage wearing grief's face. Because the basin was beautiful. The Syndicate had built their machine in the most beautiful place he had ever seen, and the machine had killed his town, and the beauty was still here, indifferent, perfect, reflecting the dawn like nothing had ever gone wrong anywhere in the world.

"You grew up downstream," Odile said. Not a question. Wren must have told her, or Tomas, or she'd read it in the file the Syndicate surely kept on everyone who'd ever crossed them. "I'm sorry. I should have said that sooner."

"Don't." He said it flat, and then, because it came out crueler than he meant, he made himself finish. "Just—don't yet. Let me look at it first."

She nodded and left him alone at the rail, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in years.

* * *

They anchored in the lee of a granite point on the lake's north shore, far enough from the station that their engine noise wouldn't carry, close enough that through Priya's salvaged scope Zephyrine could count the floodlights on the perimeter fence. Fourteen. Two guards visible at the gatehouse, one making a slow circuit of the loading dock. The station showed no other signs of life, but Odile had warned them about that too: skeleton crew, automated systems, and a security response measured in hours from the nearest Syndicate garrison—which meant, if the convoy behind them had radioed ahead, those hours might already be burning down.

"Spillway gates are closed," Priya reported from the scope. "Lake's high, though. See how the waterline comes almost to the top of the intake towers? They've been holding back more than design capacity all season. Waiting for the monsoon to give them an excuse."

"An excuse for what?" Wren asked.

"Releases," Priya said. "Controlled ones. Or—" she lowered the scope and looked at Zephyrine, and didn't finish, and didn't have to. Everyone on the boat had done the arithmetic by now. If the Syndicate had engineered one collapse twelve years ago to seize a valley, they could engineer another to bury evidence. Water destroyed paper very thoroughly. Water destroyed witnesses even more thoroughly.

"We go in tonight," Zephyrine said. "Dark side of the dam, the maintenance stair Odile described. In and out before the night shift changes."

"And if the convoy gets here first?" Tomas asked. He was at the chart table but not looking at it; he'd stopped pretending the charts mattered two days ago. Now it was just the lake, the dam, and time.

"Then we're done arguing about it," Zephyrine said. "One way or another."

Nobody laughed. Nobody needed to.

* * *

He couldn't sleep, so he took the first watch alone and let the others rack out in the cabin, and for a long time he just sat in the stern with his boots up on the transom, looking at the water.

Twelve years of miles. That was how he'd measured his life since the flood—not in years, because years implied staying somewhere long enough for them to accumulate meaning, but in miles. River miles, road miles, the distance between the place he'd slept and the place he'd sleep next. He'd told himself it was freedom. He'd told the crews he'd run since then that a man who kept moving was a man who couldn't be cornered. But sitting here at the actual source of everything, watching mist stand on the water in slow columns, he finally admitted what Mara had probably known when she was alive and would certainly know if she could see him now: he hadn't been running from the Syndicate. He'd been running downstream. Always downstream. Away from this water, away from the memory of it coming for his town in the dark, away from the question he'd never had the courage to ask.

*Was it really the monsoon?*

Everyone had said yes. The official report said yes—an unprecedented rainfall event, a levee failure, a tragedy of nature. The Syndicate had sent condolences and a settlement check, insultingly small, and Zephyrine had taken it because Dev's widow needed it and pride doesn't feed children. He'd hated them ever since in the general way you hate large things, the way you hate weather. But he had never once, in twelve years, let himself ask the specific question, because the specific question had a specific answer, and either answer was unbearable. Either the storm had simply been a storm, in which case Mara and Dev had died for nothing, no reason, no one to blame, just water doing what water does—or the storm had been used. Weaponized. A gate opened, a levee sacrificed, a valley cleared of its inconvenient towns so the Syndicate could build. And if that was true, then his grief had an owner, and owners could be made to pay.

Odile had come to him with the second answer wrapped in a contract, and he'd told himself he was only in it for the debt. Thirty days, the enforcer had said, adjusting his umbrella in the rain like a man discussing the price of fish. Thirty days and then the Syndicate takes the boat, the tools, the blood if necessary. So yes, he'd signed Odile's papers for the money. Of course he had.

But the boat had been pointed upstream the entire time, and some part of him had known, from the first mile, that he wasn't chasing money. He was chasing the question home.

The mist shifted on the lake. Somewhere across the water, a generator cycled up at the station, a low industrial heartbeat, and the floodlights stuttered and steadied. Zephyrine watched the lights and thought about his grandmother's church on the lower Kalyani, thought about the smell of the town after rain, thought about Mara laughing at him the last time he saw her alive because he'd been complaining about the cost of fuel and she'd said, *Zeph, you count everything except what matters.*

He counted what mattered now. One boat. Four people who owed him nothing and kept showing up anyway. One rival with a guilty conscience and a briefcase full of proof. And somewhere inside that sealed concrete building, a set of ledgers with his hometown's death warrant written in engineering figures.

"Can't sleep either?"

Wren dropped into the stern beside him, moving quiet the way the kid always did, and Zephyrine didn't startle because he'd heard the footsteps—he always heard Wren's footsteps now, had learned them the way you learn anything you can't afford to lose.

"Didn't try," Zephyrine said.

Wren looked out at the lake, at the dam, at the flag hanging dead-still beside the gate. The kid's face did something complicated. "It's beautiful," Wren said finally, and sounded angry about it.

"Yeah," Zephyrine said. "That's how they get you."

* * *

They went in at 0300, under a sky the color of wet slate, with the moon down and the monsoon's leading clouds already stacking up black over the western ridgeline like a fleet at anchor. Priya ran the tender in silent-electric, hugging the shadow of the north shore, while Tomas held the main boat ready to move at a moment's notice and Wren watched the approach channel with the scope. Odile sat in the bow with her case strapped across her chest like a bandolier, checking and rechecking a laminated diagram of the station's interior that she'd apparently memorized years ago and never shown anyone until now.

"Maintenance stair comes up inside the abutment," she murmured as the dam's gray mass slid closer, blocking out the stars. "Service corridor runs along the gallery level. The records vault is two decks down, behind the old instrument room. Mechanical lock—pre-digital, they never upgraded it because nobody was supposed to know the vault existed."

"And the alarms?" Zephyrine asked.

"Motion sensors in the corridors. Pressure plates at the vault door." She glanced at him, and in the dark her face was all angles and honesty. "We'll trip something. Plan on tripping something. The trick is being gone before anyone answers."

The tender kissed the concrete base of the dam with a bump soft as a knuckle on a door. Zephyrine stepped out onto a ledge barely wider than his boot soles, forty feet above black water, and looked up the sheer face of the structure that had raised and ruined the river of his childhood. Up close it wasn't architecture anymore. It was geology that hated you. Cold radiated off the concrete; water wept from seams in long mineral stains, like the dam itself was bleeding the lake it held.

He found handholds and climbed.

Inside, the station smelled the way all old institutional buildings smell—concrete dust, machine oil, floor wax, and underneath it something electrical and patient, the smell of systems waiting to be triggered. Their headlamps stayed off; Odile navigated by memory through service corridors lit every thirty meters by dim red standby bulbs. Once, twice, they froze in alcoves while a camera swept past on its track, its red eye passing over them like a lighthouse beam they'd learned to breathe through.

The instrument room was exactly where she said. The vault door behind the false panel exactly where she said. An old mechanical lock, brass and steel, older than Zephyrine, and Odile knelt in front of it with a stethoscope-style listener pressed to the mechanism, working it with fingers that shook only slightly, and Zephyrine stood watch in the corridor and listened to the building hum.

That was when the hum changed pitch.

Subtle. Almost nothing. A generator somewhere loading up, or a pump cycling—but Zephyrine had spent twenty years listening to engines tell him the truth their gauges lied about, and he knew a system waking up when he heard one. He touched Odile's shoulder. She didn't stop working. "Thirty seconds," she breathed. "Twenty-five."

Down the corridor, a light changed from red to amber.

Then the loudspeakers crackled—not an alarm, not yet, worse: a voice, calm and automated and everywhere at once.

*"Attention. Perimeter breach, gallery level. Security response initiated."*

Behind the vault door, something heavy clicked open. Odile's eyes came up to meet his in the red light, wide and bright and terrified and triumphant all at once.

"Got it," she whispered. "Help me carry."
