## 17. The Ledger Room

The station door had a lock from another century.

Odile knelt before it in the dark of the maintenance corridor, the pick trembling—not with fear, she told herself, but with the cold. The sealed station sat into the mountainside like a tooth into a jaw, concrete and rust and humming cable, and inside it was everything: twelve years of her life folded into paper, waiting to be read.

"Talk to me," Zephyrine said behind her. He kept his voice low, the way he did when he was afraid and wanted no one to know it. "How long?"

"As long as it takes." The pick caught. She eased it back a hair's width. "You could be useful instead of decorative."

"I'm watching the tunnel."

"You're watching the dark and calling it a tunnel." She felt him shift, though—felt him turn his shoulder toward the black mouth of the corridor where it swallowed the light from their single lamp. He'd been doing that since they came ashore. Watching the way back. She understood. She'd spent eleven years doing the same thing in rooms far more comfortable than this one.

The lock gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

She stood, flexed her fingers, and pushed the door.

Inside, the ledger room breathed out air that hadn't moved since the last inspection—dust and machine oil and the mineral smell of stone that had never seen sun. Emergency strips flickered on along the ceiling, half of them dead, the rest buzzing at a frequency Odile felt in her back teeth. Steel shelving ran the length of the room in ranks, and on the shelves, in gray archive boxes stenciled with the Meridian Syndicate's coiled-serpent mark, were the documents.

Not copies. Not scans. The originals.

"They really kept them," Wren said from the doorway. The kid had followed them down against every instruction, and Zephyrine hadn't had the heart—or the authority—to send them back up. Wren's eyes went shelf to shelf, wide as coins. "Why would they keep proof of what they did?"

"Because powerful people always believe they'll win," Odile said. "And winners don't destroy evidence. They file it."

She pulled the first box. KALYANI I — HYDROLOGICAL SURVEY, 11TH YEAR. Her own handwriting was on the intake manifest. *Received and verified: O. Vasseur.* Eleven years ago, a younger woman with better posture had signed her name to a system she believed was merely corrupt, not murderous. She had learned the difference the hard way, in a conference room in Meridian City, listening to men discuss flood curves the way other men discussed weather.

"Start here," she said, handing Wren a box. "Anything stamped with a red seal goes in the satchel. Anything else stays."

"And if someone comes?" Wren asked.

Odile looked at Zephyrine. He was standing very still by the door, one hand flat against the steel frame, and she saw the moment he stopped being a guide and started being a man from downstream. His jaw worked once.

"Then we run," he said. "Nobody dies for paper."

"Somebody already died for this paper," Odile said quietly. "Two somebodies. A whole town." She opened the second box. "We're just collecting what they owe."

* * *

It took forty minutes to find them.

The ledgers lived in the last rank of shelving, in boxes marked ENVIRONMENTAL COMPLIANCE—the Syndicate's favorite hiding place for its sins, because no auditor ever volunteered to read compliance files. Odile knelt on the cold floor with the boxes around her like fallen masonry and lifted out three volumes bound in green board, their spines cracked, their pages dense with figures in the cramped hand of engineers who had known exactly what they were building.

Volume One: the design specifications for the upper spillway gates. Volume Two: the stress analyses, annotated in red ink by someone senior enough to matter. And Volume Three—

Volume Three was the one.

*Directive 44-K,* she read, and her mouth went dry around the words. *In the event of seasonal overtopping exceeding projected capture capacity, controlled release through secondary channel is authorized. Downstream mitigation is the responsibility of regional administration.*

Controlled release. Secondary channel. Downstream mitigation.

Twelve years ago, in the first week of the monsoon, the dam's operators had opened gates the engineering manuals said should never have been opened at that water level. The flood that took Mara and Dev and the town of Sable's Reach and four hundred other lives had not been an act of God. It had been an act of accounting. The reservoir had been overfull because the Syndicate had promised Meridian City more power than the river could give, and rather than admit the shortfall, they had spent a town.

And Directive 44-K proved they had planned it. Signed. Dated. Sealed in red wax that had outlasted the people it condemned.

"Zephyrine," she said.

He didn't answer. He was looking at the open volume in her hands, and his face had gone somewhere she couldn't follow—a place past anger, past grief, into a flat country where nothing moved. She had seen that look once before, on a man reading his own death warrant.

"That's my town's name," he said finally. His voice came out level, which was worse than shouting. "In the margin. Here—" His finger touched the page without quite landing on it. "*Downstream mitigation: Sable's Reach assessed as acceptable loss zone.*"

"Acceptable loss zone," Wren repeated, tasting the words like something spoiled.

"They wrote it down." Zephyrine laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "They sat in a room and they wrote it down and then they filed it. Twelve years I thought—I told myself it was the river. The monsoon. Bad luck. You tell yourself that long enough, you build your whole life on it." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and terribly awake. "You built your whole career on knowing this. And you let me sign your contract anyway."

"Yes," Odile said. Because there was no version of honesty left that didn't include that word. "Because you were the only pilot alive who knew the upper Kalyani, and because I owed you the truth even if I couldn't afford to give it to you early."

"You'll pay for that later."

"I know."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Then he took the volume from her hands, carefully, the way a man handles something that might still detonate, and passed it to Wren. "Satchel. All of it. Every page with a red seal."

Wren packed fast, small hands sure despite the shaking Odile could see in their shoulders. Three volumes. Two folders of gate logs. A sheaf of telegrams between the station and Meridian City that Odile had spent eleven years imagining and here they were, banal as grocery lists. The whole architecture of a massacre, fitting into one canvas bag.

"We have it," Wren whispered. "We actually have it."

For one full breath, Odile let herself feel it. Triumph, real triumph, warm and enormous, rising through her chest like floodwater through a stairwell. Eleven years of careful anonymity, of building her cover inside the beast, of memorizing filing systems and flattering monsters—and here, in a buzzing concrete room under a mountain, it had all come down to kneeling on a cold floor and picking up a book.

That was when she heard the sound.

A tone, low and clean, rising through the station's bones. Then a second tone, a fifth above it. Then the lights changed—all of them, the dead strips snapping awake one after another down the corridor, white and merciless.

The alarm.

"No," Wren said. "No, no—"

"The motion sensors." Odile was already on her feet, mind racing backward through the schematics she'd memorized a decade ago. "The old ones. They wired the archive ranks separately—seismic triggers, in case anyone tried to remove records. I accounted for the door. I didn't—" She stopped herself. Excuses were ballast; throw them overboard. "Doesn't matter. We have maybe six minutes before the response team reaches the valley road. Less if they were already close."

"Were they already close?" Zephyrine asked.

She met his eyes and told him the truth, because she had apparently decided, sometime in the last hour, to die honest. "I don't know. But Priya's reports put their convoy a day behind us. Someone has been feeding them our positions."

"Tomas wouldn't," Wren said instantly.

"I know it wasn't Tomas," Zephyrine said, and Odile heard what he didn't say—that he knew exactly who it was, and had chosen to keep that knowledge in his pocket like a knife. She filed that away. Later. Everything was later now.

They ran.

* * *

The corridor to the loading bay was two hundred meters of flickering white light and their own footsteps thrown back at them by concrete. Odile took point with the lamp, Wren in the middle clutching the satchel like a child with a wounded animal, Zephyrine at the rear walking backward half the time, pistol drawn, watching the dark they'd come from.

At the junction marked BAY ACCESS in flaking paint, the lights ahead died.

All of them. At once.

Odile froze, arm out. "Stop."

The silence afterward had texture. Water dripping somewhere deep in the mountain. The hum of the generators, steady, indifferent. And underneath it—faint, rhythmic, wrong—the sound of boots on metal grating. More than two sets. More than four.

"Side passage," she breathed. "Twenty meters back, on the left. It comes out above the bay catwalk."

"You're sure?"

"I designed the retrofit route myself." She almost smiled. "The one good thing I ever built for them."

They doubled back in the dark, hands on the wall, and squeezed into the side passage single file. It climbed steeply, a maintenance crawl barely tall enough for Wren, and the sound of the boots grew louder below and to the right until Odile could hear voices—clipped, professional, radio-crackled.

"—bay secured. No vessel at the dock. They came overland or they're still inside."

"Find them. Vasseur will have the originals on her person or within reach. Nothing leaves this station. Am I understood?"

Odile closed her eyes.

She knew that voice. Everyone who had ever worked inside the Meridian Syndicate knew that voice—patient, courteous, faintly amused, the voice of a man who apologized while he ruined you. Corvain. The debt collector with the immaculate umbrella. She had sat across a table from him twice in her other life and both times had come away feeling like a document that had been read, stamped, and filed.

He was supposed to be a day behind them. Which meant the informant hadn't just been feeding positions. The informant had fed them the one thing that mattered: that Odile would go for the ledgers tonight, before landfall, when the monsoon itself would cover all sins.

They'd used her plan as a trap and she had walked into it holding the prize.

Below and to the right, through a grating, she glimpsed them: six figures in dark rain gear moving through the bay below, weapons slung, spreading out with the unhurried competence of professionals. And at their center, dry beneath the station roof while rain hammered the world outside, stood Corvain, umbrella furled, examining the empty dock with the expression of a man checking his watch.

"He's here," she whispered to Zephyrine. "Corvain himself."

"Then we go out the way we came in."

"There is no way we came in. That's the trap's whole shape." She pressed her forehead briefly against the cold concrete, thinking, thinking. "There's one other exit. The old survey adit, east side, pre-dam—it was used during construction and then sealed. If the seals held, it's a wall. If they didn't, it's a hundred meters of tunnel and daylight."

"And if they didn't hold and it's flooded?"

"Then we drown somewhere historic." She was already crawling. Behind her she heard Wren's breathing, quick and shallow, and Zephyrine's low voice steadying the kid—*match me, breathe when I breathe, we've swum worse*—and she thought, absurdly, in the middle of everything: *he's good at this. He was always good at this. That was never the problem.*

The adit was where she remembered it, behind a collapsed rack of conduit, its steel door swollen with rust. Zephyrine put his shoulder into it once, twice—and on the third try it shrieked open onto a throat of darkness that exhaled wet, moving air.

Air meant a way through. Moving air meant the river side.

But as they slipped into the gap, Odile looked back down through the grating one last time, and Corvain was no longer looking at the dock. He was looking directly up at the catwalk—at the side-passage entrance they had used not ninety seconds earlier—and he was smiling the small, satisfied smile of a man whose ledger had finally balanced.

He raised one hand. Not a wave. A gesture toward the bay doors, and the rain beyond them, and the valley where the monsoon was making landfall hours ahead of schedule.

"Seal the station," Corvain said pleasantly, to no one and everyone. "All of it. Let the river do the paperwork."

Above them, deep in the dam's spine, something vast began to turn.

Odile grabbed Wren's collar and hauled them both into the dark.
