## 18. Rising Water

The alarm changed pitch.

Wren had been listening to it for ten minutes—the flat, mechanical whoop of a station running on emergency power—and then, without warning, it climbed into something higher, uglier, a sound like the building itself screaming. The lights overhead flickered from white to amber to a deep pulsing red.

"That's not an intrusion alarm," Priya said. She was three steps ahead of Wren in the corridor, one hand on the wall, moving by feel as much as sight. "That's hydrological."

"Meaning what?"

Priya didn't answer right away. They were running now, Odile ahead of them with the ledger case strapped across her back like a bomb she couldn't put down, Zephyrine bringing up the rear with his pistol out and his face gray in the strobing light. Behind them, somewhere below, boots on steel stairs—many boots, moving fast.

"Meaning they've opened the spillways," Priya said. "Early. Days early."

Wren's stomach dropped through the floor. "The monsoon hasn't made landfall yet."

"It doesn't have to." Priya's voice was flat, and that flatness scared Wren more than shouting would have. "There's enough water behind this dam to drown a valley. They don't need the sky's permission. They just need to open the gates."

They burst into the main gallery of the station—a cathedral of concrete and pipework where the river came through the mountain's heart, all of it humming under their feet like a sleeping animal that had just been kicked. Emergency lights painted everything the color of a wound. And at the far end of the gallery, past the control room's shattered glass, Wren could see the spillway channel: a black throat in the dam's flank, and water beginning to move in it. Not rushing. Not yet. Just moving, the way a snake moves before it strikes.

"They're flushing us out," Zephyrine said. He'd stopped at the gallery rail, and for a moment he looked older than Wren had ever seen him—twelve years older, twenty. "Flood the tunnels, drive us toward whatever exit they're watching."

"Then we go where they aren't watching," Odile said. She'd already pulled a station schematic from her jacket, unfolding it against the rail with wet hands. Her voice was steady but her fingers weren't. "Maintenance run. It runs parallel to the penstocks, drops to the old construction adit on the downstream face. It'll be flooding first—it's low—but it connects to the service tunnel that comes out above the tailrace."

"How long do we have?" Tomas asked. He was bleeding from a cut above his eyebrow—he'd taken shrapnel when the Syndicate team breached the loading bay—but he was standing, and his hands were steady on the schematic's edge.

Priya did the math out loud, fast, the way she did everything: reservoir level, gate capacity, tunnel volume. Wren watched her lips move and hated every number.

"Eleven minutes," Priya said. "Maybe nine if they open the second gate."

* * *

The maintenance run was exactly as bad as advertised.

It started as a walk—corrugated flooring, handrails slick with condensation, pipes crowding the ceiling so low that even Wren had to duck. Their headlamps carved cones out of the dark. Behind them, the alarm kept screaming, and beneath it, growing louder by the minute, came the sound Wren would hear in dreams for the rest of their life: water filling space. A low, patient roar, like the river clearing its throat.

Zephyrine set the pace. He didn't look back. Wren knew why. Somewhere behind them was the enforcer—Voss, the man with the umbrella, the man who measured people in debts—and Zephyrine wasn't running from him so much as refusing to give him the courtesy of a glance.

"Talk to me, Priya," Zephyrine called over his shoulder.

"Water's at our heels. Fifty meters back and gaining." A pause. "The adit ramp is ahead—thirty degrees down, two hundred meters, then the junction."

"The junction floods first?"

"The junction is already flooded."

Nobody said anything for a few steps. Then Odile laughed—a short, wild sound with no humor in it at all. "Twelve years ago I sat in a conference room and signed off on flow projections for this dam. I told myself they were just numbers." Her voice cracked on the last word. "I never once imagined being inside them."

"You imagined it," Zephyrine said quietly. "That's why you're here."

The water found them at the top of the ramp.

Wren felt it first as cold around their ankles, then as a shove between the shoulder blades—the flood arriving not as a wave but as a decision, the whole weight of the reservoir pressing up the tunnel behind them. The headlamps swung wildly. Someone shouted. The corrugated flooring bucked underfoot like a deck in weather, and Wren, who had grown up on decks, did the only thing that made sense: grabbed the handrail and ran harder.

"Junction!" Priya screamed. "Left side, high ground, GO!"

The junction chamber opened around them like a wound in the mountain—a raw space of rebar and rough-poured concrete where three tunnels met. And the water was already there, churning hip-deep across the floor, dragging debris in slow circles. On the left, a metal stair climbed toward the service tunnel's mouth, maybe eight meters up the wall.

Eight meters. With the water rising.

Tomas went first, hauling himself up the stair with his bad arm, cursing steadily in three languages. Odile followed, the ledger case held above her head like an offering. Zephyrine stood at the bottom, pistol still in hand, covering the tunnel mouth they'd come from—as if Voss might simply walk out of the flood, immaculate, umbrella furled.

"Zeph!" Wren screamed. "MOVE!"

He moved. And that was when Wren heard Priya cry out.

She'd been last but one, counting them through the way she counted everything, and the flood had finally caught her—a surge off the main channel, a piece of floating pipe, something—and spun her off her feet. Wren saw her go under, saw her surface once, gasping, saw her hand slap against smooth concrete and find nothing to hold.

The water between them was chest-high and accelerating. Every instinct Wren owned said climb the stairs, climb the stairs, you're fourteen, you're light, the water wants you too.

Wren went into the water instead.

* * *

Later—much later, in a dry place, in a life that still had Priya in it—Wren would try to explain what those ninety seconds were like, and fail. There was no thinking in it. Thinking was a luxury of people standing on shore.

The current took Wren immediately. That was the thing about floodwater inside a tunnel: it didn't behave like river water. It bounced off walls, doubled back on itself, tried to drown you in six directions at once. Wren's headlamp beam whipped through brown chaos—concrete, foam, Priya's hand, gone, there again, fifteen feet downstream and sinking.

Wren caught a pipe. Lost it. Caught a second one, a valve wheel bolted to the wall, and hung there with both arms screaming while the flood tried to peel them loose. Fifteen feet. Twelve. Wren hauled along the pipe hand over hand, the cold punching the breath out of their chest, and when Priya surfaced the third time her eyes were wide and white-rimmed and fixed on nothing.

"Priya!" Wren screamed. "PRIYA! Grab my wrist!"

Her hand shot out and missed. Missed again. The third time, Wren let go of the pipe with one hand—let go, into all that roaring dark—and caught her forearm, and the pull nearly took them both under. For one endless second Wren felt the water's full opinion of them, its enormous indifference, the same indifference that had taken Mara and Dev twelve years before Wren ever knew their names.

Then Wren's other hand found the valve wheel, and held.

They got her to the wall. Got her arm over a conduit bracket. Got her feet braced. And then, inch by inch, with the flood climbing their ribs and the stair eight meters up and Tomas screaming down at them from the top, Wren walked Priya up the wall of the junction chamber—one bracket, one bolt, one breath at a time—until hands reached down out of the lamplight and dragged them both onto the stairs, onto steel, onto air.

Wren lay on the steps and coughed half the Kalyani out of their lungs.

Priya lay beside them, doing the same. After a while, without looking over, she said, "You let go of the pipe."

"Yeah."

"You were supposed to climb."

"Yeah."

A longer silence. Water thundered below them, filling the junction, erasing the floor where they'd almost died.

"I've been feeding them our positions since Serpent Bend," Priya said. It came out small and wrecked and relieved all at once, the confession of someone who no longer had the strength to carry it another meter. "My family's debts. They—"

"I know," Wren said. "Zeph knows. He used it. We fed them false positions and it worked, and then you saved us in the gorge, and now—" Wren gestured weakly at the flood, at everything. "We're even. All of it. Every debt between us. Paid."

Priya turned her head. In the red emergency light her eyes were wet, and Wren chose, generously, to blame the water.

"Debts," she said. "You sound like him."

"Learned from the best."

Above them, Zephyrine's silhouette blocked the stairwell light. He was looking down at the two of them—at Wren, specifically—with an expression Wren had no name for. Something that wasn't gratitude. Gratitude was smaller than that.

"Mara would have liked you," he said. "Terrible judgment. Absolutely fearless." His voice caught on the name, and he let it. "Up. Both of you. We're not done."

* * *

The service tunnel climbed, mercifully, away from the water—but the mountain itself seemed to be coming apart around them. The flood had shifted something in the station's bones; dust sifted from seams in the concrete, and somewhere far below, metal groaned in long, dying notes.

They emerged through a rusted hatch onto the downstream face of the dam, and the world outside was worse than the world within.

The monsoon had arrived after all—not the true landfall, not yet, but its outriders: a wall of black cloud marching up the valley, rain falling in ropes, lightning stitching the ridgelines. And below them, the tailrace was boiling. The spillway discharge thundered down the dam's face in a white cataract, and the river below was rising as they watched, brown and furious, tearing at its banks.

"There," Tomas shouted, pointing through the rain. Downstream, wedged in a side channel where they'd cached it that morning, sat the launch—still tied, still intact, a miracle nobody commented on because commenting might jinx it.

Between them and the boat: two hundred meters of exposed dam apron, and standing on it, sheltering under the intake structure's overhang, four figures in black rain gear. One of them held an umbrella, absurdly, uselessly, in wind like this.

Voss.

He wasn't pointing a gun. He didn't need to. He simply stood there, patient as arithmetic, blocking the path, and raised one hand—not a threat, a greeting—and Wren understood with terrible clarity that this was the trap's final shape. The tunnels to flush them out. The apron to funnel them. The enforcer waiting at the end of it with an offer no drowning man could refuse.

Zephyrine stepped forward, putting himself between Voss and the rest of them, and Wren saw his hand close around something in his jacket—the ledger pages, the only physical copy, the thing worth more than all their lives and priced accordingly.

And high above, the clouds finally broke open all the way, and the monsoon came down on the Kalyani's source like a verdict.
