## 20. Downstream

Tomas had navigated the Kalyani for nineteen years, and in all that time he had never once considered the river as something you could ride *backward*.

The flood changed the math. The spillway gates had opened at dawn, and by midmorning the lower gorge was no longer a river but a brown, boiling artery carrying roughly a million tons of water per hour toward the sea—and somewhere inside it, riding its shoulder like fleas on a bull, the *Kestrel's Wake*.

"Throttle down!" Priya shouted from the engine hatch, one arm hooked through the rail, hair plastered flat against her skull. "We're outrunning our own wake!"

"That's not possible!" Wren yelled back.

"Everything's possible today!"

Tomas held the wheel with both hands and let the river do most of the steering. That was the secret nobody told you about flood-running: you didn't fight it, you didn't even really pilot it. You made small corrections and prayed they were the right ones. A half-point of rudder here. A breath of throttle there. The difference between threading the channel and becoming a smear of paint on the limestone.

Behind them, the dam shrank into the rain. He kept checking anyway. Kept expecting to see the Syndicate boats come around the crest, black hulls, searchlights, the enforcer's polite gray umbrella somehow unfurled even in this.

Nothing. Just water falling out of a sky that had no bottom in it.

Zephyrine came aft from the bow, moving hand over hand along the lifeline like a man much older than he was. There was a cut above his eye that Priya had glued shut, and his left sleeve was soaked through with something that wasn't river water. He'd been bleeding when they cleared the station tunnels. Nobody had asked how badly. Nobody had wanted to know the answer while they were still inside.

"Status," Zephyrine said. Not a question. A request for permission to keep standing.

"Channel holds for another six klicks," Tomas said. "After Serpent Bend it widens out and we can breathe. Maybe."

"Maybe."

"The river's doing the work. We're just passengers."

Zephyrine looked at him for a long moment, rain streaming off his chin, and then he did something Tomas had never seen him do in twelve years of knowing him. He laughed. Short, cracked, half a cough—but a laugh.

"Passengers," he said. "Twelve years I've been running from this river. Turns out all I had to do was let it carry me."

Then he went forward again, because Zephyrine Winterbourne could only stand still for so long even when stillness was the only sane option, and Tomas turned back to the wheel and the gray wall of rain and the drowned world rushing past.

* * *

They passed the first town at midafternoon.

Rampur should have been shuttered. Flood season on the lower Kalyani meant shutters and sandbags and everyone indoors listening to the water climb their steps. Instead the whole riverfront was lined with people—hundreds of them, standing in the rain under tarps and tin roofs and bare umbrellas, every face turned toward the water.

Tomas understood why before he saw the source of it.

Every radio in Rampur was playing the same broadcast. It carried across the water in fragments, snatched up and torn apart by wind: *"—engineering ledgers, dated four years prior to the collapse, bearing the countersignature of Meridian Regional Director—"*

Odile's voice. Recorded before they'd gone into the station, or spliced together from the ledger readings themselves—Tomas couldn't tell, and it didn't matter. What mattered was that she'd built the emergency-band transmission to repeat on a loop, and the loop had escaped the high valley and come downriver faster than any boat, hopping tower to tower, town to town, the way news traveled along the Kalyani: by radio, by rumor, by the river people's own relay of signal fires and shouted messages across the narrows.

The people of Rampur were standing in the rain listening to proof that the flood which had taken their homes eleven years ago hadn't been an act of God.

A woman on the embankment raised her fist as they passed. Others followed—not cheering exactly, nothing so clean as cheering. It was more like a sound the crowd made involuntarily, a low roar that rose and fell with the loop of Odile's voice, and Tomas felt it go through the hull and up through his boots and settle somewhere behind his ribs.

Wren stood at the rail with both hands gripping it, watching the shore slide by, and said nothing at all. Which, from Wren, said everything.

"They knew," the kid finally said. "Everyone down here already knew. In their bones. They just never had the paper."

"Now they have the paper," Priya said.

* * *

Serpent Bend came and went almost gently—the same checkpoint where, weeks ago, a Syndicate enforcer had studied their forged papers with patient, terrible courtesy while logging their passage into a book that would eventually be used against them. The toll post was abandoned. The barrier arm hung crooked, snapped halfway down its length, and someone had painted across the checkpoint shack's front wall in letters two meters tall:

**THE RIVER REMEMBERS**

Below it, smaller, in a different hand: *Meridian men went in the night. If you are Meridian, the river is not done.*

Tomas took the bend wide and slow. His hands were steady. His hands had been steady for nineteen years; that was what he was for. But his mouth had gone dry, and he found himself thinking about the logbook. Somewhere in that shack was a page with their names on it, their false registry number, the date they'd passed upstream. Evidence. A thread the Syndicate could pull.

He mentioned it to Zephyrine, who considered it and then shook his head.

"Let them have it," Zephyrine said. "It doesn't matter anymore. Everything they could do to us with that page, they're going to try regardless. And everything we did—we did it in the open now." He nodded at the painted wall as it slid past. "That's the whole point. We're not hiding."

"You spent your whole life hiding," Tomas said. It wasn't an accusation. It was just true, and between the two of them it had never needed to be anything else.

"Yeah," Zephyrine said. "Working on it."

* * *

Night caught them in the wide water below the bend, where the flood spread out and lost some of its fury, and for the first time since the dam Tomas allowed himself to take stock of what they actually were.

The *Kestrel's Wake* was dying. She'd taken water in the tunnels below the station—Priya had sealed two seams with epoxy and pure stubbornness, and a third seam was weeping in a way that Priya described, with engineer's precision, as "a problem for tomorrow morning." Half their supplies had gone in the ambush at the Gorge of Bells. The fuel gauge read a quarter and lied, probably. The radar dome was gone entirely, sheared off by a tunnel lintel Tomas still dreamed about.

And the crew. He counted them the way he counted channel markers, automatically, constantly: Zephyrine at the bow, keeping watch out of habit though there was nothing left behind them to watch for. Priya below, sleeping three hours and then coming up to check the bilge, sleeping three more. Wren curled in the cockpit under a tarp, asleep the way only teenagers and cats can sleep—instantly, totally, as if the body had simply overruled the mind.

And Odile Vasseur, sitting across from Tomas at the little chart table under the dodger, drying documents that no longer needed drying.

She'd barely spoken since the crest. Since Zephyrine had stood on the dam with the ledgers in his hands and the enforcer's offer hanging in the air—*your debt, erased, everything forgiven*—and had instead keyed the emergency band and let the pages go into the flood. Tomas had watched it happen from thirty meters away through rain and terror, and even now, twelve hours later, he could not fully assemble it into sense. Zephyrine had owed the Meridian Syndicate more money than a man earns in twenty years of honest work. They had offered to erase it. All of it. One match, one moment, gone.

He'd thrown away the match.

"He'll want to talk about it eventually," Odile said, following his gaze toward the bow. "Men like him always do. Later. When the adrenaline drains out and there's nothing left but the arithmetic."

"What arithmetic?" Tomas said. "He chose right. There's no arithmetic after that."

"There's always arithmetic." She smoothed a ledger page flat—her own copy, the photographs, the negatives wrapped in oilcloth. "He gave up the debt. That means he gave up the money I promised him too. He's a man who has now lost everything twice. Those men either break or they get very quiet and very clear about what matters." She looked up, and in the lamplight her face was older than it had been at the start of this, and less armored. "I've watched him be restless for fifteen years, Tomas. I think tonight is the first time I've seen him sit still."

Tomas glanced forward. Zephyrine wasn't sitting still—he was standing his eternal watch—but there was something different in the set of his shoulders. Something set down.

The radio murmured on, cycling through the loop, and beneath Odile's recorded voice Tomas could hear the other traffic now. The band had come alive all evening. River towns calling river towns. The flood-relay network the settlements had maintained for generations, the one no law had ever managed to shut down, carrying the same words up and down three hundred kilometers of water:

*Confirming ledger contents. Confirming signatures.*

*Rampur survivors' committee convening.*

*Any Meridian vessels in your reach? Report movements.*

*The enforcer's boat passed Kalpet going downstream, running dark, not answering hails.*

Tomas sat up straighter. "Say that last part again?"

The relay repeated it. The enforcer's vessel, running dark, heading downriver ahead of them. Fleeing.

"Not answering hails," Odile said softly. "Do you understand what that means? A Meridian regional enforcer does not run dark. A Meridian enforcer stands on his deck with his lights blazing and his papers in order and dares the river to question him. Running dark means—" she paused, choosing the word with care, "—means he's no longer certain anyone will honor his papers."

"The broadcast," Tomas said.

"The broadcast. Every debt he's ever collected, every family he's ruined on behalf of that ledger—it's all in the open now, and he knows it. He isn't retreating from us." She folded the oilcloth closed. "He's retreating from *them*. From everyone. You don't fail twice in front of witnesses. Not when the witnesses have radios."

Exhaustion edged with hope—that was the feeling, Tomas decided, watching the dark water slide past. Like the last kilometer of a long portage, when your shoulders are ruined and your hands are bleeding and yet somehow your legs keep finding the next step, because somewhere ahead there is water again, real water, going the direction you chose.

* * *

Dawn found them at the mouth of the Kalyani's middle reach, where the flood met the incoming tide and the water went briefly, eerily calm—a mile of slack brown glass with the wreckage of the storm scattered across it: branches, roof tin, a child's doll seated upright on a floating door, staring at the sky.

Priya surfaced from the engine hatch looking like a woman who had fought a war in a space the size of a coffin, and reported, "She'll hold. Barely. Get me a day in a proper yard and I'll get her seaworthy. Get me two days and I'll get her pretty."

"We don't need pretty," Zephyrine said.

"Speak for yourself. I need pretty. I've earned pretty."

Wren laughed—the first real laugh since the tunnels, bright and startling as a bird—and the sound did something to the air in the cockpit, loosened a knot none of them had named. Even Zephyrine smiled, brief and unguarded, and Tomas thought: *there it is.* The thing Odile had meant. The stillness.

The radio crackled. Not the loop this time—an actual voice, live, accented with the clipped vowels of the upper-river communities. The flood-justice council, the one Priya's river people had convened the moment the broadcast reached them.

*"All vessels, all stations. This is the Kalyani Council speaking. Independent investigators are coming up from the coast—journalists, engineers, magistrates. They ask for witnesses. They ask for testimony. To every family the dam took from: they are ready to listen. To every crew that carried this truth downriver: the river owes you, and the river pays its debts."*

Silence in the cockpit. Rain, softer now, tapping the canvas.

"The river pays its debts," Priya repeated quietly. She looked at Zephyrine. "You hear that? Somebody finally said it out loud."

Zephyrine didn't answer right away. He stood at the rail with the middle reach opening ahead of them—wide, slow, going the way they were going—and Tomas watched him measure it, not in miles this time. In something else.

"Wren," he said at last. "Take the wheel."

Wren scrambled up, eyes shining, and took it, and Zephyrine let the kid hold the boat alone in open water while he stood beside them and didn't hover, didn't correct, just watched the channel and the crew and the country coming alive along the banks—people on the levees, people waving, people holding up radios as if the little boxes were lanterns.

"Tomas," Zephyrine said, without turning around. "When we make the coast—how far to the nearest yard that'll take Priya's money?"

"Vashti Point. Two days."

"Good." A pause. The rain thinned. Somewhere far downstream, faint through the morning murk, a church bell or a temple bell or just a buoy bell began to ring, and none of them moved to identify it. "Because I'm thinking we put in at Vashti, fix her properly, and then—" he stopped, and Tomas realized he was waiting for permission, from them, from the river, from whoever kept such ledgers, "—and then I don't know. First time in twelve years I don't know. It's a strange feeling."

"It's called having a choice," Priya said.

"I hate it."

"No, you don't," Wren said, at the wheel, grinning at the water.

Ahead, the river widened toward the sea, carrying everything with it—the ledgers, the doll, the enforcer's darkened boat, the names of Mara and Dev and every town the water had ever taken—and for the first time in nineteen years of reading this river, Tomas looked downstream and felt no dread at all.

Only the next channel. Only the next day.

Only the crew, choosing each other, riding the current home.
