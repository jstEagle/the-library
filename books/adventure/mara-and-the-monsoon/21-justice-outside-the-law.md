## 21. Justice Outside the Law

Priya heard the engines before she saw the boats—two flat-bottomed survey craft flying no colors at all, which on the Kalyani meant one of two things: smugglers or investigators. After everything that had happened, she'd learned to tell them apart by how they took the bend below the settlement. Smugglers cut it close. Investigators came around it slow, like men who had never trusted a river and never would.

She was mending a net on the community dock when they tied off. Three weeks since the flood. Three weeks since Zephyrine stood on the dam crest and gave away the only thing the Meridian Syndicate had ever offered him for free.

The woman who stepped onto the planks wore city boots and carried a tablet in a waterproof case, and she looked at the river people the way Priya had once looked at them herself—with the careful hunger of someone who had been told these people existed and hadn't quite believed it.

"Priya Chandrasekaran?" the woman said.

"No one's called me that in nine years."

"Then I'll use whatever name you prefer. I'm Inspector Anaya Roy, Independent Commission on Infrastructure Integrity." She held out a credential folio. "We're here because somebody broadcast eleven gigabytes of engineering ledgers on an emergency band three weeks ago, and every warrant office from Serpent Bend to the delta has been working eighteen-hour days since."

Priya set down her netting needle. "You're late."

"We're thorough," Roy said. "There's a difference, though I understand why it doesn't feel like one from where you're standing." She glanced past Priya, up the slope toward the stilt houses and the drying racks and the long communal fire pit where the river people held their flood-season councils. "I need testimony. Yours first, if you'll give it. Then the community's. Then—" she checked something on her tablet, and her voice changed, went quieter, almost reverent, "—Zephyrine Winterbourne's, if he'll speak to me at all. Half my colleagues think he's a myth."

"He's fixing a boat hull two docks down," Priya said. "He's very real. He complains about epoxy the way other men complain about weather."

Something in Roy's shoulders loosened—a fraction, the fraction of a person who had spent three weeks reading about drowned towns and bought ledgers and needed to hear that the man at the center of it all still complained about glue.

"I'll want all of it," Roy said. "The checkpoint at Serpent Bend. The sabotage. The gorge. The station."

"You'll get all of it," Priya said. "But you should know something before we start. The Syndicate didn't just build that dam. They built the debts too. My family's included. If your warrants reach as far as the loan offices, say so now, so I know whether I'm testifying or just performing."

Roy met her eyes without flinching. "The warrants reached the loan offices yesterday. Four regional directors, in custody. The debt portfolios are frozen pending review."

Priya had imagined this moment for years—in exile, on other rivers, in the bad hours before dawn. In her imagination there had been shouting, maybe tears, certainly some grand feeling that would finally put down the weight she'd carried since she was twenty-three years old and signed papers she didn't understand to save people she couldn't save.

What she actually felt was the sun on her neck and the net under her hands and a strange, quiet lightness, like a knot she'd stopped noticing coming undone.

"Well," she said. "Then sit down, Inspector. It's a long story, and the river people will want tea while I tell it."

* * *

They testified for six days.

Roy set up under the great shelter roof with a stenographer and a second investigator who asked sharper questions, and one by one the river people came forward. The old ferryman who remembered when the toll posts went up. The midwife whose village had been relocated twice, both times to worse ground, both times against signed promises. A boy of maybe sixteen who described, in a flat voice that made the stenographer stop typing twice, what the water did to his family's fields the year the spillways opened early the first time—not the flood Zephyrine's crew survived, but the rehearsal for it, twelve years of rehearsals, each one logged as weather.

Priya sat through all of it. Partly because Roy asked her to help contextualize, mostly because she owed these people her attention. They had taken her in during a season when she'd trusted no one, fed her without asking what she'd run from, and governed her by a justice that asked only one question—*what does the river require of us now?*—and answered it honestly every time. The least she could do was witness their words being written down somewhere permanent.

On the fourth day, Wren testified.

The kid had dressed for it like it was a court appearance, which in a sense it was—clean shirt, hair combed, the scar on one forearm visible where the sleeve rode up. Priya watched Roy's face as Wren described the flooding tunnels beneath the dam station, the black water rising, hauling Priya out of a side passage by the collar while the emergency lights died section by section.

"You were fourteen when this voyage began?" Roy asked.

"Fifteen now. Birthday was on the river."

"And you went anyway. Knowing the Syndicate was hunting you."

Wren shrugged, the way teenagers shrug when the true answer is too large for the gesture. "Zeph said the price of survival was either money or truth. He picked wrong, honestly. Truth was cheaper." A pause. "No—that's not right. Truth cost more. But it was worth more. There's a difference."

Roy wrote something in the margin of her notes that Priya couldn't read, but she saw the inspector underline it twice.

That evening, Priya found Zephyrine at the fire pit, doing what he always did now: nothing, deliberately, with the concentration of a man learning a new trade. He'd been bad at rest for the first week—hands twitching toward charts that didn't need checking, waking at odd hours to listen for engines. But the season was teaching him. The river people taught by not commenting, and eventually even a man who measured his life in miles started measuring it in mended hulls and full meals and the number of times Wren laughed per day.

"Inspector Roy wants you tomorrow," Priya said, sitting beside him.

"I know. She asked me tonight. Politely. Like scheduling a duel."

"Are you going to give her a hard time?"

"A little. It's traditional." He turned a cup of tea in his hands. "You testified four days. How do you feel?"

Priya considered lying, decided he'd earned better. "Lighter. And angry that it took this long. Both at once. They can coexist, turns out."

"They can," Zephyrine said. "I've been carrying that particular pair for twelve years." He drank his tea. Down at the dock, Odile Vasseur was arguing with a survey crew about draft measurements, gesturing at the water like she could command it, and Zephyrine watched her with an expression Priya had catalogued over three weeks of quiet evenings: not love, not quite; something older and more durable than that. Respect that had survived being wrong about someone.

"She's leaving soon," Priya said. "After the testimony wraps. Back downriver, then to the capital. The commission wants her consulting on the dismantling review."

"I heard."

"What are you going to tell her?"

He smiled faintly. "You'll see tomorrow. You're incapable of not eavesdropping."

"That's slander."

"It's accurate navigation," he said, and Priya laughed despite herself, because it was.

* * *

Zephyrine's testimony took one day, and half the settlement found reasons to be within earshot of the shelter.

Priya listened from the edge of the crowd as he walked Roy through it—the debt collector's umbrella, thirty days, the contract from Odile, Mara's Reach, the informant he'd refused to cast out. When he got to the dam crest, his voice didn't rise or break. It got very level, the way it did at the helm in bad water, and everyone leaning in understood they were hearing a man narrate the exact center of his own life.

"The enforcer offered to erase my debt," he said. "Full forgiveness. All I had to do was burn the ledgers. Eleven gigabytes of proof that the collapse that killed my town—and killed two of my crew—was ordered, budgeted, and signed off. I want the record to show I thought about it. Not for long. But I thought about it, and anyone who says they wouldn't is lying."

"What did you do?" Roy asked, though she knew; everyone knew; it was in the broadcast.

"I put it on the emergency band. Every repeater on the river. And I let the paper copies go into the flood." He paused. "The enforcer called it the most expensive decision of my life. He was wrong. It was the cheapest. Everything it cost, I'd already lost. Everything it bought, nobody could repossess."

Roy was quiet a moment. "Mr. Winterbourne, the commission's mandate includes restitution. Victims of the engineered collapses are entitled to claims against seized Syndicate assets. You'd be among the first eligible."

"I don't want money."

"Most people say that until the forms arrive."

A ripple of laughter through the listening crowd, and Zephyrine let himself smile. "Then lose my form. Give it to the drowned town downstream. Give it to the boy who talked about his family's fields. I had a debt once and I've recently developed strong opinions about the institution."

When it was done, Roy shook his hand in front of everyone, and Priya saw the moment land on him strangely—twelve years of running, and it ended with a handshake and a stenographer's nod, no gunfire, no flood, just the slow machinery of the law grinding into motion at last, late and imperfect and real.

* * *

Odile left on the ninth morning.

Her boat was loaded with survey gear and commission paperwork, and she stood at the dock doing final checks with the same brisk competence she'd shown running rapids, as if departure were just another rapid to be read correctly. Zephyrine walked down with Priya and Wren trailing behind, all three of them pretending this was a casual errand.

"You could come," Odile said, not looking up from a cleat she was testing. "The commission would take you on in a heartbeat. Consultant. Witness coordinator. They'd invent a title if you asked."

"I know."

"There's a real future in it. Legitimacy. Offices. A salary that arrives whether or not it rains."

"Odile."

"I'm making the pitch properly. You deserve the pitch properly."

He stepped closer and took her hand off the cleat, gently, and held it. "Twelve years ago I would have said yes to anything that kept me moving. Six months ago I would have said yes to anything that paid. Right now there's a hull two docks down that needs re-planking, and Wren needs to learn fairleads before storm season, and Tomas is drawing charts of stretches of this river that have never been charted honestly, and I—" he exhaled. "I finally live somewhere. I'm not going to make myself homeless again for a salary."

Odile studied him for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for, she found it. "You used to be the most restless man on any river I worked."

"I still am. I just stopped confusing motion with living."

"So that's a no."

"That's a *not this*. Ask me again after the dismantling review, when they decide what happens to the dam itself. If they tear it down, I want to be on the river for that. If they keep it—" his jaw tightened, "—then maybe they'll need someone stubborn enough to watch it forever. That could be a job with a home port."

"And if neither?"

"Then stay in touch," he said simply. "Radio channel's posted on the dock board. Write letters. Real ones, on paper, I'll frame them. You spent twenty years being my rival, Odile. Don't downgrade to stranger."

She laughed—an actual laugh, unguarded, and Priya realized she'd never heard it before, not once in the whole voyage. "Partnership declined, correspondence requested. You haven't changed at all."

"I've changed completely. I just changed toward the person I was supposed to be." He released her hand. "Go break the Syndicate's bones in court. We'll hold the river."

They embraced, briefly, the way old rivals embrace—like a truce both sides intend to honor. Then Odile boarded, cast off with a flourish, and took the bend below the settlement at exactly the speed of someone with nothing left to hide.

Wren watched the boat shrink. "Do you think she'll write?"

"She'll write," Zephyrine said. "She'll also send charts with corrections marked in red, because she's incapable of letting my navigation stand unchallenged."

"Will you correct back?"

"Aggressively," he said, and Wren grinned, and Priya looked at the two of them—the man who'd chosen justice over money and the kid who'd hauled her out of a flooding tunnel—and felt the last cold thing in her chest finally thaw.

* * *

The warrants came downriver on official paper a week later, and the settlement held a council to read them aloud, because the river people believed documents belonged to everyone they named.

Fourteen names. Regional directors, engineers who falsified the gauge readings, the loan officers who'd built the debt machine Priya's family had been crushed inside. The enforcer's name was on the list too—the polite man with the umbrella from a rain-soaked port city, wanted now in three jurisdictions, fled upcountry according to the last reports. Priya noticed that Zephyrine read that name without flinching, just folded the notice closed and handed it back, as if setting down a weight he'd carried so long his body had forgotten its shape.

Restitution hearings would begin in the spring. The drowned town downstream would get its land question revisited. The reservoir's displaced families would testify with lawyers provided by the commission, free of charge, a phrase Priya repeated to herself that night like a prayer in an unfamiliar language.

She stood at the dock after the council, watching the river move. Nine years an exile, and the exile had ended not with a homecoming but with a staying—here, on this stretch of water, among people who governed themselves by what the river required.

Tomas joined her, chart tube under his arm. "Storm season's six weeks out," he said. "Zeph wants the boat refitted by then. Proper refit, not our usual theology-and-duct-tape approach."

"Where are we going?" Priya asked. It wasn't settled yet. Nothing had been said.

Tomas smiled. "Downstream first—all the towns that heard the broadcast, all the places that testified. Wren wants to see every mile of it. Then upstream, after the review, to watch whatever they decide to do with that dam." He tapped the chart tube. "And Zeph says—his words—we're going as a crew, not as fugitives. First time in twelve years the route isn't an escape plan."

Priya looked downriver, toward the delta, toward the towns already tearing down Syndicate toll posts and burning loan ledgers in the streets. Somewhere out there, the world was rearranging itself around a choice a tired man had made on a dam crest in the rain.

"Six weeks," she said. "Plenty of time."

Behind them, from the direction of the boatyard, came the sound of Zephyrine arguing with Wren about fairleads, patient and endless, a man teaching a river to someone who'd inherit it—and for the first time since Priya had known him, he sounded like he planned to be there for every lesson.
