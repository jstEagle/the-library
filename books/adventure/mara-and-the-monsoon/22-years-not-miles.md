## 22. Years, Not Miles

The first thing I fixed at the settlement was a boat that wasn't mine.

It belonged to an old woman named Sefa, whose hull had been patched so many times with tar and prayer that it looked like a map of every bad decision she'd ever made on the water. I stripped it down to the ribs in the third week of the dry season and rebuilt it plank by plank while the river ran low and clear beneath the stilt village, so transparent you could count the stones on the bottom, so quiet it seemed embarrassed by everything that had happened upstream.

Wren sat cross-legged on the deck beside me, sanding a plank with the patience of someone who'd learned the hard way what happens when you rush a joint.

"Too much pressure," I said, without looking up.

"I know."

"You're still doing it."

"I *know*."

I let her figure out the rest. That was most of teaching, I'd discovered—shutting up. Twelve years of running rivers alone or nearly alone, of measuring my life in miles driven and fuel burned, and it turned out all that accumulated knowledge was worth something only when you handed it to someone else, piece by piece, and watched them drop half of it before they caught the rest.

Sefa's boat took six weeks. When we slid it off the rollers into the current, she walked its length twice, pressed her palm flat against the hull like she was taking a pulse, and said, "You've made her heavier."

"She'll last longer."

"Heavier is slower." But she was smiling, and she paid us in dried fish and a jar of river plum wine that Tomas drank half of before sundown.

That was the economy here. Nobody paid in money if they could help it. The Syndicate's paper had burned through the valley like the flood itself, and what grew back in the scorched ground was older and stranger—obligation, barter, reputation. A debt here meant something different than it had meant to me for twelve years. It meant you were known. It meant people expected you to stay long enough to settle it.

I stayed.

* * *

The dry season deepened. The Kalyani dropped until whole sandbars surfaced like the backs of sleeping animals, and the channel markers the river people maintained—their own law, their own flood-season justice—had to be moved foot by foot downstream. Priya organized the work with the brisk authority of someone who had finally stopped apologizing for existing. She'd found something here, or maybe recovered something she'd buried when the Syndicate first got its hooks into her family. Some evenings I watched her argue hydrology with men twice her age who'd read this river their whole lives, and watched them concede, grudgingly, point by point, and I understood that exile doesn't end when you go home. It ends when you find the place that fits the shape the leaving carved into you.

Tomas charted. Of course he charted. He'd commandeered the settlement's common room, pinned hand-drawn surveys of the upper gorge across three walls, and was redrawing every mile of the route we'd survived—not for money, not for the Syndicate's shipping lanes, but because he said a river nobody mapped honestly was a river waiting to kill somebody. He worked with the ledgers' aftermath spread around him: newsprint clippings, investigator bulletins, the slow public unraveling of the Meridian Syndicate like a hull coming apart in slow motion.

The warrants came in the sixth week. Odile sent word ahead of the investigators themselves—a coded burst on the emergency band we kept tuned now out of habit—and then arrived two days later on a chartered packet boat, stepping onto our dock in clothes too clean for the place, carrying a case of documents and an expression I'd never seen on her: uncertainty.

"They want testimony," she said, sitting across from me at Sefa's table while the plum wine went around. "From all of us. The dam, the spillway order, the enforcer's toll logs. Everything."

"And you?"

"I've already given mine." She turned her glass but didn't drink. "Three days of it. They have the original engineering ledgers authenticated. The collapse was ordered—signed, dated, budgeted. My name is in the margins of some pages." Her jaw tightened. "As a witness. For now."

"For now," Tomas repeated.

"The investigation has a way of expanding," Odile said. "I knew that when I started. I'll answer for what I built." She looked at me then, directly, the way she had on the dock in that rain-soaked port city a lifetime ago, when she'd offered me a contract wrapped around an old wound. "I wanted you to hear it from me before you heard it from a bulletin."

"That's why you came all this way?"

"I came because—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't actually know how to do anything except the next job, Zephyrine. And there isn't one. There's just this. So I'm learning how to sit in a room with people and say true things. It's going badly."

Priya laughed into her wine. Wren grinned. Even Tomas's mouth twitched.

"It gets easier," I said. "Not much. But some."

She stayed four days. On the last evening she stood at the rail of the packet boat and said, "The offer stands. Partnership. Anywhere in the world there's water."

"I know."

"You're staying."

"For a while."

She nodded slowly, and for once didn't try to win. "Send word when the storm season turns. If you change your mind—or even if you don't." Then she was gone downriver, trailing clean laundry and unresolved history, and I stood on the dock watching until the bend took her, feeling something I couldn't name until later, when I realized it was the absence of dread.

For twelve years, every departure had been a small death. Every arrival, a countdown. Watching a boat leave and feeling nothing but the wish for its safe passage—that was new. That was worth more than the debt had ever cost me.

* * *

The season turned the way seasons do when you're actually inside them instead of driving through them: gradually, then all at once.

First the air changed—thicker, sweeter, the smell of wet stone returning to the gorges. Then the birds left the low valleys. Then the river people began their preparations, hauling boats above the high-water lines, restocking the raised storehouses, holding the flood-season council where disputes were settled before the water rose and made old grievances dangerous.

I measured none of it in miles. I measured it in planks replaced, in knots Wren learned and taught back wrong on purpose to see if I was paying attention, in the number of evenings Tomas spent arguing about channel markers, in jars of plum wine. I measured it in a hundred small repairs to a hundred small boats, and in the day Wren soloed the settlement's run through the mid-channel rocks without me in the boat—came back white-faced and vibrating, trying to look casual, failing completely.

"Well?" she demanded.

"You cut the second eddy too fine."

"*Well?*"

"You'd have made it through Mara's Reach." I said it before I could decide whether to. Her face did something complicated. "Clean line. Good hands. You'd have made it."

She looked away fast, blinking at the river, and said, "Obviously," in a voice that cracked in the middle, and I let her have the lie of composure because I remembered being fifteen and needing it.

Mara's Reach had a marker now. The river people raised it in the second month—a post of black ironwood at the head of the rapids, hung with a single bell, in the style of their warning bells through the Gorge. They asked me to speak at the raising, and Dev's name went up beside hers, and I stood in front of sixty strangers and said the truest thing I knew: that the river hadn't killed them. That men had opened gates and called it weather. That remembering the difference was the whole work of justice, and the work wasn't finished, and wouldn't be for years—but that the water belonged to everyone downstream of it again, and that was a beginning.

Afterward, an old boatman put his hand on my shoulder and said, "Now you can stop carrying them. The bell carries them. You just steer."

I didn't sleep that night. I sat on the dock until dawn with the plum wine and watched the first real clouds of the season stack up over the western ridges, blue-black and patient, and I thought about the man I'd been standing under a debt collector's umbrella in that port city, measuring his life in miles driven because miles were the only unit that didn't require him to imagine a future.

Thirty days, the enforcer had given me. I'd used them to run.

And then the running had ended—not with a payment, not with a pardon, but with a choice made on a dam crest in rising water, letting the money drown so the truth could float. The debt had died with the Syndicate's legitimacy. The warrants had gone out. The enforcer himself had vanished somewhere ahead of the investigators, which surprised no one; men like him were always the first rats, and the fastest swimmers.

What remained was this: a dock, a season, a crew.

* * *

They came to me separately, which is how I knew it was real.

Priya first, at the work shed, while we caulked seams. "Storm season's turning," she said. "The upper settlements will need supply runs before the roads wash out. Someone should organize it."

"Someone should."

"I'm volunteering you."

"I noticed."

She drove the caulking iron home with more force than strictly necessary. "Zephyrine. I spent a year feeding your positions to the people who owned my family. You fed false ones back and never once made me say it out loud, never once made me beg." She looked at me straight. "I'm not asking permission to stay near you. I'm telling you where I'll be. The question is whether you'll be on the boat."

Tomas came at night, charts under his arm, and spread them on my table without preamble. "New markers for the dry-season channels. Revised soundings through Serpent Bend—the toll post is abandoned, by the way, stripped to the pilings. And this." He tapped a stretch of the upper gorge. "Gorge of Bells. The river people want to rehang the warning bells properly, and they want a boat that knows the water to ferry the crews. Dangerous work. Paid in fish and gratitude."

"We're rich," I said.

"We're *asked*," he corrected quietly. "There's a difference. I spent enough years being useful to people who paid. I'd like to be useful to people who ask." He rolled up his charts. "I'll be at the boat at first light either way."

Wren didn't come to me at all. Wren was already living on the boat, had been for weeks, had claimed the forward bunk and reorganized my tool locker according to a system that made no sense to anyone but her, and when I finally asked—standing on the dock as the first thunder of the season rolled down from the high country—she looked at me like I'd asked a stupid question, which, to be fair, I had.

"Where else would I be?"

* * *

We launched on the morning the monsoon made landfall.

Not fleeing it. Meeting it. That was the difference, and everyone on the dock understood it, and the river people came down to see us off the way they saw off their own—which is what we were, by then, though no ceremony had declared it. Sefa pressed another jar of plum wine into Tomas's arms. The old boatman who'd talked to me about bells touched Wren's shoulder and said something in the river dialect I didn't catch, and whatever it was, she stood a little straighter after.

The Kalyani was already brown and rising, running hard against the pilings, carrying the first branches and silt of the long wet months down from the source basin—from the valley where I'd grown up downstream of water I never knew was stolen, where the great dam stood with its gates open now under court order, breathing with the monsoon instead of hoarding against it.

I took the helm. Priya had the engine humming sweet, Tomas had the new charts open, Wren stood at the bow reading the water and calling the channel, her voice clear over the rain starting to fall in fat warm drops.

"Debris line off the left bank!"

"I see it."

"Eddy's forming past the marker—there's a backwash behind it."

"I see it," I said again, and smiled, because she was right, and because she knew I'd check anyway, and because checking was love in the language we spoke.

The rain thickened. The settlement fell astern, and then the first bend, and then the low hills, and the river opened ahead of us into the gray-green roaring distance of the wet season, wild and unowned and ours to read.

Twelve years I had driven away from storms. Miles behind me like a debt accruing interest, every horizon a creditor.

This year, the storm came to meet us and we went out to meet it, and between those two motions there was no debt at all—just a boat, a crew that had chosen each other in fire and flood and the long slow work of repair, and a river running free from the source to the sea.

"Course, Captain?" Wren called back, grinning through the rain.

I checked the compass out of habit, then didn't need it.

"Downstream," I said. "All the way downstream."
