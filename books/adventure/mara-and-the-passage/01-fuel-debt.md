## 1. Fuel Debt

The de Havilland came off the lake at 6:14 in the morning, and Freya Dravenmoor felt the old girl hesitate the way she always did now—a half-second of doubt in the engine note, a shudder through the airframe like a woman clearing her throat before saying something unpleasant.

"Come on, Otter," she said, patting the instrument panel with two fingers. "Not today."

The Beaver lifted, wallowed once in its own spray, and climbed into a sky the color of unpolished tin. Below her, Halvorsen Landing slid away: the company town laid out along the shore in neat gray rows, the mill smoke standing up straight in the still air, the fuel drums ranked behind wire fence like soldiers waiting for orders. Somewhere down there was Aldous Wren's office, and somewhere inside that office was a ledger with her name on it, and the number under her name was eleven thousand four hundred and sixty dollars, and it grew every month like something alive.

She'd stopped doing the arithmetic in her head years ago. The ledger did it for her. That was the point of a ledger.

Freya banked north over the tree line and settled into the cruise, one hand loose on the yoke, the other holding coffee gone lukewarm in a dented thermos cup. The interior opened beneath her the way it always did—black spruce to the horizon, lakes stitched between them like dropped coins, no roads, no wires, no voices except the ones she carried in on frequency 123.45 and the ones she made up herself when the silence got too thick.

Three hours to Kettle Lake. Two crates of medicine, a mail sack, a new blade for the mission sawmill. Routine. She'd flown this run forty times, maybe fifty. She knew which lakes froze first and which held open water till Christmas, knew where the caribou crossed the river in October, knew the exact spot over the Narrows where the wind came off the ridge and slapped the tail like a rude hand. The interior didn't surprise you if you paid attention. That was the bargain she'd made with it: attention for survival, hour after hour, year after year.

It was the only honest contract she had left.

* * *

Her father had owned the Otter outright. That was the part people at the Landing forgot, or never knew—how the plane had been theirs, free and clear, before the winter her parents went in through the ice on Sable Water and the company accountant appeared at the funeral with his briefcase and his condolences and a stack of papers explaining how her father's fuel account had been running on credit, how the hangar lease was in arrears, how the estate owed Halvorsen Mining nine thousand dollars and change, and wouldn't it be a shame to lose the aircraft too, a girl with no family and no trade, when there was work available for a pilot willing to fly company runs?

She'd been nineteen. She'd signed.

Eleven years later the number was bigger, not smaller, because the fuel was billed at company rates and the maintenance was billed at company rates and the hangar was billed at company rates, and the medicine runs—the ones nobody else would take, the ones that kept three hundred people alive out past the tree line—paid almost nothing, because the mission couldn't pay more, and Wren knew it, and priced her labor accordingly. Debt wasn't a chain here. Chains you could see. Debt was weather. It was everywhere, and you flew inside it, and sometimes you forgot it was there until it pressed down on you all at once.

Ilsa Voss had tried to warn her, back at the beginning. *Never let them hold your paper, girl. Fly for wages, fly for shares, fly for fish if you have to—but never let them hold your paper.* Ilsa had taught her to fly on floats before she could legally drive, had put her hands gentle on the throttle and said things like *the airplane will tell you the truth even when you don't want to hear it*. Ilsa, who kept her own battered Cub at the far end of the field and asked no questions about anyone's business, and who had gone quiet in a strange way the day Freya signed the Halvorsen contract—quiet not like disapproval but like grief, though Freya hadn't understood that then.

Now the medicine was lashed in the cargo bay behind her, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and the Otter droned north, and the radio stayed dead the way it always did past the Narrows. No repeater stations out here. The company had built them once, for its own traffic, then pulled the maintenance crews when the ore played out, and the towers stood rusting on their ridgelines like gallows nobody used anymore. Past the last tower, you were alone with your own judgment until you landed. Some pilots hated it. Freya had come to think of it as the last honest thing in the territory: out here, nobody was listening, and nobody was lying to you either.

Kettle Lake came up silver through a thin overcast, right on schedule. She set the Otter down in a long swell of spray, taxied to the mission dock, and spent an hour hauling crates while Sister Anselm counted ampoules by lamplight and complained cheerfully about the government, the weather, and the price of aviation fuel, in that order.

"You look tired," the Sister said, handing her a parcel of dried fish for the trip home. "You look like your mother looked, the last year."

"My mother looked fine."

"She looked tired," Sister Anselm said, with the mild immovability of women who had buried half the territory. "Tired is not a sin, Freya. But it is a symptom. Get some sleep before the winter takes what sleep you've been refusing."

Freya laughed, kissed the old woman's papery cheek, and took off again into the afternoon, and the words rode with her for twenty miles before the wind off the Narrows knocked them loose.

* * *

Halvorsen Landing at dusk was lit like a stage set: floodlights on the dock, floodlights on the mill, floodlights on the operations building where the company's business was conducted behind glass that nobody washed because nobody outside ever looked in. She tied down the Otter, logged the flight—two point six hours, fuel purchased, four hundred twelve dollars added to the balance—and walked across the gravel to Operations with the flight log under her arm and the particular heaviness of a debtor reporting to her creditor.

Wren was waiting. He was always waiting; she suspected he slept at his desk the way sharks were said to swim in their sleep, just to keep from missing anything.

Aldous Wren was sixty, narrow, precise, with rimless glasses and a voice like a filing cabinet closing. He ran Halvorsen's northern operations the way a man runs a clock—not cruelly, not warmly, but absolutely, and with total confidence that every gear inside him turned exactly as designed. He did not shake hands. He received them.

"Dravenmoor." He glanced at the log without taking it. "Kettle Lake. On time."

"The weather was kind."

"The weather is rarely kind. It is merely indifferent, which serves the same purpose." He turned a page on his desk blotter, a small precise movement, and looked up. "I have a job for you."

"I'm due for maintenance on the number two cylinder. I told Marsh—"

"Marsh can wait. This pays better than medicine runs." A thin smile, the kind that never reached the glasses. "Survey Team Nine. Kestrel Range. They're mapping the high country ahead of the concession renewals, and they're overdue for resupply. Provisions, mail, some instruments. You know the range?"

"Nobody knows the range. That's why they're surveying it."

Something flickered behind the lenses—amusement, or its simulation. "Quite. Three days' flying, weather permitting. Company rate plus hazard. It would reduce your balance meaningfully." He said *meaningfully* the way other men said *substantially*, and both men meant *enough to matter, not enough to free you.*

Freya looked at the chart he unrolled on the desk: the Kestrel Range in brown contour lines, blank white in the middle of it where the surveys thinned out, a penciled X marking the camp coordinates. High country. Early season. The kind of flying where the weather closed like a door and you hoped you were on the right side of it.

"When do they need it?"

"Yesterday, ideally. Tomorrow, acceptably." He rolled the chart and held it out. When she took it, he kept his fingers on it a moment longer than necessary, a small pressure, a man confirming a signature. "One thing, Dravenmoor. Team Nine works under a federal permit with company funding. Their communications are... irregular. If you find the camp quiet, you don't circle looking for trouble. You land, you deliver, you note conditions, you come home. Am I understood?"

"It's a resupply, Mr. Wren. Not a search party."

"No," Wren agreed softly. "It isn't."

She walked back across the gravel in the dark, floodlights buzzing overhead, and stopped at the fence line where the Otter sat tied down beyond the wire, and stood there a long moment with the chart rolled in her fist like a baton.

There was nothing wrong with the job. Resupply was resupply. She'd flown into a dozen camps where the radio was broken and the crew was surly and the dog had eaten the mail. But the unease sat in her chest anyway, cold and specific, the way it did before weather she couldn't yet see. Wren's fingers on the chart. *If you find the camp quiet.* Why would a man say that? What did he think she'd find?

Overhead the sky had cleared, and the first stars were out over the Kestrels, sharp and indifferent, and somewhere beyond them, in country no map agreed on, Survey Team Nine was waiting for supplies—or wasn't waiting for anything at all.

She'd find out in three days. She unlocked the hangar, checked the tie-downs twice, and went home to a cabin that smelled of kerosene and old charts, where she lay awake listening to the wind come down off the mountains, counting the hours until morning the way she'd once counted dollars.

Eleven thousand four hundred and sixty. Minus hazard pay. Plus fuel.

The ledger never slept, and neither, it seemed, did she.
