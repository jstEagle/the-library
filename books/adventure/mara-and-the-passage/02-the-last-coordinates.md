## 2. The Last Coordinates

The Kestrel Range came up out of the morning haze the way it always did—sudden and indifferent, a wall of blue-grey granite with its teeth in the clouds. Freya had been flying toward it for two hours, climbing in slow spirals to trade altitude for the fuel she couldn't afford to burn, and still it seemed to arrive too fast, as if the mountains had been waiting for her.

"Survey Nine, this is Charlie-Bravo-Four-Six," she said into the mic for the fourth time. Static answered, the same dry hiss it had given her since she'd crossed the last relay station an hour back. She keyed the radio again anyway. "Team Nine, do you copy? Resupply flight inbound from Halvorsen Field. Over."

Nothing. Not even the ghost of a carrier wave.

She checked the frequency against the flight orders clipped to her kneeboard. 126.7. Correct. She checked it again because checking things twice was how you stayed alive up here, and got the same answer both times. The set was working—she could hear the faint mutter of the Whitehorse air route bleeding through on an adjacent band, a pilot complaining about de-ice fluid. The radio worked. Team Nine just wasn't talking.

Freya eased the throttle forward a fraction and watched the fuel gauge do the thing she hated most in the world, which was move. The Otter's engine droned on, steady as a metronome, and beneath that drone she could feel the old airplane thinking, the way you could always feel a de Havilland thinking—every rivet and cable with an opinion about the weather.

The coordinates Wren had given her put the camp in a broad glacial valley on the range's eastern shoulder, a bowl the surveyors had nicknamed the Amphitheatre according to the briefing notes. She'd never been there herself. Nobody had, not really—the Kestrels were one of those places that existed on maps as contour lines and guesswork, drawn from high-altitude photo runs twenty years stale. That was the whole point of Survey Team Nine: go in on foot, put instruments on the ground, turn guesses into facts before the concession permits expired and somebody else's guesses took their place.

She found the valley by following the glacier down from the saddle, the ice running grey-brown with moraine like a river carrying its own ground-up bones. And there was the camp, exactly where the coordinates said it would be—a scatter of orange and canvas in the white, tucked against the lateral moraine below a rock rib, close enough to the glacier tongue to be convenient and far enough above it to be safe.

Or it should have been safe. It should have been a lot of things.

Freya flew the field once at circuit height, slow, flaps half down, the way Ilsa had taught her to read a strip before committing to it. *Eyes first, wheels second. An airstrip can lie to you, but only if you let it.* There was no airstrip here, just a landing zone on the glacier's ablation flat where the previous supply runs had touched down on skis. She could see the old ski tracks from the October run, weathered soft, half-filled with windblown snow.

No new tracks led away from camp. None at all.

That was the first wrong thing, and she noted it the way she noted everything, in the flat procedural voice of the checklist she kept in her head. Fresh snow had fallen two nights ago—she'd flown under the edge of that system coming north, had watched it dust the foothills like flour through a sieve. Two days of snow would bury tracks, sure. But it wouldn't bury them *evenly*. It wouldn't leave the camp looking like a photograph of itself.

She circled twice more. Smoke should have been rising from the cook tent's stove pipe. Nothing. The radio mast stood up out of the snow with its guy wires taut, antenna intact. The supply cache tarps were weighted down with rocks, corners neat. A line of survey flags ran off across the flat toward the glacier head, red pennants hanging dead-still in the cold air.

Nobody moved anywhere in all of it.

"Nine, this is Four-Six," she said one more time, knowing it was pointless, saying it because protocol was a rope and when things went wrong you held onto the rope. "I'm coming in."

* * *

The ski landing was textbook, which annoyed her, because she wanted something to be wrong with the landing, some small friction to explain the cold knot sitting behind her sternum. The skis touched, the Otter settled, and she let it run out long before swinging wide and parking tail-to-wind a hundred meters from the tents. She sat for a moment with the engine idling, scanning through the windshield.

Then she shut down, because a running engine burns fuel, and fuel was money, and money was the ledger, and the ledger was the shape of her whole life. She pulled her parka hood up, took the shotgun from behind the co-pilot's seat—it was company policy for wildlife country, bears mostly, though she'd never once needed it—and stepped down onto the glacier.

The silence hit her like a change in pressure. No generator hum. No voices. Just the enormous quiet of the ice, and somewhere far off the tick and groan of the glacier moving, a sound like a door opening very slowly in another room.

Her boots crunched. That was all the sound there was.

The camp resolved as she walked into it. Main sleeping tent, orange nylon, door zipped shut. Cook tent beside it, larger, stovepipe standing. Instrument crates stacked and lashed under a fly. The surveyors' personal gear racked on a line between two poles—parkas, mitts, a pair of socks frozen stiff as shingles. A shovel leaned against a snow block where someone had cut a kitchen well into the drift. Everything squared away. Everything in its place.

Everything exactly as it would look if eleven people had simply stepped out of existence between one breath and the next.

"Hello the camp!" Her voice went out flat across the ice and came back to her off the rock rib, thinner. "Survey Nine! Anyone?"

The cook tent's zipper ran smooth when she pulled it. Inside, the Coleman stove sat on the mess table, and when she laid her bare hand near—not on, near—the burner housing, she felt warmth. Faint. The memory of heat, the way a stone holds the day's sun after dark. Someone had cooked here this morning. Maybe hours ago. In this cold, that warmth didn't linger long.

On the table: three tin plates, washed and inverted to drain. A pot of coffee, frozen solid, still a third full. A deck of cards mid-game, solitaire or some team variant, fanned out and abandoned. A paperback novel face-down on someone's bunk roll, spine cracked at page two hundred and something.

Freya stood in the doorway of the cook tent for a long moment, letting the details file past her one by one, because that was what you did—you made the list, you kept the list, the list kept you. Plates washed. Coffee unfinished. Cards out. Book open. Stove warm.

People did not walk away from warm coffee. People did not leave a card game to freeze on the table. If they'd evacuated—if weather or wildlife or some emergency had driven them out—they'd have grabbed the book, dumped the plates, killed the stove. You didn't leave a lit stove unattended in bear country or any other kind of country; every kid in the north learned that before they learned long division.

And there were no tracks. She walked the perimeter twice, widening each loop, out past the cache, out to the edge of the ablation flat where the fresh snow lay unbroken to the moraine in every direction like a page no one had written on. Eleven people, leaving a warm camp, in fresh snow, would leave tracks. Would leave *some* sign. Even if they'd gone out onto the glacier, even if wind had scoured the ice surface, there'd be something—dropped gear, a broken flag, a note under a rock on the cook table, because that was the oldest rule of the bush: you always left word of where you'd gone.

There was no word. There was nothing.

Except the logbook.

* * *

It lay on the folding camp desk in the survey tent, which was how she found it—open, centered almost precisely on the blotter, as if someone had placed it there deliberately for the next person to find. The survey tent held the team's work tables, the drafting board, the theodolite case, the shortwave set with its headphones hung neatly on the hook. Freya stood over the logbook without touching it for a good thirty seconds.

Protocol said: do not disturb the site. Protocol said: report, withdraw, await instructions. Protocol had been written by people who assumed that whatever you found at an abandoned camp would make sense.

She touched the pages anyway. They were stiff with cold but not frozen—someone had been writing recently.

The entries were routine, beautiful, boring. Barometric readings. Instrument calibrations. *R. Okonkwo, leader — traverse to Station 14 complete, vertical angle check within tolerance.* Day after day of it, in four different hands, the careful shorthand of professionals doing careful work. She recognized Ruth Okonkwo's handwriting from the briefing file—small, slanted, unhurried. The team had been pushing stations up-valley toward the saddle for three weeks, mapping the drainage, shooting angles, living the ordinary miracle of making a map where no map had been.

The final entry was dated yesterday. It was not in Okonkwo's hand. It was not in any of the four hands she'd learned to recognize going backward through the book. The letters were large, pressed hard enough to emboss the next page, written by someone whose pen had shaken:

**PASSAGE REAL**

Two words. No coordinates appended. No signature. No time notation, though every other entry in the book carried one down to the minute.

Freya became aware that she was holding her breath, and let it out slowly, watching it fog and vanish in the cold tent air.

Passage real. Passage real. She turned the phrase over like a stone found in a boot. Survey teams didn't write poetry in official logs. They wrote numbers. Whatever the passage was—a pass through the range, a route, a feature—they'd found it, and whoever wrote this had been so far outside the boundaries of normal that the logbook's own grammar had broken around them.

She photographed everything. The camera was hers, bought secondhand with money she absolutely did not have, because a pilot who flew alone into country like this wanted a record of what she saw, and because some animal part of her had learned—flying medicine into valleys where the company preferred there be no records—that photographs were the only testimony that couldn't be restructured like a debt.

She shot the camp from the air, wide, then closer. She shot the unbroken snow in a full circle, panning, so no one could claim she'd missed a track. She shot the cook tent interior, the warm stove, the cards, the coffee. She shot the logbook closed, then open, then a close frame of the final entry with her own pencil laid beside it for scale. She shot the radio set, the antenna guys, the instrument crates, the neat rack of parkas. Forty frames. She counted them twice.

Against protocol. Every frame of it. Wren's orders had been explicit: resupply, confirm status, depart. *If the situation is anomalous, Ms. Dravenmoor, document minimally and return.* Document minimally. As if dread were something you could ration.

She stood in the survey tent with the camera heavy against her chest and made herself think it through like a professional instead of feeling it like a person. Possibilities. One: the team had walked out—destination unknown, reason unknown, leaving no tracks, no note, and their warm stove. Rejected. Two: the team had been taken—by whom, by what, leaving no struggle, no blood, no scattered gear, in a camp that looked less like a crime scene than a showroom. Possible, and worse the longer she looked at it. Three—

Three was the two words in the logbook, and she didn't have a shape for it yet, and the not-having was the worst part.

Outside, the light had changed. She noticed it the way pilots notice things—peripherally first, then all at once. The western sky over the saddle had gone the color of pewter, and the cloud deck that had been sitting at eight thousand feet that morning was now at five and lowering, boiling slowly up the valleys like smoke filling a room. The Kestrels made their own weather; everyone knew that. Everyone also knew that when the range decided to close, it closed like a trap.

Freya zipped the logbook shut—then stopped, reopened it, tore a blank page from the back, and wrote in her own square pilot's print: *CBFS resupply arrived 1030. Camp deserted, no tracks, stove warm. Final log entry: PASSAGE REAL. Departing ahead of weather. Will report. —F. Dravenmoor.* She weighed the corner of the page under the theodolite case where anyone returning would find it. It was probably useless. It was the rope, and you held the rope.

She walked back to the Otter fast now, the wind beginning to talk in the guy wires of the radio mast, a low rising note like a finger circling the rim of a glass. Preflight was quick—she'd landed forty minutes ago, nothing to check but her own nerve. She climbed in, primed, mags on, and the old radial caught on the third blade and settled into its drone, and the sound of the engine in that silent valley was suddenly so loud and so human that her eyes stung.

Taxiing out onto the flat, she took one last look at the camp through the side window. Orange tents. Dead flags. Warm stove cooling by degrees under a roof of lowering cloud. Eleven sets of gear and not one person to wear them.

*Passage real.*

She lined up, pushed the power in, and the skis hissed over the crust, and the Otter lifted off the glacier into air that was already starting to move. She turned east, away from the saddle, away from the boiling cloud, and began the long climb out of the valley with the fuel gauge falling and the photographs safe in her jacket and the two words sitting in her mind like a splinter too deep to draw.

Behind her, the Kestrel Range swallowed its own valley, and the first flakes came down fine and dry as ash, and by the time she crossed the eastern ridgeline the Amphitheatre was gone—not hidden, *gone*, erased behind white as thoroughly as if it had never been on any map at all.

Freya flew south into the failing light and understood, with the calm certainty of a woman reading her own instruments, that whatever she told Aldous Wren tonight, she would tell him less than she knew.

And that the photographs were going to matter.
