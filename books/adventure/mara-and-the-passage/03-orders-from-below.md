## 3. Orders from Below

The Halvorsen airfield at Copper Bend sat on a shelf of gravel above the river, and from the air it looked like what it was: a company town's front tooth. Two hangars, a fuel farm, a long tin-roofed operations building with the Halvorsen star stamped on it in paint that had faded to the color of old bone. Freya brought the de Havilland in low over the water, checked her gear twice, and set down on the strip with the particular care of a woman landing an aircraft she did not own and could not replace.

The tailwheel kissed gravel. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere behind her, two hundred miles north, the Kestrel Range was already swallowing its weather, and Team Nine's camp was under fresh snow by now, stove cold, logbook closed or not closed, six men and women simply gone.

She sat in the cockpit for a full minute before she climbed out. It was a habit Ilsa had taught her—*sit still until you know why your hands are shaking*—and her hands were shaking, though she'd have sworn to anyone who asked that they weren't.

Aldous Wren was waiting at the ops building. That was the first wrong thing.

Wren did not meet aircraft. In three years of flying company runs out of Copper Bend, Freya had dealt with the fuel clerk, the freight foreman, a rotating cast of junior accountants who signed her chits and never looked up from their ledgers. Wren ran the district from an office in the mine headquarters forty miles south, and he communicated by telex, in language so dry it squeaked. He had met her exactly once, at her contract signing, where he'd shaken her hand like a man testing the weight of a fish.

Now he stood on the apron in a wool overcoat too good for the gravel dust, hands clasped behind his back, watching her taxi in with the expression of a host whose guest has arrived precisely on time.

"Dravenmoor." He said it before she'd even cut the engine, which meant he'd been standing there through her whole approach. "Good flight?"

"Routine." She swung down onto the gravel. Her legs felt strange after the long haul north, the ground seeming to tilt a degree off true. "Except for the part where there was nobody at the camp."

"Yes." Wren smiled. It was a small smile, economical, the kind a man practices in a mirror until it costs him nothing. "Let's talk inside. You'll want coffee."

She didn't want coffee. She wanted to file her report, refuel, and get back into the air while the weather window held, because the barometer on the wall of her skull had been falling since the saddle country and she trusted it more than any forecast. But Wren was already walking, and she was already following, and she understood—with a clarity that arrived cold and unwelcome—that this was how it worked now. The debt didn't just own her plane. It owned the shape of her days.

***

The operations building smelled of kerosene heat and carbon paper. A clerk took her flight bag without being asked, which she hated, and poured coffee without asking how she took it, which she hated more. Wren settled behind a desk that was conspicuously clear—not a working desk but a stage—and gestured her into the chair across from it.

"Tell me about the camp."

She told him. She kept it flat and procedural, the way reports were supposed to go: arrived on schedule, 1400 local; camp intact; equipment stowed; no personnel; no sign of disturbance; fresh snowfall showing no tracks in or out; departed 1500 ahead of a building front. She did not mention the warm stove. She did not mention the logbook. She heard herself say *no sign of disturbance* and watched Wren receive it, and something in her chest went tight and strange, because she had just lied by omission to the man who owned her wings, and the lie had come out smooth as fuel through a clean line.

"You photographed the site," Wren said.

It wasn't a question. Protocol required photographs of every resupply site, and Wren had written half the protocols himself. Still, the way he said it—*you photographed the site*, mild as milk—made her feel like a card had been turned face-up on a table.

"Standard coverage," she said. "Camp layout, cache condition, airstrip."

"Of course." He opened a drawer, removed a single sheet of paper, and laid it on the desk between them, squared to the desk's edge with two fingers. "Here's what I need from you, Dravenmoor. A short report. One paragraph. Survey Team Nine has been relocated ahead of deteriorating conditions per operational planning. All equipment accounted for. No anomalies."

Freya looked at the paper. It was typed already. Typed, except for a blank line where the date and her signature would go.

"You've written my report for me."

"I've drafted a summary to save us both time." Wren folded his hands. "You'll want to review it against your recollection, naturally. If anything differs—" the small smile again, "—I'm sure we can reconcile the discrepancy."

"Six people are missing." She heard her own voice and made herself keep it level. "That's not a discrepancy. That's the whole report."

"Is it?" Wren leaned back. Outside the frosted window, a loader moved ore bins along the tram line, small and silent as toys. "What do you actually know, Dravenmoor? You know a camp was tidy. You know a stove was—what, warm? Tepid? You know snow fell on ground that showed no tracks, which is what snow does. Teams relocate. Radio fails. Men walk out over ridges you couldn't cross with an airplane. The interior eats survey crews the way it eats everything else, and the department files them as delayed, and half the time they turn up in a trapping cabin playing cards, and the other half they don't." He shrugged, a small precise motion. "What you know is weather and absence. Everything else is a story you're telling yourself at altitude."

"The logbook—"

"Was blank, I'd expect. Or near enough." His eyes came up and held hers, and for the first time she saw the thing underneath the courtesy, and it was patience. Bottomless, unhurried patience. "Here is what I know, Dravenmoor. I know you owe this company eleven thousand four hundred dollars, plus interest accruing monthly, secured against an aircraft you cannot fly out of this district without our clearance stamps. I know your license endorsements were co-signed by this office. I know that a pilot with a clean record and a cooperative disposition can expect her obligations to be reviewed favorably at the next quarterly assessment." He let that sit. "Restructured. Possibly reduced. Terms have been known to improve dramatically for pilots who demonstrate discretion."

The word landed in the room and stayed there. *Discretion.* Freya looked at the drafted report, at the blank line waiting for her name, and understood that she was being offered a price, and that the offer itself was the confession—that whatever had happened to Team Nine, Aldous Wren already knew the shape of it, and wanted her signature on a document that would make her complicit in the telling.

And the worst of it—the part she would lie awake turning over—was that some tired, frozen, debt-hollowed corner of her had wanted to take the pen. Eleven thousand dollars. Four more years of flying medicine runs at company rates, or one paragraph and a clean slate. The arithmetic was simple. The arithmetic was always simple. That was the trap of arithmetic.

"Team relocation," she said finally. "No anomalies."

"That's all it says."

"It's not all it means."

Wren studied her for a moment, and then he laughed—a short, genuine-sounding sound that frightened her more than the smile had. "You remind me of someone," he said. "Doesn't matter. Sign when you've thought it over. The clerk will hold your fuel chit until the paperwork clears." He rose, ending the audience the way a man closes a ledger. "Get some sleep, Dravenmoor. You look like you flew two hundred miles through weather."

She hadn't signed. She knew, walking out, that not signing was itself a move on a board she couldn't see the edges of, and that Wren had noted it, and filed it, and would wait.

***

Her room at the bunkhouse was eight feet wide and smelled of other pilots' cigarettes. She ate tinned beans standing at the counter, then sat on the cot with the camera case open on her knees and the camp photographs spread across the blanket like a hand of cards she didn't know how to play.

There were thirty-one exposures. She went through them slowly, the way Ilsa had taught her to run a preflight—same order, every time, no skipping, because the skip is where the failure hides.

Cache tarps, weighted and neat. Tent lines, taut. The instrument crates, lashed. The radio mast, standing. The galley fly, the food boxes stacked by weight, the medical kit she'd resupplied sitting unopened on its crate. The airstrip markers. The ridge line to the west, white and indifferent.

Then the survey line.

She'd taken those shots almost as an afterthought—protocol coverage of the flagged route leading east from camp toward the saddle, the wooden stakes Team Nine had driven at intervals to mark their traverse. Standard practice. Stake every chain, flag every stake, so the mapmakers back in the capital could tie field positions to fixed points.

In the first photograph, taken from the airstrip looking east, the line of stakes marched away toward the saddle, red flags bright against the snow.

In the second photograph, taken twenty minutes later from the edge of camp at a lower angle, the nearest stakes were bare wood.

No flags.

She set the photos side by side on the blanket and stared at them until her eyes burned. Then she dug out the third shot—the close-up of the cache area she'd taken while checking inventory—and found what she'd walked past without seeing: a scatter of torn ribbon in the trampled snow by the tool rack, red scraps, dozens of them, and beside the rack, half-buried, the glint of a pulled stake tossed down like kindling.

Somebody had gone down that survey line and stripped every flag and hauled every stake. Not lost them. Not burned them for fuel—there was a full woodpile. Pulled them, deliberately, and left the wreckage of it in plain sight of the tool rack, because whoever did the work had been interrupted, or had stopped caring, or had wanted the job done fast rather than done clean.

Teams don't pull their own stakes. Stakes are the whole product—the physical record, the thing the maps are built from. You guard them. You log them. If Ruth Okonkwo's crew had relocated, they'd have carried their books and left their stakes standing for the follow-up party.

Unless the relocation wasn't theirs.

Freya sat very still on the cot, listening to the wind work at the eaves, and arranged the photographs in sequence on the blanket: flags standing, flags gone, ribbon in the snow, stake by the tool rack. Four pictures telling one story, and the story was that somebody had begun erasing Team Nine's work before Team Nine finished leaving—or being left.

*PASSAGE REAL.*

The words surfaced unbidden, in handwriting she'd seen for less than ten seconds through an open logbook, and she pressed her palms against her eyes. She should burn the negatives. That was the smart play. Wren had told her exactly what the smart play was, priced it against her debt, and left the pen on the desk. Sign the paragraph, keep the plane, forget the range.

Instead she got up, cleared the bunkhouse table, and printed fresh copies of the four photographs in the dim red light of the developing cloth she kept for documenting cargo damage—copies Wren's clerks would never see. She sewed them, flat and thin, into the lining of her flight jacket, under the patch where Ilsa's old squadron pin used to sit.

Then she lay down in the dark and did not sleep, and somewhere past two in the morning, through the frost-furred window, she saw the fuel truck roll out to her parked aircraft and stop beside it, and a man in a company coat spend ten minutes at the Cowling with a flashlight, unhurried, thorough, going over her plane the way Wren had gone over her story.

They were checking whether she'd hidden anything aboard.

At dawn the clerk handed her the unsigned report, still waiting, and her fuel chit, and told her with practiced blandness that the northern strip was closed to civilian traffic pending an engineering inspection—effective immediately, retroactive to yesterday, duration indefinite.

The range was sealed. The only way anyone would ever learn what happened to Team Nine was if it walked out on its own feet.

Three days later, a trapper's voice crackled through the static on the evening schedule, garbled and faint, asking anyone on frequency whether the company sent someone for a man he'd found on the glacier moraine—half-frozen, out of his head, repeating numbers.

Numbers, the trapper said. Like coordinates.
