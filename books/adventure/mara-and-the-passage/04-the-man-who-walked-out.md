## 4. The Man Who Walked Out

The radio crackled to life at 6:14 in the morning, while Freya was still nursing the first coffee of the day in the operations shack, watching frost crawl up the inside of the windows.

"Kestrel Station to any station, Kestrel Station to any station." The voice was old and rough as gravel, a man talking through a bad connection and worse breath. "I got a body walking out of the moraine. Half dead. You copy?"

Freya set down her cup. "Station receiving. Say again, caller."

"A man. On foot. Came down off the glacier ice like he'd been walking for days." A pause, static chewing at the edges of his words. "He's got no coat worth mentioning. No pack. He keeps saying numbers."

"What kind of numbers?"

"Coordinates. Over and over. Won't stop."

She felt it in her chest before she understood it—the cold drop, the way a floor gives slightly underfoot when something underneath has shifted. She knew those coordinates. She had photographed them three days ago, inked into an open logbook on a table in an abandoned camp, next to two words written in a steady hand: *PASSAGE REAL.*

"Caller, identify yourself."

"Henrik Sool. I trap the east fork, forty years same line. The man's got a survey badge on him. Team Nine." Another pause, longer this time. "You're the one who flew their resupply, aren't you? Word travels. You want him or not?"

*Did she want him.* Wren's voice was already in her ear: *file it routine, Dravenmoor. Relocated. Nothing unusual.* And here was the nothing-unusual, half-frozen on a trapper's floor, repeating numbers into the dark.

"I'll come get him," she said.

* * *

Sool's cabin sat where the treeline gave up, a squat timber box with a rack of pelts frozen stiff along its outer wall and smoke standing straight out of the stovepipe in the windless cold. The trapper met her on the packed snow, a big weathered man with a beard full of ice crystals, and he looked at her plane with open suspicion.

"You Halvorsen?" he asked.

"No."

"That's not what I asked."

Freya held his gaze. "I fly for them. That's not the same thing."

He considered that for a long moment, then jerked his head toward the cabin. Inside it was dim and close, smelling of woodsmoke, tallow, and animal fat. The man lay on a cot near the stove, wrapped in every blanket Sool apparently owned, and at first glance Freya thought he was younger than she expected—mid-twenties maybe, sharp-featured, lips cracked and scabbed, hands bandaged where the cold had gotten into them.

His eyes were open. That was the worst part. They were open and fixed on the ceiling beams, and his mouth was moving.

"—forty-one degrees, eleven minutes north, sixty-three—" His voice was a ruin, scraped down to almost nothing. "—sixty-three degrees, twenty-two minutes west. Elevation—elevation matters, elevation—"

"Elias Brandt," Sool said. "Badge says so. Junior cartographer, if you can read the fine print." He crouched by the cot and pressed the back of his hand to the man's forehead with a gentleness that seemed practiced, like he'd done it for others over forty hard winters. "Fever's been climbing since yesterday. He walked maybe thirty klicks off the ice, alone, in boots that weren't made for it. Man should be dead."

"Any sign of the others?"

Sool looked at her. "You know there were others."

"I know there was a camp," Freya said carefully. "Empty."

"Then you know more than me." The trapper straightened. "I found one man. One. Forty years on this land, I've found drowned men, frozen men, men the bears got. Never once found a whole team vanish and leave one walker behind." He crossed his arms. "So I'll ask again. You Halvorsen?"

"Yes," she said. "And I'm taking him to a doctor."

Sool studied her the way she imagined he studied tracks—reading depth, age, direction, whether the thing that made them was running or walking calm. Whatever he read in her face seemed to satisfy him, or at least not dissatisfy him enough to argue.

"He won't last another night out here regardless," he said. "Take him. But hear me, pilot." He stepped close, and his voice dropped low. "Whatever company owns your wings—men don't walk thirty kilometers off a glacier by accident. Something chased him. Or something let him go and figured the cold would finish the job." He tapped his own chest, twice, slow. "The land remembers who asks honest questions. Remember that too."

* * *

Wren's order came over the radio while she was still lashing a stretcher into the cargo bay.

"Dravenmoor, confirm you are NOT diverting to Sool's position." Aldous Wren's voice was smooth as always, polished like river stone, but she could hear the grain underneath it now, the grit in the polish. "Company assets are not ambulance service. If a surveyor is stranded, that's a Search and Rescue matter. Civilian channels. You have a fuel run scheduled."

"The nearest SAR asset is nine hundred kilometers south," Freya said, cinching a strap. "The man will be dead in six hours."

"Then that is regrettable, but it is not your problem." A pause, and when Wren spoke again his tone had shifted, gone softer, which from Wren was worse than shouting. "Freya. Think about what you're doing. A man walks out of a closed survey area, delirious, talking about coordinates that don't exist on any approved map. You pick him up, you become part of the story. Right now you're just the pilot who saw an empty camp. There's value in that. There's debt relief in that."

There it was. Not even hidden anymore, not really—just dressed in reasonable words and offered with a smile she couldn't see but could perfectly imagine.

"My debt is with your finance office," she said. "Not with you."

"Everything in this territory is with me, one way or another." She could almost hear him adjusting his cuffs. "Bring the aircraft home, Dravenmoor. File your report. Be sensible."

She looked back at the cabin. Through its small window she could see Sool moving inside, tending the fire, keeping the man alive through sheer stubborn competence until someone came.

"Sensible," she repeated. Then she keyed the mic. "Dravenmoor outbound, Sool's position, medevac priority. Log it however you like."

She switched off the radio before he could answer.

* * *

Elias Brandt fought her the whole way to the plane—or some fevered version of him did. When she lifted him from the cot he came half awake, and his cracked hands seized her jacket with shocking strength.

"Don't—" His eyes found hers, and they were wrong, unfocused, looking at something behind her face. "Don't take me back. They said relocation. They said trucks would come. Ruth believed them, she believed—" A cough tore through him, wet and awful. "The stakes. We pulled the stakes. They made us pull the stakes."

"It's all right," she said, though nothing about it was. "You're safe."

"Nobody's safe." But the strength went out of him then, and he sagged against her shoulder, light as a bundle of sticks, smelling of woodsmoke and sickness and the particular sweetish stink of flesh that has been frozen and thawed. She got him into the cargo bay, wrapped him in the spare sleeping bag, wedged him secure between crates, and ran the heater line as far aft as it would reach.

Sool stood at the wingtip as she finished her checks. He handed up a battered thermos without a word. She took it.

"The east fork freezes solid in ten days," the trapper said. "After that, nobody walks anywhere. Whatever this is, pilot—it's a race now."

She climbed in, ran the start sequence, and felt the old de Havilland shudder alive around her, familiar as her own pulse. As she lifted off Sool's clearing and banked south, she caught one glimpse of the trapper below, standing motionless in the snow, watching her go. Then the trees took him, and there was only the range ahead, white and vast and keeping whatever secrets it kept.

* * *

The fever broke loose somewhere over the divide.

It started as muttering, just the coordinates again, worn smooth as river stones from repetition. Then it opened up, the way a crack in lake ice opens—one moment a seam, the next a black lane of water—and Elias Brandt began to talk.

She heard most of it through the open cockpit door, flying with one ear turned aft, unable to do anything but listen and hold the plane steady in air that was starting to move in long, heavy swells, the first breath of the weather system Sool had promised.

"...Ruth said we map what's there. That's the job. That's the whole job..." A long pause, breathing. "...but the funding memo came through and it wasn't a memo, it was a man, a company man, and he had the new sheets already printed. Already printed, do you understand? Before we surveyed. Blank valleys. Valleys with nothing in them, drawn in advance..."

Freya's hands tightened on the yoke.

"...and then the valley itself. God, the valley. Smoke from the chimneys. Herds on the high meadow, real herds, fat ones, and children—Ruth counted nineteen children from the ridge glass. Nineteen. A generation living down there and no record, no census, nothing, because they ran from the concessions twenty years ago and built a life in a place the maps say is ice..." His voice climbed, fraying. "We were ordered to erase them. Not find them. Erase them. 'Adjust the survey.' Those were the words. Adjust. Like they were a rounding error. Like they were a smudge on a lens..."

The plane bucked once in turbulence and Freya rode it out, jaw tight, eyes burning in the cold glare off the snowfields.

"...and when Ruth refused—they told us to wait at camp for the evacuation transport, and the trucks that came weren't transport trucks. Ruth saw first. Ruth always saw first. She told us to scatter, she—" His voice broke apart entirely, and what came through was not words anymore, just sound, a man grieving at altitude in the back of a borrowed airplane.

Freya flew.

She flew through the swells and the building gray in the west, and she thought about the camp. The warm stove. The open logbook. *PASSAGE REAL.* No struggle, no tracks—because the men with rifles had been careful, because the snow had been falling, because someone had wanted it neat. She thought about Wren's voice saying *relocated, nothing unusual,* and how she had filed it, how she had signed her name under a lie because the alternative was a ledger she couldn't pay.

Twenty years ago, the valley had fled the concessions. Which meant twenty years ago, someone had flown for the company that pushed them out. Someone with wings.

She didn't let herself think the name. Not yet.

Behind her, the fever-talk faded to murmurs, then to silence, then to the slow, ragged rhythm of exhausted sleep. Freya checked him at the fuel stop—a company strip, empty, where she topped the tanks with hands that shook only a little—and found his eyes closed, his breathing easier, one bandaged hand resting on his chest like a man holding something there. Protecting something. She remembered Sool saying *no pack,* and she looked at the coat they'd cut off him at the cabin, hanging stiffly from a cargo hook, and she didn't touch it. Not yet. Some things you opened in daylight, with time, with doors locked.

By the time she sighted the company field at dusk, the wind had swung around and the first flakes were coming down sideways, fat and deliberate, the sky making good on its threat. She put the de Havilland down in a gusting crosswind, feeling every year of the old girl's airframe, and rolled out toward the hangar lights.

A vehicle was waiting on the apron. Black, company-marked, engine running, wipers sweeping. Two men stood beside it with their collars up, and beside them, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring weather he owned, stood Aldous Wren.

Freya shut down the engine and sat for a moment in the ticking quiet, listening to the metal cool, feeling the sleeping man's presence behind her like ballast she'd chosen.

She had flown against orders. She had a witness in her cargo bay who could end careers, maybe the charter itself. Every sensible path ran through Wren's smile, and she had just flown around all of them.

"Well," she said to the empty cockpit, and reached for the door latch. "Here we go."

Wren raised one hand in greeting, pleasant as Sunday, and Freya Dravenmoor walked out onto the apron to meet him—with the fevered cartographer behind her, the truth sewn somewhere into a frozen coat, and the first real snow of the season coming down on all of them alike.
