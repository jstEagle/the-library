## 5. What the Mentor Knew

The fever made a country of him.

He lay in it the way he had once lain on the tundra in July, arms spread, watching cloud shadows move over ground so flat it seemed to have no opinion about anything. Except this country was warm and wrong-colored, and it moved when he did not want it to move, and somewhere above him a woman's voice kept saying words like *drink* and *hold on* and *stay with me*, and he tried to explain that he was staying, that he was holding, that he was gripping the one thing left in the world worth gripping — sewn against his ribs, under his coat, under his skin almost, a book with a canvas cover gone soft as cloth from his sweat —

*Don't let them take the book.*

Ruth's voice. Ruth's voice was everywhere in the fever. It came out of the walls of whatever room he was in. It came out of the wind. He could not see her face anymore; the fever had taken her face first, the way frost takes a window, from the edges inward. But her voice stayed whole, because her voice was the last true thing, and fevers eat lies before they eat truth.

*Elias. Write it down.*

---

There had been eleven of them at the start. Twelve counting the cook, who never counted himself. They had come up the Kestrel River in two floatplanes in the last week of August, when the water was still brown and slow and the willows were turning the color of old coins, and Elias Brandt, twenty-six years old, junior cartographer, three months out of the institute, had stood in the bow of the second plane with his heart going like a bird in a box, because this was it. This was the work he had dreamed of since he was small enough to draw maps on the backs of envelopes. Unsurveyed country. A blank on the map with his name waiting inside it.

The planes bore no company markings. That should have been the first thing. Later, much later, lying in the dark of his tent doing sums he didn't want to finish, he would understand: government survey teams flew marked aircraft, filed public manifests, carried letters of commission with seals on them. Theirs had flown gray and unmarked, and their letter of commission had a seal, but the seal was Halvorsen Mining, and Ruth Okonkwo had looked at it for a long time before she put it away.

"Money is money," their paymaster, a soft man named Corliss, had said on the gravel bar. "The ministry wants the range mapped. We're simply efficient."

"The ministry," Ruth said, "doesn't hire through mining charters." She said it lightly, almost smiling, the way she said everything, and Corliss smiled back, and nothing was settled that day, and Elias had thought nothing needed settling. He was twenty-six. He thought maps were maps.

*You thought the paper was the country,* Ruth's voice said now, in the fever, gentle and terrible. *Oh, Elias. The paper is the weapon.*

---

They worked south to north, up the tributaries, shooting angles and pacing baselines, and it was good work, honest work, the kind where your body aches at night with a clean ache and the numbers come out true. Elias drew the contour lines himself, hunched over the drafting table in the mess tent with a lamp hissing, and he was good at it, Ruth told him so twice, and both times he carried the compliment around for days like a warm stone.

It was on the ninth day that Pieter, their senior surveyor, came back from the eastern ridge line early, off-schedule, walking fast, and stood in the mess tent dripping and said: "There are people down there."

Everyone looked up.

"A valley," Pieter said. "South-facing. Sheltered. Fields, Elias. Terraced fields. Herds of some kind of shaggy cattle. Smokehouses. A church — no, not a church, a meeting hall, timber-framed, built to last. There must be two hundred of them. Maybe more. They've been there a long time."

Silence in the tent. The stove ticked.

"That's impossible," Corliss said, but he said it the way a man says something he has just realized is possible.

"They're not on any registry," Pieter said. "No census. No permits. No concessions. They're not supposed to exist."

And Ruth Okonkwo set down her coffee, and Elias watched her face do something he had never seen a face do — watched it go still the way water goes still before it freezes — and she said, quietly, "Then we've found the most important thing in this territory," and Corliss said, "Or the most inconvenient," and the two sentences crossed in the air between them like bullets, though nobody heard them yet but Elias, because Elias was twenty-six and listening, always listening, that was his gift and later it would be his curse.

---

The fever took him down again after that, down into the cold place, the moraine place, the running place, and he ran in the dream the way he had run for real, boots punching crust, lungs full of knives, the field book against his ribs and behind him — don't look, he had promised himself not to look, and he looked, everyone looks — behind him the camp burning orange against the blue snow, and men standing in a line at the edge of the firelight with rifles held low and easy, the way you hold tools you're used to.

*Company rifles.* In the fever he saw them clearly, better than he had seen anything in his life: matte barrels, oiled slings, and on the shoulder of the nearest man a patch, gray on gray, a stylized H inside a mountain outline, and the man turned his head as Elias looked, unhurried, and did not raise the rifle, because the snow was doing their work for them, and because they knew — they *knew*, the way you know a thing you've done before — that a man alone on a glacier in October is already a dead man, and dead men make no depositions.

But they hadn't counted on the trapper. Nobody counts on trappers. Trappers are the territory's own memory, moving through it on routes no company ever charted.

---

Before the burning there had been the order. He had to get the order right, in the fever he kept getting it wrong, kept hearing it in the wrong voice, and Ruth's ghost kept correcting him, patient, merciless:

*Not Wren. Wren never came into the field. It was the radio call, the third week of September. Read it back to me, Elias. You wrote it in the log.*

So he read it back, in the fever, in the dark behind his eyes, word for word, because he had written it word for word, sitting at the radio table with the headset on while Ruth stood over him with her arms folded:

**TO SURVEY TEAM NINE FROM HALVORSEN LAND DIVISION. REVISE SECTOR 7 AND 8 PER ATTACHED BASELINE. NOTE: NO INHABITED SETTLEMENTS TO APPEAR WITHIN CONCESSION BOUNDARIES. RECORD GLACIAL TERRAIN, UNINHABITED. CONFIRM RECEIPT.**

*Uninhabited,* Ruth's voice repeated. *Two hundred people, Elias. A generation born in that valley. And the word they chose was 'uninhabited.'*

She had refused. That was the spine of the whole story, the bone everything else hung from: Ruth Okonkwo had sat down at the same table an hour later and typed her refusal and made Elias witness it, made him sign beneath it, which he had understood even then was either an honor or a death sentence and could not tell which.

**RECEIPT CONFIRMED. SURVEY DATA FOR SECTORS 7 AND 8 REFLECTS FIELD OBSERVATION AS RECORDED. SETTLEMENT NOTED AT GRID REFERENCE 44-KILO-19, POPULATION ESTIMATE 200 PLUS. WE WILL NOT FALSIFY. OKONKWO, TEAM LEADER.**

Corliss had read it over their shoulders and gone pale, and then gone smooth, the way soft men go smooth, and said, "Ruth. Ruth, think about what you're doing. These permits fund next year. They fund all our contracts. One valley. Draw it as rockfall terrain, nobody will ever check, nobody will ever—"

"It's inhabited," Ruth said. "I walked their fields. I traded salt for dried fish with a woman whose grandmother was born there. You can draw rockfall over her grave if you like, Corliss. I won't hold the pencil."

And Elias, twenty-six years old, in love with maps and half in love with Ruth's certainty, had signed his name under hers and felt — this was the shameful part, the part the fever circled and circled and would not let him avoid — felt *important*. Felt that he was present at the hinge of history. He had not yet understood that hinges swing both ways, and that men with rifles were already on a floatplane out of the company's southern strip, and that the attached baseline Corliss mentioned had been drafted weeks before anyone found the valley, which meant they had known, they had *always known*, and the survey was never a survey. The survey was a search. They had hired twelve people to find what they needed erased, so that the erasure would carry a government stamp.

*Do you see it now?* Ruth asked, in the fever, in the firelight, in the snow. *We weren't mapping the country, Elias. We were laundering it.*

---

The evacuation — that was the word Corliss used afterward, *evacuation*, spoken in the past tense, already finished, a done thing, a paperwork thing — happened on a Tuesday, or a day that felt like Tuesday, gray and ordinary, first hard frost. The company men came up the river in the gray floatplanes, six of them plus a pilot, and Corliss met them on the gravel bar shaking hands like they were suppliers.

Elias was on the eastern ridge that day with Pieter, shooting a baseline. He saw the smoke rise from the direction of the camp and thought, absurdly, *the stove's gone wrong*, and then he saw that the smoke was too black for a stove, and they ran, and by the time they got down to the river flats the gray planes were lifting off, and the camp was burning, and the tents — their tents, the mess tent with the drafting table, his lamp — were burning, and Ruth was standing in the open with her hands at her sides and blood on her temple, alive, alive, thank God alive, and four men in gray stood around her in a loose semicolon of rifles.

Where were the valley people? Gone. Moved in the night, or moved that morning — herded, Elias learned later from Ruth, in whispers, in fragments, before the end: loaded into boats at dawn and taken upriver into country the company's maps showed as empty because the company had made it empty, decades ago, the first expulsion, the grandparents' expulsion, the reason the valley existed hidden at all. History repeating itself with better paperwork.

"You'll sign the revised sheets," the senior company man was saying to Ruth, not unkindly, which was the worst part, the courtesy of it. "Or you'll be listed as having perished in an accident. Those are the options. Your team gets the same choice."

"And if we sign?"

"Then you fly out Friday, paid in full, with a story about difficult terrain."

"And if we don't?"

The man looked at the burning camp. He didn't answer. He didn't need to.

That night, in the wreck of the equipment cache — they had burned the camp but not the cache, an oversight, or arrogance, the two being cousins — Ruth took the original field book, the true book, the one with the settlement recorded at 44-Kilo-19 in Elias's own careful hand, and she took a sail needle and waxed thread from the survey kit, and she sewed it into the lining of Elias's coat while he stood rigid as a tent pole.

"Why me?" he whispered.

"Because you're the youngest and the fastest and nobody's watching you," she said. "And because you signed under my name. Whatever happens to me, the record survives in someone who signed it. That's how records survive, Elias. Not in archives. In witnesses."

"Come with me."

"I'm the team leader. I stay with my team." She bit the thread off. Her face in the fire-glow was calm in a way he would spend the rest of his life trying to describe and failing. "Walk east. Find the moraine, follow it down to the tree line, keep moving. Don't use the radio. Don't trust anyone with clean hands." She had gripped his collar then, sudden, hard, pulling his forehead nearly to hers. "Elias. Listen. If I don't come out — you get that book to someone who can't be bought. A tribunal. A newspaper. Anyone. Promise me."

He promised. He was still promising it now, in the fever, in the warm dark, to a woman whose face he could no longer see, and the promise went on and on like a river under ice, and somewhere above the ice a woman's voice — not Ruth's, a different voice, younger, rougher, a pilot's voice — kept saying *hold on, hold on*, and he wanted to tell her that holding on was the only thing he knew how to do anymore, that he had been holding on since the moraine, since the snow, since—

---

Since the shot.

He had been a kilometer east, maybe less, when he heard it. One shot, flat across the ice-fields, the way a shot sounds when the air is cold enough to carry it clean. He had stopped. He had stood in the falling snow with the book against his ribs and he had not gone back, and that was the wound the fever probed and probed and could not close: not the bullet, not the burning, but the standing still. The arithmetic of one life against two hundred, done in half a second by a twenty-six-year-old with somebody else's field book sewn into his coat, and the answer had been *run*, and he had run, and he had been running ever since, through the whiteout, down the moraine, into the trapper's cabin, into the fever, into this bed with this stranger-pilot watching him with eyes that asked questions he did not yet have the strength to answer.

*Ruth?* the fever asked itself, one more time, the way it asked every night. *Ruth, did they—*

And the fever, merciful for once, gave him no answer, only the sound of one shot carried clean across the ice, and the smell of burning canvas, and the weight of the book, the blessed heavy weight of it, still there, still sewn against his ribs, still true.

---

He surfaced once, near morning, into a room he didn't know: plank walls, a stove ticking, frost ferns on a single window going gray with dawn. His coat was draped over a chair. For one lurching second he thought — *they've found it, they've cut it out* — and he made a sound, some broken animal sound, and the pilot was there instantly, Freya, that was her name, Freya, kneeling with her hands out like calming a spooked horse.

"Easy. Easy. You're safe. Nobody's touched your things."

His hand went to the coat, to the stiff seam along the lining, and found it whole, and found it *there*, and the relief went through him like warm water and took the last of his strength with it.

"There's a book," he heard himself say, from very far away, from the bottom of the ice. "In the lining. Don't — whatever comes for it — don't give it to anyone from the company. Promise me. Swear it."

Freya Dravenmoor looked at the coat for a long moment. Then at him. Something moved behind her eyes — he saw it even through the fever, saw it the way you see lightning through cloud — something that was recognition, or dread, or the beginning of a decision.

"Who's it from?" she asked quietly.

"Team Nine," Elias said. "It's the real survey. And the men who burned our camp—" the cough took him, wracked him, and he rode it out gritting his teeth, and finished anyway, because Ruth had made him promise, because promises are the only archives that can't burn: "—signed their work orders *Halvorsen Land Division*. Your company, pilot. Check the stitching. Then decide whose side you're on."

Her face went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

Outside, far off across the white field, an engine turned over — a truck engine, heavy, deliberate — and Freya's head came up sharply toward the window, and the frost ferns caught the first red light of morning, and Elias sank back down into the dark, holding on.
