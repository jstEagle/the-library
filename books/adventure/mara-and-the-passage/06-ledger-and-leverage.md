## 6. Ledger and Leverage

The summons came at six in the morning, delivered by a boy in a company jacket who didn't knock so much as let himself into Freya's rooming-house cubicle and stand there breathing until she opened her eyes.

"Mr. Wren. Operations. Now."

She dressed in the dark, fingers finding buttons by memory, and looked once at the duffel under her cot where Elias Brandt slept off the last of his fever in the infirmary two streets over—company infirmary, which was its own kind of problem—and followed the boy out into the blue-black cold.

Halvorsen's airfield office was the only building in town with electric heat that ran all night. Freya had been inside it exactly four times in six years: to sign her contract, to sign an amendment, to sign a restructuring, and to be told her engine overhaul would be added to the principal. Each time the office had smelled the same—oiled walnut, carbon paper, and beneath it something faintly antiseptic, like money trying not to smell of anything.

Aldous Wren stood at the window with his back to her, hands clasped behind him, watching the first grey light come up over the fuel drums. He was a small man made large by stillness. He did not turn around.

"Dravenmoor." Her name in his mouth sounded like a line item. "You flew a man out of the Kestrel Range yesterday. Against my instruction."

"I flew a dying man out of a glacier field," Freya said. "There's a difference."

"There is," Wren agreed pleasantly. "One of them is insubordination."

He turned then, and crossed to his desk, and sat, and folded his hands on the blotter as though preparing to discuss weather. On the desk lay a single folder, thin, dog-eared, familiar in a way that made her stomach drop before her mind caught up. She knew that folder. She'd signed it. She'd signed it three times.

Wren opened it and rotated it to face her.

"You know what this is. But I think you've forgotten what it *says*." He tapped the first page. "Principal, aircraft purchase, eight thousand four hundred. Maintenance advances, two thousand one hundred. Fuel drawn against account, ongoing. Interest, compounding, at terms you agreed to when no other lender in the territory would look at you." He looked up. His eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice. "You owe this company eleven thousand nine hundred dollars, Dravenmoor. Your plane is collateral. Your license to operate out of this strip is collateral. Technically—" he smiled, a small dry crease, "—your wings are collateral."

Freya kept her face still. Six years of practice. "I know what I owe."

"Do you? Because yesterday you behaved like a woman with nothing to lose." He closed the folder but left his hand flat on it, a paperweight on her whole life. "Here's what happens now. The man you brought out—Brandt—is delirious, malnourished, frostbitten, and talking. Do you know what he's talking about?"

"The cold," Freya said. "Coordinates."

"Coordinates." Wren nodded slowly, as if tasting the word. "Team Nine's coordinates. Which are company property. As are Team Nine's records, notes, instruments, and observations—all of it, every pencil mark, covered by the survey clause in your own contract, section fourteen, which you also signed." He leaned back. "So here is my offer, and I'll make it once. You bring me Elias Brandt and any materials he carried off the ice, and I tear this folder up. Not restructure. *Tear.* Eleven thousand nine hundred dollars, gone. The Otter becomes yours free and clear, title transferred, my signature on it by Friday. And your standing on this strip—which has been, let us say, precarious—becomes permanent."

The heater ticked. Somewhere outside a loader dropped its bucket and the ground shivered through the floorboards.

"And if I don't?" Freya asked.

Wren's expression didn't change at all, which was the answer. "Then nothing happens today. Nothing dramatic. The interest simply continues. The winter continues. And next spring, when the medicine runs are heavy and the fuel prices rise, we'll have this same conversation again, only I'll be less generous, because generosity is a perishable commodity." He stood, offering the folder across the desk like a man handing back a receipt. "You have until Thursday. Think about what your mentor would advise. Ilsa Voss always struck me as a practical woman."

He said her mentor's name lightly, carelessly, the way a man drops a coin down a well to hear how deep it goes. Freya took the folder without looking at it and walked out into the cold with her heart going hard and even, like a tachometer climbing.

* * *

Elias Brandt was sitting up when she got to the infirmary, propped against pillows, watching the window with the fixed attention of a man who had spent too long looking at nothing. The fever had broken in the night; what remained was a rawness around the eyes, a man scraped down to the grain. His hands lay on the blanket, and they were moving—faintly, endlessly, tracing shapes. Lines. Contours. A cartographer even unconscious, drawing maps on the air.

"How much do you remember?" Freya asked, pulling a chair close.

His eyes came around slowly. "Enough." His voice was gravel and rust. "You're the pilot."

"Freya."

"Elias." He almost smiled. "I know. I've been saying your name for two days. The nurse thought I was praying."

"Company nurse?"

"Company everything." He said it without bitterness, just fact, the way you'd note the weather. Then his gaze sharpened, and Freya saw the intelligence underneath the ruin, working again. "They sent you already. Wren. What did he offer?"

Her silence told him.

"The debt," Elias said. "Forgiveness for delivery. Me, and the book." His hand went to his coat, hanging on the bed rail, and pressed flat against the lining—the gesture automatic, protective, a man checking a wound. "It's still here. They haven't searched me properly. They're waiting for *you* to do it. That's cleaner for them. If the book surfaces through you, you're complicit. If it disappears through you, you're the thief. Either way Wren's hands stay white."

Freya sat with that. Outside the window, the Otter sat on the frozen strip, frost furring her struts, hers and not hers, eleven thousand nine hundred dollars of not-hers.

"What's in it?" she asked quietly. "Really."

Elias was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, the fever-glaze was gone entirely, and what was underneath was worse than fever.

"We found a valley," he said. "Past the saddle, in the rain shadow, where nobody surveys because the charts show rockfall and dead ends. It isn't rockfall. There's a pass—a real one, warm side, timber, water. And there are people, Freya. Families. Herds. A whole generation of them living unrecorded because their grandparents ran from Halvorsen's first concessions and the mountains hid them." His voice cracked and steadied. "Ruth Okonkwo—our lead—she mapped it anyway. Every house, every field, every grave. She said a map is a promise, and she wasn't going to falsify one. Three days later men with company rifles walked into camp and marched the team 'downriver for evacuation.' I ran. I took the field book. Ruth gave it to me. She—" he stopped, and his hands stopped drawing, and for a moment he was just a young man holding very still. "I don't know what happened to her. That's why I keep saying the coordinates. In case someone flies them."

Freya looked at her hands. They were steady. She'd learned that from Ilsa too—hands steady, voice level, decide on the ground not in the air.

"If I copy pages," she said, "they'll need to be the right ones."

* * *

She came back after lights-out with a pencil, a notebook, and the excuse of a final check on her patient. The night nurse owed her—Freya had flown the woman's son to the coast clinic in October, no charge, no log entry—and the nurse looked at her once, understood everything, and went to make coffee she would drink very slowly in another room.

Freya worked by penlight clamped in her teeth, coat draped over her shoulders to kill the spill of light. Elias held the field book open for her, turning pages with a librarian's care, and pointed without touching: *this one, this one, this.*

She copied fast and small. The pass coordinates, triangulated twice. The valley's bearing from the saddle. Water sources. The count of structures—forty-one dwellings, two long buildings, a burial ground. Ruth Okonkwo's neat, furious hand in the margins: *Population est. 200+. No record in any census. Concession maps 1949–present: falsified.* And on the last page they copied, a sketch of the pass itself, drawn in elevation profile, with a single word underlined three times:

PASSAGE REAL.

The same words from the camp logbook. Ruth's last entry. Freya felt the hair lift on her arms.

When it was done, Elias sewed the notebook flat against her ribs herself, inside her flight jacket's lining, with thread from the infirmary kit, his frostbitten fingers clumsy and determined. "If they take the book," he whispered, "they can't take this. Don't tell me where else you hide things. Better I don't know."

"Wren will search you tomorrow regardless," Freya said. "Let him find it. Let him think it's everything."

Elias looked at her for a long moment. "You understand what you just did. You're not a courier anymore. You're evidence."

"I've been evidence since I signed that folder," Freya said. "At least now I'm useful."

* * *

Ilsa Voss found her at dawn on the strip, breath smoking, standing in the Otter's open door running a preflight she didn't need to run. The old woman walked the frozen mud like she owned it, which in every way that mattered she had, twenty years ago, before the company bought the land out from under everyone including her.

"Wren talked to you," Ilsa said. Not a question.

"He offered to erase my debt."

"Mm." Ilsa leaned against the strut, and for a while the two of them watched the light come up the way it does over ice, slow and colorless. "Thirty years I've known Aldous Wren. Taught half this territory to fly, and watched him buy the other half. And I'll tell you the one thing I know about him for certain, girl, and you should carve it somewhere you'll see it every morning." She turned, and her eyes were the flat grey of the sky. "Wren has never forgiven anything in his life. Not a debt. Not a slight. Not a favor. He doesn't forgive—he *invests*. And every investment of his comes due with interest you haven't imagined yet. If he tears up that folder, it's because the folder costs him less than what he's buying. Ask yourself what Brandt's book is worth that he'd pay twelve thousand dollars for it."

Freya's hands went still on the doorframe.

"And ask yourself," Ilsa went on, quieter, "what happens to the people in that valley the day Wren gets what he wants. Because there's only one reason to pay that much for paper, and it isn't to put it in a museum."

The wind moved along the strip, lifting frost off the Otter's wing in a fine smoke. Freya looked at her plane—her plane, eleven thousand nine hundred dollars of borrowed sky—and felt something shift in her chest, the way load shifts in turbulence, slow and then all at once.

"Ilsa," she said. "What do you know about flying the Kestrels from the north?"

The old woman was quiet for a moment. Then she smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile.

"Come to the hangar tonight," she said. "Bring your appetite. And bring nothing you'd mind burning."
