## 7. Ilsa's Hangar

Ilsa's hangar sat at the far end of the airfield like an old dog that had earned the right to sleep where it wanted. The corrugated tin was patched in six places with metal that didn't match, and the big sliding door stuck halfway no matter how many times Freya oiled the track. Inside smelled the way it had smelled since Freya was nine years old: dope and fabric, engine oil, wood shavings, and underneath it all the faint sweet rot of old coffee that Ilsa refused to throw out because, she said, it kept the mice honest.

Freya stood in the doorway a long time before she went in.

She'd told herself she was coming for charts. That was the story. Charts of the Kestrel Range, because Ilsa had flown every fold of these mountains back when the government still bothered to name things, and if anyone knew a way through that wasn't on the company's flight plans, it was her. That was the story Freya had rehearsed on the walk over, past the fuel drums and the grounded patrol aircraft sitting on the apron like patient dogs waiting to be pointed at something.

But her hands were shaking, and you couldn't hide shaking hands from Ilsa Vantaa. The woman had read Freya's face across a cockpit for fifteen years. She'd known Freya was pregnant-scared before her first solo before Freya knew it herself.

Ilsa was under the Piper Cub she was forever rebuilding, just her boots sticking out, a socket wrench clicking in slow, unhurried strokes. She didn't come out when the door groaned open. She never did. You came to her; the hangar didn't move for anybody.

"Fuel line's weeping again," Ilsa said from under the fuselage. "Third time this month. You'd think after forty years a man would learn to stop buying cheap hose."

"I'm not here about the Cub."

The clicking stopped. A pause long enough that Freya heard the wind working at the tin roof, the tick of cooling metal, her own pulse in her ears. Then Isla rolled out on the creeper, sat up, and looked at her over the tops of her reading glasses with those pale northern eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"No," Ilsa said. "You're not."

* * *

They drank the bad coffee standing up, which was how everything serious got said in that hangar. Freya laid it out in pieces, the way she'd assemble a preflight: Elias Brandt in her spare room, fevered, talking about a valley full of people. The field book sewn into his coat lining. The pages she'd copied by lamplight while Wren slept two hangars away. The offer Wren had made—full forgiveness of a debt that had owned eleven years of her life—in exchange for delivering both of them to company hands by Thursday.

"And you're telling me this because," Ilsa said. Not a question. An invitation to finish the sentence honestly or not at all.

"Because I don't know what I'm going to do." Freya turned the mug in her hands. "That's the truth. I've been flying circuits around it all morning. Every way I look at it, somebody gets hurt. If I run, they take the plane, and the plane is—" She stopped. Started again. "Everything I have is Halvorsen's. My license might as well be. Eleven years, Ilsa. I've paid them four times what the Fox was worth and the ledger doesn't move. And now they're offering to wipe it clean if I hand them one sick man and a book."

"And?"

"And I keep seeing his face." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "He's twenty-four years old. They shot at him. They left him on a glacier. And Wren stands there with my contract in his hand smiling at me like we're discussing freight rates."

Ilsa set her mug down on the workbench with great care, the way she did everything, as if the world were made of fabric and dope and would tear if you rushed it.

"Sit down, girl."

"I can sit or I can—"

"Sit down."

Freya sat on the oil drum by the bench. Ilsa pulled off her glasses, folded them, hooked them in her collar. Then she walked to the back of the hangar, past the parts shelves, past the dead stove, to the steel locker she kept locked and that Freya had assumed held tools, or ammunition, or nothing at all.

It held a box. Wooden, army-surplus, stenciled with a serial number half worn away. Ilsa carried it back and set it on the bench between them and didn't open it right away. She put both hands flat on the lid, like a pilot resting her palms on the yoke before committing to weather.

"You know I flew for Halvorsen," she said. "Before your time. Before the debt program, even. Back when they were opening the eastern concessions."

"You mentioned it once. Said they taught you instrument flying."

"They taught me more than that." Ilsa opened the box.

Inside, wrapped in wax paper gone brittle with age, were photographs. Freya leaned in. Black-and-white, curled at the corners. A survey camp in a valley so green it looked wrong next to the grey mountains around it. Tents. A theodolite on a tripod. Men in wool shirts squinting into a camera, and one of them holding a notebook up, almost defiantly, for the lens.

And a second photograph, taken from the air. The same valley, weeks later maybe. Burned. The tents black circles. Smoke frozen mid-column by the shutter.

Freya's mouth went dry. "What is this?"

"That's the Sorrel Valley. Nineteen sixty-one." Ilsa's voice was flat, the voice she used for checklists and emergency procedures, the voice that meant the feeling underneath was too large to let through. "There was a survey team in there too. Government men, same as Team Nine. They found people living in that valley. Families. Had been there thirty years, ever since the first round of evictions when the concessions opened. Farmers, trappers, a whole community that the maps simply... declined to mention."

Freya looked at the burned camp. "And the survey team?"

"Were told to file their maps showing nothing. Empty country. Two of them did what they were told. One of them didn't—a man named Oskar Lindqvist. He filed the true survey with the land office in the capital instead." Ilsa picked up the photograph of the man with the notebook. Her thumb moved once across his face, a gesture so small and so worn that Freya understood she'd done it ten thousand times. "Halvorsen asked me to fly a party into the valley. Said it was a security matter. Said there were agitators. I was twenty-six years old and they owned my airplane and my house and my mother's medical bills, and I told myself it was agitators right up until I saw the smoke from eight thousand feet."

The hangar was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a patrol aircraft ran up its engine, wound down.

"There were no agitators," Ilsa said. "There were children. I flew the supply runs for the men who burned them out, and then I flew the surveyor's replacement team in, and then I signed the flight logs that said the valley was uninhabited, and the permits went through, and the mine opened, and I took my pay and bought this hangar and told myself for twenty years that I hadn't known. That's the trick of it, Freya. You don't have to lie to anyone else. You just have to keep not-knowing, and everyone will help you do it, because everyone is doing it too."

"Why are you telling me this?" But Freya already knew. She could feel the answer settling onto her shoulders like ballast, like the weight of the Fox on a hot day fully loaded.

"Because Wren was there." Ilsa closed the box. "Junior operations clerk. He learned then that a pilot in debt will carry anything, and he has been building that fleet of yours ever since. And because in nineteen sixty-one there was no one who came to me and said *look at what you're part of.* No one handed me the other choice. Everyone I trusted told me to fly the line and keep my mouth shut, and I did, and there is a valley east of here where people died being erased and I helped erase them." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "I am not doing it twice."

* * *

After that, things moved fast, the way they do once a decision stops pretending to be undecided.

Ilsa pulled a roll of charts from the map cabinet—the real cabinet, not the public one—and spread them across the bench, weighting the corners with socket wrenches and coffee mugs. They were hand-annotated, forty years of pencil corrections in a small fierce script. The Kestrels filled the top third, and where the official surveys showed blank white contour lines and the notation UNSURVEYED—NO KNOWN PASSAGE, Ilsa's pencil had drawn a thin thread of a route.

"The Saddle," she said, tapping it. "Everyone thinks the range is a wall. It isn't. It's a wall with a loose stone. There's a pass here—glacier on the north approach, a frozen lake on the south side you can put down on if the ice is thick enough, which in October it won't quite be, so don't. On foot it connects to the high valley system. Elias will know it. He walked out of it half-dead, which means he walked it once already."

"You've been through it?"

"Once. In sixty-one. Flying a man out who'd been shot." She said it without looking up. "The company charts show the lake as seasonal marsh. They show the pass as a ridge line. Wren's own cartographers buried it, because a pass means access, and access means witnesses." She rolled the chart, tapped it against the bench to square it, and held it out. "This copy stays here. You memorize what you need and you burn the rest."

Freya took it. Her hands had stopped shaking somewhere during the story about the smoke, and now they were very steady, steadier than she felt. "Ilsa. If I do this—if I run—they'll take the Fox. Everything I've got is leveraged against that plane. I'll be finished. No license, no aircraft, probably charges."

"Yes."

"That's it? Yes?"

"What do you want me to say? That it'll be fine?" Ilsa finally smiled, and it was the old crooked smile from Freya's first solo, the one that meant *you're going to be terrified and then you're going to be magnificent.* "It will not be fine. It will be hard and cold and it may cost you everything you own. I know exactly what that costs, because I paid the opposite price. I kept everything, and it cost me more." She put her hand over Freya's, over the rolled chart. "Eleven years ago I watched a half-starved kid wash airplanes for fuel money and I thought, there. There's a pilot. I didn't give you your wings, Freya. Nobody gives wings. I just happened to be standing in the hangar the day you grew them. They're yours. They always were. Halvorsen owns your debt. They do not own you, unless you decide they do."

Freya found she couldn't speak for a moment. She looked at the charts, at the box of photographs, at the little Cub in the corner that Ilsa had been rebuilding for a decade and would clearly be rebuilding for another.

"The fuel," she managed. "I'll need full tanks and I can't draw on the company account without flagging it."

"Already handled." Ilsa jerked her chin toward the back of the hangar, where behind a tarp stood drums—eight of them, aviation fuel, freshly sealed. "Paid cash, hauled in over three weeks, one drum at a time, in the Cub. Wren thinks I'm a sentimental old woman wasting money on a toy airplane. Let him." She counted on her fingers. "Full tanks in the Fox plus these gets you to the range, over the Saddle with reserve, and down to the railhead country if you must. After that you're on your own, because after that, so am I."

"So am I?"

"I'm coming to the capital, girl. When the tribunal sits. Somebody from sixty-one owes a testimony, and I've been in arrears for twenty years." She said it lightly, but her jaw was set like a riveted seam. "Besides. Someone has to be in the hearing room who remembers what burned valleys look like from the air."

* * *

It was nearly dark when Freya walked back across the apron, the chart rolled inside her jacket, against her ribs, warm as a second heartbeat. The patrol aircraft sat dark on the line. The floodlights buzzed. Somewhere in the operations building, Aldous Wren was no doubt drafting the paperwork that would make her either a free woman or a fugitive, depending entirely on what she did in the next forty-eight hours.

At her door she stopped. Behind her, across the field, Ilsa's hangar showed one lit window, and in it, briefly, the silhouette of an old woman standing very straight beside a wooden box.

Freya thought about the ledger. Eleven years of numbers that never moved, columns designed to be bottomless, arithmetic as a leash. She thought about Elias's fever-bright eyes repeating coordinates like a prayer. She thought about a green valley that official maps insisted had never existed, and the people in it who had been erased once already by men with company rifles and pilots willing to fly the line.

Then she went inside, woke Elias gently, and told him, "Get dressed. We're leaving tonight."

His eyes cleared for the first time in days—sharp, frightened, awake. "Tonight? The weather—"

"I know the weather." She was already packing the copied pages into a waterproof sleeve, already checking the Fox's tie-downs in her mind, already feeling the stick in her hand though her hands were empty. "There's a pass through the Kestrels that isn't on any map. And we're going to be over it before Wren knows his fuel drums are missing."

Outside, far across the dark field, a light came on in the operations building.

Wren was awake too.
