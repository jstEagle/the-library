## 8. Night Departure

The sedative Ilsa gave me was meant for a dog twice Elias's size, and even so he fought it. He fought it the way men fight drowning—quietly at first, then with everything.

"Freya." His hand closed around my wrist in the hangar's yellow light, and his eyes were clear for one terrible second, fever burned down to something cold and certain. "If they take the book, it never happened. You understand? Twenty years from now there'll be nothing but their maps."

"I understand."

"They'll say we were evacuated. They'll say it kindly." His grip tightened. "Kindness is how they do it. Kindness and paper."

Ilsa put her hand on his shoulder and pressed him gently back against the crate, and whatever was in her face made him let go. She'd been a company pilot longer than I'd been alive. Men like Elias trusted that face the way they trusted weather glass.

"He'll sleep six hours," she said to me. "Maybe seven if the cold holds. After that you want to be over the saddle or on the far side of it."

The cold held. It held all night, the kind of hard, windless frost that makes the stars look close enough to touch and turns every breath into smoke. I worked by lamplight with my coat off and my hands aching, doing things I had done ten thousand times and never once at midnight, never once with my heart going like this.

Fuel first. The Otter took sixty gallons an hour at cruise and I wanted nine hours of range, which meant tanks full plus drums lashed in the cargo bay, which meant weight, which meant I'd be heavy off the ice and slow on the climb. There was no way around it. The pass Ilsa charted for me sat at eleven thousand feet and the Kestrel Range does not negotiate.

Then the pages. I'd copied them two nights ago in the washroom of the company barracks, kneeling on the floor with the field book open on my thighs and the door locked, tracing Ruth Okonkwo's tight surveyor's hand onto butcher paper by the light under the door. Elevations. Bearings. And on the third page, in ink gone brown, a sketch of the valley itself—the river bending through it like a signature, the terraces, the long roofs of buildings that were not ruins because someone had drawn smoke rising from them.

I sewed the copies into the lining of my flight jacket with waxed thread, the same stitch Ilsa used on her harness repairs. Then I did the same for the original field book, which would ride in the cargo bay inside Elias's coat, because if Wren's people ever got their hands on me I wanted the true book as far from my body as physics allowed.

It's strange what your mind fixes on at hours like that. Standing in the hangar at three in the morning with a syringe of dog tranquilizer in my pocket and a felony taking shape in my hands, what I kept thinking about was the smell of the ledger office at Halvorsen—carbon paper and coffee and Aldous Wren's cologne, something expensive and faintly medicinal, like the whole building had been disinfected of anything human.

*Restructured,* he'd said. *We take care of our people, Freya.*

He'd said it the way you'd say *we shoot our horses* —gently, meaning well, meaning exactly what he said.

* * *

At 4:40 I woke the Otter.

She's a 1957 de Havilland Beaver that Halvorsen bought surplus and never loved, and she has a voice like a grudge—startled cough, then that big radial settling into its idle, each cylinder catching in turn until the whole engine finds its rhythm and the propeller becomes a disc of silver in the lamplight. Her name is Mara. I named her when I was twenty-two and thought naming things was a way of keeping them. She is collateral. That word has hung around her tailwheel for six years like a tag on a store animal.

Elias went in through the cargo door first, Ilsa and I passing his dead weight between us like furniture, and I strapped him down among the fuel drums and wrapped the blanket to his chin. His fever had broken sometime before midnight; now he slept the flat, untroubled sleep of the chemically conquered, and his face in the lamplight looked young—younger than mine, though he'd walked off a glacier with a company rifle detail behind him.

Ilsa stood at the cargo door while I finished the tie-downs. When I came back out she didn't hug me. She took both my hands instead, and hers were cold and rough and steady as tow bars.

"Radio silence until you're past the ridge," she said. "They'll have a man watching the tower from five o'clock. You'll be wheels-up at quarter to five, so you've got maybe four minutes where nobody's sure what they're seeing."

"And after that?"

"After that you're a thief in a stolen airplane." She said it without flinching, and I understood that she needed to say it aloud, needed us both to hear it plainly, because a thing named can be survived. "There are three patrol aircraft grounded at the field. The Norseman's down for an engine change—I checked. The Anson's got a cracked cylinder they haven't ordered parts for. But the Stinson flies, and Voss Kettering flies it, and Kettering is very good."

"Kettering's in Fort Meridian. Wren sent him south Tuesday."

"Wren recalled him yesterday afternoon. He landed at six." She let that sit. "Freya. If Kettering gets behind you, don't run the pass low. Climb early, climb ugly, get over the top with margin to spare. A man who knows the range will expect you to hug the terrain. Don't give him the terrain."

"What do I give him?"

"Nothing." She almost smiled. "Give him nothing. It's the one thing you've always been good at."

Then she hugged me, brief and fierce, her chin against my ear, and said the thing she'd flown forty miles through the dark to say: "Twenty years ago I filed a map that wasn't true, and I have carried it every day since, and I am telling you as a woman who knows: the flying home is easier than the not-flying-back."

I didn't fully understand her until later. Maybe I still don't. But I nodded, and she stepped away, and I climbed up into Mara's cockpit with my jacket crackling faintly where the pages lay sewn against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

* * *

Quarter to five. The field lights were off except the beacon, sweeping its slow white arm across the frost. I ran up the throttle and Mara rolled, heavy, sluggish, the snow squeaking under the tires, and lifted on the second try with maybe eighty feet of runway left and the trees reaching for the tail.

I turned north, into the black shoulder of the country, and throttled back to a climb that wouldn't shake the fuel drums loose, and for about ninety seconds everything in the world was engine noise and instrument glow and the enormous relief of being airborne.

Then the tower radio crackled.

"—field, anyone airborne? Say again, who is airborne?" A pause, static, then sharper: "Beacon contact departing zero-four-five, climbing. Ground, wake up Wren. Wake him *now.*"

I switched the radio off. It felt enormous, that small click—the loudest thing I'd ever done. Six years of answering every call, filing every plan, logging every hour for the company that owned my wings, ended by a switch the size of my thumbnail.

Ahead the sky was going grey-green in the east, the first suggestion of dawn, and below me the world was still night: black forest, black rivers, the frozen lakes lying pale as dropped plates. I flew toward the Kestrels by memory and starlight, climbing in slow spirals to buy altitude before the terrain forced the issue, and I let myself feel it—that thing Ilsa must have felt at twenty-two and hated herself for later. Exhilaration. The pure animal joy of a stolen airplane and an open sky and no one's permission anywhere in the world required.

It lasted eleven minutes.

* * *

The Stinson came out of the west at first light.

I saw him before he saw me—a speck against the brightening sky, low, crossing my track three miles behind—and my stomach dropped through the floor of the aircraft. Kettering. Nobody else flew a patrol pattern that patient, that wide, sweeping the valleys like a man reading a page line by line.

I did what Ilsa told me. I climbed. Full throttle, mixture rich, nose up until Mara groaned and the airspeed bled back to seventy and the drums shifted in the bay with a sound like furniture moving in the apartment above. Behind me the speck grew wings. He'd seen me. Of course he'd seen me; there was nothing else in the sky and we both knew it.

The radio came alive. Not the tower frequency—air-to-air, 126.7, the channel every bush pilot in the territory keeps tuned because it's where you call out traffic and weather and, once in a great while, trouble.

"Mara, Mara, this is Stimson patrol." Kettering's voice, unhurried, almost gentle. "Freya, I know it's you. Nobody else flies that old girl like she owes them money."

I said nothing. My thumb hovered over the transmit button and I left it there.

"You've got a sick man aboard and a heading for the pass," he went on. "Wren wants you back at the field. He's saying you took company property and a company patient. He's saying kidnapping, Freya. He's already called the federal magistrate in the capital—he'll have a warrant on the wire by noon." A pause, and when he spoke again something had shifted in his voice, some grain of reluctance, quickly smoothed over. "Turn south. Land at Meridian. I'll fly formation with you the whole way and I won't say a word to anyone about the weather you beat coming out of that valley. You have my word."

My word. From a company tracker whose word was the only currency Wren paid him in.

I looked at the altimeter—nine thousand eight hundred and climbing, the saddle at eleven thousand still hidden behind the massif ahead, its summit smoking in the dawn wind. I looked back. The Stinson had closed to two miles, holding my altitude, patient as debt.

And behind him, far to the south, a second speck. Smaller. Climbing.

The Anson. Cracked cylinder and all. Wren had found a wrench and a prayer.

"Mara," Kettering said again, and now there was no gentleness in it at all, just the flat professional sadness of a man watching someone walk into machinery. "Last chance. Turn south."

I keyed the transmit button, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect.

"Stimson patrol, Mara. Tell Wren the survey team wasn't relocated." I watched the massif slide closer, the saddle appearing now between the peaks, a notch of grey sky like a door left open. "Tell him I read the logbook. Tell him the passage is real."

I shoved the throttle to the stop and turned north into the pass.

What happened next happened fast. Kettering followed—I'll grant him that much courage—and the Anson came on behind like a dog that hadn't been asked to. The wind over the saddle hit us at fifty knots gusting, dropping the Otter two hundred feet in a single sickening breath, and I hauled her back up with my arms shaking and the stall horn singing its thin idiot note and the drums slamming in the bay, and for one long moment the mountain rose faster than I did.

Then the notch was under me. Then it was behind me.

The Kestrel Range fell away on both sides, and beyond it the land opened northward—unknown country, unmapped country, country that existed only in a cartographer's fever and the pages sewn against my ribs. The Anson turned back at the crest; whatever Wren had promised his mechanic, he hadn't promised him the far side of the range in winter.

Kettering held my altitude at the saddle for a full minute, wingtip to wingtip a mile apart, and I looked over at the Stinson's cockpit and could not see his face, only the sun flashing off the glass. Then he rocked his wings once—pilot's shorthand, *good luck* or *you're dead*, impossible to say which—and turned south, and descended, and became a speck, and became nothing.

The warrant came thirty seconds later.

Not on the radio. On the teletype relay, keyed through the civil frequency in clear text so any operator in the territory would see it, which was the point: Wren wanted the whole North to know. I watched the words print themselves across the strip of paper in the receiver tray, letter by letter, in that clattering bureaucratic voice:

**ALL STATIONS ALL STATIONS NOTICE TO AIRMEN STOP FREYA DRAVENMOOR PILOT LICENCE 4471-A WANTED FOR THEFT OF COMPANY AIRCRAFT AND PROPERTY AND UNLAWFUL REMOVAL OF PERSON UNDER MEDICAL CARE STOP AIRCRAFT DE HAVILLAND OTTER REGISTRATION HALVORSEN 12 STOP REPORT SIGHTINGS TO HALVORSEN OPERATIONS OR NEAREST CONSTABLE STOP REWARD CONSIDERED STOP**

Reward considered. As if I were a lost dog.

I read it twice. Then I folded the strip into quarters and tucked it into the pocket of the door, because some part of me understood I might need it someday—evidence of the exact moment the company stopped owning me and started hunting me, and how little difference there was between the two.

Elias stirred in the cargo bay. Through the open window behind my head I heard him murmur something—not words yet, just sound, a man surfacing slowly from deep water toward a shore he wouldn't recognize.

"It's all right," I said, not knowing if he could hear me, knowing it didn't matter. "We're over the pass. We're in the part that isn't on their maps."

Mara droned north into the morning. Below, the rivers ran the wrong direction for any chart I'd ever filed, and the mountains had names no surveyor had written down, and somewhere ahead—two days' flying if the weather held, a lifetime if it didn't—a tribunal was convening in the capital with Halvorsen's lawyers already seated and falsified maps already entered into evidence.

Behind me, the early snows had begun to fall on the Kestrel Range.

Ahead of me, everything else.
