## 9. Whiteout Over the Saddle

The storm announced itself an hour before it arrived, the way storms in the Kestrel always did—not with drama but with a change in the light, as if someone had turned down the sun's rheostat one notch at a time until Freya realized she was flying through late afternoon that should have been noon.

She'd seen it building on the western horizon since the pass came into view: a gray wall stacked up against the saddle like a debt collector waiting at the door. Patient. Certain. The barometer in her head—twenty years of flying had installed one there, more reliable than the brass instrument bolted to the panel—had been falling all morning.

"Coming down," she said over her shoulder. It was half information, half prayer.

Elias didn't answer. He was strapped into the cargo bay behind the empty fuel drums, wrapped in every blanket she owned, his fever finally broken sometime over the last ridge. He'd slept like a man paying back a decade of debt in installments, and when he woke briefly over the glacier field he'd looked out the window at the ice with an expression she couldn't read—recognition, or dread, or both.

The de Havilland bucked once, twice, and then the turbulence found its rhythm and began to shake her like a dog with a rag. She checked the clock. Two hours of fuel, maybe two and a half if she leaned the mixture until the engine complained. The pass was forty minutes ahead. The tribunal was three days away by air, less if the weather held, which it would not—the snows were coming early this year, everyone said so, and everyone had been right about everything else going wrong.

Through the windshield, the wall of gray swallowed the saddle whole.

She could see it happening ahead of her: the whiteout building from the top down, cloud pressing onto rock, rock disappearing into cloud, until there was no horizon at all, no up, no down, just a whiteness so complete that her eyes watered trying to find edges in it. Pilots had died in exactly that kind of sky, disoriented, convinced the ground was above them, easing the stick toward what their inner ear swore was level flight and driving themselves into the earth at cruise speed.

Freya Dravenmoor did not fly into whiteouts. That was rule one, learned before she could legally solo, drilled into her by Ilsa Voss in a hangar that smelled of dope and fabric and pipe smoke: *The mountain doesn't care how good you are. The mountain only cares whether you can see it.*

But the mountain also didn't care about tribunals, or permits expiring, or a valley full of people who officially did not exist. And somewhere behind her, Wren's patrol aircraft were presumably warming up, though the storm would ground them too—for now. Storms end. Warrants don't.

She scanned below. The terrain dropped away from the pass approach in a long descending bench of forest and rock, and there—there, God thank you—a lake. Frozen white, oval, maybe a mile long, sitting in a bowl like an egg in a spoon. Flat. Open. The kind of landing surface you dreamed about and never trusted, because lake ice kept its own counsel about thickness.

It wasn't the pass. It was short of the pass by fifteen miles of the worst country in the range. But it was down, and dry, and it was right here while the whiteout was right there, closing like a hand.

"Elias." She craned around. "Brace yourself. We're landing."

His eyes opened. Fever-bright still, but focused. "The pass?"

"Tomorrow."

He nodded once and pulled the blankets tighter, and something in his face told her he understood tomorrow better than most people did.

* * *

She came in low over the tree line with the flaps down and the airspeed bleeding off, watching the lake grow beneath her, reading its surface the way Ilsa had taught her to read everything—wind streaks in the snow, drift lines, the darker patches where wind had scoured to bare ice. No pressure ridges visible. No open leads. The lake had frozen clean and early, and the snow on it lay thin and even.

The touchdown itself was almost gentle. Tailwheel first, the old bird settling onto the ice with a sound like a book closing, and for one full second Freya let herself believe she'd gotten away with it.

Then the left main gear hit a patch of ice that had frozen over a submerged shelf, and the ice said no.

The crack was small—a sound like a rifle shot a mile distant—but she felt it come up through the airframe, felt the geometry of the landing gear change under her, and the tail swung left and the plane slewed sideways across the ice in a long grinding arc before she stood on the brakes and brought it to rest facing nothing in particular, engine idling, propeller turning, the whiteout wall advancing across the far shore like a tide.

She shut down. Sat still. Listened to the ticking of the cooling engine and the silence after.

When she climbed out, boots crunching, the cold hit her like a physical argument. She walked around to the left gear and crouched. The axle had cracked clean through at the bend where twenty years of hard landings had worked the metal, and the wheel sat cocked at an angle that made her stomach drop. Not repairable. Not with what she carried. Maybe not repairable at all, not here, not without a welder and a forge and a miracle.

Her plane. Her beautiful, borrowed, mortgaged plane, sitting broken on a frozen lake in the middle of nowhere, with a warrant out on its pilot.

She laughed. It came out wrong, high and thin, and she stopped it with her hand over her mouth.

Behind her, the cargo door scraped open. Elias stood in the doorway swaying, hollow-eyed, holding himself upright by the frame. He looked worse than the fever—fever at least had energy in it. This was the look of a man who had walked out of the mountains already once and knew precisely what it cost.

"The gear?" he asked.

"Dead."

"And the radio?"

She'd tried it during the descent. Static on every band, the storm front chewing the atmosphere to pieces. "Also dead. Or deaf, anyway. Might transmit. Nobody'd hear it."

He looked past her at the advancing wall of white, and then at the plane, and then at her, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she was doing. Fuel: enough to run the heater intermittently for two days, no more. Food: four days if they were careful, three if Elias's body demanded repayment for the fever. The lake was maybe six miles from the tree line, and beyond the tree line was fifteen miles of pass country that Elias had crossed once, delirious, dying, downhill.

They were uphill of that now.

"We wait," Elias said. "Storms blow through in a day. We wait it out, you fix—" He stopped. Looked at the axle again. "You can't fix that."

"No."

"So we wait, and then we walk anyway." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it.

"Or we wait," Freya said, "and the snows come early, like everyone keeps saying they will, and we're here until spring. And the tribunal sits next week without us, and Wren files his pretty maps, and the valley goes back to being a hole in the world that Halvorsen gets to fill with whatever it wants."

The first flakes arrived while she was saying it. Big, lazy, theatrical flakes, spinning down out of a sky that had gone the color of dishwater, and within minutes the far shore of the lake was gone, and then the near trees, and then the world contracted to a circle thirty yards across with a broken airplane at the center of it.

* * *

They got the plane buttoned up and dragged the sleeping bags and the heater and the food into the cargo bay, because the fuselage would hold heat better than any tent and because neither of them said aloud the other thing: that the plane was a landmark, and landmarks could be found, and being findable cut both ways.

Freya ran the heater in fifteen-minute cycles, hoarding fuel like a miser, and they sat shoulder to shoulder in the cargo bay listening to the storm lean on the airframe. Wind sang through the cracked gear strut, a thin eerie note, and snow hissed against the fabric skin, and outside the windows there was nothing at all—no lake, no shore, no world. Just white, and the small amber cave of the cabin, and the two of them in it.

Claustrophobic wasn't the word. Claustrophobia was about walls. This was about erasure. The storm hadn't trapped them; it had deleted them. She kept thinking of the survey camp, pristine, stove warm, logbook open—*PASSAGE REAL*—as if the whole team had been deleted the same way, erased from the world between one entry and the next. Men with company rifles. Men whose names were probably in the field book sewn into Elias's coat lining, or had been, before she copied the pages and hid them in the map case against her spine.

"You're doing the thing," Elias said.

"What thing?"

"Where you stare at the window like it owes you money."

She huffed something that was almost a laugh. "I'm calculating."

"Out loud might help. My experience is that calculations made silently always come out worse."

"Fine." She held up fingers. "One: we wait. Storm passes, snows arrive on schedule or early, we're stuck here until the company finds us, which they will, because a broken plane on a lake is the easiest search object in the north. Two: we wait, storm passes, we walk, but we've lost a day and the snow's deeper and we're weaker, and we miss the tribunal by hours." She folded down the third finger. "Three: we go now. Tonight, or first light. Ahead of the storm's tail, ahead of the snows, while the pass is still passable on foot. Fifteen miles. Twenty with the switchbacks. One hard day, maybe two."

"In a whiteout."

"The whiteout is here. The pass may be under it or clear above it—storms like this sit in the valleys. And Elias." She turned to look at him straight on. In the heater light his face was all planes and shadows, cheekbones sharp from the fever, eyes too bright. "You crossed this country once already. Half-dead. Alone. Downhill, yes, but you crossed it. You know the route in your bones—you've been repeating the coordinates in your sleep for three days."

"That was delirium."

"It was memory. Delirium is just memory with the doors blown open." She let that sit. "I can navigate. What I need is your recollection of the terrain—where the moraine funnels, where the glacier crevasses, where the slope turns to scree. Between the two of us we make one whole surveyor."

He was quiet a long time. The heater cycled off and the cold pressed in immediately, patient as interest accruing.

"When I walked out," he said finally, "I wasn't choosing the route. I was falling downhill and surviving it. There's a difference."

"Not as much as you think. Ruth Okonkwo trained you. You marked water sources without knowing you were marking them—I've read your field notes. Your margins are full of them. Springs, seeps, game trails. A cartographer's habit." She tapped the map case where it rode against his coat. "Your habits are better maps than my charts. My charts don't show this country at all. That's rather the point."

Another silence. Outside, a gust hit the tail and the whole airframe rocked on its cracked leg, and the singing note in the strut rose half a tone, and Freya thought absurdly: *even the plane is voting.*

"If we go," Elias said slowly, "and the storm follows us up—"

"Then we dig in and sit it out on the mountain, which kills us. Yes. I've considered."

"And if we stay—"

"—Wren finds us, takes the book, takes us, and the valley dies quietly in a filing cabinet. Also considered." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "There's no version of this where we're safe, Elias. There's only versions where the truth arrives on time or doesn't. Everything else is weather."

He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something in him, the way a load settles in an aircraft—shifting forward, center of gravity moving to where it belonged.

"First light," he said. "Not tonight. We sleep, we eat, we melt snow. Cold muscles die on moraine. And—" he hesitated, and then reached inside his coat and drew out the field book, holding it in both hands like something rescued from a fire, "—if we're walking into country where the company's trackers operate, the book shouldn't be on me. I'm the one they'll catch. I'm the slow one, still. It should be on you."

"I can't take that from you."

"You're not taking it. I'm giving it. There's a difference." His mouth twitched, the ghost of humor surfacing through the exhaustion. "Ruth gave it to me to carry out. I'm carrying it out. Just... changing aircraft mid-route."

She took it. It weighed almost nothing, the way important things always did, and she stowed it in the map case against her spine where it sat like a second heartbeat.

"First light," she agreed.

* * *

She didn't sleep much. Around two in the morning she ran the heater, melted snow in the dented pot, made them both drink warm water with the last of the sugar stirred in, and sat watching the windows while Elias slept the dreamless sleep of the genuinely spent.

The storm raged, and the plane rocked, and the singing note in the broken strut went on and on, and somewhere in the small hours Freya realized she was memorizing the sound. Filing it away. Because she knew—with the certainty that lived in her hands and not her head—that she was looking at her airplane for the last time. Whatever happened on the mountain, whatever happened at the tribunal, the old girl wasn't flying out of this lake. The company would impound her, or the ice would take her in the spring breakup, or she'd stand as evidence in some federal yard rusting under a tarp.

Twenty years of debt and dawn patrols and medicine runs into silent valleys, and it came down to this: she could keep the plane by staying, and lose everything the plane was for. Or she could walk away from it into a whiteout, carrying a dead woman's field book toward a room full of lawyers, and find out what she was worth without wings.

Ilsa's voice, from twenty years ago, from the hangar: *A pilot isn't the machine, Freya. The machine is just how you practice.*

She fed the fire one last time, and lay back, and let the storm sing her partway toward sleep.

At first light, the wind had dropped to a mutter. She unzipped the door and cold poured in like water, clean and enormous, and the sky above the lake was breaking apart into high racing scud with gaps of hard blue behind it. To the west, toward the pass, the peaks stood out black and glittering under fresh snow—and above the saddle itself, impossibly, insultingly, a window of clear air.

Fifteen miles. Two days of food. One recovering cartographer and one grounded pilot, on foot, ahead of the snows.

Freya laced her boots, slung the map case, and woke Elias with a hand on his shoulder.

"Window's open," she said. "We fly the way we always wished we could. Straight through the mountain."
