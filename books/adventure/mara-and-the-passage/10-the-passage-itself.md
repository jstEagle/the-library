## 10. The Passage Itself

**Freya**

The storm broke sometime in the night, and by first light the lake had gone glass-still, a sheet of white ice holding the sky the way a mirror holds a face. Freya stood at the wreck of her tailwheel and hated it a little, the way you hate a friend who has failed you at the worst possible moment. The Otter sat nose-down on the shore, one ski crumpled, the propeller bent into a question mark.

"Fuel's good," she said, mostly to herself. "Everything else is broken."

Behind her, Elias was already packing. He moved like a man who had done this before—had walked out of worse places than this—and that frightened her more than the mountain did. A man who could survive Team Nine's fate had learned things she didn't want to learn.

"The pass is northeast," he said, cinching the last strap on his pack. "Two days if the snow holds off. Three if it doesn't."

"And if we miss the tribunal?"

He looked at her then, and there was something in his fever-burned face that hadn't been there before—not madness, not fear. Certainty. "Then the valley stays a rumor until Halvorsen finds it again. And this time they won't bother with maps."

They crossed the frozen lake in silence, boots crunching, breath hanging in the air like small ghosts. At the far shore the land began to climb, and Freya took one last look back at the plane—her plane, or the company's plane, depending on which ledger you believed—and turned away from it without letting herself think about what that meant.

* * *

**Elias**

Elias remembered the route the way you remember a fall—the body keeps its own map of terror. Here the moraine had shifted; there the crevasse field had opened since autumn. But the bones of the passage held: the couloir behind the twin peaks, the shelf of wind-scoured stone where Ruth Okonkwo had stood with her theodolite and said, quietly, *I will not erase these people.*

He led because he had no choice. His hands still shook from the fever, and every hour he expected the sickness to come back and fold him into the snow. But the coordinates lived in him now, carved deeper than the field book ever could. The company had taken the book once. They would not take this.

"You're limping," Freya said, falling beside him.

"I'm walking," he corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is forward."

She almost smiled. In the two days since the lake she had changed—or maybe he was finally seeing what had always been there. She checked their bearing against his half-remembered sketches without questioning them. She rationed the food without complaint. And when he woke shivering in the night, she had moved closer without being asked, a wall of borrowed warmth between him and the wind.

By noon of the second day they reached the shelf, and below them the world opened.

* * *

**Freya**

Freya had flown over mountains her whole life. She knew how ranges lied to you from altitude, how valleys hid themselves in shadow, how a glacier could swallow a river whole. Nothing had prepared her for this.

The valley ran north to south, maybe eight miles long, cupped between walls of granite so sheer they seemed drawn rather than formed. A river stitched through its floor, silver-bright, braiding around islands of spruce. Smoke rose from chimneys—chimneys, plural—a dozen low roofs scattered along the terraces above the water. Fields, yellowed with late season, lay in careful strips. And on the slopes above the far end, dark shapes moved slowly through the snow-dust: cattle, or something like cattle, grazing where no map said anything could live.

"It's real," she said, and heard her own voice crack on the second word.

Elias said nothing. He was crying, silently, the tears freezing in the lines of his face, and Freya understood that he had needed this proof more than she had—that for weeks he had carried the knowledge alone, half-mad with it, and here it was, breathing smoke into the cold air.

Then she saw the watchtower.

It stood on the near ridge, a rough timber frame no higher than a barn, and before they were within a quarter mile of it a figure stepped out from behind it with a rifle leveled at them.

The woman was young, maybe Freya's age, dressed in hide and wool, dark hair whipping in the wind. She held the rifle the way Ilsa held a wrench—with total, unglamorous competence.

"That's far enough," she called. Her voice carried flat across the snow. "Names."

"Freya Dravenmoor." Freya kept her hands visible, empty. "This is Elias Brandt. He was with Survey Team Nine."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes at that name—something old and hard.

"I know about Team Nine," she said. "We watched them come down the glacier in October. We watched the men follow them." Her rifle didn't waver. "We watched the smoke from their camp stop rising. So you'll understand why I'm not going to lower this gun just because you say words."

"We're not with them," Elias said hoarsely. "Ruth Okonkwo refused to falsify the survey. They killed her for it. I ran with the original field book—it's copied, hidden, it's going to the federal tribunal in four days—"

"Four days." The woman's mouth twisted. "You have four days to save us, and you spent two of them crashed on a lake."

Freya felt the rebuke land exactly where it was aimed. "We came as fast as the weather allowed. I lost my plane doing it."

That, oddly, made the woman pause. Her gaze went past them, back toward the range they'd crossed, as if she could see the wrecked Otter from here. When she spoke again, her voice had lost some of its edge.

"Sena," she said. "My name is Sena. My father helped build that tower, after the expulsion. We've been watching that pass for twenty years, waiting for whoever came through it to be either salvation or the end of us." She lowered the rifle an inch. Not all the way. "So which are you?"

* * *

**Elias**

Elias walked down into the valley between Sena's rifle and Sena's questions, and each answer cost him something.

Yes, he had mapped their homes. No, those maps would never reach the company's hands. Yes, Ruth Okonkwo had seen the smoke too, had stood on the ridge with her instruments and recorded a population of sixty-one souls where the official survey recorded zero. No, he didn't know exactly how many of the company men had come for them. Enough. Rifled. Methodical.

"They gave us a choice," he said, when Sena asked. "Sign the falsified sheets, or stay in the range through winter. Ruth signed nothing. They—" His voice failed. He tried again. "They marched the others out. I don't know where. I ran east, into the moraine, with the field book sewn into my coat. I walked three days. A trapper found me."

Sena listened without interrupting, and Elias understood that this was a woman who had learned patience from danger. At the edge of the first terrace, people had begun to gather—an old man with a staff, two women in embroidered collars, children peering from doorways with the frank stare of kids who had never been taught to hide their looking.

A generation, Elias thought. An entire generation born and raised inside a hole in the map. They had schools. They had graves. They had a cemetery on the hillside with thirty years of markers, and not one of those deaths had ever been recorded anywhere on earth except here.

An older woman pushed through the crowd and gripped Sena's arm, speaking low and fast in a language Elias didn't know. Sena answered in the same tongue, then switched back.

"The council will hear you tonight," she told them. "Until then, you eat in my father's house, and you don't walk past the second terrace, and you don't touch anything that looks like a record. We keep our own books here. Our own names. If your tribunal fails us, those books are all we'll have left."

"What happens to us if the council says no?" Freya asked quietly.

Sena looked at her for a long moment.

"Nothing," she said. "Nobody disappears anybody in this valley. That's the whole point of it." A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "But you'd leave tomorrow, and you'd never find this place again, and in five years Halvorsen's drills would be on that ridge." She nodded up-slope, toward the tower. "So eat. Rest. Tonight you convince us you're worth dying for."

* * *

**Freya**

The house Sena's father had built sat on the middle terrace, timber and stone, with a stove that threw heat like a promise. There was soup—barley and something gamey Freya couldn't name—and bread darker and denser than any she'd bought in company towns, and after two days of trail rations she ate until she was ashamed.

Sena's father had died in the expulsion. She said it plainly, the way you'd report weather. He had gone out to meet the company men with his hands open, and they had shot him, and the valley had learned in one morning what its twenty years of hiding were worth. Freya thought of Wren saying *restructured*, smiling across his desk, and understood that men like Wren never fired the shots themselves. They just arranged the weather.

"You flew for them," Sena said. It wasn't an accusation. It was arithmetic.

"I fly for them. There's a difference, and the difference is shrinking."

"My father opened his hands." Sena set down her spoon. "I was nine. I remember thinking the men with rifles looked embarrassed. That's the part nobody believes—they were *embarrassed*. They did it anyway." She studied Freya across the table. "So when you tell me tonight that you chose truth, I'll want to know what it cost you to choose it. Not what it will cost. What it already has."

Freya thought of the ledger. Of Ilsa's face in the hangar lamplight, saying *Wren never forgives anything*. Of the warrant with her name on it, crackling over the radio somewhere behind her, getting closer with every mile she walked.

"Everything so far," she said. "And it hasn't even started collecting."

Sena nodded slowly, as if Freya had finally given a correct answer to a very long exam.

* * *

**Elias**

The council met in a long hall above the granary, and Elias stood before them with the copied pages spread on a trestle table, feeling smaller than he had ever felt in his life. These were the people he had erased. He had drawn their valley as empty ground with a steady hand and a clear conscience, because the alternative had been a rifle barrel, and he had run instead of refusing, and Ruth had paid the price of the courage he hadn't found until it was too late to matter.

He told them all of it. He did not soften himself in the telling.

When he finished, an elder with clouded eyes spoke for a long time in their own language, and the room argued in low voices, and Elias stood in the noise of it understanding that his life, and Freya's, and possibly the lives of everyone in this valley, were being weighed on scales he couldn't see.

Sena's voice cut through last.

"He came back," she said. "The surveyors came, and the company came, and every one of them left or was made to leave. This one walked back in, sick and starving, carrying the only copy of the truth. And the pilot"—she gestured at Freya—"crashed her own aircraft rather than turn around. You want a sign? There are no signs. There are only people, and what they do. Look at what they did."

Silence. Then the clouded-eyed elder spoke again, briefly, and the room settled.

"The runners go tonight," Sena translated, her voice thick. "Four of them, over the southern divide, to the railhead telegraph. The pages travel with Elias and the pilot. And the valley"—she stopped, steadied herself—"the valley sends its testimony for the first time in twenty years."

Afterward, outside the hall, under a sky salted with early stars, Sena stood beside Freya and looked north, toward the capital neither of them could see.

"Four days," she said. "And the snows are coming early this year. Everyone says so."

Freya followed her gaze. Far up the divide, the peaks wore fresh white like a warning.

"Then we'd better be faster than the weather," Freya said. "We've got a tribunal to reach—and I have a feeling we're not the only ones moving."

Down in the dark of the valley, a dog began to bark at the ridgeline, and did not stop.
