## 11. Sena's Testimony

The council fire was lit in the longhouse before dawn, because the elders believed that arguments made in darkness were arguments the mountain could not overhear. Sena had grown up with this belief the way she'd grown up with her father's hands on her shoulders—steadying, unquestioned—and so she sat now in the smoke-smell and the orange light, watching eleven old faces consider whether to trust two strangers who had walked out of the snow.

The strangers were not present. That was the first thing decided, before she'd even arrived: the pilot woman and the feverish cartographer would wait in the watchtower hut under guard until the council spoke. Sena had argued against even that, briefly, and been overruled by her grandmother, which was the natural order of things.

"You found them," said Old Harl, who kept the herd counts and had a voice like gravel sliding off a roof. "You brought them inside the line. So you'll tell us why we shouldn't put them back out."

Sena folded her hands. Around her, the longhouse was full of the small sounds of a valley waking—goats complaining in their pens below, someone splitting kindling, the creak of the great beam overhead where hams and seed sacks hung in the cold rafters. Forty families. A hundred and sixty souls, give or take the season's births and burials. Two generations of people who did not exist.

She knew exactly why they shouldn't be put back out. She also knew exactly what it might cost to keep them.

* * *

Her father had died on a night much like this one, twenty-two years ago, when the company men came up the south trail with rifles and paper. She had been six. What she remembered was not the shooting—she never saw the shooting—but the sound of her mother burning documents in the woodstove at midnight, feeding in maps and letters page by page, and the way the firelight made her mother's face look like something carved from pine. Her mother told her afterward that Papa had gone down to talk to them. Talk. The word her mother used for the rest of her life, always in that same flat voice, as if the word itself had been shot too.

The expulsion was the founding wound of the valley. Before it, there had been homesteads scattered through the low country—her family among them, and the Vasska cousins, and the Oduya line, and others, all of them holding land the government had registered and then, when Halvorsen's surveyors arrived with concession papers, quietly unregistered. Not seized. Unregistered. As if ink could reach back through time and make a man's grandfather never have plowed a field. The ones who fought went to jail or into the ground. The rest walked up into the Kestrel Range through a pass no map showed, following a guide named Torv who had found the valley as a boy while hunting ibex, and they had lived here ever since—unrecorded, untaxed, unmourned by any ledger anywhere.

It had worked for twenty-two years because of three rules, and her grandmother had helped write all three. Never come down. Never trade where you can be seen. Never, ever let an outsider leave who knows the way in.

That third rule was the one sitting in the room tonight like a loaded rifle across everyone's knees.

* * *

"The woman is a pilot," said Marta Vasska, who ran the clinic and had trained half the valley's midwives. "A pilot means aircraft. Aircraft mean the company can find this place from the air whenever it likes, and all our watching of the trails is worth nothing."

"She came in on foot," Sena said. "Through the pass. In a storm. With a sick man on her back for the last day of it." She had seen the state of them when they staggered into the watchtower clearing—the pilot's shoulders rubbed raw where the pack straps had bitten, the man Elias half-conscious and muttering coordinates like prayers. Nobody carried a stranger over frozen moraine out of calculation. You did it because putting him down meant killing him, and something in her wouldn't let go of that.

"Or she did it because the company sent her," said Harl. "Sent her in soft, sent her in hurt, so we'd open the gate ourselves. Twenty years ago they tried buying Torv's nephew with a rifle and a promise. Now they send a woman with kind eyes."

"She didn't know the pass existed until the sick man led her to it."

"How do you know?"

"Because I asked her, alone, without the guards in earshot. And because I watched her face when she first saw the valley from the saddle." Sena paused. This was the part she'd turned over all night, the part she couldn't stop turning over. "You've all seen what people look like when they find something they intend to take. There's a way the eyes go. Measuring. She wasn't measuring anything. She stood there in the snow and cried, and then she apologized to me—for walking in. Apologized. To me."

Silence around the fire. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and quit.

"And the man?" said her grandmother, from the head seat. Yeva Oduya was eighty-one years old and had not raised her voice in Sena's living memory; she never needed to. "The cartographer. He carries a book. The pilot says he carried it sewn into his coat, past men with guns, down a glacier, through a whiteout. A book about us."

"Not about us," Sena said. "About the land. But if the book says what she thinks it says—if it proves the valley is inhabited, has been inhabited, was surveyed and then erased—" She stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be taken away. She said it anyway. "Then everything the company filed with the federal tribunal is a lie. Their permits die. And we exist again."

"Existing again," said Harl, "is how my brother died."

"No," Sena said, and heard her own voice harden. "Being found by men with rifles is how your brother died. Being erased is how the rest of us have died slowly ever since. My father talked to those men and they shot him, yes. But do you know what killed him, really? Paper. A stamp in some office in the capital that said our farm belonged to nobody. They didn't need to beat us. They just needed the world to forget we were here. And we have spent twenty-two years helping them forget, because forgetting felt like safety." She looked around the fire, at the eleven faces, at her grandmother's still ones. "I'm tired of being safe the way the dead are safe."

* * *

They broke at midmorning for food and argument in smaller knots, which was the council's way—nothing decided in one heat. Sena went out into the blue-white glare and found the pilot woman sitting on the watchtower steps between her two guards, drinking tea, looking up at the peaks with an expression Sena recognized from mirrors: the look of someone doing arithmetic with distances.

Freya Dravenmoor. The name had come out of her in fragments the first night, along with the story—a mining company that owned her plane the way lenders own widows, a survey team vanished from its own camp, orders to file false reports, a stolen departure through a patrol line. Sena had listened the way her mother had taught her to listen to travelers: for the seams. Liars' stories are smooth. Truth is seamed and patched and contradicts itself in small ways. Freya's story was nothing but seams.

"You're deciding right now whether I'm worth the risk," Freya said when Sena sat down beside her. Not accusing. Just tired, and clear-eyed.

"I'm deciding whether my whole valley is worth your risk," Sena said. "It's a bigger number."

Freya nodded slowly. "Fair." She turned her tin cup in her hands. "Can I tell you something? When I saw the valley from the saddle, the first thing I thought was—I could keep this. Fly out, say nothing, and know there's one place on earth the company hasn't gotten its hooks into. One clean thing. I could just let it stay secret and safe." She shook her head. "And then I thought about what 'secret' bought you. Your kids grow up with no school beyond what your elders can teach. No medicine beyond what Marta can make. No legal existence—if a company man shoots one of you tomorrow, there's no record that you were alive to shoot. I've spent five years being owned by a piece of paper. I know what it does to a person. I can't wish that on a hundred and sixty people to keep them pretty."

Sena studied her. "You understand that telling the tribunal is also a risk. To us. If it fails—if they rule for Halvorsen anyway—we'll have traded invisible for marked."

"Yes."

"And you're asking us to spend our invisibility on your testimony."

"I'm not asking," Freya said. "Elias and I will walk out of here either way and take our chances. The book is yours to decide about. It was written on your land, about your land. I just—" she exhaled, breath steaming, "—I think a lie only works while everyone agrees to carry it. Somebody has to put it down first. I already did. The question is whether you will."

Sena sat with that. Below them, the valley lay in its morning order: smoke from forty chimneys, the herd moving along the fence line, children racing down toward the ice pond with the shrieking confidence of the unobserved. Everything she loved, arranged in plain view of anyone who climbed high enough, protected for two decades by nothing but distance and silence.

Distance was dying. Aircraft had seen to that. Silence was dying too—had been dying since the day Team Nine's survey stakes went into the ground. The only question left was whether the valley would choose its own exposure or have it chosen.

"I have to go back in," Sena said, standing. "There's a deadline. The permits expire in nineteen days, and the tribunal sits in the capital, and runners on foot move slower than lawyers on trains."

"Nineteen days." Freya's jaw tightened. "On foot through the high country, that's twelve there if the weather holds. It won't hold."

"Then we'd better hold instead."

* * *

The council reconvened at noon, and this time her grandmother spoke early, which meant the argument had already been settled in the small knots and the fire was for ceremony.

Yeva Oduya told a story Sena had heard only once before, the night her mother died. About the year of the expulsion, when the company men came, and how a young surveyor—one of theirs, a mapmaker—had slipped away from his own crew and walked up the south trail in the dark to warn the homesteads. How he'd been caught doing it, and what happened to him, and how Torv the guide had buried him above the tree line with a stone cairn because no one else would touch the body of a company man. "We owe our existence to a traitor," Yeva said, in her level voice. "A man who put down the lie he was carrying. I have spent my life being grateful to him and ashamed that we never learned his name." She looked at Sena. "My granddaughter argues that history has sent us another one. I have read the pages the pilot copied. I believe her."

Harl held out longer—he wanted the strangers escorted out blindfolded, wanted the book copied and the original burned, wanted the valley to stay a rumor. But the vote, when Yeva called it, went seven to four, and Harl took it the way mountain men take weather: grimly, and without pretending it wasn't coming.

The terms were read aloud so all could witness. Runners would leave within the day—six of them, in three pairs on separate routes, carrying sworn testimony sealed in oilskin, copies of the field book's key pages, and Sena's own account dictated overnight and pressed with the council's seal. The original field book would travel with the strongest pair, by the highest route, because originals mattered to tribunals in a way copies never quite did. The strangers would be guided to the edge of the valley's known trails and no farther; after that, their road was their own. And the pass would be watched, from this day forward, from both ends.

"One more term," Yeva added, and here her voice changed, the smallest fracture running through it. "If the runners fail—if the tribunal rules for the company—then the valley closes. Fully. Anyone who knows the way in and is not of us will be turned back at the ridge, whatever they carry, whoever they are. Including pilots with kind eyes." She looked directly at Freya, who had been brought in to hear the terms. "Do you accept that?"

Freya met the old woman's gaze without flinching. "I accept. And I'll add something you didn't ask for. If it fails, I'll come back anyway—not to get in. To warn you they're coming, and then I'll disappear wherever you point me. You have my word on it."

"Words," said Harl.

"Are all anybody has," Yeva said, "until they're tested. We'll see whose hold."

* * *

They gathered at the south gate as the light went long and copper, the six runners pulling on snowshoes and taking up their packs—dried fish, bannock, the oilskin packets lashed tight against their hearts. Sena had argued her way onto the lead pair and lost, mostly because her grandmother pointed out, with devastating gentleness, that the valley needed its advocate alive and its speaker was not expendable twice. Instead Sena stood at the gate with the original field book in her hands, the one object in the world that could make her people real again, and waited for the man who would carry it.

His name was Dain Oduya, her grandmother's grand-nephew, twenty-four years old, built like the winter itself. He took the book from her with both hands, the way you take a child.

"Nineteen days," Sena said. "Twelve to the capital if the passes hold. The tribunal sits on the seventeenth. After that, the permits lapse and everything Halvorsen filed turns to ash—but only if someone puts our truth on the record first."

Dain nodded. Then, quietly: "My grandfather was on the south trail that night. The night your father went down to talk." He tapped the book against his chest. "This is for both of them."

He turned and led his partner up the switchbacks, and the other pairs followed at intervals, and Sena watched until the snow swallowed the last of them—small dark figures climbing into the enormous white silence, carrying a hundred and sixty lives in oilskin and memory.

Behind her, Freya stood at her shoulder. Neither of them spoke for a while.

"They'll make it," Freya said finally. "People like that usually do."

"People like that," Sena said, "have never done it before."

Far to the north, past the ridgeline, a sound reached them—faint, wrong, mechanical. A drone of engines, thin with distance but unmistakable, moving along the valley's outer wall. Not passing over. Circling. Searching.

Freya went very still. "That's a patrol aircraft," she said. "Company markings. And it's flying a grid pattern—which means Wren didn't lose me." She turned to Sena, and Sena saw in her face the exact expression she'd described at the saddle: not measuring, but grieving, already, for what was coming. "He knows I found something. Which means the valley has maybe two days before men on foot reach the ridge."

The engines faded northward. The runners were an hour gone, committed to their routes, and the snow had already filled their tracks.

Sena looked at the empty trail, then at the woman beside her, and understood that the fragile thing they had built at the fire that morning was about to be tested far sooner than any of them had voted for.

"Get your boots on," she said. "Both of us. We're going to need every hour they just took away."
