## 12. Company Men on the Ridge

The dog came first.

Sena spotted it from the watchtower at dawn—a dark speck working the scree below the eastern ridge, nose down, methodical as a stitch through cloth. She didn't say anything for a long moment. She just went still in a way I'd learned to read, the way you go still when a needle jumps on an instrument panel and you're waiting to see if it's the gauge or the engine.

"Dog," she said finally. "Shepherd cross. They use them out of Halvorsen's southern camps."

I climbed the tower ladder beside her and looked where she was looking. The morning light came flat and gray off the glacier, and the speck resolved into movement, purpose. Behind it, small with distance but unmistakable, came men. Four of them, maybe five, strung out along the moraine crest like beads on a wire.

"They found the high route," I said.

"No." Sena lowered her field glasses. "They found *where* the high route goes. That's worse."

Elias was already awake. He'd slept badly—I'd heard him turning in the bunk beneath mine most of the night, the field book wrapped in oilcloth under his coat even in his sleep, one hand on it like a man holding a wound closed. Now he stood in the doorway of the longhouse with his hair uncombed and his eyes too clear, that fever-brightness gone and replaced by something harder.

"How long?" he asked.

"Half a day," Sena said. "Less if they push."

We'd known this was coming. We'd talked about it around the council fire two nights ago, in the careful way people talk about a thing they've already decided. The valley had survived twenty years by not being seen, and now it had three outsiders inside it carrying documents that could end Halvorsen's concessions—or start a war, depending on which way the company chose to read them. Wren wouldn't send lawyers first. He'd send men who knew how to make problems walk into crevasses.

The elders had argued until midnight. Some wanted us gone before the trackers arrived—take the book, take the risk, take it away from their children. Old Marta, whose face was a map of wrinkles and whose voice carried like a bell across the meeting hall, said: "Twenty years ago they came for us and we ran. If we run again, we will always be running, and my grandchildren will inherit nothing but the running." It was Sena who settled it, though. Sena, whose father's name was spoken once, quietly, and then not again.

"They can't find what they can't follow," she'd said. "So we give them something to follow."

* * *

Now, standing in the cold on the watchtower platform, I watched her lay it out with sticks and stones on the planking at our feet, quick and precise, a cartographer's daughter even if she'd never held a theodolite.

"The eastern moraine," she said, tapping a ridge of stones. "It's rotten. Meltwater underneath all summer—it hums when you walk it, did you know that? The whole slope is a drum with no skin. Kettering will know it too, if he's any good, but he'll follow us onto it anyway if what he's following looks like what he wants."

"And what does he want?"

"A fire." She looked up at me. "You said it yourself—the camp you found, Team Nine's camp, everything left behind neat as a museum. Company men think in company terms. A fire means panic. Panic means someone dropped something. And dropped things can be picked up."

Elias crouched beside us. He'd been quiet since the council, quieter than usual, and when he spoke his voice had the flat steadiness of a man reading coordinates. "The pages stay with me. High route, west fork, over the shoulder of the peak. Two days to the river, three to the telegraph at Fort Meridian if the weather holds."

"If the weather holds," I said.

"It won't," Elias said. "But I'll hold faster."

I wanted to argue. I wanted to say let me carry it, I'm the pilot, I'm used to betting my life on machinery and weather and losing gracefully. But I'd seen him move since the fever broke—careful, deliberate, testing each foothold like a man who had already fallen once off a mountain and walked out anyway. He'd walked off a glacier with a sewn book and a broken mind and made it to a trapper's cabin. He could do this.

"You'll take the rifle," Sena said to me. Not a question.

"I don't—"

"You flew supply runs into country full of men who shoot at planes. Don't tell me you've never touched one."

She was right, which was annoying. Ilsa had taught me a scattergun for bears, and I'd graduated to a bolt-action years back after a fuel stop where a drunk claim-jumper decided my cargo hold looked like his property. I knew which end was which. I took the rifle from the rack inside the tower and checked it the way I checked my magneto switches—by feel, without looking, because looking meant admitting how much my hands wanted to shake.

Sena watched me do it and something passed over her face—not approval exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone confirming that the person next to her would hold.

"One more thing," she said. "Kettering. If it comes down to him and you, close, in the rocks—" She stopped. Started again. "My father died because a company man followed orders and told himself it was mercy. Don't be a company man."

"I don't plan on being close to anyone," I said. "That's the whole design."

* * *

We built the decoy camp in the hollow below the eastern moraine while the light was still low enough to hide our movements. Sena's people were good at this—twenty years of erasing themselves from the landscape had made the whole valley fluent in the grammar of concealment—and they built a lie the way other people built fences, fast and straight and load-bearing.

A fire ring, blackened with old ash hauled up from a hearth in the village so it would smell of days, not hours. A canvas lean-to pitched sloppy, the way panicked people pitch things. A tin cup. A torn scrap of chart paper weighted under a stone—Elias copied a section of the survey by hand that morning, real coordinates, real contour lines, the kind of thing a tracker would kneel down for. And in the ash of the fire pit, half-buried where a boot heel might have kicked it, a button off a company-issue parka. Sena had a jar of them. Trophies, she called them, and didn't explain further, and I didn't ask.

The fire itself we lit late, when the trackers' smoke signals—yes, smoke signals, Kettering was operating like it was a previous century and maybe for him it was—showed them closing on the ridge line. Green boughs on hot coals, wet moss, the works. A column of white smoke standing up against the gray sky like a flag saying HERE, HERE, COME AND SEE.

Then we ran.

Not all together—that was the point. Elias went west immediately, up into the trees with the field book against his spine and a week of food in a pack Sena's brother had packed for him, dried fish and birch-bark bread and a block of something dense and sweet that the valley made from berries I couldn't name. He paused at the treeline only once, to look back. I raised a hand. He didn't raise his. He just nodded, once, the way you nod to a copilot when you both know the checklist is done and there's nothing left but the flying.

Then Sena and I went east, toward the smoke, toward the moraine, toward the men coming to kill the truth.

* * *

They were better than I expected. That's the thing nobody tells you about company security men—they're not thugs, mostly. They're professionals. Voss Kettering's team came down off the ridge in a staggered line, spaced like men who'd drilled spacing, rifles slung, moving quiet. And Kettering himself—I saw him through the scope at four hundred meters, a big man moving with a strange economy, no wasted motion, head up, reading the ground the way I read sky. Reading it and not liking it.

He stopped at the edge of the moraine. Stood there a long time. I watched him crouch, pull a glove off, press his bare hand flat against the rock.

"He knows," I breathed.

"He suspects," Sena whispered beside me. "Suspecting and knowing are different animals. Watch."

Because here was the thing about the moraine, the thing Sena's people had known for twenty years and used twice before, and the thing I understood in my bones because it was the same physics that killed pilots: unstable ground doesn't announce itself. It lets you commit. It lets you get your weight fully onto the bad decision before it moves. A rockfall isn't an ambush. It's just gravity, finally getting its paperwork in order.

Kettering waved two of his men wide, along the stable flank. Smart. But he sent the other two straight across—the shortcut, the obvious line to the smoking camp—and they went, because the smoke was right there and the day was cold and shortcuts are how professionals die.

The first man was thirty meters onto the moraine when the ground spoke.

It wasn't loud, at first. A grinding, deep down, like a stomach. Then a section of slope the size of a barn door simply let go, and the sound arrived all at once—a roar and a crack and the shriek of stone on stone—and the two men went with it, sliding, rolling, swallowed in the dust cloud that boiled up white and enormous. The shepherd cross bolted, ears flat, and vanished downslope. Even from our position two hundred meters off, the dust hit us like weather, gritting between my teeth, stinging my eyes.

"Sena—" My hand found her arm. "The men—"

"Watch."

The dust thinned. One figure stood up, coughing, alive, clinging to a boulder the size of a stove. The second—

The second didn't stand up.

For a long moment nobody moved anywhere on that mountainside. Then Kettering's voice carried across the rock, flat and hard, calling names. The survivor answered. The silence where the other name should have been answered too, in its own way.

Kettering got his man off the slope. I watched them regroup on the stable flank, and I watched Kettering look at the decoy camp, at the smoke still rising, at the torn chart paper his surviving man retrieved and handed over. I watched him read it. And then I watched him do the last thing I expected.

He laughed.

Short, bitter, aimed at nobody. He crumpled the paper, stuffed it in his pocket, and turned to face—not us, he couldn't see us, but the general direction of the valley, the general direction of whoever had built this lie—and he shouted one word across the distance.

"Clever!"

Then he gestured his men downslope, away, and they went, carrying their dead man's rifle and whatever else they carried, and the mountain settled slowly back into its long silence.

* * *

We didn't speak until we were well clear, down in the trees, moving along a game trail Sena knew by heart. My legs were shaking. Not fear exactly—or not only fear. It was the specific tremor of having watched a man die and felt relief instead of horror, and knowing what that relief said about me.

"He might have lived," I said. "The one who went under. If they dig—"

"They won't dig. Not today. The moraine's still moving." Sena walked ahead of me, sure-footed, not turning around. "And tomorrow it will be a grave with no marker, and the company will write it up as a climbing accident, because that is what companies do with their dead. They file them."

"That doesn't make it right."

"No," she said. "It makes it theirs. We gave them a choice of routes. They chose."

I thought about that the rest of the descent. About choices and routes. About Wren in his warm office offering me debt restructuring like a man offering a drink, and about the surveyors walking into the mountains because orders said walk, and about Ruth Okonkwo, whom I'd never met, refusing to falsify a map and paying whatever price she'd paid. Everyone choosing their route. The mountain just keeping score.

By late afternoon we reached the relay point—a granite shelf above the west fork where Sena's cousin waited with a signal mirror. The plan was simple: at dusk, three flashes meant Elias had made the fork clean. Two meant trouble. One meant—

Nobody said what one meant.

We waited through the long gold slide of the afternoon, Sena sitting perfectly still the way she did, me pacing, then forcing myself to sit, then pacing again. The rifle lay across my knees. Below us the valley did its evening work—smoke from cookfires, the complaint of goats, a child's voice lifted in something that wasn't quite a song. Twenty years of ordinary life, balanced on whether a feverish cartographer could beat the weather over a shoulder of rock.

Dusk came down blue and cold. The signal mirror caught the last light.

Three flashes.

Sena exhaled, and for the first time since dawn her shoulders came down from around her ears. "He's through the fork."

"Then he's got two days to the river."

"Two days," she agreed. "And we have a different problem."

She pointed east, back the way Kettering's team had withdrawn. I followed her finger and saw it: not smoke, not movement. Light. A single electric-white flicker far off on the ridge line, regular as a lighthouse. Then again. Then again.

"A heliograph?" I said. "Who are they signaling? There's no one out there."

"There's someone," Sena said quietly. "There's a second team. There was always going to be a second team—Wren didn't get where he is by betting everything on one throw." She stood, and pulled me up by the wrist, and her grip was iron. "The trackers were the hammer. Whatever's coming now is the anvil. And it's coming from the direction of your airfield, Freya. From the direction of home."

The light on the ridge blinked again—three short, one long—and somewhere beyond the darkening peaks, engines I couldn't yet hear were already turning over in the cold.
