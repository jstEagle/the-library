## 13. Kettering's Offer

They took her rifle first, then her boots.

The boots bothered her more than the rifle. A person could improvise a weapon out of almost anything in the north—a tent pole, a pot of boiling water, a well-aimed chunk of granite—but nobody walked out of a valley in November without boots. Whoever had taken hers knew the country. That was the worst part of it: they hadn't tied her up like fools or left her to freeze like amateurs. They'd simply made leaving impossible and waited for the cold to do their negotiating for them.

Freya sat on a spruce bough inside a canvas wall tent that smelled of woodsmoke and wet dog, her stockinged feet tucked under her, watching the man across the fire. Voss Kettering. She'd heard the name whispered at company airfields for years—Halvorsen's best tracker, the one they sent when a claim jumper needed finding or a strike organizer needed discouraging. She'd built him in her head as something between a wolf and a machine.

He was neither. He was a broad, weathered man in his forties with a grey-shot beard and hands that moved with the unhurried economy of someone who'd spent thirty years doing small necessary things in cold places. Right now those hands were feeding split birch into a sheet-iron stove, one piece at a time, as if the fire were a debt he intended to pay slowly and in full.

Outside, two of his men circled the camp on snowshoes, their lamps swinging. The decoy fire she and Sena had lit on the far ridge had done its work drawing them onto the moraine; the collapse of the ice bridge under the second man had done the rest. One broken ankle, one very angry tracker, and Freya face-down in a drift with a knee in her back before she'd gotten three hundred yards toward the high route where Elias had already vanished with the field book sewn into his coat.

Sena was gone. Kettering's men had come back empty-handed from the direction she'd fled, and Kettering had said only one word about it—"good"—in a tone Freya was still trying to decode.

"You'll want these back eventually." He nodded at her boots, drying upside down on pegs near the stove. "Frostbite's no way to lose toes. I've seen it."

"Kind of you."

"It's practical." He set another log in. "Dead witnesses are loud. Frozen ones are louder. You'd be surprised how much paperwork a corpse generates."

"That's supposed to reassure me?"

"No." He looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were pale and level, the eyes of a man who had spent so long reading sign that he now read people the same way—tracks, age, direction, intent. "It's supposed to be honest. I find honesty works better than reassurance. Reassurance is what Wren sells."

The name landed between them like a stone dropped down a well. Freya kept her face still. Somewhere beyond the tent walls the wind moved through the valley's edge, and beneath it, faintly, she could hear the valley itself—the low bell of a goat, a door, a child's voice quickly hushed. They'd pulled back from this camp when the trackers came, Sena had told her, the whole settlement folding itself into the timber like it had practiced. Which, she realized now, it probably had. Twenty years of practice.

Kettering poured coffee from a dented pot into a tin cup and held it out. She didn't take it.

"There's nothing in it," he said. "If I wanted to drug you I'd have done it while you were unconscious. You snore, by the way."

"I don't snore."

"Like a two-stroke with a bad bearing." He set the cup on a crate within her reach and went back to the stove. "Drink it or don't. Cold night either way."

She waited until he'd turned away, then took the cup. It was good coffee, which annoyed her more than bad coffee would have.

* * *

For an hour he didn't talk, and neither did she. He mended a snowshoe binding with sinew and an awl, working by lamplight, and she catalogued everything: the tent, the gear, the two men outside, the layout of the camp, the bearing of the high route, the hours until dawn. Old habit. Ilsa had drilled it into her before Freya could legally solo—*know your exits even when you're staying put, girl, especially when you're staying put.*

"Your mentor," Kettering said, without looking up from the binding. "Ilsa Vang. Flew the Kestrel route back in '04, '05. Before your time."

Freya's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "You know Ilsa?"

"I did contract work for Halvorsen then too. Different hat, same wolf." He pulled the sinew tight with his teeth. "She flew supplies to a survey crew up the Sorel drainage. Crew found something. Company decided the something didn't exist. Her job was to fly the falsified maps out and the real ones never came back down." He glanced at her. "Sound familiar?"

"You're fishing."

"I'm making conversation. It's been a long day and one of my men has a compound fracture and I've run out of things to say to him." He tied off the binding, tested it, seemed satisfied. "But yes. I'm also fishing. You came over that ridge with a half-dead cartographer and a stolen field book, and you burned a decoy fire like you'd studied for the exam. That's not a thief's work. Thieves run light and fast. You ran heavy and slow because you were carrying something you thought mattered more than your skin. I wanted to know who taught you to think that way."

"And if it was Ilsa?"

"Then the company owes that woman a second apology, and won't pay the first one." He set the snowshoe down. "Which brings us to business."

"Is that what this is?"

"Everything's business. That's the first thing Halvorsen teaches you and the last thing anyone believes." He reached into his coat—not fast, deliberately slow enough that she saw every inch of the motion—and drew out a folded sheaf of paper, soft-cornered, much handled. "You know what this is?"

She knew. The shape of it, the weight, the way the pages fanned. She'd spent a night in Ilsa's hangar copying key pages from a book just like it.

"Where did you get that."

"Off Elias Brandt's body?" He let it hang, watched her go white, and then shook his head once, almost gently. "No. Off the copy he was carrying. He's fine—or as fine as a man can be crossing the saddle in November. We found the decoy cache your friend staged in the talus. Empty ration tins, a spare coat, a dummy bundle wrapped around river stones. Good work, honestly. My men bought it for six hours." He tapped the papers against his knee. "This is my own copy. I've been carrying it since the railhead."

"You're lying."

"Am I?" He unfolded the sheaf and held it toward the lamp, and she saw the cramped surveyor's hand, the coordinates, the margin notes in Ruth Okonkwo's voice—she recognized the style from the photographs she'd taken in the abandoned camp, the same disciplined slant, the same habit of underlining twice. And there, in the corner of the first page, a small inked stamp she knew intimately: *HALVORSEN MINING — SURVEY DIVISION — TEAM NINE.*

"The original went south with Brandt," Kettering said. "Or east, or wherever you sent him. This—" he weighed it in his palm, "—is the copy I made at the railhead while everyone was watching the telegraph office instead of me. Which is the only reason I'm sitting here talking instead of burning this camp and reporting you dead in a crevasse."

The fire ticked. Outside, a boot crunched past the tent wall and faded.

"Why," Freya said. It wasn't a question so much as a demand for the floor to stop moving.

"Because I read it." He said it simply, the way a man admits to a disease. "All of it. Okonkwo was thorough. Coordinates, census counts, the watchtowers, the school register—did you know they keep a school register? Thirty-one children, Freya. She counted the children." His jaw worked. "And then the orders. Dated, signed, routed through Wren's office. *Render the drainage unrecorded. Relocate survey personnel per contingency.* Contingency." He laughed, short and airless. "Fourteen years I've worked for these people. I told myself we did rough work but legal work. Claims, evictions, the occasional persuasion. The stuff the country runs on and pretends it doesn't. But this—" he folded the pages along their worn creases, "—this isn't rough work. This is erasing people. Whole families. And I carried the maps that buried them, some of them, years ago. Maybe I flew fuel to the crew that did it."

"So defect."

"With what?" For the first time his voice cracked open and she saw the man inside the tracker, and the man was tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix. "I have a wife with a bad heart in Copper Landing. A daughter at the territorial school who wants to be a doctor, and there's exactly one scholarship pipeline out of that town and it runs through Halvorsen money. My brother lost his leg in their Number Four adit and lives on a pension the company can cancel with a signature. If I stand up in that tribunal room tomorrow, every one of them is finished inside a month. Not fired. *Finished.* Wren doesn't fire people. He unwinds them." He looked at her, and there was no threat in it, only arithmetic. "You're free to call that cowardice. I've called it worse myself, most nights. But it's the truth of my ledger, and I've learned not to lie about ledgers. You of all people should understand a ledger."

Freya stared into her coffee. She did understand. She understood it so well it felt like being seen through—every choice she'd made since the day she'd signed for the de Havilland with Halvorsen's ink still wet on the lien.

"What do you want from me?" she asked.

"To give you back your boots," Kettering said, "and a warning."

* * *

He talked until the stove burned low. The warning had three parts, and he laid them out like a man marking a trail for someone who'd have to walk it in the dark.

First: the tribunal was not what the radio bulletins made it sound like. Three commissioners, one of them appointed on Halvorsen's recommendation, one of them old and vain and flattered easily, and only the third—Commissioner Adeyemi—actually interested in the truth. "Adeyemi will believe you," Kettering said. "Adeyemi believing you will accomplish nothing unless you can make the other two believe you in the same hour. Truth doesn't win tribunals. Weight wins tribunals. Bring weight."

Second: Wren had already filed. The falsified surveys were entered, certified, stamped, beautiful. "He's not going to argue with your evidence. He's going to argue with *you*. Expect him to produce your debt contract, your theft charge, the warrant naming you a kidnapper. He'll offer the room a simple story: a desperate debtor stole company property and recruited a madman to forge a conspiracy. It's a good story. People want it to be true, because if it's true, then nothing needs to change." He fed the stove its last log. "Your answer can't be innocence. Nobody at a tribunal is innocent. Your answer has to be *pattern*. Show them this happened before. Show them it's a system. Systems scare commissions. Individuals just get sentenced."

Third—and here he paused long enough that she thought he might not say it—he leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving his face into planes and shadow.

"Wren will offer you a deal. Tomorrow, tonight, at the parley, whenever he gets you alone. Full forgiveness. The plane, the debt, maybe even the charge, gone, in exchange for silence or for Brandt. He'll make it sound generous. It isn't. I've watched him make that offer eleven times in fourteen years. Nine took it. All nine are still on his books, and all nine hate themselves, and he uses the hatred like ballast." His eyes held hers. "One refused. A surveyor, eight years back, up the Corren. Refused Wren to his face, walked out of the parley clean. Died that winter in a climbing accident on a slope he'd climbed twenty times. I investigated it. I was ordered to close the file, and I closed it, and I have regretted closing it every single day since." He straightened. "So when you refuse him—and you will refuse him, I can see it sitting in you like a nail—don't refuse him alone. Make sure there are people standing next to you when you say it. Witnesses. Cameras if God grants them. Never be alone with Aldous Wren after you've cost him something. Promise me that much."

"Why do you care what happens to me?"

He was quiet a moment. Then he reached into his breast pocket again, and this time what he brought out was small and worn and personal: a photograph, creased white at the folds. A woman with a bad heart, laughing, arm around a gap-toothed girl holding up a fish nearly as long as herself.

"My daughter asked me last month what I actually do at work," he said. "Age nine, and she's already figured out that I never answer questions about work. And I gave her the company line, the one I've given for fourteen years—*we help people find what they're looking for.* And she looked at me with those clear eyes and said, *like the map people?*" He put the photograph away with great care. "I don't know what she meant. Maybe nothing. Maybe she'd heard the older kids talking. But I lay awake that night thinking about map people, and then your cartographer's book landed in front of me, and I read about thirty-one children who aren't on any map, and I understood that I have spent my whole career helping people *not* find what they're looking for." He shrugged, a big slow movement, like a mountain settling. "I can't fix fourteen years. I can't fix my ledger. But I can leave one pair of boots unguarded and one tent flap unlaced and one tracker who suddenly can't seem to locate a fugitive in broad daylight. That's what I care about. It isn't much. It's what I've got."

* * *

He woke her before first light, or perhaps she'd never quite slept—hard to say, in that country, in that season. The stove had gone to coals. He crouched beside her bedroll and set her boots down beside it, laces tucked in, dry and warm.

"Your friend Sena crossed the river at the falls, heading south-southwest, probably for the railhead. She moves well. Tell her the ford below the falls is undercut on the north bank—she'll want to cross above it." He stood. "My men will report that you overpowered me during the night and escaped. I'll have a bruise to support the story. Try to look dangerous."

Freya pulled the boots on, laced them with fingers that wouldn't quite steady. At the tent flap she stopped.

"The field book," she said. "Your copy. What happens to it?"

Kettering looked at her for a long moment, and then he took the worn sheaf from his coat and pressed it into her hands, closing her fingers over it with both of his, the way you'd hand someone something breakable.

"Weight," he said. "Bring weight."

Then he lifted the flap, and the cold came in like a blessing, and the eastern sky over the Kestrels had gone the color of a fresh scar, and somewhere south, past the frozen falls and the undercut bank and two hundred miles of country that officially did not exist, a tribunal was convening on lies—and Freya Dravenmoor stepped out into the blue dark with a stolen book against her ribs and a dead man's warning in her ears, running for the truth before the snows, before Wren, before the world finished deciding she was a criminal.
