## 14. Downriver, Out of Maps

The river took them the way a thief takes a purse—quickly, and without any intention of giving it back.

Freya had expected the current to be their enemy. Instead it carried them, the canvas canoe riding high and light under Sena's paddle strokes, the banks unspooling past in ribbons of spruce and grey scree. Elias had gone up the high route two days ago with the field book sewn back into his coat, and every hour since, Freya had been doing arithmetic she hated: distance against deadline, deadline against weather, weather against the speed of men with company rifles.

"You're counting again," Sena said from the bow. She didn't turn around. "I can hear it. It sounds like a clock that's wrong."

"I'm navigating."

"You're brooding with a map in your hands." Sena's paddle dipped, lifted, dripped. "There's a difference. Navigators look where they're going."

Freya looked where she was going. The river bent ahead into a canyon whose walls rose sheer and rust-colored, streaked with old water stains like tear tracks down a face. Nothing about it was on any chart she'd ever flown over. That was the whole point of it.

"Your people chose this route well," she admitted.

"My people didn't choose anything. That's rather the theme." Sena said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "My grandfather chose the valley. My father chose to go back for the herd when the company men came. Everyone else just got chosen *for*."

The canoe slid into the canyon's shadow and the temperature dropped ten degrees at once. Freya pulled her collar up and let the silence do its work. She had learned that about Sena in the two days since the watchtower: she talked when talking was useful and went quiet when quiet was honest, and she could tell the difference better than anyone Freya had ever met—including, especially, herself.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar below the canyon mouth, where the river widened and slowed and the mountains behind them caught the last light like coals raked over. Sena built a small fire because the cold demanded it and hid it with a screen of green boughs because caution demanded more.

"My father's name was Tomas," she said, abruptly, to the fire.

Freya looked up from the chart she'd been pretending to study.

"He went out on the third day of the expulsion. The others had already gone up-valley—he made them go, he carried the children who couldn't walk and sent his own wife ahead with them. Then he went back for the horses." Sena turned a stick in the flames. "The horses, Freya. Not gold, not the survey stakes, not revenge. Horses. Because my mother couldn't ride and the pass was too steep to walk with a child on your back, and he thought—" her voice did something complicated and then steadied— "he thought they'd take stock, not lives. He thought men who came to steal land would draw some line at killing a man for animals."

"What happened?"

"The company report says he fell into the river." She looked at Freya directly now. "The family story says three shots. I've had twenty years to decide which one I believe, and I'll tell you what I decided: both can be true if you write the report yourself."

Freya put the chart away. There was nothing on it she needed.

"I never knew my parents," she said. It came out flatter than she intended, so she tried again. "I mean—I knew them for six years. Plane crash, up near the coast. Bad weather, bad fuel gauge, bad luck stacked three deep. After that I went through foster homes the way water goes through gravel. And then Ilsa found me. She had a hangar and no patience and a rule that anybody who hung around long enough got taught something whether they wanted it or not."

"She gave you your wings."

"She gave me everything. Which is the problem." Freya fed a branch to the fire and watched it catch. "Because everything I have, I have through her—and then Halvorsen put a price tag on all of it. The plane's theirs. The license is mortgaged. Every hour I fly, I owe them more, and the only way out they ever offered was obedience. You know what that's like. A debt is just a leash you agreed to."

Sena was quiet a moment. "And Ilsa? Where does she stand on the leash?"

"On the other end of it, holding it out to me, telling me to cut it." Freya almost smiled. "She flew for them once. She helped bury a survey—the same kind of survey, the same kind of lie. Twenty years carrying it. When I told her what Wren wanted, she didn't argue. She just started pumping fuel."

"That's love," Sena said simply. "The kind that costs something."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upstream an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"Sena. When we get to the railhead—if we get there—what happens to you? Back to the valley?"

"If the tribunal recognizes us, the valley becomes a place. On maps. With a name and borders and tax collectors, probably." Her mouth curved. "If it doesn't, the valley becomes a memory, and memories don't hold title. Either way I go home. But one of those ways, I go home to something that can't be taken twice."

* * *

The second day the river narrowed and quickened, and talk became a luxury they couldn't afford. Sena read the water like text—here a submerged shelf, there a sweeper leaning out from the bank hungry for canoes—and called the lines in a low, even voice while Freya paddled until her shoulders burned. Twice they portaged around rapids, hauling the canoe over ground no cartographer had ever troubled to record, and both times Freya thought of Elias somewhere above them, crossing the same country blind, on fever-memory and nerve.

At midday they eddied out beneath a cliff face where someone had carved marks into the stone—old marks, lichen-filled, a tally of some kind running thirty feet along the base of the rock.

"Counting what?" Freya asked.

"Herds. Seasons. Maybe just proof." Sena traced one groove with a gloved finger. "We've always written things down where the company wouldn't think to look. My grandmother kept the valley's births and deaths inside a hymnal, in the margins, in a cipher. The company searched our cabins twice and never opened the hymnal. Why would they? Who hides a census in a songbook?" She glanced sideways at Freya. "You hide things in plain sight too. I watched you copy that field book. You wrote your copies as fuel calculations."

"It worked."

"It worked because nobody reads a woman's arithmetic." Sena said it without anger, but Freya felt it land anyway, somewhere under the ribs. "That's the whole architecture of it, isn't it? The permits, the falsified maps, the debt. It all rests on deciding whose counts and whose doesn't. Whose surveys are real. Whose dead are 'fell into the river.'"

Freya didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't an indictment, and Sena deserved better than agreement bought cheap.

* * *

The third night the temperature fell hard enough that the canoe's canvas stiffened and the river began growing teeth along its edges—ice forming first in the slack water near the banks, thin plates of it clicking together in the current like distant applause. Winter was coming down out of the north on schedule, and somewhere south of them a tribunal was convening on schedule, and between those two schedules were forty miles of river and one railhead.

They lay side by side by the fire, backs to the same log, sharing warmth the way travelers do and then some, and neither of them moved away.

"Tell me something true," Sena said. "Not mission-true. Real-true."

Freya stared at the sparks climbing. "I'm terrified. Not of Wren, not of the tribunal. Of after. My whole life has been a debt—a direction, a number I owed. If the charter dies, the debt dies, and then I'm just... a woman with a skill and no chain. I don't know how to be free. Nobody ever taught me that course."

Sena was silent long enough that Freya thought she'd fallen asleep. Then: "When I was twelve, after my father, I stopped speaking for most of a year. My mother thought I'd broken. I hadn't. I was practicing. I was deciding exactly which words I would spend the rest of my life saying, so I'd never waste one on a lie." She shifted, her shoulder warm against Freya's. "I've spent three of them on you already, in my head. None of them were lies."

Freya's heart did something unscheduled. "What kind of words?"

"The kind you don't say yet. The kind you earn." Sena's voice was steady, but her hand found Freya's sleeve and stayed there. "We're forged right now, you and I. Cold does that. Purpose does that. Whatever this is—it deserves to be said in spring, in daylight, by people who exist on official maps. Not whispered on a gravel bar by two ghosts."

"That may be the most romantic thing anyone's ever threatened me with."

"I don't threaten." Sena's laugh was soft and brief. "I testify."

* * *

They reached the railhead country at dusk on the fourth day, where the river flattened and the trees gave way to cut stumps and the first rude shacks of the timber camps. Smoke stood straight up from chimneys in the still air. Somewhere beyond the low ridge, a train whistle sounded—one long note, mournful and mechanical, the sound of the world they were re-entering.

Sena beached the canoe in the reeds and they crouched in the shallows, looking at the lights of the town strung along the far bank like a necklace someone had dropped.

"No telegraph traffic on the line all day," Freya murmured. "I don't like it. Wren's not quiet by nature. Quiet means he's already bought everything worth buying."

"Then we don't walk in the front."

"We walk in separate. You stay with the canoe until I find Elias and see the board. If the station's clean, I signal from the water tower. If it's not—"

"If it's not, you come back down this bank and we become ghosts again." Sena gripped her arm. "Freya. Whatever's in there—whatever he offers you—we already know what you're worth. Don't let him set the price."

Freya held her eyes for a moment longer than navigation required. Then she squeezed her hand once, climbed the bank, and walked toward the lights—toward the telegraph office with its windows glowing yellow, and the constable standing outside it who was very carefully not looking at her, and the station platform where two company men were walking a handcuffed figure toward the freight shed.

A figure with a familiar coat.

Elias.
