## 15. The Railhead Trap

The railhead announced itself before we saw it: a smudge of coal smoke standing straight up in the cold air, the way smoke does when there's a town under it. Sena spotted it first from the ridge above the river, and she stopped walking so abruptly that I nearly ran into her back.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's Corben's Landing." I'd flown over it a hundred times—fuel stops, mail drops, the kind of town that existed because the railway did and would die when the railway moved on. From the air it was nothing. A curve of track along the frozen river, a water tower, forty roofs, one long warehouse with HALVORSEN FREIGHT painted on its side in letters you could read from two thousand feet. "Population maybe three hundred. One hotel. One constable."

"And Wren?"

I had been asking myself the same question for two days, ever since the telegram office in the last settlement before the river country, where I'd wired ahead under a false name to check the tribunal schedule and felt the clerk's eyes linger on my face a half-second too long. Maybe nothing. Maybe I was tired and paranoid and reading meaning into a bored man's glance. But the wire I'd sent had been answered by return wire within the hour—which meant someone at the other end was sitting on the line waiting—and the answer had confirmed the tribunal date with a courtesy so elaborate it read like an apology delivered in advance.

"Wren doesn't chase," I said. "He waits. He owns every door between here and the capital, and he just stands behind one of them until you walk through."

Sena looked at the town the way her people must have looked at survey stakes coming up out of the ground twenty years ago—the way you look at a thing that means the end of a world or the beginning of one, depending on who holds the hammer. Then she shouldered her pack higher and started down.

* * *

We came into Corben's Landing from the north, off the ice road, past stacked cordwood and a dog team chained to a sled that raised its head and decided we weren't worth barking at. The town smelled of coal and horse and frying fat. Ordinary sounds. A man splitting wood. Two kids throwing a frozen rag back and forth. Somewhere a piano being badly played through an open saloon door.

Elias walked between us with his collar up and the field book flat against his chest under his coat, where it had lived since the valley, sewn back into place by Sena's own hand with sinew thread. He'd rebuilt the missing pages from memory on the train south—or what passed for a train, a work engine running supplies up the line—and his handwriting had gone small and precise again, the cartographer reasserting himself over the fever case. He looked like a man now instead of a ghost. That was the trouble. Ghosts don't get recognized.

"Telegraph office first," I said. "I want to see who's coming and going on the wire."

"You think they're watching the wire?"

"I think Wren bought this town before we left the valley." I didn't know it yet. I want to be honest about that—I *guessed* it, the way you guess weather by the ache in an old injury. What I knew for certain came later, and it came all at once.

The telegraph office sat across from the depot, a narrow building with frost ferns climbing the inside of its windows. Through the glass I could see the operator at his key, and beside him a second chair, and in the second chair a man wearing a good wool coat that no railway clerk in Corben's Landing could afford, doing nothing at all except listening to the instrument chatter and watching the street.

I turned us around before Sena could ask. We took the long way, past the hotel, past the constable's office—where a second man in a good coat stood on the steps smoking, unhurried, positioned exactly where he could see both the platform and the road in from the ice. Company men don't stand like trappers or like soldiers. They stand like furniture placed by a decorator. Once you've seen it you can't unsee it.

"They're not looking for three people," Elias said quietly. We'd stopped in the lee of the freight warehouse, among crates stenciled with mining numbers. "They're watching the station. Which means they know we need the train."

"Everyone needs the train. It's the only thing leaving."

"Then they don't have to find us. They just have to wait for us to come to them." He said it without drama, the way he said everything now, and something in the flatness of it made my chest hurt. On the glacier he'd been half-mad and I'd carried him. In the valley he'd been a witness. Here he was cargo again—precious cargo, the field book and the memory inside him—and cargo gets seized.

"We go tonight," Sena said. "There'll be a dark window between the last freight and the morning passenger. We get on the platform, we stay against the buildings, we're three shapes in the dark."

"And if they've got a lamp on the platform?"

She looked at me. "Then we fight."

Neither of them asked me if I had a better plan. That was its own kind of information. The plan was thin and we all knew it, and we were going to do it anyway, because the tribunal convened in thirty-six hours and the falsified maps were already filed and the only things in the world that could beat them were riding against Elias's ribs.

* * *

We waited out the daylight hours in the loft of a livery stable, paying the hostler too much for hay and silence, lying in the dark among the smell of horses and leather while the town went about its business below us. I slept in pieces. Between the pieces I kept seeing the camp in the Kestrel Range—the stove still warm, the logbook open to PASSAGE REAL—and I kept seeing Aldous Wren's face as it had been in his office, pleasant and patient, telling me how debts could be restructured. He had never once raised his voice at me. He had never needed to. Every terrible thing Halvorsen had done in these mountains had been done in the tone of a man discussing freight rates.

Near midnight the wind shifted and brought the sound of the yard: couplings clanking, a switch engine breathing hard, the last freight pulling out south with a long moan that faded down the river valley. After that, quiet. The kind of quiet towns make when they've spent everything they had for the day.

We crossed the yard single file, Sena leading, hugging the shadow of the water tower's legs. Frost squeaked under our boots. The passenger train stood at the platform, steam venting in slow white ghosts from its joints, one carriage lamp burning yellow behind frosted glass. Three hundred yards of open ground between the warehouse wall and the nearest door.

We were halfway across when the lamps came on.

Not one lamp—all of them, along the platform eave and above the depot doors, four electric bulbs flaring white in the cold, and the whole yard stood revealed like a stage set with us on it, mid-stride, nowhere to hide. And at the top of the depot steps, framed in the light, hands folded in front of him, stood a man in a long dark coat.

Aldous Wren.

"Miss Dravenmoor," he called, and his voice carried across the frozen yard without effort, without anger, in that freight-rate tone. "You look cold. Come inside where it's warm."

Beside me, Elias went very still. I felt Sena shift her weight onto her back foot, the hunter's movement, calculating distance and cover and finding none of either worth the arithmetic.

"Elias," I said, barely a breath. "Run for the train. Now."

"I'm not leaving—"

"*Now.*"

He ran. To this day I don't know if it was courage or obedience or simply that his body obeyed the urgency in my voice before his mind could argue. He got ten steps toward the carriage door before two men detached themselves from the shadows beneath the platform—one from each side, no hurry at all, the way you close a gate on a steer that has nowhere else to go—and took him by the arms. He fought. I'll say that for him; the fever had burned the softness out of him and he fought like a man who knew exactly what was in his coat. For about six seconds. Then a third man came from behind and put him face-down on the boards, and another knelt and cut the coat open with a knife, careful as a surgeon, and lifted out the field book and held it up to the light like a fisherman displaying a trout.

"No!" Elias's voice cracked across the yard, raw and furious. "Freya, the book—it's the book—"

"I can see it, Elias."

Wren descended the steps at his own pace, took the field book, turned it over once in his gloved hands, and opened it to a random page as though checking a ledger entry. Whatever he saw seemed to satisfy him. He closed it.

"The original," he said, almost warmly. "And the copies you made, I assume, are somewhere I'll never find, which is why I'm pleased to conduct this conversation civilly." He looked past me at Sena, and for a moment the pleasantness thinned and I saw the assessor underneath, the man who priced things. "You must be the valley's representative. Miss Dravenmoor has cost me a great deal of money on your account."

"She's cost you the truth," Sena said. "That's different from money."

"Is it?" Wren smiled faintly. "In my experience the exchange rate is quite favorable."

Then he turned back to me, and the smile stayed but the warmth drained out of it, and what remained was worse than any threat he could have made.

"Here is the situation as I understand it," he said. "My men are holding your cartographer and my property. The morning train arrives in five hours. The tribunal convenes tomorrow at ten. Without the book, your testimony is the word of a debt-ridden pilot with a theft charge pending against her, against the sworn surveys of a licensed engineering firm. You understand how that arithmetic works. You've seen it work your whole life." He glanced at Elias, kneeling on the boards between two men, breath steaming, watching me with an expression I couldn't bear. "He, similarly, is a junior employee who deserted his post and suffered a mental episode. Tragic. There are doctors in the capital who will confirm whatever needs confirming."

"And if I walk away?" My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Me and her, right now, into the dark. You've got no charge that sticks to Sena. Let us go and testify anyway."

"Testify to what? Photographs of an empty camp?" He shook his head gently, a teacher correcting a promising student. "No, Miss Dravenmoor. You won't walk away. Not because I'd stop you—I've no interest in chasing women through snowdrifts—but because you won't leave him. I've watched you since the Kestrel Range. You collect people. It's your defining flaw." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice, and for a moment it was just the two of us in the noise of the venting steam. "So here is my offer, and I'll make it once. The book stays with me. The cartographer goes home to his mother with a medical discharge and a sealed record—he'll be a footnote, harmless, and frankly happier for it. In exchange, you come with me tonight. You sign a new contract—full forgiveness of principal and interest, a clean license, even the patrol route you always wanted. And you tell the tribunal tomorrow that you found the camp abandoned, filed your report accurately, and have no knowledge of any populated valley. That's all. One witness, recanted. The maps stand, the permits renew, everyone lives."

"And Team Nine?"

Something flickered behind his eyes—not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt. Calculation, interrupted. "Team Nine is a question that no longer exists," he said.

Behind him, Elias was on his feet now, held by the arms, and he was shaking his head at me, slowly, deliberately, *no, Freya, don't*, and Sena had moved up beside me close enough that our shoulders touched, and she said, very low, in the voice she used for decisions rather than feelings:

"He will take the deal and bury us all anyway. Men like this keep no bargains. My father learned that."

"I know," I said.

But I also knew the other thing, the thing Wren had counted on, the thing that had been true since a fevered man whispered coordinates at me in a trapper's cabin: I could not stand in this yard and watch them carry Elias away and the book with him, and board a train tomorrow with nothing but my word against a corporation's paper. If I ran, I saved myself and lost everything else. If I traded, I might lose everything including myself—but I'd be standing in the room where it happened, and Ilsa had taught me that the pilot's job is to be present at the controls when the weather turns, not to have landed safe beforehand.

"Send him to the train," I said. "Unharmed, on the morning passenger, with his discharge papers in his pocket. I'll come with you now. Alone."

"Freya—" Elias's voice broke.

"Get on the train, Elias. Rebuild your pages again if you have to. But live." I looked at Wren. "That's my price. Take it or spend all night negotiating with a woman who has nothing left to lose, which I promise you is more expensive."

For a long moment Wren studied me, and I had the sense of a man weighing freight, running his thumb down a manifest, finding the numbers acceptable. Then he nodded once, and gestured, and the men holding Elias loosened their grip.

"Put him in the waiting room," Wren said. "He departs at dawn." He turned back to me, and extended his hand as though concluding a fuel purchase. "Welcome back to the company, Miss Dravenmoor."

I didn't take his hand. I looked past him instead—at the dark southern end of the yard, where the rails ran away toward the capital, and at the ridge beyond the town where, hours ago, I'd thought I saw a light blink twice and go dark, and told myself I'd imagined it.

Because I hadn't imagined it. And as Wren's men walked me toward the depot door, I heard it: far off down the valley, thin and high and unmistakable, an aircraft engine turning over in the cold.

Someone had flown all night to reach this railhead.

And I would have known that engine sound anywhere in the world.
