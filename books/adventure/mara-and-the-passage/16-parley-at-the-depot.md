## 16. Parley at the Depot

The telegraph office smelled of coal smoke and old varnish, and it was the most dangerous room in the territory.

Freya knew that before she crossed the threshold. She'd spent the walk from the rail yard cataloguing exits—two windows painted over from the inside, one door behind her now closing on Wren's man's shoulder, a stovepipe flue too narrow for anything but regret. The constable who'd let her in hadn't met her eyes. Bought men never did. They looked at your hands instead, waiting for you to make them into a problem.

Aldous Wren sat behind the operator's desk like he'd been born to it. He'd shed his field coat somewhere between the range and this frozen railhead; what remained was wool and precision, a man pressed and buttoned against the cold as if cold were a rumor he refused to confirm. His gloves were folded on the blotter. His hands were bare.

"Dravenmoor." He didn't stand. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy."

"There is no other guy. That's rather the point of you."

She kept her feet. There was a chair across from him and she wasn't going to give it the satisfaction. Through the frosted glass of the inner door she could see the shapes of two more of his men, patient as fence posts. Somewhere out in the yard, Elias Brandt was sitting in a locked freight office with the field book in a canvas satchel at his feet, and every minute she stood here trading words was a minute closer to both of them being on a northbound company flight by morning.

"You took him off a public platform," she said.

"I took custody of a company employee suffering a mental break, in the presence of a local constable who agrees with my assessment." Wren's voice was mild, almost kind, which was how she'd always known to be afraid of it. "The boy's been delirious since the glacier. Half the town heard him raving about passages and valleys. It's a mercy, really, what we're doing for him."

"He's not raving anymore. He's remembering."

"Memory is such an unreliable instrument." Wren turned his bare hands palm-up on the desk, a small gesture of openness that fooled no one, least of all himself. "But then, you know that better than anyone. You've been flying on memory for weeks, haven't you? Memory of a camp you had no business photographing. Memory of pages you copied out of property that doesn't belong to you. Memory of a night departure that cost Halvorsen Mining eleven hundred gallons of aviation fuel and the use of a patrol aircraft."

"The patrol aircraft iced its carburetor," Freya said. "I heard."

Something moved at the corner of Wren's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment of the hit, filed away for later.

He opened the desk drawer and laid her contract on the blotter, squared to the desk's edge, the way he squared everything. Her name was at the top. Below it, the figure—the number that had lived in her chest for six years like a second heartbeat, the price of a plane and a dead season and a mentor's faith converted into compound interest.

"I'm authorized to tear this up," Wren said.

The stove ticked. Outside, a switch engine groaned through the yard, coupling cars with a sound like bones settling.

"Fully," he went on. "Not restructured. Forgiven. The Otter becomes yours, free and clear, title transferred before you're back at the airfield. And the theft charge—" he tapped the contract once, lightly, "—becomes a clerical error. Inventory miscounted. These things happen in remote operations. I'll write the letter myself."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, you hand me the copies you made of the Okonkwo field book, you sign a statement confirming Survey Team Nine was relocated under standard protocol, and you take Mr. Brandt home to his mother in Veldt Harbor, where he will spend a quiet convalescence being cared for by people who love him." Wren folded his hands. "And you go back to work. Medicine runs, mail, resupply. The work you're good at. The work you were meant for."

It was obscene how good it sounded. That was the thing about Wren she'd never learned to defend against—he didn't offer you corruption. He offered you your own life back, polished, with the ugly parts sanded down. Six years of debt gone. The Otter hers. Ilsa's hangar and the lake at dawn and the long blue corridors of the interior, all of it returned, all it would cost being the truth about eight missing people and a valley full of families who'd earned their existence the hard way.

"That's it," she said. "That's the whole offer."

"That's the whole offer."

"No clause about what happens to Elias after Veldt Harbor."

Wren's expression didn't change, but the room got colder anyway. "Mr. Brandt's care is a private matter between him and his family."

"His family will be very surprised to hear he has one. He told me once he was raised by an aunt who died when he was nineteen."

"Then perhaps I misspoke." Wren picked up a pen, unhurried, and held it out to her across the desk. "Miss Dravenmoor. You are a pilot, not a martyr. Martyrs require audiences, and I promise you there will be no audience. Whatever you think you know, whatever you've copied, whatever the boy remembers—it will be three voices against a corporation's records, and two of those voices will be recanting. Yours, because you'll have signed. His, because no one will hear him say otherwise."

She looked at the pen. Black lacquer, gold nib, the kind of pen that signed things that couldn't be unsigned.

*You came here to trade,* she reminded herself. *Remember what you came here to trade.*

Because that was the truth of why she stood in this room. Not courage. Arithmetic. The tribunal convened in four days in the capital, nine hundred miles south, and the last train south left this railhead tomorrow at dawn—if it left at all, with Wren owning the telegraph office and the constable and possibly the stationmaster too. Elias was in custody. The field book was in a satchel ten feet from Wren's men. Sena was hidden in the pines above the yard with a rifle and instructions to run south on foot if Freya didn't come out within the hour, and Freya had hated every word of those instructions, and Sena had taken them anyway because they'd run out of better ones.

There was only one currency in this room Wren wanted more than the copied pages.

"All right," Freya said.

Wren's pen paused mid-offer.

"But not the statement. I'm not signing that I saw nothing, because I did see something, and if I ever have to explain my signature to a judge I'd rather it be a lie about loyalty than a lie about facts. Here's what I'll do instead." She heard her own voice go flat and steady, the voice she used on short final in crosswinds. "I'll keep my mouth shut. At the tribunal, wherever, whenever—I say nothing. Team Nine stays relocated. The valley stays off the maps. You get your permits renewed and your falsified surveys stand, and I fly medicine runs until my hands give out, and neither of us ever mentions the Kestrel Range again."

"And the copies?"

"Burned. Tonight, in front of you. And Elias goes free—on the southbound train, actually, with a ticket and an apology, because if you're going to make me a liar I want him as far from this territory as steel can carry him."

Wren set the pen down. He studied her for a long moment with the particular patience of a man doing division.

"You understand," he said, "that what you're offering is worth less than what I asked for. Silence decays. A signature doesn't. In five years you could wake up with a conscience and a grudge and a working airplane, and I'd have bought nothing but time."

"So add a clause," Freya said. "You're good at clauses."

"I am." He almost smiled. "Here's mine. The boy rides south as far as Grayling Junction. At Grayling Junction there will be a car, and the car will take him to a doctor, and the doctor will determine whether he's fit to travel further or needs institutional care. Given his history of delirium, I expect the latter. Indefinitely."

"That's not freedom."

"It's the best freedom on offer in this room, Miss Dravenmoor, for either of you." Wren leaned back. "Which brings us to the arithmetic you've been avoiding. You came in here to trade yourself. So let's price you honestly. Your silence: worth something, for a while. Your copies: worth less than you think, because I already have the original. Your testimony: worthless, because dead women don't testify—and please don't look shocked, we're past the point where pretending helps you."

The stove ticked. Freya made herself breathe.

"What I actually want," Wren continued, conversationally, "is the girl. Sena. The one your trapper friend didn't report and your geologist didn't mention and you didn't put in any of your reports, because you thought I wasn't paying attention. She is the only living witness who was born in that valley, and she is currently, unless I'm badly mistaken, freezing in the tree line above my rail yard with a rifle she barely knows how to use. Give me her location, and everything else you've offered is accepted as stated. Contract torn up. Plane titled. Boy on the train."

"No."

"Give me her location and the whole ledger closes, Freya. Everything. Even the parts you don't know I know about."

"There is no version of this where I hand you a living person."

"There is exactly one version of this where anyone walks out of this room whole," Wren said, "and you are running out of time to choose it. My men are in the trees already. They've been in the trees since the afternoon train came in. Whatever you think you're protecting, the only question left is whether she freezes up there tonight or comes down warm."

Freya's mind went white and clear, the way it did when the engine quit and there was exactly one field in reach. He was lying—or half-lying. Men in the trees, maybe. Men who'd found her, no. Sena had grown up in country harder than this, and she'd been gone from the tree line twenty minutes after they'd parted, moving like smoke. But it didn't matter whether Wren could find her. What mattered was that Freya had walked into this office believing she had something to trade, and she'd just watched him price every item on her table and find it all wanting, and the only thing left on the table was herself.

So she put herself on it.

"Then here's my final offer," she said. "Me. Not my silence—my custody. I come north with you tonight, voluntarily, publicly, as the woman who stole company property and abducted a company employee. You parade me however you want. I'll confess to anything you put in front of me, on the record, in writing, and I'll do it convincingly, because unlike your forged surveys I'm actually guilty of the crimes you'd charge me with. Every word I might say at that tribunal dies in my throat the moment I sign. You don't need Sena. You need the story closed, and I am the story. I'm the loose end with a pilot's license and a motive. Take me apart and there's nothing left to testify."

Wren was quiet for a moment.

"You'd servitude yourself," he said, "for a cartographer you've known a month and a girl you've known three days."

"For everyone in that valley," Freya said. "For Ruth Okonkwo, whose body is still in a crevasse your men put her in. For Ilsa, who's carried your garbage for twenty years. Yeah. I'd servitude myself. I'd do worse. That's the part you've never understood about me, Aldous—you keep pricing me like I'm trying to get out of debt, and I stopped trying to get out of debt the day I found that camp."

Something flickered behind Wren's eyes—not doubt, nothing so useful as doubt, but recalculation. He reached for the contract. Tore it slowly along the fold, once, twice, and laid the halves on the desk between them like a dealt hand.

"New terms," he said. "You sign a full confession—theft, abduction, obstruction. You come north tonight. The boy goes south unharmed, and I withdraw the institutional order. And the girl—" he raised a hand before Freya could speak, "—stays untouched, provided she stays lost. If she surfaces at that tribunal, all agreements void. Can you live with that arithmetic?"

Freya looked at the torn contract. At the pen. At the frosted glass and the fence-post silhouettes behind it.

She could live with it. That was the horror of it—she could live with all of it, and the valley would still exist, and the maps would still lie, and someday some other survey team would walk into the Kestrel Range with orders and rifles, and her confession would be sitting in a company file proving exactly how far a woman would go and exactly how little it bought.

"Yes," she said, because the alternative was Elias in a locked ward and Sena hunted, and a person can only spend what she has. "Paper first. Then I sign."

Wren slid a typed page from the drawer—he'd had it prepared, of course; he'd had every version prepared—and turned it toward her, and Freya picked up the black lacquer pen, and the nib touched paper—

—and the outer door opened.

Not burst. Opened, with the small apologetic click of someone raised in that hangar generation that believed doors deserved courtesy. Cold air rolled in with the smell of avgas and woodsmoke, and a voice that Freya had known since she was nine years old said, from the doorway:

"Aldous Wren. Still making young women sign things in rooms with painted-over windows."

Ilsa Varg stood in the frame with snow in her grey braid and her old flight jacket open over a sweater, and behind her, filling the doorway with the specific bulk of a man who had decided, sometime during the flight north, to stop being a company asset, stood Voss Kettering.

Wren rose for the first time. "This is a private—"

"I chartered in on the mail flight," Ilsa said, walking past his men as if they were furniture, because to her they were; she'd drunk with their fathers. "Public enough. And I brought a witness who'd like to read what you're having the girl sign, because he's seen the original orders for Team Nine, and he's decided the tribunal might be interested in them after all. Family livelihoods being what they are." She glanced at Kettering, who said nothing, which from Kettering was a speech.

"Aldous," Ilsa said, and now her voice changed, dropped the banter, went to the place she kept for engine failures and funerals. "Twenty years ago I flew the maps out of a valley just like that one and told myself it was necessary, and I have flown with that weight ever since, and I am telling you as the woman who taught this girl everything she knows about staying alive in the air: you are not going to bury her the way they buried me. Sit down."

Wren sat. Slowly. His eyes went from Ilsa to Kettering to Freya—to the pen still in her hand, the ink line she'd begun, a single small stroke on the confession that now meant nothing and everything.

Freya set the pen down.

"Get Elias," she said to Kettering. "The satchel comes with him."

Kettering nodded once and went. Behind the frosted glass, the fence posts shifted, uncertain, awaiting orders that Wren—for the first time in Freya's memory—did not appear to have.

Outside, far down the yard, the southbound train began to move, coupling by coupling, gathering itself toward the capital and the morning.
