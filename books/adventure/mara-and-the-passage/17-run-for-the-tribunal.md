## 17. Run for the Tribunal

Ilsa came through the depot door like weather.

She was seventy-one years old and she still filled a room the way a low-pressure system fills a valley—something in the air changed before you understood why. Wren saw her first. I watched his face do something I had never seen it do: lose its arithmetic. For one full second, Aldous Wren, operations chief of Halvorsen Mining, Northern District, was just a man standing in an office with blood on his ledger.

"Ilsa Voss," he said. "You're a long way from your hangar."

"You're a long way from honest," she said. "But here we both are."

I didn't wait for the conversation to find its footing. The field book sat on Wren's desk where he'd set it down to gloat—a fat oilcloth-wrapped rectangle with Elias's careful cartographer's hand on the spine, twenty years of Ruth Okonkwo's stubbornness sewn into a coat and carried out of a killing ground. It was four feet away. It might as well have been four miles. Between me and it stood Wren, and behind Wren stood two of his security men with their coats unbuttoned over holsters, and behind them the door Ilsa had left swinging open against the cold.

Wren turned back to me. He'd found his face again, and what he put on top of it was pity, which was worse than anger.

"Freya. Think about what happens next. You walk out of here with nothing, and my men walk out with you, and by morning you're a footnote in a freight manifest." He tapped the desk once, lightly, almost fondly. "Or you sit down, we finish our arrangement, and tomorrow you fly home free."

"Home," I said.

"The airfield. Your plane."

"My plane is impounded under your charter. My debt died when this tribunal convened. All you own now is paper, Aldous, and paper burns."

Something moved behind his eyes—the first honest thing he'd shown me all night—and I knew then that he was going to signal his men, and I knew roughly when. Men like Wren always looked at the door before they committed to violence. They check their exit the way other people check their conscience.

He looked at the door.

I took the field book off the desk and threw the lamp at him.

It wasn't a plan. It was a decision made by the part of me that had learned to act inside three seconds because up in the air, three seconds is the difference between a correction and a crater. The lamp caught Wren across the shoulder and burst against the wall, and the office went dark except for the stove's red eye and the blue flash of the shorted bulb, and in that dark I heard Ilsa's cane crack across somebody's forearm with a sound like ice letting go.

"RUN, GIRL," she said, in the voice she'd used on me when I was nineteen and freezing up on short final. The voice that did not permit hesitation.

I ran. Through the dark, past the desk, shoulder into the inner door that led to the freight platform, and I heard Ilsa behind me moving faster than a woman with a cane had any right to move, and I heard Wren shouting—"Stop them, stop them, the book, GET THE BOOK"—and then the night opened up around me and I was running across frozen gravel with my breath tearing out of me in white flags.

The platform lights were on. Of course they were; Wren owned the constable, and the constable owned the switches. Two shapes detached from the shadow of the water tower and started toward me at a jog, professional, unhurried, men who had done this before and expected it to go the way it always went.

"Elias!" I shouted at the freight shed. "NOW!"

The shed door banged open and Elias Brandt came out sideways, wrists still bound in front of him with wire, and he did not hesitate either. He'd spent a week walking out of a glacier country with a dead man's field book sewn into his coat; hesitation had been cut out of him somewhere on the moraine. He hit the first guard from the blind side—a shoulder to the ribs, clumsy and total, the way exhausted men fight—and the two of them went down into the gravel together, and I brought the field book's oilcloth wrapping down across the second man's wrist as he reached for his holster, and he swore and staggered, and then Ilsa was there with her cane hooked behind the first guard's ankle and the whole knot of us went sprawling and rolling and getting up again, and somewhere behind us a pistol went off and the bullet hit the water tower with a sound like a bell dying.

"Truck," Ilsa gasped. "Company yard. Third row. Keys in the visor—they never learn."

Of course she knew where the company trucks were. She'd flown for Halvorsen for eleven years. She probably knew which trucks had bad starters.

We ran. Elias stumbled, went down on one knee, and I hauled him up by the bound wrists and we ran together, three-legged and graceless, while behind us the platform boiled with flashlight beams and shouting and Wren's voice cracking upward into a register I'd never heard from him, the register of a man watching his whole architecture come down. Another shot. Gravel kicked up near my heel like a small gray bird taking flight.

The company yard gate was chained. Ilsa stopped ten feet short, breathing hard, and pointed with the cane. "Through the fence gap, behind the fuel drums. And Freya—" she grabbed my sleeve, and her eyes in the dark were exactly the eyes that had taught me a crosswind landing, patient and absolutely certain, "—the ford. Not the bridge. The bridge has a constable on it now, if he's worth his bribe."

"The ford's ice won't hold a truck."

"The ford's ice held a truck every spring breakup for twenty years, and I drove it every time." She pushed me toward the fence. "Go."

"What about you?"

"I'm going to stand in the middle of the yard," she said, "and be a very old woman having a heart attack, until they decide I'm more trouble than evidence." Her mouth did the thing I had loved since I was a child hanging on the hangar fence—half smile, all iron. "They'll figure it out eventually. By then you'll be on the train. Now GO."

* * *

The truck was a Halvorsen flatbed with a plow blade bolted to the front and a starter that caught on the third try, which Ilsa would have counted as good luck. Elias got the passenger door open with his bound hands and I slid behind the wheel and killed the headlights before we cleared the fuel drums, driving by moonlight and memory, the plow blade shrieking once along a drum rim as we squeezed through the fence gap and out onto the river road.

Behind us the yard lit up. Headlights everywhere, four, five, swinging wide, and an engine note I recognized with a cold drop in my stomach: Kettering's tracked vehicle, the one that could go anywhere and usually did.

"They'll radio ahead," Elias said. His voice was still wrecked from the fever and the wire had chewed his wrists raw, but his mind was working; I could hear it working, the cartographer plotting. "Road junction at Miller's Cut. If they hold the junction, we're done."

"We're not going to the junction." I put the truck into the long slide down toward the riverbank, snow spraying off the tires. "We're going through the river."

Elias looked at me. Then at the river—a broad white plain under the moon, cracked and refrozen, black leads of open water showing along the far channel like veins. Then back at me.

"That's ice."

"That's ice," I agreed. "Hold on to something that isn't me."

I've landed on frozen lakes in an aircraft with eighteen hundred pounds of momentum and no brakes, so I will tell you what nobody tells you about ice: it doesn't break all at once, and it doesn't break quietly. We went out onto the river at speed because slow is death—you want the weight distributed, you want to keep moving, you want to trust the same physics that lets a bush plane touch down on a lake and live. The ice talked the whole way. Long groans rolling out ahead of us like whales calling, cracks shooting away from the plow blade in white lightning, and once, directly under the cab, a boom so deep I felt it in my teeth and Elias made a sound I'd only heard from him once before, on the glacier, saying Ruth Okonkwo's name.

Then the far bank rose up and we were on gravel and snow and alive, and behind us the ice was still ringing, and the headlights at the near shore pulled up in a neat, furious row and stopped.

Because of course they stopped. Kettering wasn't on the ice. Kettering knew the river. Whatever else Voss Kettering was—company man, tracker, hunter of a girl and a sick cartographer—he was not a man who drowned himself to make a point.

"He'll go around," Elias said, watching the far headlights swing away upstream. "To the rail bridge. Twenty minutes, maybe less."

"Then we have twenty minutes." I wrestled the wheel through the switchbacks above the channel, engine howling, the field book jammed between my feet where I could feel it with my boot like a heartbeat. "Railhead's six miles. Last southbound train is at midnight. After that, nothing until Thursday, and Thursday is a day too late."

"How do you know there's a last train?"

"Because Wren bought the telegraph office," I said. "And whoever buys the wires buys the timetable. There's a last train tonight, Elias. There's always a last train when someone needs there to be."

* * *

We made the railhead with nine minutes to spare and no truck, because the truck died a half mile out with steam screaming from its radiator, and we came the rest of the way at a dead run down the middle of the road like fools in a story. The station was nearly empty—one lamp burning, frost furring the platform edges, a single car coupled behind the locomotive with its windows lit amber against the dark. The conductor was already standing on the steps with his watch out.

"No tickets," I gasped.

"Then no ride." But he was looking at Elias—at the ruined wrists, the wire still hanging from one of them, the hollowed-out face of a man who had walked out of a glacier—and something in his expression shifted. Conductors see everything on their lines. They know which passengers are running.

"Please," I said. "There are men coming behind us who work for the mining company. There's a tribunal in the capital tomorrow and everything depends on—" I stopped, because I was about to say *everything depends on this book*, and I realized I didn't need to explain. Everyone in these mountains knew what Halvorsen permits meant. Everyone had lost something to them.

The conductor looked down the empty track, then at his watch, then at me.

"Ladies' car, third seat," he said. "My sister lives up the Kestrel. Company men shot her dog for wandering near a stake line." He stepped aside. "Don't make me regret knowing you."

We got Elias up the steps and into the seat, and I dropped onto the bench beside him with the field book in my lap and my lungs on fire, and for about forty seconds I allowed myself to believe it was over.

Then the locomotive shuddered, gathered itself, began to move—and down the platform, running hard, boots hammering the boards, came Voss Kettering.

Alone. No men behind him. Coat open, breath streaming, and in his hand not a rifle but a telegraph flimsy, which he thrust up at the window as the train gathered speed, and I leaned out and took it because some animal instinct told me that a man runs that hard to hand you something, not to take it.

The train pulled away. Kettering stood on the platform getting smaller, and I unfolded the flimsy under the amber light.

It was a copy of a company dispatch, routed through the telegraph office Wren thought he owned:

**TO ALL UNITS. PURSUIT SUSPENDED PENDING VERIFICATION OF SIGHTING, MILLER'S CUT, 2340 HRS. SUBJECTS PRESUMED HEADING EAST BY ROAD. HOLD JUNCTION. —KETTERING**

Miller's Cut was thirty miles east. There was nothing at Miller's Cut but a frozen crossroads.

Elias read it over my shoulder. "He sent his own men the wrong way."

"He read the field book," I said. Chapter thirteen of a debt I hadn't known I owed. "In the parley, after he took me. He read it, and he couldn't unknow it, and tonight he chose which side of the ice to stand on."

Elias sank back against the seat. The wire was finally off his wrists—I'd cut it with the conductor's freight knife somewhere in the last mile, though I honestly couldn't remember doing it—and his hands shook as he laid them flat on his knees. Outside, the snow began again, fat and silent, erasing the road behind us, erasing Miller's Cut, erasing everything except the rails running south.

"Tomorrow," he said. "The tribunal convenes at ten."

"And Halvorsen's lawyers filed their maps yesterday. Polished ones. Beautiful ones." I wrapped my arms around the field book and felt Ruth Okonkwo's stubbornness pressing back against my ribs. "So tomorrow we don't bring them a book. We bring them a truth they can't file away."

The train rocked south through the falling snow, and somewhere behind us Ilsa was standing in a company yard being old and inconvenient, and somewhere ahead the capital was sleeping under the same storm, and I let my head rest against the cold glass and watched the dark country slide past.

Ten o'clock tomorrow. Everything we had, on one table, in one morning.

I closed my eyes and started composing my statement.
