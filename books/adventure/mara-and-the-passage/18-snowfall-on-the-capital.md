## 18. Snowfall on the Capital

The train took the curves like a man walking a ledge in the dark—slow, deliberate, testing every step before committing his weight. Elias felt each one in his ribs, where Kettering's men had worked him over at the railhead, and he was grateful for the pain. Pain meant the fever was gone. Pain meant he was still here, still carrying what he carried.

The freight car smelled of creosote and cold iron. They'd wedged themselves between pallets of timber bound for the capital, and the swaying made the lantern Freya had rigged swing in slow arcs, throwing shadows that climbed the walls and slid back down. Sena slept against the timber, her rifle across her knees even now, even asleep. Ilsa sat opposite Elias with her hands wrapped around a tin cup of tea she hadn't touched, watching him the way she watched weather come in.

"You're staring at my coat," Ilsa said.

"I'm staring at the field book." It lay open on his lap, the original, the one Ruth Okonkwo had sewn into his lining and died for. Pages 40 through 47 were gone—torn out at the railhead when Wren's constable had patted him down, quick and professional, taking exactly what Wren had told them to take. Seven days of readings. The valley's true elevations. The bearings that proved the pass existed and that the people in it had been there long enough to have rights no concession could touch.

"Can you get them back?" Freya asked. She sat cross-legged on the floor with a legal pad balanced on one knee, writing by lantern light, her handwriting cramped from the train's motion. She'd been drafting her statement since dusk. She'd rewritten the opening four times.

"I can rebuild them," Elias said. "Not perfectly. But close."

Ilsa set down her untouched tea. "Close is a word lawyers eat for breakfast."

"Then we make it better than close." He turned to a blank page in the back of the book, the one Ruth had left empty, and pressed his pencil flat against it until the lead shone. "I drew these pages twice already. Once in the field, once as fair copies for the company archive. That was the job—junior cartographer, everything duplicated. I've seen every line three times." He looked up. "You don't forget numbers you've written in ink at forty below. Your fingers remember them."

Freya stopped writing. "You're sure?"

"No," he said honestly. "But Ruth used to say a map isn't the territory—it's an argument about the territory. And I've been making this argument in my head for two months, every night, in every camp they put me in. The pages are gone. The argument isn't."

* * *

He started with the barometric readings, because those were purest—numbers without interpretation, the kind of thing a mind holds like a stone held in a fist. Day forty-one: 4,112 feet at the saddle. Day forty-two: 3,890 at the valley floor. He wrote them in columns, checked each against the next, and found the rhythm of Ruth's survey protocol rising up through his memory like water finding its level. She'd insisted on morning and evening readings, always paired, always dated, because single readings could be dismissed but patterns couldn't. *A pattern is a witness,* she used to say. *One number is an opinion.*

By midnight he had the columns. By two he had the triangulation notes—the three peaks they'd sighted from, the angles, the calculated positions of the watchtower and the terraced fields below it. His hand shook on some of the decimals and he marked those with a small circle, honest about the uncertainty, because Ruth would have circled them too. She'd had no patience for false precision. *If you don't know, draw the dotted line,* she said. *Dotted lines tell the truth too.*

Sena woke somewhere past three and watched him work without speaking. When he paused to flex his cramping hand, she said, "The tower. You have the tower?"

"Northeast corner of the valley floor. Bearing 047 from the western ridge."

"That's my father's tower." Her voice was flat, careful, the way ice is careful underfoot. "He built it before you were born, probably. Before your maps had any lines there at all."

Elias met her eyes. "That's the whole point of page 44. We surveyed the construction. Mortar weathering, timber age, the terrace walls. Ruth spent a full day on it. She wanted the valley's age written in things no one could argue with—stone and rot and tree rings." He tapped the rebuilt column. "It's all here. Your father's tower is going to testify."

Sena looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded once, got up, and added wood to the little stove they'd rigged from a biscuit tin, and Elias understood that something had shifted—not forgiveness, maybe not yet trust, but acknowledgment. He had carried her father's tower in his head through every bad night, and she knew it now.

* * *

Toward dawn the train stopped at a water tower, and Ilsa went forward to buy newspapers from the platform kiosk while Freya read her draft aloud in a low voice, testing it.

"'On the fourteenth of October, I landed at Survey Team Nine's camp in the Kestrel Range and found it abandoned...'" She frowned. "Too much like a report. Wren will say I invented all of it."

"He'll say that no matter how you write it," Ilsa said, coming back up the aisle with folded papers. "So write it like yourself. Juries believe pilots more than prose."

She dropped the newspapers on the timber. The top one was the capital's morning edition, and the headline hit like cold water:

**TRIBUNAL TO HEAR KESTREL CONCESSION CLAIMS THURSDAY — HALVORSEN FILES COMPLETED SURVEYS**

Elias read it twice. Below the fold, a photograph of Aldous Wren on the courthouse steps, gray coat, gray smile, flanked by men carrying document cases. The caption called the Halvorsen submission "exhaustive and uncontested."

"They filed yesterday," Ilsa said. "Full falsified set. Every page stamped, certified, notarized. Wren didn't lose time."

"Of course he didn't." Freya's voice had gone quiet and hard. "The permits expire Thursday at noon. If their surveys stand unchallenged, the concessions renew automatically. We're not delivering evidence anymore. We're racing a clock."

Elias looked out the gap in the freight car door. Behind them, far to the north, the sky over the Kestrel Range had gone the color of pewter, and he could see the snow line creeping down the ridges like a tide coming in. The early snows. Right on schedule. Somewhere under that white were the pulled survey stakes, the warm stove, the logbook open to *PASSAGE REAL*—the whole frozen scene of it, waiting for spring thaw to give up its secrets, if spring came before the lawyers did.

"How far to the capital?" Sena asked.

Ilsa checked her watch against the station clock. "We reach the rail yards at six tomorrow morning. Tribunal convenes at nine."

"And if the train's late?"

"Then we run," Freya said simply, and went back to her statement.

* * *

Elias worked through the last dark hours, and something strange happened as he wrote: the fever dreams came back, but changed. Instead of running, he was drawing. Ruth stood beside him in the dream, pointing at the dotted lines, saying *good, good, now the river,* and when he woke with the pencil still moving in his hand, the eastern watercourse lay complete on the page, every bend matching the memory of it, and the sky outside the door gap had gone pale gray, then rose, then the hard clean blue of a day that intended to be cold and clear.

He sat back and looked at what he'd made. Seven pages rebuilt. Not copies—testimony. Every figure initialed E.B., every reconstruction noted as such in the margin, because Ruth had taught him that a map's honesty lived in its margins. *The margins are where the map keeps its conscience.*

Freya read the reconstructed pages in silence. Then she read them again. Then she looked up, and Elias saw in her face the same thing he'd seen in Ruth's, that first morning in the range, when she'd looked at the mountains and known exactly what they were worth.

"It'll hold," she said. "Wren will attack it. He'll call it fabrication. But it matches the photographs, and it matches Sena's account, and it matches twenty years of Ilsa's guilt." She squared the pages and slipped them inside the field book beside the torn stubs of the originals. "And it matches the truth, which is the only thing in that courtroom that doesn't need rehearsing."

The train began its long descent toward the capital, and behind them the snow reached the passes and closed them, white and final, sealing the valley in for the winter—safe, for now, and officially nonexistent until nine o'clock tomorrow morning.

Elias buttoned the field book into his coat, over his heart, where Ruth had sewn it, and watched the capital's smoke rise ahead like a question the whole country was finally going to answer.

At six-oh-four, the train slowed into the rail yards—and Elias saw, drawn up along the platform, a row of black motorcars and a cluster of gray coats, and at their center, checking his pocket watch, Aldous Wren.
