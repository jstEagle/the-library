## 19. The Hearing

The tribunal chamber had been a customs house once, back when the capital still believed in stamping things and letting them go. Now it was all dark wood and brass fittings and a raised bench where three commissioners sat beneath the federal seal, and Freya thought, standing at the witness table with her hands cuffed loosely in front of her—*they let you testify in irons here, if the charge is theft*—that the room smelled like every room where someone had ever decided her life for her. Paper and polish and cold radiators.

The middle commissioner was named Ostrander. He was old, dry as a filed report, and he read the charges without looking up: unlawful appropriation of company property, conspiracy to defraud Halvorsen Mining and Survey, obstruction of a federal concession review. Then he looked up.

"Dravenmoor. You understand these proceedings are also the final session of the Kestrel Range concession review. The permits expire at midnight tonight. Whatever this tribunal decides about you, it decides today about the range."

"I understand."

"Then we'll begin with the company's presentation." Ostrander's eyes went past her to the long table on the left, where Aldous Wren sat in a charcoal suit that probably cost more than her annual fuel bill, flanked by two lawyers whose faces were smooth as river stones. "Operations Chief Wren. The floor is yours."

Wren rose like a man who had rehearsed rising. And he had. Freya had watched him work rooms for years—the airfield briefings, the debt reviews, the quiet conversations where your interest rate crept up while he smiled—and she knew his instrument was not lying. It was *precision*. He never said anything false. He just arranged true things until they pointed somewhere else.

"Honorable commissioners." His voice filled the chamber without effort. "I'll be brief, because the facts are simple. Halvorsen Mining commissioned Survey Team Nine at considerable expense to map the Kestrel Range for mineral assessment. That survey was completed. The maps were filed. The team was then rotated out—a standard procedure, seasonal rotation, documented here—" a lawyer slid a bound folder down the table; another lawyer received it and passed it forward with clerical reverence "—and reassigned to the Copperline survey. Every member of Team Nine is alive, accounted for, and currently employed elsewhere in the company structure."

He paused, letting the folder land on the commissioners' bench like a verdict.

"The woman before you," Wren continued, turning now, and Freya felt the whole room turn with him, "stole a company aircraft. Stole proprietary survey materials. Abducted an employee of this company who, I will note, has a documented history of psychological instability following a childhood head injury—"

"That's not—" Elias started from the gallery, and one of the lawyers smiled faintly, because that was the point. Let him object. Objecting proved he was excitable. Excitable proved unstable.

"—and flew into federal territory carrying documents she had no right to possess, based on claims so fantastical that no survey office, no mapping bureau, and no prior review has ever substantiated them." Wren folded his hands. "There is no valley in the Kestrel Range. There never was. There is a woman with debts exceeding eleven thousand crowns to my employer, who stood to have those debts extinguished by discrediting our filings. Ask yourselves what her incentive is. Then ask what ours could possibly be. We own the permits either way. Why would we falsify anything?"

It was good. Freya hated how good it was. Eleven thousand crowns was a real number with real weight, and the room's arithmetic did exactly what Wren wanted it to do: a desperate pilot versus a corporation that owned everything already—who needed to cheat?

Ostrander made a small note. The commissioner on his left, a heavyset woman named Brandt-Voss—no relation to anyone, though Elias had laughed bitterly at the name when the docket arrived—asked whether the company would produce Team Nine's members for testimony.

"They've been offered," Wren said smoothly. "Most are deployed at remote stations. Travel in winter is hazardous. We submitted sworn statements instead."

"Sworn by whom?"

"By the team members themselves. Notarized at company facilities."

Freya watched Ostrander look at the notarization stamps, and she watched him not ask the obvious question, which was that a notary employed by Halvorsen could stamp a confession to murder if you brought him lunch. The machinery of legitimacy. She'd spent six years watching it grind against people in the valleys, always politely, always with paperwork.

"Dravenmoor." Ostrander set down his pen. "Your presentation."

She stood. Her cuffs clinked against the table edge, and a bailiff glanced at Ostrander, who waved a hand—leave them—and Freya decided to make them part of the record instead of hiding them.

"Commissioners, you're looking at a thief. That part is true. I stole the field book. I stole the photographs. I flew a plane I don't own through airspace I wasn't cleared for." She let it sit. "I want that on the record first, because everything else I say will sound more believable if you know I'm not pretending to be innocent. I'm pretending to be nothing. I'm just telling you what I saw."

She laid the photographs on the evidence table, one by one, in the order she'd taken them, because order mattered, because sequence was the only thing a bush pilot trusted.

"Team Nine's camp, found intact on the fourth of last month. Stove warm. Logbook open. Fresh snow around every tent, unbroken—no tracks leaving, none arriving. Here's the logbook entry itself." She turned the enlargement toward the bench. *PASSAGE REAL.* Two words. Ruth Okonkwo's handwriting; Elias had confirmed it twice under oath in the affidavit. "Ruth Okonkwo, team leader, twenty-two years with the survey service. Where is she, Chief Wren? You say everyone's been rotated to Copperline. I've flown over Copperline four times since then. There's no new camp there. There's no new anything."

"Objection to characterization," one of the river-stone lawyers began, but Ostrander said, "Overruled. It's a question. Answer it or decline to, Chief Wren, and we'll weigh the declining."

Wren declined, gracefully, with a small regretful smile, and Freya pressed on before the grace could settle.

She produced the copied pages next—Elias's originals were sealed with the clerk, too fragile to handle twice—and read Ruth Okonkwo's field notes aloud. The bearings. The elevation readings for a pass that appeared on no chart. The population counts, done in Ruth's careful hand: dwellings, herds, a watchtower, smoke from cook fires, *inhabited, occupied continuously, residents state arrival during the Halvorsen expulsions of twenty years ago.*

"These aren't my words. They're not Elias Brandt's. They're the senior cartographer's, written before anyone told her to erase them. And here—" Freya held up the last page, the one she'd copied in Wren's own operations building while he slept thirty feet away "—is the amendment order. Signed routing slip, company letterhead, dated three days after Ruth's final entry. 'Revise Section 7. Remove all features west of the saddle line. Reclassify as unsurveyed.' Unsurveyed. After she'd surveyed it. Commissioners, you don't order a place erased unless it exists."

The chamber had gone very quiet. Even the radiator seemed to have stopped ticking.

Elias testified next. They'd cleaned him up, gotten food into him, and he walked to the stand thin and steady, and when the oath was administered his voice didn't shake at all, which surprised Freya, who had held him through nights when it shook constantly.

He told it plainly. The valley. The orders that came down mid-survey. Ruth refusing. The men with company rifles arriving at dawn—not security contractors, he said, company men, payroll records would show it if payroll records survived—and the team given a choice between reassignment and something worse. How he ran with the field book sewn into his coat lining and walked forty miles of moraine with a broken compass and a fever.

"They told us the valley was a rounding error," Elias said. "Ruth said, no—it's people. And Wren's man said, that's a matter of perspective." He looked directly at Wren when he said it. Wren looked back with the patience of a man watching weather he intended to fly through.

Then the telegram. A clerk read it into the record in a flat bureaucratic voice that somehow made it worse, made it truer: *SWORN STATEMENT, SENA OKAFOR, KESTREL VALLEY RESIDENT. MY FATHER DIED IN THE EXPULSION OF TWENTY YEARS AGO. WE HAVE LIVED HERE SINCE, UNRECORDED, BECAUSE YOUR MAPS SAY NO ONE IS HERE. THE SURVEYOR RUTH OKONKWO CAME TO US WITH RESPECT AND LEFT US ALIVE. I TESTIFY THAT THE VALLEY EXISTS AND THAT HALVORSEN KNOWS IT HAS ALWAYS EXISTED.* Sent from the railhead relay, witnessed by the station agent, timestamped yesterday.

Freya watched the commissioners. Ostrander's face gave away nothing, but Brandt-Voss had stopped taking notes, and the third commissioner—young, appointment still wet behind the ears—was looking at Wren the way you look at a stove you've suddenly realized is hot.

And then Wren stood for cross-examination, and Freya understood that everything so far had been the overture.

"Ms. Dravenmoor." He approached the witness table slowly, hands clasped behind his back, a man strolling through his own orchard. "You owe Halvorsen Mining eleven thousand, four hundred and six crowns. Is that correct?"

"It's in your ledger. I've never seen the arithmetic."

"The ledger is audited annually. Yes or no."

"Yes."

"And this debt was incurred how? Aircraft purchase? Fuel advances? Emergency repairs you authorized yourself?"

"Some of each. The repairs kept me alive."

"Mm." He turned to the bench. "Commissioners, I want to be fair to this woman, because I've known her professionally for years and she is, in her way, a fine pilot." He said it kindly, and it landed like a knife wrapped in cloth. "But consider her position. Consider that Halvorsen, weeks ago, offered her full forgiveness of that debt—eleven thousand crowns—in exchange for one simple act: returning stolen property and a distressed employee. She refused. She chose instead to keep the property, keep the employee, and fly to this tribunal. Ask yourselves: what does a rational debtor do?" He spread his hands. "Unless the payoff is larger than the debt. Unless someone promised her something worth more than eleven thousand crowns. Amnesty, perhaps. A new employer. A hero's welcome. I don't know what was promised. I only know what she refused."

"I refused because it was hush money," Freya said.

"You refused because you were offered a better price elsewhere. That's not conviction, Ms. Dravenmoor. That's negotiation." He let the word hang. "And there's the matter of the aircraft. A de Havilland, serial 4-9-1-7, currently impounded, currently valued at well beyond her debt—which means even her crime, properly liquidated, leaves her solvent. She gambled a plane she didn't own and lost nothing but other people's money. This entire proceeding, from her side, is a wager with house funds."

The room shifted. Freya felt it physically, a settling in the benches behind her, the way a glacier makes a sound before it moves. He was doing it again. Arranging true things. Every fact he'd laid out was accurate, and together they described a woman who had bet stolen goods on a long shot, and the commissioners were men and women of ledgers themselves, and ledgers were the language they dreamed in.

Brandt-Voss leaned toward her microphone. "Ms. Dravenmoor. The theft charge is not in dispute?"

"No, Commissioner."

"And you understood, when you took the field book, that you were committing a crime?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the alternative was letting a lie become a map. Maps outlive all of us. You can pardon a thief. You can't unpardon a valley that starves because it was never drawn."

Silence. Somewhere in the gallery a cough, quickly smothered.

Ostrander consulted his papers for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was the voice of a man closing accounts.

"The tribunal notes the following. The company's documentation is complete, consistent, and notarized. The respondent's evidence is compelling but originates from a confessed thief and a witness of questioned reliability, corroborated by a telegram from an unverified settlement in disputed territory." He looked up. "The tribunal is inclined to find the company's filings presumptively valid, and the respondent's claims insufficiently corroborated by any party with standing or clean hands. Unless—" and here he paused, and Freya's stomach dropped, because she heard the door opening in his voice, the narrow door that only opens once "—the tribunal hears further material evidence before the close of session. Which occurs in one hour."

One hour. Midnight was the permits. One hour was everything.

Freya sat down. Her cuffs rattled. Across the aisle, Wren allowed himself the smallest adjustment of his cufflink, a man checking his watch against a train he knew was on time.

And then the doors at the back of the chamber opened, and the sound came again—that pre-glacier sound—and Freya turned, and her heart did something complicated and painful, because walking up the aisle, coat still dusted with runway grit, flying goggles pushed up into gray hair, came Ilsa Voss, and behind her, moving like a man reporting for a shift he'd dreaded his whole life, came Voss Kettering, carrying a document case with both hands, like it weighed what it actually weighed.

Wren saw them. For the first time in six years, Freya watched something move behind Aldous Wren's face that was not precision.

Ilsa reached the bar of the tribunal, set her palms flat on the wood, and said, in the voice she'd used to talk Freya down through her first whiteout:

"Commissioners. My name is Ilsa Voss. I flew for Halvorsen twenty years ago. And I'm here to confess."
