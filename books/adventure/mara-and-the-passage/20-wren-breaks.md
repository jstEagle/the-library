## 20. Wren Breaks

The tribunal chamber smelled of cold iron and old paper, the way government buildings do when they've been heated too long by radiators that were old before anyone in the room was born. Freya sat at the witness table with her hands flat on the wood, because if she didn't pin them down they'd shake. Elias sat beside her, upright now, color back in his face, the field book in an evidence sleeve between them like a patient on a gurney.

Across the aisle, Halvorsen Mining had brought lawyers the way other men brought dogs—six of them, sleek and quiet, arranged in a crescent around Aldous Wren. He wore a gray suit she'd never seen before. It fit him badly. Men who spent their lives behind desks always looked wrong out in daylight, and Wren looked wrong this morning in every particular: too polished, too still, too much like a man rehearsing.

The presiding commissioner, a narrow woman named Hargrove with reading glasses on a chain, called the session to order at nine sharp. Outside the tall windows, snow came down without wind, straight and businesslike, as if the weather too had filed its testimony and was waiting to be cross-examined.

Wren went first. Of course he did. Companies always testified first; they paid for the room's time before anyone else could spend it.

He was good. She had to give him that. He stood at the lectern with his hands folded and told the story they had built for him—the story she'd once been offered a piece of—and he told it smoothly, with just enough weariness to sound honest. Survey Team Nine had completed its work. The Kestrel Range survey found no permanent habitation. The team had been rotated out early due to weather. One junior cartographer, Mr. Brandt, had suffered exposure and disorientation; his claims of a populated valley were the product of hypothermia and, Wren suggested gently, ambition. The maps filed with the tribunal were accurate. The permits should be renewed. Nothing here warranted the federal government's attention beyond a rubber stamp.

He produced the falsified surveys like a magician producing doves—clean, certified, stamped. He produced affidavits from two Team Nine members whose names Freya didn't recognize, sworn statements that they'd seen no one, no smoke, no tracks. He even produced a letter, dated three weeks after the disappearance, allegedly signed by Ruth Okonkwo confirming the empty-valley finding.

Freya stared at that letter until her eyes burned. Ruth Okonkwo, who had refused to falsify a single contour line. Ruth Okonkwo, whom Elias had last seen being marched into the trees by men with company rifles. Her signature sat there on the page, neat and dead and convenient.

When Wren finished, Hargrove thanked him in a voice like a closing door, and the morning broke for recess, and Freya realized her hands had gone numb against the wood.

"They're going to bury us," Elias said quietly. "Paper against paper. Theirs is notarized."

"Ours is true," Freya said.

Elias almost smiled. "I used to believe that mattered."

* * *

They called her after lunch.

She walked to the stand feeling the way she felt on short final in a crosswind—everything narrowed to the next three seconds, then the next. She gave her name, her license number, her years flying the interior. She described the medicine runs, the debt ledger, the order to resupply Team Nine. She described the camp: stove warm, logbook open, the final entry. *PASSAGE REAL.* No tracks in fresh snow. No people.

She described the pulled survey stakes, and she watched Wren's jaw tighten at that—he hadn't known she'd photographed them.

Then the photographs, projected onto the screen the stenographers used. The camp pristine. The logbook. And then the ones she'd taken on later flights, flying the route Elias had given her: the watchtower, the terraced fields under snow, the smoke from a valley that officially did not exist.

Halvorsen's lead counsel rose to cross-examine, and he was exactly what six lawyers' fees bought: courteous, surgical, relentless. He established that she was indebted to Halvorsen Mining. He established that she had stolen company property—fuel, charts, an aircraft. He established that she had fled a warrant, that her copilot was a man adjudged delirious by company doctors, that her sole corroborating witness was a woman named Sena who had lived illegally inside a concession area for twenty years and therefore had every motive to lie.

"And you expect this tribunal," he said, turning to the commissioners, "to void a lawful charter on the testimony of a debtor, a thief, a madman, and a squatter?"

"I expect it to look at the photographs," Freya said. "Smoke doesn't care who's in debt."

A murmur ran the room. Hargrove didn't gavel it down.

But Freya could feel the room wavering all the same, the way she could feel ice flexing under a wheel. Truth was heavy, but so was paper, and paper was stacked higher on their side. When she stepped down, Elias caught her eye and shook his head slightly—not despair, just arithmetic. They were losing. Slowly, politely, by inches, they were losing.

Then the doors at the back of the chamber opened, and Ilsa Vann walked in.

* * *

Freya had never seen Ilsa in anything but coveralls or flight gear. Now she came up the aisle in a wool coat older than Freya, gray hair pinned back, walking with the deliberate pace of a woman who had decided something expensive and intended to get full value for it. She did not look at Freya. Freya understood why, and it nearly undid her more than if she had.

"I'm requesting to testify," Ilsa said to Hargrove. "I have no counsel. I have a statement. It will take ten minutes."

Halvorsen's counsel was on his feet instantly—"This is irregular, the witness is unvetted, she has a documented history with—" and Hargrove cut him off with two fingers.

"The tribunal will hear her."

Ilsa took the stand and swore in, and her voice, when it came, was the flat, unhurried voice she used for radio calls in bad weather. No drama in it. That was what made it terrible.

"My name is Ilsa Vann. Twenty-two years ago I flew for Halvorsen Mining out of the Corriedale station. In the autumn of that year I was ordered to ferry a survey crew into the Ashcombe drainage and to bring out only three of them. I was told the rest had been reassigned overland." She paused. "That was a lie. I learned it was a lie eleven days later, when I flew the same drainage and saw burned cabins where the map said nothing stood. There were families in that valley. My passengers had helped remove them. Some of them did not survive the removal. I carried the men who did it, and I carried the supplies that fed them, and I said nothing for twenty-two years because I owed the company for my aircraft and my license, and because I told myself the debt made me innocent. It did not. Debt is not innocence. I am living proof of the pattern this tribunal is examining, because I was its instrument."

The room had gone absolutely still. Even the radiators seemed quieter.

"When Freya Dravenmoor came to me with the story of Team Nine," Ilsa went on, "I recognized it. Same company. Same method. Find a valley with people in it, erase the people, erase the map, keep the permit alive. I gave her fuel and charts because I would not be an instrument twice. And I am here today because silence is how this continues, and I am done paying rent on my guilt."

Halvorsen's counsel rose again, softer now, almost pitying. "Ms. Vann, you admit you concealed evidence of a crime for over two decades. Why should this tribunal credit the word of a self-confessed accomplice?"

"Because I'm confessing," Ilsa said, "and your client is still denying. One of those things costs something. Ask yourself which."

Hargrove asked for the Ashcombe records to be entered into evidence. They existed—Ilsa had written to the provincial archive herself, three weeks ago, and the retrieval notice was already in the tribunal's file. Freya watched the Halvorsen crescent close ranks around Wren, watched him whisper rapidly to counsel, watched the gray suit stop fitting him altogether.

And then the doors opened again.

* * *

Voss Kettering came up the aisle the way he moved through timber—unhurried, certain of his footing, impossible to stop without violence. He was still in his field coat. He had snow in his hair. He carried a document tube slung across his back like a rifle, and every lawyer in the room understood simultaneously what it was and began talking at once.

Hargrove let them talk for exactly four seconds.

"Mr. Kettering. Identify yourself."

"Voss Kettering. Security contractor, Halvorsen Mining, fourteen years. I led the detail sent to locate Survey Team Nine." He set the document tube on the rail and unscrewed the cap with steady hands. "These are the internal orders for that operation. Not the survey orders. The other ones."

He drew out a sheaf of pages, each stamped with the company's confidential mark, and laid them on the rail one by one, reading as he went, his voice level as a lake in calm weather.

"Directive one: establish perimeter at grid seven-four-north, deny egress. Directive two: escort survey personnel to extraction point under security protocol. Directive three, initialed A. Wren, countersigned by the office of the vice-president for concessions: upon completion of map revision, survey personnel are to be transported out-of-district and their field records destroyed. Personnel are not to return to company territory. Families are to be informed of reassignment overseas."

Elias made a small sound beside Freya, and she reached under the table and gripped his wrist, and he held still.

"'Out-of-district,'" Hargrove repeated. "Is that a euphemism, Mr. Kettering?"

"It's a destination," Kettering said. "I've flown supply to it. It's a camp with no fence and no road and no record. Ruth Okonkwo is alive. So are five others. Two are not—I won't soften that—but the company's own manifest lists them as 'reassigned,' which means somewhere there's paperwork saying they never died, and somewhere there's a man who signed it." He turned, slowly, and looked at Wren, and there was no hatred in his face at all, which was worse than hatred. "You told us they'd be compensated and relocated. You told us the valley was empty. You showed us the falsified survey and told us it was real. Fourteen years I worked for you, and I believed you, because believing was cheaper than checking. I'm checking now."

"Why?" Hargrove asked. "What changed?"

Kettering considered the question honestly, which was perhaps the most damning thing anyone had done all day.

"A woman let me go when she could have left me on the moraine," he said. "I've been thinking about why ever since. This is why."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way avalanches happen fast—slow buildup, then everything at once.

Halvorsen's lead counsel demanded the documents be excluded as stolen property. Hargrove asked Kettering how he'd obtained them. Kettering said, "From the safe in the operations annex, this morning, using codes I was given when I was trusted." Counsel said that was theft. Kettering said, "Yes." Just that. Yes. And something in the plainness of it broke the room's spine.

Because here was the thing Freya saw from the witness table, the thing she would remember for the rest of her life: the machinery of silence only worked while everyone kept feeding it. The debt, the warrant, the falsified maps, the forged letter, the euphemisms—all of it ran on ordinary men doing their jobs and not asking. And the moment one man stood up in public and said *yes, I stole it, and here is what your theft was hiding*, the machine had nowhere to put him. He wasn't outside it anymore. He was inside it, testifying, and the machine began, very quietly, to eat itself.

Wren's counsel asked for a recess. Hargrove denied it. Counsel whispered. Wren whispered back, sharper. Then counsel stood and announced that his client wished to cooperate fully with the tribunal, effective immediately, and that any further questions regarding internal directives should be directed to officers of the company above Mr. Wren's authority.

Above Mr. Wren's authority.

It was done so smoothly that for a moment the room didn't register it. Then it did. A ripple went through the gallery like wind through wheat. Wren had just fed his superiors into the fire to save himself, and everyone in the chamber watched him do it, and everyone understood that someone above him was at that very moment preparing to do the same to him. The machine eating itself, course by course, upward.

Wren stood at the lectern looking like a man watching his own name being removed from a building. His mouth opened. Closed. When he finally spoke, his voice had lost all its polish.

"I acted within the scope of my instructions," he said. "Every directive I issued originated above me. I have memoranda. I have initials. I have—" and he stopped, because he heard it too, the absurdity, the smallness of it. Twenty years of power reduced to *I have initials.*

"You have a great deal," Hargrove said dryly. "You'll surrender all of it tonight. The tribunal sits again at nine tomorrow to hear the question of the concessions. Mr. Wren, you are remanded to federal custody pending charges. Mr. Kettering, your documents are entered. Ms. Vann, your statement stands. This session is adjourned."

The gavel came down, and the chamber erupted—not cheering, nothing so clean, but the roar of a room full of people realizing at the same instant that the ground had shifted beneath all of them.

Through it, Freya sat perfectly still, holding Elias's wrist, watching Ilsa come down the aisle toward her with snowmelt dripping off her coat, watching Kettering shoulder his empty document tube and nod to her once, a pilot's nod, the kind you give across a ramp when the weather's finally broken.

It wasn't over. Tomorrow the permits themselves were on the table, and Halvorsen's remaining lawyers had all night, and somewhere above Wren there were men with deeper pockets and longer reach who had just learned they were on the menu.

But the machine was broken. She could see the pieces of it scattered across the chamber floor—the forged letter, the false maps, the directives with Wren's initials—and for the first time since Team Nine's camp, since the warm stove and the open logbook and the words *PASSAGE REAL*, Freya let herself breathe.

Ilsa stopped at the table. Up close her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear.

"Ten minutes," she said. "Told you it wouldn't take long."

"What happens tomorrow?" Elias asked.

Ilsa looked at the windows, at the snow falling straight and businesslike past the glass, and something in her face was almost young.

"Now we find out," she said, "what the truth weighs."
