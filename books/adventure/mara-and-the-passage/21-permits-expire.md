## 21. Permits Expire

The tribunal chamber had been a courthouse once, before the territory grew past its own institutions and the hearings moved into the old customs hall on the riverfront. You could still see what it used to be—the brass rail where import clerks had queued, the high windows fogged with river damp, the smell of coal smoke and old paper that no amount of scrubbing would lift. Now it held folding chairs, a raised bench for three commissioners, and a crowd that had been waiting twenty years without knowing it.

Freya sat in the second row with her hands flat on her knees, because if she didn't give them a job they'd start shaking.

Elias was beside her, upright in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, his color finally back. On her other side sat Ilsa, who had not spoken since dawn except to say, *Sit straight. They can smell fear through wool.* Sena's sworn testimony had arrived by telegram two nights ago, relayed through three stations and a telegraph operator who, mercifully, had not yet been bought. It lay in the record now, read aloud on the first morning in a clerk's flat voice: names of families, dates of the expulsion, the number of graves below the watchtower. Forty-one. Freya had counted along silently, and by thirty she'd stopped breathing, and Ilsa had put one hand over hers and left it there until it was done.

The third day belonged to Wren.

He'd dressed for it—grey suit, company pin, the whole architecture of respectability—and he testified like a man reading a menu. The survey data was sound. The Kestrel concession boundaries were accurate to within accepted tolerances. Any suggestion of an inhabited valley was the invention of a disgruntled junior cartographer and a bush pilot with a documented history of debt, insubordination, and theft.

That last word landed in the room like a dropped wrench.

Commissioner Adeyemi, the federal one, the only one of the three who didn't look half-asleep, asked to see the theft documentation. Wren produced it with the smoothness of a man who'd rehearsed this part especially. Freya watched the pages go from his hand to the bench and understood exactly what she was looking at: her own signature on the fuel ledgers, the contract she'd signed at nineteen when Halvorsen bought out her father's debts along with everyone else's in the valley towns, and the incident report from the night she'd walked out of the airfield with Elias and a copied field book. *Unauthorized removal of proprietary materials. Value assessed at substantial.*

"Substantial," Adeyemi repeated. "It's a notebook."

"It is the company's property, Commissioner. And the defendant took it knowing its value—which is precisely why she took it." Wren turned to face the room, and found Freya's eyes, and smiled the way a man smiles when he thinks he's already won. "I don't dispute that something happened in the Kestrel Range. I dispute the character of the witnesses. A thief. A deserter. A woman"—and here he gestured gently at Ilsa—"who has already admitted under oath to falsifying a federal survey twenty years ago. The tribunal is being asked to void permits worth millions on the word of confessed criminals."

The room wavered. Freya felt it waver, the way you feel a wing go soft in turbulence—not a crash, just the sick knowledge that the air under you isn't holding the way it should.

Then Kettering walked in.

She'd known he might. He'd told her at the railhead, in that flat voice of his, that he'd decide what he decided when he decided it, and she hadn't expected anything, which was probably why seeing him in the doorway in his company security coat, hat in his hands, hit her so hard in the chest. He crossed the floor without hurrying. He handed an envelope to the bailiff. He did not look at Wren.

"The internal orders for Survey Team Nine," he said. "Signed authorization for 'removal and site sterilization.' My signature is on the transport manifest. I'd like to amend my prior testimony."

Wren said his name once, quietly, the way you'd say the name of a dog that had gotten loose. Kettering didn't turn around.

The afternoon went long. Adeyemi read the removal order aloud twice. The other commissioners passed papers between them and whispered. Wren's lawyers conferred in a huddle that kept getting tighter, and somewhere in the middle of it Wren himself began to come apart—not loudly, nothing so useful as a confession shouted across the floor, but in the way his answers started arriving a half-second late, the way his eyes went to the doors, calculating exits. By four o'clock he was naming names. His superiors. The permit division. The man in the capital who'd signed off on the falsified maps before Team Nine ever flew north. He gave them up like a man bailing water, faster and faster, each name a bucket thrown overboard, and it saved nothing and everyone heard everything.

Freya watched him do it and felt—she searched for hatred and couldn't find it. Only a tired recognition. Wren was what the ledger made. She'd been one payment away from being him.

* * *

The ruling came the next morning, and the snow came with it.

The early snows had caught them on the train south, as she'd known they would; now they fell on the capital proper, fat and unhurried, frosting the customs hall's high windows so the light inside went grey and even, like flying in cloud. The chamber was fuller than yesterday. Word had moved overnight—word always moved—and there were faces in the back rows she didn't know: newspaper men, a delegation of homesteaders, two women in valley wool coats who might have come down with Sena's runners and who sat with their hands folded and their chins up as though they'd paid for their seats.

Commissioner Adeyemi read the findings standing up.

The Halvorsen concessions in the Kestrel Range were voided, effective immediately, on grounds of fraud in the original permitting. The falsified surveys were struck from the record. The inhabited valley—entered into the record under the name its people used for it, a word the clerk pronounced carefully and slightly wrong—was recognized as occupied territory with title claims to be adjudicated in favor of its residents. Federal protection, pending. A census, promised. An inquiry into the deaths of Ruth Okonkwo and the six members of Survey Team Nine whose bodies had never been found, opened that same hour.

Amnesty was granted to all persons who had cooperated with the tribunal's investigation. Ilsa Voss. Elias Brandt. Voss Kettering. Even the telegraph operator got named, which made someone in the back laugh, briefly, wetly.

Adeyemi paused at the last page. Freya knew what was coming the way you know weather. She'd known since the night she refused the settlement, sitting across from the company's lawyer in a room that smelled of ink, when he'd slid the paper toward her and she'd pushed it back and seen in his face that he'd expected exactly that.

"In the matter of Freya Dravenmoor," Adeyemi said, "the charge of theft remains. The tribunal notes that the stolen materials proved decisive to these proceedings, and notes further the defendant's cooperation. However, amnesty attaches to testimony given freely, and the court finds that the defendant acted throughout under duress of debt—a condition this tribunal finds itself without authority to excuse. The charge stands unless dismissed by settlement with the injured party. Halvorsen Mining, whose charter is hereby dissolved, has transferred its remaining assets and legal interests to receivership. The receivers have offered the following resolution."

The clerk read it out. Full forgiveness of the debt. Return of the aircraft, the de Havilland, impounded these three weeks at the company field. All charges withdrawn. In exchange: withdrawal of her statement, sealing of her photographs, silence.

Her plane. Her wings. Everything she'd flown north to save, handed back across the table.

The room turned toward her. She could feel it turning, a hundred faces, and among them Ilsa's, steady, giving nothing away, and Elias's, frightened for her, and Kettering's at the back near the doors, arms folded, watching her with the same flat patience he'd shown her the night he let her walk.

Adeyemi looked down at her over the rim of his glasses. "Does the defendant wish to respond?"

Freya stood. Her knees held. She'd spent nineteen years taking jobs she couldn't refuse, and here was the first refusal that mattered, dressed up as a gift, and she found that her voice, when it came, was almost steady.

"No, sir. I decline the settlement."

"You understand the charge will proceed?"

"I understand." She made herself look at the receivers' table, at the lawyer with the paper still extended like a plate of food. "You want me to say the photographs are lies. They're not lies. You want me to un-see the camp, and the pulled stakes, and the forty-one graves. I won't. Keep the plane. Keep the debt—it died with your charter anyway, didn't it? That's what the receivership means. Nobody owns me anymore." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "I choose the record."

The gavel came down. The concessions were dead. Outside, the snow kept falling on a city that would be different by spring, and Freya Dravenmoor stood in it—pilot without a plane, debtor without a debt, free in every direction and none of them easy—and let the room empty around her while Ilsa's hand found her shoulder and stayed.

* * *

They walked out together into the white.

The streets were muffled, lamplit, beautiful in the way snow makes even ugly things beautiful for an evening. Elias walked ahead with his collar up, talking with his hands the way he did when the fever of an idea took him—something about teaching, about drawing the valley properly at last, every stream and herd trail, a map that told the truth. Ilsa listened and occasionally corrected his compass bearings out of pure habit.

At the corner outside the boarding house where they'd all been sleeping in shifts, Freya stopped.

"What?" Ilsa said.

"The plane's impounded at the company field. Receivership field, I suppose, now." She said it lightly and it didn't come out lightly. "They'll auction it eventually. Or scrap it. She's twenty-six years old and she needs a new fabric patch on the port wing and somebody who'll warm her oil properly in cold weather, and nobody at an auction knows any of that."

Ilsa looked at her for a long moment. Snow gathered on the brim of her hat and she didn't brush it off.

"You know," she said, "when I buried that survey twenty years ago, I told myself I had no choice. Debt, duty, the mentor I owed everything to. And then I spent two decades flying medicine runs and pretending the ballast wasn't there." She reached out and straightened Freya's collar, an old gesture, older than their trouble. "You chose worse than I did and better than I did, both in one morning. So here's what I know about you, girl. You've never once kept a plane that mattered. You've only ever kept the flying."

Inside, the boarding house smelled of stew and woodsmoke, and there was a telegram waiting at the desk, forwarded twice, the paper soft from handling. Freya opened it standing in the hallway with her gloves still on.

It was from Sena. Four lines. The runners had arrived home safe. The elders had voted. And the valley—her valley, the passage made real—had taken up a collection: furs, carvings, three months of trade goods, sent downriver to the railhead to be sold, the proceeds already pledged.

For the purchase of one aircraft at receivership auction. *Condition: the pilot comes with it,* the telegram finished. *We need someone who knows the pass. Spring is coming, and we have a clinic to stock.*

Freya read it twice. Then she laughed—once, startled, the first real laugh in weeks—and handed it to Ilsa, and went up the stairs to tell Elias that he'd need to draw one more map after all.

The hearing was over. The permits had expired. And somewhere north, under the snow, a valley waited for its mail.
