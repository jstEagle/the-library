## 1. The Weight of Water

The tide came into Sallow Bend twice a day and took something with it each time it left. Noor Thornbury had watched it do this for eleven days from the window of a rented room that smelled of fish oil and other people's cigarettes, and she had come to think of the river as a creditor too — patient, indifferent, certain to be paid.

She was down to her last coin when the notice went up on the harbor board.

SALVAGE CONTRACT — DREDGING OF SHALLOWS, OLD CHANNEL MOUTH. WAGES PAID ON COMPLETION. APPLY HARBOR MASTER.

The paper was new; the ink was already curling in the damp. Noor read it three times, doing the arithmetic she always did — wages against days, days against the debt, the debt against the distance between here and anywhere else. The numbers had not improved since yesterday. They were not going to improve. Eleven days she'd been in Sallow Bend, sleeping in shifts, eating once daily, and every morning she checked the ferry schedule for men in gray coats, and every morning they weren't there yet, which meant only that they hadn't arrived *yet*.

Bray & Associates did not forget a debt. She had signed their papers with a steady hand four years ago, back when her signature was worth something, back when she was Engineer Thornbury of the Meridian Line and not a woman counting coins over a harbor board in a town whose name sounded like a cough.

She took the contract.

* * *

Harbor Master Odell was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he trimmed himself and badly, and hands that had clearly once known rope and now mostly knew ledgers. He looked Noor up and down when she presented herself in his office above the customs shed — the canvas coat, the engineer's boots worn through at the heel, the eyes that didn't flinch because flinching cost energy she didn't have.

"You've done dredging work," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've done river work." She let him draw his own conclusions. "Dredging is the easy part."

"Easy." He snorted. "The old channel mouth silted shut eight years back when they closed the upper line. Barges drawing more than four feet ground out on the bar now. Consortium shipping wants it open by month's end or they reroute to Corvess and this port dries up and blows away." He said *Consortium* the way people said *weather* — as a force, not an institution. "I can pay two men. You'll take half-share?"

"I'll take full share and do both men's work."

He studied her again, longer this time. "You're running from something."

"I'm running *toward* wages."

That got a short laugh out of him, the kind that ends conversations rather than begins them. He pushed the contract across the desk. She read every line — habit, and hard lesson — signed, and walked down to the water.

The dredge was a barge called the *Patient Magdalen*, which struck Noor as either very hopeful or very bitter naming, and on it waited the other half of her crew: a boy of perhaps fourteen named Tam, all elbows and quick eyes, who turned out to be Odell's sister's son and came with the barge like rust comes with iron.

"You're the engineer?" Tam said, looking at her hands.

"I'm the wages," Noor said. "Show me the winch."

* * *

The work was exactly what she'd promised Odell it would be: brutal, mechanical, and beneath her training in ways she tried not to count. For six days they worked the old channel mouth at low tide, the grab-crane swinging out over the brown water and coming back up dripping mud, silt, broken pilings, the skeletons of small boats, one astonishing quantity of boot leather. Noor ran the winch and read the river while she worked — reading rivers was the one thing they had never been able to take from her, not the bankruptcy, not the hearings, not the gray coats she could feel circling somewhere south of her like weather.

The river here was wrong, and she knew it within the first hour.

Not polluted-wrong. *Shaped*-wrong. A healthy river braids and shifts but keeps its logic; this one had been pinched. The current ran too fast for the width of the channel, the way water runs when something upstream has narrowed its throat. And the silt they were pulling up wasn't ordinary riverbed muck. It lay in layers, fine strata like the pages of a closed book, as if the whole delta had been buried quickly, deliberately, under a single catastrophic deposit.

"Dam upriver," she said to Tam on the third day, nodding at the layered spoil heaps. "Big one. Thirty years ago, maybe more."

Tam shrugged, hauling a chain. "River's always been like this. My gran says it used to be wider." He said it without interest, the way children say things that are actually enormous. "There's towns under there, she says. Under the water past the bend. But gran says lots of things."

Noor looked upstream, where the river came down out of hills she couldn't see the top of, and filed it away next to everything else she couldn't afford to care about.

On the sixth day, the crane brought up the chest.

* * *

She felt it before she saw it — a change in the winch's rhythm, the cable going taut in a way that meant weight, real weight, not mud-weight but mass with edges. She eased the brake. The grab came up out of the water slow, streaming, and hanging half-spilled from its jaws was an iron chest the size of a sea chest, crusted grey-green with thirty years of river, chains still wrapped around it where someone had secured it — or weighted it.

"Drop it on deck," Tam said, excited. "Treasure—"

"It's not treasure, it's ballast." But she swung it in anyway, because curiosity was the one appetite that had survived her poverty intact, and set it down on the planking with a boom that shivered the whole barge.

They broke the chains with a cold chisel and a maul, Noor doing the striking because Tam's arms were noodles and he'd only hurt himself. The crust of river-stone cracked away from the lid in plates. Underneath, the iron was black and sound, uncorroded, sealed better than any salvage had a right to be.

And stamped into the lid, centered, deliberate, was a seal.

Noor knew seals. She had spent four years learning what institutional marks meant, which crests could seize cargo and which could seize *people*. This one she did not know. It was a circle enclosing a stylized river — three lines converging into one — with a crown of small marks around the rim that might have been letters in an alphabet she couldn't read, angular and old-fashioned, nothing like the Consortium's clean geometric stamp or the royal arms of any nation on her atlas.

"Never seen it," Tam said, tracing it with a finger. "It's pretty, though."

Everything about the chest was wrong. Salvage corrodes; this hadn't. River things get scattered; this had been chained and sunk with intent. And the seal was crisp under the crust, as if the chest had been lowered into the water recently — or as if the water itself had been told to leave it alone.

"Odell should see this," Tam said.

"No." The word came out of her faster than she intended. Then, because the boy was watching her with those quick eyes: "Contract says salvage goes to the contractor. That's me. Harbor master gets his percentage when I declare it, and I'll declare it when I know what I'm declaring. Understand?"

Tam understood. Children always understand the shape of a lie long before they understand its content.

They finished the day's dredging. Noor worked with the chest sitting behind her in her peripheral vision the entire time, the way you sit with a stranger on a night train.

* * *

She opened it that night in the customs shed, alone, by lamplight, with a pry bar and no witnesses — which was, she would think later, the moment the whole rest of her life began, though it felt at the time like nothing more than stubbornness and greed.

The lock was good but the hinge pins were the weak point, as hinge pins always are. Two strikes apiece and the lid came up with a groan of breaking corrosion, and the smell that rose out of it was not river-smell. It was oilcloth and ink and something faintly sweet, preservative, the smell of things kept against time.

Inside, packed in oiled wool: papers. Hundreds of them. Bundled, tied with waxed cord, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as the day they'd been sealed. Ledgers with spines numbered in that same angular alphabet. Folded maps, stiff with age. A tin box of pens. And on top of it all, bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, a single volume with a clasp, stamped on its cover with the river-and-crown seal in silver.

Noor sat back on her heels and let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

Someone had packed this chest carefully. Someone had expected it to wait a long time. Someone had sunk it in a river mouth off a drowned channel beside a drowned town, and chained it down, and walked away — or been made to walk away.

She lifted the grey volume. It was heavier than its size suggested. The clasp opened without resistance, as if it had been waiting.

The first page was a title page, and the words on it were in two languages. The second script meant nothing to her. The first was trade-common, the plain tongue every commercial clerk learned, and it read:

**THE PROVISION OF THE VESSIAN LINES AND HEADWATERS. Being the Full Register of Claims, Rights, and Grants appertaining to the Rivers, Rails, and Works of the Nation of Vessia, held in trust for its lawful bearers in perpetuity.**

Below it, in smaller type: *To bear this book is to hold the provision. To hold the provision is to hold the works.*

Noor turned pages with hands that had gone very steady, the way her hands always went steady at the exact moment they shouldn't. Rail schedules. Water rights, listed by tributary, by season, by cubic foot. Deeds of easement. Grant after grant after grant, each bearing signatures and countersignatures, each referencing a nation — *Vessia* — that she had never heard of, that appeared on none of the six atlases she had owned in her life, that was not mentioned in any gazetteer or tariff schedule or treaty annex she had ever filed, and she had filed thousands.

A nation erased so thoroughly that even its absence had been erased.

And threaded through every page, in the margins, in the headers, in the formal language of grants and reversions, ran the clause that made her heart do something complicated against her ribs:

*...and whosoever bears this Provision may present it for satisfaction of any lawful debt, claim, or lien whatsoever, such satisfaction to be rendered in full from the assets herein enumerated.*

Any lawful debt. Any lien. *Whatsoever.*

Four years ago she had signed away her name to Bray & Associates for a sum she could recite to the penny in her sleep — 11,400 crowns, plus interest compounding like a disease. She had run from three cities. She had slept in coal sheds. She had taken a dredging contract in a dying port because the alternative was worse, and the gray coats were patient, and Mr. Quill — she thought of his name the way you think of a tooth that aches — did not lose track of things.

This book, if it was what it claimed to be, could erase all of it. One presentation. One signature. *Satisfaction to be rendered in full.*

She sat in the lamplight in the empty customs shed with a dead nation's ledger in her lap and let herself want it, openly, for one full minute — the way you let yourself look at a fire when you're cold. Eleven thousand four hundred crowns. Her name back. Her license back. A train under her hands again, a line under her feet, the road open north.

Then she heard footsteps on the dock boards outside, and the lamp flame leaned, and she closed the Provision and wrapped it in the oilcloth and got it into her coat before the door opened, because wanting things openly was how you lost them.

* * *

It was Odell, lantern in hand, come to check on his barge. He found Noor sitting among the opened bundles with the lamplight on her face, and whatever he saw in her expression made him stop in the doorway.

"Chest," he said. Not a question. Word traveled fast in Sallow Bend; it had probably reached him before she'd finished prying the hinges.

"Salvage," Noor said. "Contracted to me."

"Where is it?"

"Contents are declared tomorrow. Percentage per the contract." She kept her voice level, bored, the voice of a woman with nothing to hide, and she saw it land wrong — saw Odell's eyes go past her to the empty space where the chest stood, to the wool packing strewn on the floor, to the coat that hung oddly heavy on her frame.

"That's not how it's done here," Odell said slowly. "Salvage of that size gets reported to the Consortium office. Shipping authority. Anything raised out of the old channel gets logged, especially—" He stopped.

"Especially what?"

He didn't answer. He crossed the shed instead, and picked up the chest itself — easier for him than for her, being built like a dock crane — and turned it toward the light. And Noor watched the color go out of the harbor master's face the way tide goes out of a flat: fast, total, leaving everything exposed.

His thumb was resting on the seal. The three lines converging into one.

"Where," Odell said, and his voice had changed entirely, gone quiet and careful, a man walking on ice, "did you find this."

"In your channel. Under forty years of silt. Harbor master, it's a chest of old papers, it's—"

"*Put it back.*"

The words came out of him like something wrenched loose. He set the chest down as if it burned him and wiped his palms on his coat, and he was looking at Noor now with something that was not anger. It was fear, and worse than fear, it was fear *for* her, which she had not been on the receiving end of in four years and had forgotten how to refuse.

"Mr. Odell—"

"You listen to me." He stepped close, and the lantern threw his shadow huge up the wall behind him. "Thirty-one years ago there was a country upriver. You won't find it written anywhere, don't bother looking, I looked when I was young and stupid. There was a war nobody calls a war, and then there were new maps, and then there was a dam, and everything that country was went into the water or into the ground. And the men who did it—" his voice dropped further, "—they keep lists. Of names. Of things that surface. Boats that go out to the old channel and come back with something in them do not come back to this harbor twice. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Noor understood. She understood with the specific, sick clarity of a woman who has just realized the thing in her coat is not salvage but a lit fuse.

"The Consortium doesn't care about a chest of papers," she said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"They care about *that* seal more than they care about guns, girl. I don't know why. I don't want to know why. Knowing why is how people end up in the water." He straightened, and for a moment the soft harbor master was gone and something older and harder looked out of his face. "Tomorrow morning you take that chest out on the barge and you sink it in deep water past the bar, and you tell me what was in it, and I will forget it, and we will call your contract complete and paid. That is the offer. It expires at dawn."

He took up his lantern and went to the door, and paused there without turning around.

"And Miss Thornbury — whoever sold you that contract knew the shallows were being dredged this week. Think about who benefits from a stranger pulling things out of that river with her own hands, in front of witnesses."

The door closed behind him.

Noor stood alone in the customs shed with the lamp burning low and the Provision heavy against her ribs, and outside, far down the black river, she could hear the water moving — patient, indifferent, certain to be paid — carrying something down from the drowned hills that had finally, after thirty years, decided to surface.
