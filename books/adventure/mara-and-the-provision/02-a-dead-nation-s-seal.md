## 2. A Dead Nation's Seal

The lockkeeper's cottage sat where the river narrowed to a throat of stone, wedged between the last working lock gate and a lean-to stacked with tarps that had gone gray as fish skin. Noor carried the chest the whole way from Sallow Bend on her back, and by the time she reached the door she'd stopped noticing its weight — which was itself a kind of warning. A body learns to forget what it can't put down.

She knocked with her boot because her hands were full.

The man who answered was old in the way river men got old: bent at the middle, hands like knotted rope, eyes the color of water that had seen everything float past and kept none of it. He looked at her, then at the chest, and something moved behind his face that she would spend the rest of this story trying to name.

"You're the salvage woman," he said.

"Noor Thornbury." She shifted the chest's weight. "Harbor master said you'd know what this mark is."

"Did he." The lockkeeper stepped back from his own doorway, which told her more than any answer would have. "Did he now."

Inside smelled of lamp oil, dried eel, and decades. A single room, a stove, a wall of pegs hung with tools worn to the shape of one man's grip. He didn't offer her a chair. He stood by the stove with his back to the warmth, arms folded, and stared at the chest as if it were a dog he suspected of having bitten someone once.

"Set it down," he said. "On the mat. Not the table."

Noor set it down. The iron rang against the floorboards, a dull, deep note, like a bell drowned in mud.

The seal sat on the lid's face, cast into the metal itself rather than stamped on — whoever had made this chest had expected it to outlast the paint of lesser marks. A circle within a circle. Inside the inner ring, a design she'd taken for decoration: three wavy lines crossed by a single straight one, and above them, small and precise, an open hand.

"Well?" she said.

The old man crouched, slowly, joints cracking like green wood. He did not touch the seal. He held his palm above it, close enough to feel heat if there had been any, and for a long moment the only sound in the room was the river working at the lock gates outside.

"That's Vessia," he said finally.

"Vessia."

"The nation that isn't." He rose again, slower than he'd gone down. "You're too young to remember it. Maybe your grandmother's maps had it. Mine did. I burned mine."

"Burned them why?"

"Because everyone did." He said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd say *because it rained*. "Thirty years ago there was a country upriver. Good farmland, better water, and rail lines that ran through the mountains like thread through cloth. Then came the Consortium, and the Provision Wars, or whatever they called them in the papers — and when it was over, Vessia wasn't on any map anymore. Not conquered. *Erased.* There's a difference, girl, and it matters. Conquered land gets a new name on the map. Erased land gets no map at all."

Noor looked at the seal. Three wavy lines — a river. The straight line across them — a bridge, or a dam, or a rail line; she was an engineer, she knew how few shapes separated those things. And the open hand above, palm out.

"What does the hand mean?"

"Provision," the old man said. "Vessia's founding law. The idea that water and passage belong to everyone the river touches, and that some things are held in trust, not owned. Pretty idea. It's what got them killed." He turned away from the chest as though it had begun to stink. "Take it back out to the shallows and sink it. Deeper than you found it."

"I salvaged it under contract. The harbor master—"

"The harbor master," the lockkeeper said, "would sell his mother's teeth for pipe money, and has. Listen to me. I've worked this lock forty-one years. I've seen what comes down this river when people start pulling things up that were meant to stay down. In 'ninety-one a dredge crew brought up a crate of Vessian flags off the sunken ferry at Corver's Reach. Sold them for rags. Within a month two of them were dead — accidents, the papers said — and the third left the river country entirely and was never heard from again. That was just *flags*, Thornbury. Cloth and dye." He pointed at the chest with his chin. "That is a nation's seal, cast in iron, sealed shut, and sunk where nobody would dredge. Somebody went to great expense and greater trouble to make sure that thing stayed lost. You want to know why? Open it, and find out. But don't come crying to me after, and don't bring it back here."

He opened the door. Outside, the light had gone the color of pewter, and the river ran dark and quick through the gates.

"Throw it back," he said. "That's my counsel, freely given, and worth every coin you paid for it."

* * *

She didn't throw it back.

Not out of courage — she wants that understood, whoever reads this, if anyone ever does. Courage would have been dropping it in the deepest hole the river owned and walking away light. What she felt instead was smaller and uglier and more honest: she was four hundred crowns in debt to men who broke fingers for interest, her engineer's license was a joke in three provinces, and the only thing she owned outright was a coat with a torn lining. An unclaimed chest of unknown contents was the first property the world had offered her in six years. She was not going to give it back to a river.

But she didn't open it in the lockkeeper's house either. Some animal part of her had registered the old man's fear and filed it where fear belongs — in the body, not the head. So she walked a mile downstream along the towpath, past the last tumbledown of the town, to a ruined tollhouse half-swallowed by blackberry bramble, and she built a small fire in its broken hearth because the evening was cold, and she set the chest on the flagstones, and she looked at it for a long time while the light died.

Then she pried it open.

The lock was good work — a three-lever mechanism, salt-welded after thirty years underwater — but she was an engineer, and engineers are the people other people call when a thing must be opened that does not wish to be. She used two drift pins and a pry bar from her kit, and it took her the better part of an hour, and when the lid finally gave with a groan of tearing rust, the sound seemed louder than it could possibly have been, carrying across the water the way sounds do at dusk.

Inside, packed in wool gone hard as slate, was paper.

A great deal of paper. Ledgers, folios, documents bound in ribbon that had once been blue and was now the gray of drowned sky. And on top, wrapped separately in oilcloth, a single volume — long, narrow, bound in leather the color of dried blood, with brass corners and a clasp shaped like the same open hand.

Noor wiped the mud from her hands on her trousers before she touched it. She didn't examine the instinct then. Later she would understand it: some part of her already knew she was handling scripture.

The oilcloth came away. The first page was heavy stock, lettered in a formal hand, ink faded but stubborn:

*THE PROVISION OF THE RIVER VESS AND ALL ITS WATERS, HEADS, AND PASSAGES.*

*Being the true and perpetual record of claim, trust, and grant, executed under the seal of the Provisional Republic of Vessia, and binding upon all parties, successors, and assigns, in perpetuity.*

Below that, columns. Page after page after page of columns, and Noor — who had spent her life reading schedules, freight manifests, water tables, gauge clearances — read them the way other people read letters from home.

The closed lines. Every one of them. The mountain corridors the Consortium had shuttered thirty years ago and never reopened, the passes that appeared on no commercial map, the tunnels her own guild called *the dead veins* — here they were itemized, surveyed, granted: *the Line of the Nine Locks, with right-of-way entire; the High Pass trunk and its ventilation galleries; the Vess Gorge incline, both cables...* And threaded through the rail grants, inseparable from them, the water rights. Headwaters. Springs. The sources of the river itself, named by names she'd never heard and described in terms so precise she could have staked them in the field from the text alone.

It was, she realized slowly, a deed. Not to land — to *flow*. To everything that moved through the erased country: trains and water alike, the two currencies of the river world, held in one document under one seal.

And at the back of the volume, a final section. A clause headed in larger letters, with blank lines ruled beneath it for signatures — lines that remained empty, waiting, after thirty years underwater.

*And should the bearer of this Provision present it to any party holding claim, lien, or charge against the bearer's person or estate, such party shall render satisfaction in full, for the Provision supersedes all instruments whatsoever, being prior in law and higher in covenant.*

Noor read it three times.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with a strange detachment, as though watching someone else's tremor from a distance — the engineer observing a gauge creep into the red.

Four hundred crowns. That was the number that lived in her chest like a hook in a fish's jaw. Four hundred crowns to Quill & Associates, Factors and Debt Recovery, accruing interest at a rate designed by men who found arithmetic beautiful only when it hurt someone. Four hundred crowns since the valley, since the closure she'd signed, since the flood she'd certified as *within tolerances* — and every collection agent, every threat, every door slammed in her face traced back to that figure like cracks tracing back to a flaw in the pour.

And this book said: *render satisfaction in full.*

She sat back on her heels in the ruined tollhouse and let herself imagine it, properly imagine it, the way she hadn't allowed herself to imagine anything good in years. Walking into Quill's office — she'd never seen it, but she'd built it in her mind many times, all mahogany and menace — and laying this volume on the desk. Watching the courteous monsters read it. Watching the number die. Walking out with nothing behind her for the first time in six years. Her name clean. Her license — well, maybe not her license, maybe that was gone forever, but her *debt*, the debt was the thing with teeth, the debt was the leash—

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the water a night bird called, three notes, descending.

And underneath the temptation, quiet as silt settling, another thought: *why is it here?*

Nations do not lose their founding deeds. They hide them. They smuggle them out in the bottoms of boats and sew them into coat linings and bury them in lead boxes, and they do it because the paper matters more than the armies — because a deed can sleep for a century and wake up still true. Someone had packed this chest with loving care, sealed it, weighted it, and sunk it in the shallows of a minor port town, and the lockkeeper had gone white at the sight of the seal, and somewhere upstream a country that did not exist was still missing whatever this was.

If she sold it — even to erase her own debt — she'd be selling it to the people who had done the erasing. The thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome, and she pushed it down where she pushed everything else she couldn't afford to feel.

She was still holding the Provision when she heard the hoofbeats.

Two horses, maybe three, coming along the towpath from the direction of town at a trot too brisk for the hour. Noor moved the way she'd learned to move in bad years: fast, silent, decisions made below the level of thought. The Provision went back into its oilcloth, the oilcloth into her coat — the coat with the torn lining, the one possession the world couldn't repossess because it wasn't worth taking — and the chest's remaining papers she swept back under the wool and closed the lid as best she could with the broken lock hanging loose.

Then she was out the back of the tollhouse and into the bramble, thorns finding every seam of her clothes, and she lay flat in the wet weeds and watched the road.

Three riders. Gray coats, cut plain and expensive, the kind of plain that costs more than ornament. They drew up at the tollhouse and dismounted, and one of them raised a lantern, and the light swung across the hearth where her fire was still burning, still warm, still hers.

One of them crouched and touched the coals. Even at this distance Noor saw him rub ash between his fingers, deliberate as a priest.

"They were here," he said. His voice carried flat across the water. "Recently. Find the chest."

Noor pressed herself into the mud of the riverbank, the Provision hard against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and understood with perfect clarity that the harbor master had sold her name before the sun went down — and that whatever she had pulled out of the shallows, she was now the only person in the world standing between it and the men who had sunk it there.

The lantern light swept the brambles.

She began to crawl.
