## 3. Men in Gray Coats

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Noor knew every legitimate hour for knocking in Sallow Bend. Creditors knocked at dawn, when they hoped to catch you abed and ashamed. The harbormaster's runners knocked at midday, when the tide offices were open and a signature could be witnessed. Drunks knocked at all hours but had no patience for doors that didn't answer. This knock — three measured raps, evenly spaced, at half past nine in the evening, when the lamplighter was still working his way down Rope Street — belonged to none of those categories.

She put down her mug of chicory and did not move.

The room above the chandlery was one room, and it held almost nothing: a cot, a washstand, a trunk with a false bottom full of tools she'd sworn she'd never need again, and a window overlooking the yard where the chandlery stored its barrels of pitch. She'd chosen it for the window. Everyone in Sallow Bend chose their rooms for something — proximity to the taproom, distance from the flood line, a landlady who didn't ask questions. Noor had chosen hers for the roof.

Three more raps. Then a voice, mild as milk: "Engineer Thornbury. We know you're awake. The lamp behind you is lit."

She looked over her shoulder. The curtain was thin. Of course it was thin. Everything in Sallow Bend was thin — the walls, the ice on the river in a hard winter, the difference between being poor and being nothing at all.

"I'm not an engineer," she said through the door. "Not anymore."

"May we come in regardless?"

* * *

There were two of them, and they wore gray coats of a cut she recognized without knowing where from — long, plain, buttoned to the throat, the kind of coat that cost more than it looked like it cost. The taller one had a face like a ledger page: ruled lines, nothing written on it yet. The shorter one carried a leather document case strapped across his chest like a bandolier, and it was the case, not the man, that made Noor's stomach drop. She had spent eleven years around men who carried things in cases. Survey instruments went in cases. Contracts went in cases. Things that could end a career or a town went in cases.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said, not offering chairs because there was only one.

"Mr. Corve," said the tall one, touching his hat brim without removing it. "This is Mr. Sennet. We represent the Consortium Office of Reclamation." He let the name sit in the room like smoke. "You filed a salvage declaration yesterday. Item: one iron chest, sealed, recovered from the shallows off the old ferry pilings. Declaration number 4-4-1-9."

"I remember my own paperwork."

"We'd like to see the item."

Noor leaned against the wall and folded her arms, and made herself look bored, which took work, because her heart was going like a shunting engine. "Then you'll want the harbormaster's office. Salvage items are impounded pending adjudication. That's the law here. I don't make the law."

"The item isn't in the impound shed, Engineer." Corve's voice stayed pleasant. "We checked this afternoon. The shed holds two crates of drowned rope and a church bell. No chest."

"Then someone's stolen it, and you should be talking to them."

Sennet opened his document case. From it he took a single sheet of paper, unfolded it with two hands as though it might bruise, and read aloud. "Declaration 4-4-1-9, salvage rights assigned to N. Thornbury, engineer, late of the Meridian & Coast Line. Witnessed by Oskar Brant, harbormaster." He folded it again. "Mr. Brant was very helpful. He described the chest in detail. He described your reaction to it in greater detail."

The room got very small. Noor kept her face still by thinking about bearings, about the specific friction of a worn axle box, about anything mechanical and dull. *Oskar sold me.* Not even for much, probably. A few crowns. Or not for money at all — for the pleasure of watching a stranger with gray coats walk into the chandlery and know that Oskar Brant mattered to somebody important. In a town like Sallow Bend, that was worth more than crowns.

"He's a talkative man," she said. "Talks to gulls if they'll listen."

"He talked to us." Corve stepped closer, and now the pleasantness thinned enough to see what was under it. "Here is what happens next, Engineer, and I'll say it once so we can both stop pretending. The Consortium has an interest in certain historical materials. That interest predates you and will outlast you. You found an item. The item belongs to the Consortium under the Provisional Claims Act of — " he paused, savoring it, or perhaps just checking — "of some years ago. If you produce the item, you will be compensated. Fairly. Generously, even. Your debts are a matter of public record; we've read them. All of them." His eyes flicked down and up, taking her measure the way an assessor takes the measure of a building slated for demolition. "If you do not produce the item, then you are concealing Consortium property, and the matter leaves Sallow Bend, and you will not enjoy where it goes."

"And if I say I threw it back?"

"Then you're a fool who dredged up a sealed chest, declared it, and threw it back into the river rather than sell it." Corve smiled for the first time, and the smile was the worst thing in the room. "We wouldn't believe you either."

They left a card on the table — heavy stock, embossed, the Consortium crest stamped in gray ink that looked wet — and told her to present herself at the customs house by noon tomorrow. Then they went down the stairs, and she heard the chandlery's dog, which barked at everything, bark at nothing at all as they passed, and go silent again, quick and low, the way animals go quiet around certain kinds of people.

Noor sat on the cot until the chicory went cold.

* * *

The chest wasn't in the impound shed. It wasn't in her room. It was wrapped in tarpaulin and sunk in twelve feet of black water under the rotting pilings of the old ferry landing, tied to a chain she'd bought with the last of her honest money, and she went to it now, in the dark, because she needed to hold it and think.

The river at night made its slow sounds against the pilings — a knock, a shift, a sigh of current. She crouched on the planking with a hand-lamp shuttered to a coin of light and hauled the chain up link by link, and the chest came dripping into the world again, and there was the seal under the crusted mud, patient as ever: a circle, a bridge, a star above the span.

A dead nation's mark. The lockkeeper had spat when he said the name. *Vessia. There's no such place, girl. There hasn't been for thirty years. Throw it back.*

And inside it, the thing she had read twice by candlelight and then stopped reading because reading it felt like standing too close to a rail edge: the Provision. A ledger bound in gray leather, its first page granting — in language so old-fashioned it took her a moment to parse — absolute claim to the closed lines, the closed stations, the closed waters of Vessia, to the bearer and the bearer's assigns, in perpetuity, until dissolved.

*Until dissolved.* Even the paper admitted it could be undone. But only by its bearer, and only in a way she hadn't yet understood.

Her debts came to four hundred and eighty crowns, plus interest compounding like rust. Her disgrace was a matter of public record in three provinces. And somewhere in a leather case on a tall man's chest was a sheet of paper proving that two men in gray coats already knew her name, her debt, and her face.

She should give it to them. That was the sane arithmetic. Take their compensation, sign whatever they put in front of her, walk away with maybe enough to start over in a country where nobody had heard of the Kestrel Valley closure. That was what the old Noor would have done — the Noor who signed closure orders because the numbers said to, because the men above her said to, because it was easier to believe the numbers than to look at the valley.

But the numbers had never added up right since the day she'd pulled this chest out of the shallows, and she knew it, sitting on the rotten planks with the seal cold under her thumb.

"They'll take it and burn it," she said aloud, to the river. "Whatever it is. They'll burn it, and the water stays theirs, and the lines stay shut, and some other engineer signs the next order."

The river said nothing. Rivers were good at that.

* * *

She spent the rest of the night becoming a different kind of thief.

First the coat. Her good coat, the heavy wool one with the deep pockets — she turned it inside out on the floor, unpicked the lining along the left side with a sailmaker's needle, and sewed the Provision flat against her ribs, between wool and lining, in stitches tight enough to survive a drowning. It changed how the coat hung. Nobody would notice unless they knew her. She practiced moving in it until the stiffness stopped feeling like anything.

Then the engine.

Every river town kept its dead locomotives the way graveyards keep their stones — upright, ignored, slowly returning to the ground. Sallow Bend's lay on a stub of track behind the salt works: a Meridian & Coast Class 9 tank engine, decommissioned six years back, officially stripped, unofficially intact because the scrappers' quote had been too low to insult and too high to accept. Noor had walked past it forty times in her months in Sallow Bend and each time had looked at it the way a surgeon looks at a wound she isn't allowed to treat.

Now she treated it.

By midnight she had the cab door open and a shuttered lamp hanging from the handrail. The firebox was cold and scaled but sound. The boiler — she crawled along the barrel with the lamp in her teeth, tapping, listening, tapping — rang true where it mattered and dull in two places she marked with chalk. The gauge glasses were cracked. The regulator packing was dry as bone and would leak steam like a whispering informer, but a whispering engine could still pull. The wheels turned when she barred them over, grinding, protesting, alive. Someone had kept oil in the axle boxes. Some other ghost had loved this machine before her.

She worked until her shoulders burned and her fingers were black to the wrist, and the whole time the fear rode her like a passenger, quiet, gripping the handrail, and she let it ride, because fear was fuel if you stoked it right. She'd learned that in the Kestrel Valley too, though she'd used it for worse purposes then.

Toward the gray hour before dawn, she sat in the cab with the fire lit low behind her, drinking water from a jug, and made her list. Water. Bread. The tools from the trunk's false bottom. The chalk marks said the boiler would hold pressure for a day, maybe two, if she didn't ask for speed. The old mainline ran north out of Sallow Bend along the river toward the headwaters — closed, rusted, unpatrolled, or patrolled so rarely that the patrols themselves had become rumors. Closed lines. The Provision claimed them. She was, she realized with a laugh that came out wrong, about to travel on property she technically owned.

*Until dissolved,* said the paper against her ribs.

* * *

Noon came to the customs house, and Noor Thornbury did not.

She watched instead from the loft of the salt works, through a gap in the boards, as the gray coats stood in the yard below at half past eleven, checking pocket watches, and then as a third man joined them — not gray-coated, but wearing the harbormaster's Sunday jacket, Oskar Brant himself, gesturing up the street toward her lodging, eager as a man showing off a garden. She watched Corve listen to him. She watched Corve turn and look down Rope Street, unhurried, a man consulting a map he had already memorized.

Then she watched him send Sennet running toward the chandlery, and watched him himself walk — walk, not run, because men like Corve never ran — toward the salt works siding.

He knew. Not everything, but enough. The engine was the only way out of Sallow Bend that didn't use the road or the river, and she'd been seen buying chain and oil and bread in quantities that told their own story. Oskar had sold her name; the town had sold her route; towns always did.

Well. Let him come to the siding. Let him watch.

She dropped the firebox damper wide and fed it the salt-soaked scrap wood the works kept for kindling, and the fire roared up hungry, and the gauge needle began its slow climb out of the dead zone. Steam crept into the pipes with a sound like the building clearing its throat after years of silence. Below, Corve stopped at the edge of the yard and shaded his eyes against the noon glare, and even at that distance she saw him understand, and admired, and hated, and reached inside his coat.

Noor put her hand on the regulator.

"One chance," she called down, her voice cracking across the yard like a coupler taking strain. "You want the chest? It's at the bottom of the river under the old ferry pilings, chained down. Bring divers. Bring your paper. See how much your Act of some-years-ago is worth underwater."

Corve said something to the man beside him, quietly. The man started back up the street at a run.

"It's a lie, of course," she called, because he should know, because she wanted him to know. "But you'll check anyway. You're paper men. You can't not."

The gauge touched the line she'd chalked on the glass. Enough. Not comfortable, not safe, but enough — enough for the yard, the points, the northbound stub, the long rusted grade along the river. She thought of the lockkeeper spitting the name *Vessia*. She thought of Tam-less, mapless, debt-ridden years stretching ahead of her if she climbed down and surrendered, and of a sealed bridge-and-star, and of a valley she had once helped drown because the numbers said to.

The numbers didn't say to anymore.

"Sorry, old girl," she murmured to the engine, and hauled the regulator open.

The Class 9 moved the way the dead move when they decide not to be — lurching, shrieking, shedding six years of rust in orange sheets — and gathered itself, and rolled. Behind her, in the yard, a pistol cracked and something sang off the cab roof, and Noor laughed, high and wild, a sound she didn't recognize as her own. The points at the yard mouth were set for the scrap spur; she took them anyway, wheels screaming sideways through the throw, and the engine straightened onto the old northbound main with a jolt that nearly put her through the gauge glass.

Then Sallow Bend fell away behind her — the chandlery, the impound shed, the customs house, Oskar Brant's Sunday jacket, four hundred and eighty crowns of debt, the whole small drowning world of it — and the rails ran north beside the brown river, toward the hills, toward the closed country, toward whatever waited at the headwaters behind thirty years of silence.

The Provision pressed warm against her ribs with every beat of her heart, like a second pulse.

Let them come after her. Let them bring their gray coats and their acts and their pocket watches. She was done being salvage.

Somewhere ahead, past the flooded stations and the patrolled banks and the passes that would close with the snow, a dead nation was waiting for someone to read its mail — and Noor Thornbury, disgraced, debt-ridden, and free for the first time in eleven years, was carrying it.
