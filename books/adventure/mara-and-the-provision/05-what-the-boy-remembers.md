## 5. What the Boy Remembers

The river folk took them in the way water takes a stone — without ceremony, without question, closing over the intrusion as though it had always been part of the current.

Ilya Voss had spent eleven years learning not to trust shelter. A signal box with a stove and a locked door, that was shelter. A roof you could see the sky through, that was shelter. But a warm room full of strangers, in a stilt-house settlement called Ferrow's Landing where the floorboards breathed with the tide of the river beneath them — that was exposure. That was a map of himself drawn by other hands.

He sat near the wall anyway, because old habits were load-bearing, and watched Noor Thornbury do what she did best: make herself useful until suspicion drowned.

She'd been at it for two days now. The woman who'd fled Sallow Bend on a dead engine with a nation's deed sewn into her coat had arrived at the landing soaked, shivering, and penniless, and within an hour of drying off she was under a neighbor's barge re-packing a leaking stuffing box with tarred oakum, sleeves rolled to the elbow, talking shaft alignment with a fisherman twice her width. By nightfall three households had offered her work. By the second morning, no one asked anymore why they'd come.

It was, Ilya thought, a kind of genius he couldn't imitate. He could draw a city from memory down to its drain grates. He could not make a room want him in it.

"You're staring at my soup like it owes you money."

Maren Ferrow, matriarch of the landing, set the bowl in front of him without waiting for an answer. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a mooring piling, with forearms that had hauled nets since before the Consortium existed as anything but a shipping syndicate's letterhead. She'd taken one look at their papers — Ilya's forged transit cards, good enough for checkpoints, useless against a grandmother's eye — and said nothing at all, which was more frightening than any question.

"Thank you," Ilya said.

"Mm." She sat across from him, which she never did, and lowered her voice to the register women of her age used when the children were asleep but not reliably so. "Your woman fixed the Hollis barge's shaft in an afternoon. Been grinding for a season. You don't ask payment."

"She asks payment," Ilya said carefully. "Passage upstream. That's all we—"

"I know what you're after. Everyone knows. The engineer and the map-man walking the closed line, asking after the headwaters like pilgrims." Maren turned her bowl slowly on the table, not eating. "I'll tell you what I told the last ones who came through asking about the source, nine years back. Two men, gray coats under their river clothes. They went up. Nothing came back down."

Ilya kept his face still. "We're not Consortium."

"No," Maren agreed. "They were. That's the thing about the source, cartographer. It doesn't care what you are. It only cares what you carry." Her eyes went, briefly, to the shape of Noor's coat hanging by the door — heavier than a coat should be, and both of them knew it. "And it cares what you're willing to lose."

Before Ilya could decide whether that was warning or prophecy, the door banged open and the cold came in with the boy.

* * *

Tam was eight years old, maybe nine — born after the erasure, which on the landing meant born into a world where Vessia had never been, where the maps showed Consortium water from the mountains to the delta and the word *Vessian* meant nothing except an accent some old people still slipped into. He was small and quick and permanently damp, with hair the color of river clay and a habit of arriving exactly where adults didn't want him.

He arrived now between Ilya and the window, dripping, holding a stick he'd whittled into something that was either a boat or a weapon.

"Grandmam says you're a drawer," Tam announced.

"A cartographer."

"That's a drawer." The boy wedged himself onto the bench uninvited. "Draw me the river."

"Which river?"

"All of it. From here to where it starts."

Ilya looked at him. Children asked for things whole; that was their nature. Adults knew better than to want the entire river — they wanted stretches, claims, crossings. He found a scrap of paper in his coat, a corner trimmed off a working sheet, and a stub of pencil worn to the length of his thumb.

He drew the channel first, freehand, the way it actually ran — the oxbow below Ferrow's Landing, the narrows, the drowned station country where the dam's raising had put whole towns under brown water thirty years ago. His hand did this without consulting him. Eleven years of exile and the river still lived in his fingers.

Then, because the boy was watching and would notice, he drew the rest. Upstream. The gorge country. And at the top, where every official map ended in a consortium crest and a printed legend reading CLOSED WATERS — PROVISION AUTHORITY, he drew the mountains, and the valley behind them, and marked the dam with a small square, and wrote nothing at all beside it.

Tam studied the drawing with the ferocious concentration of the young, lips moving.

"This bit's wrong," he said finally, tapping the upper gorge.

Ilya went very still. "Wrong how?"

"The bends." The boy traced the channel with his stick-finger. "There's a song. My gran's gran taught it to the little kids, and they teach it to us. It goes—" and he sang, tuneless and certain, in a language Ilya had not heard spoken aloud in eleven years:

*"Sarnu veth, sarnu kai,*
*el mara dolen ai.*
*Bend at the willow, bend at the stone,*
*the water goes home, the water goes home."*

The words were Vessian. Old dialect — river dialect, the lowland speech, softened and slurred the way children soften everything. *Sarnu veth, sarnu kai.* Bend at the willow, bend at the stone. And the third line — *el mara dolen ai* — the water remembers its way home.

Ilya's pencil snapped in his hand.

He didn't hear the rest of the verse. He heard his mother singing it, hanging wash in the wind off the reservoir country, before there was a reservoir. He heard schoolchildren in Dolen Kai chanting it in skipping rhythm while Consortium surveyors measured their street for the closure notices. He heard it the way you hear a bell that stopped ringing decades ago and is suddenly, impossibly, ringing again in a stilt-house on a river that officially had no memory of ever being anyone's home.

"Where," Ilya said, and his voice came out wrong, scraped, "did you learn that?"

Tam shrugged, baffled by the question. "Everybody knows it. It's a counting song. You sing it when you jump rope. There's another one for skipping stones." He frowned at Ilya's expression. "Did I say it wrong?"

"No." Ilya pressed the broken pencil halves flat on the table. "No, you said it perfectly."

Because that was the thing — the unbearable, impossible thing. The boy hadn't said it wrong. He'd said it *right*, with the vowel lengths intact, with the dialect's soft *d* where the Consortium's standardized tongue used a hard one. A boy born years after the last Vessian school was shuttered, whose grandmother's grandmother might have been Vessian or might simply have learned the rhyme from someone who was — and the language had come down to him whole, folded inside a children's game, passed hand to hand like contraband too small for anyone to notice carrying.

They burned the books. Ilya had watched the burnings from a surveyor's window, had signed the closure orders that sent archivists to prison, had told himself the maps he smuggled out in his own head were enough, that a people reduced to one man's memory was a people preserved. He had believed, in the way the guilty believe convenient things, that erasure worked.

But you cannot purge a skipping rhyme. You cannot raid a playground. The Consortium had seized the records, dammed the waters, redrew every chart from the mountains to the sea — and all the while the actual archive had been walking around barefoot, jumping rope, singing *the water goes home* in perfect river dialect, generation after generation, and no one with a gray coat had thought to listen.

*Ilya realized memory survived the purge in children's rhymes.*

He put his head in his hands, right there at Maren Ferrow's table, and wept — quietly, horribly, the way men weep who have forgotten how, in short graceless bursts he couldn't stop. Tam watched him with wide eyes. Somewhere behind them the floorboards creaked; Maren had come back and stood saying nothing, arms folded, having apparently known all along what the map-man was.

"It's just a song," Tam said finally, uneasy.

"It's a map," Ilya managed. "You've been singing a map your whole life and nobody told you."

* * *

That evening the landing's children gathered on the walkways between the stilt-houses, as they did every evening when the lamps were lit, and played the games their parents had played and their grandparents before them. Ilya stood at the rail of Maren's porch with a lamp turned low and listened like a man taking dictation from ghosts.

There was the rope-jumping song, which counted the bends of the upper gorge — *sarnu veth, sarnu kai* — and got every one of them right, including the double bend at the willow flats that appeared on no surviving chart Ilya owned. There was a clapping game whose nonsense syllables resolved, once you heard them as language instead of noise, into place names: *Kessa-tol, Kessa-dan, Kessa-mara-undervai* — the three locks of the old Kessa flight, demolished in the first year of the closure. There was a rhyme for choosing who was "it" that named, in order, the villages of the drowned valley, ending always with the same word — *dolenvai*, the farthest village, the last one chosen, the one that went under first.

"They don't know what they're saying," Maren said beside him. Not unkindly. "Words their great-grandmothers carried. You keep a thing long enough without knowing what it is, it gets heavy anyway. That's why they hold onto it. Children know weight even when they don't know meaning."

"The Consortium doesn't know either," Ilya said. "That's the only reason it survived. They catalogued every book, every ledger, every gravestone inscription in the province. They had forms for confiscating lullabies, I saw the forms. But nobody thought to write down what children say to each other, because nobody thinks children are keeping anything." He laughed, and it hurt. "Thirty years of the most thorough erasure in history, and the whole national memory survived in a jump-rope chant. In *nonsense syllables*. We were never in the archives at all. We were in the playground."

"And now?"

"Now—" He stopped. Below, Tam had finished a round of the rope game and was looking up at the porch, at Ilya, with the particular gravity children deploy when they have decided something about an adult.

The boy climbed the ladder, sat down on the boards, and said, "You cried earlier. About the song."

"Yes."

"Is it a sad song?"

Ilya considered lying, decided the boy had earned better. "It's a sad song that became a happy one. It's about water finding its way home after a long time away."

Tam nodded slowly, processing this, then delivered the blow with the flat accuracy of childhood: "My gran says our family came from up past the drowned places. Before. She says we used to be somebody." He picked at a splinter in the decking. "The schoolbooks say there wasn't anybody. Just empty water country that the Provision people improved. Was my gran lying?"

The question hung there, small and enormous. On the far shore a patrol lamp swept the bank — routine, probably, the Consortium's nightly breathing — and went dark again.

"No," Ilya said. "Your gran wasn't lying. There was a country there. There were songs and laws and a way of measuring land by where the water wanted to go, and there were people — your people — who kept all of it so well that even after everything, it's still coming out of your mouth in rhymes." He knelt so they were level. "They tried to erase it, Tam. They nearly succeeded. But they made one mistake."

"What mistake?"

"They thought memory lived in paper. Paper burns." Ilya touched the boy's chest, lightly, over the heart. "This doesn't."

Tam looked down at the hand, then up, and something shifted in his face — the moment, Ilya would think later, when a child stops being merely a child and becomes a keeper. "Teach me what the words mean," he said. "All of them. The rope song, the clapping one, all of it. If it's a map, I want to read it."

Behind them, unheard until now, Noor stood in the doorway with the lamplight behind her, and Ilya saw her hand press flat against her coat — against the ledger sleeping inside it, the Provision, the paper claim to everything the rhymes described. Paper and breath, facing each other across a doorway.

"We'll need paper," Ilya said hoarsely. "A lot of it. And ink. And time."

"We leave when the Hollis barge loads," Noor said quietly. "Two days. Convoy going upstream." Her eyes hadn't left Tam. "Can he be ready?"

"He was born ready," Ilya said. "He just didn't know what for."

Below them, the children started the rope song again, voices rising thin and clear over the black water — *bend at the willow, bend at the stone* — and far upstream, where the river came down out of the closed country, the dark held its silence, and waited.
