## 6. Debt Collectors on the Night Train

The night train out of Sallow Bend was never meant to carry passengers. It was a freight run, three cars of ore and one of salted fish, and the conductor — a woman with a face like a closed ledger — agreed to take Noor as far as Kettering Junction for the price of not asking why a railroad engineer was riding in the brake car with her boots braced against a crate of pickled herring.

Noor paid in the only coin she had left: she checked the axles.

She knelt between the wheels with a lantern while the train took on water, running her thumb along the bearing housings the way she'd done ten thousand times before, back when her signature meant something and men tipped their hats to it. The left rear axle on the third car was running hot. She flagged the fireman, told him to ease off through the Kettering grade or they'd all arrive as smoke, and he looked at her with the particular suspicion workingmen reserve for anyone who knows too much about their trade without explaining how.

"You an engineer?" he asked.

"I used to be."

"Used to be." He spat into the ballast. "That's like saying you used to be honest."

She let him have it. Honesty was a currency she'd been spending down for three years now, and the account was nearly empty. In its place she carried sixty pounds of iron chest lid's worth of secrets sewn into the lining of her coat — no, not the coat; the ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth and stitched flat against her ribs where a man might once have kept a Bible. Every jolt of the rails pressed it against her like a second heartbeat.

*Provision of Vessia.* Grant of absolute claim to the closed lines, the headwaters, the water rights feeding them. A dead nation's last will and testament, made out to whoever held the paper.

Made out, at present, to a disgraced engineer with eleven debts and a name that got doors shut in her face from Sallow Bend to the sea.

The train pulled out at midnight. Noor sat in the brake car with the hatch cracked to let the coal smoke out and watched the river country slide past in the dark — black water, blacker reeds, the occasional lamp of a lockhouse burning low. Somewhere behind her, the harbor master was telling a story to men in gray coats. She'd seen it happen even as she ran: the way his eyes went to her retreating back, then away, then to the agents again, calculating. He hadn't sold her out of malice. He'd sold her out of arithmetic. That was worse. Malice you could hate. Arithmetic just *was*.

She dozed against the brake housing and dreamed of water going where she told it to go, and woke when the train began to climb.

Kettering Junction was a scar of a town built where two rail lines crossed and neither had anything to say to the other anymore. The eastbound had been closed since the Consolidation — that was what they called it now, the Consolidation, as if the swallowing of forty small nations and their railways were a medical procedure — and its tracks rusted into the weeds like veins gone cold. But the northbound still ran, and the junction still had a station house, a telegraph office, and a hotel with a dining room that served mutton to traveling men at all hours.

Noor meant to change trains and keep moving. She got as far as the platform.

He was standing under the station clock.

She knew him before she knew his name, which was itself a kind of horror, because she'd never met him and yet some animal part of her brain catalogued him instantly as *danger*, the way it would catalogue a snake on a path. He was a small man, neat as a pin, wearing a gray coat cut close and a gray hat set at an angle of perfect modesty. He held a leather case in both hands in front of him, like a choirboy with a hymnal. His face was pleasant. His eyes did all the work, and they were doing it now, moving across the disembarking crowd with the unhurried thoroughness of a man reading a document line by line.

They stopped on her.

He smiled. It was a good smile. It reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse.

"Miss Thornbury," he said, crossing the platform, and his voice carried just far enough and no farther. "Or may I call you Engineer? I understand you were quite accomplished, once."

Noor's hand found the strap of her bag. Behind the smile, behind the pleasant voice, her mind was running the numbers the way it ran axle temperatures: exits, distances, weights. Platform. Two ways off — the stairs down to the road, the gap between the freight shed and the water tower. Her legs were strong but she'd been sleeping in brake cars for two nights. He was small but there were two more men by the ticket window wearing gray coats cut from the same cloth, and they had begun, very casually, to drift.

"And you are?" she said.

"Quill. Mr. Quill." He inclined his head a degree, no more. "Of the Consortium Office of Reclamation. Though 'reclamation' rather oversells us. We mostly file things." He opened the leather case and produced a folder, square and gray, tied with gray string. "I've been looking forward to this conversation for some time. May we sit? The dining room keeps late hours, and I find negotiations go better with mutton."

"I'm not negotiating."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You're stalling. Which is a form of negotiation, only slower. Come. Sit. If you'd rather run, run — but I'll tell you honestly, Miss Thornbury, that the next town has fewer exits than this one, and the one after that has none at all. I am not your fastest pursuer. I am merely your most patient one."

There was nothing threatening in it. That was the trick of him, she realized later. There was never anything threatening in it. He spoke the way a timetable spoke: here is the schedule, here are the stops, the train does not hate you, the train simply runs.

She sat. Because her legs decided to, mostly, and because a corner table in a lit room with witnesses was better ground than a dark platform with two drifting men in gray coats.

* * *

The dining room smelled of mutton fat and lamp oil. Quill ordered for both of them without asking her — "the stew, two, and whatever tea isn't criminal" — and folded his hands on the tablecloth like a man settling in for a sermon he'd given many times.

"Do you know what I admire about engineers?" he said.

"Their humility."

"Their relationship to debt." He smiled again. "Every bridge is a loan against gravity. Every tunnel, a mortgage on the mountain. You people live your whole professional lives owing the earth money, and paying it back in precision. It's the closest thing to honesty I encounter in my work." He untied the gray string. "Which brings me to yours."

He laid papers on the table one by one, squaring each edge with the one before, a little ritual of order. "Eleven creditors. Principal, interest, penalties, consolidated twice — badly, I might add; whoever brokered your second consolidation was robbing you with both hands. Total exposure as of this morning: four thousand, two hundred and six marks, fourteen pence." He tapped the final sheet. "Your signature appears on all of them. A lovely signature, incidentally. Decisive downstrokes. You don't hesitate when you commit ink to paper. I've always thought a person's handwriting tells me more than their interrogation would."

"What do you want, Quill?"

"The same thing every man at this table wants, when he sits across from a debtor. Payment." He leaned back. "But I'm not here for the debt. The debt is a door handle. I'm here for what's behind the door."

The tea came. He thanked the girl by name — Hesta, apparently; he knew everyone's name, that was part of the machinery of him — and waited until she'd gone.

"On the ninth of this month," he said, "a salvage dredge working the Sallow Bend shallows brought up an iron chest. The harbor master logged it. The chest was subsequently reported missing, along with one Noor Thornbury, engineer, discharged, indebted, and — until recently — entirely unremarkable." He sipped his tea. "I want to be clear that I bear you no ill will for taking it. Salvage law is salvage law. Finders, keepers, all the rest of the folk wisdom we use to dress up greed. What I object to is the *item*. Certain documents are not property, Miss Thornbury. They are load-bearing walls. Remove one and the building comes down on everyone, including the person holding the crowbar."

"And if I say I threw it back?"

"Then I'd say you're lying, and I'd say it without any pleasure, because I dislike calling people liars. It's so final." He turned a paper toward her. It was a sketch — a drawing of her, done from description, good enough to sting. Beneath it, a list of towns. "You boarded a freight at midnight. Freight doesn't stop at passenger stations. It stops at junctions, yards, and water towers. There are nine junctions between here and the northern passes. My colleagues are covering seven of them. I took this one because I hoped we could spare each other the tedium."

Noor looked at the sketch of herself for a long moment. Then she picked up her tea and drank it, slowly, and put it down, and made herself meet his eyes.

"There is no chest," she said. "There's a lid. I kept the lid because the iron's good and I'm poor. The inside was empty except for rot and a dead rat. Whatever your Consortium lost in that river, the river's had thirty years to eat it."

Quill listened to the whole lie with his head tilted slightly, like a man listening to a piano and hearing the one string that's flat.

"That's very good," he said warmly. "Specific. The rat is a nice touch — detail is the skeleton of a convincing lie. But here's my difficulty." He drew another paper from the case. "Three days ago, an old lockkeeper named Aldous Wren was visited by persons unknown and asked what he knew about Vessian seals. He told them a story about a young engineer who came to his lockhouse, and about what she chose to do instead of throwing a certain chest back in the river. Mr. Wren is eighty-one years old and frightened of everything, which makes him truthful, and truthful people are so rare these days that when we find one we build entire operations around them."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree. Noor kept her hands still around the teacup and thought: *Aldous. You gentle old fool. I hope they didn't hurt you.*

"He's fine," Quill said, reading her face with that awful mildness. "We don't hurt people, Miss Thornbury. Hurting people creates paperwork, and paperwork is sacred to us. He's fine. He simply talks. Most people talk, eventually. It's nothing personal — talking is just what pressure does to a container." He folded his hands again. "So. Let us skip to the part where we both admit the Provision exists, and discuss what happens next."

The word went through her like current through a rail. *Provision.* He'd said it plainly, in a public dining room, over mutton, and the word sat there on the tablecloth between them glowing like something radioactive.

"I don't know what that is," she said.

"It's a ledger," Quill said. "It's a grant. It is, depending on your taste for poetry, either a dead nation's suicide note or its resurrection charter. It grants its bearer claim to the closed lines — all of them — and to the headwaters, and to the water rights feeding every river from the mountains to the sea. Do you understand what that means? Not the words. The *weight*." He leaned forward, and for the first time something moved behind the pleasantness — hunger, maybe, or awe; with him it was hard to tell the difference. "Whoever holds that paper holds the taps for half a continent. Every mill, every city cistern, every barge channel, every field of wheat between here and the coast. Nations have gone to war for less and called it glory. The Consortium erased a country rather than let that paper circulate. And now it's sitting in a railway hotel, across the table from a woman who owes me four thousand marks, and I am offering—" he paused, savoring it, "—to make her rich."

"How rich."

"Debtless. That's the foundation. Four thousand two hundred six marks, forgiven, tonight, with signatures. Above that: a salary. A position. The Consortium employs engineers — someone has to maintain the closed lines, survey the headwaters, administer the water. You'd want for nothing. You'd be *somebody* again. Your name would open doors instead of closing them." He slid a fresh sheet across the table. Blank, except for a heading in gray ink: TERMS OF ACQUISITION. "All it requires is the document, and your signature at the bottom. One honest stroke. I'm told you don't hesitate when you commit ink to paper."

Noor looked at the blank page. And here was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that would keep her awake in brake cars and marsh camps for months: for three full seconds, she wanted it.

Not the money. The *unburdening*. Four thousand marks lifting off her shoulders like floodwater receding. Her name spoken without the sour aftertaste. To walk into a yard again and have men touch their hats. To be, once more, the person her father had believed he was raising. Three seconds of wanting, bright and total as a struck match.

Then she felt the ledger against her ribs, and beneath the oilcloth and the stitching she felt — absurdly, impossibly — it seemed to feel her back. A dead nation's claim, warm as a swallowed coal. And she understood, with the clarity of a signal lamp coming into focus down a dark line, that the paper was worth exactly as much as she was weak.

If she signed, it was only paper. If she refused, it was a country.

"Not interested," she said.

Quill sighed, genuinely, and gathered his papers with small precise movements, squaring the edges, retying the gray string. "May I tell you something professional to professional? Engineer to engineer?" He didn't wait for permission. "I've read your file. All of it. The Merrow Valley closure. Your testimony, your discharge, the inquiry. And I want you to know that I don't think you're a bad engineer. I think you were a good engineer who was asked to do arithmetic instead of ethics, and you did the arithmetic beautifully, and it broke you. That's not a condemnation. Half the Consortium is built on people like you." He stood, settled his coat, set his hat at its angle of modest perfection. "The offer stands. It will stand in Kettering, and in Harrow Weir, and at the foot of the mountains, however high you climb. Offers from the Consortium are like water, Miss Thornbury. They find their level, and they do not evaporate."

He put a coin on the table for the tea — exact change, of course — and then paused, one hand on the chair back, and looked at her with something that was almost kindness.

"One more thing, and then I'll let you enjoy your mutton. The boy."

The room went very quiet around her. "What boy?"

"The river folk you've been traveling with. The one who sings." Quill's voice stayed gentle, conversational, terrible. "Children born after the Consolidation aren't supposed to remember Vessia. The songs were collected. The names were changed. And yet here's a child singing in a dead language, and here you are, and here's a certain cartographer whose file I could recite from memory, and suddenly a simple document recovery begins to look like—" he searched for the word and found it with relish— "*sedition*. I can make sedition go away, Miss Thornbury. That's also on the terms sheet. Or I can let it grow. Sedition is like water too." He touched his hat brim. "Think of the boy when you're deciding. He has such a clear voice."

He left. The dining room noise resumed around her like water closing over a dropped stone.

* * *

She didn't take the northbound. She walked the dark platform to where the freight shed met the water tower, checked the sightlines twice, and slipped out along the closed eastbound line, walking the ties into the dark with the ledger pressing its slow heartbeat against her ribs.

Behind her, Kettering Junction burned its few lamps against the night. Ahead, the closed rails ran straight as a ruled line into hills she couldn't see yet, toward a source she was beginning to understand had been dammed for reasons much older and colder than her debt.

Quill had offered to buy the paper. He'd offered to buy her. And he'd mentioned Tam in the same breath as the terms sheet, which meant he already knew the shape of her — knew that the fastest way to move Noor Thornbury was no longer through her debts.

Somewhere ahead on those dead rails, an exiled cartographer was waiting in a signal box with maps of a country that no longer existed. She walked faster.

The Provision rode against her heart, and for the first time she understood that she wasn't carrying it.

It was carrying her.
