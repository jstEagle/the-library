## 7. Upstream

The barge convoy left Halloway Lock at dawn, three flat-bottomed hulls lashed bow to stern and pushing a raft of coal barges ahead of them like a dowry. Noor watched the lock gates swing shut behind them from beneath a tarpaulin stretched over stacked crates of salted fish, and she did not breathe easy until the last of Halloway's chimneys had sunk below the tree line.

"Coast is clear," she said.

"It was never not clear," Tam said. "You just like saying it."

She lifted the tarpaulin an inch and looked at him. He was sitting cross-legged on a crate with his knees drawn up, watching the banks slide past with the unblinking attention of a heron. Eight years old and already he had the river in him — she'd seen it in the way he read the water, calling the shallows before Ilya's charts confirmed them, naming the drowned fences under the surface by the way the current broke over them.

"Keep your voice down anyway," she said. "Sound carries on water like it carries on rails."

Ilya was at the stern end of their hiding place, wedged between two crates with his maps spread across his knees despite the fact that he knew every bend of this channel by heart. He drew anyway. He was always drawing. His pencil moved in small, quick strokes, annotating a chart that was thirty years out of date with corrections only he could see: *cutbank eroded here; towpath gone; new shoal since the closure.* A man keeping faith with a country that no longer existed, one pencil line at a time.

"The patrol boats run the commercial channel twice a day," he said without looking up. "Midmorning and midafternoon. Gray hulls, twin screws, a searchlight even in daylight because they want you to see it coming. We'll hear them before we see them — they run their engines loud on purpose. Intimidation through noise."

"You know a lot about their boats."

"I surveyed for them." He said it flatly, the way a man says a thing he has said so many times it has worn smooth as river stone. "You learn the machinery of the people who own you."

Noor settled back against the crates. The Provision rode against her ribs where she'd sewn it into the lining of her coat, a stiff rectangular weight that she was beginning to suspect she would feel for the rest of her life, the way an amputee feels a hand. She'd taken to sleeping on her side with her arm curled over it. She told herself it was to keep it safe. She suspected it was closer to the way you hold something you're afraid will be taken, or afraid you'll use.

The channel widened as they went upstream. The river folk worked their boats around them without comment — a deckhand who passed them hard bread and hard cheese and asked no questions; a woman mending netting two barges over who caught Tam's eye and taught him, by silent demonstration, how to splice a frayed line. Nobody asked their names. On the commercial channel, Noor was learning, anonymity was a courtesy the poor extended to one another.

* * *

The first patrol came at midmorning, exactly as Ilya had promised.

They heard it first: a low diesel growl upstream, too steady to be a workboat. Ilya's pencil stopped. Tam went still in a way that made Noor's chest tighten — a child shouldn't know how to go still like that, all at once, like a snuffed lamp.

"Under," she breathed, and they pulled the tarpaulin down and burrowed into the gap between the fish crates and the coal sacks, Noor last, dragging the canvas closed behind them. The world shrank to smells — brine, coal dust, tar — and the sound of her own heart and, faintly, Tam breathing beside her. She put her hand on his shoulder. He didn't flinch. He leaned into it, a small warm weight, and she thought: *this is a bad idea, caring about anything on this river,* and then she thought: *too late.*

The gray boat came alongside close enough that its wake rocked the barges. Boots on deck above them. A voice, bored and official: "Convoy manifest."

The convoy master's voice, unhurried: "Coal, salt fish, timber, general freight. Same as Tuesday."

A pause. Paper rustling. Through a gap in the tarpaulin Noor could see a sliver of gray coat, a black boot, the butt of a rifle slung carelessly backward.

"Any stowaways?"

"On my boats? They'd have to pay me for the privilege."

Someone laughed — the wrong someone, Noor thought, laughing at a Consortium man was a good way to get your whole convoy searched — but the boots moved on. The searchlight swept across the tarpaulin once, a dull orange glow through the canvas, and slid away. The diesel growl receded upstream, hunting slower prey or none at all.

They stayed under for ten more minutes anyway. When they emerged, Tam was grinning like they'd won something.

"They didn't even look," he whispered.

"They looked exactly as much as they needed to," Ilya said grimly. "That's what frightens me. They weren't searching. They were reminding us they can."

* * *

In the long afternoon, with the banks sliding past — willow and alder, ruined towpaths, the occasional stump of a drowned fence standing out of the water like a grave marker — Noor took out the Provision.

Not the whole ledger. She'd learned better than to unfold it in the open. But there was a page she'd torn free the night before they fled Sallow Bend, a single leaf she kept folded in her breast pocket, and it was this she smoothed now on her knee. It was a schedule of headwater allocations, columns of figures in a clerk's precise hand, and above the columns, in larger script, the opening line of the provision itself:

*Let the waters be counted, for the counting is the keeping.*

Tam edged closer. He'd been watching the paper since Halloway Lock, she realized. Watching it the way other children watched sweets.

"That's Vessian," he said.

"How do you know that?"

"I don't. I just — know." He frowned, frustrated with himself. "It sounds like the songs. The ones I sing. Mama says I got them off a grandmother but Mama never heard them sung anywhere else."

Noor looked at the line again. *Let the waters be counted.* She'd read it a hundred times and never heard it. Now, hearing Tam say the words in his accent — the vowels rounded differently than hers, older somehow — she heard it. It wasn't bureaucratic language at all. It was almost a verse.

"Do you want to learn to read it?" she asked.

He looked up sharply, the way a dog looks up when it thinks it's been offered food. "Truly?"

"Truly. But we do it quiet, and we do it slow, and if a patrol comes, this is fish inventory." She tapped the columns. "Numbers are numbers. Nobody arrests a boy for learning arithmetic."

So they began. She started with the letters she could point to in the words themselves — the great looping *W* of *waters*, the doubled *o* of *counting*. Tam learned the way he did everything on the river: all at once, greedily, with his whole body leaning into it. Within an hour he was picking out *the* and *is* and *keeping*, sounding them under his breath, and when he got *keeping* right on the first try Noor felt something crack open in her chest that she hadn't planned on.

"There," she said, gruffer than she meant to. "You're literate. Don't let it go to your head."

"What does the whole thing say? The whole book?"

Noor hesitated. Across the crates, Ilya had stopped drawing. He was watching them both with an expression she couldn't parse — hunger and grief and something like fear braided together.

"The whole book says who owns the water," she said finally. "Every line, every headwater, every gate and weir from the mountains to the sea. And it says whoever holds the book holds all of it."

"Who holds it now?"

"You're looking at her."

Tam considered this with the terrifying seriousness of children. "Then you own the river?"

"No." She folded the page away. "Owning things is what got everyone into this mess. Ask me again in a month."

But he kept looking at her with those eyes, and she understood that she had just become, in some permanent way, larger than she'd been that morning. To a boy born after the erasure, she was the woman who held the dead nation's paper. She didn't want the weight of it. She took it anyway, the way she'd taken the salvage contract, the way she'd taken everything — because refusing wasn't something the river ever really offered.

* * *

They moored that night in a backwater off the main channel, tied up among a dozen river boats whose families slept aboard, cooksmoke mingling over the water. An old woman brought them stew without being asked and refused payment with a look that ended the discussion. Tam ate two bowls and fell asleep mid-sentence, mid-word, his head dropping against Noor's arm as though it had always belonged there.

She sat very still, letting him sleep, and stared at the firelight on the water.

"He sings in his sleep sometimes," Ilya said quietly. He'd come to sit near her, his maps rolled and bound. "Vessian. Did you know that?"

"No."

"Last night, in the marsh camp. I couldn't sleep and I heard him singing — a harvest song. *The reapers walk the water-meadows, the scythe is silver, the sheaf is gold.*" He spoke the translation slowly, tasting it. "My mother sang that. In Vessia. Before." He was quiet a moment. "Thirty years, Noor. They burned the registries. They burned the churches' records. They renamed every town on every signpost. And the song survives in a boy who was born nine years after the last map was destroyed, because some grandmother hummed it while she worked and he caught it like you catch a cold."

"Memory's stubborn."

"Memory is the only thing they couldn't dam." He looked at her, firelit, and for once there was no bitterness in his face, only a kind of exhausted wonder. "Do you understand what you're carrying? Not the claim. Anyone could forge a claim. You're carrying proof. A ledger in the hand that wrote it, with the seal, with the allocations, with the names of every keeper of every gate. When we reach the source—"

"If we reach the source."

"*When.*" He said it with a cartographer's certainty, the certainty of a man who has measured the distance himself. "When we reach it, that book is going to tell us where they put the records. Every vault, every spillway archive. Thirty years of a people, sealed in concrete. And you—" he nodded at her coat, at the stiff shape against her ribs, "—you're the key that opens it."

"And Quill wants the key."

"Quill wants the key," Ilya agreed. "Which is why we travel by night and hide by day and trust no one past the third barge." He paused. "Except each other. And the boy."

"Especially the boy," Noor said. "He's the only one of us who sleeps properly."

Ilya smiled — a rare event, brief and crooked — and went to check the mooring lines. Noor sat with Tam's warm weight against her arm and listened to the river move in the dark, and found, to her considerable alarm, that she was humming something under her breath. A tune she didn't know. A tune with rounded vowels in it somewhere, maybe, or maybe just the shape of one.

* * *

The second day upstream, the channel narrowed and the patrols thickened.

They saw two gray boats before noon, running opposite sweeps, and once — worst — a patrol launch moored at a toll station on the bank, its crew lazily inspecting a line of waiting barges. Their convoy master took them wide around the station through a side cut that added hours, muttering about lost tolls, and Noor lay under the tarpaulin with Tam pressed beside her and listened to the boy's heart beat fast against her shoulder until the danger passed.

"Teach me more," he whispered afterward, as soon as it was safe to speak. "While we wait. Teach me the next part."

So she unfolded the torn page again, low between their bodies, and they worked through the allocation columns — *gate seven, spring rise, forty measures to the lower fields* — and Tam, reading haltingly, suddenly stopped.

"This number." He pointed. "Forty. That's in the song. *Forty gates and forty keepers, forty springs beneath the stone.*"

Noor went cold. "Say that again."

"*Forty gates and forty keepers.*" He sang it, soft, uncertain of the melody but sure of the words. "Mama's version only goes to twenty. I always thought she forgot the rest."

Ilya was suddenly there, crouched over them, his eyes moving from the column to the boy's mouth as though the song were written on it. "How many gates does the ledger list?" he demanded of Noor.

She counted quickly, finger racing down the margin. "Forty-one. Forty active, one struck through. Marked *closed by order*."

"Which one?"

"The last one. Gate forty-one." She traced the entry. "Headwater source. Allocation suspended indefinitely."

For a long moment nobody spoke. The barges creaked. Somewhere downstream a patrol engine growled, far off, like thunder that hadn't decided yet.

"That's the dam," Ilya said at last, very quietly. "That's the entry they wrote when they built it. The Provision lists the source as a gate, Noor. Which means the Provision doesn't just grant claim to the water." His eyes came up to hers, and she saw the whole architecture of it land in him at once, terrible and bright. "It grants claim to the dam. To what's inside it. The bearer doesn't need to breach anything. The bearer has the legal right to open the gate."

"Legal rights haven't slowed them down so far."

"No. But it means when we stand in front of that concrete, we won't be criminals breaking a wall." Something fierce kindled in his face, the first fire she'd seen in it since the signal box. "We'll be keepers opening a gate that was always ours."

Tam looked between them, sensing the change in the air the way he sensed shoals. "Gate forty-one," he said, and began, very softly, to sing. "*Forty gates and forty keepers, and the last gate keeps the spring.*"

Noor pulled the tarpaulin tighter around the three of them and held the torn page against the light, and downstream, faint but steady, the engines of the gray boats went on hunting.
