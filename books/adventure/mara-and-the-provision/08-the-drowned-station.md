## 8. The Drowned Station

I had told myself I would know it when we passed. I had told myself a great many things in thirty years, and most of them had been lies of the comfortable kind — the kind you tell so you can keep walking.

The barge *Meridian's Daughter* rode low in the water, wedged between a load of timber tarpaulined against the drizzle and a hold full of salted fish that smelled like the whole river country's apology. Noor stood at the rail with her hood up, the Provision sewn flat against her ribs beneath a coat that had seen better decades. Tam was below, asleep in the gap between crates where he'd made himself a nest out of rope ends and an old sailcloth, and I envied him that with an ache I hadn't felt since I was young enough to sleep on command.

We were three days out from the marsh channels, riding the commercial convoy because it was the only traffic the Consortium patrols didn't board twice. The river here ran wide and slow and wrong. That was the word for it. Wrong. A river this wide should have been loud. It should have shouldered its banks and argued with every bend. Instead it moved like something sedated, glassy and deep, holding the gray sky without a single ripple of complaint.

Because it wasn't a river anymore. It was a reservoir wearing a river's name.

"Voss." Noor didn't look at me. She'd learned to read my silences the way I read contour lines — by what was missing from them. "You've gone stiff as a survey stake. What is it?"

"Nothing," I said.

She turned then. She had a way of turning that made lying feel like a waste of both our time. "Nothing," she repeated, flat as a datum.

I looked downriver instead of answering, which was cowardice dressed as navigation, and that was when I saw it.

* * *

It rose out of the water maybe two hundred yards off our starboard bow: a brick tower, truncated, its upper third sheared away not by demolition but by design — by a waterline drawn in some Consortium office by men who had never stood in the rain. The tower's remaining windows stared at us, dark and patient, and around its base the current made small obedient eddies, combing the drowned streets below like fingers through hair.

Kestrel Hollow Station. Junction 14 on the old Vessian Eastern Line. The tower had been the signal box. My father had climbed those stairs six mornings out of seven for nineteen years, and I had climbed them with him on school holidays, learning to set the levers, learning that a railway was just a promise written in steel and kept on schedule.

"You knew," Noor said quietly. It wasn't a question. She'd been watching me watch the tower.

"I knew we'd pass it." My voice came out strange, distant, like sound carried over water. "I didn't know it would look like—" I stopped. There was no honest end to that sentence. Like nothing. Like a wound that had been bandaged with a lake.

The convoy slid past. From the deck we could see more of it now: rooftops just under the surface, pale rectangles where roads used to be, the black comb of a chimney standing clear of the water like a man drowning who refuses to stop waving. A church spire leaned at an angle no architect intended. And there, half-submerged but unmistakable to anyone who had ever walked its platform, the station itself — the long low shed with its arched windows, its iron columns furred green with thirty years of silt, its clock face still mounted above the entrance, hands stopped at ten minutes past four.

They had left the clock. That was the detail that broke something loose in my chest. They had raised the water over an entire town and left the clock running until it stopped, or perhaps they'd stopped it themselves, some clerk's final entry in the ledger of Kestrel Hollow: *ceased, 4:10, cause — provision.*

"My father worked that signal box," I said.

Noor said nothing. She was good at that. Most people fill silence like they're afraid it will report them; Noor let silence work for her.

"The town was called Kestrel Hollow. Population, last census before the closure, eleven thousand four hundred and six." I recited it the way you recite anything you've been forced to memorize — precisely, and with hatred. "There were orchards on the south slope. Cherries. The soil was wrong for wheat and right for almost everything else, and the people knew it, and they were proud of it, and none of that mattered."

"Why did they flood it?"

And there it was. The question I had been carrying upriver like ballast, waiting for someone to ask it, dreading and praying for it in equal measure.

"Because of the dam," I said. "Or rather, the dam needed a demonstration. You can't close a headwater valley without showing the downstream towns what obedience looks like. Kestrel Hollow sat in the path of the new reservoir line. The Consortium offered relocation compensation at rates calculated to insult, and when the town refused — when they petitioned, when they filed grievances, when they did everything the old law said they could do — the Consortium discovered that the old law had been dissolved along with the nation." I watched the chimney slide astern. "They gave them nine days. Then they closed the upstream gates early. Not all of them. Just enough. Just a taste, to loosen the holdouts. And then, on the ninth day—"

I couldn't finish it. Thirty years, and I still couldn't finish it.

"They came at night," Tam said.

I spun. He was standing at the rail beside us, barefoot, sleep-creased, his eyes fixed on the drowned station with an expression no child should have available to them. "How long have you been there?"

"A while." He shrugged, unrepentant. "My gran sang about it. *Nine days and the water came, nine days and the water came.* I thought it was just a song." He frowned at the clock face, fading behind us now. "It's a true one, isn't it."

"It's a true one," I said.

* * *

That evening we ate in the lee of the timber stack — hard bread, smoked fish, tea brewed from needles because proper tea cost more than the convoy's insurance. The barge crew kept their distance from us; the captain had been paid not to ask questions, and he honored the arrangement with the diligence of a man whose questions might otherwise cost him his license. Rain ticked on the tarpaulin. Somewhere ahead, unseen, the dam waited at the top of the world with its vaults sealed into the spillways, though only Noor and I knew that part, and neither of us had said it aloud since the marshes.

Tam fell asleep mid-sentence, the way children do, as if consciousness were a door he could simply walk out of. Noor pulled the sailcloth over him and settled back against the crates, and for a while we listened to the water slap the hull.

"You surveyed it," she said.

Not a question either. She'd had three days to assemble it — the way I flinched at reservoirs, the precision of my knowledge, the pre-erasure maps I'd produced from a hidden sleeve of oilcloth back in the signal box like a magician producing doves. Ilya Voss, exiled cartographer, keeper of the closed lines. Of course she'd done the arithmetic. She was an engineer. Arithmetic was her native tongue.

"Yes," I said.

"Tell me."

"It's not a short story."

"We're on a barge going eight kilometers an hour toward the most guarded structure in the river country." She tore a piece of bread and handed me half. "I've got time."

So I told her.

"In the last year of Vessia, I was twenty-six years old. Junior surveyor, Eastern District, and good at it — good enough that the Ministry gave me the Kestrel Hollow watershed assessment. An honor, they called it. A career-making posting." I turned the bread over in my fingers without eating it. "What I didn't understand — what none of us junior men understood — was that the assessment wasn't a study. It was a verdict looking for a signature. The Consortium had already bought the ministries. Our directorate answered to a commission that answered to men whose names never appeared on any document I was allowed to see. My job was to walk the valley and produce numbers, and the numbers had already been decided. All I had to do was find them."

"And you did."

"I found them beautifully." The self-loathing in it was old and smooth, worn like a river stone. "I mapped the flood line. I calculated displacement figures — eleven thousand four hundred and six souls, minus the ones the compensation tables classified as 'non-resident,' which was a fiction I wrote myself, if you want the truth of it. I identified the orchard soils as 'marginal agricultural value.' Marginal. I stood in a cherry orchard in June with the fruit heavy on the branches and wrote the word *marginal* in my field book, and I got a commendation." I finally looked at her. "Six months later they closed the gates. Nine days, Noor. I had nine days' warning, because I'd signed the schedule too, and I did nothing. I told myself there was nothing to be done. I told myself the numbers would have found their shape with or without me. I told myself I was keeping my head down so I could help later. And then the water came, and everyone I might have helped later was under it, and I ran west with my maps sewn into my coat—"

I stopped. Blinked. Realized my hands were shaking.

"—which I notice is exactly what you did in Sallow Bend," I finished. "Sewn into your coat. Funny how the same trick keeps working."

Noor was quiet for a long moment. The rain thickened, then eased.

"You could have told me earlier," she said at last.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because you look at me like I'm useful," I said. "And I wanted to keep being looked at that way for as long as possible. Because the moment you knew, you'd have to decide whether a man who measured a valley for its own drowning deserves to be the one leading you back up to the source. And I didn't want you deciding that. I wanted to arrive first."

"That's not trust, Voss."

"No," I agreed. "It's the opposite of trust. It's what I have instead."

She chewed her bread. Watched the water. When she spoke again, her voice had shifted — not softer, exactly, but leveled, the way she talked about gradients and loads. "When I was twenty-four, I countersigned a closure order on the Marrow Line. There was a viaduct with a cracked pier. The repair estimate came in high, the traffic projections came in low, and I did the math that said abandon the span and reroute. Except the reroute put the line through Alderfen Fen, and the fen villages were promised — in writing, by my office — that the drainage works would protect them. The works were value-engineered. The winter floods took three villages. Nobody drowned, if you count floating bodies as nobody." She brushed crumbs from her knee. "I signed the papers. I told myself the math was sound. The math *was* sound. That's the horror of it — it was sound, and it was monstrous, and both things were true in the same column."

I stared at her. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you've spent three days dying of shame on my behalf, and I'm tired of watching it." Her jaw tightened. "You want absolution? I can't give it. What I can tell you is that the men who built that dam aren't haunted like you are. Quill isn't haunted. The directors aren't haunted. Whatever else we are, you and I are the kind who carry it, and the kind who carry it are the only kind I want standing next to me when we get to that concrete face. So." She tossed me the rest of the bread. "Carry it. But walk."

* * *

Near midnight I couldn't sleep, and went forward to stand my own kind of watch. The rain had stopped. The clouds had torn open along the eastern hills, and moonlight lay on the reservoir like sheet metal.

That was when I saw the bell buoy.

It marked the drowned crossing where the Eastern Line had once run north out of Kestrel Hollow — the Consortium had left navigational markers along the old rights-of-way, a bitter courtesy, charting the graves so commerce wouldn't snag on them. The buoy rocked in our wake, and its bell tolled, slow and irregular, and the sound carried across the flat water the way sound carries over nothing else — clean, mournful, arriving from everywhere at once.

And below us, thirty meters down, if the moonlight and my memory could be believed, the rails still ran. Straight and true through the flooded dark, from the drowned platform to the drowned junction, toward the drowned orchards, the whole steel nervous system of a murdered country still laid out in perfect order, waiting.

Waiting for someone to come back with a map.

I heard bare feet on the deck plates and didn't turn. Tam settled beside me at the rail, hugging his knees, and together we listened to the bell.

"Ilya?" he said, after a long time. "The songs my gran sang. The nine-days one, and the cherry-tree one, and the one about the girl who holds the river's name in her mouth — where did she learn them? She was born after. Same as me."

"I don't know," I said. And then, because the bell was tolling and the moon was on the water and lies had gotten heavy: "But I think I know how we find out. Songs travel the way water does. Downhill. Downstream. Which means somewhere upstream, near the source, near the dam—" I looked at him, at this boy who remembered a country he'd never seen, "—someone kept singing long enough for the songs to reach your gran. Someone up there remembers everything."

Tam nodded slowly, gravely, as if I'd confirmed something he'd suspected all along.

Behind us, from the direction of the stern, a lamp blinked — three short, one long. The convoy's night signal, passed barge to barge from the lead vessel far ahead.

I knew the code. Every man who'd ever worked the Eastern Line knew the code.

*Stop and hold. Inspection party aboard.*

Downriver, a searchlight swept the water, and began to come toward us.
