## 9. Quill's Bargain

The launch *Candor* ran the channel at half-throttle, her hull painted the gray of wet slate, her wake deliberately modest. Mr. Quill stood at the rail with his hands folded behind him and watched the river do what rivers did — carry things.

He had always liked waterways for their honesty. A road could be argued with; a road could be rerouted, renamed, closed on a Tuesday and reopened on a Friday under a different authority. But a river went where it went, and everything floating upon it was going somewhere on purpose. Cargo had destinations. Destinations had owners. Owners had paper. And paper, in the end, was the only country Quill had ever sworn allegiance to.

"Convoy sighted," said the mate, a broad woman named Hesk whose face had been professionally emptied of expression years ago. "Four barges, commercial registry, out of Lower Marn. Towing lashed, running lights proper."

"Registry numbers?"

Hesk read them off a flimsy. Quill listened with his eyes shut, the way other men savored music. Four numbers, four sets of stamps, four little histories of tolls paid and manifests signed. Somewhere in that arithmetic, if the harbor master of Sallow Bend had earned his bribe, was a woman named Noor Thornbury and a document that should not exist.

"Signal them to heave to for inspection," he said. "Routine cargo audit. Use the standard form."

"The standard form, sir?"

"Form Seven-C. *Notice of Interdiction Pending Verification.*" He opened his eyes. "We are not pirates, Hesk. We are auditors. The distinction matters enormously to the people we stop, which is precisely why we maintain it."

* * *

Quill had been with the Consortium twenty-two years, and in that time he had learned one doctrine so thoroughly it had worn grooves into him like water wearing stone: guns make martyrs; paper makes orphans.

It was not an aphorism the Directorate taught openly. It lived instead in the curriculum between the lines, in case studies delivered without commentary. He thought of them sometimes on long runs like this one, the way old soldiers thought of battles.

There had been the uprising in the Kessel coal towns, forty years back. The Consortium had put it down with rifles, efficiently enough, and spent the next thirty years putting down its memory instead. Every grave was a question a child eventually asked. Every veteran was a witness who would not die quickly enough. Violence, Quill had come to understand, was a debt instrument with terrible terms — you suppressed the principal and the interest compounded underground, in kitchens, in songs.

And then there was Vessia.

Vessia had not been put down with rifles. Not primarily. Oh, there had been rifles at the end, when the borders were redrawn and the holdouts in the headwater towns made their stand, but the true work had been clerical. Treaties voided by procedural finding. Deeds reclassified as claims pending review, and reviews pending indefinitely. Birth registers seized as "records of strategic hydrological significance." Rail charters dissolved. Names struck from timetables, from maps, from the standardized curricula of eleven nations. In seven years, Vessia went from being a country to being a discrepancy, and from a discrepancy to being nothing anyone could prove had ever existed.

No mass graves required. Just filing cabinets, and a dam.

That was the part Quill admired, in the way a locksmith admires a fine mechanism even when he is locking a door against someone he might otherwise pity. The Consortium had understood something most governments never learn: people will forgive you almost anything except making them doubt themselves. Take a man's land and he hates you. Take his history and he doubts whether the hating is justified. Doubt is cheaper than bullets and lasts considerably longer.

Which was why the Provision terrified the Directorate more than any army ever had.

A single ledger, properly executed under the old Vessian water law, granting its bearer claim to the closed lines and the headwaters. One piece of paper that, if produced before any tribunal still bound by pre-erasure compacts — and several such tribunals technically still existed, sleeping in treaty annexes no one had bothered to formally kill — could unwind thirty years of settlement. Not with force. With *standing*. The Consortium could shoot an army. It could not shoot a signature.

"You're smiling, sir," Hesk observed.

"I'm reviewing doctrine," Quill said.

* * *

The convoy came about sluggishly, barges wallowing as the tow line slackened. Quill watched through his glass as a deckhand on the lead barge — a heavyset man with a foreman's swagger — raised a lantern and shouted a challenge across the water.

"*Candor*, Consortium Hydrological Authority. You are directed to hold position for manifest verification under Statute Eleven. Noncompliance will be noted."

Noted. That was the beauty of it. Everything was noted. The Consortium rarely needed to punish anyone directly; it needed only to write the note, and let the note travel ahead of the man like weather. A flagged manifest meant no lockkeeper would take his toll. No insurer would cover his hull. No bank would touch his season's earnings. The gun had become unnecessary because the ledger did all the shooting.

The launch drew alongside the lead barge and held there, engines idling, close enough that Quill could see the grain of the deck planking, the tarred rope, the faces of the crew gathering along the rail with the particular wariness of working men who had done nothing wrong and knew that this counted for less than they wished.

He boarded first, as he always did. It was a small theater and he performed it faithfully: coat brushed, gloves clean, voice pitched at the exact register of a man who regretted the inconvenience. Courtesy was not weakness. Courtesy was the uniform of absolute confidence. Men became dangerous when they felt cornered, and no one feels cornered by a polite man holding a clipboard.

"Evening," Quill said to the foreman, producing Form Seven-C and laying it flat on a cargo crate as though it were a guest at table. "Routine audit. I'll need your manifests, crew rolls, and bill of lading for each vessel. My people will walk the holds. You'll be underway within the hour, assuming the paperwork agrees with itself."

"And if it doesn't agree with itself?" the foreman asked.

Quill looked at him with mild, genuine interest, the look of a man being asked whether water was wet. "Then the paperwork and I will have a longer evening than either of us planned."

* * *

The search began at the bow barge and moved aft. Quill did not personally rummage through tar barrels and rice sacks; that was what inspectors were for, and besides, his real work was never in the holds. His real work was in watching faces.

He stationed himself amidships where he could see the whole length of the convoy and let Hesk run the physical sweep while he conducted the other kind. He chatted. He was good at chatting — a talent he had cultivated deliberately, the way a fencer cultivates a wrist. He asked the cook about freight rates on the lower channel. He complimented a young deckhand's knotwork and asked where he'd learned it. He mentioned, idly, to no one in particular, that there were rumors of salvage coming out of Sallow Bend, some story about a chest, and watched the word move through the crew like a current under ice.

Faces told you things cargo couldn't. Most of the crew showed him the flat boredom of men enduring an inspection they'd endured a hundred times. Two showed fear, which was common and meant nothing; half the river had reason to fear an audit. But the cook — a lean woman with river-gray hair — had gone very still when he said *Sallow Bend*, and stillness, in Quill's experience, was the loudest sound a person could make.

He drifted toward her.

"Good coffee," he said, accepting a tin cup he hadn't asked for. People gave him things when they were nervous. It was a reflex worth encouraging. "You run this route long?"

"Eleven years."

"Long time. You'd have seen the channel change, then. Before the closures." He sipped. "They say there was a station upriver once. Drowned when they raised the weir. You remember it?"

Her hands kept moving, wiping down the galley board, but slower now. "I remember water," she said. "Water covers most things up here."

"It does," Quill agreed pleasantly. "That's rather the point of it."

He left her then, because pressing was how you taught people to lie better, and he preferred his witnesses stupid with relief. He filed her away. Stillness at the name Sallow Bend. Stillness at the drowned station. If the Thornbury woman was aboard, the cook either knew it or suspected it, and either way she would spend the next hour deciding whether to sell a stranger to save herself. Quill had found that most people decided correctly, by his standards, given sufficient time and sufficient visible consequence.

"Hold three on the second barge," Hesk reported, appearing at his elbow with her slate. "Rice sacks stacked two deep over a false floor. Cavity's empty, but the tarpaulin over it is fresh. Cut recently, from the inside."

Quill turned that over. An empty cavity with fresh cuts meant cargo recently removed or a person recently hidden — or a person currently elsewhere on the vessel, having relocated the moment the *Candor*'s running lights appeared. Fugitives were rarely stupid twice.

"And the passenger lists?"

"Foreman swears it's crew only. Crew roll shows nine names on the lead barge." Hesk paused, and Quill appreciated that she paused; she had learned that he liked to be handed facts with their edges showing. "I count ten pairs of boots drying on the stern line."

Quill smiled. It was, he knew, a pleasant smile — he had practiced it in a mirror until it stopped being practice. "How careless of someone."

"Orders, sir?"

"We finish the audit properly," Quill said. "Every hold, every locker, every bilge space. Slowly. We are thorough men, Hesk. It's why they pay us." He glanced down the dark length of the channel, upstream, where the water came from — where everything came from, ultimately, though only a handful of living people knew how literally that was true. "And have the launch signal the *Meridian*. Captain Aldous can bring his boat up and sit across the channel at the narrows below the bend. If our tenth pair of boots decides to go over the side tonight, I'd like the river to have a door closed on it."

* * *

While the inspectors worked, Quill retired to the *Candor*'s small cabin and did what he did best, which was read.

His files traveled with him in a locked dispatch case, and he opened it now with the small ceremony he allowed himself — the brass catches, the oiled leather, the neat folders in their precise order. He reread the Thornbury file, though he knew it nearly by heart.

Noor Thornbury. Railroad engineer, formerly of the Meridian Line consortium staff, discharged following the Vale Corren incident. Quill had read the incident report four times, and each time he found it more interesting than scandalous. She had signed a closure order. The closure had flooded a valley village. The engineering had been sound; the paperwork had been immaculate; the only failure had been human, and it had been hers alone to carry, because the men above her had seen the flood coming and arranged for their signatures to be elsewhere. She had taken the fall with a straight back and then, instead of drowning quietly in drink like most of them, had gone into debt salvage and begun hauling other people's losses out of rivers.

That, Quill thought, was the detail that mattered. Not the disgrace — disgrace was common as mud. The direction of travel afterward. She had gone *down*, into the water, into the lost property of the river country. People who went down tended to find things.

And she had found, if the harbor master's information was worth the coin it cost, a chest bearing a seal that had made a grown harbor official go pale. Quill had interviewed the man himself, briefly, in a room that smelled of tar and fear. The man had described the seal three times, and each description matched the others, and each description matched the impression preserved in the Consortium's own restricted archive: the Vessian provision mark, the crossed key and flowing line, last officially recorded thirty-one years ago aboard a records barge that had officially sunk with all documents lost.

Officially sunk. Unofficially salvaged by persons unknown and lost again for three decades, until a broke engineer dredged it out of the shallows like a coin from a wishing well.

Quill closed the folder and sat for a moment with his fingertips steepled.

Here was the thing he understood about the Provision that the Directorate, in its panic, kept forgetting: the document's power was not really legal. Legal power was a fiction maintained by consensus, and the Consortium had spent thirty years dismantling the consensus that Vessia had ever existed. No tribunal would rule for the bearer. No court would hear the claim. On paper — on *their* paper — the Provision was a curiosity.

But paper had a second life that no bureau controlled. Paper circulated. Paper was believed. And somewhere out on this river, in the reeds and the lockhouses and the children's rhymes the archivists had never thought to purge because they were beneath notice, the belief still ran like groundwater. If the Provision surfaced publicly — if a crowd ever saw it, if a lockkeeper read it aloud, if it passed hand to hand the way songs passed hand to hand — then the Consortium's erasure would develop a leak. And leaks, in Quill's long experience, were how drowned countries learned to breathe.

So the orders from the Directorate were unambiguous, and Quill had written half of them himself: recover the document intact. Recover the bearer intact, if practical. Leave no scene. A dead engineer and a burned barge would raise exactly the questions the Consortium had spent thirty years burying. Silence was the product. Everything else was process.

Which meant, Quill reflected, that he would have to be courteous.

* * *

Near midnight, Hesk brought him the night's accounting. The search of the convoy had concluded. Ten crew accounted for, nine names matching. The fresh-cut cavity in hold three empty. The cook's galley searched twice, the second time on Quill's specific instruction, and nothing found but coffee, rice, and a child's wooden whistle that the cook claimed belonged to her nephew.

"A nephew," Quill repeated.

"So she says."

"Is there a boy aboard?"

"No boy listed. No boy found." Hesk hesitated. "Though the third barge carries deck cargo to the ceiling. We'd need six hours to strip it properly."

Quill considered the river outside the porthole, black and patient. Six hours. And somewhere in that hour, a pair of boots going over the side, a swimmer in the dark, a shape reaching the far bank where the reeds started and the law, effectively, ended. Or no swimmer at all, and six hours wasted while the real quarry watched his lanterns from a reed bed and memorized his procedures.

He had been outmaneuvered before. He did not enjoy it, but he respected it, the way he respected weather. What he refused to do was blunder. Blundering men burned barges and created martyrs, and martyrs were just paper that wrote itself.

"Log the audit as complete," he said. "Manifests verified, no irregularities requiring detention. Issue the release form. Apologize to the foreman for the delay — sincerely, Hesk, sincerity costs us nothing and buys more than gunfire ever has."

"And the cavity? The boots?"

"Note them. Note everything. We are not charging anyone tonight." He rose, took his coat from its hook, and settled it across his shoulders with the small pleasure of a man putting on his profession. "We are letting the river think it won. Rivers that think they've won get careless about what they carry."

He stepped out onto the deck. The convoy was already getting underway again, tow lines tightening, lanterns swinging, the great slow animals of commerce resuming their journey upstream toward the narrows, toward the bend, toward whatever waited in the dark water ahead of them. Quill watched the lead barge's stern recede and let his gaze rest on the figure at its rail — a silhouette in a borrowed oilskin, standing too still, watching him back.

He tipped his hat. The silhouette did not move.

"Signal the *Meridian*," he said quietly. "Captain Aldous is to shadow the convoy to the narrows and hold there. And send a wire to the toll city — the gate at the weir is to begin requiring identity papers for all deck passengers, effective tomorrow morning." He smiled at the black water, at the drowned station somewhere beneath it, at the whole patient architecture of the world. "Everyone on that river is going to need to be somebody on paper very soon. Let's see who can't manage it."

Behind him, the wireless crackled awake, already turning his words into the only kind of truth the Consortium had ever feared losing: the kind written down.
