## 10. Overboard

The search lanterns came up the channel an hour before dawn, and Noor knew it was over before she heard the first shout.

She had been sleeping in the hold of the *Merry Adeline*, wedged between bales of raw cotton that smelled of river rot and diesel, with Tam curled against her side and Ilya sitting upright against the hull as he always did, as if sleep were a country he'd been exiled from too. The barge convoy had made good time for three days — six barges lashed in pairs, riding low with grain and timber, sliding down the commercial channel between levees stitched with willow. Three days of pretending to be cargo. Three days of Noor teaching Tam his letters by lamplight, tracing the Provision's looping script on the deck boards while he sounded out words like *headwater* and *claim* and *in perpetuity*, and three days of feeling almost, dangerously safe.

Then the lanterns.

"Patrol launch," Ilya said quietly. He didn't need to look. He'd been listening to engines the way other men listened to music. "Two of them. They're coming up both banks."

Noor pressed her palm flat against the ledger beneath her coat, where it rode sewn into the lining like a second heart. The canvas was stiff there now, worn smooth from her hand's constant return to it. She'd caught herself doing it the way a gambler touches a lucky coin — except this coin could buy back her name or burn down everything the Consortium had built, depending on whose hands held it next.

"Under the cotton," she said. "Both of you. Now."

Tam went without argument. He was good at hiding; he'd grown up doing it. But at the last moment he looked up at her with those too-old eyes and said, "You'll wake me if they find you?"

"They won't find me," Noor lied, and pushed him gently down among the bales.

Ilya paused beside her. He was a long, dry man, all angles and ink stains, and when he spoke his voice was barely a breath. "If they board, don't run. Running confirms. Sit still, look bored, let them think you're a barge-hand." A pause. "You can look bored. You've had practice."

"I've had practice at being hunted, Voss. It's different."

"It isn't." And then he folded himself down into the dark between the bales and vanished, which was its own kind of cartography — a man who knew how to disappear into any landscape, even one made of cotton and fear.

Noor climbed the ladder to the deck and became a stevedore.

The *Merry Adeline*'s real crew were river folk, broad-shouldered and close-mouthed, and they'd taken the captain's fee without asking why two passengers slept below. Noor took a position near the stern rail, hauled a coil of rope onto her shoulder, and stared at nothing with the particular emptiness of a woman who has worked since before light. The sky was going gray-green in the east. Mist stood on the water in columns. Somewhere upstream a lock bell rang once and quit.

The patrol launches came alongside in a wash of searchlight. Consortium gray, twin engines, men in gray coats standing at the rails with carbines slung and clipboards in hand — because that was the thing about the Consortium, Noor had learned. They never came with guns first. They came with paper. The guns were just punctuation.

A loudhailer crackled. "Convoy will hold station. This is a provision inspection under Article Nine. All hands remain visible. All holds will be searched."

Article Nine. Noor had read enough of the Provision by now to know what articles were for. Articles were how you erased a nation politely, one clause at a time.

The boarding party came up the *Adeline*'s side on a rope ladder, four of them, led by a thin officer with a waxed mustache and the weary courtesy of a customs man who has done this ten thousand times and expects to do it ten thousand more. He carried not a weapon but a leather folio, and inside the folio, Noor would have bet her remaining teeth, was a description of her. Height, hair, the scar on her left forearm from a coupling accident eleven years ago. Maybe even the compass — brass-cased, her father's, engraved *N.K.* on the lid. Quill's people were thorough. Quill's people had probably written the manual on thorough.

"Names?" the officer asked the captain, pen already moving.

The captain gave them. River names, honest ones. The pen scratched.

"Holds?"

"Grain forward, timber aft. Cotton midships."

"Cotton." The officer's pen stopped. He looked up, and his gaze drifted along the deck with the mild, terrible patience of a man fishing. "Cotton doesn't move this season. Cotton moves after harvest, and harvest was two months gone." He smiled thinly. "But paperwork is paperwork. We'll have a look anyway."

Noor kept her breathing slow. Kept the rope coil on her shoulder. Below her feet, under three feet of cotton bales, a dead nation's claim to every closed line and headwater in the river country lay sewn into a disgraced engineer's coat, and a boy who sang songs no one had taught him lay curled around the sound of boots overhead.

They started forward. Barge by barge. Hold by hold.

She counted the intervals. Two barges. Three. The searchlights swept the water, found nothing but mist, swept again. On the fourth barge someone kicked open a hatch and the sound carried flat across the water. On the fifth, voices rose — a crewman arguing about his cargo, getting nowhere.

Then the officer came aboard the *Adeline*, and his boots crossed the deck toward the midship hold, and Noor understood with a cold, settling clarity that it was finished. Cotton that shouldn't be moving. A passenger manifest with two names too many. A woman at the rail trying so hard to look bored that boredom itself became a confession. They would pull the bales. They would find Ilya first, then Tam, then they would find the stiff seam in her coat lining, and Mr. Quill — courteous, patient Mr. Quill, who wrote letters like love notes and meant every threat in them — would finally get his paper back.

Her hand moved to the compass in her pocket without her deciding it. Brass, warm from her body. Her father's. The one fixed possession she'd carried through disgrace and debt and three hundred miles of hunted river, the needle swinging north no matter how far south her life went. It was the last thing she owned that had belonged to the woman she'd been before the valley flooded, before the signature, before Sallow Bend and salvage contracts and iron chests pulled from the shallows.

There was deep water off the starboard side. The current ran hard there, toward the marsh country, toward the reed mazes where launches couldn't follow and maps went soft.

The officer reached the midship hatch. Put his hand on it.

And Tam began to sing.

It came up through the deck boards, thin and clear, a child's voice rising out of the hold like something surfacing from deep water. Noor's blood went to ice — *no, boy, no, stay down* — but then she heard the song itself and understood. It was the ploughman's round, the one he'd hummed the first night on the barge, the one the old women at the fire had gone silent over. Vessian. Older than the erasure. A song about water finding its way home.

The officer's hand stopped on the hatch.

From the banks, out of the mist, another voice picked it up. Then another. Then a dozen — rough river voices, old and young, from the levee villages where the convoy had moored these past nights, where Tam had traded songs for bread and Noor hadn't thought anything of it. The melody rolled across the channel, call and answer, the way work songs travel, the way grief travels, and the mist seemed to carry it further than sound should go.

The crew of the *Adeline* began to sing too. The captain. The stevedores. Noor stood frozen with her rope coil, and then, because it would be stranger not to, she moved her lips though she didn't know the words.

Boats were putting out from the banks. Skiffs, punts, a leaky ferry crowded with families, drifting toward the convoy with lanterns lit — dozens of them, converging, filling the channel between the patrol launches and the barges until the water was a confusion of hulls and lights and voices. Not hostile. Nothing the gray coats could cite. Just river folk come out at first light to sing a child's song, crowding the water the way crowds gather anywhere, making a search impossible, making a boarding a spectacle, making themselves witnesses.

The officer stood on the deck with his folio half-open, and Noor watched him do the arithmetic. Four men, carbines, a crowd of sixty singing civilians, a channel full of boats. Whatever orders he had, none of them covered this. The Consortium feared paper more than guns — Ilya had told her that — but a crowd singing a forbidden nation's songs wasn't paper either. It was something older, and he had no article for it.

"Clear the channel," he said into his loudhailer, and his voice cracked slightly on the second word, and nobody cleared anything.

In the confusion — skiffs bumping hulls, lanterns swinging, the launches backing water to avoid crushing a punt full of children — Noor moved.

She went over the starboard rail feet-first, a diver's entry she'd learned dredging wrecks in colder water than this, and the river took her with a slap and a roar. The cold hit like a fist. She'd shed her boots at the rail — they'd sink, they'd anchor her — and she swam down and downstream on the current's own pull, letting it drag her under the hulls, under the tangle of skiffs, away from the searchlights that stabbed the water in blind white columns above her.

The coat dragged at her. The ledger sewn into its lining weighed like an anchor, a dead nation pulling her down, and for one black moment she considered cutting it free, letting the river have the whole cursed thing, buying her life with its loss.

Instead she kicked harder.

She surfaced thirty yards downstream behind a moored timber barge, gasping, ears roaring, and clawed along its shadowed flank hand over hand. Above and behind, the singing continued, louder if anything, and she heard the officer shouting orders that dissolved into the crowd noise like sugar into tea. They wouldn't find her. They might find Ilya and Tam — no. No, the boy had planned this. She realized it with a jolt that had nothing to do with the cold: Tam hadn't panicked. Tam had *acted*. Born after the erasure, remembering anyway, and now using the memory like a tool, like a lever. He'd drawn the crowd the way a lightning rod draws the strike, and the strike had missed them all.

She only hoped Ilya got him out the far side.

The compass was gone.

She discovered it at the third barge, when her hand went to her pocket out of habit and found wet linen and nothing else. It must have torn loose in the dive — the pocket seam, weakened by weeks of worry, giving way at exactly the moment everything else did. She hung in the black water under the barge's counter and thought about swimming back for it, and the current laughed at her. Even if she'd dared, even if the searchlights weren't sweeping the surface in slow white blades, the river had already decided. Things given to the river stayed with the river. That was the oldest law in the valley, older than Vessia, older than the Consortium.

Her father's compass, needle swinging north somewhere in the dark under the hulls, pointing at a home that had never existed for anyone but him.

Noor laughed — a single breathless bark that the water swallowed — and struck out for the bank.

* * *

The reeds took her in like a conspiracy.

She came ashore a mile downstream, in the drowned willow scrub where the marsh country began, and lay in the mud among the roots until her shaking stopped. The sky had gone properly light now, gray-gold, and the mist was burning off the water in rags. From the channel, faint with distance, she could still hear it — the singing, thinner now, scattered, but continuing. The convoy moving on. The search abandoned or postponed. She couldn't tell which, and lying there in the mud with her heart slamming, she understood she couldn't wait to find out.

She got up. Wringing, shivering, one boot missing, coat heavy with river water and one impossible book. She wrung what she could from the hem and started walking along the reed line, north and upstream, toward the marsh country Ilya had marked on his stolen survey maps. Toward the archivist, if the old man's rumors were true. Toward the source, eventually, though she didn't let herself think that word yet. *Source* was a word like a door you couldn't unopen.

She was a mile along, cutting through a stand of dead cattails, when a whistle came — soft, three notes, the signal they'd agreed on weeks ago and never used. She turned with her heart in her throat.

Ilya stood at the edge of the reeds with Tam on his back, piggyback, the boy's head lolling with exhaustion. Both soaked. Both grinning — Ilya grinning, which on his face was such a geological event that Noor nearly sat down in the mud.

"You went overboard," Ilya said.

"The alternative was a folio with my description in it."

"We went out through the coal scuttle in the galley." He set Tam down. The boy swayed, dead on his feet, but his eyes found hers and held, fierce and bright. "He planned it," Ilya said quietly. "The singing. He told me beforehand — *when the boots reach the middle hold, I sing, and the river answers.* His words." Ilya shook his head slowly. "Thirty years the Consortium burned books and buried records and renamed every town twice, and the memory survived in a nine-year-old's throat. They should be terrified of him. They have no idea."

"He lost us our boots," Tam croaked. "Both pairs. Mine are floating to the sea right now."

"Mine too," Noor said. "Along with my compass."

Tam's face fell. He knew what the compass was. He'd watched her check it every morning like a woman checking whether the world still pointed anywhere.

"It's all right," she said, and was surprised to find she half meant it. "It pointed north. North is where all my debts live." She put her hand on his head, briefly. "You sang well."

"I learned it from the water," Tam said, matter-of-fact, the way he said all his impossible things. "The old ladies say the river remembers the words even when the people forget. I just listened."

Noor looked upstream, where the channel bent away toward the marsh country and, beyond it, beyond everything, the dammed headwaters of a river that used to belong to a nation that used to exist. Somewhere behind her, a patrol launch was reporting to a courteous man named Quill that the paper had slipped through his fingers again, and that man was already writing his next letter. Ahead of her lay reed mazes, a dying archivist, a mountain, and a wall of concrete with a nation's memory sealed in its spillways.

Her coat dripped. Her body shook. The ledger pressed against her ribs, heavier than water, heavier than debt.

"Come on," she said. "We've got a country to drown."

They went up into the reeds together, three sets of wet footprints filling behind them with river, and the river carried the rest away south — the boots, the compass, the woman she had been — toward the sea, where all lost things learn to keep quiet.
