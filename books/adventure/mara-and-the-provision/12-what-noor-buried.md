## 12. What Noor Buried

The marsh at night was a country of whispers. Reeds hissed against each other in the wind off the water, and somewhere out in the black a bittern boomed like a pump losing pressure — a sound Noor knew from a hundred nights spent sleeping in signal sheds along dead lines. She had always found it comforting. Tonight it sounded like something drowning.

They had made camp on a hummock of packed silt where the reeds thinned enough to build a fire without burning the whole delta down. The archivist — Maren, she'd finally learned, though the old woman answered to nothing so readily as to the rustle of her own papers — had given them a corner of the cache shed for their blankets and a warning: *No lamps after dark. The Consortium's patrol skiffs run the main channel on moonless nights, and your fire is visible for a mile across open water.*

So they built the fire small, in a pit lined with river clay, and fed it with dry cattail heads that burned fast and sweet-smelling and gave off more light than heat. Tam sat cross-legged closest to it, the Provision open on his knees. He was reading again — actually reading now, halting but real, his finger tracking under each line of Vessian script with the fierce concentration of a boy who had decided that learning was an act of war.

"*Item forty-one,*" he sounded out. "*Water rights appurtenant to the headwaters... shall descend to the bearer... in perpetuity.*" He looked up. "What's perpetuity?"

"Forever," Ilya said from the shadows beyond the firelight. He was mending one of his maps with rice paste and strips of linen, working by feel as much as sight, his long fingers moving with the certainty of a man who had drawn these lines ten thousand times. "It means whoever holds this paper owns the river until the paper burns."

"And we're not going to burn it?"

"No," Noor said.

Tam nodded, satisfied, and went back to item forty-one.

Noor watched him over the flames. Eight years old, maybe nine — born three years after the erasure, after the maps were pulped and the names struck from the ledgers, after whatever had been done at the source turned a living nation into a rumor. And still the songs lived in him. Still he sang them at night when he thought they were asleep, little looping melodies with words none of the river folk could translate, and Ilya would lie awake listening with his eyes shut like a man hearing his mother's voice through a wall.

*Memory survives in children's rhymes,* Ilya had said. *The Consortium burned every archive and never thought to search the playgrounds.*

She should have felt hopeful. Instead she felt the weight of the thing she'd been carrying since the marsh cache — heavier than the ledger, heavier than the chest had ever been. It wasn't the documents Maren kept. It was what Maren had said to her that first evening, standing among the shelves of rescued papers with her lantern held high: *You have an engineer's hands. Whose signature closed your line?*

Hers. That was whose. And she had been walking around with it folded up inside her like a splinter working toward the heart, and tonight, with the fire low and Tam reading and Ilya humming absently under his breath as he pasted, she understood that she could not carry it into the mountains. There was no room left inside her for both the splinter and the climb.

"I need to tell you both something," she said.

Tam looked up. Ilya's hands went still.

Noor fed another handful of cattails to the fire and watched them flare. She had rehearsed this speech a dozen times in the last two days — on the barge, in the reeds, lying awake while Tam dreamed and Ilya pretended to. Every version had been shorter than the last. Now, with their faces turned toward her across the small orange light, all the versions evaporated and she simply started where the truth started, which was with a bridge.

"There's a valley called Corren's Hollow," she said. "East of the Marrow range, on the upper Alden line. Before the closure there were about four hundred people living there. Farmers, mostly. A mill. A chapel with a bell they claimed was older than any map." She heard her own voice go flat and technical, the voice she used for load calculations, and made herself keep going anyway. "The Alden ran through the valley floor. Good line. Profitable. But the Consortium wanted the water."

Ilya set down his paste brush very carefully.

"The upper Alden feeds three downstream cities," Noor said. "There was a scheme — I saw the memos, years later, when I was digging through what was left of my file. Reservoir siting studies. They'd identified the Hollow as an ideal basin back before I was born. The only thing in the way was the village, and the only thing protecting the village was the fact that flooding it would be a scandal. So they waited. And then they found an engineer who would sign the inspection report."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, the bittern boomed again.

"They didn't ask me to flood anything," she said. "That's the part I want you to understand, because I spent six years telling myself it mattered. They asked me to close the line. Seasonal maintenance, they called it. Embankment instability south of the viaduct. My report would justify the closure order, and the closure order would evacuate the service traffic, and once the trains stopped running through the Hollow, the village would die on its own within a season or two — no freight, no mail, no way in or out except the river road, which flooded every spring anyway. Clean. Deniable. Nobody floods anybody; the economics just quietly do the murder instead."

"You signed," Ilya said. Not a question.

"I inspected that embankment myself. Twice." Her hands were steady in her lap. She made herself look at them. "The southern approach was sound. I wrote it in my field notes — *no structural deficiency observed, recommend continued operation with seasonal monitoring.* And then I got a visit from a man from the regional office. Very courteous. Very reasonable. He explained that my career assessment was due in the spring, that I had debts — I've always had debts — and that senior engineers who demonstrated *flexibility* tended to find their assessments went well. He didn't threaten anything. He didn't have to. He just laid the arithmetic out on the table between us and let me look at it: sign the closure, clear your debts, make section chief by thirty. Or don't, and spend the rest of your life inspecting culverts in the salt flats."

"What did you write?" Tam asked. His voice was very quiet. He had closed the Provision and was holding it against his chest with both arms, the way he held things he loved.

Noor met the boy's eyes across the fire. He deserved the whole of it, unsoftened.

"I wrote that the embankment showed progressive subsidence consistent with foundation washout," she said. "I fabricated readings. I invented a monitoring history going back eighteen months. I drew graphs, Tam. Beautiful ones. I'm good at graphs." The self-loathing in that was old and worn smooth, a stone she'd carried so long it fit her hand perfectly. "The closure order came down in eleven days. The last train through the Hollow ran in October. By the following summer they'd begun the reservoir works, and the village — what was left of it, because most everyone had moved on by then, because that's how it works, you don't have to drown people if you can make leaving seem like their own idea — the village went under forty feet of water. The chapel bell is still down there. Divers say you can hear it ring sometimes when the current moves the clapper."

Silence. The reeds whispered. Somewhere far off, a night bird cried twice and quit.

"Why are you telling us this?" Ilya asked. His voice had an edge she hadn't heard from him before — not anger exactly, but the careful flatness of a man handling something sharp. "Now. Why now?"

"Because Maren asked me whose signature closed my line, and I lied to her." Noor turned her palms up toward the fire, as if to warm hands that weren't cold. "And because tomorrow we start climbing toward the source, and I've been telling myself a story the whole way here. That I'm different from Quill. From the men in gray coats. From whoever signed the orders that erased your country. I've been telling myself I'm on this side of the line because I'm helping you now, because I'm going to breach the dam, because the ledger chose me somehow and that makes it providence instead of luck." She shook her head. "But I know how dams get built, Ilya. I've read the engineering reports on yours. The Consortium didn't drown Vessia in a day either. First they closed the lines — *seasonal maintenance*, I'd bet my life on it. Then they rerouted the traffic. Then they let the economics do the murder. Same arithmetic. Same courteous man from the regional office, sitting across the table from some Vessian engineer with debts, laying it all out nice and reasonable." She looked at her hands again. "And somewhere in your country there was a woman with an engineer's training who signed the report. Maybe she told herself it mattered that nobody asked her to flood anything. Maybe she was very good at graphs too."

Ilya said nothing for a long time. The fire burned down to embers and he fed it another handful of cattails without looking away from her.

"You think I haven't done the same sum?" he said finally. "You're not the only one at this fire with blood on the paperwork, Kavanagh."

"I know about the survey," Noor said. "The closure survey. You told me on the barge."

"I told you I was coerced. I did not tell you what I surveyed." He drew a breath, and Noor realized she was watching a man perform on himself the same surgery she'd just performed — cutting out the softened version, showing the raw shape beneath. "In the last year before the erasure, the Consortium commissioned a hydrographic survey of the Vessian headwaters. Officially it was for flood control. I was young, I was the best cartographer in the eastern district, and I was flattered to be asked. I mapped every stream, every basin, every aquifer feeding the Vessian heartland. I produced the most complete water inventory in the nation's history." His mouth twisted. "Two years later, when the dam went up, the engineers used my survey to site it. Every figure. Every contour. I had drawn the blueprint of our own drowning, and I hadn't even known it, because I was twenty-six and someone important had shaken my hand."

"That's not the same—"

"It's exactly the same," Ilya said, "and the difference between us is only that I hid behind ignorance and you hid behind coercion, and neither hiding place holds water." He almost smiled at his own pun, and it was a terrible smile. "Maren knows. She's known for thirty years — she was the one who pulled my name off the collaboration lists when the resistance archives were being sorted, because she said a mapmaker's guilt was worth more than a martyr's purity. She said the ones who helped build the machine make the best saboteurs, because they know where the bolts are." He looked at Noor across the fire. "I think she saw the same thing in you. That's why she asked whose signature it was. She wasn't testing whether you were clean. She was checking whether you knew where the bolts were."

Tam had been silent so long that Noor had half forgotten him. Now he spoke, still hugging the Provision, his chin tucked down.

"My da says everybody in the river country has a story like that," he said. "Something they did to eat. Something they signed." He frowned at the fire. "He says the Consortium counts on it. On everybody having a splinter, so nobody feels clean enough to point fingers."

Out of the mouths of babes, Noor's grandmother used to say, and the thought arrived with such force that she had to look away for a moment, blinking at the dark water beyond the firelight.

"Your da sounds like a wise man," she managed.

"He drowns eels," Tam said solemnly. "It's not complicated work. You have time to think."

A laugh broke out of Noor before she could stop it — short, cracked, closer to a sob than she'd have admitted. Even Ilya huffed something that might have been amusement. The terrible knot in her chest loosened by one turn. Not undone. One turn.

"So what happens now?" she asked. "Tomorrow we climb. Quill is behind us with a column and a hostage's worth of leverage, and the passes close with the freeze, and I'm supposed to stand in front of that dam and dissolve a nation's claim with a signature — *my* signature, again, one more document, one more line where my name makes the world different." She spread her hands. "How do I trust myself with it? How do either of you?"

Ilya considered the question with the seriousness he brought to everything, turning it over the way he turned his maps, checking the margins.

"Because you're telling me the truth beside a fire when you could have waited until the summit and told me after," he said at last. "Quill would have waited until after. The engineer who signed your report — she would have waited until after, too, and then told herself the climbing made it moot." He picked up his paste brush again. "The corrupt don't confess in advance, Kavanagh. They confess never, or they confess in memoirs. You're doing it now, in the mud, to a child and an exile, with everything to lose." He touched the mended map at his knee. "That's the only credential I've got left to offer anyone. Take it or don't."

Noor looked at Tam. The boy was watching her with those too-old eyes, and after a moment he unfolded one arm from around the Provision and held the ledger out to her — not handing it over, just offering the weight of it, the way you offer a tool to someone whose hands are shaking.

"You should hold it tonight," he said. "I get it every night. It's your turn."

She took it. It was lighter than she remembered, or her arms were stronger than she knew. She sat with it in her lap and felt the fire's warmth come up through the leather cover, and for the first time since Sallow Bend the paper under her hands felt less like a debt and more like a duty — which was not the same as feeling worthy of it, but was, she suspected, the closest a person like her would ever come.

They banked the fire and rolled into their blankets. Tam fell asleep mid-sentence, as boys do, one hand still curled around the space where the ledger had been. Ilya's breathing slowed into the rhythm of a man who had put something down.

Noor lay awake a while longer, listening to the marsh breathe. The bittern boomed. The reeds hissed. And far to the north, beyond the black shoulder of the hills, the dam stood in the dark holding back a river and a nation, waiting for her the way all her signatures had waited — patiently, indifferently, certain that in the end she would write her name.

Near dawn, Ilya shook her awake with a hand clamped gentle over her mouth, his eyes wide in the gray light.

"Lanterns on the channel," he breathed. "Three of them. Coming up the slough — and they're not running patrol patterns." He swallowed. "Kavanagh, they're running *survey* patterns. Someone's mapping the marsh."

Someone who already knew where the archivist's cache was hidden.
