## 13. The Toll Gate

The city announced itself before they could see it — a smell of coal smoke and wet stone rolling down the valley on the morning wind, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of a bell that rang in threes. Tam counted them from the reed bank where they'd slept, his breath fogging in the cold.

"Three rings," Ilya said beside him, already folding the oilcloth over the archive satchel. "Toll bell. Means the gates are open and the ledgers are being read. We go through as cargo handlers. Noor has papers."

"I don't like cities," Tam said.

"Noor doesn't either." Ilya's mouth did the thing it sometimes did, the almost-smile that never finished itself. "But cities have paper, and paper is how we win."

Tam didn't understand that yet. He understood rivers, and reeds, and which of the river folk's songs were safe to sing aloud and which had to stay under his breath like a secret swallowed whole. He was nine years old and he had been born after the erasure, whatever an erasure was, and all he knew was that certain words made grown-ups go quiet and look at the water.

Noor came up the bank with her boots squelching, hauling the handcart they'd traded two days of barge work for. Her face had the gray set to it it always got near Consortium territory.

"Gates open till dusk," she said. "We walk through, we keep our eyes down, we buy nothing we don't need, and Tam—" she crouched so their eyes were level, and her voice went low and careful, the way it did when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed, "—nobody sings today. Not humming. Not tapping. Nothing."

"I know."

"It's not a punishment."

"I *know*." He hated when she said it like that, gentle, as if he were made of eggshell. He wasn't. He'd been swept down a weir current last month and come up laughing. But he nodded anyway, because Noor's fear had a weight to it, and weight you carried whether you understood it or not.

They joined the road where it thickened with carts and drovers and mud-splattered pilgrims walking toward the toll city of Grayharbor Reach, which sat across the river like a cork in a bottle — walls on three sides, water gate on the fourth, and above everything a chain tower whose great links ran down into the current, ready to be raised and stop any boat that hadn't paid. Tam had seen chain towers before, from far off, little black teeth on the horizon. Up close it was worse. The chain itself was thicker than his waist, and every link was stamped with the same mark: a circle crossed by a bar, the Provision mark, the mark that was on the tollbooths and the patrol launches and the gray coats.

"What does it mean?" he asked Ilya, pointing. "The circle with the line through it."

Ilya looked at it a long time. Longer than a man should look at a symbol he saw every day.

"It means *provided for*," he said finally. "That's what they called it. The Provisioning. When they closed Vessia, they didn't say erased. They said the nation had been *provisionally administered* for its own good. The circle is the seal of care. The bar is the closure." His voice had gone flat as a frozen pond. "They stamped it on everything. Even the bread ration tickets, for a while. Imagine that. Eating your own conquest, one loaf at a time."

Tam turned the shape over in his head. A circle was a wheel, a coin, a ring. A bar was a rail, a dam, a locked door. He decided he didn't like it, and said so, and Ilya laughed — a real one this time, short and startled out of him — and put a hand briefly on Tam's shoulder, heavy as an anchor and just as steadying.

* * *

Inside the walls, Grayharbor Reach was all stone and noise. The streets funneled toward the toll plaza, where a hundred people queued at iron booths while clerks with green visors read papers by lamplight even though it was midmorning. Everything smelled of ink. That was the thing Tam noticed most, the thing he'd remember: not the guards, not the chain tower groaning overhead as a barge passed through the water gate, but the *ink*. The whole city smelled like a schoolroom that had drowned.

Noor's papers got them through the pedestrian gate with only one long pause, a clerk comparing Noor's face to a description sheet with the leisurely cruelty of a man who enjoyed being feared. Tam kept his eyes on the cobbles and counted the cracks between stones until the clerk waved them through, bored at last.

Then the plaza opened up, and Tam forgot to keep his eyes down.

The murals covered the customs house wall, three stories of them, painted in colors so bright they hurt against the gray morning — gold and river-blue and triumphant red. And Tam knew, the way he knew things he'd never been taught, that the blue was wrong. It was too bright, too clean, a blue no river ever held. It was the blue of paint, not water.

He stopped dead in the stream of traffic. Noor grabbed his collar and hauled him along, but he twisted in her grip, craning back.

The mural showed a great river flowing out of mountains into waiting hands — dozens of hands, upturned, generous, gray-coated hands. Beneath it, in letters tall as Tam himself:

**THE WATERS ARE PROVIDED FOR ALL**

And below that, smaller panels, each one a scene with a caption. Tam read them because reading was the newest thing he had and he used it on everything, hungry as a gull. One panel showed a burning city — and he felt his stomach drop, because the towers in the painting, the bridge with the double arch, the hill crowned with a bell tower — he *knew* that skyline. Not from pictures. From a song. *Under the twin arches the eels sleep in silver,* went the rhyme the old women sang when the nets came up empty, and here were the twin arches, burning, with the caption:

**VESIA'S REBELLION ENDED IN THE PROVISION OF PEACE**

Another panel: engineers in white coats raising a dam, sunlight bursting from behind it like a second dawn. **THE FORBIDDEN SOURCE, TAMED FOR THE COMMON GOOD.** Another: children at desks, smiling, holding new books. **FORGETTING IS THE FIRST GIFT OF ORDER.**

"Forgetting is the first gift," Tam whispered.

"Walk," Noor said through her teeth.

"They burned the twin arches." His voice cracked and climbed. "That's Marlow Ford, that's the eel song, that's—"

"*Walk.*"

She dragged him half a block before his legs agreed to work. Ilya fell in beside them, and Tam saw that the cartographer had gone the color of paper left out in rain. He had seen the murals too. He had seen the burning arches rendered in cheerful gold, and something in his face had quietly broken and been hastily patched.

"They gave it a festival day," Ilya said softly, to no one. "There's a festival. They celebrate it. Every year, with ribbons."

* * *

They took a room over a chandler's shop near the water gate, paid for with Noor's last honest coin, and waited for dark the way soldiers wait — in pieces, taking turns at the shuttered window. Below, the toll plaza lit itself with lamps, and the murals glowed under them, smug and bright, and Tam lay on the pallet staring at the ceiling and feeling a new thing grow in his chest.

It wasn't fear. He knew fear; fear was the weir current, fear was Quill's gray coat. This was hotter and it had teeth. It sat under his ribs like a swallowed coal.

They had painted his song burning. They had painted it *happy*.

Near midnight he heard Noor and Ilya murmuring over the table, maps and the archive satchel spread between them, arguing in the low voices of people who have argued before. Something about passes, about winter, about the marsh archivist's route north. Tam slid off the pallet without a board creaking — a skill learned from years of small spaces and stolen naps — and slipped out into the stairwell shadow.

The chandler's shop had a back door. The back door had a bad latch. Tam had found both facts within an hour of arriving, not because he meant anything by it, but because knowing how a place leaked was simply how he looked at the world. River folk said he had eel-blood. His mother, before the fever took the barge and her with it, had said he had *her* curiosity, and said it like an apology.

The night air hit him cold and smelling of ink. The plaza was nearly empty — one lamplighter making his rounds, two guards at the customs house door stamping their feet against the cold, their attention aimed at a wine jug passed between them.

The bookstall was at the plaza's edge, shuttered for the night, but the stallkeeper was also the city's licensed printer — Tam had watched him that afternoon, pulling sheets, and seen the stacks drying on racks behind the stall: slim green-bound volumes with the circle-and-bar stamped in gilt.

*A Complete History of the Provisional Administration of the Vessian Territories, Revised and Authorized.*

He'd stood there reading the title for a long time that afternoon, while Noor bargained for lamp oil, and the coal under his ribs had glowed.

Now he crossed the plaza flat-footed and quick, the way you cross thin ice, and crouched in the printer's doorway where the shadows pooled deepest. The drying racks stood just inside the open workshop window — the stallkeeper was lazy about locking, or trusted the guards, or had drunk his own courage to sleep. Tam reached through and lifted a copy off the rack. The ink was barely dry. It smudged his fingers green.

His heart banged so hard he could feel it in his ears, in his wrists, in his teeth. He had stolen bread before, apples, a knife once. This was different. This was heavier, though it weighed less than an apple. He tucked it inside his shirt, against his skin, and walked back across the plaza at a pace he forced to stay slow, past the guards, past the glowing mural where the twin arches burned in festive gold, and he stopped — one heartbeat, two — and looked up at the painted fire.

*I know what you really looked like,* he told the mural silently. *You looked like silver eels and bell-song. You looked like my mother dancing on a deck. You're a lie with a lamp shining on it.*

Then he went home through the dark, and the coal under his ribs burned steady instead of hot, and that was somehow worse, and better.

* * *

Noor caught him at the window, coming back in. Of course she did. She was sitting in the dark with her boots on, and she didn't even look surprised — just tired in a way that went all the way through her.

"Give it here."

He gave it. She turned the little green book over in her hands, reading the spine, the seal, the authorization line at the front. Ilya leaned in from the table, and then both of them were reading by candle stub, pages whispering, and Tam sat on the pallet and watched their faces do things faces weren't supposed to do.

Because the book was *wrong*. Not wrong like mistakes. Wrong like a map drawn by someone who wanted you lost.

In the official history, Vessia had rebelled — risen in armed mobs, poisoned reservoirs, burned its own granaries, and the Consortium had stepped in, sorrowful and reluctant, to provide order. There had been no dam seizure; there had been a *voluntary consolidation of hydraulic assets*. There had been no purge of records; there had been an *orderly transfer of documentation to secure federal vaults*. The drowned station, the sunken town Ilya grew up in — it appeared in exactly one sentence, as *the regrettable but necessary inundation of a structurally unsound settlement*, its residents described as *resettled with compensation*.

Ilya made a sound Tam had never heard a grown man make, low and strangled, and put the book down flat, and pressed both palms on top of it as if it might climb off the table.

"Resettled," he said. "With compensation." His hands were shaking. "My father was a lockmaster. My mother taught at the church school. There was no compensation. There was a train platform and a count and—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "They wrote it down wrong on purpose. Every word. It's not ignorance. Ignorance you can fix. This is *craft*. Someone sat at a desk and built this lie plank by plank and signed it."

"That's what Quill is," Noor said. Her voice was even, but Tam saw her thumb pressing white against the book's edge. "Not the guns. The desk. The guns are just how the desk enforces its grammar."

Tam couldn't hold still anymore. He was up, and the words were coming out of him fast and stumbling, the coal flaring.

"But people will know. People who were there, they'll know it's lies."

"Some do." Ilya looked at him. "Most are dead, or scattered, or quiet. And the children—" he gestured at Tam, gently, helplessly, "—the children get the green books in school, and the songs get thinner every generation, worn down like coins passed hand to hand. In sixty years the rhymes will be nonsense syllables and nobody will know they were ever a country. That's the design. Not to kill the memory. To *starve* it slow, so no one remembers there was ever a meal."

"Then we feed it," Tam said.

Both adults looked at him.

"We've got the real papers. In the satchel. The archivist's cache, the Provision, all of it." He was talking with his whole body now, hands carving the air. "We take the green book and the true book and we put them side by side and show everyone, and they'll see—"

"See what?" Noor asked, and it wasn't cruel. It was the question of someone who had believed something like this once and had the belief beaten out of her, and was asking honestly, sadly, wanting to be proven wrong.

"People aren't stupid," Tam said. "The river folk sing the songs. They don't even know why anymore, and they still sing them. Because the songs are *true* and true weighs more. You put a true thing next to a lie, people can feel the difference, like—" he groped for it, "—like telling a real current from a slack tide. Your legs know. Even if your head's confused, your legs know."

Silence. The candle guttered. Outside, the toll bell rang the hour — three, four, five — and somewhere below a barge horn answered, low and patient.

Ilya picked the green book up again, slowly, and looked at Tam with an expression Tam couldn't name yet, though he'd find the word for it years later, telling this story to his own students in a free country: the expression of a man watching a small light he'd thought extinguished catch again in an unexpected place.

"The boy's right," Ilya said. "Not about the method. About the weight." He set the stolen history on the table next to the archive satchel, side by side, lie and truth, green cloth and oilcloth. "We carry both. When we reach people who can read, we let them compare. Paper against paper. That's the war. That was always the war."

"And if Quill catches us carrying it?" Noor asked.

"Then Quill catches us carrying proof," Ilya said, "that his Consortium is afraid of a nine-year-old's memory. Let him explain that at his next audit."

Noor almost smiled. Then she looked at Tam, and the smile folded away into something more careful.

"You stole that past two armed guards," she said. "Don't do it again without telling me. Not because you did wrong." She crouched to his level, the way she did, eye to eye. "Because next time I'm going to be the one keeping watch. Understood?"

"Understood," Tam said, and meant it, and also already knew, in the eel-blooded private country of himself, that rules made in warm rooms had a way of dissolving in cold streets. But that was later. For now he lay back on the pallet with the green book hidden under his shirt again — he'd refused to surrender it; Noor had let him win that one, he suspected, on purpose — and felt its small hard weight rise and fall with his breathing.

A lie, sleeping against his heart. Tomorrow it would go to work as evidence.

* * *

They left Grayharbor Reach at first light, joining a caravan of gravel wagons heading north on the towpath road. At the water gate, the chain tower loomed, and a clerk stamped their transit papers, and nobody looked twice at a cartographer, a disgraced engineer, and a boy.

Except one man.

He stood at the gatehouse window in a gray coat that fit him better than uniforms usually fit men, doing nothing so obvious as watching them — reading a dispatch, in fact, unhurried, elegant, a gentleman passing time. But as they passed beneath the arch, Tam felt it, the way you feel a change in current before your eyes confirm it: a pressure, a turning.

He glanced up. The man in the window lowered the dispatch. Their eyes met for exactly one second.

He smiled. Politely. The way a clerk smiles when your papers are, technically, in order.

Tam walked on and didn't turn around, because turning around would tell the man he'd been recognized, and the coal under his ribs had gone very cold and very bright all at once. He put his hand inside his coat, over the green book, and matched Noor's pace step for step.

Behind them, faint over the water, the toll bell began to ring — not the hour, not the shift change. A pattern Tam had never heard. Fast, repeated, urgent.

The search bell.

Somewhere back in the city, someone had counted the printer's stock.
