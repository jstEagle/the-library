## 14. Betrayal at the Weir

The nephew's name was Dario, and Noor had never trusted the way he looked at things.

He had joined them two days out of the marsh, meeting them at a ferry crossing with his uncle's blessing and a mule loaded with dried fish and lamp oil — supplies for the ascent, he said, that no one else in the lowlands would dare sell them now that Quill's patrols were thick as midges on every road. He was twenty, maybe twenty-two, with soft hands and quick eyes and a habit of counting. Counting the bundles on the mule. Counting the coins Ilya paid him. Counting, once, when he thought she wasn't watching, the shape of the ledger through her coat.

She should have said something then. She told herself later, a hundred times, that she should have said something then.

But he was the archivist's blood, and the old man was dying, and trust was a currency they could not afford to hoard.

* * *

They climbed out of the marsh country on the third morning, following a track that was less a road than an argument between stone and water. The river ran beside them, narrower now, faster, its voice rising from a murmur to a constant argument. Tam walked ahead most days, hopping the rocks, singing under his breath — Vessian songs, always Vessian songs, though he'd begun picking up new verses from Ilya, correcting himself with a frown of concentration that made Noor's chest ache.

"The weir is here." Ilya spread his survey map on a flat boulder at their noon stop, weighting the corners with stones. His finger traced the blue line upstream to where it kinked around a shoulder of granite. "Old regulation weir. Built before the Closure to meter the flow into the irrigation channels. The Consortium kept it working — they need the water measured even if they won't admit where it comes from."

"Meaning there'll be staff," Noor said.

"A keeper, maybe two. They rotate them through from the toll cities. Low-grade men. Bored men." He glanced up at her. "And past the weir, the track leaves the river. Three days' climb to the headwaters, and after the first ridge there is nothing but my maps and winter coming early."

Noor studied the map without really seeing it. She was listening — to the water, to the wind off the scree, to the small animal silence of the slopes above them. For two days she'd had a feeling like a splinter worked deep under the skin: the sense of being watched by something patient. She'd blamed the patrols. She'd blamed her own nerves, which had been fraying since the marsh, since the archivist's papery hands had pressed the truth into hers — *the dam holds the records, engineer, all of them, everything they drowned.*

"Dario," she said suddenly. "Where is he?"

Ilya looked up. "Scouting ahead. He said the mule needed watering at the next spring."

"He's been scouting ahead for an hour."

Tam stopped singing.

Ilya folded the map slowly, and Noor watched the understanding move across his face like weather. "His uncle trusted him," the cartographer said quietly. It wasn't a defense. It was grief arriving ahead of the proof.

"His uncle is dying and can't follow us," Noor said. "Dario knows exactly what we carry and exactly where we're going, and this morning I caught him marking our pace against his watch." She stood, brushing grit from her knees. "We move now. Off the track. High line, through the rocks. If I'm wrong, we lose half a day. If I'm right—"

She didn't finish it. She didn't have to.

* * *

They lost the half day anyway, and more, hauling themselves along a goat path above the river while the light went long and copper and the temperature fell like a debt called in. Tam, to his credit, did not complain. He climbed like the river folk's child he was, sure-footed and silent, and only once, near dusk, did he look back down at the valley and say, "Do you think Dario meant to do it? From the beginning?"

"People rarely mean to," Noor said. "They mean to survive. The rest arranges itself around that."

"That's what my mother says about the flood men."

"It's what everyone says about everyone," Noor said, "and it's true, and it doesn't forgive anything."

They made cold camp in a hollow below a leaning slab of granite, no fire, sharing dried fish and the last of the marsh honey. Ilya checked his instruments by feel in the dark. Noor sat with her back to the stone and the ledger's hard rectangle against her ribs, sewn into the lining of her coat where it had lived since Sallow Bend, and tried to sleep the way engineers sleep — one ear open for the sound of things about to fail.

What woke her, hours later, was not a sound. It was the absence of one. The river's voice had changed pitch, flattened, gone strangely formal, the way water sounds when it meets a structure built to argue with it.

"We're close to the weir," she whispered.

Ilya was already awake. Of course he was. "By the sound, less than a mile downstream of us. The track rejoins the river just above it." He paused. "Noor. If Dario sold us, Quill will not come up the mountain behind us. There's no need. He'll simply be *at* the weir. Waiting where the land funnels us to him."

"Then we go over the top of the ridge instead—"

"In the dark, in frost, with a boy, carrying everything we own?" Ilya shook his head. "The ridge path is a month of survey work I've never done. I know my limits on paper. On that rock, in this light, I'd kill us."

Noor lay back and stared at the stars and felt the trap drawing shut with the slow, courteous patience of everything Quill did. He would be there in the morning. She knew it the way she knew a bridge was rotten — not from evidence but from the load it carried.

"Sleep," she told Tam, who was pretending to. "Tomorrow we'll need your legs more than your opinions."

He grinned in the dark, briefly, and then the grin faded, because he was a boy who remembered too much, including how to be afraid.

* * *

They came down to the weir at first light, and it was worse than she'd imagined, because it was better.

The structure was old Vessian work, dressed granite and iron gearing, elegant as a sentence — a low wall across the river with sluice gates and a keeper's house perched on the near bank like a bird on a rail. Water sheeted over the crest in a clean white veil. Mist stood above the pool below it. And on the gravel apron before the keeper's house, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back, watching the water as though it were the only thing in the valley worth his attention, stood Mr. Quill.

Four gray coats flanked him. A fifth held three horses and, saddled among them, a mule with a familiar halter.

"No," Tam breathed.

Dario stood apart from the gray coats, wrapped in a new blanket coat that still had the shop creases in it. He wouldn't look at them. He looked at the mule, at the ground, at the water, anywhere but at the three people he'd fed and guided and laughed with over dried fish, and his face was the face of a man who had already done his crying in private and found it hadn't helped.

Quill turned when their boots crunched the gravel. He smiled the way a stamped form smiles.

"Engineer Kavanagh." He inclined his head. "You've been difficult to reach. I want you to know I take no pleasure in the inconvenience. The mountains are no place for correspondence."

"You could have written," Noor said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, in a distant, disconnected way, the way you're proud of a signal lamp still burning in a storm.

"I did. Several times. Your forwarding addresses were creative." He gestured mildly at the weir, the mist, the morning. "A beautiful piece of work, this. Pre-Closure. The Vessians understood water the way your Consortium understands freight — as something that must be *accounted for*. Do you know why I admire you, Engineer? Because you also understand accounting. You signed a closure order once. You know that every act of provision requires someone willing to hold the pen."

"Don't," Ilya said softly. It was almost a warning to himself.

"Cartographer Voss." Quill nodded to him with genuine courtesy. "Your surveys were excellent. That was never in dispute. Only your loyalty."

"My loyalty was purchased with my brother's life," Ilya said. "Yours is purchased with a pension. We are not so different, except that I am ashamed."

Something flickered behind Quill's glasses — not anger. Recognition. He let it pass and turned back to Noor, and now his voice dropped into the register he must have used in ten thousand conference rooms, the register of reasonable men concluding reasonable business.

"Here is the final offer, and I give you my word it is final, because after today the terms change. You surrender the Provision. In exchange: your debt is dissolved. Not paid — *dissolved*, as if it had never existed. Your engineering license restored. Your name struck from the register of defaulters. And passage — real passage, papers, a berth, a future — anywhere the rails run." He spread his hands. "You came up this mountain to trade a piece of paper for a nation's ghost. I am offering to trade it for your life. That is not a threat, Engineer. It is arithmetic."

Noor looked at him for a long moment. Then past him — at Dario, still studying the ground; at the gray coats; at the white veil of the water falling over stone that some dead Vessian mason had fitted so well it had outlived his language.

"Do you know what I buried, Mr. Quill?" she said. "Down in the marsh, around a fire, I told these two what I did. I signed a closure that put a valley under twelve feet of water. Forty-one people relocated, the report said. Relocated. As if they were furniture." She stepped forward. "I spent six years telling myself it was arithmetic too. So believe me when I tell you I know exactly what your offer weighs. And the answer is no."

Quill sighed, and it was almost tender. "I confess I hoped you'd say that."

"Why?"

"Because hope is what makes refusal expensive." He snapped his fingers — a small, dry sound, absurdly domestic — and the nearest gray coat moved.

Not toward Noor. Toward Tam.

It happened fast and it happened slowly, both at once, the way disasters do. Noor saw the man's hand close on the boy's collar; saw Tam twist, quick as an eel, river-quick; saw the second gray coat step in from the blind side that the boy couldn't watch because he was watching her. She was moving — she was already moving, three strides, four, her mind running the geometry and finding it insoluble, forty feet of gravel and two grown men between her and him—

"Engineer." Quill's voice cracked across the morning like a gauge glass breaking. "One more step and this becomes a very different negotiation."

She stopped. Gravel rolled away from her boot downhill and clicked against stone, and the sound of it was the loudest thing in the world.

They held Tam between them, one fist knotted in his collar, his heels barely scraping the ground. He wasn't crying. He was looking at her — straight at her, furious and terrified and trusting, and his lips moved around the first line of a song, the old one, the one the river children sang without knowing why, and Noor understood it was not for comfort. It was a message. *Remember me. Remember this. Whatever happens, carry it.*

Her hands wanted to do murder. Her hands were trained, instead, to calculate loads, and the load here was unbearable and the answer was not in any table.

"You said the terms change after today," she said. Her voice did not shake. Later she would wonder at that; now she only noticed it, distantly, as an engineer notices a machine holding under strain it was never rated for. "These are the changed terms, then. A child."

"A guarantee," Quill corrected. "Insurance. You are a woman who has already thrown away her license, her credit, her compass, and her good name. What remains that you value? Him." He adjusted his glasses. "He comes with me down the mountain. You and Voss continue your pilgrimage. When you stand before the dam — and you will, you're far too stubborn to turn back — you send word, and we complete the exchange. The Provision for the boy. Everyone ascends their own mountain, Engineer. Even me."

"And if we don't send word?"

"Then I return him to the toll cities, and he becomes a ward of the Provision Office, and boys raised in those dormitories grow into very useful, very empty men." Quill said it without cruelty, which was the worst of it. "I don't want that. I suspect you don't either. But I am a servant of an institution, and institutions keep accounts longer than hearts do."

Ilya's hand closed on Noor's arm — not restraining her, anchoring her, the way you anchor a thing you're afraid will blow away. "Noor," he said, very low. "The maps. The passes. Winter. We cannot fight four guns and a frozen ridge. We cannot. You know it."

She knew it. God help her, she knew it, and knowing it felt exactly like the day she'd signed the closure order: the moment when arithmetic wins and something in you goes into receivership.

"Tam," she said.

"I'm all right," he said, and his voice cracked on *all*, and he hated that she heard it, and she heard it. "Noor. Listen. The spillway vaults — the archivist said the combination's in the Provision, the last clause, the numbers nobody reads. Don't trade it. Don't you *dare* trade it. Promise me you won't—"

"Enough," said the gray coat holding him, and shook him, gently, horribly, the way you shake a parcel to guess its contents.

Noor crossed the gravel until she stood an arm's length from the boy, close enough that Quill's men tensed and Quill, watching, allowed it — allowed her this much rope, because rope was what insurance was made of. She took Tam's face in both hands. His jaw was set like his mother's, like all the river folk's, like people who had already survived one drowning.

"Three days," she said. "Climb high, sleep warm, eat everything they give you, and count everything they show you — you're good at counting, better than he is. And sing. Sing loud enough that the whole valley learns it wrong from you." She pressed her forehead to his, one second, no more. "I'm coming with the paper. Both of them. The one in my coat and the one in your head. Do you hear me?"

"Yes," Tam whispered.

"Then go learn the enemy's house. You remember too much anyway. Make it useful."

The gray coats drew him back, and he went, walking, not dragged — he made himself walk, spine straight, chin up, a small figure in a borrowed coat descending the valley road between tall gray men, and Noor stood in the mist of the weir and watched until the road bent and took him, and then watched the bend itself for a long time after it had nothing left to give.

Quill mounted his horse. Atop it he looked down at her, and for one unguarded instant there was something almost like respect in his face, or perhaps only recognition — the look of a man seeing another instrument that refuses to read zero.

"Three days, Engineer. After that, the account closes." He gathered the reins. "You should know — I take no pleasure in the boy. But I have learned that everything worth having is held by someone who loves it more than they love themselves. That is the entire secret of my profession."

"Then your profession is theft," Noor said.

"My profession," Quill said, turning his horse, "*is provision.*"

They went down the valley in a clatter of hooves, taking the mule and the blanket-coated nephew with them. Dario looked back once, at the last bend, and whatever his face was doing, the distance made it unreadable, and Noor decided she would never spend another hour of her life trying to read it.

The mist off the weir settled over the gravel where the boy had stood.

Ilya spoke first, quietly, already unrolling his maps against the keeper's house wall with hands that weren't quite steady. "Three days. The high route adds two. We climb now, climb fast, and we don't stop."

Noor pulled the sewing knife from her pocket and slit open her coat lining, and the Provision came out into the grey morning light, heavy as a heart. She held it a moment — the paper that could buy her back, the paper that could buy Tam free, the paper that could, if the dying archivist had told the truth, dissolve every claim ever written against a river.

"Then we climb," she said, "and we don't stop."

Above the weir, far up the white shoulders of the mountain, the forbidden headwaters waited behind thirty years of concrete — and somewhere on the road below, a boy was counting.
