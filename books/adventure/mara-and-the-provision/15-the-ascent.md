## 15. The Ascent

The maps were older than I was, and they were the only honest thing left in the valley.

I had drawn them at twenty-three, a junior surveyor with ink-stained fingers and a government stipend, walking the headwaters of the Vessa with a theodolite on my back and a boy's certainty that every line I laid down would outlive me. Thirty years later I spread those same sheets across a flat boulder above the tree line and weighted the corners with stones, and the river country I had loved lay beneath my hands in faded brown contour lines like the veins of something sleeping.

"They'll close the passes within the week," Noor said. She stood with her back to me, watching the trail we'd climbed. "First hard freeze. Quill knows it too. He's not hurrying because he doesn't have to."

"He has to now." I tapped the map. "There's one road up the Vessa gorge, and it dies at the old survey camp. After that there are two ways to the dam — the switchback trail on the north face, or the aqueduct gallery cut into the rock. The gallery is gated. The trail is open but exposed. If he reaches the camp before we do, he can sit on both."

"Then we don't let him reach the camp." She turned. Three days of climbing had carved her down to wire and stubbornness; the bruise from the weir had gone yellow along her jaw, and she wore exhaustion the way other people wore coats. But her eyes were clear. They had been clear since the night she signed the dissolution clause and burned our last way back. "How far?"

"Two days if the weather holds. One day if it doesn't and we're fools about it."

"We're fools about it," Tam said.

He was crouched at the edge of the boulder, chipping ice off a seep with his thumbnail, and he said it with the flat authority of a boy who had been cold for three days and had decided cold was simply the price of admission. He'd stopped complaining on the second morning, somewhere above the drowned orchards, and I understood then that something in him had finished changing. He was no longer a child traveling with us. He was a child traveling *toward* something.

"The gallery," Noor said. "If we can force the gate."

"You can't force it. It's Consortium steel, thirty years rusted shut—" I caught myself, and she almost smiled.

"But you know another way in."

"I know the man who built the gate." I folded the map along its old creases, careful of the soft places where the paper had gone translucent with handling. "Ardan Kessel. Chief engineer of the headwater works. He surveyed the gallery with me the summer before the closure. He drank Vessian plum brandy and complained about his wife and taught me how to read water pressure by ear." I paused. "He also designed the emergency maintenance hatches. Because Ardan Kessel did not trust the Consortium, even while he built for them. Especially then."

Noor looked at me for a long moment. "You've known this since the marsh archive."

"I've known it since before the erasure," I said. "I just didn't know why I remembered it until now."

That was the truth of the whole ascent, though I didn't say it aloud: everything I had spent thirty years trying to forget was suddenly load-bearing. Every name, every contour, every overheard complaint in a survey tent — all of it had become a road.

* * *

We climbed through the afternoon into cloud, and the cloud thickened toward evening until the world shrank to ten feet of scree and the sound of the river far below us, muffled now, as if the mountain were keeping it secret. The Vessa ran somewhere under that white silence, running free for the first time in thirty years, and I found myself orienting by it without meaning to — the way you orient by a voice in a dark house.

Tam walked between us. We'd put him in the middle on the first morning, after the scree slope near the weir nearly took him sideways, and he hadn't protested. He saved his breath. But he counted things aloud when the climbing got bad — steps, switchbacks, birds — a habit I recognized from the river folk, from children taught to count their way through fear. On the long traverse above the gorge he counted forty-one cairns, and at each one he touched the top stone, and I realized he was doing what I did with my maps: making the world hold still by naming it.

At dusk we reached the ruins of the old toll station — a stone hut with its roof fallen in, half-swallowed by lichen. My map marked it as Survey Point Nine. I had slept here once with six men and a cook stove, and Kessel had gotten drunk on the brandy and sung until the others threw boots at him.

I sang nothing. But I stood in the doorway while Noor saw to Tam's boots, and I let myself remember it whole: the laughter, the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, Kessel's terrible voice cracking on the high notes. The Consortium had taken the country, but they had never figured out how to take the evenings. Those lived in bodies, and bodies kept their own archives.

"There's a spring behind the east wall," I said. "It doesn't freeze. Kessel found it. He used to say the mountain was leaking its own secrets."

Noor filled the bottles in the dark. When she came back she sat beside me against the wall, close enough that I could feel the warmth coming off her shoulder, and for a while neither of us spoke.

"Ilya." Her voice was low, pitched under Tam's breathing. "The clause I signed. Dissolving the provision. It voided my claim. It voided my debt. But it also means nobody owns the water now. Not us, not them. When we breach that dam, everything downstream changes hands by flood."

"I know."

"Do you? Because Quill will offer the lowlands order. He'll offer them pipes and schedules and someone to blame. Fear runs downhill faster than water, and these people have been drinking from the Consortium's taps for thirty years." She was quiet a moment. "I'm not asking whether we do it. I'm asking if you understand what we're doing it *to*."

I thought about my hometown under the reservoir, about the church spire that showed on low-water years like a finger pointing at the sky. I thought about the archivist dying in the reeds, pressing rescued paper into our hands like a man passing a flame.

"I understand that a map is a promise," I said finally. "And the Consortium broke every promise on mine. Whatever comes after the flood, at least it will come after honestly. At least the lowlands will get to draw their own lines."

She nodded slowly, and in the dark I heard her exhale, and it sounded like a woman setting down something heavy she'd been carrying alone.

Above us, past the broken roof, the first stars came out through tearing cloud. And below us, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I had hoped not to hear for another day: engines. Many of them, low-geared, grinding up the gorge road.

Quill's column had entered the mountains.

* * *

We moved before dawn, by starlight, with the temperature falling so fast that frost formed on my eyelashes between blinks. The trail left the treeline entirely and became a ledge cut into the north face of the gorge — my own survey line, blasted by Vessian work crews in the year before the closure, wide enough for a cart then, narrowed now by thirty winters of rockfall to a hand's width in places.

I went first. Not because I was the strongest climber — Noor was, by a distance, and she knew it and let me pretend otherwise — but because I was the only one who knew which ledges were real. A map tells you where the path is. Only memory tells you where the path *was*, and where the mountain has since eaten it.

Twice I chose wrong. Twice I backed along the ledge with my heart slamming and redirected us, and both times Noor followed without a word of reproach, and both times I hated myself a little more efficiently than the time before. Guilt is a fine fuel. It burns hot and dirty and it gets you up a mountain.

Below us, the column moved. We could see them now whenever the cloud tore — a gray thread winding up the gorge road, lanterns at intervals, pack animals, and at the head of it, if you squinted, something that might have been a launch-mounted searchlight being hauled piece by piece toward the passes. Quill traveled like an institution. He brought paperwork even here; I could see the document cases strapped to the mules.

"He's bringing the archive seals," Noor said, beside me at a rest stop, watching the same thread of light. "Official seizure orders. He means to make the breach a crime retroactively. Seal the site, seal the records, seal us."

"Paper," I said.

"Paper," she agreed, and there was something almost like humor in it, grim as gravel. "He fights with paper. So do we. Mine's sewn in my coat and yours is in your head. Let's see whose holds."

Tam, further down the ledge with the packs, was singing. Quietly, under his breath — a hauling song, a rope-song, the kind river men sang pulling barges against current. His breath made little clouds, and the song made small ghosts of itself in the cold, and I listened to a child carry a dead nation up a mountain in four notes and a rhythm, and I made myself keep moving.

Near midday the cloud closed for good and it began to snow — not falling snow but flying snow, horizontal, granular, stinging. The temperature dropped through freezing and kept going. I called the halt at a hollow behind a rib of granite, and we pressed together out of the wind, and I got out the map one last time before the light failed.

"We're here." My finger shook, and not only from cold. "Survey Point Twelve. The gallery mouth is two hours beyond, around this spur. The gate is steel, but—" I traced the line. "Kessel's maintenance hatch. It opens from the outside with a keyway he showed exactly one person how to find. It's behind this fall face. You have to know the rock."

"And you know the rock," Noor said.

"I know the rock," I said. "Thirty years ago I swore I'd forget it. Turns out forgetting was the one survey I ever failed."

Tam laughed — a short, startled bark, there and gone, the first laugh in two days — and then buried his face in his collar, and we pressed on.

* * *

The last two hours were the worst of my life, and I include the night the soldiers came to my mapping office and burned the national atlas page by page while I watched.

The snow thickened until the mountain abolished itself. I navigated by counting paces and by the feel of the slope under my boots and by a memory so old it had gone geological. Once I stepped onto a ledge that wasn't there and dropped to my elbow before Noor's fist closed on my coat and held me against the face of the world, and for a moment we hung there, two old fools pinned to a cliff in a white roar, and she shouted something I couldn't hear over the wind, and whatever it was, I decided to believe it was encouragement.

Then the spur, and the fall face — a sweep of frozen waterfall, blue-green ice furred with snow — and behind it, if my memory served, the hatch.

My memory served.

I chipped the ice away with the geologist's hammer we'd carried since the marshes, working blind, hands past feeling, guided by the shape of a summer afternoon three decades dead: *third course of stone, left of the seep, a keyway like a slanted eye.* The iron rim emerged from the rock like something surfacing from deep water. The mechanism was stiff with rust but intact — Kessel built things to be betrayed and still function — and when I put my weight on the lever it groaned, resisted, groaned again, and gave.

Cold air breathed out of the mountain, smelling of stone and old water and, impossibly, faintly of machine oil. Preserved air. Sealed air. The gallery had been waiting thirty years with its lungs full.

Noor got Tam through first, then herself, then me, and we dragged the hatch shut behind us as the storm arrived in earnest, howling against the rock like something cheated.

Inside: darkness, then Noor's lamp, then the gallery opening ahead of us — a vaulted tunnel cut through living rock, big enough for the maintenance carts of a vanished nation, running level and true toward the dam. Water sounded somewhere below the floor, in the mountain's own channels, moving the way water moves when it is patient.

And on the walls — I stopped. I actually stopped walking, and Noor bumped into me, and neither of us cared.

Painted on the concrete, in the quick white strokes of workmen signing their work: names. Hundreds of names. Crew rosters, shift tallies, dates, jokes, a crude drawing of a dog, a woman's name inside a heart, and over and over, in letters large and small, careful and hurried:

*Vessia. Vessia. Here worked Tomas Rehl. Vessia lives. Vessia.*

The Consortium had sealed the gallery and forgotten it, and the men who built it had written their country onto the walls of the mountain in the one place no censor would ever look.

Tam walked the length of the nearest panel with the lamp held high, reading names aloud, stumbling on the old spellings, getting them right on the second try. Noor stood with her hand flat against the concrete, over a painted date — the last month of legal Vessia — and she was crying without any noise at all, which I think surprised her more than it surprised me.

"They hid it in plain sight," she said at last. "All this time. Right under the dam."

"Ardan Kessel's crew," I said. My voice came out rough. "Every one of these names is a witness."

Ahead, the gallery ran straight into the dark, and from very far down its length came a new sound — a deep, rhythmic vibration through the rock, felt in the teeth more than the ears. The dam. The spillways. Thirty years of held-back river turning the concrete into a drum.

And behind us, muffled by the hatch and the mountain and the storm, another sound had joined the wind: voices. Lantern-light through the ice we hadn't fully cleared. Boots on the ledge.

They had followed us up the face. Of course they had. Quill had never needed the pass open — he had only needed us to show him the way.

Noor killed the lamp. In the sudden black, her hand found my sleeve.

"How far to the spillway vault?" she whispered.

I listened to the mountain hum its held note, and I answered the way I'd answered every hard question since the marshes.

"Close enough," I said. "Run."
