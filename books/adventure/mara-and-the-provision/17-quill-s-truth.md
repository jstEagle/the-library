## 17. Quill's Truth

The launch had been built for comfort, which was the first thing Quill had insisted upon when the Consortium commissioned its patrol fleet. Guns were cheap. Comfort was leverage. A man who had slept badly made poor decisions; a man who had dined well and dried his boots by a coal stove could be reasoned with, and reasoning was Quill's entire profession. He had not fired a weapon in nineteen years of service. He had never needed to. Paper did the killing, and paper never missed.

He stood at the rail now as the launch ground up through the last rapids below the headwaters, watching the gorge walls close in like a ledger being shut. The water here ran fast and thin and cold, the color of old glass, and it made him think — as it always did — of accounts. Every gallon that passed beneath him was an entry. Every entry was owed to someone. That was the whole architecture of the world, if you were honest about it: not nations, not flags, not the sentimental fictions cartographers drew in their warm rooms. Just water, moving downhill, and the men who kept the books on where it was allowed to go.

Behind him, in the cabin, the boy sat wrapped in a blanket he didn't need, drinking cocoa he hadn't asked for.

Quill had given strict instructions that the boy be treated gently. Not out of kindness — kindness was a currency he spent as deliberately as any other, and he spent it rarely — but because frightened children screamed and struggled and had to be carried, whereas a child who believed himself a guest would walk exactly where you wanted him to walk, and thank you for the tour. Tam had come aboard at the weir without a single tear. He had only looked back once, at the place where Noor Kavanagh had stood on the stone with her refusal still on her face, and then he had climbed into the launch like a small dignitary accepting an escort.

That had interested Quill more than the Provision ever had.

* * *

He reviewed the file again while the crew worked the lines through the narrows. He liked to review files. It was a form of prayer, he supposed, if prayer were honest.

Noor Thornbury. Former engineer, Northern Consolidated Lines, dismissed following the Vale Corren incident — the Commission's phrase for it, though everyone in the trade knew what it meant. She had signed the closure order on a line she knew, or should have known, sat above an unstable aquifer. The valley village drowned in forty minutes. Eleven dead, or fourteen, depending on which survey you credited; the numbers had never agreed, and Quill had long ago concluded that they disagreed because no one with authority had wanted them to agree. She had been stripped of her license, sued into ruin by three separate creditors, and turned loose into the river country to carry her debt like a millstone with her name stamped on it.

And then she had pulled a chest out of the shallows at Sallow Bend, and everything the Consortium had spent thirty years burying had come up dripping into the daylight.

Quill turned the page. Photographs: the harbor master, pale; the lockkeeper, older, hands shaking around a cup he never drank from; the signal box on the closed line, its window lit. Ilya Voss. Of course Voss. Quill had signed the man's exile papers himself, eleven years ago, and had always suspected it was the one signature in his career he'd regret. Exiled men kept maps. Exiled men kept grudges. And exiled men, given half a chance, kept company with women who pulled iron chests out of rivers.

The third photograph was the boy. Taken by a bank agent at the toll city, grainy, the child mid-stride with a stolen book under his arm. Quill had studied that photograph longer than any other document in the file. A boy born after the erasure, singing songs that had been officially deleted before his parents met. Songs do not survive in archives. Archives burn. Songs survive in children, and children are the one archive no edict can reach, because you cannot confiscate what is sung under breath while skipping stones.

Which was why the Consortium had never feared rebellion. Rebellions could be bought, starved, shot, and — most efficiently — forgotten. What the Consortium feared was memory, and memory traveled by routes no patrol boat could blockade.

He closed the file. Above the gorge, the light was going the color of tarnished silver, and somewhere ahead, hidden behind two ridgelines of black granite, the dam waited — the largest thing the Consortium had ever built and the smallest thing it had ever admitted to.

* * *

They made the final approach on foot, because the last mile of the service road had been blasted years ago and replaced with a funicular that answered to a single key, and Quill held the single key. The boy walked beside him without being told to. Quill noted this. Children who walked beside their captors rather than behind them were either very brave or very clever, and in Quill's experience the clever ones were the dangerous ones, and the brave ones were merely the ones who died first.

"You're not what I expected," the boy said, as they climbed. His voice had the flatness children use when they are deciding whether to hate you.

"What did you expect?"

"Gray coats. Men who don't talk."

"I talk," Quill said. "It's cheaper than the alternative." He stepped over a rusted cable drum. "Do you know why we wear gray, Tam? Not black. Black is for mourning, and we have nothing to mourn. Gray is the color of paperwork. Of carbon copies. Of the space between yes and no. We are the color of things not yet decided, and we have decided that nothing will ever be decided without us."

"That's a stupid color," Tam said.

Quill smiled. "Yes," he said. "It is. But it holds."

The dam appeared all at once, the way it always did — one moment there was granite and pine and the road bending, and then the trees fell away and there it was: the face of it, four hundred feet of poured concrete rising out of the mountain's throat, stamped across its brow with the Consortium crest, the interlocked rings wide enough to be read from the far shore of the reservoir it held. Behind it, the water stood dark and enormous and perfectly still, a lake that had no business existing at this altitude, a lake that had drowned three valleys and a national border and, folded into its spillway vaults, sealed in concrete behind blast doors, the entire written memory of a country called Vessia.

The boy stopped walking. Quill watched him look at it, and saw something pass over the child's face that he had seen on the faces of men twice his age standing in front of banks they could not pay: the understanding of scale. Of how large a thing could be and still call itself nothing.

"It's ugly," Tam said finally.

"Yes," Quill agreed. "Ugly things hold best. Beautiful things get defended out of love, and love runs out. Ugly things are defended out of interest, and interest never does."

* * *

He found them at the crest, where the service gallery opened onto the parapet walk. They had come up the eastern approach — he'd known they would; Voss's pre-erasure surveys showed a maintenance stair behind the old weather station, and exiles always came home by the doors they'd once used — and they stood now near the parapet's edge, two figures against the vast still water, wind pulling at their clothes.

Noor Thornbury heard them before she saw them. He watched her turn, watched her take in the gray coats fanning along the parapet — six of them, unarmed-looking, which was itself a kind of armament — and then find the boy, and he watched what happened to her face. It was not relief and it was not guilt. It was arithmetic. He recognized it because it was his own expression. For one strange moment they might have been colleagues.

"Mr. Quill," she said.

"Engineer Thornbury." He inclined his head. "You've made me chase you across four hundred miles of dying river. I want you to know I've enjoyed almost none of it. The food alone—"

"Let him go."

"Of course." He gestured, and the nearest coat took Tam's shoulder and steered him aside, not roughly, to the lee of the gallery door. "He's been a model guest. Asked excellent questions. You're raising him well, or perhaps he's raising himself; the file is unclear." He came forward until only ten feet of wet concrete lay between them, and stopped, because distance mattered in negotiations, and he had never once in his career closed it too soon. "Before we discuss terms, I'm going to tell you the truth. All of it. You've earned that much, and frankly, you'll make a better decision with it than without it, and I want your decision to be good."

She said nothing. Beside her, Voss had gone very still, the way men go still when they recognize the shape of a trap and can't yet see the door.

"The official history," Quill said, "which you've doubtless had recited to you by now by our young friend, is that Vessia rebelled. That the Provision Government armed the headwater provinces, threatened the corridor treaties, and was dissolved for the security of the region. There are murals. There are textbooks. There is a holiday." He let the wind take a beat. "None of it is true. Vessia never raised an army. Vessia raised a ledger."

He reached into his coat and produced, with the care of a man handling a relic, a folded sheet of old paper — brittle, foxed, stamped with the same seal that rode sewn into Noor's lining thirty feet away.

"The original water compact. Signed by every nation on this watershed, sixty-one years ago. Do you know what it says? It says the source is common. It says no signatory may dam, divert, or claim the headwaters, and that the flow shall be measured jointly, by Vessian surveyors, because Vessian surveyors were the only ones every government trusted." He smiled thinly. "Trust. There was so much of it, once. It was the most dangerous document on the continent. Not because of what it granted — because of what it prevented. Whoever holds the source holds every downstream nation by the throat. Turn a wheel, and Kestrel's wheat fails. Close a gate, and the Sallow delta turns to salt. Vessia was erased not because it rebelled, Mr. Thornbury, but because it refused to. Because it stood on this mountain and said: the water belongs to everyone, and we will measure it fairly, forever."

He let the paper catch the wind, just slightly, just enough that Noor's eyes followed it.

"So we buried it. We buried the compact, and the census rolls, and the court records, and the land titles, and the birth registers — everything that proved a people existed and owned what they owned. We poured them into the spillway vaults and poured concrete over them, and we built the largest dam on the continent on top of the grave, and we called the whole operation a provision. Which, I grant you, was elegant. Even I admire it, and I wrote half the memos." He folded the compact away. "Thirty years of perfect quiet. One river, one hand on the wheel, six grateful downstream governments that never ask where their rain comes from. And then a bankrupt engineer dredging for salvage fees pulls the master copy of the Provision out of a sandbar."

* * *

Noor had not moved. Quill gave her credit for that; most people shifted, fidgeted, filled silence with noise. She simply stood, wind-flattened, eyes narrowed, doing what engineers did: loading the facts and watching for the failure point.

"You're telling me this," she said slowly, "because you want me to understand there's no going back. That selling the Provision to my creditors wouldn't save me. You'd bury me with it."

"I'm telling you this," Quill said, "because I want to offer you a job."

Beside her, Voss made a sound — half laugh, half wound.

"Think about what I've just described," Quill went on, and his voice changed, dropped its polish, became almost conversational, which was the most dangerous register he owned. "A single hand on the wheel. Six nations fed or starved by the turning of it. Thirty years without a war on this watershed — do you know why? Not treaties. Not goodwill. Plumbing. Every drought that would have become a border war became an irrigation schedule instead. Every famine that would have marched an army became a freight manifest. I have kept more peace with gate schedules than all the diplomats on the continent have kept with signatures." He spread his hands. "Is it theft? Of course it's theft. It is also the only reason the Sallow delta ate last winter. The world is not divided into thieves and honest men, Engineer. It is divided into those who steal carefully and those who steal carelessly, and the careless ones drown villages."

He saw it land. He saw it land in her face the way he'd known it would — because he had read her file, all of it, including the parts her creditors never found. Vale Corren. Forty minutes. Eleven dead, or fourteen.

"You signed a closure once," Quill said quietly. "You did the mathematics and you signed, and the water went where the mathematics sent it, and people died, and the line stayed open, and the region stayed fed. You have already done the work I'm offering you. The only difference between us is that I've never pretended otherwise, and you've spent nine years drowning in it." He stepped forward, one pace, onto the last stretch of concrete between them. "So here are the terms. You bring me the Provision. In exchange: your debts — all three instruments — purchased and burned. Your license restored. A commission. Headwater Division. You would hold the source, Ms. Thornbury. You, personally, with my signature behind yours. You would decide whose fields drink and whose don't, and you would do it competently, which is more than anyone in this Consortium's history has managed, and more than a breached dam and a free river will ever manage, because a free river feeds whoever happens to live nearest it and lets the rest starve."

Behind him, Tam had come out of the lee of the doorway. Quill heard the boy's steps and did not turn. Let him hear. Children should learn early what the world actually cost.

"And Vessia?" Noor said. Her voice was level. Too level. "The vaults. The records. The people your concrete is sitting on."

"Amnesty for the archivists. A museum, eventually — a real one, funded, honest, in some agreeable river town. Memory preserved, curated, harmless." Quill shrugged. "The dead keep beautifully, Ms. Thornbury. It's the living who insist on land."

"No," said the boy.

Quill turned. Tam stood at the edge of the parapet group, blanket gone, fists at his sides, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word and he said it again anyway, louder, the second time in tune — and Quill realized with a small cold shock that the boy was singing, that the no had a melody to it, an old rising cadence, and that two of the gray coats had gone faintly rigid hearing it, because they were river-born, and they knew the tune from somewhere they could not name.

"The answer," Noor said, "is no."

Quill looked back at her. He had offered this bargain four times in his career. Three times it had been accepted, and those three men now administered watersheds and slept soundly. Once it had been refused, and that man was a line in a file Quill kept in a drawer he did not show visitors.

"You're going to fire charges you don't have," he said. "Against a structure you couldn't crack with army artillery. On a mountain closing down for winter, with my column an hour behind me and your boy standing next to mine."

"Probably," Noor said. And then she put her hand inside her coat, and brought out not the Provision but a coil of wire and a detonator body salvaged from a signal box, and Ilya Voss — who had surveyed these spillways under duress thirty years ago and had evidently spent every year since learning exactly where the concrete was thinnest — stepped away from the parapet edge toward the gallery stairs, and began, in the accent of a drowned country, to count.

Quill did not draw a weapon. He had not fired one in nineteen years.

Instead, very calmly, he began calculating how much water four hundred feet of head pressure moved in the first ninety seconds after a breach — and whether the funicular key in his pocket would matter at all.
