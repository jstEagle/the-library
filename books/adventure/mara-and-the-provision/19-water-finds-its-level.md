## 19. Water Finds Its Level

Tam heard the dam break before he understood what he was hearing.

It came up the valley as a sound with no edges — not a crash, not a roar, but a pressure, like the whole world clearing its throat. The reeds around the marsh channel shivered though there was no wind. Birds went up off the water in a single gray sheet. Old Sefa, the ferrywoman who had hidden them two nights past, stopped mid-oar and said one word in Vessian that Tam had never been taught and knew anyway: *Runa.* The river. As if it were a name being called.

Then the water came.

He stood on the levee at Marrow's Crossing because Sefa had put him there, her cracked hand flat between his shoulder blades, and pushed him up the earthen stair ahead of her. Below, the channel that had been a slow brown ribbon all his life swelled like something inhaling. It did not arrive so much as *arrive* — a wall of churned foam and torn reed and one enormous, impossible log turning end over end like a man tumbling down a stairwell. The willows at the bend went under without a fight. The old toll bridge — the one where the Consortium men checked papers and Tam had once watched his father take off his hat and hold it to his chest for no reason anyone would explain — groaned once, lifted its whole span like a drawbridge being raised by ghosts, and folded into the flood.

The bridge was gone. Just gone. And with it, Tam understood dimly, the checkpoint, the ledger of names, the stamp that decided who could cross and who had to stay on their own side of a line drawn by men in gray coats.

"Look," he said, and his voice came out small and strange. "Sefa. Look at the bridge."

"I'm looking, boy." She was not looking at the bridge. She was looking upstream, toward the mountains, where the sky above the drowned valley had turned the color of milk, and she was smiling in a way that frightened him more than the water did. "Thirty years I've paid the headwater tax. Thirty years they told us the river belonged to somebody else." She spat over the levee, an old habit, deliberate as prayer. "Let them come collect."

* * *

The flood took its time deciding what to keep.

By midmorning the water had spread across the low fields until the valley looked like a second sky lying on its back, cloud-colored and moving. Fences vanished. Boundary stones — Tam had always thought of them as teeth, little stone teeth biting the earth into plots and parishes and tax districts — disappeared one by one, and where each one went under, nothing marked the difference between one man's ground and another's. A Consortium survey post, a plank tower the color of bone that had watched the crossing for as long as Tam could remember, leaned, sighed, and lay down in the current like an animal bedding itself.

People came up onto the levees the way ants come out after rain. First the ferry families, then the eel-catchers from the oxbow, then folk from the inland farms driving their animals ahead of them, carts stacked with hens and grandmothers and copper pots. Nobody screamed. That was the thing Tam would remember all his life, the thing he would try and fail to explain to people who hadn't seen it: nobody screamed. They climbed the levee and turned around and watched the water erase the borders of their lives, and they were quiet, and some of them wept, and some of them laughed, and most of them did both at once, which is a sound Tam had never heard before and has never heard since.

An old man near the ferry landing knelt down and put his palm flat on the wet grass of the levee crown, as if taking a pulse.

"The meadows," he said. "My father's meadows. Under it." He didn't sound angry. He sounded like a man reading a letter he'd waited a long time for.

"They'll say it's ruin," said his wife.

"They'll say whatever's written down," the old man answered. "But look at it. Look at it, Marta. There's no line down the middle anymore. You can't see where Vessia ended and the rest began. Because it doesn't."

Tam's chest went tight. He looked. He couldn't see it either — couldn't see anything but water going wherever water pleased, which was, he realized, everywhere the lines used to be.

* * *

He didn't decide to sing. That was the strange part, the part he'd turn over and over in the years after. His mouth simply opened, the way it always did when the song came, the way it had come to him since before he could remember — in the reed beds, in the dark of the barge hold while Noor traced letters under his finger and Ilya listened from the doorway with his face turned away. The tune walked up and then fell, like feet climbing a levee and stepping off the far side, and the words were words he'd never been taught in any language anyone claimed to speak:

*Ondra vesh, ondra vesh,*
*the water remembers what the paper forgets...*

He sang it the way you breathe. Around him the levee went still, section by section, the way wind goes through a field of grain. Faces turned. An eel-catcher woman with mud to her knees dropped her basket. Somewhere behind him a baby stopped crying.

When he finished, the silence lasted three heartbeats. Then the old man with the wet palm — the one whose father's meadows were drowning — picked up the next verse.

Tam spun around. "You know it?"

"My mother sang it." The man's voice was rough, uncertain, like a door opening on a room locked for thirty years. "I haven't — I thought I'd lost the middle of it. The middle of it, boy, how does the middle go?"

So Tam taught it back to him, standing on the levee at Marrow's Crossing with the flood roaring approval below, and the old man sang it, and his wife sang it, badly, laughing, and then the eel-catcher woman sang a different verse entirely, one Tam had never heard, and then a ferryman added a line about the winter crossings, and the song grew hands and passed itself along the levee like a bucket at a fire — except the fire was the opposite of a fire, it was a country coming back up out of the water.

That was when Tam understood what Ilya had tried to tell him in the marsh, the night the dying archivist unrolled his rescued papers by lamplight: *They burned the books, Tam. But songs don't live in books. Songs live in children, and children are the one archive nobody thinks to search.* He had thought it was just something old men said. Now he stood inside it. He was the archive. All these scattered verses living in grandmothers' mouths and boatmen's humming and a boy born after the erasure who sang what he was never taught — the purge had missed them, had rolled right over them like the water rolling over boundary stones, because you cannot drown what keeps surfacing in the young.

A girl his own age, barefoot, hair full of straw, tugged his sleeve. "Sing the one about the lockkeeper's daughter. My gran hums it but she only knows four words."

"I'll trade you," Tam said. "What's the one your gran hums?"

And that was how the afternoon went: children trading songs like coins, correcting each other, arguing over verses, piecing the country together out of fragments the way Ilya pieced maps together out of memory. Every scrap someone had been told to forget. Every scrap kept anyway.

* * *

Toward evening the first boats came down.

Not rescue boats. Not Consortium launches with their gray paint and their loudspeakers. Fishing boats, skiffs, a coal barge riding dangerously high and empty, all of them crowded with people from the upper valley — people from the drowned towns, the ones whose homes had gone under when the dam first closed the waters thirty years ago and who had been resettled, reassigned, renamed. They came down the new current waving anything white, and when they reached the crossing they shouted up at the levees, and their voices carried strange over the wide water:

"The dam's breached! The face is broken! The vaults are open!"

"The records! The archive vaults — they're washing downstream —"

"Papers in the water! Papers everywhere! The Provision's void — a woman signed it void —"

Tam gripped the levee grass. *Noor.* He'd been swept free of Quill's launch before the breach; he'd been carried downriver half-drowned and deposited on a sandbar like driftwood, and everything since was blur and borrowed boats. He didn't know if she'd made it off the dam face. He didn't know about Ilya. He didn't know about Quill, whether the gray-coated man had fallen or climbed or was even now coming down this same current with orders in his pocket.

But the people on the boats kept shouting, and one of them, a woman with a bandaged arm, called out a description: *"There was an engineer — a tall woman, scarred hand — she read the clause aloud off the top of the spillway and then the charges went — they're saying she signed the whole provision dead, every claim, every debt —"*

Every claim. Every debt. Tam thought of Mr. Quill's courteous voice, the way he'd held Tam's shoulder on the launch and said, *Your friend owes a great deal, boy. Paper is patient.* Well. The paper wasn't patient anymore. The paper was pulp, floating down the valley alongside the boundary stones and the toll ledgers and the official histories, all of it dissolving together into brown water, equal at last.

"She's alive," the bandaged woman added, seeing Tam's face. "Last anyone saw, she was on the high abutment with a cartographer. Walking out. Slow, but walking."

Tam sat down hard on the levee and laughed until he cried, and the levee laughed with him, because by then everyone was crying or laughing or both, and no one asked why.

* * *

Night came down on a valley with no borders.

Fires bloomed along the levees — dozens of them, hundreds, the whole crescent of the high ground ringed with light like a necklace. Nobody had organized it. Nobody had to. People shared bread with strangers whose fields had flooded into theirs, and discovered, checking names against the old geography, that their grandparents had been neighbors before the lines were redrawn. A man found the family his grandfather had been deported from. Two women argued joyfully about which side of a vanished stream a chapel had stood on, and resolved it by agreeing that it didn't matter, the stream was back now and could stand wherever it liked.

Consortium authority did not so much collapse as evaporate. At the crossing, the tollhouse — the little gray office where papers were stamped — stood empty, its door swinging, its ledger open on the desk and its pages curling in the damp. Someone had laid a fish on it. Tam walked past and saw the ink running, the record of crossings blurring into a gray wash, and he felt no triumph, exactly. He felt the way the valley looked: scoured, rearranged, waiting to find its level.

Near midnight, the crowd on the main levee thickened, and Tam was pushed gently forward — Sefa's hand again, between his shoulder blades, the same push, up the same earthen stair — until he stood on the flood-marker post, the old stone pillar that recorded the historic high waters, dates chiseled up its sides like a ladder. Someone had thrust a torch into his hand.

"They want the songs," Sefa said. "All of them. Everything you've got, boy. Tonight, while they're gathered. Tomorrow the gray coats regroup — they always regroup — and this—" she swept her arm at the fires, the crowd, the wide dark water — "this window doesn't stay open. So sing."

Tam looked out at the faces. Farmers and ferrymen, eel-catchers and grandmothers, children on shoulders, the bandaged woman from the boats, the old man who'd recovered the middle of the verse. Behind them, the flood lay silver under the moon, flat and vast and level, touching every bank alike, asking nothing, owning nothing, belonging to whoever needed crossing.

He thought of Noor signing the clause. Of Ilya saying, *maps are promises.* Of the archivist dying among his rescued papers, whispering, *keep it moving downstream.*

He lifted the torch and began with the oldest song he knew, the one that started *ondra vesh*, and by the second verse the whole levee was singing, and by the third, other levees downstream had caught it, the sound traveling along the river like a lamp being handed from window to window, and Tam sang until his voice cracked and then kept singing anyway, because tonight the river had a thousand voices and his was only one of them, and that — he understood, finally — was the whole point of everything.

Far downstream, past the last of the fires, the water ran on through the broken country toward the sea, carrying papers, carrying songs, carrying news.

And somewhere along its banks, Tam was certain, a tall woman with a scarred hand was walking north beside a cartographer — and the river would bring them back to him.
