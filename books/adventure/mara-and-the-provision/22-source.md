## 22. Source

The road up the headwaters had been a warpath once. Now it was just a road.

Noor walked it in the last week of thaw, when the passes were still bleeding snowmelt and the switchbacks ran silver with runoff. A year ago she had climbed this same grade with Ilya Voss one stumbling step behind her, both of them half-dead from cold, pursued by a column of Consortium men who believed — with the serene confidence of people who had never been contradicted by a river — that paper could hold back water.

She had come back alone. Not because she wanted to be alone. Because some things you have to check on yourself, the way you check a bridge you built, or a lie you told, or a grave you dug.

The road smelled of pine and wet stone. In the low places, wildflowers had come up through the old rail bed — white ones first, then purple, colonizing the ties like settlers moving into an abandoned house. The rails themselves were gone; scavengers had pulled them within months of the breach, and Noor couldn't find it in herself to mind. Iron that had carried nothing but closure orders for thirty years deserved better work.

She walked all morning. By noon the trees thinned and the air changed, took on that mineral coldness that comes off deep water, and she crested the last shoulder of granite and saw it.

The dam.

Even broken, even gutted, it was enormous — a gray wall of concrete two hundred feet high wedged between the canyon walls, stamped across its face with the Consortium crest, the interlocked rings that had meant *provision* to everyone downstream and *obedience* to everyone upstream. But the crest was split now, right through the center, where the spillway charges had done their work. A gap forty yards wide opened in the heart of the structure, ragged-edged, and through it the river came down in three great braided channels, white and loud and utterly indifferent to everything human beings had ever decided about it.

Noor stood on the overlook and let the sound of it fill her the way it had filled her the first time, standing on this same rock with her hand on the plunger wire, terror and certainty occupying the same body at the same moment.

She had expected to feel something else this time. Triumph, maybe. Or grief. She felt instead what she always felt at good engineering: an appreciation for how long a thing would last. The breach was already softening at its edges, concrete crumbling into gravel, gravel into sand. In fifty years there would be no wall at all, only a gorge with some odd geometric rubble in it, and children would argue about whether anyone had really built a dam here.

Good, she thought. Let them argue. Arguing is how memory stays alive. Monuments are how it dies.

* * *

She picked her way down to the structure itself, along a path that hadn't existed a year ago — a goat track, really, worn into the scree by other pilgrims. That was another thing she'd expected and still wasn't prepared for: the others. Ribbons tied to a rusted ladder rung. A child's boot, long outgrown, set upright on a ledge like an offering. Someone had painted words on the lower face of the dam in careful Vessian script, and though Noor's reading of the language had never gotten past functional, she recognized the shape of the letters now, the way you recognize a song even when you can't sing it. Tam could have translated it for her. Tam could translate anything these days. The boy had grown four inches and acquired a following, which he bore with the exasperated dignity of a boy who mostly wanted to be climbing things.

At the base of the breach, where the main channel poured through, the spray hung in the air like weather. Noor worked her way around to the eastern abutment, to the shelf of stone above the old intake works, and there she stopped, because the water coming through the broken concrete was running clean.

Not brown with thirty years of silt, not choked with the drowned archive's paper pulp, as it had been in those first terrible weeks when the flood carried whole forests of documents down the valley — ledgers and land rolls and birth registers tumbling through the drowned towns like migrating birds. Clean. Spring-water clear, green-white over the stones, so that she could see the bottom of the shallows, see the pebbles sorted by size as if by a patient hand.

The source was doing what sources do. It didn't know it had ever been forbidden.

She sat down on the stone, unwrapped the bread and hard cheese she'd carried up, and ate slowly, watching the light move on the water. This was the thing nobody had warned her about in all the years of her disgrace — not the shame, not the debt, not even Quill's courteous voice reading terms aloud in the dark. What nobody warned her about was how quiet life could get afterward, when the thing you were running from and the thing you were running toward turned out to be the same place, and you finally arrived, and there was nothing left to do but sit down.

* * *

She thought about her compass.

It had gone into the river off the barge, a year and a season ago, torn loose when she went over the side with the Provision sewn against her ribs — the needle case ripped from her coat by the current, spinning away into the brown water while she kicked for the reeds. Her father's compass. Brass-cased, glass-cracked, the one object she'd kept through the bankruptcy and the blacklisting and every closed door after. Losing it had hurt worse than the cold water did. It had felt, at the time, like losing the part of herself that knew which way was north.

Ilya had found it. Of course he had. Six weeks later, working the shallows below the ferry crossing with a dredge net, hunting fragments of the archive the way other men hunted fish, he'd brought it up tangled in a register of Vessian marriages, 1889 to 1903. He'd cleaned it, fitted new glass, reset the needle on a lodestone he kept for his survey instruments, and mailed it to her care of the Sallow Bend harbor master — the same harbor master who had once sold her name to the Consortium and now, chastened and half-forgiven, handled her post.

*Found your north,* the note said, in his precise cartographer's hand. *You were carrying it all along. The needle just needed reminding.*

She'd carried it since. Through the winter of the water councils, through the spring of the reopened lines, through all the tedious, miraculous paperwork of a country reassembling itself from what the flood had given back. And somewhere in that winter she had understood why she couldn't keep it.

A compass is a tool for finding your way to a place you're not yet. She was here. There was nowhere left to get to.

But you don't throw away your father's compass. You give it work.

* * *

She finished eating and took it out of her pack.

It looked good in the light — Ilya's new glass catching the sun, the brass polished but not refinished, the dents left honestly in place. She held it flat on her palm and watched the needle swing, settle, hold steady on north, which here meant upriver, into the mountains, toward whatever unguarded snowfield the water came from. Toward the blank space at the top of Ilya's new map — the map that hung now in the council hall at Sallow Bend, and in the schoolhouse at the drowned station town, and in a hundred other rooms, with its deliberate empty places and its single line of text beneath the title block: *To be completed by those who live there.*

Tam had asked her once, sprawled on the barge deck with the Provision open on his chest, teaching himself letters off its clauses: *What happens when the whole map's filled in?*

*Ilya says a finished map is a dead map,* she'd told him. *He leaves blanks on purpose.*

*Why?*

*So somebody has a reason to go look.*

The needle held. North. Upstream. Always had been, if she was honest — every contract she'd ever taken, every line she'd ever surveyed, all of it pointed the same direction, toward the source of things. She'd just spent most of her life pretending the pointing was about money.

She stood, crossed to the base of the dam where the oldest concrete met the living rock, and knelt. There was a hollow there, a socket where some long-vanished instrument mount had been bolted before the erasure — a surveyor's fitting, Ilya had told her, from the original Vessian headwater survey, the one he'd helped betray and then spent his life undoing. Water seeped through the crack above it and dripped into the hollow, keeping it full, keeping it clean.

She set the compass in it, needle north, face up, where the spring water could reach it.

It sat there in the running water, brass going bright, then green, then bright again, and Noor understood that she was not leaving it. She was stationing it. The way you station a benchmark, a fixed point from which all future measurements are taken. Someday some child would climb the goat track and find a compass embedded in the ruins of a dam, needle true, and would carry it down the mountain and use it to go looking, and that was better than any vault, any ledger, any provision. Memory doesn't need a keeper. It needs a bearing.

She stood and brushed the dirt from her knees.

Below her, the river came through the broken wall without pause or ceremony, the way it had before the wall, the way it would after every wall. Down in the valley it would be turning the wheels of the reopened mills, filling the irrigation cuts, running under the provisional borders that towns were already arguing about redrawing — arguing loudly, messily, in assembly halls and taverns, which Ilya insisted was the sound a country makes when it's alive. Somewhere down there Tam was probably late for something. Somewhere down there Ilya was drawing, leaving blanks.

Noor shouldered her pack and started down the mountain, taking the long way, along the old rail bed where the flowers were coming up.

There was a contract waiting in Sallow Bend — a survey of the reopened high line, forty miles of track through country no engineer had properly measured in thirty years, and they wanted it done by someone who understood what water does to a valley. They'd offered her a crew. She'd asked for one apprentice instead, on the condition that he be allowed to bring his songs.

The road went down. The river went down beside it, faster, the way rivers do, and she let it pass her without minding. She knew where it was going. It knew where she was going. Between them, they had it all covered.

Behind her, in its hollow of stone, the needle held steady on north, and the clean water ran over it, and needed no keeper at all.
