## 1. The Blacklist

Seven years, Lena Nakamura had decided, was a stupid way to measure a life. Years were for people whose lives happened to them in rooms — offices, courtrooms, kitchens. Hers happened on trails, and trails came in miles.

So she measured in miles. Two thousand, four hundred and change since the incident, if you counted the switchbacks, which she did, because switchbacks were where she'd done most of her living lately. She kept the tally in a battered notebook that had once belonged to her father, along with his habit of underlining things twice when they mattered and three times when they were lies.

The trail this morning was a mud chute above the border town of Corven's Hollow, where the government paid eleven dollars an hour for people to carry gravel uphill so that tourists could slip downhill with dignity. Lena swung her pick-mattock into the slope, levered out a rock the size of a loaf of bread, and thought, not for the first time, that she had once been quoted in a national magazine as *the finest high-route guide of her generation*.

The quote had been accurate at the time. The magazine had not sent a correction.

"Yo, Nakamura." Denny, the crew lead, materialized beside her with the particular cheerfulness of a man who had never been blamed for anything. "You're digging that section like it insulted your mother."

"It's good practice," Lena said.

"For what?"

"For digging."

Denny laughed like this was the funniest thing he'd heard all season, which it possibly was, and moved off to tell someone else about drainage angles. Lena watched him go and felt the familiar thing settle over her — not sadness exactly, more like weather. A low front of resignation that had been parked over her life so long she'd stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a hum.

Seven years ago she had stood in a hearing room in the capital, hands flat on a table, and told six men in good jackets that the Halvorsen Expedition's route above the icefall was a death sentence, that the client list included two teenagers, and that she would not sign off on it. Seven years ago the company's lawyers had produced seventeen witnesses who remembered things differently. Seven years ago the blacklist went up, quiet as frost, and every guiding outfit, every expedition broker, every park service from here to the coast quietly stopped returning her calls.

She hadn't even lost the argument dramatically. That was the part that still stung on cold mornings. There had been no shouting match, no scandal, no moment she could replay and fix. Just paperwork moving through channels, and then a door closing so smoothly she'd spent two years checking whether it had actually shut.

It had.

She drove the mattock in again. The rock came free. Somewhere below, the Kestrel River ran brown and busy with snowmelt, heading north toward country she tried not to think about. The Kestrel rose in the Rivergate — everyone knew that much — a sealed canyon at the head of the watershed that no permit office issued permits for and no map in her father's collection showed in anything better than a dotted line. Her father had drawn that dotted line himself, thirty years ago, and had gone to his grave with the word *fraud* stapled to his name because of what he'd claimed lay beyond it.

Lena had inherited exactly two things from Elias Nakamura: his notebooks, and the family talent for telling the truth in rooms full of people who preferred otherwise.

She was twenty-nine years old, she had walked two thousand four hundred miles away from one mistake that wasn't hers, and she was carrying gravel for eleven dollars an hour in a town whose idea of excitement was a dispute over the diner's pie rotation.

This was fine. She had made her peace with fine.

The peace lasted until lunch.

* * *

The man was waiting by the crew truck, and everything about him was wrong for Corven's Hollow. His coat was the kind of gray that costs money. His boots had never met mud. He held a leather folder against his chest like a hymnal, and he watched the crew come down off the slope with the patient attention of a heron watching water.

Lena spotted him from fifty yards and knew, with the flat certainty of long experience, that he was there for her. Nobody else on the crew warranted a heron.

"You're Nakamura," he said, when she got close. Not a question. He offered a hand. "Aldous Vane, Ministry of Water and Boundaries. I've been looking for you."

"That's a shame," Lena said, and kept walking toward the truck.

He fell into step beside her, unhurried, as though being rebuffed were simply another item on his itinerary. "I imagine you get a lot of people looking for you."

"I get Denny looking for me. Denny wants the culvert cleared by Thursday." She dropped her pack on the tailgate and started unbuckling her gloves. "If you're from an outfitter, the answer's no. If you're from a journalist writing a retrospective piece, the answer's also no, but slower."

"Neither." Vane opened the folder. "Six weeks ago, a ministry survey team entered the upper Kestrel corridor. Four people. They were due out on the twenty-second. They have not come out."

Lena's hands went still on the buckle. It was a small stillness, half a second, but she saw him register it, and she disliked him a little for how carefully he was watching.

"That's a search-and-rescue matter," she said. "That's not my department. My department is gravel."

"The official SAR response was mounted and withdrawn." His voice stayed level, pleasant, a voice designed for reading terms aloud. "The gorge is closed terrain. After nine days the ministry concluded the team is beyond recovery and sealed the file. I'm authorized to reopen it. Privately."

"And you want me because—"

"Because you are, by every record I could pull — including some records that were supposed to be pulled — the best route-finder working anywhere near this watershed. And because you're available." He said *available* the way other men said *unemployed*, without cruelty, just precision, and Lena felt the old frost creep up the back of her neck.

"No," she said.

Vane turned a page in the folder. "The compensation is substantial."

"I said no."

"Triple your current rate. Per day. Plus completion bonus." He let that sit. Down the slope, Denny was hollering something about Thursday and the culvert, and the words *triple your current rate* hung in the air between them like a coin spinning.

Lena looked at the river instead of at him. This was the trap of need: it didn't argue with pride, it just waited beside it, humming. She had a bank account containing four hundred and twelve dollars and a plan to be in another country by spring — a real plan, saved-for, dreamed-over, the kind of plan that required money she was going to earn one gravel-carrying day at a time for the next eleven years.

"What kind of survey team," she said slowly, "does a ministry send into closed terrain?"

For the first time, Vane paused. It was a small pause, well-managed, but she caught it, the way she'd catch a hollow sound underfoot.

"A hydrographic survey," he said. "Routine."

"Routine surveys don't vanish. And routine ministries don't hire blacklisted guides off trail crews." She finally looked at him straight. "What aren't you telling me, Mr. Vane?"

"What I'm telling you," Vane said, closing the folder, "is that four people are missing behind the Rivergate, and the one person alive who knows that canyon's approach better than any map does is currently being paid eleven dollars an hour to move rocks." He produced a card — heavy stock, embossed, absurd in this place — and set it on the tailgate. "The contract is ready when you are. I'm at the Ferrow's Landing station through Friday."

He walked away down the road, gray coat, unhurried, a man who had never once been rained on unexpectedly.

Lena picked up the card. Turned it over. Put it in her breast pocket, telling herself she only wanted it for the weight, something to throw at a bear.

Then she shouldered her pack and went back up the slope to dig, because the culvert wasn't going to clear itself, and because walking was the only prayer she knew.

* * *

That night in her rented room above the feed store, she took out her father's notebook — the early one, the one with the contour-line lessons in the margins in his cramped, patient hand. *Read the lines like rings in a tree,* he'd written when she was eight. *Close together means steep. A circle with no exit means a summit or a sinkhole. The map never lies, Lena. People lie using maps.*

People lie using maps. She'd underlined that one twice herself, years later, in pencil, before she understood it was a warning and not a philosophy.

She sat on the bed with the ministry card face-down on the blanket and made the honest list, because her father had taught her that too: make the honest list, then decide, then live with it.

Reasons to say no. The Rivergate was closed for reasons nobody stated, which meant the reasons were bad. Vane was a ministry man with a smooth pause where answers should be. And the last time she'd taken a contract that smelled wrong, it had cost her seven years.

Reasons to say yes. Four hundred and twelve dollars. Four missing people who might, against all odds, be alive. And underneath those, in handwriting she didn't want to examine too closely: the Kestrel rose in the Rivergate, and her father had dotted that line, and she had spent seven years walking away from things and had gotten very, very good at it, and maybe — just maybe — she was tired.

Maybe she wanted to walk toward something once, even if it was a mistake. Especially if it was hers to choose.

Outside, the Kestrel muttered in the dark, running north toward the gorge it came out of, patient as anything. Rivers didn't measure their lives in miles either, she thought. They just went where the land told them, and wore the land down doing it.

Lena turned the card over. Read the number. Memorized it out of spite, then caught herself reaching for the phone anyway.

"One contract," she said to the empty room, to the river, to the dotted line on a dead man's map. "Money for a fresh start abroad. Nothing else. I find them, I get paid, I leave."

The room, sensibly, said nothing.

In the morning she called the number before she could talk herself out of it, and Aldous Vane answered on the first ring, as though he'd been holding the phone since Friday, and said, "Miss Nakamura. I hoped you'd see reason."

And Lena, packing her gear with hands that were steadier than she felt, told herself the small cold feeling in her stomach was just hunger, and believed it almost all the way to Ferrow's Landing.
