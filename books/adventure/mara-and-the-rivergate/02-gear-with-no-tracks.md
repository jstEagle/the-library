## 2. Gear With No Tracks

Lena had a rule about other people's campsites: you could read them the way her father used to read contour lines. Every camp was a sentence written in dirt and nylon, and most of them said something like *we were tired, we ate badly, we argued about the stove*. Seven years of walking through other people's sentences had made her fluent.

The survey team's last camp said nothing at all.

She stood at the edge of it for a long moment before stepping in, the way you'd pause at the threshold of a house where something felt wrong. The site sat on a gravel bench above the Kestrel, maybe two hours past the last reliable trail, tucked against a wall of granite that rose so sheer it looked planed by hand. Four tents — government-issue dome tents, the kind that came in colors no mountain had ever thought of wearing. All four pitched. All four staked properly. All four empty.

"Hello?" she called, because procedure was procedure, even when every hair on her arms already knew the answer.

Nothing answered except the river, talking to itself in its low constant voice.

She circled the perimeter first, boots careful, eyes down. This was the part of search work nobody romanticized: reading ground. And the ground here was good for it — fine gravel and old snowmelt silt, the kind of surface that held a print for days, held a print practically until spring. She found the team's prints easily enough. Dozens of them. Boots with a distinctive worn heel on the left side — the team lead, probably, favoring one leg. A lighter tread, narrow, someone who walked on the balls of their feet. A heavy set that punched deep, someone carrying too much pack or carrying worry.

The prints arrived at the camp from downstream. They milled around it — fire ring, water runs to the river and back, a path to the latrine trench they'd dug with proper depth and proper distance, somebody here had read the manual, possibly memorized it — and then.

And then they stopped.

Not faded. Not scattered into confusion. Stopped. The way a sentence stops mid-word.

Lena crouched over the clearest patch of silt and stared at it until her knees complained. The last coherent line of footprints led toward the upstream end of the bench, toward the canyon narrows where the Rivergate began in earnest — and then there was simply clean ground. Smooth, unbroken, unmarked ground, stretching away toward the dark slot of the gorge as if the whole team had been lifted off the earth by something considerate enough not to scuff the gravel.

She'd seen people try to hide their tracks before. Fugitives, smugglers, once a very apologetic poacher. Hiding tracks well was nearly impossible; hiding tracks *perfectly*, on ideal tracking terrain, for six people, without leaving so much as a displaced pebble at the margin? She couldn't have done it. She was fairly sure nobody alive could have done it.

Which left options she didn't like, arranged in an order she liked even less: either six trained surveyors had walked out of this camp without touching the ground, or someone had gone to extraordinary lengths to make it look that way, and both of those possibilities had teeth.

"All right," she murmured to the camp, because talking to empty places was a habit she'd stopped apologizing for somewhere around year three of exile. "Let's see what you're actually saying."

* * *

The tents told the next part of the story, and the story was nonsense.

Tent one: sleeping bag unrolled, sleeping bag *used* — the hollow of a body still pressed into the synthetic fill. Boots placed neatly beside the door, socks tucked inside them. A paperback novel face-down on the ground cloth, spine cracked a third of the way in.

Tent two: same. Bag slept in, gear squared away, a half-finished letter in careful handwriting, pen capped and laid parallel to the page like a soldier at attention.

Tent three belonged, by the clutter, to the junior member — Ryn Okafor, twenty-four years old per the file Vane had given her, the youngest of the six. Her bag was slept in, her headlamp hung from the tent pole on its little hook, and beside it sat a tin of ginger candies with the lid off, as if she'd been sucking one right up until the moment the world interrupted.

Tent four, the lead's, was the tidiest of all, which told Lena he was the kind of man who tidied when stressed rather than the kind who scattered. His field journal lay on top of his pack. Closed. She didn't touch it — not yet, not without Vane present to witness, because she'd learned what happened to people who touched things without witnesses. That lesson had cost her seven years.

Then the kitchen.

The food storage was what finally raised the skin on her arms. Six people, eleven days into a fourteen-day survey rotation, and the food cache was *full*. Not picked-over, not rationed-down-to-the-dregs the way every expedition cache gets in its final days. Full. Sealed bags of rice and lentils stacked in a bear hang they'd built correctly — correctly, she noted, another manual-reader — with a week's worth of calories still hanging in the tree like a promise nobody came back to collect.

Eleven days in, nobody eats like this. Eleven days in, people are hungry. Eleven days in, if you leave, you take the food, because whatever's out there is never scarier than an empty stomach. Lena had walked out of a whiteout once with half a chocolate bar and a packet of instant soup and had considered herself rich.

Nobody leaves a full cache. Nobody leaves their boots. Nobody leaves a book at the good part.

Unless they left all at once, together, in a hurry — but then the ground would say so. The ground said nothing. The ground said *nothing happened here*, in the flat confident voice of a liar who'd rehearsed.

She sat back on her heels in the middle of the camp and let the dread arrive properly, letting it settle over her shoulders like a pack with bad weight distribution. Because here was the thing she'd been circling since the first tent: mountains obeyed rules. That was the whole comfort of them, the whole religion of them. Cold flowed downhill. Water found the low way. People got hungry, got cold, got scared, and every one of those needs left a mark you could follow. A mountain was honest the way arithmetic was honest. You might not like the answer, but the answer didn't change to spare your feelings.

This camp broke arithmetic.

"Found something?"

She hadn't heard him coming, which annoyed her more than it should have. Aldous Vane stood at the edge of the site in field clothes that were new enough to still hold their creases, a man whose boots had clearly never been resoled because they'd never been asked to work. He had the smooth, unhurried manner of someone who had never once been rained on unexpectedly. Behind him, two ministry technicians hovered with cameras and evidence bags, looking deeply unhappy about the altitude, the mud, and possibly existence itself.

"Your boot prints," Lena said, "are ruining my crime scene."

Something flickered across Vane's face — amusement, maybe, filed under things to be dealt with later. "Ms. Nakamura. You've been busy."

"I've been reading." She stood, brushed off her knees, and pointed with her chin at the silt. "Come look at this and tell me what you see."

Vane looked. He was good at looking, she'd give him that — patient, thorough, the gaze of a man reading a contract. Then he straightened and smiled the small, closed smile of a man who has finished reading a contract and found the clause he likes.

"Muddy ground," he said. "Snowmelt season. Prints wash out."

"Not in twelve hours, they don't. Not under tree cover, not on graded silt." She kept her voice level, professional, the voice she used for clients who wanted to argue about turnaround times. "Six people walked into this camp. Nobody walked out. Their food's untouched. Their boots are here. Their *book* is here, mid-chapter." She paused. "You've read the site report from the flyover. What did the thermal imaging show?"

"Anomalies," Vane said pleasantly. "Instruments at altitude are unreliable."

"Instruments at altitude are exactly why we pay for instruments."

"Ms. Nakamura." He turned to face her fully, and for just a moment the pleasantness thinned and she saw the machinery underneath, the thing that ran on schedules and signatures and the quiet disposal of inconvenient facts. "People go missing in wilderness. It's tragic, it's regrettable, and it is almost always mundane. Rivers take them. Ravines take them. Occasionally people take themselves." He glanced at the tents, at the neat boots, and something in his expression suggested he'd already decided what this site meant and would not be taking further questions. "Panic. Disorientation. They dropped their gear and wandered. It happens."

"In six directions at once, leaving no prints, barefoot, in snowmelt season?" She let that sit. "I've spent seven years walking country like this, Mr. Vane. I have made every mistake a person can make on a mountain and lived to invoice for it. I know what panic looks like in the dirt. This isn't panic. Panic is loud. This—" she gestured at the whole silent, orderly, impossible camp— "this is quiet. Nothing about this is mundane."

"Then I'm sure your expertise will uncover the mundane explanation eventually." He said it kindly, which was somehow worse. "Until then, I'd ask you to keep your theories between us. Families read the papers. Speculation is cruel."

It was, Lena reflected, a masterful sentence. It wrapped a gag order in sympathy and tied it off with a bow. She'd met men like him in her former life — the ones who never raised their voices because the system spoke for them, softly, in triplicate.

"Fine," she said. "Between us. One question, though, also between us." She nodded upstream, toward the narrows, where the canyon walls drew together and the light went thin and green and the river's voice changed pitch, lower, older, like something clearing its throat. "Why does your survey map end two kilometers short of the headwall? Every other tributary on this river is mapped to the source. Except this one. There's a gap in your own paperwork shaped exactly like the place your team vanished."

For the first time, Vane's smile took effort.

"Cartographic priorities," he said. "Some terrain isn't worth the ink."

"Every terrain is worth the ink," Lena said. "My father taught me that."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes at the name — quick, unreadable, gone. He checked his watch, an expensive gesture, and announced that the technicians would document the site and she should proceed upstream at first light, radio check at noon, and wasn't the weather holding nicely.

He walked back down the trail the way he'd come, creased and dry, and Lena watched him go and understood, with the particular clarity that comes after too many hours staring at broken ground, that she had just watched a man lie to her about a gap in a map, and that he had flinched at her father's name, and that neither of those facts was going to let her sleep tonight.

* * *

She made camp fifty meters downstream of the site, close enough to keep an eye on it, far enough that she wouldn't have to look at the boots anymore. She ate noodles. She checked her radio, which crackled with static and the ghost of Vane's base station, then gave up the ghost entirely as the walls closed in — day one, and already the outside world had become a rumor.

Full dark came early in the gorge. She lay in her bag listening to the river and running the camp through her head again, print by print, tent by tent, looking for the flaw in her own reading the way her father had taught her to check a contour loop for the break that meant a cliff.

*Check it twice, Lenny. The mountain only lies to people who lie to themselves.*

"Thanks, Dad," she muttered. "Very helpful. Very comforting."

But she did check it twice. And twice, the answer came back the same: six people had gone to bed in that camp, and the mountain had swallowed them whole without disturbing a single stone, and the food was still hanging in the tree.

Somewhere past midnight, half-asleep, she heard it — or thought she heard it. Under the river's voice, faint and high, from somewhere up in the black walls of the gorge. A sound with rhythm to it. Almost like knocking.

Almost like someone, in a cave above the waterline, tapping out a signal to a darkness that wasn't listening.

Lena opened her eyes and lay very still in the dark, and did not sleep again.
