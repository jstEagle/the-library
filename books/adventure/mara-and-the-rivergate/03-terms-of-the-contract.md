## 3. Terms of the Contract

The liaison's office turned out to be a room above the feed store, which told Lena most of what she needed to know about how seriously the ministry took this search. A real operation would have rented the hotel conference room. This was a man with a folding table, a briefcase, and a view of a dumpster where someone had recently abandoned a mattress.

Aldous Vane stood when she came in, which she appreciated until she noticed he'd positioned himself so the light fell on his face and left hers in shadow. Old trick. She'd seen expedition leaders do it to new hires. It said: *I will be doing the looking here.*

"Miss Nakamura." He extended a hand. His grip was calibrated — firm enough to seem honest, soft enough to remind her he'd never hauled a pack in his life. "Thank you for coming. I know your time is—"

"It's not," Lena said. "I have nowhere else to be. That's why you found me in a border town working trail crew."

Something flickered behind his eyes — appreciation, maybe, or the small satisfaction of a man confirming that his research had been accurate. "Candor. Good. I'll be candid back." He gestured to the chair across the table. "Sit. This won't take long, and then you can decide whether you hate me."

"I usually need breakfast first for that."

He smiled like a man who kept a mental ledger of everyone who made jokes at his expense. Lena filed that away too.

The briefcase opened with a click that sounded expensive. From it he produced a folder — actual paper, actual ink, the kind of document that existed so it could be burned later without leaving a server somewhere. He laid it flat and turned it to face her.

"Triple your last contracted rate," he said. "Plus hazard pay, plus equipment allowance, plus a completion bonus that I suspect is more money than you've seen since—" He paused, delicately. "Since before."

"Before," Lena agreed.

"The survey team entered the Rivergate eleven days ago. Four people. Radio contact lost on day two. The ministry has been unable to mount an adequate search because—" he spread his hands, a gesture of bureaucratic helplessness so practiced it looked almost sincere, "—of jurisdictional complications. The gorge sits between three administrative districts, none of whom want the expense. You are not government personnel. You are a private contractor. You can go where they cannot, quickly, and quietly."

"And find them."

"And find them. Or find out what happened to them. Either outcome resolves the situation."

*Resolves.* Lena turned the word over. Most people would say *brings them home*. Vane talked about missing human beings the way an accountant talked about discrepancies.

She pulled the folder toward her and started reading. She read contracts the way she read terrain — slowly, looking for the places where the ground dropped away without warning.

The first pages were standard. Scope of work. Deliverables. She got to page four and stopped.

"Absolute secrecy," she read aloud. "'Contractor shall not disclose the existence, terms, or findings of this engagement to any party, governmental or private, during or after the term of engagement.' During *or after*." She looked up. "After how long?"

"Indefinitely."

"Indefinitely." She let the word sit there between them like a rock she'd pulled out of a streambed. "So if I find survivors, I can't tell their families?"

"You'll report to me. I'll handle notifications."

"You'll handle—" She set the paper down. "Mr. Vane. If someone's mother is sitting in a house in the lowlands wondering if her son is alive, and I'm the one who finds him, you're telling me I'm contractually forbidden from picking up a phone."

"I'm telling you that information flows through proper channels. Improper channels are how rumors start, and rumors near the Rivergate have historically been..." he searched for the word, "...destabilizing."

Lena studied him. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, with the particular stillness of men who spent their careers in rooms where other people did the walking. But his eyes were not lazy. They tracked her hands, her posture, the way her thumb pressed against the edge of the folder. He was reading her the way she read scree slopes — for signs of what might give way.

She flipped to page six and found the clause she'd been half expecting, though seeing it in print still landed like cold water down the collar.

"'All maps, surveys, specimens, records, instruments, and discoveries of any nature encountered within the operational area shall become the exclusive property of the Ministry of Water and Infrastructure, regardless of origin.'"

"Regardless of origin," she repeated.

"Yes."

"So if I find something that belonged to my family—"

"Then the ministry owns it." He said it pleasantly, the way you'd confirm a lunch order. "I understand that sounds severe, Miss Nakamura. But consider what's actually in that canyon. Survey equipment. Possibly bodies. Possibly nothing. Nobody is going to find your family silverware in there."

*No,* Lena thought. *But somebody might find my father's name.*

She didn't say it. Seven years of exile had taught her that her father's name was a door best kept shut in the company of officials; open it and everything cold in the world came pouring through.

Instead she said, "And if I refuse the ownership clause?"

Vane folded his hands. "Then I hire someone less careful. There are perhaps five guides in the province qualified for the Rivergate in winter. Two are dead. One is seventy. One drinks. That leaves you, and frankly, Miss Nakamura, the drinking one has better references than you do right now."

It was cruelly put, and it was true, and they both knew he'd rehearsed it. Lena felt the old heat rise in her chest — the specific anger of being handled by someone who had done his homework on your worst year.

But underneath the anger, quieter and more humiliating, was arithmetic. Trail crew paid eleven a day plus beans. This contract paid more than she'd made in the seven years since the incident, combined. Enough for the fresh start she'd been promising herself since year two. A boat ticket south. A town where nobody knew the word *blacklist*, where "Nakamura" was just a name and not a cautionary tale told to new hires around cook fires.

*It's just money,* she told herself. *You're not selling anything. You're buying a way out.*

She was aware, even as she thought it, that this was the kind of sentence people said right before they sold something.

* * *

Her father had taught her to read contour lines when she was seven, on the kitchen floor of a house that smelled of pencil shavings and strong tea.

He'd spread one of his own maps across the linoleum — he was always spreading maps across surfaces, tables, beds, once, memorably, the roof of the car — and traced his finger along a cluster of brown lines.

"What do you see, little wren?"

"Squiggles."

"Squiggles," he agreed gravely. "Now. Closer together here, farther apart there. What does that mean?"

"That the mapmaker got tired?"

He laughed — her father laughed easily, which people later used against him, as if joy were evidence of fraud — and tapped the tight cluster of lines. "Closer means steeper. The lines are like stairs. When the stairs crowd together, the mountain is standing up fast. When they stretch out—" his finger slid to the loose, lazy curves near the map's edge, "—the land is lying down to rest. Every line is a promise the ground makes about its own shape. Your job, if you're any good, is to believe the ground and check the mapmaker."

"Even you?"

"*Especially* me." He'd said it lightly, but she remembered, even at seven, catching something under it. Something that sounded, in retrospect, like a man practicing a defense. "Never trust a map because of who drew it, Lena. Trust it because it matches the mountain. And if it doesn't match the mountain—" he'd tapped her nose, "—then either the map is wrong, or somebody wants it wrong. And knowing which is the whole profession."

She'd nodded solemnly and gone back to her cereal, and he'd sat there a while longer, looking at his own contours like a man reading letters from someone far away.

*Trust it because it matches the mountain.*

She was thirty-four years old, sitting above a feed store, and she could still hear him saying it.

* * *

"There's a rider on page nine," Vane said, when she'd been quiet too long. "Standard for sensitive engagements. It gives me authority to terminate the contract and order your immediate extraction if, in my judgment, continuing poses unacceptable risk."

"In *your* judgment."

"I'm reasonable, Miss Nakamura. Ask anyone." He said it without a trace of irony, which was somehow the most unsettling thing he'd done all morning. "I don't want heroics. I want a competent professional who goes up the river, establishes what happened, and comes back. If the situation deteriorates beyond your capacity, you withdraw. That's not cowardice. That's the contract working."

Lena looked at the signature line. Her name wouldn't even fit properly in the space provided; it never did, the pen always running out of room at the final vowel, as if the world had budgeted only so much space for her.

Seven years ago she'd signed a different document — an incident report, actually, four pages describing unsafe anchors and a route decision made by a man whose judgment was impaired by altitude and ego. She'd signed that honestly, believing paperwork protected people. The report had vanished. The man's family owned three resorts. Lena's name had acquired a permanent asterisk.

Now here was another piece of paper, and this one wasn't asking for her honesty. It was asking for her silence, her findings, and — buried in the clean legal language like a crevasse under fresh snow — whatever she might discover about a canyon her father had drawn thirty years ago and been destroyed for drawing.

*Walk away,* said the part of her that had learned caution the hard way, mile by mile, winter by winter.

*Go,* said the older part, the seven-year-old on the kitchen floor, the one who believed maps could be true and that believing them was a kind of love.

*It's just money,* said the part in the middle, the tired one, and she recognized the tone. It was the same voice she used on cold mornings when the sensible thing was to stay in the bag and the necessary thing was to get up. It was the voice of self-deception, and she knew its sound perfectly, because she'd been listening to it for seven years.

She picked up the pen.

"One condition," she said. "Not in the contract. Just between us."

Vane raised an eyebrow, inviting her to proceed, clearly reserving the right to decline.

"If I find survivors, they ride out with me. Whatever the notification protocols say. I am not leaving living people in that canyon while we sort out jurisdictions."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind Vane's pleasant expression, some calculation completing itself at speed. Then he smiled, warm as a stove.

"Agreed. Bring back anyone you find." He slid the folder an inch closer to her. "Though I should prepare you, Miss Nakamura. Eleven days, in that country, in this season? Survivors are unlikely. What you'll most likely find is evidence. And evidence is exactly what the ministry needs handled properly."

*Evidence,* Lena thought, signing. *Handled properly.* As if the truth were a shipment of glassware.

She wrote her name in the too-small space, the pen dying on the final vowel exactly as it always did, and Vane countersigned beneath it with a flourish that suggested he'd practiced that too.

"Wonderful." He produced a second copy, already prepared, which meant he'd expected her to sign from the moment she walked in. Maybe from the moment he'd first read her file. "Departure is yours to schedule, but sooner is better. Start from Ferrow's Landing — it's the last settlement before the gorge. You'll find the locals... unhelpful, generally. Don't let it slow you down."

"Why would they be unhelpful?"

"They're superstitious about the Rivergate. River towns often are." He closed the briefcase with that same expensive click. "Oh — and Miss Nakamura. The team leader. Dr. Emmerich. If you find any of his field notes, those especially come to me. Unread, ideally. Scientists scribble. Half of what they write down is speculation, and speculation in the wrong hands becomes rumor."

"Of course," Lena said, and heard her father's voice, faint and amused, from thirty years and one kitchen floor away: *either the map is wrong, or somebody wants it wrong.*

She stood, shook the calibrated hand again, and walked out into the cold bright street, where the wind off the mountains smelled like snow and diesel and, if you breathed just right, like nothing at all.

Three days later she stepped off the mail barge at Ferrow's Landing with forty pounds of gear, a contract she already regretted, and a fresh start folded up in her breast pocket like a map of a country that might not exist.

The ferrywoman saw her coming from across the water, took one long look at her face, and began untying the rope.

"Well," the old woman called, in a voice that carried over the current like it had been waiting years to say it. "Another Nakamura. The river does keep sending you people back."
