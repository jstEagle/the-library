## 4. Downstream People

Ferrow's Landing smelled of woodsmoke and fish guts and, underneath both, something older — wet stone, river silt, the mineral breath of a canyon that had been exhaling at the town's back for ten thousand years. Lena noticed it the moment she stepped off the mail truck: a smell she'd know anywhere, because her father had once held a jar of it under her nose and said, *That's what a river thinks like.*

She was twenty-nine years old, carrying a pack that weighed almost as much as her regrets, and she had exactly one plan: find a guide who knew the upper gorge, pay them whatever the search contract allowed, and be standing at the mouth of the Rivergate within two days.

The plan survived approximately forty minutes.

* * *

The Landing was a strung-together kind of place, three streets deep against the hillside, built on stilts and stubbornness where the Kestrel widened out of its last gorge and decided, apparently on a whim, to become navigable. Houses leaned over the water on pilings black with age. Boats knocked gently against docks. A dog of no discernible breed watched her from a porch with the professional indifference of an animal that had seen every kind of stranger arrive and most of them leave again.

Lena went to the tavern first, because in river towns taverns were where guides lived when they weren't working, and because she'd been eating trail food for six days and wanted soup that hadn't been reconstituted from a foil packet.

The tavern was called the Drowned Compass, which she took as either a very bad omen or local humor, and inside it was warm and dim and full of the particular silence that falls over a room when a stranger walks in and everyone performs the act of not looking at them.

"Soup," she told the woman behind the bar, "and whatever advice you sell by the bowl."

The woman — broad, gray-streaked, forearms like dock rope — ladled out a bowl of fish stew without comment and then said, "Advice is free here. It's just usually not worth the price."

"I'm looking for someone to take me upriver. Past the gorge."

The ladle stopped moving.

It was a small thing, really. A pause of maybe two seconds. But in a room that quiet, two seconds rang like a dropped pan. Lena felt the attention of the whole tavern swing toward her, the way water swings toward a hole in a hull.

"Past the gorge," the barwoman repeated. "You mean the Rivergate."

"If that's what it's called here, yes."

"It's called that everywhere." The barwoman set down the ladle and studied her. "Who's asking?"

"Lena Nakamura. I'm contracted for a government search." She produced the folded authorization, because paperwork had a way of ending conversations faster than charm did.

It did not end this one. The barwoman glanced at the paper, glanced back at her face, and something flickered there — recognition, or the shadow of it.

"Nakamura," she said, tasting the name. "Huh."

"Huh?"

"Sit down and eat your soup, Nakamura. I'll see who's drinking."

* * *

What followed was the strangest recruitment drive of Lena's career. She spent the afternoon going table to table, or rather being invisibly steered from table to table by the barwoman, whose name turned out to be Odile, and who had the manner of a woman running an auction where nothing was actually for sale.

The first guide, a leathery man named Brannoc with hands like tree roots, listened to her offer, named a price three times the going rate, and then — as she reached for her wallet — said, "Actually, no. Can't do it."

"You just named a price."

"I name prices all day. Doesn't mean I mean them." He turned back to his beer with the finality of a door closing.

The second was younger, a wiry woman mending a net in the corner, who heard Lena out with genuine interest and then said, "I'd take you to the mouth of the gorge. Not past it. Nobody goes past it."

"Why not? It's a canyon, not a curse."

"That's what my uncle said." The woman tied off a knot. "He also said the Rivergate keeps its own. He said it right up until he stopped saying anything at all, which was the day he went in with a survey crew in 'ninety-one. They found his boat. They found his hat. They found eleven other hats, none of them his, all neatly stacked on a rock like somebody was expecting company." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and unafraid, which somehow made it worse. "I don't go where hats get stacked."

By the fourth refusal, Lena had stopped being surprised and started being fascinated, in the way a cartographer is fascinated by a blank space on a map. Every person in the tavern had a version of the same story. The gorge kept its own. The river remembered. Don't ask the Gate for anything you can't afford to lose. One old man simply patted her hand and said, "You seem nice, dear. Go home," which was so at odds with everything else that she nearly laughed.

But under the folklore, she caught something else — a pattern. The refusals weren't superstitious exactly. They were *specific*. These people knew things about the upper gorge. Their stories had dates and details and the flat tone of firsthand experience worn smooth by repetition. This wasn't a town afraid of a canyon. This was a town that had made a decision about a canyon, generations ago, and enforced it socially ever since.

Which meant the decision had a reason.

And reasons, unlike curses, could be mapped.

* * *

Evening came down off the hills and turned the river to hammered copper. Lena sat on the dock behind the tavern with her boots off and her feet in water cold enough to make her toes file formal complaints, eating bread Odile had pressed on her ("for the road, since you're clearly too stubborn to have one") and reviewing her options.

Option one: go alone. She'd done harder approaches solo. But the gorge was a slot canyon with a live river in it, and even her arrogance had limits; solo technical canyon descents with unknown hydraulics were how good guides became cautionary tales.

Option two: hire a boat to the mouth only, and scout the entrance on foot. Possible. Slow. Probably fatal to her schedule.

Option three: keep asking questions until somebody slipped.

She was favoring option three when she saw the ferrywoman.

The woman was poling a flat-bottomed ferry across the last light of the current, ferrying — of all things — a single passenger and a bicycle, with the unhurried economy of someone who had made the same crossing perhaps forty thousand times and intended to make it forty thousand more. She was old in the way of river people: not frail, but weathered down to essentials, like driftwood. When she brought the ferry alongside the dock, Lena watched her tie off a line with one hand while steadying the boat with her pole, and thought: *there*. That was competence. That was the thing she'd been looking for all day, wearing a cardigan.

"Excuse me," Lena said, before she could talk herself out of it. "Do you take passengers upriver?"

The old woman finished tying the line. Then she looked at Lena — a long, unhurried look, the kind that started at the eyes and worked outward, cataloguing — and said, "Up past the Landing? No. Downstream only. Always downstream."

"The gorge, then. You won't go near it."

"I didn't say that." She gathered her pole. "I said I don't carry people to it. Different thing."

"What's the difference?"

The ferrywoman smiled, and it was not entirely a friendly smile; it had edges. "A knife and a wound are different things too, girl. Want me to explain that one as well?"

Lena laughed despite herself. "Fair. What's your name?"

"Sela."

"Sela. I'm—"

"I know who you are." Sela stepped onto the dock, and the boards barely registered her weight. "Town's been talking about you since noon. Government searcher. Big contract. Asking after the Gate." She paused, and her gaze sharpened into something almost gentle. "Nakamura, isn't it?"

"Yes."

Something moved behind the old woman's eyes — not surprise. Recognition, arriving late, like thunder after lightning. She looked at Lena for a long moment, and Lena had the distinct, disorienting sense of being measured against a standard she couldn't see.

"Your father asked the same question," Sela said.

The words landed somewhere below Lena's ribs and stayed there.

"My father," Lena said carefully, "was a mapmaker. He died seven years ago. He never came here."

"No," Sela agreed mildly. "He never came *here*."

"What does that mean?"

"It means what it says. Old women don't do poetry; we do accuracy." Sela picked up her pole and moved back toward the ferry, and Lena followed, because following was the only sane response to a sentence like that.

"Sela. Please. If you knew him—"

"I never met the man." The ferrywoman settled onto her thwart and looked up at Lena across four feet of darkening air. "But my mother ferried him. And her mother ferried the ones who ferried him, if you follow the thread back far enough. There are names this river keeps, girl, and yours is one of them. Has been since before you were born. We don't carry Nakamuras to the Gate." She pushed off from the dock, and the current took the boat with a soft complaint of wood on wood. "We wait for them to come asking. They always come asking."

The ferry slid out into the copper light. Lena stood on the dock with her heart doing something complicated and unwelcome.

"How do you know my name?" she called.

Sela didn't answer directly. She let the ferry drift a few yards downstream, and then, without turning around, without raising her voice above the sound of the water, she said:

"He asked us the same question you asked today. Who would take him up the gorge. And we told him what we tell everyone. And he went anyway." A pause, filled by the river. "And thirty years later, everyone in this valley still knows exactly how that turned out."

*Thirty years.* Lena's mind seized on the number and refused to let go. Thirty years ago her father had been in his twenties, a young field cartographer with a government contract and a reputation still intact. Thirty years ago he had supposedly been mapping drainage basins four hundred miles east. That was the official record. She'd read it. She'd read everything there was to read about Tomas Nakamura, because reading it had been a form of grief, and later a form of anger, and eventually a form of homework.

Thirty years ago, according to every document she'd ever seen, her father had never set foot in the Kestrel watershed.

Sela's ferry touched the far bank. The old woman secured it, unhurried, and then stood looking back across the water at Lena — a small dark figure against the last of the light, watching her with what might have been pity and might have been patience and was probably both.

"Your stew's getting cold!" Odile hollered from the tavern door behind her, and the ordinary words broke the spell like a stone through ice.

* * *

Lena didn't sleep much that night. She lay in the narrow room Odile rented her — clean, cheap, smelling faintly of cedar — and stared at the ceiling beams and turned the day over like a stone, examining its underside.

Her father had asked the same question. In this town. Thirty years ago.

Either Sela was lying, or mistaken, or senile — possible, all three, though the woman's eyes had been sharp as a compass needle — or the official record of Tomas Nakamura's career had a hole in it shaped exactly like the Kestrel River.

And if there was a hole in his record, then there was a reason for the hole, and reasons were drawn by people.

Lena got up, lit the lamp, and spread her father's old notebooks on the bed — the ones she carried the way other people carried photographs. She'd been through them a hundred times. Field notes, contour sketches, marginalia in his cramped leftward-slanting hand. She'd always read them as the work of a brilliant man slowly coming apart, the maps growing stranger as the years went on, until the final notebooks were full of symbols nobody could identify and the government examiners had declared them fabrications.

She'd always assumed the strangeness was decline.

Now, with Sela's voice in her ears, she paged through them differently. The strange symbols clustered. They clustered around watersheds. Around *this* watershed — the Kestrel appeared in fragments, in corners, in a sketch of a canyon wall marked with a symbol like a gate, like a doorway, like—

Like the mark she'd need to look for.

"Keeps its own," she murmured to the empty room. "They always come asking."

Her whole life, she had understood her family's disgrace as a simple story: brilliant man, flawed man, falsified his maps, got caught, got destroyed, and his daughter inherited the wreckage. Seven years ago she'd added her own chapter to the same story — her own mistake, her own blacklist — and she'd believed, with the comfortable certainty of the self-punishing, that she was simply her father's daughter, doomed to the same honest stupidity in a dishonest world.

But sitting here, in a river town that had been waiting thirty years for a Nakamura to come asking, the story wouldn't hold its shape. Honest stupidity didn't leave holes in government records. Disgrace didn't send old ferrywomen cryptic messages across a river. Mistakes didn't stack hats on rocks.

Something had happened here. Something her father had found, or been shown, or been sworn to. And whatever it was, it had cost him everything — and now, apparently, it was waiting for her.

Lena closed the notebook. Outside, the river ran on in the dark, patient as anything that had already won.

In the morning, she decided, she'd find Sela again. The old woman owed her a better answer than a riddle, and if she wouldn't give one freely, Lena had learned long ago that the fastest way to an answer was to start walking toward the question.

Downstream people, the town called itself. Fine.

She'd go upstream alone if she had to.

But as she finally drifted toward sleep, one image kept returning, gentle and terrible in equal measure: an old woman on a ferry, not turning around, saying *your father asked the same question* — and meaning, unmistakably, *and now it's your turn.*
