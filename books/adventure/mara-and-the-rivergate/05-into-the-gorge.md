## 5. Into the Gorge

The Rivergate did not look like a gate. It looked like a mistake the mountain had made and then tried to hide.

Lena stood at its mouth on the first morning, pack heavy on her shoulders, and studied the place where the Kestrel River simply stopped being a river and became something else — a seam of green water forced between two walls of black stone so close together that she could have spat across the top of the gorge, if she'd had anything to spit and someone to spit at. The walls rose four hundred feet without a single handhold worth the name, polished smooth by ten thousand years of patient water, and somewhere down in that slot of shadow the river ran fast and cold and entirely indifferent to her plans.

"Keeps its own," she said aloud, to nobody. Sela's words had followed her up the trail like a stray dog.

She checked her radio out of habit, though there was nothing to check. Vane's last transmission from the lowlands had been crisp and corporate: *Maintain contact schedule at 0800 and 2000 hours. Report findings immediately. The ministry values your discretion.* Discretion. In Vane's mouth the word had the texture of a gag.

"Copy that," Lena told the radio, and clipped it to her shoulder strap where it sat like a small black promise she already suspected she couldn't keep.

The first hundred meters were almost pleasant. Almost. A gravel bench ran along the north wall, wide enough for one boot at a time, and the morning sun reached down far enough to warm the top of her head even while her feet stayed in shadow. She walked with the river roaring below her left shoulder, close enough that its spray found her face, and for a little while it was just walking — which was, after seven years, the thing she was best at in the whole world.

Her father used to say that walking was thinking with your legs. He'd said it the same summer he taught her contour lines, crouched over a map spread on the kitchen table, his big blunt finger tracing the brown rings around some imaginary peak. "See how they crowd together here? That's not just steep ground, Lena. That's the mountain shouting." He'd made her read maps like other parents made children read bedtime stories, and she had fallen asleep more nights than she could count with her cheek on the legend box, dreaming in elevation bands.

Now she was walking into a canyon that appeared on none of those maps. Not on her father's, not on the government's, not on the hand-drawn tourist sketches Ferrow's Landing sold to fishermen. The Rivergate was a blank space with a river through it, and blank spaces, in her experience, were never actually empty. They were just places where somebody had decided the truth was inconvenient.

The gravel bench ended at midday.

It didn't fade or narrow. It ended, sheared off against a wall of stone that dropped straight into whitewater, as though the mountain had drawn a line and dared her to cross it. Lena stood at the break and looked at what came next: a traverse across the river itself, over a fallen slab of rock angled like a church roof, slick with spray, with the Kestrel chewing at its base in a way that sounded distinctly personal.

"All right," she told the river conversationally. "Let's not make this weird."

She crossed it the way her father had taught her to cross everything — three points of contact, weight low, no sudden declarations of confidence. Halfway across, her right boot skated on wet stone and her stomach performed a small gymnastics routine, but she locked her hips against the slope and waited until her heart agreed to continue. On the far side she sat down, let her legs dangle over the drop, and ate half a ration bar while the river tried its best to be louder than her own breathing.

That was when she noticed the light going.

Not sunset light — it wasn't even two o'clock. But ahead, the walls leaned toward each other overhead, and the strip of sky between them narrowed from a ribbon to a thread to a memory. Down in the gorge proper, the Kestrel ran through a slot so tight that the rock overhead nearly met, and what daylight filtered through arrived green and submarine, the color of deep lakes and old secrets.

Lena had guided clients through canyons on three continents. She knew slots. She knew how they swallowed sound and heat and, occasionally, people. This one was different. This one felt less like geology and more like architecture — as if the gorge hadn't been carved by accident but built, deliberately, as a corridor to somewhere someone very much did not want visitors finding.

"Professional unease," she murmured, giving the feeling its clinical name because naming things kept them at arm's length. It was a trick she'd learned from a therapist in her blacklist years, back when the nightmares had teeth. *Name it and it shrinks.* Professional unease. Dread. Pick one.

She picked neither, shouldered her pack, and went in.

* * *

Inside the slot, the world reduced itself to essentials: water, stone, and the next step.

Navigation was brutal in a way that would have been funny if she'd had anyone to laugh with. There was no trail, obviously. There was barely any bank. She moved upstream by a grammar of small decisions — a ledge here, a boulder hop there, a stretch where she waded thigh-deep through current that shoved at her like a drunk uncle, all hands and no apology. The water was snowmelt-cold even in late summer, cold enough that twenty minutes of wading turned her legs into borrowed limbs she operated on faith alone.

She measured progress in small victories. A dry ledge wide enough to sleep on. A chockstone the size of a bus that formed a natural bridge over a pour-off. A seep in the north wall, trickling from a crack, that meant she could drink without treating the silty main channel. Each one she noted in her field book with the terse shorthand of her trade, and each note was also a message to whoever came looking for her body: *she was here, and she knew what she was doing.*

The radio died on schedule.

At 0800 the next morning she climbed onto the highest rock available, held the unit above her head like an offering, and got nothing but the soft hiss of atmosphere. At 2000 the same. By the third day she stopped climbing. The walls were too high, the signal had nowhere to go, and somewhere in the back of her mind a small voice observed that this suited Vane fine — a guide who couldn't report in was a guide who could be described however he liked later. Missing. Off-schedule. Possibly compromised.

*Possibly complicit,* she thought, and hated how easily the word fit. Seven years of practice had taught her exactly how quickly a narrative could harden around a person's name. One bad report, one powerful man embarrassed, and suddenly you weren't a professional who'd flagged unsafe conditions — you were *difficult*. Unreliable. The kind of person who lost clients in mountains.

She shook it off and kept moving, because that was the other thing seven years had taught her: the story other people told about you mattered exactly as much as you let it, and the river didn't care about any of it. The river only cared about gradient and time. There was a strange comfort in that. Out here, she wasn't the disgraced Nakamura girl. She was just a body moving upstream, competent and alone, judged solely by whether she made the next ledge.

On the fourth day, the canyon gave her a gift.

* * *

It came in the form of a marker.

She'd been climbing a rubble fan where a side ravine dumped its debris into the gorge, hands and boots working independently while her mind wandered somewhere warmer, when her eye caught the glint of metal in a crack above the waterline. She froze, then traversed to it with the exaggerated calm of someone trying not to scare a wild animal.

A survey marker. Brass, government-stamped, anchored into the stone with expansion bolts — the real thing, expensive, the kind of hardware Vane's ministry deployed in threes and logged in triplicate. Except this one had been defaced. Not weathered, not vandalized in any random way. *Defaced*, deliberately and precisely: the stamped serial number had been peened flat with a punch, and beneath it, cut into the brass with something sharp, was a symbol.

A circle, open at the bottom. A single vertical stroke descending through the gap, like a plumb line dropped through a broken ring.

Lena stared at it for a long time. Long enough for the spray to bead on her eyelashes. Long enough for the cold to climb through her jacket and settle in her chest, though the cold wasn't really what made her shiver.

She knew that symbol.

She knew it the way you know a lullaby — not with your mind but somewhere lower, in the body, in the years before language. Because her father had drawn it. Dozens of times. In the margins of his notebooks, in the corners of letters, once — memorably — in the condensation on the bathroom mirror when she was nine, so that it greeted her at tooth-brushing height like a tiny signature from another world. She'd asked him what it meant, and he'd smiled in that way he had, the smile that meant *I know something I'm not allowed to say*, and he'd said:

"It's a surveyor's mark, little fish. For places that don't want to be surveyed."

She'd thought it was a joke. A private game. All children think their fathers are keeping small secrets; it never occurs to you that the secret might be the size of a valley.

Lena sat down on the rubble, uncaring of the wet, and pulled her field book out. With numb fingers she drew the symbol from memory beside her notes on the marker's location — and yes. Yes. The proportions matched. The gap at the bottom of the circle, the length of the stroke. Her father's mark, cut into a government survey marker, in a canyon that officially did not exist, thirty years after he'd supposedly never been here.

And the marker itself — she looked at it again, properly now, with her guide's eye instead of her daughter's. It had been placed by a team. Bolted, leveled, photographed. And then someone had come back and killed it, carefully, the way you'd kill a witness. Which meant the survey team hadn't just vanished. They'd found something, marked it, and then *unmarked* it — or someone had unmarked it for them.

Ryn Okafor, junior team member, missing. Four others, missing. Equipment stacked neat as a shop display, food untouched, no tracks leaving camp. Vane's smooth voice saying *anomalies happen in remote terrain* in the tone of a man reading someone else's script.

Lena closed the field book and pressed it flat between her palms, and something shifted in her chest — a tectonic thing, slow and enormous, the kind of movement you feel more than hear.

For six days she had told herself this was a job. Triple pay, fresh start abroad, money in the bank and a plane ticket out of a country that had spent seven years teaching her she wasn't wanted. That was the story. That was the contract.

But contracts were stories too, and she had just watched this one dissolve in green canyon light. Because her father had stood where she was standing. Her father had touched this stone, or stone like it, and cut his secret sign into the record of men who erased things. Whatever had destroyed him — the fraud charges, the disgrace, the long gray years of him dying quietly in a rented room while the cartographic establishment patted itself on the back — whatever that machinery was, it had started turning here. In this gorge. Around this water.

She wasn't searching for a missing survey team anymore. She understood that now, sitting in the dark of the slot with her father's symbol drying in her notebook. The team was a door, and Vane had hired her to knock on it, and she was done pretending she didn't want to know what was behind it.

"Pilgrimage," she said aloud, testing the word. It sounded too holy for someone with wet socks. But it was accurate, and accuracy was the family trade.

She laughed — a short, startled sound that the canyon took and folded away somewhere — and got up, and kept moving.

* * *

That night she camped on a ledge above a long pool where the river briefly remembered how to be gentle. She ate the last of the good rations, saved the bad ones for tomorrow, and lay watching the thread of stars overhead, the only part of the sky the gorge allowed her.

Somewhere upstream — faint, almost imagined, more vibration than sound — the water changed its voice. A deeper note underneath the rush, rhythmic, steady. Like falling water, but wrong for a waterfall. Too regular. Too patient.

Lena listened until sleep took her, her father's open circle drawn on the inside of her eyelids, and dreamed of contour lines crowding close around a shape she couldn't quite read.

In the morning, she followed the sound.
