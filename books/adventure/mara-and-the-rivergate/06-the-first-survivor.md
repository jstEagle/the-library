## 6. The First Survivor

Ryn Okafor had learned three things in eleven days inside the Rivergate, and none of them were on the survey syllabus.

One: hunger was not a single sensation but a chorus, and each voice had its own opinion. There was the dull bass note in the belly that never quite shut up. There was the sharp treble of a headache that lived behind the eyes. And there was the strange, embarrassing soprano — the way food thoughts colonized everything, so that Ryn had spent an entire afternoon composing a mental inventory of every meal ever eaten, ranked by regret at having eaten them too fast. The dehydrated lasagna from the second night of the expedition, which everyone had complained about, now occupied the top spot like a saint.

Two: the canyon kept its own hours. Light came down the slot in a thin gray ribbon for perhaps four hours a day, and the rest was a blue-dark that made even familiar rock look like something considering whether to eat you.

Three: Dr. Imogen Hale, team leader, twenty years with the ministry, two commendations, one bad knee — had walked into the Kestrel River on purpose.

Ryn still could not say it plainly, even in the privacy of their own skull. The mind kept trying to sand the edges off it. *She waded in. She lost her footing. The current took her.* But Ryn had been watching. Ryn had been the only one watching, crouched behind a fallen slab with a sprained ankle and a mouth full of dust, when Hale had stood on the gravel bar with the team's maps in her hands — all of them, the originals, the field copies, the annotated aerials — and fed them into the water piece by piece like a woman feeding bread to something she loved and feared in equal measure.

Then she had turned, looked directly at the slab where Ryn was hiding — looked *at* it, Ryn would swear to that until dying day — and said, in a voice pitched not to carry but somehow carrying anyway:

"Stay out of the records, Okafor. Stay out of the water. And stay alive."

Then she had walked in up to her knees, up to her waist, and the river, which had been loud all day, went briefly, horribly quiet around her, and then she was gone.

* * *

The cave was not much of a cave. It was a hollow above the waterline where the canyon wall had shrugged off a chunk of itself sometime in the last century, leaving a pocket maybe eight feet deep, six wide, and four high at the mouth, tapering to nothing. It was dry. That was its entire résumé, and Ryn had come to love it the way you love a coworker who is useless but reliable.

Ryn had gotten to it by climbing — badly, on a bad ankle, using a seam in the rock and a great deal of profanity learned from the drilling crews back home. Eleven days ago the climb had taken forty minutes. This morning it had taken nearly two hours, and Ryn had lain at the top afterward, cheek against cold stone, breathing like a bellows, thinking: *this is how it happens. Not dramatically. You just get slower, and slower, and one day the ledge is too far.*

The supplies situation could be summarized by the word "situation." One energy bar, half-eaten, saved for emergencies and redefined as "emergency" twice daily since day five. A squeeze bottle of water from a seep that dripped cold and mineral-tasting but clean. Three survey pencils. A notebook, mostly full. And the thing Ryn had risked everything to keep: a single page torn from the edge of a map, folded to the size of a stamp and sewn into the lining of a jacket pocket with dental floss.

On it, in Hale's tight hand, was a sketch of the canyon wall near the warm channel, and four words: *behind the fall, ask him.*

Ask who, Hale had never said. Ask *him*. As if the canyon had a landlord.

Ryn unfolded the page for the hundredth time, more from habit than need — the words were printed on the inside of Ryn's eyelids by now — and refolded it, and told the chorus of hungers to be quiet, and did what had become the whole structure of the days: listened.

Listening in the Rivergate was a skill. The river filled every silence the way water fills a boot, but under it there were other sounds, and you learned to sort them. Rockfall had a crack-then-rumble signature. Wind came in slow swells. Birds — the few that worked the gorge — moved in short bursts. And people, Ryn had learned, people made a sound no animal made: the irregular scrape-and-pause of someone testing their weight before committing it.

Eleven days, and Ryn had heard nothing but rock, wind, bird, and the river's endless monologue.

Today, somewhere below the cave, someone scraped, paused, and committed.

* * *

The first feeling was terror, and Ryn wanted to be honest about that, at least internally, because honesty was all the company left. Terror arrived whole, no build-up, the way a door slams. It put Ryn flat against the back wall of the hollow with a hand clamped over their own mouth, heart going like a fist on wood.

Because there were only two kinds of people who could be climbing up the Rivergate. Lost ones, or sent ones.

And Ryn knew which kind the ministry would send if they'd found anything out. Vane's briefing before the expedition had been cheerful in the way that made skin crawl — all smiles and triple per diems and one line delivered lightly, looking at his tablet, not at anyone: *"Of course, if anything should happen to any of you, the ministry's first concern will be containment."*

Containment. Ryn had laughed about it that night with Priya over cards. Nobody was laughing now. Priya was gone — walked out of camp on the ninth night with the others, following Hale's quiet instruction, downstream toward Ferrow's Landing, and God knew if they'd made it. Ryn had stayed because of the ankle. The ankle that had probably saved their life, or ended it; the jury was still deliberating.

The scraping sound came again. Closer. Below and to the left, along the seam Ryn had climbed. Whoever it was, they were good — the pauses were short, confident — but they were climbing alone, without rope anchors that Ryn could hear, and they were climbing *up*, away from the lowlands, deeper into the gorge.

Nobody climbed deeper into the Rivergate unless they were looking for something.

Or someone.

Ryn's mind, starved and unhinged, offered helpful scenarios. Scenario one: a ministry retrieval specialist, here to contain Ryn specifically, which required believing Vane knew Ryn existed, which required believing one of the others had talked, which — Priya wouldn't, Marisol wouldn't, but Okonkwo had family and debts, and fear made people honest in the worst direction. Scenario two: a rescue party, real, official, with radios and stretchers and hot food, and the thought of hot food was so vivid it was practically a hallucination already — broth, just imagine, broth with noodles —

Scenario three, which Ryn hated most because it felt truest: someone else entirely. Someone from whatever place Hale had meant by *ask him.* Someone who would look at Ryn — outsider, surveyor, map-carrier — and decide the canyon needed one less mouth.

The footsteps stopped. Directly below, by the sound of it. Twenty feet down, maybe less.

Ryn eased forward on elbows, careful of the scree at the hollow's lip, and looked down through the gray ribbon of light.

A figure stood on a ledge below, face turned upward, and even at this distance and in this light Ryn could see the figure was studying the wall — reading it, actually reading it, the way you read a page, tracing lines with their eyes. Not blundering. Not searching randomly. *Navigating.*

And then the figure did something that dropped straight through Ryn's terror and landed somewhere older and stranger.

They took out a notebook. Held it up. Compared it to the rock face. And the gesture — the tilt of the head, the squint, the little unconscious tap of pencil against chin — was a gesture Ryn knew. It was a cartographer's gesture. It was Hale's gesture. It was the gesture of people who believed the world could be known and written down, and Ryn felt, absurdly, tearfully, in the middle of mortal fear, a rush of homesickness for a category of person.

A surveyor. Or something like one. Alone. Unmarked. Climbing toward the warm channel without knowing it was there.

Which meant either the ministry had gotten very subtle, or this was nobody's agent at all.

"Hello," Ryn started to say, and got about a third of the H out before survival instinct clamped a hand over it. *Don't announce yourself to a canyon, idiot. Watch first. Hale's rule. Rule one, rule everything: watch first.*

So Ryn watched, and the watcher watched the rock, and the river ran on below them both, patient as a creditor, and Ryn became aware of a fourth thing the Rivergate had taught them, unbidden, unwelcome:

Hope was worse than hunger. Hunger just hurt. Hope made you stupid — made you lean out over the drop toward a stranger because a stranger might have food, might have news, might know whether Priya made it past the gorge, might be able to say the words *you're going to be okay* and mean them.

The figure below shifted their pack, tested the next hold, and began to climb again — up, along the seam, toward the ledge below the hollow. Toward Ryn.

Toward the only hiding place left.

Ryn pressed back into the dark of the hollow, pulled the knife — the small one, the gear knife, barely a letter-opener with ambitions — and held it the way you hold a pen: correctly, and with no idea what to write.

Fifteen feet. Ten. The scrapes and pauses came steady as a heartbeat, and underneath them Ryn could hear breathing now, human breathing, controlled and even, and — faintly, impossibly, in this drowned gray throat of stone — the person below was humming. Quietly. Badly. Something tuneless and cheerful, the way you hum when you've been walking alone a long time and have forgotten anyone can hear.

Nobody hummed on a kill mission. Ryn was fairly sure. Ryn had exactly one data point about kill missions and it was Vane's smile, but still. Fairly sure.

Five feet. The humming stopped. A hand appeared at the lip of the ledge below the hollow — gloved, competent, reaching for the final hold — and Ryn, starving, terrified, eleven days past the end of the world, heard their own voice come out cracked and rusty and louder than intended:

"Don't move."

The hand froze.

Above the waterline, in the last of the gray light, Ryn Okafor pointed a letter-opener at a stranger and tried to remember how Hale would do it — calm, level, in charge — and managed, at last, the rest of the sentence:

"Who sent you?"
