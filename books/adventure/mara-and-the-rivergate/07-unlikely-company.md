## 7. Unlikely Company

*Ryn*

The sound came again, and this time it was not the river.

Ryn Okafor had spent eleven days learning to tell the canyon's noises apart, the way you learn to tell apart the voices in a house where you're hiding. The river had its moods — a low mutter at dawn when the cold slowed it down, a full-throated argument by afternoon. Rockfall had a particular crack-and-rattle, like someone dropping dishes one floor up. Wind through the slot above made a sound like breathing, which had nearly ended Ryn on the second night, heart hammering against the cave floor until the breathing stopped and started again, patient, indifferent.

But footsteps on wet stone were new.

Ryn pressed flat against the back wall of the cave and did the arithmetic that had kept them alive so far: one person, climbing from below, moving slowly but competently — no radio chatter, no team rhythm of several boots out of step. One person meant either a miracle or an executioner, and Ryn had stopped believing in miracles somewhere around day four, when the last of the energy bars was gone and the river trout had proven smarter than the fishing line.

The footsteps paused directly beneath the overhang. A long silence. Then a voice — low, female, roughened by days of dust — said to nobody in particular:

"I know you're up there."

Ryn's whole body went cold, which took some doing, given how cold it already was.

"Your fire smoke," the voice continued. "Day before yesterday. You banked it wrong. Green wood, wet stone behind it, smoke goes sideways into the draft instead of up. I've been watching this wall for two days waiting for you to cook again."

*Amateur,* Ryn thought, with a flash of genuine humiliation. Eleven days starving in a hole and undone by bad firecraft. Dr. Halloway would have been merciless about it. Dr. Halloway, who had walked into the river with her maps held over her head like an offering, who had told Ryn to run upstream and not look back, and whose voice still visited most nights around three in the morning.

"I'm not ministry," the voice said. "If I were, I'd have brought friends. Nobody sends one person to do a silencing." A pause. "Well. Nobody competent."

That was such an odd thing for a killer to say that Ryn almost laughed, and the laugh almost became a sob, and Ryn bit down on both and stayed silent.

The voice waited. Then, quieter: "I'm looking for the survey team. Six people. Last known position forty kilometers downstream. I'm hired to find them, not—" a hesitation Ryn heard clearly, "—not anything else. My name is Nakamura. Lena Nakamura."

The name went through Ryn like a fishhook.

Nakamura. The name was on the old field notes, in the margin of the map Dr. Halloway had burned page by page in the river's edge shallows while the rest of the team stood frozen and Vane's satellite phone buzzed in his pocket. *Nakamura, 1994,* scrawled beside a contour line that shouldn't have existed, beside a passage marked in faded pencil: *warm channel, west wall, follow the smell of green.*

"You're the cartographer's daughter," Ryn said, before deciding whether to say anything at all.

Silence below. Long enough that Ryn thought maybe they'd imagined saying it aloud. Then:

"Say that again?"

Ryn closed their eyes. Every instinct said stay hidden, stay silent, starve quietly rather than die loudly. But eleven days is a long time, and hunger does something to your judgment, and besides — the voice hadn't sounded like ministry. Ministry sounded like Aldous Vane, all smooth surfaces and reassurances that felt like being measured for a coffin.

"The survey team found something," Ryn called down, voice cracking from disuse. "And your father found it first. Thirty years ago. It's written in our field notes."

Another silence. When the voice spoke again, it had changed — lower, careful, like someone walking on ice they'd just realized was ice.

"Come down," Lena Nakamura said. "Or let me come up. But we should talk. And you sound like you haven't eaten in a week, because I can hear your stomach from here."

"That's slander," Ryn said. "It's been more than a week."

* * *

*Lena*

The kid — and Ryn Okafor was a kid, twenty-three at most, all elbows and hollow cheeks and eyes too old for the face — watched her climb the last three meters to the cave with the expression of someone watching a spider approach across a ceiling.

Lena pulled herself onto the ledge and stayed crouched, hands visible, a good four meters away. She'd learned that trick young, working trips with clients who spooked easy. Hands visible, weight low, voice even. *You're a large animal, Nakamura,* her father used to say. *Act like it.*

Up close, Ryn looked worse than the voice suggested. Lips cracked. Fingers trembling with something beyond cold. But the eyes tracked her steadily, cataloguing — her gear, her boots, the radio clipped dead on her shoulder strap, the knife at her hip.

"Ministry issue radio," Ryn said.

"Dead since day one. The gorge eats signal." Lena unclipped it and set it on the stone between them, deliberately, like laying down a card she didn't want. "Take it if you want. Throw it in the river. I'd honestly applaud."

"Why should I believe you're not here to finish what Vane started?"

"Because finishing things isn't why he hires people like me." Lena settled back on her heels. "He hires people like me because we're cheap, desperate, and deniable. If I vanish in this canyon, the report writes itself: experienced guide, solo ascent, tragic accident. He doesn't need me silenced, Okafor. He needs me *spent.*"

Something flickered across the kid's face — recognition, maybe. The look of someone who has also been spent.

"Eat first," Lena said, and dug out her food bag. "Then talk. Starving people make bad witnesses and worse decisions. Trust me, I've made both."

She tossed over a ration bar and a strip of dried fish. Ryn caught both on reflex and then stared at them like they might be rigged.

"It's not poisoned. If I wanted you dead I'd just wait. You look like a strong breeze could take you."

"Gee, thanks." But Ryn tore open the bar, and then ate with the terrible efficiency of true hunger, and Lena turned politely toward the canyon mouth and gave them the dignity of pretending not to watch.

While the kid ate, Lena studied the cave. Bedroll of woven reeds — clever, local material. A seep line channeled along the back wall into a hollowed stone, dripping slow and steady. Fish bones picked immaculately clean. A tally scratched into the rock: eleven marks.

Eleven days alone, after whatever happened to six trained surveyors. Lena had seen people crack in far better conditions.

"What happened to your team?" she asked, without turning around.

A long pause. Chewing sounds. Then, quieter: "You'll think I'm insane."

"Okafor, I've spent seven years being professionally thought insane. I've developed a tolerance."

* * *

It came out in pieces, between bites, the way hard stories always do — Ryn facing the canyon mouth now, knees drawn up, speaking to the river as if it were the only listener who wouldn't flinch.

"We came up the gorge following the thermal anomaly. That was the whole mission, officially — geothermal survey for the dam feasibility study. Vane ran it personally, which should have been the first warning. Ministries don't send deputy directors on routine surveys." Ryn's jaw tightened. "We found the warm-water channel on day nine. A side current coming out of the west wall, thirty degrees, carrying silt that doesn't match any upstream source. Dr. Halloway traced it to a seam behind the falls — a natural passage, going *back* into the rock. Warm air coming out of it. Green smell. Like growing things."

Lena's skin prickled. *Warm channel, west wall, follow the smell of green.* Her father's handwriting, thirty years old, describing exactly this.

"She wanted to report it. Full findings, immediate transmission. And Vane—" Ryn laughed, a small broken sound, "—Vane got very calm. That's the thing about him. Everyone expects anger. He goes calm. He ordered the readings sealed. Ordered us to log the channel as a minor seep, nothing significant. Said the data was 'premature.' Dr. Halloway argued with him for two hours. I've never heard anyone argue with Vane for two minutes."

"And then?"

Ryn was quiet long enough that the river filled the space.

"Then, on the last night, she woke us all. She'd burned the maps — the official ones, the ones Vane controlled. She held the ashes over the water and she said—" Ryn's voice wavered, steadied. "She said some maps are confessions. And that hers was about to be. She told us the valley behind the wall couldn't be allowed to end up in Vane's reports. Told us to scatter, go home, forget what we saw, or the ministry would make sure we forgot. And then she walked into the river."

Lena sat very still.

"Not slipped," Ryn said fiercely, turning to face her. "People will say she slipped. She *walked.* She chose it. She was sixty-one years old and she chose the river over letting Vane turn what she found into a drowning machine, and I don't know if that was courage or despair but I know it wasn't an accident."

"I believe you," Lena said.

"Do you?" Ryn searched her face. "Because everyone believes what's convenient. Vane believes she slipped. The ministry believes we scattered and died of incompetence. What do *you* believe, Nakamura? Why are you really here?"

It was a fair question, and Lena took her time with it, because the honest answer had been rearranging itself inside her ever since she'd seen the defaced survey marker three days ago — the symbol cut into the brass, the same spiral-with-a-bar symbol from her father's notebooks, the one he'd drawn in the margins of every map he'd ever trusted.

"I was hired to find survivors," she said. "I think Vane expected me to find bodies. Or nothing. Either works for him — a closed file, a tragedy, a clean path to the dam contract." She looked at the kid. "But I'm here because my father mapped this gorge in 1994. He drew the warm channel. He drew the passage behind the falls. And when he published partial maps of it, the profession destroyed him for it. Called it fabrication. Fraud. He died with that word attached to his name, and mine got attached to a different scandal, and I have spent seven years measuring my life in miles walked because miles don't ask what you're running from."

Ryn stared at her. "Your father is *the* Nakamura? The Rivergate map? We studied it in training — everyone said it was beautiful and fake."

"It was neither." Lena heard her own voice go strange. "It was accurate. Which in this canyon appears to be the dangerous thing to be."

* * *

They sat with that for a while. The light in the slot was failing, the way it did at four in the afternoon this deep in the gorge, dusk arriving early like a creditor.

"So what happens now?" Ryn asked finally. "You report finding me. Vane extracts us. Case closed, dam approved, valley—" the kid stopped, hearing it.

"Erased," Lena finished. "Flooded, sealed, filed under classified. Whatever's behind that wall, it dies the moment I make my radio call. If my radio worked." She glanced at the dead unit lying between them and felt, absurdly, grateful to the gorge for eating its signal.

"So don't call."

"If I don't call, I'm in breach of contract. If I'm in breach of contract, I'm a suspect, not a rescuer. Vane gets to write me however he wants." Lena rubbed her face with both hands. "And there are five others unaccounted for. Your teammates. I can't just—"

"There aren't." Ryn's voice had gone flat. "There's me. The others took the supply raft downstream on the third night, before Halloway walked into the water. Vane let them go — he wanted witnesses gone, not dead. Dead people get investigated. Terrified people just keep their mouths shut forever." A bitter pause. "It works. I'd have kept my mouth shut too, if I hadn't gotten separated in the flood surge trying to follow the raft. Ended up back here. Ended up hiding."

"From whom?"

"From whoever Vane sent next." Ryn looked at her directly. "Which was supposed to be you."

The words hung there. Lena let them.

"Yes," she said. "It was."

"And now?"

Lena looked out at the dying light on the black water, at the seam in the west wall where — now that she knew to look — the faintest shimmer of warmer air bent the view of the rock behind the falls. Her father had seen it thirty years ago and paid everything for the seeing. Dr. Halloway had seen it and chosen the river. Somewhere behind that rock was a valley that smelled of green things in the middle of winter, and somewhere downstream was a smooth-voiced man assembling the machinery to drown it.

"Now," Lena said, "you show me the channel. And then we figure out how two people the ministry considers expendable become the most inconvenient witnesses it's ever had."

Ryn studied her for a long moment. Then the kid did something Lena hadn't seen yet: smiled. It was crooked and exhausted and it transformed the whole hollow face.

"You know," Ryn said, "Halloway would have liked you. She had a rule — never trust anyone the ministry trusts, and never give up on anyone it doesn't."

"Sounds like a woman who read the fine print."

"She wrote half of it." Ryn got stiffly to their feet, swayed, steadied against the wall, and nodded toward the dark seam behind the falls. "It's a wet climb and the passage narrows twice. You'll hate it."

"Okafor, I once guided a honeymoon couple through a whiteout on the Kestrel Divide. I have a high tolerance for hating things."

Ryn snorted — the first real laugh, thin but real — and began gathering the woven bedroll, the fishing line, the eleven-day tally left scratched in stone.

Lena shouldered her pack and looked one last time at the dead radio on the ledge. Leaving it felt like leaving a door open behind her. But doors, she was beginning to understand, only mattered to people who intended to go back the way they came.

Behind the falls, the warm air breathed out of the rock, smelling impossibly of moss and growing things — and beneath that scent, faint but unmistakable, woodsmoke.

Someone was keeping a fire in there.

Someone was alive.
