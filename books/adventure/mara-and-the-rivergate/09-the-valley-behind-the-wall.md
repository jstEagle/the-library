## 9. The Valley Behind the Wall

The passage behind the waterfall was not a passage so much as an argument the mountain was having with itself — stone groaning, water roaring somewhere to their left, and the air growing warmer with every step until Lena had to strip off her outer shell and tie it around her waist.

"Tell me again why it's warm," Ryn said behind her, voice bouncing off wet rock. "Caves aren't warm. Caves are the opposite of warm. That's the whole deal with caves."

"Geothermal," Lena said. "There's a heat source under this whole massif. Feel the walls."

Ryn pressed a palm against the stone and made a sound like a kettle deciding not to whistle. "That's wrong. That's beautifully, completely wrong. The survey models said—"

"The survey models said a lot of things." Lena kept climbing. The floor of the channel sloped upward now, slick with mineral film that glowed faintly orange where the light from Ryn's headlamp caught it. Her father's phrase still sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. *Some maps are confessions.* She'd heard him say it at their kitchen table, pencil tucked behind his ear, when she was eleven and thought he was being dramatic. Cartographers were dramatic people; she understood that now. You spent your life drawing the world as it was, and the world kept insisting on being something else.

The channel turned, narrowed, turned again — and then the sound of the river changed.

Lena stopped. Ahead, maybe thirty meters, the black throat of the cave opened onto a smear of green.

Not gray. Not white. *Green.*

"It's January," she said stupidly.

"I know what month it is," Ryn whispered. "I've been counting them."

They came out of the wall into light.

* * *

The valley was a bowl held in two cupped hands of mountainside, perhaps three kilometers across, and every inch of it was alive. Grass — actual grass, deep and wind-combed — ran down from the scree slopes to a braided stream that wandered the valley floor like it had nowhere in particular to be. Steam rose from a dozen springs along its banks, ghosting up through the warm air, and where the water pooled it lay turquoise over white mineral shelves. Willows. Birch, stunted but stubborn. On the south-facing slope, terraced gardens stepped down in neat green shelves, and among them moved figures — small at this distance, dark-clothed, unhurried.

And above it all, at the head of the valley, a cliff face split by a cleft from which the Kestrel River poured out — young and narrow and utterly certain of itself, beginning the long journey down through the gorge they'd just climbed out of.

"That's it," Ryn said. "That's the source. The actual source. Every map I've ever seen puts the headwaters forty kilometers north, above the tree line, in the ice field."

"Every map is wrong," Lena said. Then, quieter: "Every published map is wrong."

Because she could see it now, the way her father must have seen it thirty years ago. The valley sat inside a fold of geothermal warmth like an egg in a hand. The springs kept the gorge's upper reaches ice-free year-round, which meant the river ran full even in winter, which meant the hydrology everyone had modeled from satellite data was fiction from the first contour line. Her father hadn't misdrawn the Kestrel. He had drawn it *truly*, and the truth had been too inconvenient to survive.

She stood there long enough that Ryn shifted uncomfortably beside her.

"Lena."

"Yeah."

"You're crying."

"I'm not." She wiped her face. "It's the steam."

"It's absolutely the steam," Ryn agreed kindly, and let it go, which Lena decided was the single most decent thing anyone had done for her in seven years.

* * *

They followed the stream down toward the terraces, and that was when Lena noticed the details that turned wonder into unease. A footbridge, mortised and pegged, no nails. A drying rack hung with fish cut in a way she recognized from old river cultures. A watchtower — small, wooden, built into a birch stand so that you'd never see it unless you were looking — and someone in it who was definitely looking.

"They knew we were coming," she murmured. "The moment we came out of the falls."

"Is that good or bad?"

"Depends what they do next."

What they did next was step out of the willows.

There were six of them, materializing with the ease of people who had practiced this, dressed in oiled wool and hide, faces weathered and watchful. Five carried bows. The sixth — a broad woman with iron-gray hair braided tight to her skull — carried nothing at all, which somehow frightened Lena more than the arrows did.

The five bows were drawn. Not theatrically, not as warning. Drawn the way you draw a bow when the decision has already been made and the arrow is a formality.

Lena raised her hands slowly, keeping them wide and empty. Beside her, Ryn went pale and did the same.

"We're not armed," Lena said. "We're looking for survivors of a survey team. This one—" she tipped her head toward Ryn, "—is one of them. I'm a guide. I was hired to search."

The gray-haired woman studied her with an expression Lena couldn't read — not hostility exactly, though the arrows were hostile enough. Something older than hostility. Something like recognition running backward.

"Nakamura," the woman said.

It wasn't a question, and it landed in Lena's chest like a dropped stone. "How do you—"

"Your father stood where you're standing." The woman's voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of someone delivering a verdict rather than starting a conversation. "Thirty-one years ago. Same season. He came out of the falls with his hands up, too."

The steam drifted between them. Somewhere upstream, the river chuckled over its stones, indifferent to history repeating.

"My name is Ivo Marsh's question to answer, not mine," the woman went on. "But I'll tell you this much, guide, so you understand where you are. This valley exists because our great-grandparents ran from people who drew maps. Maps are how they found us last time. So when a cartographer's daughter walks out of the wall carrying survey equipment—" her eyes flicked to Ryn's pack, to the government-issue GPS unit clipped to its strap, "—you'll forgive us if hospitality isn't our first instinct."

One of the archers said something low in a language Lena didn't know — old vowels, river-smoothed — and the gray-haired woman answered without turning her head.

"Put those down before someone sneezes," she added, almost as an afterthought, and the bows lowered — though Lena noted the arrows stayed nocked, and the archers stayed spread in a loose crescent that would funnel any runner straight back toward the cliff.

Professional. These weren't farmers who happened to own weapons. These were farmers who had *decided*, generations ago, never to be surprised again.

"I'm Sera," the woman said. "You'll walk ahead of us. You'll keep your hands visible. And when we reach the longhouse, you'll speak to Ivo Marsh, and whatever he decides, that's what happens. Understood?"

"And if it isn't?" Ryn asked, very quietly.

Sera looked at Ryn the way one looks at weather. "Then you'll learn why nobody downstream will guide you past the gorge. We don't kill messengers here. But we've had thirty years to get good at making sure messages stop arriving."

* * *

They walked the length of the valley as prisoners who were also, confusingly, guests. Children watched from behind garden terraces and then, deciding the strangers were insufficiently interesting, went back to hauling irrigation channels with wooden spades. An old man mending a net nodded to them with genuine friendliness, and Sera cleared her throat at him until the nod became something more neutral. Dogs — real dogs, shaggy and self-important — trotted alongside for a while, decided Lena smelled acceptable, and lost interest.

It should have felt idyllic. Warm air in January, springwater, gardens under snow-line. It was the kind of place that existed in the maps her father used to draw in the margins of his surveys — little imaginary valleys labeled *here be better weather*. And yet every fence post was set a hand deeper than it needed to be, every gate latched from the inside, every sightline across the valley covered by some structure that could hold a defender. Paradise built like a fortress, because paradise was exactly the thing that got people hunted.

"He wasn't lying," Ryn murmured as they crossed the footbridge. "Your father. About any of it."

"No," Lena said. "He wasn't."

And that was the wound of it, laid open fresh. Seven years she'd carried her own disgrace like a stone she deserved, and all the while her father had carried his — a bigger stone, a fraud's brand burned into a true map — and neither of them had ever been permitted to put theirs down.

The longhouse stood at the valley's center, timbered and low-hipped, smoke rising from a ridge vent. Outside its door, waiting, stood a man in his seventies, straight-backed, with a cartographer's hands — Lena knew those hands on sight, the ink-stained calluses, the way the fingers curled slightly as if always holding a pencil that wasn't there.

He looked at her for a long moment. Then at the falls behind her. Then back.

"So," Ivo Marsh said. "He sent his daughter after all."

"He didn't send me," Lena said. "I didn't know any of this existed until yesterday."

Ivo's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "No," he said. "He wouldn't have. That was the terms." He pushed open the door, and warm air and woodsmoke rolled out to meet them. "Come in, Nakamura. You and I are going to talk about debts. Yours, mine, and especially your father's."

Behind them, Sera posted two archers at the door — facing *inward* — and Lena understood, walking into the firelight, that she had traded the gorge's cold prison for a warmer one.

And that the interview had already begun.
