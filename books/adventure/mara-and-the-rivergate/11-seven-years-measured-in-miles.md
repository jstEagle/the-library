## 11. Seven Years Measured in Miles

They gave her a room with a window.

That was the first thing Lena noticed about being a prisoner in paradise — the courtesy of it. The room sat at the edge of the elder's longhouse, above the drying racks where pale strips of fish curled in the geothermal warmth, and the window looked out on the whole impossible bowl of the valley: green terraces stepping down to a river that ran clear as glass, steam rising from vents along its banks like the earth itself was breathing. Snow crowned the rim of the canyon wall in every direction, and none of it ever came down here. The Kestrel's true source, warm and unhurried, feeding a secret.

A prisoner's window with a view like that was either kindness or a message. With Ivo Marsh, she suspected it was both.

Three days now since the bows had lowered — not out of trust, but because Tamsin had vouched for her, hand flat against her own chest in some old gesture of surety, and Ryn had backed her up with the shaking voice of someone who'd watched their team leader walk into a river. Three days of good food, warm water, and doors that closed behind her without locking but didn't open either. The guards were polite. One of them, a broad-shouldered woman named Petra who smelled perpetually of woodsmoke, had taken to leaving a cup of something herbal outside Lena's door each morning, minty and faintly bitter, and saying nothing when Lena drank it.

"They're deciding what you are," Ryn had told her, apologetic, on the first evening. "It took them two weeks to decide about me."

"And what did they decide?"

"That I was frightened rather than dangerous." Ryn had smiled crookedly. "Frightened gets fed. Dangerous gets watched. You're getting both, so they haven't made up their minds."

So Lena waited, and did what she always did when the world refused to move: she measured miles.

It was an old habit, older than the blacklist. Her father had taught it to her without meaning to — *a body can't count years, little bird, years are too big, but a body can count miles* — and after the incident, after the hearings and the blackballing and the slow erosion of everything she'd built, she had quietly stopped counting years altogether. Seven years became eleven thousand miles of trail crew work. Seven years became the height of the Sawtooth traverse done four times, the length of the Copperline drainage done nine, the endless gray arithmetic of switchbacks. It was a way of living that kept grief at arm's length: you couldn't mourn a life you weren't measuring in the ordinary units.

She stood at the window now and counted the miles visible from it. Terraced fields, maybe a mile of them end to end. The river's visible run, perhaps three before it bent behind the hot springs. The canyon wall opposite, half a mile up and sheer. A small map. She could have drawn it from memory by tomorrow, and probably would, because drawing maps was the other thing she did when the world refused to move, and because somewhere beneath the waiting there was a thought taking shape that she wasn't ready to look at directly yet.

The thought looked like this: *my father stood in this valley.*

She turned away from the window before the thought could finish forming.

* * *

On the fourth day, they let her walk.

Not free — never free; Petra trailed her at twenty paces with the patient air of a woman herding something large and potentially stupid — but walking. Ivo Marsh found her at the terraces, where she'd stopped to watch an old man teach three children to set fish weirs in the warm shallows, their laughter carrying across the water like something from a country she'd once been a citizen of.

"You draw," Ivo said, nodding at the slate she carried. She'd been sketching the weir design, purely from habit, and caught herself doing it too late.

"I'm a cartographer."

"You're a trail guide with a criminal record." His voice wasn't cruel. That was the unsettling part. It was the voice of a man reading a ledger aloud. "The ministry's file on you traveled with Vane's people, and Vane's people left copies. We read everything they carry. It's how we've stayed alive."

"Then you know the record is wrong."

"I know the record exists." Ivo folded his hands. He was a spare man, gray-bearded, with eyes the color of the river in November. "Come walk with me, Nakamura. There's a debt in this valley with your family name on it, and while the elders argue about interest, I'd rather you understood the principal."

They walked the terrace path together, Petra trailing, past fields of something like barley and orchards of small hard apples, past a smithy whose forge ran off a vented spring so the fire never died. Children stared at her openly. An old woman spat — not at her, Lena noted, just near her, in the general direction of outsiders — and went back to her weaving.

"Thirty-one years ago," Ivo said, "a man came down the warm channel alone. Half-frozen, half-starved, with a surveyor's eye and a notebook full of contour lines. He'd followed the same evidence your survey team followed. He stood where you stood." Ivo glanced sideways. "You have his chin. Did you know that?"

Lena said nothing. Her throat had gone tight.

"He stayed with us a season. Learned our names. Drew our valley — the only accurate map of it that has ever existed, or will exist if I have anything to say about it. And he swore an oath, on the water itself, that no line of his would ever lead another soul through the Rivergate." Ivo's jaw worked. "Then he went home to the lowlands, and the pressure came. Funding, they say. Institutions. Men in clean boots who explained that a map of a blank space was worth more than a map of a hidden one, unless the hidden one could be owned. And he published. Partial maps. A valley hinted at but never located, contours that matched nothing any expedition could find. They called him a fraud, Nakamura. They took his credentials, his standing, his name. Because he broke his word to us, and because the men he wouldn't serve decided a ruined cartographer was safer than a silenced one."

The path climbed toward the rim shelf where the watchfires burned at night. Lena stopped walking.

"He never told me," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Any of it. He just — stopped mapping. When I was twelve he put his instruments in a box and got a job teaching, and my mother said he'd lost his nerve, and I believed her. I believed he'd been a fraud. I was *ashamed* of him."

"He was ashamed of himself," Ivo said. "There's a difference, and it matters, but it won't comfort you."

It didn't. She stood on the path with the warm wind coming up the valley and felt thirty years rearrange themselves around her like a rockfall settling. Her father hadn't failed. He'd been *broken* — deliberately, efficiently, by people whose names she'd never know, for reasons that fit in a ledger next to dam contracts and insurance settlements and the careers of important men. And then—

And then the same thing had happened to her.

* * *

She hadn't thought about the Meridian Expedition in almost two years. Not properly. She kept it the way you keep a broken tooth: aware of it, careful around it, never pressing.

But that night, in the room with the window, with the valley breathing steam below her, the tooth cracked open.

Seven years ago. The Corvane-Meridian high-altitude traverse, forty clients at eight thousand meters' equivalent, the biggest commercial contract of her young career. She'd been the senior guide. She'd noticed things in the first week — the fixed lines strung on rotten anchors to save cost, the weather forecasting outsourced to a service with no station above the treeline, the oxygen reserves quietly halved because the client list had overbooked. She'd documented all of it. Photographs, notes, dates. And on the rest day at High Camp, she'd filed a formal safety report through the company's own channel, because she was twenty-six and believed the channel existed for exactly that purpose.

What happened next had a rhythm she'd only now begun to hear. First, silence. Then a meeting where a director named Halloran — smooth, reasonable, a man who spoke about *risk tolerance* the way priests spoke about grace — explained that the report was premature, that the season's economics were already committed, that a guide's job was to manage conditions, not publicize them. Then, when she filed anyway with the regional authority, the machinery engaged. Her certifications came under review. Two clients, coached or bought, gave statements about her judgment. The avalanche on the descent — the one that killed Marcus Webb, the one that happened exactly where her report said it would happen — became, in the official account, a tragedy attributable to *guiding error*. Hers. The report itself vanished from the record entirely, unfiled, unacknowledged, a document that had never existed. The inquiry cleared the company. The company's parent firm held contracts with the very ministry that now, seven years later, paid Aldous Vane to make things disappear.

She had spent seven years believing she'd made a mistake. That was the elegant cruelty of it — they hadn't needed to convince the world she was guilty, only her. A blacklisted guide who secretly believes she deserves it doesn't fight. She walks. Eleven thousand miles of walking, head down, paying a debt that was never hers.

Lena sat on the edge of the narrow bed in the warm dark and shook with rage — real rage, the kind she hadn't allowed herself since the hearings, rage with a taste like copper. At Halloran. At the inquiry panel with its pre-written conclusions. At every client who'd signed the settlement and gone home. At herself, most of all, for swallowing it, for letting them write her story in their ledger and calling it humility.

And underneath the rage, cooling it slowly the way snow cools a burn, came the clarity.

Her father's fate and hers were not similar stories. They were the *same* story. Twice told, thirty years apart, by the same kind of men with the same clean boots. He had drawn true maps and been called a fraud; she had written a true report and been called negligent. In both cases the record — the official, laminated, authoritative record — had been written by the people with the most to lose from the truth. And in both cases the victim had been handed the pen last, invited to sign the version of events that destroyed them, and punished not for lying but for refusing to lie quickly enough.

*Who writes the records,* she thought, *owns what happened.*

Her father had understood that at the end. *Some maps are confessions,* he'd written, and she'd heard the phrase secondhand from a dying man's memory in a cave, and it had taken her until this warm dark room to understand it fully. His final map — the one the elders kept, the one she'd glimpsed rolled in oilcloth in Ivo's archive — wasn't a confession of fraud. It was a confession of what had been done to him, encoded in contour lines, addressed to whoever came after him with the eyes to read it.

He'd been writing to her. Thirty years ahead of time, in the only language they shared.

Lena lay back and laughed — a short, cracked sound, startling herself. Somewhere below, a dog barked once and settled. The rage was still there, but it had changed shape, the way ice changes shape when the sun finds it. Rage that only burns is fuel for other people's fires. This was something else. This was rage with a destination.

They had buried her father with a fraud's name. They had buried her under a mistake she never made. Fine. She knew now where the shovels were kept: in the records. And records, unlike mountains, could be moved.

* * *

In the morning, Petra brought the mint tea and, for the first time, a summons.

"Elders want you at the longhouse. Midmorning." The woman paused, and something almost human crossed her weathered face. "Wear your good boots. Tamsin says you'll be walking far today, one way or the other."

Lena drank the tea, shouldered her pack out of pure habit, and checked the slate sketches she'd made of the valley — the weirs, the terraces, the spring-fed forge, the watchfire shelves. Four days of captivity, and she'd mapped the place anyway. Some habits were just inheritance wearing different clothes.

At the door, she stopped and looked back at the window with its view of the green and steaming bowl of the world.

"Miles," she said quietly, to no one, to her father, to the girl she'd been at twenty-six signing her own destruction. "I'm done counting yours."

The longhouse doors stood open when she arrived, and inside, beyond the fire pit, Ivo Marsh sat flanked by the council — and beside him, on the table, laid out with terrible care, lay her father's final map, unrolled and weighted at the corners with river stones.

And Ivo's face, when he looked up at her, was the face of a man who had just read something in it that scared him.
