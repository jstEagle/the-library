## 12. Vane Ascending

Aldous Vane had a theory about disasters, and it was this: a disaster was simply information that had arrived too early. Handle the timing, and you handled the disaster. He had built an entire career on that principle, first in hydrology, then in infrastructure planning, then in the Ministry of Waterways and Regional Development, where he now occupied a corner office on the fourth floor with a view of the Kestrel River's industrial reach — grey water, grey barges, grey sky, all of it obedient.

The missing survey team was, at present, unhandled information. That would be corrected by Friday.

He stood at his window with a cup of tea going lukewarm in his hand and reviewed the situation the way he reviewed everything: as a ledger. Six members of the Kestrel Headwaters Survey Team, unaccounted for, eleven days. One junior member, Ryn Okafor, also missing but — according to the last satellite pass — plausibly alive somewhere in the gorge system. And one Lena Nakamura, disgraced mountain guide, currently under contract to the Ministry through a liaison arrangement Vane had personally authorized, which meant deniable, which meant his.

Three categories of problem. Three categories of solution.

His assistant, a young man named Pell with the anxious energy of a spaniel, knocked twice and entered carrying a folder Vane had not asked for.

"The press office is asking for a statement, sir. They've got the families talking to the *Kestrel Courier*. Mrs. Anselmo — she's the mother of the team's geologist — gave an interview from her porch. She was crying."

"Of course she was crying," Vane said mildly. "Grief is the most photogenic emotion we have. Did she say anything actionable?"

Pell blinked. "Actionable, sir?"

"Did she blame anyone? Name anyone? Grief needs a shape, Pell. If we don't give it one, the newspapers will, and they have terrible instincts." He turned from the window. "What did she say?"

"She said — " Pell consulted his notes. "She said 'somebody sent those children up there, and somebody knew what was up there.'"

Vane sipped his tea. Somewhere behind his sternum, a small cold engine turned over and settled into its idle. "Mrs. Anselmo is more perceptive than half my staff," he said. "Note that down. Not for the file. For me."

* * *

The press conference was held at eleven, in the ministry atrium, beneath a mural of the Kestrel watershed rendered in heroic blues — a map so simplified it constituted fiction, which Vane had always felt made it the most honest artwork in the building. He spoke for six minutes without notes, because notes suggested uncertainty, and uncertainty was the one thing a bureaucrat could never survive.

"We will not speculate," he told the assembled reporters, "because speculation is a cruelty dressed up as concern. What I can tell you is this: the Ministry has authorized a full-scale search-and-rescue operation, the largest in the region's history. Helicopters. Ground teams. Hydrological survey support." He let the word *hydrological* sit there, plain and technical, a stone dropped into still water. "We will find them, or we will find out what happened to them, and either way the families will have answers."

A woman from the Courier raised her hand. "Mr. Vane, there are reports that a private guide was contracted before the official operation began. A Lena Nakamura. Is that true?"

"It is true that the Ministry retains consultants," Vane said, with the small regretful smile he practiced in mirrors until it stopped feeling like lying. "Ms. Nakamura was engaged briefly, in a preliminary capacity. Her contract has since been terminated."

"Terminated why?"

"Because," Vane said, and here he allowed himself a pause long enough to seem reluctant, "the Ministry received information suggesting Ms. Nakamura may have been the last person to see several members of the team alive. We are not accusing her of anything. She is a person of interest in an ongoing inquiry, nothing more. But you will understand that we could not, in good conscience, continue to employ her while that inquiry proceeds."

He watched the sentence land. He watched pens move. By evening it would be everywhere: *person of interest*, repeated until it sounded like *suspect*, repeated until it sounded like *guilty*. He hadn't accused her of anything. He had merely arranged the furniture of the room so that everyone else drew the obvious conclusion. This was, in Vane's experience, the difference between a lie and a policy.

"Is there any truth," another reporter called, "to rumors about the upper gorge? That the survey team found something?"

"There is always a rumor about the upper gorge," Vane said, and got his laugh. "The Rivergate has been frightening people since before this ministry existed. It keeps its own, the locals say." Another laugh. "Well. We intend to disappoint it."

* * *

In the afternoon, the real meeting happened — the one that didn't appear on any calendar, held in the conference room reserved for procurement hearings because nobody ever attended procurement hearings.

There were five of them: Vane; Director Halloway from the dam authority, a heavy man who sweated ambition; two engineers from Corvane Hydrostructural, the firm whose bid for the Upper Kestrel project had been stalled for three years by exactly one obstacle; and a quiet man from the Deputy Minister's office who said almost nothing and therefore said the most important things.

"The problem," Vane began, standing at the head of the table with a laser pointer he never actually used, "is that the Upper Kestrel site was rejected in the original feasibility study due to, quote, 'unresolved hydrological anomalies in the gorge system.' Unmapped channels. Inconsistent flow data. The engineers couldn't guarantee the reservoir would hold, and the review board wouldn't sign off on a dam above four hundred downstream residents without guarantees." He clicked to a slide showing the gorge in cross-section, all clean lines and confident shading. "That study was based on surface surveys. The Nakamura cartography — the father's work — suggested the river behaves differently than our models assume. The board used his fraud conviction to dismiss his data entirely. Convenient, but inconvenient in equal measure, because if he was right, the site is dangerous. And if he was wrong, the site is still unmapped, and unmapped is uninsurable."

"And the survey team?" Halloway asked.

"The survey team was tasked with resolving the question definitively. They found something behind the canyon wall — a warm-water channel, possibly a geothermal-fed source system — and their leader destroyed their own field records and walked into the river. I want to be clear about what that means." Vane set down the pointer. "It means whatever is back there cannot be permitted to exist on paper. A hidden valley with an independent water source, occupied by an unregistered settlement, upstream of a four-hundred-million-crown dam contract? The environmental review alone would take a decade. The land claims would take longer. The project dies, the contract dies, and the region stays poor and grateful about it."

"So we make sure it doesn't exist on paper," the quiet man said. It wasn't a question.

"We make sure it doesn't exist at all," Vane corrected gently. "Paper can leak. Paper can be photographed. Paper can end up in a journalist's inbox attached to a self-righteous cover letter." He advanced to the next slide: the gorge, again, but this time with a proposed structure marked in red. "The diversion dam at the upper narrows. Officially, it's a rescue-support installation — a cofferdam to control flow while ground teams search the canyon. Unofficially, once it's up and the reservoir fills, the warm channel floods. The passage drowns. Whatever valley exists behind that wall becomes inaccessible except by air, and we control the airspace. No maps. No claims. No story. In eighteen months the whole question quietly closes itself, and the Upper Kestrel project proceeds on clean, boring, defensible data."

One of the Corvane engineers raised a hand like a schoolboy. "The flood modeling. If we impound at the narrows, and the Nakamura data was even partially correct about subsurface channels—"

"Then the model accounts for it," Vane said. "I've had the model revised. It accounts for everything. That's what models are for."

The engineer looked like he wanted to say something else, decided against it, and wrote a note instead. Vane noted the note. People who wrote notes were either conscientious or dangerous, and the two categories overlapped more than he liked.

"Costs?" Halloway asked.

"Modest. The cofferdam is already budgeted under emergency response authority, which means no competitive bidding and no public comment period. Emergency authority lasts ninety days. The reservoir reaches critical level in sixty." Vane smiled at the room, warm as a heating vent. "By the time anyone thinks to ask questions, the questions will be underwater."

The quiet man from the Deputy Minister's office spoke for the second time. "And the guide? Nakamura?"

"Ah." Vane gathered his papers. "Ms. Nakamura is in the gorge, presumably heading toward the very thing we're making disappear. Which presents both a risk and an opportunity. A risk, because she's competent — genuinely competent, whatever her file says — and because she has a personal history with that terrain we only partly understand. An opportunity, because if she finds survivors, or evidence, or anything at all, she'll bring it out through Ferrow's Landing, where our people will be waiting. And if she doesn't come out—" He shrugged, a small economical motion. "Then the gorge keeps its own. The locals will tell you it always has."

"You sound almost fond of her," Halloway said.

"I'm fond of efficiency," Vane said. "Ms. Nakamura is doing our reconnaissance for us, free of charge, and taking all the risk. When she's served that purpose, she becomes a suspect in a missing-persons inquiry with a documented grudge against the Ministry. Frankly, she's the most useful employee I've ever had, and she doesn't know she works for me." He snapped the folder shut. "Which reminds me. Someone get me her personnel file. All of it. The incident seven years ago, the blacklist, the appeals — everything. If I'm going to bury a woman professionally, I'd like to know which shovel fits best."

* * *

That evening he worked late, alone, the corridor outside dark, the river lights burning orange beyond the glass. He laid out the operation on his desk like a hand of cards: the cofferdam authorization, signed; the helicopter schedule; the communications blackout order for the upper gorge, justified as "interference mitigation"; the warrantless seizure request for any documents recovered in the search area; and, clipped neatly at the top, the press release naming Lena Nakamura a person of interest, timed for tomorrow morning, after she would be too deep in the canyon to answer it.

He read each document twice. He initialled each page. There was a version of himself, he supposed — some younger Aldous, some graduate student who'd gone into hydrology because he loved rivers — who might have hesitated here. But that boy had grown up and learned the truth of rivers, which is that they are not beautiful, they are not free, they are not wild. They are water obeying gradients, and every good thing in civilization — light, warmth, the evening meal — comes from putting gradients to work. The valley behind the wall was not a paradise. It was inefficiency wearing flowers. Four hundred people downstream needed power, needed flood control, needed jobs. Against that arithmetic, a few dozen squatters in a hole in the mountains were a rounding error, and sentiment was just noise in the data.

Besides, he told himself, folding his glasses away, no one would drown. The passage would fill slowly, over weeks. Anyone inside would have ample time to walk out — into a world that would ask them for papers they didn't have, but that was hardly his department. He was not destroying anyone. He was correcting a map.

His phone buzzed. Pell, texting from the press office: *Courier running the Nakamura story front page. Also — odd thing — ferry operator in Ferrow's Landing, woman named Sela, gave a statement to a stringer. Said, quote, "the river remembers who's owed." Should I flag it?*

Vane looked at the message for a long moment. The river remembers who's owed. Superstition, obviously. Ferry-women and their folklore, the same people who claimed the Rivergate kept its own, the same people who'd refused to guide the survey team, which was how the whole delay started.

But he thought of old Nakamura — the cartographer, the fraud — and of the file he'd requested, and of the fact that in thirty years of dealing with the Kestrel, every single person who'd gone looking behind that wall had come back changed or hadn't come back at all.

*Flag it,* he typed. *And Pell — find out everything about that ferrywoman. Everyone in Ferrow's Landing knows something. Start peeling.*

He set the phone face-down, poured out the cold tea, and stood a moment at the window. Downstream, the barges moved their slow cargo through the dark, and the river went on being useful, being managed, being his.

Eleven days, he thought. In eleven days the cofferdam closes, and in sixty the water rises, and the last unmapped place in the Kestrel watershed becomes a smooth blue rectangle on someone's slide deck.

He just had to make sure one disgraced guide didn't walk out of the canyon with the arithmetic in her pocket.

In the gorge, forty miles upstream, a radio sat silent on a ledge above black water, and somewhere below it, faintly, the river ran warm.
