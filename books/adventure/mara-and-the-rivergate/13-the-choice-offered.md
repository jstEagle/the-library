## 13. The Choice Offered

Ivo Marsh had spent sixty-one years learning that the hardest conversations were never the ones you dreaded most. They were the ones that arrived wearing comfortable clothes.

He dressed for this one carefully, the way his mother had taught him to dress for funerals and negotiations — which in the valley's history had often been the same event. Good wool coat. The silver clasp his grandmother had carried across three borders sewn into her hem. Boots polished, though no one in the Long House would look at his boots. It wasn't about being seen. It was about feeling like a man who had made his decisions on purpose.

The council fire was already lit when he came down. Tamsin had built it too hot — she always did, impatience expressed in kindling — and the flames stood up straight and eager like soldiers at attention. Ivo nudged a log apart with his boot to let the fire breathe sideways. Patience, he wanted to tell it. And her. And me.

"Is she awake?" he asked.

"Been awake since before dawn," said Tamsin. "I watched her from the granary. She did that thing with her pack straps again. The counting thing."

"The counting thing," Ivo repeated, because it was important, in a council of elders, to occasionally admit you had no idea what the young people were saying.

"You know. She loosens them, tightens them, loosens them. Like she's checking whether she can leave." Tamsin fed another branch to the fire. "Grandpa, nobody counts their straps if they're planning to stay."

Ivo looked at his granddaughter — nineteen, sharp as a frost edge, and entirely too perceptive for the family's collective comfort — and said nothing, because the observation was correct, and correct observations from teenagers should never be rewarded.

* * *

They convened in the Long House at midmorning: Ivo, Elder Sorrel with her hands folded like a closed book, old Fenwick who mostly slept but whose silences carried more weight than other men's speeches, and two others whose families had held seats since the Crossing. Lena Nakamura sat across the fire from them, spine straight, hands still, and Ivo thought: *there is a woman who has been judged by strangers so many times she has learned to make it easy for them.*

Ryn Okafor sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. The boy — no, not a boy anymore; the gorge had burned whatever childhood Ryn had brought into it right off them — kept glancing between Lena and the elders like someone watching a card game where every hand was held face down.

"Lena Nakamura," Ivo began formally, "you entered our valley uninvited."

"I did," she said. No apology, no excuse. Just the fact, set down on the table like a stone.

"You found the passage your father found."

"I found what was left of his map of it. In pieces. In my own memory."

Sorrel stirred. "And you understand why that is a problem."

"I understand that thirty years ago a man stood roughly where I'm sitting, made a promise, and broke it." Lena's voice didn't waver, but Ivo saw her hands press flat against her knees, one small violence contained. "I've spent my whole life paying for that promise. So yes. I understand."

Fenwick opened one eye. This was, by Fenwick standards, an impassioned intervention.

Ivo raised a hand, and the room settled. He had rehearsed this speech four times in the night, walking the perimeter path under a sky full of unfamiliar-to-her stars, and now that the moment came, the words felt both heavier and simpler than they had in the dark.

"Here is what we offer," he said. "You stay. You take a name among us — not hiding yours, but adding to it, the way all of us added to ours once. You work. You learn the springs, the terraces, the winter stores. In exchange, we write to the lowlands through channels older than any ministry. There are people downstream who owe us favors and don't know it yet. Your record can be cleaned quietly. Quietly matters. A cleared name announced loudly brings questions, and questions bring surveyors, and surveyors bring men like Vane."

He paused. "You would be safe. Anonymous. Whole. That is the offer."

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, a child laughed — the unrehearsed, ridiculous laugh of a valley kid who had never once wondered whether the world beyond the wall would have room for her.

"And if I refuse?" Lena asked.

Ivo hated this part. He had hated it when the council drafted it, hated it when he'd argued — unsuccessfully — to soften it, and hated it now, watching her face as he delivered it.

"If you refuse, you leave the way you came, alone, and we seal the passage behind you. If you speak of this valley afterward — to anyone, ever — you will be treated as an enemy. Not hunted. We are not Vane. But shut out. Permanently. Your father's maps, his notes, everything the council holds — you would never see them again."

Silence. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

It was Ryn who broke it. "Take the deal."

Lena turned. "Ryn—"

"No, listen." Ryn leaned forward, and Ivo watched the terrible arithmetic of the gorge still running behind those eyes, the weeks of hunger and terror compressing into urgency. "I *lived* out there. I ate candle wax. I drank water with things swimming in it that I decided not to identify. The valley kept me alive when the ministry wrote me off as a line item. This place is real. The name they'll clear for you — that's paper. Take the real thing."

"It's not paper to me," Lena said quietly. "Seven years, Ryn. Seven years of trail crews and border towns and people checking my references twice and then a third time just to enjoy it. You think I don't want my name back? I have dreamed about it. I have *practiced* hearing it spoken without flinching." Her voice caught, steadied. "But."

"But what?"

"But I've heard this offer before." She looked around the circle, and her gaze landed on Ivo with a weight that made him glad he was sitting down. "Not these words. Other words. 'Sign here and the incident goes away.' 'Stay quiet and your career recovers.' 'Cooperate and there will always be a place for you.' Every one of them true, technically. Every one of them a door that only locks from the inside." She shook her head slowly. "This isn't an invitation. It's a blacklist with a garden in it."

Tamsin, at the doorway, made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Sorrel shot her a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces.

Ivo felt something twist in his chest that was not quite anger and not quite admiration. It was the feeling he got whenever someone described the valley accurately from the outside — the strange grief of seeing your sanctuary named correctly by a stranger.

"You're wrong about one thing," he said.

"Am I?"

"A gilded cage is still given gold." He leaned forward. "We are offering you gold, Ms. Nakamura. Real gold. Safety, community, a life. Most people in the lowlands never get offered half of it. Don't mistake the shape of the gift for contempt of the giver."

"Oh, I don't." For the first time, something warmed in her face — not softness exactly, but recognition. "I know you believe you're offering me shelter. My father believed he was offering the world accurate maps. Both of you were doing the best you could with the tools you had." She stood — slowly, deliberately, so no one could read it as aggression — and looked around the Long House at the beams overhead, each one cut and raised by hands three, four, five generations gone. "But I've been told where to stand and what silence costs my whole adult life. I'm done buying safety with erasure. Mine or anyone else's."

"And your name?" Sorrel asked. "You'd walk away from it?"

"My name was taken from me once by people who thought truth was a liability." Lena's chin lifted. "If it comes back, it comes back whole. With the valley in it or not at all."

* * *

Afterward, Ivo walked the terrace path above the eastern springs, which was where he went when he needed to argue with himself and lose honestly.

Tamsin caught up with him at the second switchback, which surprised him not at all. His granddaughter moved through his defenses the way water moved through limestone: patiently, inevitably, along lines he hadn't known existed until they were already open.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"You argued for mercy in the council last night. Grandpa Fenwick told me. Now you handed her an ultimatum with a bow on it. Which one is the real you?"

Ivo considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, which took them most of the switchback. "Both," he said finally. "That's the trouble with being old. You contain multitudes, and most of them disagree before breakfast."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one available." He stopped at the overlook, where the whole valley spread below — green fields steaming faintly where the springs ran under them, smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless cold, the impossible, improbable warmth of the place that had swallowed his great-grandparents' generation whole and asked only that they never speak of it. "Your grandmother used to say the valley doesn't need walls. It needs *reasons*. Walls keep people out. Reasons keep them wanting to stay. And I gave that woman a wall today, Tamsin, and called it a reason, and she saw straight through it, and the worst part—" he exhaled, breath fogging, "—the worst part is I think she's right."

Tamsin was quiet for a moment. Then: "So change the offer."

"It's not mine to change."

"It's yours to *start*, though. Somebody has to go first. That's how every rule in this valley got bent eventually. Somebody's grandfather went first and everyone pretended it had always been allowed."

Ivo looked down at her — this fierce, impatient girl who had argued for mercy toward Ryn when the watchers caught her stealing from the archive, who had volunteered for the descent without hesitation, who carried her convictions the way her late mother had carried water: without spilling, without complaint, up any slope required. He felt the particular vertigo of realizing your grandchildren have become the adults.

"There's another problem," he said. "One you don't know about. One almost nobody knows about."

"What problem?"

"Her father's field notes. The ones we confiscated thirty years ago." Ivo rubbed his jaw. "I was the one who took them. I was young, and the council was frightened, and I told myself we were protecting the valley. We read them, of course. All of them. And there's a map in there, Tamsin. A final one. Not terrain." He hesitated. "Water."

"Water?"

"The whole system. The gorge, the river, the falls behind the wall. Annotated." He heard his own voice go flat, the way it did when the content was too heavy to carry with inflection. "Annotated with a warning. About what happens downstream if anyone dams the upper gorge."

Tamsin had gone very still. "Vane's dam."

"Vane's dam."

"How bad?"

Ivo looked out over the valley, over the warm fields and the straight smoke and the children laughing somewhere out of sight, and thought about Ferrow's Landing, about Sela's ferry rope and the crooked little houses along the levee, about hundreds of people asleep at night trusting the river to stay in its bed.

"Bad enough," he said, "that I'm no longer sure which side of this bargain we're standing on."

Below them, near the guest hut, Lena Nakamura was packing her straps again. Loosen. Tighten. Loosen.

Counting whether she could leave.

Or, Ivo thought with a fresh and uncomfortable chill, counting how long she had left to convince us to let her read what her father had written — before men with drills reached the gorge, and the choice stopped being ours to offer at all.
