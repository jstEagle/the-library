## 15. Fracture

Ryn Okafor had always been good at small things. Small repairs. Small kindnesses. Small lies.

It was the last one that was going to get them killed.

They lay awake in the guest hut — *their* hut now, three weeks a resident of paradise — listening to the geothermal springs breathe somewhere beneath the floorboards like the valley itself was sleeping, and told themselves the same lie they'd been polishing for days: *I'm not really going to do it. I'm just going to look at it one more time.*

The map archive sat in the longhouse at the center of the settlement, in a cedar chest older than any nation Ryn could name, and everyone here treated it like scripture. Which, Ryn supposed, it was. Every route out of the gorge. Every passage behind every waterfall. The locations of the hot vents that kept the valley ice-free. If Aldous Vane got his hands on even half of it, he wouldn't need to dam the Rivergate at all. He'd just walk in through the front door with a smile and a clipboard.

And if Ryn Okafor walked into Ferrow's Landing carrying that chest under their arm, Vane would make all of this go away. The missing-persons report. The suspicion. The quiet questions about why a junior survey tech had survived when five others hadn't. Amnesty, Vane had called it, back when Ryn still thought of him as *Mr. Vane, sir* — amnesty for anyone who cooperates.

Ryn turned over. Beside them, on the shelf, sat a carved wooden bird Tamsin had made them, lopsided, its beak more suggestion than anatomy. "It's a kestrel," she'd said. "Obviously." It was not obviously anything. Ryn loved it completely.

That was the problem. That was exactly the problem. You couldn't steal from people who gave you ugly birds.

But you also couldn't stand in front of a firing squad — or whatever the valley equivalent was, bows probably, definitely bows — because your conscience was cleaner than your survival instincts. Ryn had watched Marsh Okonkwo, their team leader, fold his own maps into a neat square and hand them to the river like a man posting a letter. Ryn had watched him walk into the water after them. Fear had been living in Ryn's chest ever since, a tenant who never paid rent and rearranged the furniture at night.

*I'm just going to look,* Ryn told the ceiling. *One more time.*

The lie was getting threadbare. Even Ryn didn't believe it anymore.

* * *

The longhouse was unlocked at night because locks were, apparently, a concept the valley had never needed. That fact alone should have stopped Ryn at the threshold. A place that doesn't lock its doors is a place that has never imagined betrayal — and Ryn was about to introduce it, personally, with a demonstration.

Inside, the archive chest sat beneath a single oil lamp someone had left burning, which felt less like negligence and more like an invitation to be seen. Ryn knelt anyway. The cedar smell rose up, old and sweet, and inside, wrapped in oiled cloth, lay the maps. Her father's maps — Nakamura's — at the top, his precise handwriting marching across the margins like ants that knew where they were going.

Ryn took only three sheets. The western passages. Enough to prove value, not enough to gut the place. A thief with a conscience, which is to say a thief who would be caught, because thieves with consciences hesitate, and hesitation is loud.

"Put them down."

Ryn's whole body went cold. They turned, slowly, the way you turn when you already know what you'll see.

Tamsin stood in the doorway with a bow in her hands, unstrung but present, and behind her two of the watchers — Bren and the silent one everyone called Otter — flanked her like punctuation. Tamsin's face did something complicated: hurt first, then fury arriving to cover it, then hurt again underneath.

"The lamp," she said. "We leave the lamp lit so anyone coming in honest can see what they're doing. So they can stop and think." Her voice cracked on *think*. "You looked right at it."

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and Ryn saw it was shaking. "Don't say my name like we're friends. Friends don't—" She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice was flat and official, the voice of the council speaking through her mouth. "You'll come to the elders' circle at dawn. Take nothing else. Walk ahead of us."

Walking ahead of your captors, Ryn discovered, is a strange kind of escort. The three of them followed close enough that Ryn could hear Bren's breathing, and the whole way across the frozen-edgeless ground — no frost here, never frost, the springs kept the valley soft — Ryn catalogued everything they were about to lose. The ugly wooden bird. The bread that tasted of something other than ration packs. Lena's terrible jokes, delivered with the timing of a rockslide.

The gorge. They were going to put Ryn out into the gorge alone, the way the valley had done once before to a man named Nakamura, and the gorge kept its own, Sela had said, and look how well that had gone for everyone.

Dawn couldn't come fast enough and also should never come at all.

* * *

The elders' circle met in the open air, because Ivo Marsh believed judgments rendered indoors were judgments hiding from the sky. There were seven elders, and Ivo sat among them rather than above them, though everyone knew whose word bent the rest.

Ryn stood in the center with the three stolen maps laid at their feet like evidence at a trial, which was exactly what they were.

"You were starving in a cave," Ivo said. Not unkindly. That was the worst part — Ivo's voice stayed level and warm while he dismantled you. "You were found half-dead, and we fed you. You were afraid, and we housed you. Tell me what fear bought you tonight."

"A way out," Ryn said. Their voice came out smaller than intended. "Vane offered amnesty. For cooperation. I thought — I thought if I brought him something valuable enough, he'd let me go home. He'd stop looking for me. He'd stop—" They gestured vaguely upward, toward the world beyond the walls, where helicopters existed and ministries wrote reports. "He'd stop being inevitable."

"And instead," Ivo said, "you made us inevitable too. Now we know our western passages are worth selling. Now we know our guests calculate prices." He looked around the circle. "The precedent is old. We have only ever had one answer for this."

"Exile," said Elder Wren, an ancient woman whose voice sounded like wind through wire.

"Into the gorge," said another. "Alone. As was done before."

Ryn's knees went interesting. Somewhere very far away, a spring gurgled cheerfully, the valley's idea of background music for a sentencing.

"That was different," said a voice from the edge of the circle.

Lena Nakamura stepped forward, and Ryn could have kissed her, except that her expression suggested she might strangle them first, and honestly both options felt deserved.

"Ivo," she said. "Thirty years ago you exiled my father for keeping half a promise. And what did it get you? It got you a dead man's name and a daughter standing here asking you to do it again to someone else." She crouched, picked up one of the stolen maps, and held it up. "Look at what Ryn took. Three sheets. The western passages — the ones farthest from the entrance, the ones Vane would take months to use. Ryn wasn't trying to destroy you. Ryn was trying to buy a ticket out of a nightmare." She straightened. "I've been in that nightmare. Seven years of it. I know exactly what exile costs, because I paid it, and I'm telling you — the person you send out that wall doesn't come back as a warning to others. They come back as a story about you."

"They betrayed us," Wren said.

"Yes," Lena agreed, without hesitation, and Ryn flinched. "Completely. Cowardly. Stupidly. All true. And here's what else is true: six weeks ago this person watched their team leader drown himself rather than give up your location, and they kept your secret through starvation and terror and grief, alone in a cave, for eleven days. People are not one thing. If we exile everyone who breaks, we will eventually run out of people, and then the valley will be perfectly safe and perfectly empty." She turned to face Ryn, and her eyes were furious and tired and, underneath it, something that looked painfully like recognition. "I'm not arguing mercy because Ryn deserves it. I'm arguing it because somebody argued it for me, once, when I hadn't earned it yet. That's the whole trick of it. Mercy isn't a reward. It's a bet."

Silence. The springs gurgled. Otter, the silent watcher, made a sound that might have been a laugh or a cough.

Ivo studied Lena for a long moment. Then Ryn. Then the maps.

"Fear is honest," he said finally. "I trust it more than loyalty, which lies beautifully. But a bet, then." He addressed Ryn directly. "Nakamura stakes her standing on yours. If you betray this valley again, she shares your sentence. Do you understand what you're holding?"

Ryn looked at Lena. Lena looked back with the expression of someone who had just handed over the only thing she had left and dared the universe to make it matter.

"Yeah," Ryn said, and their voice broke, and they let it. "Yeah, I understand."

* * *

Afterward, Tamsin found Ryn sitting on the aqueduct wall, doing the breathing exercise Lena had taught them — four counts in, hold, six counts out — badly.

"So," Tamsin said, dropping down beside them. She still had the bow slung across her back, which Ryn chose to interpret as closure rather than threat. "You were going to sell us out."

"I was going to try to sell you out. I hadn't gotten past the kneeling part. Turns out I'm bad at crime."

"Terrible," Tamsin agreed. "You hesitated at the lamp. Everyone knows you commit to the lamp." She was quiet a moment, picking at the wood of the wall. "I made you a bird."

"It's a great bird."

"It's a terrible bird and you know it." She bumped her shoulder against theirs, gently, the way you test whether ice will hold. "Lena vouched for you. Do you know what that cost her? Those elders were ready to send her home with nothing. She spent her only coin on you."

Ryn stared out at the terraced gardens, the smoke rising from the bake-house, the whole improbable green impossibility of the place. "Why would she do that?"

"Because she knows what the gorge does to people," Tamsin said simply. "Because somebody once bet on her." She stood, brushed off her trousers, and paused. "Also because she needs you. She won't say it, so I'm saying it for her. Scouts came in an hour ago from the lower watch. Vane's crews are in the gorge. Drilling equipment, blasting charges, moving fast. Lena's putting together a party to go downriver tonight and beat them to the dam site." Tamsin looked down at Ryn, and her voice softened by exactly one degree. "She asked for you. By name. So — are you coming, or are you going to keep failing at crime?"

Ryn looked at the ugly wooden bird waiting on the shelf back in the hut, at the valley glowing faintly with borrowed heat, at the woman across the compound already checking rope coils like the night was a problem she intended to solve personally.

"Get me a pack," Ryn said, standing. "Apparently I'm better at redemption than burglary."

Tamsin grinned. "Everyone is. It's easier."
