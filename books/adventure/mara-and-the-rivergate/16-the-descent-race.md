## 16. The Descent Race

The news came up the valley on the wings of a bird that had no business carrying it.

One of the scouts — a wiry young man named Pell who could read weather the way other people read menus — came sprinting through the terraced fields at dusk with a dead pigeon in his fist and an expression like a man who'd swallowed something that fought back.

"Ministry band," he panted, holding it out to Ivo's daughter as though she might want to admire it. "Leg band. Message capsule. It went down in the spruce stand above the hot falls."

Tamsin Marsh cracked the capsule open with her thumbnail, unrolled the slip of paper inside, and read it twice. Then she read it a third time, slower, and Lena watched the color leave her face by degrees, like water draining out of a lock.

"What is it?" Lena asked, though some part of her already knew. Some part of her had known since Vane's name first entered the valley's vocabulary, the way you know a storm is coming from the ache in an old break.

Tamsin handed her the paper without a word.

It was written in surveyor's shorthand — Lena recognized the notation system instantly, because she'd grown up doodling it in the margins of her schoolbooks while her father drilled her on contour intervals. *Crews mobilized. Gorge entry via north rim haul road. Demolition equipment, two rigs, charge station at narrows. Timeline accelerated. Seal passage before week's end.*

Below that, in a different hand, one line that made Lena's stomach drop through the floor of the world:

*Subject N. presumed in valley. Treat accordingly.*

"Treat accordingly," Tamsin repeated flatly. "That's bureaucratic for *shoot on sight*, isn't it."

"It's bureaucratic for whatever the person holding the paper wants it to mean," Lena said. "Which is worse."

Ivo Marsh stood at the edge of the firelight, his hands folded behind his back, his face doing what it always did — revealing nothing, weighing everything. The elders had spent three days deliberating about her, and then three more about the map, and now the deliberation was over because the world had stopped waiting for committees.

"They'll dam the narrows first," Ivo said quietly. "Flood the lower gorge. Drown the passage behind the falls. By the time anyone downstream understands what happened, there will be nothing left to prove anything." He turned to Lena. "You said your father's warning data would stop them."

"My father's warning data is forty pages of field notes in a language only three people alive can read fluently," Lena said. "And one of them is standing right here." She tapped her own chest. "But notes don't stop drills. Notes don't stop charges. The only thing that stops men like Vane is being *seen*. Witnesses. Radios. Names attached to faces attached to the truth, all of it downstream of him before he can bury it."

"So we go down," Tamsin said.

It wasn't a question, but everyone treated it like one anyway, which told Lena everything about how the Marsh family conducted arguments.

"The descent takes four days in summer," Pell said. "Five if the cataract is running high."

"And it's winter," Tamsin said.

"And it's winter," Pell agreed, with the cheerful fatalism of a man who had clearly never once been talked out of anything.

Lena looked around the fire. Ryn sat hunched at its edge, still too thin, still flinching at loud noises, but present — present in a way they hadn't been a week ago. The two scouts, Pell and a quiet woman named Odile who hadn't spoken more than ten words in Lena's hearing but who moved through the valley's terrain like water finding its level. And Tamsin, who had inherited her father's stillness and none of his patience.

"I can't ask any of you to do this," Lena said. "If Vane's people catch us, the valley stays secret. That's the whole point of you staying behind. If I'm caught alone—"

"You'll be shot," Tamsin said, "and your father's map will burn in your pack, and we'll have lost the only person who can translate it under pressure. No. You don't get to be noble alone. It doesn't work. Believe me, my father has tried."

Something in Lena's chest loosened and hurt at the same time. Seven years of going everywhere alone, of measuring her life in solo miles, and here was a stranger telling her, in front of witnesses, that solitary nobility was simply incompetence with better lighting.

"All right," she said. "Then we do it my way. We travel light, we travel dark, and we don't touch the river trail below the second bend — Vane will have spotters on every vantage. We take the ledge route."

Pell made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a prayer. "The ledge route hasn't been run in twenty years."

"Good," Lena said. "Then there won't be any footprints for them to find."

* * *

They left at moonset, five figures moving down through the terraces into the throat of the valley, where the Kestrel gathered itself from its hundred warm springs and committed, at last, to becoming a river.

Lena carried her father's field notes in a waterproof sleeve against her spine, where they rode like a second heartbeat. She'd copied the critical pages onto her body in grease pencil — the flood calculations, the gauge readings, the numbers that proved the gorge couldn't hold a reservoir without sending a wall of water into Ferrow's Landing — because packs got lost, but skin stayed with you until it didn't.

Ryn walked beside her for the first hour without speaking. Then, in the blue dark: "I've been down this stretch once. With the team. Before." A pause. "Before Aldous."

"You don't have to come."

"Yes, I do." Ryn's voice was steady in a way Lena hadn't heard yet. "I watched him destroy our maps. I watched Marisol walk into the river. I hid in a cave and ate lichen for eleven days and told myself surviving was enough." They kicked a stone off the trail, watched it clatter away into blackness. "Surviving isn't enough. I want to be there when he sees my face."

"That's the healthiest reason for revenge I've ever heard," Lena said.

"It's not revenge. It's testimony." Ryn considered. "Though I wouldn't cry if it were also revenge."

The ledge route was exactly as bad as advertised. It traversed the canyon wall a hundred feet above the churning water, a shelf of fractured limestone rarely wider than a boot and occasionally narrower than a promise. In summer it was exposed to sun-softened rock that crumbled under weight. In winter it was glazed with ice, which meant every step was a negotiation and every negotiation could end in the river.

Odile led. She carried no rope, used almost no hands, and moved with such economy that Lena, who had spent seven years earning her bread on rock, felt like a toddler watching someone parallel park. Twice Odile stopped, tested a hold, found it wanting, and silently rerouted the entire line thirty feet up a face that Lena would have called unclimbable in daylight with a rack of gear and a good mood.

"She does that," Tamsin murmured, catching Lena's expression. "Don't take it personally. She once rerouted my wedding procession around a wasp nest. My husband thought it was symbolic."

By the third hour Lena had stopped thinking in words. There was only the next hold, the next step, the ice singing faintly under her crampons, the river roaring somewhere below like a crowd that had been waiting a long time for the show to start. Her father's voice lived in her head on terrain like this — *the mountain tells you what it wants, Lena-girl; your job is just to listen without arguing* — and for the first time in years the voice sounded less like a ghost and more like company.

They made the second bend by gray dawn and cached their climbing gear in a hollow beneath an overhang, because from here down the route dropped into the gorge proper, and speed mattered more than safety now. Speed always cost something. Lena had learned that equation young and never once gotten a discount.

* * *

They slept four hours in a scree cave that smelled of fox, ate cold, and moved again. The second day was the river trail, fast and exposed, and they covered distance that should have taken a day and a half in nine hours, because fear is an excellent pace-setter.

It was on the second evening, at the top of the cataract, that the mountain presented its bill.

Lena heard it before she saw it — a change in the river's voice, from roar to something deeper, a subsonic complaint that she felt in her molars. She held up a fist and the column stopped. Below them the gorge pinched to a slot barely six meters wide, and the entire Kestrel — swollen with winter melt from the warm springs, carrying more water than it had any right to carry — funneled into that gap and fell.

Not a waterfall. A cataract. Forty feet of vertical chaos, the whole river compressed into a single fist of white, and below it a recirculating hydraulic that ate anything that entered and kept it, turning it over and over like a dog worrying a bone.

"There's a portage trail," Pell said. "Right side. The valley runners used it for the fur trade."

Lena found it. Or rather, she found where it had been. Twenty years of freeze-thaw had peeled most of it off the cliff face, and what remained was a sloping ramp of ice-slicked slab angling down toward the pool at the base of the falls — a pool that fed directly into the hydraulic.

"We rope up," she said. "Fixed line, one at a time, everyone else anchored behind the lip. If anyone goes in the water—" She stopped. Everyone knew what happened if anyone went in the water. The hydraulic didn't negotiate.

Odile went first, because of course she did, flowing down the ramp like she was descending stairs in her own house. Then Tamsin. Then Ryn, who paused at the lip, looked down at the falling river, and said, very quietly, "Marisol went in at a place like this."

"I know," Lena said.

"She didn't hesitate. She just — stepped. Like it was a door."

Lena put her hand on Ryn's shoulder. "This isn't a door. This is a ramp, and you're roped, and I'm on belay, and I have not lost anyone on my rope in seven years and I am not starting tonight. Go."

Ryn went. Lena paid out the line, watching every meter, feeling the rope's small live tremors the way you feel a pulse. Ryn reached the bottom, stumbled, caught themselves on Odile's arm, and gave a thumbs-up that shook only slightly.

Pell descended. Then Lena herself, last, coiling the rope as she came, and it was halfway down the ramp — forty feet of ice-glazed slab with the cataract thundering beside her, spray freezing on her sleeves, her world reduced to the width of her boots — that the mountain collected its fee.

The slab let go.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. No crack, no warning groan. Just a section of ramp the size of a door peeling away beneath her left foot with the soft, apologetic sigh of stone that had been waiting decades for an excuse. Lena's boot went with it. Her weight swung onto the rope, her right crampon skated across glassy ice and found nothing, and suddenly she was falling — not free-falling, the rope caught her within two meters, but penduluming out over the pool with the cataract's spray hitting her like thrown gravel and the hydraulic churning below, close enough to feel its breath, cold and enormous and patient.

She stopped swinging. She hung. The rope hummed above her, anchored somewhere up the slope, and below her boots the white water rolled and rolled and rolled.

*Lena-girl,* her father said, in the calm voice he'd used when she was nine and stuck on a pitch above the lake, *stop looking at the fall. Look at the rock.*

She looked at the rock. There wasn't any. There was the rope, and there was the anchor sixty feet up, and between them a sheer slab with one seam running diagonally, thin as a pencil line.

"Lena!" Tamsin's voice, shredded by the roar. "Are you hit?"

"Not yet!" she shouted back, and began to climb.

It took eleven minutes. She counted them, because counting was a thing her hands and feet could do while the rest of her screamed. Prusik up the rope to the seam, jam a tool into the pencil-line crack, weight it, weight it again, kick in, move, prusik, jam, kick, move. The spray iced her lashes shut. Her forearms burned so badly by minute eight that she genuinely considered, for one lucid half-second, letting go — and then she thought of forty pages of field notes riding against her spine, and of Sela waiting at her ferry with the river running past her pilings, and of a valley full of children who had done nothing wrong except be born behind a waterfall, and she swore at her father's ghost and climbed another ten feet.

Her hand broke over the lip of solid ground and Tamsin's closed around her wrist and hauled, and Lena lay on frozen gravel gasping like a landed fish while four people hovered over her wearing identical expressions of controlled panic.

"Your rope anchor held," Odile said. It was the longest sentence Lena had ever heard her produce, and it was, Lena would later realize, a compliment of the highest order.

"The slab didn't," Lena managed.

"No." Odile looked down the ramp, at the gap where the world had removed a piece of itself. "Nothing will hold that route now. We're committed. Downriver or nothing."

Downriver or nothing. Lena lay on her back and laughed — a ragged, breathless, slightly unhinged laugh that she couldn't stop, that tasted of ice and relief and the specific giddiness of having briefly died and changed her mind. Tamsin stared at her. Ryn started laughing too, helplessly, and then Pell, and even Odile's mouth did something at one corner that on another face would have been called a smile.

"Every mile," Lena said, sitting up, wiping her eyes with a mitt that left her face dirtier than before, "is bought with pain. My father wrote that. I used to think it was grim." She flexed her fingers, watching them shake, watching them steady. "It's not grim. It's just honest accounting."

* * *

They moved through the third night without stopping, because the fourth day belonged to Vane unless they stole it back.

The gorge changed character as they dropped — wider now, the walls stepping back, old-growth cedar crowding the banks, the river spreading and deepening and slowing as if gathering itself for the long run to Ferrow's Landing. Twice they saw lights on the north rim: headlamps, moving in disciplined lines, and once the distant percussive stutter of a generator. Vane's crews were in the gorge. Hauling drill steel down a haul road that hadn't existed when Lena was born, building a wound into the canyon that would never close.

At midnight they crossed the haul road itself.

It cut along the eastern bank, graded and graveled, absurdly new, and they crouched in the cedar shadow and watched a supply truck grind past with its lights hooded, its bed loaded with crates stenciled with the ministry crest. Lena counted crates. Charge crates. Enough shaped explosive to bring down the narrows and seal the passage behind the falls forever — and, if her father's numbers were right, enough misdirected water in the failure year to erase Ferrow's Landing from the map.

"He really believes he's the reasonable one," Ryn whispered.

"They always do," Lena said. "That's what makes them dangerous. Men who know they're villains are careful. Men who think they're saving everyone—" She watched the truck's taillights shrink downstream. "Men like that will drown a town and call it progress."

Tamsin touched her arm. "How far to the narrows?"

Lena consulted the map in her head — her father's map, the real one, the one drawn in ink and apology. "Six hours, if the trail holds. Four, if we're stupid."

"And if we're smart?"

"Smart gets there after the charges are set." Lena stood, rolled her shoulders, felt every one of the day's miles settle into her bones like ballast. "So we're going to be stupid. Quickly."

They went. Downriver or nothing.
