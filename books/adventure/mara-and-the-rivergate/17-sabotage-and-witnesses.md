## 17. Sabotage and Witnesses

Tamsin Marsh had grown up believing the world ended at the canyon wall. Her father had taught her everything else — how to read water, how to hold a bow, how to sit still for six hours in the reeds waiting for a goat that never came — but the world beyond the Rivergate existed for her mostly as rumor, delivered in the voices of traders who came up the warm channel twice a year and left smelling of smoke and lies.

Now she crouched on a granite shelf above the Kestrel's upper gorge, looking down at a worksite that smelled of diesel and ambition, and thought: *so this is what a lie looks like when it hires people.*

There were forty of them, maybe fifty. Canvas tents in neat military rows. A drilling rig the size of a barn, its mast lit by floodlights even though the sun was still up. Cable spools. A crane barge moored against the far bank. And everywhere, the noise — the grinding, hammering, shouting noise of men building something that, if it were finished, would put the valley and everything in it under sixty feet of cold water.

"They're ahead of schedule," Lena said beside her. She'd been still for so long that Tamsin had twice checked she was breathing. "Vane told the ministry spring. He's already pouring foundations."

"Can we stop it?" Ryn whispered from Tamsin's other side. Ryn had not stopped shivering since the cataract, two nights ago, and Tamsin had privately decided not to mention it, because mentioning it would only make it worse. Some shivers were load-bearing.

Lena studied the rig for a long moment. "We can stop the rig. The question is what happens after."

"That's the kind of question my father asks," Tamsin said, "right before everyone starts arguing."

"Then I'll fit right in."

* * *

They went down in the last hour of light, when the work crews rotated and the floodlights hadn't quite taken over. Lena led, because Lena always led now — there was something about the way she moved through broken ground that Tamsin had decided to steal and make her own, a kind of unhurried certainty, as if the mountain had already agreed to whatever she was going to do next. The two valley scouts, Pell and Odo, flanked them, and Ryn brought up the rear looking, in Tamsin's opinion, like a man being escorted to his own funeral by people he'd only recently met.

The rig sat at the mouth of the old survey tunnel — the one her father's maps called the Diversion, the one Vane meant to use to strangle the river. Two workers stood at its base, drinking from thermoses, arguing about a card game with the passion of men who had not yet been told their employer was planning a catastrophe.

Lena took them from behind with the efficiency of someone who had done this before and wished she hadn't. One moment the workers were arguing about whether a seven of hearts beat a jack in some game Tamsin had never heard of; the next they were face-down in the gravel with zip ties on their wrists and Lena crouched in front of them, saying, in a voice that was almost kind, "Nobody's going to hurt you. I need you to listen."

Tamsin went for the rig itself. Odo had drilled her on this for two days — *cut the hydraulic lines low, pull the governor linkage, and for the love of the river do not touch anything red* — and she did it with hands that only shook a little. The great machine sighed and slumped, its mast tilting like a tired animal settling down to sleep. Somewhere inside it, something valuable made a sad, expensive noise.

"Was that supposed to happen?" she called.

"No," Odo called back. "But it's fine. Probably."

Pell had the radio shack — a corrugated shed full of equipment that hummed like a hive. He came out holding a transmitter unit the size of a loaf of bread, and Lena took it from him with a strange expression, half hunger and half grief.

"This is a field broadcast set," she said. "Ministry issue. Same model I used to carry."

"You used to work for them," Ryn said. It wasn't a question. It was also, Tamsin noticed, not entirely friendly.

"I used to be useful to them," Lena said. "It's different." She turned the unit over in her hands. "My father's notes are in here." She tapped her jacket, where a waterproof roll rode against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "The flood calculations. The downstream projections. Every number Vane buried."

"And you're going to say them out loud," Tamsin said.

"I'm going to *broadcast* them." Lena almost smiled. It wasn't a happy smile. It was the smile of someone who had finally found the right tool after seven years of using the wrong ones. "Every radio on this site, and every radio Vane's people carry downstream. He wanted the data erased. So we'll do the opposite of erasing."

* * *

The crew chief's name was Harlan Voss, and they found him in the command tent doing what Tamsin would later describe, generously, as accounting — a ledger open, a bottle of something amber at his elbow, his boots up on a crate marked EXPLOSIVES, CLASS 1.2 in stenciled letters that made Pell's eye twitch.

Voss was fifty-ish, built like a door, with a gray mustache and the particular weathering of a man who had spent thirty years outdoors on other people's projects. When Lena stepped into the tent, he looked up, and Tamsin watched his face do three things in quick succession: confusion, recognition, and then a kind of weary relief, as if he'd been waiting all evening for an interruption and was grateful it had finally arrived.

"Lena Nakamura," he said. "They've got your face on a bulletin down at Ferrow's Landing. 'Armed and possibly unstable.' I'd have said the artist flattered you."

"They usually do." Lena set the transmitter on his desk, right on top of the ledger. "Chief Voss. I need about ten minutes of your time, and then you're going to have to make a decision."

"I don't make decisions. I make schedules." Voss didn't reach for the sidearm Tamsin could see under his maps, which Tamsin appreciated. "Who are these people? The kid's been crying."

"I have not," Ryn said, in a voice that suggested he had, recently, and knew it.

"They're witnesses," Lena said. "So am I. Voss, do you know what this site is actually for?"

"Diversion tunnel, headpond, low-profile dam. Ministry contract, phase two of the Kestrel regulation project. Flood control, power generation, irrigation for the lowland farms." He recited it the way a man recites grace before a meal — automatically, and without much appetite. "I know my contract."

"Do you know what's behind the canyon wall?"

Voss's mustache moved. "Behind the — there's nothing behind the canyon wall. Rock. I've cored it. Forty meters of granite, then more granite. The ministry's own surveys—"

"Are wrong." Lena pulled the waterproof roll from her jacket and unrolled it across his desk, on top of everything else, the way her father's maps had a way of ending up on top of everything else. "These are the ministry's own surveys. The ones that were never filed. Taken by a team that went in last autumn. One of them is sitting behind you, and he can tell you what happened to the rest of them."

Ryn cleared his throat. It was not a commanding sound. "The team lead destroyed the official maps," he said. "He said — he said some maps are confessions. Then he walked into the river. I watched him do it. I've been hiding in a cave for four months eating fish I caught with my hands, which, for the record, is not something I recommend to anyone."

Voss looked at him. Then at the maps. Then at Lena, and something in his face shifted — the look of a man who had spent a career following schedules and had just been handed a document that wasn't on any of them.

"What am I looking at?"

"Water volumes." Lena's voice changed — flattened, professional, the voice of a guide briefing a client on the one thing that would kill them. "Your headpond sits on a geothermal channel system. It's not a reservoir site, Voss, it's a kettle with a lid on it. The canyon can't hold a regulated pool — the pressure finds the fractures, the fractures widen, and when it lets go, it doesn't let go politely. My father ran the numbers thirty years ago. Your own survey team confirmed them last autumn. If this dam fills to design capacity, the failure wave hits Ferrow's Landing in under two hours. Two hours, at night, with no warning, in a town of nine hundred people."

The tent was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a generator throbbed. Tamsin found she was holding her own hands to keep them still.

Voss looked down at the numbers for a long time. He was not a stupid man — Tamsin could see that now, see it in the way he read, top to bottom, checking the arithmetic the way he probably checked a load calculation. When he looked up, his mustache had gone very still.

"I was told the geothermal data was anomalous," he said. "Instrument error. That's why the second survey team went in — to re-verify."

"And then the second team vanished, and instead of asking what they found, the ministry sent a bulletin with my face on it."

"The ministry sent *me*," Voss said, "with a completion deadline and a bonus clause." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Nakamura. I've been building things for thirty years. Bridges, weirs, two hydro plants. I have never —" He stopped. Started again. "If what you're saying is true, then every man on this site is standing on the trigger of a flood that kills a town. So my question is not whether you're right. My question is: why should I believe you, and not the ministry?"

It was, Tamsin thought, the first honest question anyone had asked since they'd left the valley.

"Because I'm the one showing you the math," Lena said, "and the ministry is the one who told you the people who found it disappeared."

* * *

Lena used the transmitter at full dark.

She sat on an equipment crate outside the command tent with the broadcast unit in her lap and her father's notes open on her knees, and she read the data into the radio in a clear, unhurried voice — volumes, pressures, failure projections, downstream arrival times, all of it, twice, the way you'd read it to someone you loved and needed to understand. Tamsin stood in the shadow of the rig and watched the worksite listen.

They came out of their tents one by one, holding their hand radios the way people hold things they suddenly aren't sure of. Fifty men and a dozen women, in hard hats and wool coats, standing in the floodlights with their faces tilted toward a voice coming out of a little box. Nobody spoke. The only sound was the river, running cold and fast and free below them, and Lena's voice, steady as a contour line.

Tamsin thought about her father, up in the valley, sitting by the stove while the elders argued about what to do with the daughter of the man who had broken his promise. Ivo Marsh believed in gates. He believed the world outside was a flood of a different kind, and that the only safety was a wall and a closed door. And here was his daughter, standing in the middle of the outside, watching a woman throw the door wide open on purpose.

*Maybe,* Tamsin thought, *that's the difference between a wall and a voice. A wall keeps people out. A voice lets them decide.*

When Lena finished, she set the unit down and said, into the sudden silence, "That's everything. Every number. You can check them yourselves — the core samples in your own storage tents match. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to trust the river, because it's the one thing on this site that doesn't work for the ministry."

The silence held for three heartbeats. Then it broke — and it broke in half, which was the thing Tamsin would remember afterward, the thing she would carry home and say aloud in the council lodge until her father finally heard it.

Half the crews began packing. Quietly, deliberately, men rolling bedrolls and loading trucks, a crane operator climbing down from his cab and walking off the site without a word to anyone. Voss stood in the middle of it with his radio in his hand and did not stop them.

The other half clustered around the site's second transmitter, and their radios crackled with a different conversation — short, urgent, frightened. Tamsin caught fragments on the scout's monitoring set. *—situation at the upper site, repeat, unauthorized persons—* *—she's here, the Nakamura woman, she's broadcasting—* *—requesting instructions, requesting instructions—*

And then, cutting through the static, a voice Tamsin had never heard but knew instantly, the way you know a snake by its sound rather than its shape. Smooth. Reasonable. Almost gentle.

*"This is Aldous Vane. All personnel, hold your positions. The individual broadcasting is a fugitive with a documented history of sabotage. The data she is reading is fabricated. Do not engage. Ministry response teams are en route. Hold your positions."*

Ryn made a small sound. "He's coming."

"He was already coming," Lena said. She stood up and rolled the notes closed, and Tamsin saw her hand shake — just once, just a little, and then steady itself. "We heard the barge engines an hour ago. He didn't wait for our permission and he isn't waiting for theirs." She looked across the worksite, at the crews splitting like a river around a stone, at Voss standing in the lights with a decision on his face, at the dark mouth of the diversion tunnel where the demolition charges sat in their crates, waiting to be told what to do.

"Voss," Lena said. "Where does Vane keep his detonator controls?"

Voss looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the tunnel, and Tamsin watched thirty years of schedules die quietly behind his eyes.

"The charge station," he said. "At the tunnel head. And Nakamura — his response teams are maybe two hours out. Less, if he pushed them."

Lena was already moving.
