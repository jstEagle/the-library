## 18. Vane's Reckoning

Aldous Vane had always believed that a man could be measured by how he behaved in the last ten minutes of a plan. Most men, he'd found, spent those minutes badly — shouting, praying, blaming. Vane intended to spend his checking figures.

The problem was that the figures had stopped cooperating.

He stood at the field command post above the upper gorge, a canvas tent that smelled of diesel and wet wool, and watched the radio operator's face do something a ministry-trained man's face should never do: waver.

"Say again, Crew Four," the operator said into his handset.

Static. Then a voice Vane recognized as Okafor's crew chief — Brandt, a solid man, a man who had once held a drill line for eleven hours in freezing spray without complaint — saying words that made no sense at all.

"—saying we're standing down. The flood data checks out. I've got a cartographer's seal on these numbers older than my career, and if she's right, your diversion wall puts forty feet of water over Ferrow's Landing inside—"

"Brandt." Vane crossed the tent in four strides and took the handset himself. He kept his voice pleasant, the voice he used for donors and deputy ministers. "This is Aldous Vane. You are to disregard any documents presented to you by unauthorized personnel. That is an order."

A pause on the line, long enough that Vane could hear wind moving through it, and beneath the wind, faintly, the river — which had been making noise all week but which he was only now beginning to actually hear.

"Mr. Vane," Brandt said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse than insubordination, "there's a woman down here who read us her father's survey notes by lantern light while my best driller cried into his coffee. I've got nineteen men asking me why nobody told them about Ferrow's Landing. Sir, what *should* I tell them?"

Vane looked at the handset for a moment. Then he set it down gently, as one sets down a thing that has bitten him.

* * *

Here is what Vane understood about himself, and had understood since he was a boy arranging his toy soldiers into defensible positions on the nursery floor: he was not a cruel man. He wanted to be clear about that, even now, even alone. Cruelty was inefficient. Cruelty left marks, and marks invited inquiries, and inquiries were how careers died. What Vane practiced was something closer to tidiness. The world produced inconvenient things — valleys, witnesses, old maps in dead men's handwriting — and someone had to tidy them away, or nothing would ever be built. Dams would never rise. Rivers would never be useful. Civilization, in Vane's experience, was mostly the art of deciding which small truths could be drowned without anyone downstream noticing.

For thirty years he had been very good at deciding.

And then, in the space of nine days, a disgraced mountain guide had walked into his gorge, found his buried survey team, uncovered his buried valley, and broadcast his buried arithmetic to every radio within forty miles of the Kestrel.

It wasn't the data that stung, though the data was bad enough. It was the *manner* of it. She hadn't shouted. She hadn't accused. According to Brandt's mutinous report, she had simply sat the crews down, poured coffee from their own thermos, and read aloud — in a level, tired voice — the flood projections her father had calculated three decades ago in a notebook with a water-stained cover. Forty feet over Ferrow's Landing. Eleven hundred residents. A ferry crossing that had run since before the ministry existed.

"She thanked us for our time," Brandt had said, in a tone of profound bewilderment. "At the end. Like we were the ones doing her a favor."

Vane had spent the last hour trying to locate the flaw in that performance and had failed, which frightened him more than anything else this week. He was a man who found flaws professionally. If Lena Nakamura had no visible seam, it meant she was not performing at all. It meant she believed it.

Which meant the numbers were probably real.

Which meant everything after this point was arithmetic of a different kind.

* * *

He stepped out of the tent into the grey afternoon. Below him, the worksite clung to the gorge wall like a barnacle colony: scaffolding, generators, the great idle drilling rig with its bit still lodged in rock it would never be permitted to finish biting. Half the crews had already downed tools. He could see them gathered near the cable landing, passing around photocopies — actual paper, God help him, paper traveled, paper couldn't be recalled the way a signal could — and he watched one young worker look up at the command post with an expression Vane had seen only twice before in his career, both times in courtrooms.

Above the worksite, wedged into a shelf of granite near the old survey tunnel mouth, sat the charge station: a steel cabinet, a control panel, and the diversion array — fourteen emplacement charges designed, in the original engineering drawings, to drop a temporary cofferdam and steer the river through the tunnel while permanent works were built. Controlled. Measured. A tool, like any other.

In its current configuration, with the tunnel unlined and the cofferdam incomplete, triggering the array early would produce something else entirely: a surge. Not the catastrophic forty-foot wall — the geometry wouldn't allow it — but a violent, churning pulse of water through the lower gorge. Enough to scour the worksite clean. Enough to take the scaffolding, the rig, the records tent, the photocopied papers, the radios, and everyone slow enough to still be standing in the channel when it came.

Enough, Vane noted with the detached precision he prided himself on, to bury every piece of evidence in forty miles of canyon silt, and to make the death of any remaining witnesses indistinguishable from an industrial accident.

His hands were steady. He noticed this and approved of it. Whatever else happened in the next hour, his hands would be steady, and when the inquiry came — and there would be an inquiry; there was always an inquiry — he would explain, truthfully, that he had acted to prevent a greater disaster. That the array had been unstable. That he had ordered an emergency controlled detonation to relieve pressure on the incomplete works. There would be questions about timing. There were always questions about timing. But dead gorges kept no minutes, and drowned records signed no statements.

The mathematics were ugly. Vane did not flinch from ugly mathematics; that was what separated men who built things from men who wrote letters about building things.

He was halfway to the switchback trail when the thought arrived, uninvited, in his father's voice — a schoolmaster's voice, thirty years dead: *Aldous, a sum done quickly is still wrong.*

He climbed faster.

* * *

The charge station door stuck, as it had stuck all week, and for one absurd moment Vane stood wrestling with a steel handle in the wind while the entire architecture of his life waited behind it. Then it gave, and he was inside, and the familiar smell of the place — machine oil, cold metal, the ozone ghost of the generator — steadied him the way ledgers steadied other men.

The panel was old ministry stock, built the year he'd joined, and he knew it the way he knew his own signature. Master arm. Array select. Timer. He set the timer first — six minutes, because less would look deliberate and more might allow interference — and his finger hovered over the arm switch.

Through the station's single grimy window, he saw movement on the trail below.

Not the workers. Workers moved in groups, in the shuffling way of men whose day had been cancelled. This was a single figure coming up the switchbacks at a pace Vane recognized from personnel files and seven-year-old incident reports: fast, economical, infuriatingly unhurried, the walk of a woman who had been measuring mountains since before Vane had learned to fake sincerity at parties.

Behind her, smaller figures — two of them, one carrying rope, one — was that the Marsh girl? It was. Ivo Marsh's daughter, whom the file photographs had never quite captured, because file photographs did not capture people who looked at you as though you were a contour line they intended to redraw.

"No," Vane said aloud, to the window, to the gorge, to the general unfairness of the world. "No, you are *late*. You are precisely eight hours late. You were meant to be negotiating with the elders while I finished this."

The universe declined to apologize.

He threw the master arm. The panel woke with a hum he felt in his teeth. Somewhere below, buried in the rock along the tunnel line, fourteen charges began listening for instruction. The countdown display lit: 05:40. 05:39.

And then he did the last thing, the thing he had told himself all the way up the trail he would not do, because it was sentimental and sentimental men died in gorges: he looked at the timer and thought about Ferrow's Landing. About Sela — he knew the name, of course he knew the name, he had a file on every soul within fifty miles of his projects — poling her ferry across the morning water. About the boy Brandt mentioned, the driller crying into his coffee, which meant the man had family there too, or had simply understood the numbers faster than his superiors, which amounted to the same indictment.

*A sum done quickly is still wrong.*

"Then let it be wrong quickly," Vane said, and reached for the array select.

* * *

The door hit the wall hard enough to rattle the panel.

She came in low, which he should have expected, water bottle in one hand and no weapon in the other, which he had not expected at all. Behind her the Marsh girl filled the doorway with a coil of climbing rope and the expression of someone who had been raised on stories about men exactly like him and had found the stories flattering.

"Step away from the console, Mr. Vane."

"Ms. Nakamura." He turned, unhurried, and gestured graciously at the room, the host to the end. "You'll forgive me if I don't offer tea. Do come in. You're just in time to watch me prevent a catastrophe."

"You're starting one."

"I'm *concluding* one." And here, God help him, he felt the old warmth rise, the genuine pleasure of explaining things to people who hadn't done the reading. "That array is sitting on an unfinished diversion system. The tunnel is unlined. The cofferdam is at sixty percent. In its current state, spring melt alone could breach the works and take out the lower gorge anyway — I have the models, I can show you the—" He caught himself, because she was looking at him the way one looks at a clock that has stopped: not angrily. Just noting the time. "I am preventing a worse flood," he said, more quietly. "You may choose to believe that."

"I read your models." She said it simply. "They were in the records tent. Your own engineers flagged the tunnel lining in March. You cut it from the budget in April."

"The budget is not your concern."

"The budget is the reason there's a countdown running right now."

04:12. The display ticked between them, patient as water. Vane became aware that his hand was still resting on the array select, and that hers was closer to the abort key than his was to anything, and that the Marsh girl had drifted — casually, terribly casually — to stand beside the generator cutoff.

"You can't stop it," Vane said. "Even if you kill power, the arm circuit holds charge for ninety seconds. I built in the redundancy myself." He smiled, and was distantly surprised to find the smile felt real. "You see? Tidy."

"Tidy," Lena Nakamura repeated. And then she did the one thing he had prepared for least: she took her hand off the abort key entirely, folded her arms, and leaned against the console as though settling in for weather. "Okay. Let's talk, then. We've got four minutes."

"We have nothing to discuss."

"Sure we do. I want to know why." Her head tilted, and there was something in her face that was not hatred, which unsettled him far more than hatred would have. Curiosity, maybe. Or recognition. "Seven years ago I got blacklisted for telling the truth about a dead climber. My father got erased for telling the truth about this valley. And you're about to drown a canyon to protect a contract. So I keep wondering — what happened to you, Mr. Vane? Because nobody starts out doing this. Somebody taught you the math where people are rounding errors. I'd like to know who."

03:20.

And Aldous Vane, who had answers for ministers and juries and mirrors, found that he had none for her. The silence stretched. Outside, the river rose against the gorge walls, patient, enormous, keeping its own counsel, and the countdown counted, and somewhere below a man named Brandt was evacuating his crews up the cable landing because a woman had bought them time with a lantern and a dead man's notebook.

02:55.

Vane's hand moved for the array select.

Lena moved faster.

* * *

What happened in the next ninety seconds would later be described, in three separate official inquiries, in ways that agreed on almost nothing except the sound — a flat metallic crash, the generator note dying mid-hum, and then the strange ringing quiet of a gorge holding its breath.

Vane would remember fragments. The Marsh girl cutting the generator feed. The emergency lights bleeding red across the panel. Nakamura's shoulder hitting him — not a blow, exactly, more like a rockfall, impersonal and total — and the two of them grappling across the console while the timer glowed 01:30, 01:29, and his fingers found the select switch and hers found his wrist, and her grip was the grip of a woman who hauled herself up ice walls for a living, which is to say it did not negotiate.

"You don't have to do this," she said through her teeth. "There's a version of tonight where you're just a bureaucrat who lost. Take it."

"There's a version," Vane gasped, "where I'm a hero. I've read it. I wrote it."

00:58.

He twisted free. His hand slammed the select. The panel chirped acceptance — array locked, sequence armed — and for half a heartbeat Aldous Vane felt the old clean satisfaction of a decision executed, the sum completed, the world tidied.

Then he saw what she had done.

Her hand came off the console and she stepped back, breathing hard, and she was smiling — a small, exhausted, terrible smile — and she said, "Check the array selection, Mr. Vane."

He looked down. The display, in its indifferent green characters, told him what he had armed. Not the diversion array. Not the fourteen charges along the tunnel line, poised to send his surge through the lower gorge.

The abandoned survey tunnel. The dead adit above the waterline, drilled years ago and sealed, leading nowhere, holding nothing, threatening no one but itself.

While they grappled — while his wrist was pinned, while the Marsh girl bled the generator — someone had re-keyed the selection. He would learn later that Tamsin Marsh had grown up in a valley full of engineers' children and could strip a ministry control panel blindfolded. Later. In the moment there was only the display, and the countdown, and the understanding arriving in him like cold water rising:

00:31.

"You can't reroute a live sequence," Vane whispered. "The interlocks—"

"Your interlocks route to whatever's selected," Lena said. "Your engineer flagged that too. March. You cut it from the budget."

00:19.

He lunged for the abort key and the Marsh girl was simply *there*, rope-wrapped arms crossed over the panel like a gate, and behind her Nakamura watched him with that same terrible gentleness, and Vane understood, in the last seconds, that he had never been fighting her at all. He had been fighting the river's memory of his father's voice, and the river had won some time ago.

00:06.

The first charge spoke — a deep, felt-more-than-heard concussion through the rock, up through his boots, into his chest.

00:02.

The gorge shook. Dust breathed out of the tunnel mouth above them like the canyon exhaling. And Aldous Vane, ministerial architect of the Kestrel project, thirty years of tidy decisions behind him, did the only thing left in his ledger: he ran — out the door, onto the shelf, into the shaking dark — toward the tunnel, toward the one place the evidence of this night might still be rewritten, his voice cracking upward into the roar:

"You can't bury me too—"

The mountain, which keeps its own, answered him.
