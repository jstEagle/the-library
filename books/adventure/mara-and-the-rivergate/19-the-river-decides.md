## 19. The River Decides

The charge station sat in a notch of the gorge wall like a rotten tooth — a prefab steel box bolted to the rock above the dam worksite, its cable umbilical running down the cliff face in a black spine. Lena saw it from two hundred meters out, lit by floodlights that turned the falling river into a curtain of blown glass.

Behind her, Tamsin crouched low against the scree, breathing hard. Ryn was somewhere below with the scouts, holding the switchyard they'd already cut dead. The radios on the workers' belts had gone quiet one by one as Lena's father's data poured through them — pressure curves, flood projections, the arithmetic of what Vane's "controlled diversion" would actually do to Ferrow's Landing. Some crews had simply put down their tools and walked. Others had run for the comms tent.

And Aldous Vane had run for the charge station.

"He's got maybe four minutes of head start," Tamsin said. Her voice was steady, but her knuckles were white around the climbing pick she'd carried the whole descent. "The countdown — you heard it on the radio?"

"I heard it." Eight minutes when it started. That had been three minutes ago.

Lena looked at the cliff face between them and the notch. It wasn't vertical — nothing in the Rivergate ever was, the rock liked to lie about itself, offering handholds that were lies and cracks that were gifts. Her father had taught her that. *The mountain is not your enemy, Lena. It's just honest in a language most people refuse to learn.*

She started climbing.

* * *

The route took eleven minutes and cost her a fingernail. She came over the lip of the notch on her forearms, hauling herself onto the steel grating of the platform, and found the door of the charge station standing open and Vane inside, both hands buried in the detonator console like a man elbow-deep in a drain.

He didn't hear her at first. The countdown display threw green light across his face: **02:47**. He'd already keyed the arming sequence. She could see the main charge line — the thick orange cable running out the floor port and down toward the diversion tunnel mouth where the demolition crew had packed four hundred kilos of emulsion explosive over the past week.

"Step away from the console, Mr. Vane."

He flinched so hard he cracked his elbow on the console frame. When he turned, his face did something complicated — surprise rearranging itself into the smooth ministerial calm he wore like a hard hat, except now it kept slipping.

"Nakamura." He said her name the way a man names a stain. "You're supposed to be—"

"Dead? Drowned? A suspect?" She stepped fully onto the platform, water hammering somewhere below them, spray drifting up cold as snowmelt. "You should really stop outsourcing your imagination to press releases."

"You don't understand what's at stake here." He straightened, smoothing his jacket, and for one absurd moment he actually looked like he was about to deliver a briefing. "This valley is an anomaly. An unregulated population, an unsurveyed hydrology, a geological hazard sitting directly upstream of a billion-crown infrastructure investment. What I'm doing is *responsible*."

"With four hundred kilos of explosive and a flood wave that would reach Ferrow's Landing in ninety minutes."

"The models are contested—"

"They're *your* models, Aldous. My father drew the real ones thirty years ago, and you've known it since before you hired me." She moved left, angling toward the console. "You didn't send me upriver to find the survey team. You sent me to find out how much they'd learned, and whether anyone downwind of the truth was still breathing."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not guilt. Calculation. His gaze dropped to her hands, then to the gap between them, then to the open gorge beyond the platform railing.

**02:19.**

"You can't disarm it," he said quietly. "Even if you knew the code. The firing circuit's sealed. I made sure of that."

"I know."

"Then this is theater."

"It's engineering," Lena said. "There's a difference. Theater needs an audience. Engineering just needs a load path."

His brow furrowed — genuinely furrowed, the first honest expression she'd seen on him — and in that half-second of confusion she lunged for the console.

* * *

They went down together.

It was not a fight in any clean sense; there were no swings, no stances, none of the choreography she'd imagined during seven years of walking. It was two people grappling over a metal box while a river tried to drown them both. Vane was stronger than he looked and more desperate than he was strong, which evened things out in ways that frightened her. His thumb found the manual override cover and slammed it shut. Her fingers found his wrist and bent it back until something crunched and he screamed a word she didn't bother to remember.

**01:52.**

She got him off the console by driving her shoulder into his sternum, and they staggered out onto the grating, and for one horrible moment the railing was all that held them above the gorge. The river roared beneath them, white and indifferent, carrying away the last of the survey records she'd fed it days ago — her father's maps, dissolving into the current, going wherever water goes.

"This is my career," Vane gasped, close enough that she could smell the coffee on his breath. "Thirty years. Do you know what happens to men like me when a project like this dies? There's no blacklist for us, Nakamura. There's just the end."

"There's a difference between the end and the bottom of a gorge," she said. "I'd recommend choosing the first one."

He chose neither. He twisted free — she'd underestimated how much fight was left in a cornered bureaucrat — and scrambled back toward the console, and she understood with terrible clarity that he wasn't going to try to stop the countdown.

He was going to trigger it early.

"No—" She caught his ankle. He kicked free. His palm came down on the manual fire key—

—and stopped, because Ryn Okafor came over the lip of the notch behind him with a climbing pick raised like a tiny, furious thunderbolt, and froze, because hitting a man with a pick is easier to imagine than to do.

That half-second saved Vane's life and nearly ended everyone else's. He wrenched the key down.

The console chirped. The display blinked from **01:31** to **00:15**, amber now, committed. Somewhere below them, deep in the diversion tunnel, detonators woke up and began counting in their own silent language.

"What did you do?" Ryn whispered.

"What I had to," Vane said, and there it was at last, naked on his face: not reason, not responsibility, just a small man's terror wearing a large man's arithmetic. "A controlled breach. Contained. The records burn, the passage seals, and in six months nobody will even remember there was a question."

"The flood wave will go downstream," Lena said. "Through the narrows. Through Ferrow's Landing. You know that. Your own engineers told you that."

"My own engineers signed nondisclosure agreements."

**00:11.**

Lena's mind went very quiet and very fast, the way it did on bad ice, the way it had the day everything fell apart seven years ago — except this time the clarity came without the panic riding on top of it. She looked at the console. Main charge: diversion tunnel, sealed circuit, no code, no time. Secondary lines: the old survey tunnel — her father's tunnel, the one the original team had drilled twenty years ago and abandoned when they found the warm channel instead — still wired from the preliminary blasting phase, still listed on the console's secondary panel, still armed.

Four hundred kilos in the wrong place would kill a town. Four hundred kilos in the right place would collapse a dead-end tunnel into itself, a fist closing in an empty glove.

She couldn't disarm the main charge.

But a firing system only does what you tell it. And a reroute is just a lie told with wires.

"Tamsin!" she shouted over the rail. "The secondary panel — red junction box, left side!"

Tamsin was already moving, scrambling up the last meters of the route, and Lena turned back to the console and tore the access cover off the secondary bus with her bare hands, skinning two knuckles and not caring. Inside: a forest of terminals, color-coded by some contractor who had never imagined a cartographer's daughter would be rewiring his work by countdown light.

**00:07.**

"Which ones?" Tamsin shouted, arriving breathless beside her.

"Orange to gray. Swap them." Her father's voice in her head, patient as contour lines: *The map doesn't change, Lena. Only what you choose to follow.* "Trust me."

"That's not reassuring!"

"Nothing true ever is. Swap them!"

**00:04.**

Vane lunged. Ryn — bless Ryn, terrified, trembling, brave Ryn — did not hit him with the pick but did throw it, and it caught him across the shins with a clang that folded him to the grating like a folding chair.

Lena crossed the wires.

**00:01.**

For one heartbeat, nothing. The river roared. The floodlights hummed. Vane looked up at her from his knees with an expression that was almost, almost a smile —

Then the world punched them in the chest.

* * *

The sound arrived twice: once through the air, once through the rock, and the rock's version was older and angrier. The platform jumped under their feet. Dust boiled out of the hillside two hundred meters upstream, a gray exhalation from the mouth of the old survey tunnel, and then the whole slope above it seemed to shrug — a slow, muscular settling, tons upon tons of stone deciding, collectively, to sit down.

The diversion tunnel mouth, forty meters below it, vanished. Not collapsed — *swallowed*, entombed under the fresh rubble of the survey drift, its four hundred kilos of emulsion explosive crushed into silence before a single charge fired. The main blast line, severed at the junction box, lay dead in its conduit.

The shockwave rolled down the gorge and met the river, and the river — the Kestrel, the honest one, the one that had been carrying her father's maps toward the sea all week — rose up in a single white shoulder and slapped the cliff face below them, throwing spray fifty meters into the air, drenching the platform, the console, the three of them clinging to the railing.

Then it settled. Then it roared on, unchanged, unhurt, unbothered.

Lena became aware, gradually, of sounds. Tamsin laughing — high, disbelieving, slightly hysterical laughter. Ryn saying "oh" over and over, a small flat syllable, like a bell that had forgotten its note. Her own heart, slamming.

And Vane, still on his knees on the wet grating, staring at the dust cloud where his project used to be.

"It's done," he said. Not to her. To the console, maybe, or to the river. "It's contained. The passage is sealed. Technically—" and here his voice climbed into the register of a man reading terms aloud to a jury that has already left the building, "technically the objective was achieved. The anomaly is inaccessible. The record will show—"

"The record," Lena said, "is currently floating downstream in waterproof bags, along with your signature on the demolition order, your private correspondence with the ministry, and a very detailed account of the last eight minutes that Tamsin recorded on the scout radio. The river keeps better archives than you do, Aldous. It never loses anything. It just moves it somewhere new."

He stood up. Slowly. Water dripped off his ruined jacket. For a moment she thought — hoped, in a way that surprised her — that he might simply sit back down and let the gorge have him, the way the survey team leader had walked into the current with his maps in his arms. Some men, confronted with the thing they've built collapsing, build themselves a smaller version inside and move in.

Vane moved toward the edge instead. One step. Two.

"Don't," Lena said.

"Why not?" He spread his arms, and the spray beaded on his face like sweat. "You've taken everything. The project, the name, the—"

"I've taken nothing. You gave it all away, one reasonable-sounding decision at a time." She held out her hand. It was shaking. She let it shake. "There are crews down there who heard the flood numbers on their own radios, Aldous. Some of them have families in Ferrow's Landing. They're going to want to talk to someone, and I promise you the conversation goes better if you're alive for it."

He looked at her hand. He looked at the river.

And then the river decided.

Whether it was the spray-slick grating, or the settling rock shifting under the platform, or simply the gorge collecting a debt — she never knew. His foot slid. His arms windmilled. Their fingers brushed — hers closing on air, his closing on nothing — and then he was gone over the rail, and the white water took him the way it takes everything: completely, without malice, without ceremony.

Ryn screamed. Tamsin grabbed Lena's arm. They hung over the railing together, staring down into the churning dark, and for ten seconds, twenty, Lena searched the foam below the falls with her father's eyes, reading the water the way he'd taught her, looking for the eddy where a body would fetch up.

The eddy was empty.

The river ran on, south, toward Ferrow's Landing, toward Sela's ferry, toward the sea — carrying its cargo of paper and secrets and one smooth-talking man who had believed, until the very last second, that water could be managed.

Lena straightened up. Her hands wouldn't stop shaking, so she pressed them flat against the cold railing and let the vibration of the river come up through the steel and into her bones, steadying her the way it had steadied her for seven years, the way it always would.

"We need to get down," Tamsin said quietly. "The crews. Someone has to tell them what happened before they hear it wrong."

"In a minute." Lena watched the current. Somewhere downstream, her father's maps were coming ashore. Somewhere downstream, people were picking them up. "In a minute."

Ryn touched her sleeve. "Did we win?"

Lena thought about it. About the valley behind the wall, warm and green and guarded. About a father restored by paper and a mother who'd died believing otherwise. About a man in the river and a town that would never know how close it had come.

"We survived," she said. "Tomorrow we find out if that counts as winning."

Far below, at the mouth of the worksite, floodlights were flickering on one by one — and among them, moving fast up the gorge trail, a lantern that did not belong to any crew chief she recognized.

Someone was coming up to meet them.
