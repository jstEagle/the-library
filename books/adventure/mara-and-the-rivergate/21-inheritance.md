## 21. Inheritance

The invitation came on the third morning after the flood that wasn't, delivered not by an elder or a runner but by a nine-year-old girl named Petra who appeared at Lena's door with a basket of bread still warm enough to fog the cold air.

"You're wanted at the Long House," the girl announced, with the gravity of someone delivering a war summons. "Ivo says come fed. He says you argue better when you've eaten."

"Does he now."

"He says it's the Nakamura in you." Petra's eyes went wide the moment she said it, as if she'd let slip a state secret, and she thrust the basket forward like a shield. "The bread's for you either way."

Lena took it and managed, mostly, to keep her face steady until the girl had scampered off between the stone houses. The Nakamura in you. Thirty years after her father had walked out of this valley with his promises folded into his pack, his name was still a thing people here knew how to use — sometimes as an accusation, and lately, to her continuing bewilderment, as a compliment.

She ate two rolls standing up, because Ivo had been right about the arguing.

* * *

The Long House smelled of cedar smoke and lamp oil, and every elder in the valley was already seated when Lena arrived, which meant they had been discussing her for some time. Ivo Marsh occupied the head bench with the air of a man who had been arguing on her behalf and losing gracefully. Tamsin stood by the window with her arms crossed, still favoring the shoulder she'd wrenched on the descent through the gorge, watching Lena with an expression that had softened over the past three days from suspicion into something almost like respect.

Ryn sat apart from everyone, wrapped in a borrowed blanket, looking like a person who had recently been forgiven for something enormous and hadn't yet figured out where to put their hands.

"Lena Nakamura." Ivo's voice filled the room without effort. "Three days ago you saved this valley. Two weeks ago you were its enemy. Before that, your father was both of those things in the same year." He spread his hands. "We are not a people who move quickly. But we have decided, and I am instructed to say it plainly so there is no room for the misunderstanding we are all fond of hiding inside."

He paused. Lena braced herself.

"Stay. Not as a guest under watch. As one of us. There is a house on the south slope with a good roof and a view of the springs. There is work — the valley has needed a mapmaker since your father left, and God knows we've been drawing our boundaries on bark and guessing. You would want for nothing. Your name would be safe here, whatever it becomes out there."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms do when everyone present already knows what the answer will be and is waiting to see if they're wrong.

Lena looked down at her hands. They were still scabbed from the charge station, from the rope burn at the cataract, from seven years of holding onto things that kept trying to leave.

"I can't," she said.

Tamsin uncrossed her arms. Ryn's head came up. Ivo, to his credit, didn't flinch.

"I spent seven years learning what it costs to be erased from a place," Lena went on, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she'd expected. "And then I spent one week learning what it costs to be kept in one. Both times, somebody else drew the boundary and told me where I belonged." She looked around the room, at these faces that had every reason to hate her name and had chosen, painstakingly, not to. "You're offering me a good version of it. Maybe the best version anyone's ever offered me. But it's still a wall with me on one side of it. And I've had enough of walls to last several lifetimes."

"The world out there cleared your name," Ivo said. "On paper. Do you believe paper?"

"No," Lena admitted. "But I believe in walking out anyway. That's kind of my whole method."

A sound escaped Tamsin by the window — half laugh, half cough — and quickly smothered.

Ivo studied her for a long moment. Then he did something she had never seen him do: he smiled, small and crooked, like a door opening a crack.

"Your father said nearly the same thing. Word for word, near enough. 'I can't stay, but I won't lie for you either.'" He leaned forward. "So here is what we offer instead, and think carefully before refusing twice, because our pride is only slightly sturdier than yours. Come back. Once a year, when the pass opens. Survey our boundaries honestly — not for any ministry, not for any dam, but for us. Teach whoever we send to learn. Carry nothing out that we haven't seen with our own eyes. In exchange, we will trust you with the one thing we have never trusted an outsider with."

"Which is?"

"The truth about where we are. Written down. Kept by you, in your keeping, so that if anything ever happens to this valley — flood, fire, men like Vane — there is one honest map of us in the world, held by someone who chose us rather than being born to us." Ivo's eyes were steady. "Your father promised secrecy and gave us silence instead. We are asking for the opposite arrangement. Noise, but honest noise. Can you live with that bargain?"

Lena found, to her surprise, that her throat had gone tight.

An annual envoy. A cartographer in residence, one month a year. Her father's work — the real work, the work underneath the disgrace — picked up and done properly, with consent on both ends this time. It wasn't staying. It wasn't leaving. It was something she didn't have a word for yet, which was, she suspected, exactly what inheritance felt like before you grew into it.

"Yes," she said. "I can live with that."

* * *

The packing took less time than the goodbyes, which was always the way of it.

Her gear had been returned from the evidence lockup in Ferrow's Landing — minus the items the ministry had confiscated and would never admit to confiscating — and she laid it all out on the floor of her borrowed room the night before departure, doing inventory by lamplight. Spare rope. The backup stove. Two extra base layers, wool, the good kind she'd bought with the first installment of the settlement pay. A tent she'd carried through the gorge and never once needed, because the valley had taken them in.

Ryn appeared in the doorway while she was rolling the sleeping bag, moving with the careful newness of someone relearning how to occupy space.

"Heard you turned down a house," Ryn said.

"Heard you turned down a medal," Lena countered. The valley council had wanted to honor Ryn formally; Ryn had asked instead that the ceremony be skipped and the budget spent on winter stores. "We're a matched set, you and I."

Ryn came in and sat down heavily on the windowsill, blanket and all. Up close, the starvation was still visible — the wrists, the hollows — though three days of the valley's cooking had begun the slow work of undoing it. "I've been thinking about what happens next. For me."

"And?"

"And I'm not staying either." A quick, defensive lift of the chin. "Not because — I mean, they've been nothing but kind, once they stopped pointing arrows at me. It's just." Ryn's hands worked at the blanket's fringe. "I joined the survey team to see the world measured. Turns out the measuring was the rotten part. But the seeing—" A breath. "I don't want to hide somewhere perfect. I want to go where maps are made badly and make them better. Which sounds insane given that maps are what ruined both of us."

"It's not insane." Lena reached into her kit and pulled out the spare rope, the backup stove, the wool layers, the tent, stacking them in a neat pile on the bed. "It's the sanest thing anyone's said to me all week. Here."

Ryn stared at the pile. "That's your gear."

"That's my *spare* gear. There's a difference, and the difference is that mine has a recommendation attached." Lena fished a folded page from her breast pocket — she'd written it that afternoon, in the careful block letters she used for documents that mattered — and held it out. "My name's worth something again. Not much. Enough. Sela vouches for you at Ferrow's Landing, and nobody crosses Sela. The trail school in Corvane owes me a favor from before, and favors from before count double now that I'm officially un-disgraced. Tell them Lena Nakamura sent you and that I'll answer for your work personally."

Ryn took the letter like it might detonate. "Why? I stole from you. From the archive. I'd have traded your father's maps to Vane for my own skin."

"You were starving and terrified, and you got caught before you could do harm, and you confessed before anyone even accused you." Lena shrugged. "I know exactly what fear does to a person with no good options. I did seven years of it. If exile is the price of being scared, then nobody ever gets to come home, and I refuse to live in that world." She met Ryn's eyes. "Besides. You stood in front of a drilling rig with me and didn't run. That's the entry on your record I choose to read."

Ryn's jaw worked. For a moment Lena thought the kid might actually cry, and then Ryn did the thing people do instead, which was get angry at the furniture. "You can't just — hand someone a life."

"Sure I can. Somebody handed me mine last week. Ministry press release, very official." She pushed the pile closer. "Take the stove. Argue with me tomorrow, when you've slept."

Ryn took the stove.

* * *

They left at first light, the whole valley turning out to see them to the passage behind the waterfall — which was, Lena reflected, the strangest front door any community had ever chosen, and also the most sensible.

Ivo walked beside her as far as the warm channel would allow. "First survey comes in spring," he said. "When the ice bridges hold. We'll send word downstream through Sela — she's carried our messages longer than you've been alive, by the way, so mind how you speak of her ferrying."

"I had a feeling about Sela."

"Everyone has a feeling about Sela. She collects them." At the lip of the passage, where the waterfall thundered past close enough to mist their faces, Ivo stopped and looked at her with an expression she couldn't fully read — grief and relief sharing a bench. "Your father stood here. Right here. He cried, if you want the truth of it, though he'd have denied it to his grave, and largely did." Ivo's voice roughened. "Tell him, wherever he is, that the debt is paid. Not forgiven — paid. There's a difference, and we are a people who keep the difference."

"I'll tell him," Lena said, and found she meant it literally, in the way she'd started meaning things lately: she would stand at his grave in the spring and say the words out loud, because some messages deserved the dignity of delivery.

Then the waterfall curtain swallowed them, and the cold stone throat of the gorge took them back, and the valley was behind them — not gone, never gone now, but folded away like a letter in a pocket, carried against the heart.

* * *

They camped that night above the waterline in the cave where Ryn had nearly starved, because Ryn insisted, wanting — needing — to sleep one night in the place and take its power back. Lena built a small fire at the mouth and they ate stewed valley lentils, which Ryn declared superior to every ration pack ever issued, and watched the river run silver under the stars.

"So," Ryn said eventually. "Spring. You come back, teach the mapping, carry the true map out. What happens the other eleven months?"

Lena poked the fire. "I walk. There's a range east of Corvane nobody's surveyed properly since the last century. There's a coast road. There's—" She laughed at herself, quietly. "There's a lot of miles. Turns out I measure my life in them. Always have."

"Where do they point?" Ryn asked — and Lena realized the kid had been listening, really listening, back in the lowlands, to whatever fragments of her story had made the rounds.

"Outward," she said. "And back. Both directions at once, from now on." She glanced over. "That's the trick nobody tells you. You spend years thinking you have to choose between the road and the home. Then one day somebody offers you a door that swings both ways, and you feel like an idiot for all the years you spent picking locks."

Ryn considered this with the seriousness the kid brought to everything, then nodded once, decisively, the way decisions get made at twenty-three. "Both ways," Ryn agreed. "Starting with downstream. Because if I miss the morning ferry, Sela will leave without me, and I've heard what she does to people who make her wait."

"She makes them wait longer," Lena said. "It's educational."

Ryn grinned — the first full grin Lena had seen from them, wide and unguarded and young — and rolled into the sleeping bag that was, as of yesterday, permanently borrowed.

Lena stayed up a while longer, firelight on her scabbed knuckles, the river talking its endless talk below. Somewhere upstream, behind a wall of falling water, a valley slept warm under stars it had never shown the sky. Somewhere downstream, a ferrywoman was mending nets and pretending not to count the days until news arrived. Somewhere in the ground, her father's warning ran through the rock like a vein of ore, finally mined, finally believed.

Inheritance, it turned out, wasn't a house or a name or even a map. It was a route. Drawn honestly, walked willingly, handed on intact.

She banked the fire and slept, and dreamed of contour lines rising gently toward a pass she'd never climbed, in ranges she hadn't met yet, waiting patient as rivers for the people who would come to read them.
