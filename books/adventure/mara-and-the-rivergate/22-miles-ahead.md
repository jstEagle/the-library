## 22. Miles Ahead

Dawn came to the mouth of the Rivergate the way it always had — slowly, grudgingly, as if the canyon were deciding whether to let the light in at all.

Lena stood on the gravel bar where the Kestrel widened and forgot its temper, watching the first sun find the sandstone walls and set them burning rose-gold. Seven years ago she would have measured this moment in what it cost her: the cold seeping through her boot soles, the ache in her shoulders from a pack that never got lighter no matter how much she gave away, the particular loneliness of being the first person awake in a country that didn't want her.

Now she found herself measuring it differently.

*Four hundred meters,* she thought, eyeing the stretch of river where the water ran smooth over buried stone. *Maybe four-fifty.* The ferry crossing at Ferrow's Landing was eleven miles downstream, most of it gentle, one section of riffles she'd want to portage around rather than argue with. Eleven miles. She could be there by mid-afternoon if she pushed, or by tomorrow morning if she let herself walk like a person instead of like a woman fleeing something.

She decided, experimentally, to try walking like a person.

It was harder than it sounded. Seven years of exile teaches you a gait — head down, pace steady, eyes cataloguing escape routes you'll never need. Lena caught herself doing it now, halfway along the bank, and made herself stop. Made herself look up at the cliffs going amber. Made herself notice that the air smelled like wet sage and snowmelt and, faintly, woodsmoke from somewhere upstream — the valley's morning fires, carried down through the gate on the current of warm air that poured out of the canyon like breath.

The Rivergate kept its own. That's what the people of Ferrow's Landing said, crossing themselves and refusing her money. And they'd been right, in their way. It had kept Ryn. It had kept Tamsin and Ivo and the two hundred souls behind the waterfall who wanted nothing from the world except to be left alone by it. It had even, in its patient geological way, kept her father — his last map, his true maps, his confession written in contour lines.

And for twenty-two days it had kept her, too.

She stopped at the edge of the gorge's shadow and turned to look back one more time. The slot canyon swallowed the river into darkness, the same darkness she'd walked into alone with a radio that died on day one and a dread she couldn't name. From here, knowing what she knew, the darkness looked different. Not a throat. A doorway.

"Keep them," she said quietly, to the canyon, to the valley, to whoever was listening. "I'll come back next spring."

Somewhere above, a raven answered — or laughed; with ravens it was always hard to tell — and Lena smiled for the first time in miles.

* * *

Her legs remembered the trail even when her mind wandered, which was fortunate, because her mind wandered constantly now. It kept returning to the same small moments, turning them over like stones:

Ryn's face when Lena handed over the spare gear at the valley gate — the junior surveyor holding a thirty-year-old compass like it might bite. *"I don't know how to thank you."* *"You don't," Lena had said. "You use it. That's the whole thanks."*

Tamsin pressing a folded square of oilcloth into her hands at the last second, refusing to explain, only saying, *"Open it when you're home."* Lena hadn't opened it yet. She was saving it. She wasn't sure for what — maybe for the ferry, maybe for a room with a door that locked, maybe just for a day when she needed proof that someone, somewhere, had thought well enough of her to give her a gift without conditions attached.

Ivo Marsh's handshake, which had started the month as an interrogation grip and ended it as something almost paternal. *"Your father broke his word under pressure, girl. You kept yours under worse. The debt is paid — more than paid. Don't you dare carry it another mile."*

She was trying not to. She suspected it would take practice. Debts inherited young have a way of growing into the skeleton.

By mid-morning she'd reached the long gravel flats below the cataract — the one that had nearly killed them all on the descent, the night everything hung in the balance. In daylight it looked almost friendly, white water braiding over dark stone, spray hanging in the air like something the river was dreaming. She ate lunch sitting on a boulder with her boots off, feet in the shallows, and the cold bit so hard she yelped, then laughed, then sat there anyway because it felt honest. The Kestrel didn't do lukewarm. The Kestrel did not believe in easing anyone into anything.

"You're showing off," she told it.

The river, naturally, ignored her. Rivers were like cats that way. Like certain government liaisons, too, though Vane would never again be in a position to ignore anyone. They'd pulled him out of the gorge two days after the flood-that-wasn't — alive, half-drowned, and babbling about sabotage and traitors, while the workers he'd tried to bury stood on the banks and testified against him in voices that shook and then, gradually, didn't. The ministry had moved fast after that. Ministries always move fast when the alternative is being seen standing still next to a man who ordered a flood.

Her record had been cleared by a clerk's signature, three sentences long. Her father's name restored by a longer document, read aloud in a hearing room she hadn't bothered to attend. She'd listened to the recording instead, alone in her rented room in Ferrow's Landing, and found she felt less vindication than she'd expected and something else she hadn't expected at all: relief for a dead man who could no longer feel it, and a strange tenderness toward the seven-year-old girl kneeling on a kitchen floor learning to read contour lines from a father who was telling the truth the whole time.

*"See how the lines crowd together here? That means steep. Steep isn't bad, Lena-bird. Steep is just honest about itself."*

"Yeah, Dad," she said to the river. "I finally learned that one."

* * *

She reached the outskirts of Ferrow's Landing as the light went long and gold, and the town looked exactly as it had when she'd first walked in — which is to say, it looked like it was actively pretending not to notice her. Curtains twitched. A boy hauling nets on the dock stared until his mother smacked his shoulder and dragged him inside. Lena had expected this, and found, to her mild surprise, that it didn't sting the way it once would have. These people had refused to guide her, had muttered that the Rivergate keeps its own, had watched her walk into the gorge with the flat pity reserved for the already-dead.

Now she was walking back out of it, and word had traveled faster than she had. Of course it had. Tamsin's scouts had gone ahead days ago; the ministry's own rescue crews had talked; and somewhere in between, the story had grown the way stories do in river towns, acquiring rapids and monsters and a heroine who probably wrestled the cataract bare-handed.

Lena intended to correct none of it. Let them have the version with the wrestling. The truth was stranger anyway.

She was halfway up the lane toward Sela's place when the old ferrywoman appeared in her doorway, arms crossed, taking inventory of her with one long look.

"Huh," Sela said. "Thinner. Filthier. Standing straighter. Same face."

"That's me. Consistent."

"Your father said the same thing, coming out of the gate the first time. Thinner, filthier, straighter." Sela's voice did something complicated on the last word. She stepped aside from the door. "Well? Are you going to stand in my yard like a lost mule, or are you coming in? There's stew. There's been stew for three weeks. I've been making stew on faith, girl, and faith makes terrible leftovers."

Lena laughed — really laughed, the kind that surprised her on its way out — and followed her inside.

The stew was excellent. Faith, it turned out, made fine stew; it was only the waiting that soured. Sela fed her and interrogated her in alternating courses, and Lena gave her the story in pieces, leaving out exactly those parts that weren't hers to tell. Sela listened the way old ferrymen listen — saying little, missing nothing — and when Lena finished, the old woman was quiet a long moment, looking into her bowl.

"I told you your father asked the same question," she said at last. "He asked it right where you're sitting. *What does the Rivergate keep?* I never answered him. Didn't know myself, then." She looked up. "Do you know what I think now?"

"Tell me."

"I think it keeps what's given to it. He gave it his secret, and it kept that. Gave it his name, too, poor fool, when the world took it away." Sela tapped the table with one knuckle. "You gave it something better. You gave it a choice, and then you gave it back. That's why you can walk away smiling and he couldn't."

Lena turned her bowl slowly on the table. "You make it sound simple."

"It is simple. Simple isn't the same as easy. Never has been." Sela stood, collected the bowls, and paused at the stove with her back turned. "Ferry runs at dawn. You'll want to be on it."

"Not staying, then?"

Sela glanced over her shoulder, and there was something in her face that was almost — but not quite — a smile. "Girl, I've watched people leave this town my whole life. Most of them leave running from something. A few leave walking toward something. The ones in the second group always come back." She nodded at the window, where the last light lay on the water. "This town will still be here. So will I. Now eat the rest of that bread before I feed it to the gulls, who've done nothing to deserve it."

* * *

Dawn, again. Lena was beginning to suspect her life ran on dawn the way other lives ran on calendars.

She stood at the landing with her pack shouldered, watching Sela ready the ferry — a flat-bottomed barge older than either of them, guided across the current by a cable thick as a wrist and a system of pulleys Sela operated with the tenderness of a woman handling a musical instrument. The mist was coming off the water in slow ribbons. Upstream, barely visible, the mouth of the Rivergate held its darkness against the brightening sky, keeping its own.

Tamsin's oilcloth packet rode in the outer pocket of her pack. She'd saved opening it for this — she'd known somehow, since the moment it was pressed into her hands, that it belonged to a threshold and not a room. She unwound the cloth now, standing in the mist, and inside found a single sheet of drafting paper, fresh, crisp, unmistakably new.

A map. Hand-inked. The upper Kestrel, drawn with a cartographer's care — but drawn differently than any map she'd ever seen. The gorge was rendered in loving detail, every rapid named, every eddy noted. And beyond the wall, where the hidden valley lay, the contour lines didn't stop. They continued — but rendered in a style she recognized with a jolt that went through her like the cold river water: her father's hand. His exact hand, the distinctive way he hooked his elevation numbers, copied faithfully by someone who had studied those confiscated notebooks for years.

At the bottom, in Tamsin's smaller, neater script, a single line:

*For the envoy. The valley drew its own borders; we thought someone should draw its roads. Come spring.*

Lena stood there a long time, letting the mist settle on her hair, holding the paper the way you hold something that has just rearranged the furniture of your life. An annual envoy. Her father's work, begun in secrecy and shame, continued in openness and honor. Her name on the valley's business, and the valley's trust under her name, each repairing the other.

"Well?" Sela called from the ferry, one hand on the cable. "Are you boarding or are you composing poetry on my dock?"

"Both," Lena said, folding the map carefully into the oilcloth. "But mostly boarding."

She stepped onto the barge. The cable took up the strain with a groan, and the far bank began, slowly, to come nearer — the town waking up, smoke rising from chimneys, the boy from yesterday already back on the dock staring openly now, because apparently heroes were permitted to be stared at. Somewhere behind her the river ran down from the valley, carrying cold clean water past everything that had tried to own it, going where it was going, unhurried, certain, asking no one's permission.

Lena leaned on the rail and did the arithmetic one last time, the way she'd done it every morning for seven years. Miles walked. Miles ahead.

Eleven to the lowlands. Two hundred to the rail line. Three thousand, eventually, to wherever a cleared name and a full pack could take her — which, she was beginning to understand, was nowhere near as far as she'd once feared and considerably farther than she'd once dared.

But the number that mattered was the smallest one. Next spring. One winter away. A valley behind a wall, a compass in a young surveyor's hands, a map with blank spaces waiting for honest lines.

The ferry touched the far bank with a soft knock, like a door answering.

Lena Nakamura shouldered her pack, nodded once to Sela, and started up the dock into the morning — measuring her life, as always, in miles.

But for the first time, every mile pointed home.
