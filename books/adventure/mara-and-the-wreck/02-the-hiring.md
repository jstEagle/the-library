## 2. The Hiring

Callow's Landing smelled like every river town Mirabel had ever hated: fish rot and lamp oil, wet rope drying in the sun, the sweet-sick stink of a place that had given up on drainage a generation ago. The town sat where the salt flats gave out and the drowned country began, stacked on stilts along a brown channel that moved with the lazy confidence of something that had already won.

She came in on foot, which marked her immediately. Nobody walked into Callow's Landing. They arrived by boat, sweating and relieved, or they didn't arrive at all. A woman alone, dusty to the knees, leading no animal and carrying nothing visible but a bedroll and a water skin—she felt the town's eyes slide over her and dismiss her, one by one, dockhand by dockhand, and she let them. Invisibility was a discipline. You practiced it the way a knife-fighter practiced draws.

The thief's trail had brought her here. That was the strange part. She'd followed him for nine days across country that should have erased any man's passage—the salt flats kept no secrets because they kept nothing at all—and his prints had ended at a river that appeared on none of her maps, a wide brown vein cutting through ground that was supposed to be dead pan and gypsum. Her compass had known about it before she did. It had been pulling east-northeast for two days while she stubbornly held her heading, until finally she'd surrendered to it, and an hour later there was the water, sudden as a wound.

She didn't think about what that meant. She put it away with everything else she didn't think about: the debt, the name attached to the debt, the reason a woman who wanted nothing from anyone carried a cracked brass compass older than she was.

* * *

The Landing's only establishment worth the name was the Drowned Dog, a drinking house built on the roof of whatever it had drowned, its floorboards warped into gentle waves that made every patron look slightly seasick. Mirabel took a corner table with her back to the wall and ordered tea she had no intention of drinking hot, because hot tea meant sitting still, and sitting still meant being a fixture, and fixtures got remembered.

She listened instead. Ten minutes of listening told her more than ten days of tracking.

The expedition had come through three weeks ago. Sef Adama's name came up four times in the first hour, always with a slight pause before it, the way people said the names of men who paid well and asked hard questions. He'd hired locally—a deck crew of six, a pilot named something-or-other who'd drunk his advance inside a week and been replaced. And he'd had a surveyor with him, a quiet man, good with instruments, who'd left in the night eleven days ago with a strongbox and no boat.

Eleven days. Mirabel did the arithmetic against the boot prints she'd been following and felt the small cold click of confirmation. Same man. Same timeline. Which meant Adama hadn't hired her yet—but he would, because a man who'd lost a surveyor and a strongbox and couldn't get answers out of a river town this small was a man shopping for exactly what she sold.

She just had to decide whether to sell it to him.

The problem wasn't the work. Tracking a thief upriver into flooded country was honest labor, and she was good at it, and the monsoon season closing in would thin the competition to nothing. The problem was the double pay she could already feel coming, the way you felt weather in an old scar. Double pay meant a debt of effort. Effort meant investment. Investment meant that when the job went wrong—and jobs like this always went wrong—she'd be too deep to walk away clean. Her whole life was built on the principle that you never owed anyone more than you could carry, and she owed enough already. More than enough. There was a number, and a name behind the number, and every coin she earned went down that hole like stones into a well, and the well never filled, and the voice at the bottom never stopped asking.

Double pay would fill it faster. That was the greed talking, and she knew its voice intimately, the way you know a sibling's footsteps.

Instinct said something else. Instinct said: a surveyor doesn't steal a strongbox and run toward harder country. Surveyors are map people, and map people run toward things, not away from them. Whatever was in that box, the thief hadn't taken money. He'd taken a destination. And destinations, in the drowned country, were worth more than money, and worth more than her, probably, if it came to weighing lives on someone else's scale.

She was still turning it over when Sef Adama found her, which told her he'd been looking longer than she'd been listening.

* * *

He was not what she expected. She'd built him from the talk: big, loud, a man who spent money like ballast. Instead he was lean and gray at the temples, with the careful movements of someone who'd broken bones before and learned their lessons, and he sat down across from her without asking, which should have annoyed her but instead interested her. Men who sat without asking were men who'd read her first.

"Mirabel Voss," he said. Not a question.

"Depends who's paying."

"Sef Adama." He set a folded paper on the table between them, square and official-looking, and didn't touch it again. "You tracked a man across the salt flats. Nine days. The border station at Kessel logged your water purchase."

"The border station sells water to everyone."

"They don't sell it to everyone in quantities that suggest solo travel into the flats in late dry season." His eyes were pale, almost colorless, and very steady. "I've been asking after trackers for six days. Every name I got was a drunk or a liar or both. Then a boatman mentions a woman came in on foot from the east, and I thought: nobody walks out of the flats. Unless they're following something."

"Or running from something."

"What are you running from?"

"Nothing. That's the point of walking."

Almost, he smiled. "My surveyor is named Ellery Vance. Eleven nights ago he opened my safe, took a document case, and went into the flooded country without a boat, which means someone picked him up, which means this wasn't panic. It was planned. I want him found. I want my property back. And I want to know who helped him, because I pay my people enough that betrayal should be expensive, and I intend to make it so."

Mirabel turned her tea slowly on its saucer. "Property. Paper?"

"Why does that matter?"

"It matters because paper runs differently than coin. Coin buys silence. Paper makes people stupid and brave at the same time. If I'm hunting a man with coin, I look in gambling dens. If I'm hunting a man with paper, I look wherever the paper points." She looked up. "What's the paper?"

Adama was quiet for a moment, weighing her. She watched him decide how much truth the transaction required, and she respected that he did the math openly rather than lying cheap.

"A chart," he said. "Survey work. Old survey work, predating the flood, recovered from an archive downcoast. Vance authenticated it. Apparently it authenticated itself right out of my safe."

"And you want it back because—"

"Because I've spent two years and most of my capital mounting this expedition, and that chart is the difference between an expedition and a boating holiday." He leaned forward slightly. "I'll be plain with you, Voss, because I'm told you're plain back. There are rumors attached to that chart. Rumors about what it leads to. I don't care about rumors. I care about finding Vance before the monsoon closes the channels, because after that, he's gone until next year, and next year there will be other people looking, and some of them are worse than me."

"Worse how?"

"Worse organized."

There it was. She'd heard the shape of it in the tavern talk already—broker colors seen upriver, a syndicate buying claims along the drowned country's edge, men whose interest in old charts would be professional rather than curious. Harrow. The name had come up twice, both times quietly, the way you say the name of a dog that bites.

"Double pay," Adama said. "Standard rates doubled, plus a completion bonus. Paid half now, half on delivery of Vance and my property. I provide the boat, the crew, the supplies. You provide the finding."

Half now. Half now meant coin in hand tonight, coin that went down the well, coin that bought another month of the voice at the bottom staying quiet. She hated how loudly that part of her answered.

"And if I find him and he's dead?" she asked. "Or if the paper's destroyed? What's half a job worth?"

"Then you're paid for the finding, not the fetching. I'm fair. Ask anyone." He almost smiled again. "Ask anyone who isn't currently robbing me."

Mirabel looked at the folded paper on the table. Contract, presumably. She didn't reach for it yet. This was the last moment in which she could stand up, leave coin on the board for the tea, and walk out of Callow's Landing with her rule intact—stay invisible, stay unowned, stay light enough to vanish. Every instinct she'd polished over fifteen years of solitary work said this job was too rich, too urgent, too wrapped in other people's secrets. Rich jobs were bait. Urgent jobs were traps. Jobs wrapped in secrets meant you'd be the secret eventually, the loose end somebody paid worse men to tie off.

But the well. The well at the bottom of everything, with its patient voice, its arithmetic that never came out even. Double pay was six months of silence. Six months of waking up owing no one anything except the original debt, which was different, which was hers, which she'd chosen in a way she couldn't undo and wouldn't explain.

Greed wore instinct down the way water wore stone. Not fast. Completely.

"One condition," she said.

Adama raised an eyebrow.

"I work alone in the field. Your crew poles the boat; I read the country. Nobody questions my route, nobody follows me when I go ahead, and nobody touches my kit." She paused. "And when we find him, he comes back alive if it can be managed. Dead men answer no questions, and I have a feeling yours has answers you haven't thought to ask for yet."

Something flickered across Adama's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "Agreed," he said, too smoothly. "Alive preferred."

He slid the contract across. She read all of it, every line, while he waited with the patience of a man who'd expected her to skim. The language was clean. The numbers were real. The clause about jurisdiction in the event of death was standard and chilling, as such clauses always were, and she signed anyway, because the well was calling and she'd never yet learned to cover her ears.

Half payment came in a canvas pouch, heavy and honest. She counted it at the table, coin by coin, because trust was for people who could afford it, and Adama watched her count without offense, which she noted as one more point in his favor and one more reason to distrust him. Good men were usually hiding the best inventory.

"Boat leaves tomorrow at first light," he said, standing. "North dock, the *Corvid*. My deckhand Toma will show you your berth."

"Toma," she repeated. One of the names from the tavern talk. Young, quick, talked too much. "How long has he worked for you?"

"Two years. Why?"

"No reason." She pocketed the pouch. "I like to know who's aboard when a surveyor vanishes out of a locked camp. Vanishing takes help, Mr. Adama. Somebody rowed that man somewhere. Somebody saw. And somebodies on boats have a way of being the same somebodies who open safes."

Adama stood very still for a moment, and she watched the thought land behind his pale eyes—that he'd just hired the tracker and handed her a suspect list that included his own crew, and that she'd done it deliberately, on day one, to see what he'd do about it.

"You're more expensive than your rate," he said finally.

"Everyone is. That's why I charge double." She rose, shouldering her bedroll. "First light. Don't wait for me—I'm always early, and I hate being watched while I work."

* * *

She spent the evening doing what she always did in a new town: learning its exits. There were four out of Callow's Landing by water and one by land, the land route doubling back southeast across a spine of high ground toward the flats, passable until the monsoon made it a swamp. She walked it a mile out and back in the failing light, fixed its landmarks, and returned through the stilt-town's back ways, past drying nets and shuttered windows, past a shrine to some river saint crusted with salt and offerings.

At the shrine she stopped, because her compass had stopped her—tugging at its needle like a hound at a leash, swinging gently until it pointed not north, not south, but northeast. Upriver. Into the drowned country. Toward whatever the stolen chart led to, toward the thief, toward the thing her grandfather's cracked brass instrument seemed to want her to find as badly as Sef Adama did.

She'd told herself on the flats that the pull was coincidence, magnetism, damage. Standing in the dark with the river breathing below her and the rain-smell of the coming monsoon thick in the air, coincidence was getting harder to believe. The compass knew the way. The compass had always known the way. And she had just signed a contract to follow it into deep water for a man who thought he was buying a tracker, when what he might actually be buying was a key.

She closed the compass case. The tug vanished, the way it always did, leaving only the ordinary dark.

"Tomorrow," she told the river, and went to find her berth, and did not sleep much, and told herself that was the tea.
