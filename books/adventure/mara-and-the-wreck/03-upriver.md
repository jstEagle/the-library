## 3. Upriver

The boat left Callow's Landing an hour before dawn, when the river still belonged to the mist and the mist belonged to no one.

Mirabel stood at the stern with her arms folded, watching the town shrink behind them—first the fuel depot, then the crane at the cannery, then the water tower with its peeling paint, until Callow's Landing was just a gray smudge on the gray water and she could pretend she'd never been there at all. That was her preferred way of leaving places: as if they'd never happened.

The boat was called the *Pelican*, though nothing about it suggested a bird. It was forty feet of riveted iron built for hauling freight, now stripped to benches and jerry cans and a wheelhouse the size of a closet. Sef Adama had packed it with six crew, two weeks of rations, a crate of survey equipment, and Mirabel, who had insisted on riding outside where she could see everything.

She'd spent the night before in the hiring office going over Sef's charts, and she hadn't liked what she saw. The river beyond the salt flats wasn't on any of them. There was a blank space where the drowned country should have been—a rectangle of unmarked paper with a single notation in faded ink: *survey incomplete*. Sef had caught her looking and said, "That's why we're going," in the tone of a man who thought that answered something.

It didn't answer anything. Blank spaces on maps were where people went to die, or where other people had already died and nobody wanted to write it down.

Now the flats fell away astern and the water changed character. Out here it moved slow and brown-green, thick with silt, carrying whole trees on its surface like it was showing off. The channel was wide enough at first that you couldn't see either bank, but by midmorning the forest closed in—cottonwood and willow standing to their shoulders in floodwater, their lower trunks black with rot, Spanish moss hanging off them like the hair of drowned men.

"Keep your eyes on the snags," Sef called from the wheelhouse. He was a compact man, fifty or so, with a surveyor's squint and hands that looked like they'd done fieldwork before they'd done paperwork. "They drift down from the high country this time of year. Hit one wrong and we open a seam."

Mirabel didn't answer. She was watching the banks instead, or what passed for banks—the ragged line where water met ground, shifting hourly now that the monsoon was building in the mountains. A ranger learned to read that line the way a gambler reads faces. And the line was telling her things she didn't like.

* * *

Toma felt the change before he could name it.

He was twenty-three, wiry, quick with a wrench, and he'd been working riverboats since he was fifteen—first for his uncle, then for two seasons under Harrow's flag, which was the part of his history he kept folded up small and hidden, like a letter you don't want anyone to find. Now he crouched in the engine bay aft of the cargo hold with his ear against the housing, listening to the *Pelican*'s old diesel labor through the current.

The rhythm was wrong.

Not broken—nothing so clean as broken. Just a hitch, every fourth or fifth revolution, a fractional stumble like a man walking with a stone in his boot. Toma had heard a thousand engines lie their way through a season, and this one was lying.

He pulled the inspection plate and ran his fingers along the fuel line. Dry where it should be damp, damp where it should be dry. Someone had been in here recently. The hose clamps were turned to angles that didn't match his own work from the morning they loaded—and he'd done that work himself, alone, before first light, because he always did the fuel lines himself. He didn't trust anyone else's clamps. He especially didn't trust his own memory of doing them, not anymore, not since Harrow.

He tightened everything, wiped the fittings, sat back on his heels, and told himself it was nothing. Vibration loosens things. River water gets everywhere. Boats are just problems that float.

But the thought wouldn't leave him alone, and the thought had a shape, and the shape was a man named Harrow who paid well for information and better for silence.

* * *

By noon the channel had narrowed to maybe sixty feet, and the flooded forest pressed close enough that the overhanging branches swept the rail. Mirabel rode the bow rail with one hand and watched the water ahead, reading it the way she read everything: for exits.

There weren't any. That was the problem with rivers. They only went one way, and everyone knew it, including anyone who might want to hurt you.

Sef came forward during the noon halt—they'd tied off to a dead cottonwood to eat cold rice and let the engine cool—and stood beside her, chewing slowly, looking at the same water she was.

"You've gone quiet," he said.

"I'm usually quiet."

"Quieter." He smiled a little. "You've been staring at the treeline for an hour. See something?"

"Seeing is free," Mirabel said. "I'm deciding whether it's worth saying."

"That sounds expensive."

"It is." She ate another mouthful of rice. Then, because instinct told her the man deserved one honest thing and greed told her she needed this job too much to sabotage it: "Your advance camp went silent yesterday afternoon. You're heading upriver into a narrowing channel with no map, in monsoon season, toward whatever made them go quiet. If I were planning an ambush, I couldn't design better terrain."

Sef didn't flinch. She gave him credit for that. Most employers got defensive when you described their expedition as a coffin.

"And yet," he said.

"And yet I'm on the boat."

"Why?"

Because the pay was double and the debt was real and the compass in her pocket had cracked its glass face three years ago and pointed, ever since, somewhere that wasn't north. Because the thief she was tracking had walked into this same drowned country and Mirabel had followed boot prints across forty miles of salt to a river that shouldn't exist, and following was the only thing she was good at.

"Because whoever took your surveyor knows this water," she said instead. "And I want to know how."

Sef nodded slowly, as if that were the whole truth. Maybe it was close enough.

* * *

The radio died at four in the afternoon.

Toma was topside when it happened, coiling rope, and he heard the whole thing through the wheelhouse window. The advance camp's frequency crackled up out of the static—Denna, the camp's comms officer, a woman whose voice he'd memorized over two days of scheduled check-ins, flat and professional and faintly bored.

"*Pelican*, be advised, we've had visitors at the camp. Unidentified, arrived by water, four—"

Static swallowed her. Three seconds, five. Then her voice again, thinner, faster:

"—repeat, four persons, they're asking about the sur—"

Then nothing. Not static, exactly. Static has texture, a living grain to it. This was a hole. A clean absence, like a page torn out of a book.

Sef worked the set for twenty minutes. Called on every frequency, emergency bands included. Nothing. He tried the relay station back at the Landing and got a bored operator who confirmed the advance camp's transmitter had dropped off the air at 16:07 and stayed off.

"Equipment failure," the operator offered.

"Mid-sentence?" Sef said.

The operator had no answer for that.

Toma stood in the wheelhouse doorway while Sef stared at the dead radio, and the cold feeling in his gut got a name. He'd seen transmitters fail. Capacitors blew, antennas grounded out, batteries died—all the time, all ordinary. But they failed into noise. They didn't fail into silence, precisely between words, precisely after the word *visitors*.

Somebody had cut that signal. Somebody who knew the frequency, knew the schedule, and knew how to kill a transmission without killing the transmitter—or who was standing next to it with a knife.

And somebody, he thought, with the certainty settling into him like river water into bilge, somebody had also been inside the *Pelican*'s engine bay. Two sabotages on one boat. Or—one saboteur with two jobs.

He looked around the wheelhouse at the faces there: Sef, jaw tight; the pilot, Okon, hands steady on the wheel but eyes flicking to the banks; the two deckhands, Ferro and Bess, whispering; and through the doorway, at the stern rail, the ranger woman, watching the water like it owed her money.

Six people aboard. One of them, possibly, was the reason the radio was dead.

Possibly it was him. The thought arrived uninvited and would not leave. Harrow had bought things from him before. Small things. Routes, schedules, the name of a man who could be bribed. What if Harrow had bought more than he remembered selling? What if the price of forgetting was that you forgot?

He went below and checked the fuel lines again. The clamps were fine. He'd fixed them at noon. Whatever had been loosened had been loosened once, and either it was done or—

Or it wasn't finished yet.

* * *

Mirabel heard the radio die from the stern, and what struck her wasn't the loss of contact. It was the quality of the silence afterward.

For two days the radio had been a heartbeat in the background—check-ins at dawn and dusk, Denna's dry voice reporting water levels and mosquito counts and nothing worth hearing. You stopped noticing a heartbeat. Then it stopped, and your body noticed for you, some animal part of you sitting straight up in the dark of yourself saying *that sound is supposed to be there.*

She moved forward along the rail, past the whispering deckhands, and put her shoulder against the wheelhouse frame where she could watch both Sef and the river.

"They knew the frequency," she said, when Sef lowered the handset. "And the schedule."

"Or they found the camp and destroyed the set."

"A destroyed set makes noise when it dies. Sparks, feedback, something. This was clean." She let that sit. "Whoever did it was calm. Calm means practiced. Practiced means paid."

Sef looked at her. In the wheelhouse's dim light his face was hard to read, which she suspected was a skill he'd practiced too. "You think someone hired them. Like I hired you."

"I think everyone on this river is hired by someone," Mirabel said. "The question is whether any of us know all our employers."

Okon, at the wheel, made a low sound—not quite a laugh. "Ranger talks like a preacher."

"Preachers believe in something," Mirabel said. "I just count."

She counted now. Six aboard. Four visitors at the advance camp, per the last words anyone would hear from it. A thief somewhere ahead with a stolen map. A broker named Harrow whose colors Toma had gone stiff-faced about when she'd mentioned the name at dinner the first night—an involuntary reaction, a half-second flinch, the kind of tell she collected the way other people collected coins.

And a compass in her pocket pointing somewhere that wasn't north, deeper into this drowned country, as if it had appointments to keep.

The channel narrowed again as the light went long and copper. Fifty feet. Forty. The flooded trees leaned over them, and beneath the hull the bottom was invisible—ten feet down, twenty, nobody knew, and the sonar had been acting erratic since the flats. The water ahead bent westward around a bluff of black rock, and past the bend there was nothing visible but more water and more drowned forest and, high on a ridge above the treeline, a single column of smoke rising thin and straight into the windless sky.

Nobody said anything about the smoke. Everybody saw it.

Sef ordered full speed. The *Pelican*'s engine stumbled—that hitch again, every fourth or fifth revolution—and Toma, who had come up on deck without anyone noticing, stood at the rail with grease to his elbows and an expression Mirabel recognized from mirrors: a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

The bend came. The boat took it slow, hull groaning, and the smoke disappeared behind the bluff, and then the channel opened into a long straight reach of black water walled in by drowned timber, and at the far end of the reach, where the last light lay on the surface like spilled oil, Mirabel saw the thing that turned her unease into dread and nailed the dread down.

A boat. Tied off among the trees, half-sunk, listing hard to port. No lights. No movement. Its hull was scorched along the waterline.

It was flying no colors at all—which on this river meant the same thing as flying them torn down.

"Okon," Sef said quietly. "Kill the engine."

The diesel coughed and died, and in the sudden silence the only sounds were water dripping off the overhanging branches, and the far-off mutter of thunder building in the mountains, and—faint, carried flat across the black water from the direction of the sunken boat, or maybe from the dark shore beyond it, impossible to say—the single dry click of something being cocked.

Mirabel's hand found her rifle.

Ahead, the channel narrowed to less than thirty feet, and the dark came down like a lid.
