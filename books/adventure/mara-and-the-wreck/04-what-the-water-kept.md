## 4. What the Water Kept

The advance camp sat on a shelf of mud where the channel bent north, and from two hundred meters out Mirabel could already tell it was dead.

Not abandoned—dead. Abandoned camps had a particular look: gear stacked, tarps folded, the tidy geometry of people who meant to come back. This one had been opened up like a gutted fish. Canvas torn from the frames. Crates split along their seams, contents scattered across the mud in a fan that told her the looters had worked fast and hadn't cared who read their sign afterward.

"Kill the engine," she said.

Sef Adama cut it without argument. The boat drifted the last stretch in silence, sliding through water the color of steeped tea, and Mirabel stood at the bow with her weight low and her hand nowhere near her knife, because reaching for a weapon was the first thing an amateur did and the last thing a survivor needed to be seen doing.

Toma crouched at the stern with the boathook, ready to fend off. He'd barely spoken since the radio died mid-sentence yesterday—since Oyelaran's voice had chopped into static halfway through *contact made, they're not what we*—and now he stared at the camp like a man reading his own obituary.

"Nobody on the perimeter," Sef said quietly.

"Nobody living."

She let him absorb that. The mud shelf rose maybe a meter above the waterline, black and slick, stitched with drowned grass gone brown at the tips. Two tents still stood, half-collapsed, their poles snapped. A cook fire had been flooded out and relit twice since—the ash layers told her that—and the most recent burn was cold.

"Recent," she said. "A day. Maybe less."

"You can tell that from ash?"

"I can tell that from everything." She pointed without looking. "Boot traffic in the mud, four sets, heavy men, coming from the water side. They beached there—" a broken reed bed, flattened in a long scar "—came up fast, hit the camp while it was sleeping or split up. See how the tents are torn but nothing's burned? They wanted the contents, not the people scared off. And this—" she nodded at a dark stain soaked deep into the mud near the fire ring, flies working it "—is where somebody bled out or close to it."

Sef's jaw tightened. "Oyelaran?"

"Could be. Could be anyone." She stepped off the bow onto the mud, testing it, found it would hold, and moved up the slope in a crouch. Behind her she heard Sef follow, heavier, less practiced, and Toma holding the boat with the hook, breathing through his mouth.

Up close the camp was worse. Papers everywhere—or the shredded ghosts of papers, pulped by rain and trampled into the mud until the whole site looked like it had snowed wet gray rags. Survey notes. Charts. Whatever the advance team had been compiling about the channels upstream, someone had destroyed it with a thoroughness that went beyond theft. You don't shred a man's maps unless you don't want him finding his way home.

Her cracked compass hung at her neck under her shirt, and she was distantly aware of it, the way you're aware of a bruise. It didn't point north. It never had. But standing in the wreckage of other people's work, she felt it sitting against her sternum like a small hot coal, and she hated that she noticed.

"Toma," Sef called. "Check the supply crates."

"They're empty. All of them." Toma's voice came thin. "Even the ones marked medical."

Mirabel moved toward the largest tent, the command tent, its entrance flapped open like a wound. Inside: a folding table overturned, a lamp smashed, a cot stripped of its blanket. And in the corner, half under the collapsed frame—

Movement.

She had her knife out before her brain caught up, which annoyed her, because her rule was *never draw what you won't use*, and then she saw it was a man. Curled on his side, knees to chest, one hand still gripping the tent pole as if he'd been holding the sky up when it fell.

He was alive. Barely.

"Sef," she said, and didn't take her eyes off the man. "I've got a survivor."

* * *

His name was Pell, according to the tag sewn into his collar, and he was somewhere past delirium. Fever had him; infection had him worse—a gash along his ribs, bound with a strip of shirt that had soaked through and dried black. They got him onto the boat, onto the deck in the shade of the canopy, and Ilsa—no, not Ilsa yet, that came later, Mirabel corrected herself, *the woman wasn't in this story yet*—they got water into him and Sef's medical kit open, and for an hour the expedition's purpose shrank to keeping one man's heart beating.

Mirabel stayed back and searched.

It was what she was for. Sef had hired her to track, and tracking didn't stop because the trail got complicated. While Toma held Pell's head during the worst of the fever shakes and Sef cleaned the wound with hands that shook only a little, Mirabel went through the camp the way water goes through sand—patiently, into every crack.

What she found, in the lining of the overturned table, taped flat and missed by whoever had gutted the place:

A fragment of chart. Heavy paper, old, older than anything else in the camp. The edges were browned and soft, and the drafting on it was fine enough to be engraving—contour lines of a river system that no current map admitted existed, depth soundings in a cramped antique hand, and at the center, circled twice in faded red ink, a mark shaped like a ship.

Beneath it, in the same cramped hand, two words: *the Wreck.*

And lower still, almost worn away by handling, a third notation that stopped her breath:

*Deeds sealed in hold. Voss survey.*

Voss.

She stood very still, the fragment in both hands, and let the river sound fill her ears until it sounded like blood.

Her grandfather had been a surveyor. That much her mother had said, once, drunk, in the way you say things you immediately wish you'd swallowed. Her grandfather had drawn charts for the pre-flood authorities, back when the valley was dry land and the towns had names instead of depths. And her family's debt—the debt that had followed Mirabel since she was nineteen, the debt she couldn't name aloud because naming it meant admitting what it had been *for*, what her grandfather had sold or signed or surrendered to keep them all fed through the bad years—that debt had always been a shapeless thing. Weather. Fate. The desert taking its cut.

It had never been a piece of paper with her own name on it.

"Found something?"

Sef's voice behind her. She turned, and some instinct older than honesty made her angle the fragment away—but he'd already seen, or seen enough, because his eyes went to the paper and stayed there with an expression she couldn't file: not surprise. Recognition.

"That's why they came," he said quietly. "Not for money. For that."

"The thief stole a map."

"The thief stole *proof*." Sef crouched beside her, keeping his voice below Toma's hearing, below the fevered muttering from the boat. "Oyelaran didn't run off with payroll, Voss. He ran off with the only surviving record of where the Wreck sits and what's inside it. Pre-flood water rights. Deeds. Legal title to every liter that falls in this valley, sealed in a hold that's been underwater for sixty years." He exhaled. "Whoever holds those deeds owns the monsoon."

"And your expedition was looking for it because—"

"Because my backers want to find it first. Yes." He met her eyes, and for once didn't dress it up. "I'm not going to pretend this is science, Mirabel. It's salvage. It's a claim. And now Harrow's people have hit my advance camp and shredded my charts and left me one dying man, which tells me they intend for nobody else to reach it."

On the boat, Pell began to speak.

At first it was just sound—wet, rhythmic, the babble of a man whose filter had burned away. Then words surfaced, and Mirabel moved to the rail to listen, and wished she hadn't.

"*...the paper...*" Pell's eyes were open, fixed on nothing, on some middle distance where the dead walked. "*He took the paper, he took it, he said it was ours, he said it was owed, he kept saying owed, owed, like a prayer... the Wreck, you have to understand, the Wreck isn't a ship, it's a bank, it's a grave, it's—*" A coughing fit bent him double, Toma easing him down. "*They knew my grandfather's name. How did they know my grandfather's name?*"

Mirabel's hand went, without permission, to the compass at her neck.

*Owed.* The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Everyone in this story was chasing an inheritance. The thief, who believed the deeds were rightfully his. Harrow, who bought and sold other people's legacies by the kilo. Sef, whose backers wanted title to the rain itself. And her—the debt she carried, the nameless debt, suddenly had coordinates. Suddenly had a drafting style she recognized. Suddenly had her own surname inked on a chart fragment hidden in a murdered camp.

The theft had never been about coin. She understood that now, standing over a fevered man who was haunted by grandfathers, watching the brown water slide past. It was about inheritance—who owned the past, and who got to make the future pay for it.

Pell's hand shot out and caught her wrist. His grip was shockingly strong for a dying man, and his eyes found hers and focused, just for a second, lucid as glass.

"You have the compass," he whispered. "I saw it. In the tent. You have *his* compass."

Then the fever took him back under, and his hand slid from her wrist, and Mirabel Voss stood at the rail of a leaking boat in the drowned country, holding a stolen map fragment, knowing three things she couldn't un-know.

Her grandfather had surveyed these waters. Her compass pointed to the Wreck. And whatever she was owed—or owed—was waiting upstream, sealed in a hold, sixty years under.

Sef appeared beside her. He looked at the fragment in her hand, then at her face, and asked the question she'd been dreading since Callow's Landing.

"Voss. What exactly is it your family does own?"
