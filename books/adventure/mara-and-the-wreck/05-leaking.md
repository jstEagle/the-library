## 5. Leaking

Toma heard the boat before he saw the water.

That was the thing nobody told you about hulls — they talked. A sound hull hummed, low and even, a note you stopped hearing after a week aboard. This one had started to sing flat somewhere past the second channel marker, and by the time the squall line came up out of the west like a wall falling over, the *Cormorant* was carrying two notes instead of one, and the second note was wrong.

He was in the engine bay when it happened, which meant he felt it before anyone else. The bilge pump kicked on. Then it kicked on again forty seconds later. Then again, and this time it stayed on.

"Captain," he said into the deck tube, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, because that was a skill too, and he'd learned it from a man named Harrow. "We're taking water."

The reply took three seconds. "How much?"

"Enough."

Above him, boots crossed the deck — Sef Adama's heavy tread, then Mirabel Voss's lighter ones, quick, always quick, like she was afraid of being caught standing still. Toma wiped his hands on a rag that was already more grease than cloth and climbed the ladder into weather.

The sky had gone the color of wet slate. Ahead, the drowned forest stood hip-deep in brown water, dead cedars leaning at angles that made the whole world look like it was tilting. And beyond them, maybe two kilometers off, a shape broke the surface that wasn't a tree — pale stone, ridged, running with rain.

"Steeple," Mirabel said. She'd come up beside him without a sound. She did that. "There'll be a roof under us. Solid footing."

"You can tell that from here?"

"I can tell there's stone." Her eyes never left the horizon. "Stone doesn't float away."

Sef had the wheel, both hands on it, jaw set the way it got when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Can we make the far bank before it hits?"

"No," Toma said. He'd been doing his own arithmetic. "Not with the pump fighting for its life. We beach or we sink, Captain. Those are the options."

For a moment Sef looked like a man about to argue with the river itself. Then the first gust hit — not rain yet, just wind, but it shoved the *Cormorant* sideways a full meter and tore a loose halyard into a shrieking flag — and the argument ended.

"Voss. Get us to that steeple."

Mirabel moved to the bow without a word, and Toma watched her read the water the way other people read signs. She pointed left, then right, then held up a flat hand: slow. Sef followed her like she'd been born at his wheel, and Toma thought, not for the first time, that the ranger was worth every coin they were paying her and probably more, and that a man who could read water like that didn't stay hired help for long.

Then the rain arrived, all at once, like a door slamming, and there was no more time for thinking.

* * *

They got lines around the steeple's shoulders while the lightning walked the treeline behind them. It was ugly work — Toma chest-deep in floodwater, hauling rope over slick stone while the current tried to peel him off and hand him to the forest — but the old masonry held, and by full dark the *Cormorant* was lashed tight against the church's exposed crown, riding out the storm with the whole drowned building underneath it like a fist.

Inside the cabin, they pumped. That was the night, in total: pump, bail, listen to the hull talk, pump again.

Toma found the seam an hour after midnight, by feel, in the dark of the forward hold where the lantern wouldn't reach. It ran along a strake near the keel — not a crack, exactly. A separation. Two planks that had been fastened together once by men who knew their trade, now eased apart by years of strain and one bad season of monsoon, weeping river through the gap in a thin silver thread.

He put his thumb against it. Cold water pressed back, patient as anything alive.

"We can't fix this," he told Sef later, in the cabin, while Mirabel slept sitting up against the chart locker with her compass in her fist. Even asleep she held it. "Caulk might buy us days. Not weeks. We need a proper yard, and the nearest yard is Callow's Landing, and Callow's Landing is behind us."

Sef sat with his elbows on his knees, watching the lantern flame lean in the draft. "How many days?"

"If we don't push hard? Four. Five."

"And if we do?"

Toma shrugged. "Two. Maybe one, if the channels get mean."

Sef nodded slowly, and Toma recognized the look — the surveyor's look, the man weighing distances against fuel against time, except now the equation had water in it, rising. "The Wreck," Sef said quietly. "It's four days' run if the weather holds."

"The weather isn't holding."

"No." Sef rubbed his face. "No, it isn't."

In the corner, Mirabel opened one eye. Toma hadn't been sure she was asleep at all. "Then we find another way," she said. "There's always another way through flooded country. You just have to ask the right person."

"Who?" Sef asked.

"That's tomorrow's problem," she said, and closed her eye again, and Toma understood that the conversation was over whether the captain liked it or not.

* * *

He took the middle watch alone, which was how he wanted it.

Rain hammered the cabin roof and the river breathed against the hull, and Toma sat in the dark with the pump handle across his knees and let himself think about Harrow, which he tried not to do, ever, because thinking about Harrow was like picking at a scab that went all the way down to the bone.

Dessin Harrow ran claims the way spiders run webs. Not a thief — worse, in Toma's opinion, because a thief at least had to be brave. Harrow bought things. Debts, routes, silences, men. He'd bought Toma at nineteen, when Toma was a deckhand with a sick mother and a ledger full of small numbers that added up to one large hole. Three seasons Toma had worked Harrow's boats, running survey crews up channels that weren't on any legal map, marking where the old boundaries used to sit before the flood redrew everything. Marking where the paper lay sleeping.

Because that was the secret at the bottom of the drowned country, the one Harrow had built a business on: the flood hadn't destroyed the old records. It had buried them. Water rights, land grants, deeds signed and sealed before the rivers rose — all of it locked away in safes and strongrooms and sealed holds across the submerged valley, waiting for whoever was patient enough, and ruthless enough, to go down and get them.

Harrow had been patient for twenty years. Ruthless he'd had from the start.

And Toma had been his hands, until the night a survey crew went upriver and didn't come back, and Toma looked at what was left of the money in his account and understood exactly what it had cost, and walked out. Or thought he had. Men like Harrow didn't sell you your freedom; they just moved you to a longer leash.

Which was why, when Sef Adama had offered him a berth on an expedition chasing a stolen map into the deep drowned country, Toma should have said no. Should have smelled it. A map to the Wreck — Harrow had whispered about the Wreck for years, the pre-flood barge, the sealed hold, the mother lode of paper. If Sef had the map fragment, then Harrow wanted the map fragment, and everyone aboard this leaking boat was already swimming in someone else's web.

Toma had taken the job anyway. Double pay. His mother's ledger again, always the ledger.

He looked over at Mirabel, asleep against the chart locker, rain-light flickering across her face. She trusted nothing and no one — he'd watched her check the mooring lines twice tonight, watched her sleep with a weapon in reach and a compass in her fist — and somehow that made her easier to be around than trusting people. You didn't owe a woman like that your story. You just owed her honest work.

But Sef. Sef trusted him. Called him by name. Left him alone with the charts.

Toma turned the pump handle over in his hands and hated himself in the quiet, professional way he'd gotten good at.

Because there was a radio in the stern locker, a shortwave set Sef kept for emergencies, and twice since Callow's Landing Toma had woken in the night with the certain knowledge that he could send one transmission — coordinates, heading, the words *they have the map* — and Harrow would clear his mother's debt before dawn. One message. Thirty seconds. And then whatever came up that river afterward would be Harrow's men, and Sef and the ranger would learn what Toma's price was, and it would be paid in their blood.

He hadn't sent it. He told himself that counted for something.

He told himself that every night, and every night it sounded thinner.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping, the squall spent, the river running high and fast with everything it had swallowed overnight. Toma was bailing the last of the seepage when Mirabel appeared at the hold hatch, crouched, and looked at the seam for a long moment without touching it.

"Caulked before," she said. "Old work. Somebody patched this hull and did it cheap."

"Sef says the *Cormorant*'s been in the family thirty years."

"Thirty years ago somebody loved this boat." She straightened. "Now it's a tool. Tools leak."

She said it without judgment, which was somehow worse, and climbed out toward the deck. Toma followed her up into the washed morning air and found Sef at the rail, studying the drowned forest through cracked binoculars, and Ilsa—

Ilsa Renn was already aboard.

Toma froze with one foot on the ladder. The poler stood amidships like she'd grown there, lean and sun-browned, a long push-pole across her shoulders, a dugout tied off astern that hadn't been there last night. She was watching him watch her, and her expression held the mild amusement of someone who found landlubbers endlessly entertaining.

"She came with the light," Sef said, not lowering the binoculars. "Paddled up while we were sleeping. Says she knows a route to the Wreck that avoids the main channels."

"Do you trust her?"

"I trust that she found us in a squall, in the dark, on a stretch of river she claims is empty." Sef finally lowered the glasses. His face was tired and set. "Nobody finds this boat by accident, Toma. So either she's the luckiest woman on the water, or she was looking. Either way, she wants to talk terms, and I'd rather hear them above board than wonder about them below it."

Ilsa smiled at Toma like she'd heard every word. "Morning," she said. "You're taking water. I can hear it from fifty meters — your hull sings flat."

"So I've been told."

"Fixable. Nothing's unfixable on the water. Question is what things cost." She swung the pole down and rested it against the rail, and her eyes went around the boat — the engine housing, the fuel drums, the chart locker where the stolen map lived, and last, longest, Mirabel Voss, who stared back with the flat patience of a snake on a warm rock.

"Everyone aboard has a price," Ilsa said pleasantly. "Mine's simple. Cut of whatever's in the Wreck, fair shares, and I take you through water Harrow's boats can't follow. His drafts are too deep for the backchannels. Yours barely floats as it is." She patted the rail almost fondly. "So. Do we deal, or do I paddle home and you find out what the main channel costs?"

Toma looked at Sef. Sef looked at Mirabel. And Mirabel, arms folded, said:

"How do we know you're not Harrow's?"

Ilsa laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised, like the question had actually pleased her. "Oh, I've worked for Harrow," she said. "Everyone on this river has worked for Harrow. Ask your deckhand."

The morning went very still. Toma felt the words land in his chest like ballast dropped from a height.

"Ilsa," he started, and couldn't finish, because there was nothing to finish with. She only shrugged, unbothered, watching him with those bright river-reading eyes.

"He hasn't told you? Well." She picked her pole back up. "Now everybody aboard has shown their price. Shall we talk shares?"

Sef's gaze came around to Toma slowly, and Toma stood on his own deck, in his own boots, and waited for the question he'd been dreading since Callow's Landing.

"Toma," Sef said quietly. "What is she talking about?"
