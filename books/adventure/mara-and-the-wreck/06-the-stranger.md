## 6. The Stranger

The channel died at dawn.

Not gradually — not the way water usually gives up on itself, thinning into mud and then into suggestion. This one simply ended, a wall of collapsed bank across the current like a fist slammed down in the middle of a sentence. Behind it, the flooded forest went on for miles, black cypress crowns standing in still water, and somewhere past that, if Sef's fragment of map was worth anything, the advance camp.

Mirabel stood at the bow with her boots braced against the wet planking and watched the blockage settle. Whole trees had come down the channel in the night, tangled root to root, packed with silt until they'd made land where there was no land. The water backed up behind it, brown and patient, already looking for another way through.

"That's that," Toma said from the engine well. He said it the way men say things when they're hoping to be contradicted.

Sef didn't contradict him. He stood at the wheel with his jaw working, turning the map fragment over in his hands as though the paper might have changed overnight. "There's a backwater," he said finally. "The surveyor's notes mention it. South fork, maybe two kilometers down, cuts around this whole collapse."

"Surveyor's notes," Mirabel repeated. "From the man who stole your map and ran."

"He wrote those before he ran." Sef's voice was flat. "He was good at his job right up until he wasn't."

She let that sit. She was getting better at letting things sit — it was most of what tracking taught you. You could want to move so badly your teeth ached, and the trail would still only open when it opened. Impatience was just another way of losing.

They found the south fork an hour later, and it was wrong.

Mirabel knew it was wrong the way she knew weather coming: not by any single sign but by the sum of them. The water moved too slow for its width. The banks were soft, undercut, hung with vines that should have been torn away by current. And there were no birds. Two days upriver she'd gotten used to the racket of herons and whistling ducks, the constant commentary of the drowned forest. Here there was nothing. The silence had weight, like the air in a room where someone has just stopped breathing.

"Doesn't feel right," Toma said, echoing her without knowing it.

"Maps don't feel," Sef said. But he brought the boat in slower than he needed to.

They were maybe four hundred meters into the fork when the water began to pull.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the horror of it. No whirlpool, no roar — just a gentle lean, the bow swinging a few degrees off true, the current suddenly interested in them. Mirabel watched a branch drift past going faster than the boat, then faster than it should have been able to go anywhere, and understood.

"It's draining," she said. "This whole fork drains somewhere. We're standing in the throat of it."

"Reverse," Sef said, and Toma threw the engine hard over, and the boat came about in a wide stupid arc while the current took hold of the stern like a hand closing.

What happened next happened fast and badly. The engine caught, coughed, caught again. The stern swung toward the near bank where the water folded over a submerged shelf, and Mirabel saw the boil line ahead of them — a seam of white no wider than a rope, marking where the bottom dropped away into whatever swallowed this channel every monsoon. Sef shouted something. Toma shouted something else. The boat wallowed, shipping water over the gunwale in a cold slap, and Mirabel grabbed the painter coil and looked around for anything to throw, anything to tie off to, and found nothing but water and more water.

Then there was a voice.

Not from the boat. From off to the left, upstream, out of the screen of drowned willows — a woman's voice, unhurried, almost bored.

"Pole's under your feet. Left side. Now."

Mirabel looked down. A barge pole lay along the port gunwale, one end wedged under the rail, the other trailing in the current — thrown, or placed, or simply *there*, the way things appeared in dreams. She didn't think about it. She hauled it up, drove it down into the water, and felt it strike bottom with a jolt that ran up both arms.

"Hold it there. Don't push. Hold."

She held. The pole bent. The current leaned on the boat and the boat leaned on the pole and the pole leaned on the riverbed, and for three seconds nothing moved at all except Mirabel's breath, which came in ragged pulls.

Then the current shifted — some deep valve opening or closing below — and the pull slackened, and the boat slid sideways out of the throat of the fork and into dead calm water among the willows.

A longboat sat there. She hadn't seen it until now, which should have been impossible; it was twelve feet of dark wood riding low, and the woman poling it stood motionless as a heron, one foot forward, the pole planted, watching them with an expression of mild professional interest, the way a surgeon looks at a wound she's already closed.

"You came in on the drain," the woman said. "Nobody comes in on the drain twice."

* * *

Her name was Ilsa Renn, and she wanted a cut.

They rafted the boats together in the lee of the willows while the fork behind them finished its argument with itself — a low grinding sound, like the river clearing its throat, and then a section of bank the size of a house slid into the channel and was gone. Toma watched it go with his hands flat on the gunwale, steadying himself or praying; Mirabel couldn't tell which.

Ilsa Renn sat on the thwart of her longboat with the pole across her knees and ate dried fish without offering any. She was perhaps forty, hard-used, with a face like a river stone — everything soft worn off long ago — and eyes the color of tea left standing. She wore a canvas coat gone green with mold at the seams and she looked at each of them in turn the way Mirabel herself looked at tracks: reading, weighing, filing away.

"So," she said. "Broker's colors on your fuel drums, a stolen map in the chief's pocket, and a tracker who carries her compass in her hand instead of her pocket like she doesn't trust it." She bit off another piece of fish. "Which means either you're rich or you're doomed, and I do business with both."

"We haven't hired you," Sef said.

"No," Ilsa agreed. "You've been rescued by me. Different thing. Costs more."

Mirabel almost smiled. She kept it off her face, because the first rule of staying invisible was never showing anyone what amused you, but it was close. There was a particular kind of person who survived out here alone, and Mirabel had spent years being one of them, and she recognized the breed the way a dog recognizes another dog. Ilsa Renn answered to nobody, slept in shifts of one, and had priced her own life so carefully that she'd never once had to spend it.

"What kind of cut?" Sef asked, because he was the kind of man who negotiated even while the bank was falling into the river.

"Ten percent of whatever's at the end of your map."

"There's no map."

Ilsa looked at him. She looked at him for a long time, chewing, and Sef held the look, and lost it. His hand drifted toward his coat pocket — a small movement, unconscious, fatal — and Ilsa nodded once, satisfied, the way you nod at a card that confirms your read.

"Ten percent," she said, "of whatever's at the end of the map in your pocket. Or you can go find your own way through the backwaters, and I'll pole on, and we'll see which of us the river likes better." She spat a fish bone into the water. "I know every channel between here and the high ground. I know which ones are real and which ones the flood made up last season. Your surveyor knew too. That's why he's alive, probably. Are you?"

Mirabel spoke before she meant to. "You knew him? The surveyor?"

"Ilsa Renn knows everyone who moves on this water." She said it without pride, the way you'd say *the rain falls*. "Skinny fellow. Soft hands. Bought passage from me six weeks back, headed downriver, paid in broker scrip." Her tea-colored eyes came around to Mirabel and stayed there. "He asked me about the Wreck."

The word landed in the boat like a dropped tool. Toma went still. Sef's face did something complicated and settled into nothing.

"And?" Mirabel said.

"And I told him what I tell everyone. There's no Wreck. There's a story about a Wreck, and stories float better than barges." She stood, planted her pole, and pushed off from the willows with one smooth motion, her longboat sliding alongside theirs. "But stories pay the same as cargo if you carry them far enough. Ten percent. Yes or no."

Sef looked at Mirabel. It was the first time he'd done that — turned to her for a decision rather than giving her one — and she understood that he was more rattled than he was showing, that the rescue had cost him something in front of his crew, and that he wanted her to be the one who said yes so it wouldn't be his weakness.

"Yes," Mirabel said.

* * *

Ilsa Renn read the water like scripture, and like scripture it was mostly interpretation.

She led them west out of the dead fork into a maze of backwaters that no chart acknowledged — channels that split and rejoined, that passed beneath living roots arched like cathedral ribs, that narrowed until the willows brushed both gunwales at once. She never consulted anything. She'd stand in her longboat with the pole planted, head tilted slightly, listening — actually listening, Mirabel realized, to the water itself. Where it gurgled under the hull she read depth. Where it whispered through reeds she read current. Once she stopped them entirely for ten minutes because, she said, the water on their left had changed its mind, and sure enough, twenty minutes later a raft of debris came barreling down a side slough they would have crossed directly.

"How long have you lived out here?" Mirabel asked her, on the second evening, when they'd tied up in a stand of dead oaks and the mosquitoes rose off the water in columns.

"Longer than the flood. Shorter than the drought." Ilsa was mending a crack in her pole with tarred twine, working by touch in the failing light. "You're desert people, all three of you. I can tell. Desert people think water is one thing. It's not. It's a thousand animals, and every one of them is hungry."

"Is that how you see it?"

"That's how it is." She pulled the twine tight with her teeth. "Your surveyor saw it that way too, near the end. Came out here soft and came back different. Kept asking me what the water *keeps*." She glanced up. "Strange question. Water doesn't keep. Water spends."

Mirabel turned her cracked compass over in her palm, the way she did a hundred times a day without thinking. The needle swung, settled, pointed — not north. It hadn't pointed north in years, though she'd told no one, not even herself in plain words. It pointed somewhere ahead of them, into the drowned country, steady as a debt.

Ilsa's eyes flicked down to it. Stayed a moment too long.

"That's not a desert compass," she said.

"It points home," Mirabel said, which was the lie she'd built her whole life on, and Ilsa Renn looked at her with the mild professional interest of a surgeon examining a wound she hadn't closed yet, and said nothing at all.

* * *

On the third morning they came around a bend of living cane and Ilsa raised one hand, flat, and they all stopped.

Smoke. Not camp smoke — cookfire smoke, banked and old, but recent enough. And beneath it, faint on the still air, the sound of an engine idling low, the way men idle engines when they don't want to be heard but can't bear to be blind.

"Backwater," Ilsa breathed, and poled them off the main channel into a slough so narrow the cane scraped the paint off both boats. They waited there, engines off, breath shallow, while the sound grew and then passed: a flat-bottomed workboat moving upriver, flying no flag, carrying four men and too much fuel to be honest about.

Mirabel counted them without lifting her binoculars above the cane line. Four men, armed, moving fast, headed the same direction they were headed.

"Claim-jumpers," Toma whispered. "Harrow's men."

"You know that how?" Sef asked sharply.

Toma's mouth opened and closed. Mirabel watched him not answer, and filed it away next to everything else she'd filed away since Callow's Landing — the broker's colors on the drums, the route Sef claimed was secret, the surveyor who'd known exactly where to run.

Ilsa poled them onward in silence for an hour. When the sound of the engine had long faded, she finally spoke, not turning around, her voice pitched for Mirabel alone across the narrow water between the boats.

"Whatever's at the end of your map," she said, "there are people willing to kill for it, and one of them is sitting in your crew."

She planted the pole and pushed, and the longboat slid forward into the drowned forest, and Mirabel followed, holding her compass, following a needle that had never once pointed home.
