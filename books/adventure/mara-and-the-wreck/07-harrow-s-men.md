## 7. Harrow's Men

Toma saw the smoke first, a gray thread standing off the eastern bank where no camp should be. He said nothing about it. He'd been saying nothing for six days, and by now the silence had grown its own weight, a stone he carried behind his ribs that got heavier every time Sef Adama laughed at something Ilsa Renn said or Mirabel Voss cleaned her rifle with the patience of a woman who had nothing to hide.

The thread of smoke was Harrow's men. Toma knew it the way you know your own handwriting. He'd sold them the route three nights ago in Callow's Landing, in the back room of the fuel depot, for four hundred scrip and a promise: *you don't touch the girl with the compass, and you don't touch me.* He'd told himself it wasn't betrayal. It was insurance. A man who worked boats on this river learned early that everybody aboard was going to drown someday, and the smart ones bought their life jackets in advance.

He watched the smoke and steered the boat true, and hated himself in the quiet professional way he'd gotten good at.

"You see that?" Ilsa asked from the bow, her pole resting across her knees like a sleeping snake.

"Brush fire," Toma said. "Dry season residue."

"It's monsoon season," she said.

"Residue doesn't check the calendar."

She looked at him a moment too long, then turned back to the water. That was the thing about Ilsa—she read water the way priests read scripture, but she read people almost as well, and Toma felt her attention pass over him like a searchlight sweeping a hull, finding nothing it could prove.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a drowned granary, the roofline breaking the current just enough to give them slack water. Sef rationed out rice and dried fish, and Mirabel took first watch without being asked, which she always did, as if sleep were a debt she refused to owe anyone.

Toma lay awake listening to the river. It talked all night here—water worrying at the drowned doorframes, the groan of timber settling—and under it, fainter, another sound. An engine. Not theirs. Theirs was a two-stroke with a stutter in it he could diagnose blind. This one was smoother, a broker boat, the kind Harrow's syndicate ran up from the delta: muffled exhaust, shallow draft, running dark.

He sat up. Across the embers, Sef was already awake too, and the old man's eyes in the firelight were not surprised. That frightened Toma more than the engine did.

"They've been with us since the Landing," Sef said quietly. "Haven't they."

Toma's mouth went dry. "I don't know what—"

"Don't." Sef's voice didn't rise. It never rose. It just got closer, the way deep water does. "I hired a thief to find a thief, and I hired a deckhand because he was cheap and knew the channel. I'm not a fool, Toma. I'm a man who can't afford better. There's a difference, and I live on it."

Mirabel came out of the dark with her rifle slung, moving like the night owed her money. "Two boats," she said. "Behind the granary. Running lights off. They're waiting for us to move."

"Waiting," Sef said, "or herding."

Nobody answered him. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the black water, the smooth engine idled down to nothing, and the silence after it was worse than the sound.

* * *

The skirmish, when it came, came at the hour when even honest men are stupidest—the dead stretch before dawn.

Toma heard the grapnel before anyone: a soft iron kiss against the hull, aft, where the fuel drums were lashed. Then a shape over the transom, quick and practiced, and the flat crack of Mirabel's rifle splitting the night into before and after.

What followed lasted maybe ninety seconds and felt like a season. Ilsa put her pole through a boarding line and levered a man off the gunwale into the river. Sef fired the flare gun point-blank into the second boat's canopy, and the canvas went up like a struck match, lighting the whole channel orange—enough for Toma to see the colors on their hulls. Green and white chevrons. Broker colors. Harrow's house flag, painted neat as a church sign.

And enough for him to see the lead man's face, lit by his own burning boat, look straight at Toma and nod. Not a threat. A confirmation. *Good work. Stay bought.*

Then they were gone, sliding back into the dark, dragging their burning shame with them, and the channel was quiet except for the hiss of the river swallowing sparks.

Toma turned and found the fuel drums slashed. Both of them. Diesel bleeding out into the bilge in slow black ribbons, the smell of it thick as confession.

"We've got maybe half a tank," he said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Maybe less."

Sef stood over the ruined drums, flare pistol still in hand, and did not look at Toma. That was the worst part. The not-looking. It was the look you gave a man you'd already decided about.

"Ilsa," Sef said. "How far to the next fuel cache?"

"Three days poling, if the channels hold." She wrung river water from her sleeve. "If they don't, we walk, and there's nowhere to walk to."

"Then we pole." Sef finally turned, and his eyes found Toma the way a hook finds a fish. "But first, we settle accounts. Because those men knew where we'd beach. They knew our watch rotation—they hit us on the changeover, when Mirabel was walking the perimeter and I was below. Somebody told them."

The fire had burned down to red teeth. Everyone was looking at Toma. Even the river seemed to go quiet, holding its breath around the drowned granary, waiting.

Toma thought about lying. He was good at lying; he'd been doing it since Callow's Landing, telling himself stories with clean endings. But Mirabel had come to stand beside Sef, and she wasn't looking at him with anger. She was looking at him with recognition—the flat, tired look of someone who had carried a secret debt herself and knew exactly what the stone weighed.

So he told it. All of it, ugly and plain. The back room at the fuel depot. The four hundred scrip. The promise that nobody would touch him if he passed along the route, the channels, the camps. How he'd told himself it was insurance, how he'd known it was betrayal, how he'd done it anyway because a drowning man will grab anything that floats, even if it's somebody else's head.

When he finished, the silence was so complete he could hear the diesel finishing its bleed into the bilge.

"Four hundred," Ilsa said at last. She spat over the side. "You priced us at four hundred."

"It wasn't about the money," Toma said, and heard immediately what a lie that was, and corrected it, because the night had burned away his talent for anything else. "It was. That's the truth. It was about the money and being scared and wanting to be the one who survives. I sold the route. I didn't sell you. I swear on the boat—I didn't tell them about the Wreck. They think we're surveying. They think it's salvage."

"Do they?" Mirabel said softly. "Because they're following us anyway."

That landed in him like cold water. He hadn't thought of it. He hadn't let himself think of it. If Harrow's men believed it was salvage, why shadow the boat day after day? Why herd instead of take?

"Because whatever we're really looking for," Sef said slowly, "they want us to find it. And then they want to be there when we do."

* * *

They argued while the sky went gray at the edges. Sef wanted to put Toma ashore at the next solid ground—a day's poling north, a mudbank, a long walk to nowhere. Ilsa said leave him on any bank and Harrow's men would fish him out inside an hour, and everything he knew would walk back to the broker double-armed. Mirabel said nothing for a long time, sitting on the gunwale with her rifle across her knees, watching the water run.

Finally she spoke.

"He sold the route. He didn't sell the map, because he doesn't know about the map. Right?"

Toma shook his head. "I never saw any map. I swear it."

"And he can't un-sell what he knows. But he knows their boats, their colors, their habits. Better than any of us." She looked at Sef. "Every man aboard has a price. You said it yourself, or near enough. The question isn't whether he's bought. The question is whether he stays bought by us from here on."

"That's not trust," Sef said.

"No," Mirabel agreed. "It's arithmetic. Trust is for afterward, if we live."

Sef looked at Toma for a long time. The old man's face in the gray light was unreadable as the flooded country itself—all channels and drowned doors, no way to know which one opened onto solid ground.

"You'll pole first shift," Sef said finally. "Every day. Until my arms give out and yours with them. And when we find what we're looking for—if we find it—you'll be the last man to touch it. That's not forgiveness. That's the price list."

Toma nodded, and found his eyes stinging, which embarrassed him more than the confession had. "Yes, sir."

Ilsa snorted and shouldered her pole. "Sentiment," she said. "The river will sort him if he slips again. It sorts everyone."

* * *

They poled out of the granary slack as the sun came up properly, the drowned country opening around them like a drowned book—rooftops, treetops, the pale crown of a church they'd have to beach on tonight if the squalls came. Half a tank of diesel. Three days of channel. Broker boats somewhere behind them, patient as interest accruing.

Toma took first shift, as promised, driving the pole into the brown water and hauling, driving and hauling, until his shoulders screamed. And it was on that shift, alone at the bow with the others sleeping, that he saw it: a green-and-white chevron painted on a drowned fencepost, fresh, the paint still bright. A marker. A trail of them, spaced like breadcrumbs, leading not behind them but ahead—upriver, deeper into the flooded country, toward whatever waited at the end of the channel.

Harrow's men weren't following them anymore.

They were showing them the way.

Toma stood with the pole dripping in his hands, and understood that the price of his betrayal hadn't been paid yet—it had only been posted. Whatever lay ahead, at the end of that trail of bright paint, they were all walking into it together, and he had drawn the map.

He woke Sef, and showed him the fencepost, and watched the old man's face go carefully blank.

"How far," Sef said quietly, "to the fork in the channel?"

Ilsa, already awake, already reading the water, answered for both of them.

"Half a day," she said. "And every finger of it marked."
