## 8. Charts in the Dark

Sef didn't sleep that night. I knew because I didn't either, and because his lamp burned past midnight in the wheelhouse, a yellow smear against the black glass of the windows. The boat lay tied to a dead cottonwood, half-sunk and holding on out of habit, and the river breathed around us in the dark like something enormous deciding whether to swallow.

I told myself I wasn't going to knock.

I told myself that for two hours.

The rain had stopped around dusk and hadn't started again, which in monsoon country is not mercy but a held breath. Mosquitoes found every seam in my jacket. Somewhere downstream a frog chorus rose and fell like a town cheering something terrible. And through it all, that lamp in the wheelhouse, steady as a pulse.

When I finally climbed the ladder, Sef looked up from the table like he'd been waiting the whole time. Maybe he had. He'd laid out an oilcloth, and on the oilcloth sat a tin box no bigger than a Bible, dented at one corner, its lock already sprung.

"Couldn't sleep either," he said.

"I sleep fine."

"You've been standing on my ladder for a full minute deciding whether to lie to me about it." He pushed a stool out with his boot. "Sit down, Voss. You should see this before we go any further. Before anyone else does."

That last part was the hook, and he knew it. I sat.

* * *

The map fragment was wrapped in waxed linen, and when he unrolled it across the oilcloth, the first thing I felt was not curiosity. It was recognition — the ugly kind, the kind that arrives in your stomach before your head can argue with it.

The sheet was old survey stock, heavy paper gone soft at the edges, water-stained along one margin where someone had once dropped it or waded with it. Ink in two colors: black for the land as it was, red for the land as it would be. A flood line drawn in confident strokes. Channels projected where channels did not yet exist. And in the lower corner, small and precise, a drafting mark — a compass rose with one needle bent, drawn so fine you'd miss it unless you'd grown up watching the same hand draw it a hundred times over a kitchen table while the wind came off the flats and the lamp guttered and a man who smelled of graphite and pipe smoke said, *always sign your work, Mira-girl, even when nobody's looking. Especially then.*

A bent needle. A broken north.

My grandfather's mark.

"Sef," I said, and my voice came out flat, which took work, "where did Adama's surveyor get this?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He leaned back, arms crossed, watching me the way he watched the channel ahead — reading me for snags. "Kellis pulled it from a deed-box in a broker's office in Harrow's territory. Paid for it with money that wasn't his and a face nobody will ever trust again. Then he ran upriver instead of back to us, which tells you he understood what he was carrying better than I did when I hired him."

"And what is he carrying?"

Sef turned the fragment toward the lamp. His finger traced the red flood line up to a point where the ink changed — where the draftsman's hand shifted, steadier, older, the lines drawn with a certainty the younger hand couldn't match. There, boxed in red, a notation in a cramped cipher that I also knew, because I'd spent one whole winter of my childhood cracking it out of boredom and been whipped for it: coordinates. A hull position. And beneath it, in plain letters this time, three words that made the boat seem very quiet around us.

*Sealed. Pre-flood. Intact.*

"The Wreck," Sef said. "Not salvage. Not a story. A barge that went aground in the last weeks before the water came, carrying everything the old water authority couldn't bear to leave behind. Deeds. Adjudications. Sealed grants going back ninety years — before the flood, before the compacts, before anybody decided who owned what out here." He tapped the notation. "If those papers are intact, they don't just describe water rights. They *are* water rights. Older than anything Harrow's syndicate holds. Older than the settlements. Whoever walks off that barge with them owns the rivers."

"Or burns them," I said, before I could stop myself.

He looked at me then, really looked, and I understood I'd said too much. But there was no taking it back, and Sef Adama was not a man who let a thing like that pass without turning it over.

"Burning them would be a crime," he said slowly. "Against every claim-holder on this water. Against the towns downstream."

"Would it?" I kept my voice level. "Or would it be the first honest act anyone's committed on this river in a hundred years?"

The lamp hissed. Outside, the frogs went silent all at once, then resumed, as if something had walked between them.

"You've heard of the Wreck before," Sef said.

Everyone had heard of the Wreck. It was a story deckhands told, a rumor brokers denied, a shape under the water that every drowned-country pilot swore they'd almost seen. That wasn't what he was asking, and we both knew it.

"I've heard of the man who drew that map," I said.

* * *

Here is what I had never told anyone, and what I now watched myself telling Sef Adama in a lamplit wheelhouse on a dead river, because the alternative was lying to a man whose boat I needed, and I have never been able to lie well to people who feed me:

My grandfather was a surveyor. Not the romantic kind — not the explorer with the brass instruments and the memoirs. The other kind. The kind governments hire when they intend to take something and want the taking to look like arithmetic. He surveyed the salt flats. He surveyed the highlands. And in the years before the flood, when the water authority began quietly buying up rights along every channel that would matter once the valleys drowned, he was the man who drew the maps that made the buying possible.

He didn't think of it that way. I know that. He thought of himself as a craftsman. He drew beautiful maps — I'm telling you, that fragment on the table was beautiful, the lettering alone was worth study — and beauty lets men believe their work is clean. He signed everything. Even when nobody was looking. *Especially then.*

And when the water came, faster than the projections said — his own projections, I later learned, which he had adjusted downward because the authority asked him to, because a lower flood line meant cheaper land — he did one thing with his hands that wasn't arithmetic. He built a compass.

Not a compass that pointed north. A compass that pointed toward the thing he couldn't undo. Toward the coordinates he'd drafted into the sealed record, the barge, the deeds, the whole weight of what the water had been sent to bury. He gave it to my mother. My mother gave it to me the year she died, along with a debt she wouldn't explain and a sentence I've carried longer than the compass: *We owe the river more than we were paid.*

I used to think she meant guilt. Some abstract inheritance of shame, the kind you can set down by being decent.

Looking at the fragment under Sef's lamp, I understood she'd meant it literally. We owed the river. There was paper somewhere that said exactly how much, and whose name was on it, and mine was close enough to touch.

* * *

"Voss." Sef's voice came from far away, then near again. "You've gone white as the salt you came off of. Talk to me."

"My grandfather drew that chart." I put my finger beside the bent-needle mark, not touching it. "This is his hand. This cipher is his. And this—" I moved to the older, steadier section, the red-boxed coordinates, and stopped, because saying it aloud would make it true in front of a witness. "This is his master work. The one they buried him with honors for."

Sef sat very still. To his credit, he didn't ask the obvious question — *what did your grandfather do* — immediately. He asked the worse one.

"What do you owe, Mirabel?"

"Not money." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's the joke. Everyone assumes it's money. A ranger working salt contracts for hazard pay, eating dust for coin — sure, she's paying down a debt. Numbers. Ledger stuff." I shook my head. "It's not numbers. It's a name. My family's name is on paper in that hold, Sef. Maybe as grantor. Maybe as guarantor. Maybe as the hand that made the theft legal. I don't know which. I've spent eleven years not knowing which, and I told myself that was discipline, staying ignorant, staying invisible—"

"It was fear," Sef said. Not cruelly. Just accurately, the way he read water.

"It was both. They're usually the same thing."

He turned back to the fragment. For a long moment neither of us spoke, and the river breathed, and the lamp painted our shadows on the wet glass like two more passengers neither of us had agreed to carry.

"If your name is on those deeds," he said at last, "then you're not crew on this expedition anymore. You're a claimant. Possibly the only living claimant." He looked up. "Do you understand what that makes you worth? Harrow has syndicate lawyers who'd trade a fleet for a signature with standing. Every claim-jumper on this water would cut your throat for the right to forge it. And if the papers say what I think they might say—"

"Don't."

"—then you don't owe a debt, Voss. You *hold* one. Somebody owes *you.*"

I stood up. The stool scraped, loud in the small room, and outside something splashed — a fish, a gator, nothing — and I stood there with my hands flat on the table over my grandfather's beautiful, damning lines, and I felt the thing I had worn as armor for eleven years shift against my skin.

Invisibility had been a choice. That's what I'd always believed. Nobody knows my face, nobody knows my name, nobody can collect what I owe because nobody knows I exist. It had felt like freedom. It had even, some nights, felt like virtue.

But a person doesn't choose invisibility. A person chooses hiding. And hiding is just debt with the bill turned face-down.

"Mira-girl," my grandfather used to say, inking in a channel that would drown forty families, "always sign your work."

He had. Ninety years of paper down there in the dark, signed, witnessed, sealed. And somewhere in it, in a hand I knew better than my own, maybe mine.

* * *

"We go on," Sef said. "You understand that. Whatever's in that hold — I've got investors, I've got a charter, I've got Kellis to find. I can't turn back because the cargo turns out to be personal."

"I wouldn't ask you to."

"But I need to know which way you'll jump when we open it." He said it plainly, no threat in it, which somehow made it heavier. "Because if you're planning to take those deeds and sell them — to Harrow, to anyone — I'd rather learn it now than at gunpoint. And if you're planning to burn them—"

"Then you'd rather learn that now too."

"Then I'd have to decide what I do about it." He held my eyes. "I like you, Voss. That's not nothing on this river. But I didn't hire a conscience. I hired a tracker."

"No," I said. "You hired my grandfather's compass, whether you know it or not. You just didn't know you were hiring the whole family."

His jaw tightened. "What's that supposed to mean?"

I looked down at the fragment one more time. At the flood line drawn low. At the red channels that became real. At the bent needle in the corner, signing the drowning of a country in the most elegant hand I will ever see.

"Nothing," I said. "Get some sleep, Sef. Tomorrow we run the narrows, and Ilsa says the water's rising faster than the charts allow for."

I climbed down the ladder into the dark, and stood a while at the rail with the compass in my pocket, its bent needle straining against its housing like a dog at a door. Pointing south and east. Pointing, as it always had, toward the Wreck. Toward the paper. Toward whatever my family had done and whatever was left of it, sealed in a hull under a hill of water, waiting for someone to come and settle accounts.

Eleven years I'd carried a debt I couldn't name.

Now it had a shape. It was rectangular, water-stained, drawn in two colors of ink, and it was three days' poling upriver.

Behind me, in the wheelhouse, Sef's lamp went out. Ahead, far off through the drowned trees, lightning flickered without sound — the storm system Ilsa had been muttering about all evening, coming down out of the hills toward the narrows.

And from the bow of the boat, faint but unmistakable, Toma's voice, low and urgent, talking to someone on a radio that was supposed to be dead.
