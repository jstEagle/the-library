## 9. Deadfall Channel

Ilsa Renn had poled the drowned country for eleven years, and in that time she'd learned one law above all others: the water never forgets where it's been hurt.

That was the trouble with the Deadfall. Every channel out here had a history, but the Deadfall wore its scars on the surface like a confession. She could read them from the bow of Sef Adama's crippled boat—the water too smooth where it should have riffled, the reed clusters standing at angles no current would choose, the half-submerged fence posts of some pre-flood farm laid out in a line that wasn't a line at all but a throat, narrowing, waiting to swallow whatever came up it hungry for a shortcut.

"You're sure about this," the ranger said behind her. Mirabel Voss. The woman stood like she was made of wire and patience, and she watched Ilsa the way Ilsa watched the water—which was to say, constantly, and without ever quite blinking.

"No," Ilsa said. "But I'm right about it."

"That's not the same thing."

"Never has been." She planted her pole and shoved, feeling the bottom give that soft, rotten resistance she hated—silt over old pavement, the bones of a town under two meters of brown water. "The main channel's three days around through the open flats. Harrow's boats are sitting on it like flies on a wound. This cuts us down to one day, maybe less if the rain holds off."

"And the snares?"

She didn't turn around. "You've been listening."

"I've been counting," Mirabel said. "You've changed course four times since dawn without saying why. You keep looking at the water like it owes you money. And yesterday you told Toma the shortcut was suicide, and this morning you told Sef it was the only way. One of those is a lie."

Both, actually, Ilsa thought. But she said, "The water changes overnight. What was suicide yesterday might be the only door today. That's not lying. That's reading."

Behind Mirabel, Sef Adama sat propped against the engine housing with his maps spread across his knees, pretending he wasn't listening, which was its own kind of lie. The man ran an expedition the way a schoolmaster runs a classroom—everything noted, everything filed—and Ilsa had taken his coin knowing full well what kind of man paid double for a surveyor who'd already run. Men who paid double were men who expected betrayal. They just never expected it from the person holding the pole.

* * *

Here was the thing Ilsa had not told any of them, the thing she carried in her chest like a swallowed hook:

Harrow had paid her too.

Not much. Four hundred in scrip and a promise of more—a promise worth exactly as much as Harrow's promises always turned out to be worth, which was nothing, which was less than nothing, because the interest on a broken promise from Corwin Harrow was collected in blood or silence, whichever you had more of. He'd found her in Callow's Landing six weeks ago, bought her dinner, complimented her knowledge of the backwaters, and asked her one question: *If a survey crew comes through your channels heading for the Wreck, how long could you make the trip take?*

That was all. Not sabotage. Not violence. Just time. Slow them down, lose them in the dead water, let the monsoon do the rest. And Ilsa—who was broke, who was tired, whose boat had a cracked hull she couldn't afford to patch—had said yes before the second drink arrived.

She'd told herself it was nothing. A few wasted days. Nobody drowns from being lost slowly.

Then she'd seen the advance camp.

She hadn't been there when it happened—Harrow's work, done by other hands—but she'd poled past the aftermath two days later, because she made herself look, because looking was the only penance she knew how to perform. Looted to the bare boards. A cookfire still warm. And a man face-down in the shallows with his hands tied behind him, alive when they left him, the water rising around him by inches, so slow and so patient that he'd have heard it coming for hours.

He'd been dead by the time she got there. She'd cut him loose anyway. Buried him above the floodline under a cairn of brick from a drowned chimney, and put a stone on top, and sat beside him until dark.

That was the night she decided. Not out of virtue—Ilsa Renn did not trust herself to know what virtue felt like anymore—but out of arithmetic. Harrow's promise was worth nothing. Her name, whatever else it was worth, was the only thing she owned outright, and she'd already spent most of it. There wasn't much left to sell. She intended to stop selling.

So when Sef Adama's boat came limping out of the collapsing channel with its engine drowned and its crew soaked to the soul, and Ilsa had pulled them out—not for charity, she'd told herself, for a cut, for coin, for the same old reasons—she had known, even then, that the coin was a story she was telling them so they wouldn't ask the real question.

The real question was: *why else does anyone do anything?*

* * *

The Deadfall opened ahead of them like a wound in the forest.

It hadn't always been called that. Before the flood it had probably been somebody's valley, somebody's road, somebody's life. Now the drowned trees stood in ranks on either side, gray and leafless, their upper branches hung with drifting weed like hair, and between them the water ran flat and green-black and utterly silent. No birds. No frogs. Even the insects kept away from it, and Ilsa had learned to trust that absence more than any chart.

"Toma," she said. "Take the stern pole. When I raise my hand, backwater. Don't argue, don't ask. Just do it."

The deckhand took the pole without a word. He'd been quiet since the skirmish with Harrow's men—quiet in the way a man is quiet after he's confessed something unforgivable and been forgiven anyway, which Ilsa privately thought was harder to carry than the sin itself. His hands were steady, though. She gave him credit for that.

Sef eased forward. "How deep?"

"Deep enough to float us. Shallow enough to see the bottom, if there's anything on the bottom you need to see." She pointed with her chin at a stretch of water thirty meters ahead, unremarkable to every eye aboard except hers. "See the reeds there? Growing in a circle. Current doesn't grow things in circles. Something under there is catching debris and holding it. Could be a snag. Could be a net. Could be one of Harrow's harpoon rigs, set on a pivot under the mud."

"A harpoon rig," Mirabel repeated flatly.

"Whaling gear, originally. Pre-flood salvage, refitted. A weighted spear on a spring arm, buried in the muck, triggered by a trip-line strung across the channel. You hit the line, the arm comes up out of the mud at speed, and—" She shrugged. "The river takes its toll."

"And you know this how?" Sef asked.

Because I sold you to him, she thought. Because I sat in a boathouse in Callow's Landing while a man with soft hands drew me a diagram of exactly these devices and explained, with a craftsman's pride, how many of them he'd seeded through the drowned country, and I nodded along and took his money and told myself it was only information, only words, words don't kill anybody.

"Because I've tripped one," she said aloud. "Three years ago. It went through the hull of my old boat six inches below where I was sitting. I swam out. My partner didn't."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The boat drifted. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered like a debt collector at the door.

"So we go around the circles," Mirabel said finally. "All of them."

"We go around the circles, we watch for the lines—thin monofilament, you'll never see it directly, but it catches light wrong, a glint, a shimmer where there shouldn't be one—we stay in the middle of the channel where the water's deepest, because the traps are set where a poler would naturally push, close to the banks." She looked back at Sef. "And we move slow. Speed kills out here faster than anything Harrow owns."

"Slow is what gets us caught," Sef said.

"Fast is what gets us killed. Caught, we can survive."

Mirabel met her eyes across the length of the boat, and something passed between them that Ilsa didn't have a name for—an audit, maybe, one professional assessing another's ledger. The ranger had been watching her for days, Ilsa knew. Had noticed the course changes, the hesitations, the way Ilsa's eyes flicked to the treeline whenever a heron flushed. Ilsa had wondered more than once whether the woman had already worked it out, whether she was simply waiting, the way the water waited, patient and total.

If she had, she'd said nothing. That frightened Ilsa more than accusation would have.

* * *

They made two kilometers in the first hour, which was good going for the Deadfall, and Ilsa allowed herself the dangerous luxury of thinking it might hold.

It was the third snare that nearly ended them.

She saw the trip-line with fifty meters to spare—a shimmer across the channel at knee height, anchored to a drowned fence post on the left and a living sapling on the right, sagging just enough to look like drifting weed. She raised her hand. Toma backwatered. They slid around it in a wide, respectful arc, and Ilsa marked the spot in her memory the way she marked everything: not on paper, never on paper, but in the body, in the turns of her own spine.

The fourth snare she didn't see at all.

Later—much later, in the worst hours of the night—she would take it apart a hundred times and understand exactly what had fooled her. The line wasn't strung across the channel. It was strung *under* the surface, a hand's breadth below, invisible against the dark water, set not for a boat but for a poler standing at the bow with his pole in the water. Set, in other words, for her. Harrow knew how she worked. Harrow had taught half the polers on this coast how she worked. The trap was personal, and that fact sat in her stomach like cold iron as the line whispered against the hull—

—and the mud forty meters ahead erupted.

She heard it before she saw it: a sound like a door being kicked open underwater, a boom of displaced water, and then the arm came up out of the channel bottom trailing sheets of black silt, a twelve-foot lever of salvaged steel with a whaling harpoon lashed to its head, and it swung across the channel in a flat, scything arc with the whole weight of the river's patience behind it.

"DOWN!" Ilsa screamed, and threw herself flat, and heard the harpoon pass overhead with a sound like torn cloth stretched into a scream.

It missed her by the width of a prayer. It did not miss Sef Adama.

He'd been standing amidships, braced against the engine housing, and the harpoon's shaft caught him high on the left side, below the collarbone, punching through the meat of the shoulder rather than the chest—two inches of difference between a wound and a funeral—and the impact spun him off his feet and slammed him against the gunwale. The rig's spring arm, spent, crashed back into the water. The boat rocked. The channel went silent again, that awful green silence, as if the river had swallowed and was satisfied.

"Sef!" Mirabel was moving before the boat stopped rocking, dropping to her knees beside him, hands hovering over the wound without touching it. Blood welled between her fingers, shockingly red against the gray morning. "Toma—get us out of the channel, get us to cover, now—"

"There's a cut in the east bank," Ilsa heard herself say, her voice coming from somewhere outside her body, level and cold, the voice she used when everything else was screaming. "Two hundred meters. Old canal mouth. Take it."

Toma took it. The boat slid into the narrow cut, under the dripping canopy of drowned willows, and the green silence of the Deadfall closed behind them like a door.

* * *

The wound was bad, but it was the kind of bad that had rules.

Ilsa had stitched men before—out here you learned or you buried people—and she knelt across from Mirabel in the bottom of the boat and watched the ranger work with a focus that bordered on ferocity. Mirabel had stripped Sef to the waist, packed the entry wound with moss, and was working a probe along the harpoon's path with hands that did not shake. The barbed head had torn free on impact—thank God, thank whatever governs the odds on drowning rivers—but the shaft had gone deep, and there was a question underneath the bleeding that nobody wanted to say aloud: whether it had nicked the lung.

"Breathe," Mirabel ordered Sef. "Slow. Tell me if it burns."

"It burns," Sef said, through his teeth. "Everything burns. Ask a better question."

"Did the air come out anywhere? Bubbling? Hissing?"

A pause. "No."

"Then the lung's intact. You're lucky."

"Lucky." Sef laughed, and the laugh cost him; he went gray around the mouth. "I've been stabbed by a piece of equipment older than my grandfather, in a channel named after corpses, guided by a woman who—" He stopped. Looked at Ilsa. His eyes were fever-bright and terribly clear. "Who knew exactly what that was."

The words dropped into the boat like a stone into still water.

Ilsa held his gaze. She could feel Mirabel's attention swing onto her like a searchlight, could feel Toma freeze at the stern pole, and she understood that the moment had come, the one she'd been rowing toward since the cairn on the floodline, since the boathouse in Callow's Landing, since the second drink she should never have accepted.

"Yes," she said. "I knew."

She told it plainly, because plainness was all she had left. The dinner. The offer. The four hundred in scrip, spent within the month on a hull patch that had failed anyway. The diagram of the harpoon rigs, drawn in Harrow's neat broker's hand. The instruction: *slow them down.* The advance camp, afterward, and the man in the shallows, and the cairn of chimney brick.

"I took his money," she finished. "I told myself it was just time I was selling. Just days. Then I saw what his days cost, and I decided the ledger was closed. That's why I pulled you out of the collapse. That's why I'm taking you through the Deadfall instead of losing you in the flats, which is what I was paid to do, and which would have been easier, and safer, and smarter." She spread her hands. "Loyalty bought isn't loyalty. I know that. But it can be changed. Mine changed. That's the whole of it."

The willows dripped. Somewhere behind them, far down the channel, the current made a small, indifferent sound.

"You led us into a channel you knew was mined," Mirabel said quietly. She hadn't stopped working on Sef's shoulder—threading the needle, tying off, her hands steady as machinery—but her voice had a temperature now, low and exact.

"I led us through it *alive*. Around every trap I knew of. We're in cover, the bleeding's stopping, and Harrow's boats are three hours behind us instead of three minutes. Judge it however you want. Those are the facts."

"The facts include that you lied to us for—how long? Since Callow's Landing?"

"Since before Callow's Landing."

Mirabel tied the last stitch and sat back on her heels. For a long moment she looked at Ilsa with those flat desert eyes, and Ilsa understood that she was not being weighed for forgiveness. Forgiveness wasn't on the table. She was being weighed for use—whether a woman with Harrow's maps in her head and Harrow's methods in her hands was worth more inside the boat than outside it.

"You knew the traps because you helped place the doctrine," Mirabel said. "Which means you can unplace it. Which means from here on, you go first. Every channel. Every crossing. You walk point, Ilsa Renn, until I say otherwise. That's not trust. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic I can live with," Ilsa said.

"Good." Mirabel turned back to Sef, tore a strip from a tarp, began binding the shoulder. "Because there's a fork ahead—your charts show it, I've seen them—and Harrow knows we're coming, and we've lost half a day, and the man who owns this expedition can't lift his left arm. So whatever you're carrying besides guilt, whatever else you haven't told us, you'll tell us tonight. All of it. Or I put you off this boat at the fork and you can explain your ledger to the river."

Ilsa held her gaze and did not flinch. "Tonight," she said. "All of it."

And she meant it—or she meant to mean it—but even as she said it, her mind was already moving ahead, down the dark fork in the channel, to the thing she truly hadn't told them. Not the payment. Not the betrayal. The smaller, sharper thing: that Harrow's diagram of the harpoon rigs had been drawn on the corner of a chart, and that the chart had shown the Wreck's approach channels in detail no surveyor should have possessed, and that in the margin, in a cramped antique hand, someone had written coordinates beside a single word.

*Voss.*

She'd assumed it meant the thief. She'd assumed it meant the map.

But she'd watched Mirabel Voss navigate for five days now without once consulting a chart, watched her find channels by instinct, watched her stand at the bow with a cracked brass compass in her hand that never seemed to point quite north—and Ilsa Renn, who read water like scripture, was beginning to suspect that the word on the margin of Harrow's chart wasn't a name at all.

It was a bloodline. And it was sitting ten feet away from her, stitching a wounded man's shoulder, keeping its secrets the way the Deadfall kept its snares: silently, patiently, and just below the surface.
