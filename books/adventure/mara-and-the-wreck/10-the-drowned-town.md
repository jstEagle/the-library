## 10. The Drowned Town

The village announced itself by a bell.

Not ringing—no one had rung it in forty years—but by its shape breaking the water a half-mile ahead, green with weed, tilted like a drunk leaning on a wall. Ilsa saw it first and put her pole down without a word. Sef lifted his head from the bandage Mirabel had strapped across his harpoon wound that morning and squinted through the drizzle.

"Kettle Row," he said.

"You know it?"

"Everyone who works this river knows it. It went under in the second flood. Whole town signed its water over to a broker rather than fight the levee tax." He said it flatly, the way you'd read a headline off a stone. "There'll be dry rooms in the church loft if the roof held."

Mirabel looked at the bell again. A town that had surrendered didn't drown fighting. It drowned sitting down. She'd grown up around people like that—people who called surrender prudence right up until the water reached their chins—and she had promised herself at fourteen she would never be one of them. Then she'd spent eleven years paying a debt she couldn't name aloud, which was its own kind of drowning, slower and drier.

"We beach there," she said. "Sef needs a night out of the rain or that wound goes bad, and we need a fire."

Ilsa glanced back at her, one eyebrow up. "Your expedition, ranger."

"It's his expedition. I'm just the tracker."

"And he's just the man bleeding into his own shirt." Ilsa turned forward and began poling them toward the bell. "Kettle Row it is."

* * *

They came in through what had been the high street. The rooftops made a broken archipelago under the surface, tile and tin furred white with mineral stain, and here and there a chimney stood clear of the water like a periscope. Ilsa threaded the boat between two of them with the ease of a woman walking her own hallway.

"There," she said, nodding at a stone building on a slight rise—the only rise in sight. The church. Its steeple had come down in some earlier storm; the nave's roof was mostly intact, sagging in the middle where the water had been leaning on it for decades. A wooden stair climbed the outside wall to a loft door, and the lower six steps were gone, but the rest held when Toma tested them with an oar.

Toma hadn't said ten words since the fork. Since the ambush. Since he'd volunteered as decoy and they'd left him behind and then found him three hours later floating downstream on a section of hull, alive, half-drowned, silent as ballast. He moved now like a man carrying something heavy in his chest and refusing to set it down. When Mirabel caught his eye he looked away first, every time.

She helped Sef up the stairs—his arm over her shoulder, his breath coming in short controlled pulls that fooled neither of them—and got him into the loft while Ilsa tied off below. The loft was dry. That was the miracle of it: forty years of monsoons and the old builders' joinery had held, the room above the nave smelling of dust and pigeon droppings and, faintly, of cedar. Someone had stored things up here once. Someone had expected to come back.

There was even a hearth—a small iron stove, vented through the roof, and beside it a stack of split wood wrapped in oilcloth. Whoever had prepared this room had prepared it against exactly the world they were living in now.

"Faith of a kind," Ilsa said, when Mirabel said so. "Or guilt. Those build the same shelters."

They got a fire going. Sef drank water laced with the last of their willow bark and slept, finally, without the shallow startle that had haunted him since Deadfall Channel. Toma sat by the stove turning his hands over and over, palms then backs, like a man checking whether they still belonged to him. And Mirabel, because she could not sit still, because sitting still meant thinking, took a lamp and went down into the body of the church.

* * *

The nave was flooded to the depth of about four feet, black and still, holding the lamplight the way a mirror holds a face. Pews floated in their rows, or the ghosts of pews—waterlogged frames soft as bread. The altar had drifted loose and lay canted against a pillar. Above the waterline, the walls showed tide-marks in bands, each one a year the river had come up and gone down and come up higher.

Mirabel waded the aisle, lamp held high, telling herself she was looking for anything useful: rope, tools, sealed stores. She was a tracker. Tracking was looking for what other people left behind. That was all this was.

But the town kept talking to her.

Names, mostly. People paint their names on things when they believe in tomorrow—on door lintels, on boats, on the brass plates of shops. *Oyelaran & Sons, Boatwright.* *M. Duquesne, Dry Goods.* The letters were salt-eaten but legible, and every one of them was a family that had signed its water away and gone under anyway, because paper promises float about as well as paper boats.

At the back of the nave, past the last pew, there had been an office. A partition wall still stood above the water, and a door hung open on it, and inside—she had to duck through the doorway, wading, the lamp guttering in the close air—there were shelves. Most had collapsed into pulp. But on the highest shelf, above every flood line, sat a row of boxes.

Deed-boxes. Tin, the cheap kind, the kind every claim office in the territory used before the flood. She knew them the way you know your own handwriting. Her grandfather had kept his papers in one exactly like it, green tin with a hasp that bit your thumb if you were careless, and she had spent her childhood forbidden to touch it, and had touched it anyway, once, and seen nothing but dense script and stamps she couldn't read, and been beaten for the touching and not the reading.

Her hand was on the nearest box before she decided to reach for it.

It came off the shelf light as a bird. Water hadn't touched it. She set it on the partition ledge, wiped the dust with her sleeve, and looked at the lid.

And stopped breathing.

Carved into the tin—not stamped, not printed, *carved*, with a sharp blade by someone patient—was a name:

**VOSS.**

Below it, smaller, in the same sure hand: *A. Voss, Surveyor — Rights & Boundaries.*

The lamp shook in her grip. She set it down. Her hands wouldn't stop shaking, so she pressed them flat against the lid, over the name, as if she could hold the word still by force.

She knew that hand. Of course she knew it. She'd spent chapter after chapter of her life trying not to know it—the drafting style on the stolen map fragment that Sef had shown her by lantern light, the way the R's kicked, the way the double-s ran together like a rope with a kink in it. She had told herself it was coincidence. Charts traveled; surveyors copied one another; styles spread like accents. Half the territory's old maps came out of the same school.

But this wasn't a chart. This was a deed-box, in a drowned town four hundred miles from anywhere her grandfather had ever admitted to being, with his name cut into it by his own knife.

He'd been here. He'd worked here. Kettle Row hadn't just *signed* its water over to a broker—someone had surveyed the boundaries, drawn the lines, sealed the deeds. Someone named Voss. Her grandfather hadn't merely owned the compass that pointed at the Wreck. He had built the machinery of surrender, town by town, line by line, and the debt her family carried—the debt she'd been paying since she was fourteen, coin by coin, to a creditor nobody would name—had been incurred buying something. She'd always assumed land. A failed farm, a mortgaged boat. Something ordinary and shameful.

It wasn't a farm.

It was rights. Water rights. The same rights that lay sealed in the Wreck. Her grandfather hadn't lost them—he'd *held* them, or helped hold them, or brokered them, and whatever bargain had gone wrong had left the Voss name owing on the very paper everyone on this river was killing each other to find.

"Inheritance stops being abstract," she said aloud, to the empty office, "when it has your face on it."

Her voice sounded wrong in the flooded room. Too loud. Too much like his.

* * *

She didn't remember climbing back to the loft. She was there, though, box under her arm, and both of them were awake and watching her—Ilsa by the stove, Toma in the corner, and Sef propped on one elbow with his color better and his eyes too sharp for a wounded man.

"You found something," Sef said.

Mirabel set the box on the floor between them. For a long moment she considered lying. She was good at lying; she'd had practice; invisibility was mostly lying done well. But Toma had sold their route out of fear and it had cost them everything, and she'd watched what a secret did to a crew from the inside. And Sef had shown her the map fragment when he didn't have to. Fair was fair, or it was nothing.

"My grandfather's name is on this," she said. "A. Voss. Surveyor. He drew the boundaries for this town's surrender. Probably for a dozen towns like it." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, armor over the shaking. "The map fragment you're carrying—the drafting style you asked me about in the dark, whether I recognized it. I lied. I recognized it the way you recognize your own front door."

Ilsa let out a low breath. "The compass."

"The compass points at the Wreck because he built it from the same charts. Maybe from the same *surveys*. I don't know yet. I know my family owes a debt I've never been allowed to see the ledger for, and I know that debt smells like water rights." She looked at Sef. "You hired me to track a thief. You should know the thief may have been tracking something that belongs—or owed—to me."

The stove ticked. Rain found a new seam somewhere and began a thin percussion against the roof.

Sef was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the voice of a man walking on a frozen channel. "In Callow's Landing, I picked you because you were the best tracker on the flats and the cheapest one who'd say yes. I'll admit that plainly. But I also picked you because your name came up in Harrow's files."

The floor seemed to tilt. "What files?"

"Harrow's syndicate keeps a register of everyone with a claim interest older than the flood. Old families. Dormant rights. You're in it, Mirabel. *Voss, M., heir apparent, status: unlocated.* I thought it might be useful, having the heir along. I told myself it was a bonus, not a reason." He met her eyes and didn't flinch, which she gave him credit for. "So no. I'm not surprised. I'm surprised you told me."

"Heir *apparent*," Ilsa repeated softly. She was looking at Mirabel the way she looked at water before trusting it. "You're not chasing the Wreck, girl. You're *inheriting* it."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it. That's why it's worth killing for. The people who want it are all dead or bought." Ilsa fed a stick into the stove. "Harrow wants it. Harrow's men want it. And now Harrow knows the heir isn't unlocated anymore. She's poling up his river with a burned-out engine and a wounded surveyor."

Toma spoke for the first time in two days. His voice came out rusted. "Then we should give her to him."

"Toma—" Sef started.

"No. Listen." Toma leaned forward, hands still working, palms and backs. "I sold our route because Harrow's men had knives and we had oars. I was wrong, and I paid, and I'm not asking anyone's forgiveness. But I learned something on that hull, floating. Harrow doesn't want the deeds destroyed and he doesn't want them public. He wants them *quiet*. One buyer, one signature, one story that never reaches the common courts." He looked at Mirabel, and there was no malice in it, which was worse. "You're the signature. You're the quiet. Everything we've bled for since the fork—Toma's blood, Sef's arm, Ilsa's channel—it all ends at your hand. Either you sign his paper or you burn his prize. There's no third door."

"There's always a third door," Ilsa said. "That's what channels are."

"Not this river," Toma said. "Not anymore."

Mirabel knelt and put her hand on the deed-box lid again, over the carved name. The tin was cold. Somewhere under the black water below them, a whole town's worth of signatures lay dissolving in the dark—people who had trusted the paper, trusted the surveyor, trusted the lines drawn neat and true by a man whose granddaughter now carried his compass and his debts and, apparently, his claim.

She thought about burning the box. Right there, in the stove, and telling no one, and walking south into the desert owning nothing but her name—which was, when she counted it honestly, almost nothing at all.

She didn't. Because the box was evidence, and evidence was leverage, and leverage was the only language men like Harrow respected. And because some stubborn, furious part of her—the part that had followed boot prints across the salt flats into a river no map admitted to—wanted to stand in front of the Wreck and *know*, with documents, exactly what her family had done.

"Nobody gives anybody to anybody," she said. "We go on. We reach the Wreck. And when Harrow makes his offer—and he will make an offer, Toma, men like him always do—I'll be the one who answers it. Not you. Not Sef. Me."

Sef nodded slowly. Ilsa smiled, thin as a blade. Toma turned back to the stove and his hands.

Outside, the rain thickened, and far off across the drowned rooftops, thunder rolled down the valley like something enormous changing its mind.

Mirabel slept badly, the deed-box under her head like a stone pillow, and dreamed of her grandfather's knife cutting her name into tin—one letter at a time, patient as water—and woke before dawn to Ilsa's hand on her shoulder and Ilsa's voice low and urgent in her ear:

"Ranger. Lights on the water. Three boats, running dark, and they're flying broker colors."
