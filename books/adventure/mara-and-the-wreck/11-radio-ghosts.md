## 11. Radio Ghosts

The transmitter came alive on the third night in the drowned town, and Sef Adama knew it was trouble before he touched it.

He'd been checking the gear out of habit more than hope. The expedition's radio had died mid-sentence a week ago, somewhere upriver, and he'd carried the dead set like a stone in his kit ever since—proof of how badly this venture had gone, how far past the edge of any map they were. But Ilsa Renn had pulled a working unit off the wall of the submerged village's last standing house, pried it from a bracket still bolted to plaster gone soft as bread, and spent an afternoon drying its guts over a cookfire with the patience of a woman who'd salvaged a hundred drowned things.

"It'll hold a signal," she'd said, handing it to him at dusk. "Short range. Maybe long, if the weather's right."

The weather was not right. The monsoon had settled in for good now, a low gray ceiling that leaked rain in slow threads, and the water around them lay flat as poured tin, broken only by rooftops and the tips of drowned trees. They'd tied the boat under the lean-to of a collapsed granary, high enough that only the lower story stood underwater, and Mirabel Voss had taken first watch without being asked.

Sef sat in the granary loft with the transmitter on his knees and worked the dial through static.

He wasn't looking for anything. That was what he told himself. He was just confirming what they already knew—that the advance camp was silent, that Harrow's network owned every frequency worth owning in this drowned country, that they were alone. Confirmation was a kind of comfort. Even bad news, when you already knew its shape, was easier than silence.

Then the static broke.

A voice came through, thin and clipped, the way voices sound when they've been compressed by distance and cheap equipment: *"—repeat, all stations, the Voss woman is confirmed. Grandfather's line. She's carrying the instrument. Harrow wants her brought in breathing. The map is secondary. I say again—"*

Sef's hand froze on the dial.

*"—the Wreck coordinates are compromised. Assume she has them. All stations converge on the fork channels. Nobody touches her until Harrow says. Nobody touches the paper."*

Static swallowed the rest. Sef turned the dial slowly, hunting, and caught fragments of two more transmissions—the flat procedural chatter of men coordinating a net, grid references, fuel counts, a bored mention of rations running low at some station called Nine. Then nothing but rain-hiss.

He sat there a long time with the transmitter warm against his palms.

*The Voss woman is confirmed.*

He thought about the way she watched the water. Not the way a ranger watches it—reading current, reading depth—but the way a person reads a letter they shouldn't have. He thought about the compass she checked too often for someone who claimed not to need it, the cracked brass case she kept wrapped in cloth like a relic or a wound. He thought about the night at the leaking hull, when Toma had asked her where she'd learned to read flood-country charts and she'd said *around*, and Toma had laughed, and she hadn't.

He thought about the map fragment. The drafting style Sef had shown her in the dark of the boat, the fine ruled lines and the little stamped initials in the corner—*E.V.*—and how her face had done something complicated and then done nothing at all.

*Grandfather's line.*

Sef closed the transmitter's case and did not move for a while.

Rain ticked against the granary roof. Below, Ilsa slept sitting up, her pole across her knees, her breathing even as a metronome. Toma was on second watch, which meant Mirabel would come up soon to hand off—or rather, Toma would come down, because Mirabel never handed anything off; she simply appeared where she was needed and vanished when she wasn't.

That was the thing about Mirabel Voss, Sef thought. He'd hired a tracker and gotten a ghost. And ghosts, he was learning, had histories, and histories had prices, and he had already paid one price too high on this river to pay another blind.

Footsteps on the ladder. She came up into the loft with rain beaded on her shoulders and her hood back, hair dark and flat with wet, and stopped when she saw the transmitter in his lap.

Her eyes went to it the way a man's eyes go to a drawn knife.

"You got it working," she said.

"Ilsa did." He kept his voice level. "Picked up some traffic."

"Anything useful?"

"That depends." He turned the transmitter over once in his hands, slow, giving himself time. "You want to tell me who E.V. is?"

The rain filled the pause. It was a long pause. Long enough that Sef counted the drips coming through the roof—one, two, three—and watched her decide, visibly, whether to lie.

"No," she said.

And there it was. Not even a good lie. Not even an attempt. Just the flat refusal of a woman who'd been lying so long by omission that outright denial had become more effort than truth.

"Mirabel." He set the transmitter down between them, deliberate, the way you'd set down a card you didn't want to show. "I picked up Harrow's network tonight. They know your name. They know your family. They used a word I'm going to make you say out loud, because if I say it, I might be wrong, and if I'm wrong, I'm about to do something unforgivable to the best tracker I've ever hired."

She stood very still. Water dripped off her sleeve onto the floorboards, one drop at a time, patient as everything else in this drowned country.

"They said," Sef continued, "that you're confirmed. Grandfather's line. That you're carrying the instrument. That the map is secondary to you." He looked up at her. "So either Harrow's people are chasing a coincidence, or you've known what we were walking into since Callow's Landing, and you let me spend my crew, my boat, and my money finding out for you."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It probably isn't. Fairness left this expedition somewhere around the deadfall channel, along with my engine and half my fuel. I'm not asking for fair. I'm asking for true."

Mirabel lowered herself onto the floor across from him, cross-legged, the way she sat watch—weight forward, always ready to move. In the lamplight her face was stripped of everything she usually wore, all that dry desert armor, and underneath it she looked younger and more tired than he'd ever let himself see.

"My grandfather," she said, "was a surveyor. Before the flood. He worked the salt flats and the river country, charting channels that don't exist anymore. His name was Elias Voss."

"E.V.," Sef said quietly.

"He drew the original charts. The ones the Wreck's deeds were filed against. And after the flood, when the rights got tangled and the brokers started circling, he built something." Her hand went, without her seeming to notice, to the compass at her belt. "Not a compass. Everyone calls it a compass. It doesn't point north. It points toward the Wreck. He made it from his own charts—a key, tuned to the one place in this country that matters. And when he died, my mother sold everything they had to keep the claims alive on paper. Sold it, and borrowed more, and the debt came down to me. A debt I couldn't even name, because naming it meant admitting what it was for."

"The rights," Sef said. "Your family bought water rights to a shipwreck."

"My family bought a rumor," Mirabel said. "And paid for it for thirty years. And then a thief stole a map that turned out to be drawn in my grandfather's hand, and I took a contract to chase him, and I told myself it was coin. Just coin. Debt doesn't care what you tell yourself, though. It knew. I knew." She looked at him straight, finally. "You're right. I knew what the Wreck was before you ever showed me the fragment. I should have told you in Callow's Landing. I didn't, because telling you meant you might turn back, and I needed to get here."

Sef exhaled slowly. Somewhere below, Toma shifted at his watch, boots scraping wood.

"Why?" he asked. "Why does it matter so much? If the debt's yours, walk away. Let Harrow have his paper. There are other contracts."

"Because it's not just mine." Her voice stayed low, but something moved under it, tectonic. "Those deeds predate the flood. If they're valid, they're valid for everyone downstream whose water Harrow's syndicate has been bleeding for a decade. My grandfather drew those charts because he believed the country belonged to the people on it. Then his own children mortgaged that belief to survive, and the brokers have been collecting interest on it ever since. I'm the last Voss. Whatever happens to those deeds happens because of me."

"And Harrow knows it."

"Harrow's wanted the Wreck for years. He could never find it—the channels changed, the old charts went stale. But my grandfather's compass doesn't go stale. It can't. It points at the Wreck the way a wound points at itself." She almost smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see. "Harrow doesn't need me for the map anymore. He needs me for this." She touched the compass. "And now his whole network knows I'm carrying it."

Sef leaned back against the wall. Rain drummed. He made himself think like an expedition chief instead of a man who'd just found out his hire had been a lever all along.

"Toma," he said at last. "Does he know?"

"No."

"Ilsa?"

"No."

"They will. By tomorrow. Because I won't lie to them on a boat this small, and neither should you." He held her gaze. "Here's what I need from you, Mirabel. Not an apology. An accounting. Everything you know about Harrow's dispositions, everything that transmission implied, everything your grandfather's charts might tell us that the stolen fragment doesn't. Because if his men are converging on the fork channels, we're not tracking anymore. We're tracked. And I plan our route knowing what you actually bring, not what I hoped you did."

"All right," she said.

"And one more thing." He paused, choosing the words. "Whatever you're planning to do at the Wreck—burn them, sign them, drown them—I need to know before we get there. Not because I get a vote. Because my crew dies or doesn't die based on what you choose, and I won't have them spending their lives on a secret."

Mirabel was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice was rougher.

"I haven't chosen yet."

"Then choose fast," Sef said. "Because the storm season won't wait, Harrow won't wait, and I'm starting to suspect the river won't either."

She nodded once, gathered her gear, and went down the ladder without another word. Sef listened to her boots cross the lower floor, heard the low exchange with Toma at the watch point, heard the granary settle back into rain and dripping quiet.

He picked up the transmitter again and turned the dial through the empty frequencies, listening to the country breathe its static. Once, faintly, he thought he caught a carrier wave—someone out there, transmitting nothing, just letting the network know it was awake. Watching. Counting boats.

He shut the case and lay back and did not sleep.

Near dawn, Ilsa climbed up silently and sat beside him, following his gaze out at the gray water.

"Well?" she said. She'd heard. Of course she'd heard; the woman heard everything on a boat that size. "What's the play, chief?"

Sef watched the light come up thin and colorless over the drowned town, over the rooftops and the flooded streets and the far dark line of the channel fork, where Harrow's men were even now tightening a net built around a woman and a piece of brass.

"We run the fork," he said. "Before they finish closing it."

Isla was quiet a moment. "And her?"

Sef thought about the lie she'd told—no, worse than a lie, the truth she'd withheld—and the strange, stubborn gravity she carried, like something the whole river was slowly pulling toward center.

"She comes with us," he said. "But from here on, she rows."

* * *

They broke camp in the first hard rain of the morning, and Sef put Mirabel at the oars himself, wordless, and watched her take them without complaint—which somehow made it worse, or better, he couldn't tell. Ilsa poled the bow, reading the drowned streets, calling turns in her flat scripture voice. Toma ran the small sail they'd rigged from salvage, glancing between the three of them like a man counting exits.

By midmorning the fork channels were in sight: two dark throats in the drowned forest, separated by a spine of high ground, both of them choked with snare-poles and old trap lines that Ilsa named aloud as they passed, one by one, like a litany.

And on the spine of high ground between them, small in the rain, a figure stood watching them. Not moving. Not signaling. Just watching, the way a man watches a thing he has already been paid to understand.

Sef felt the boat's mood change around him—Toma's hands going still on the sail, Ilsa's pole pausing mid-stroke, Mirabel bending harder over the oars as if she could out-row being known.

Then Mirabel lifted her head, and her hand went to the compass at her belt, and Sef saw her face change as she read whatever the cracked brass needle was telling her.

"Sef," she said quietly. "It's pointing past him. Straight through the fork. And it's pulling harder than it's ever pulled."

The needle trembled against its glass, straining east, and the rain came down harder, and somewhere behind them—faint, mechanical, wrong—a sound rolled across the water that none of them had heard in days.

An engine. More than one.
