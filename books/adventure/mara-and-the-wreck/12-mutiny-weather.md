## 12. Mutiny Weather

The storm found them on the third day of the long channel, where the drowned forest thinned to black snags and the water went the color of old tin. Toma felt it before he saw it—a pressure in his ears, a hush that fell over the reeds like a held breath. Then the sky split open down the middle and everything came sideways.

"Down!" Ilsa's voice cut through the roar. "Get her nose into it or she'll broach!"

Toma threw himself at the tiller while Sef wrestled the canvas down. The boat bucked under him, a living thing trying to shed its riders. Rain hit his face like thrown gravel. Ahead, the channel they'd been following simply vanished—no far bank, no tree line, just gray water hammering gray water to the horizon.

He'd grown up on this river. He'd told them so back in Callow's Landing, when Sef was hiring, and it had been true then. But he'd grown up on the river in dry season, when the channels held their shapes and the water had manners. This was something else. This was the river remembering what it used to be, before the flood took the towns, before the maps stopped meaning anything.

The bow swung. He hauled it back. His arms burned. Somewhere behind him Mirabel was bailing with a cooking pot, methodical as a metronome, and Ilsa stood braced against the mast step reading the water the way other people read scripture—lips moving, eyes narrowed, translating.

"There," Ilsa shouted. "Left of the dead cedar. There's a cut."

Toma couldn't see any cut. He steered toward the cedar anyway, because the alternative was trusting his own eyes, and his own eyes were showing him nothing but the end of the world.

The cut, when it came, was barely wider than the boat. Walls of cane closed over them and the storm dropped away to a muffled drumming overhead, and suddenly they were gliding through a green corridor with rain filtering down through the leaves in fat, slow drops. The water here was still. Almost polite.

Sef sat down hard on the cargo crate and put his head in his hands.

Nobody spoke for a long time. That was the first hour of the storm, though none of them knew it yet—the quiet inside the noise, the eye of something that wasn't done with them.

* * *

The rations had been failing since Deadfall Channel. Toma had been counting. Counting was his job now, self-appointed, because someone had to know the truth even if nobody said it aloud: four tins of salt fish, half a sack of meal gone damp and clotted, a jar of oil, and the last of the dried fruit that Mirabel doled out one piece at a time like a woman paying out a rope over a cliff.

Three days of storm. That was the forecast Ilsa gave, squinting at the light through the canopy, and three days meant three days of not poling, not moving, not eating anything they didn't have to.

They tied off to two cypress trunks whose crowns made an island above the flood, and there they sat, four people in a boat the length of a coffin, listening to the world try to drown itself again.

It was Toma who said it first, on the second night, because hunger does that—it strips you down until the true thing is the only thing left in your mouth.

"We should turn back."

The word fell into the boat like a stone into a well. They all heard it going down.

Ilsa didn't look up from the map fragment she kept folded in her oilskin pocket. She'd taken to studying it by candle stub every evening, tracing channels with one cracked fingernail. "Back to where?"

"Callow's Landing. Or anywhere. Downstream, at least. We've got maybe—" He did the arithmetic again, hoping it had changed. It hadn't. "Maybe five days of food if we starve ourselves thin. The Wreck is eight days up if the weather breaks, longer if it doesn't. And after we get there we still have to come back."

"After we get there," Sef said quietly, "we won't need to come back hungry."

"That's a gamble."

"Everything is a gamble." Sef lifted his head. In the candlelight his face looked older than it had a week ago; the wound from the harpoon trap had healed crooked, pulling one eyebrow up in a permanent question. "You think turning back isn't? You think Harrow's men don't know every channel between here and the Landing? We run, we run straight into their arms, and we arrive with nothing to bargain with. At least ahead there's the Wreck. At least ahead there's a chance the deeds are worth more than our lives."

"You don't know that."

"No," Sef admitted. "I don't."

Mirabel said nothing. She was sitting at the stern with her compass open in her palm, watching its needle tremble—not north, never north, but toward something upstream that only she seemed able to feel. Toma watched her watch it. He'd known for days, the way you know a thing about a person without being told: that the compass was pointing her somewhere specific, and that she was afraid of wherever it was.

"Mirabel," he said. "You've got a vote in this. Say something."

She closed the compass. Slow, deliberate, like shutting a door on a room full of noise.

"I say we go on."

"Why?" It came out of him harder than he meant it to, and he heard the whine underneath it, the fear, and hated himself for both. "Because your needle says so?"

"Because turning back means we did all of this for nothing." Her voice was flat, tired, absolutely steady. "Because Toma died on this river once already—I watched him volunteer as a decoy at the fork, I watched Harrow's men take him, and I will not spend that for nothing."

"I'm right here," Toma said.

"I know you are." Something flickered across her face—relief, or guilt, or both wearing the same coat. "That's the point. You came back. Which means the price hasn't been paid yet. It's still sitting there waiting for us."

* * *

They didn't sleep much that night. The storm eased around midnight to a steady drumming and the boat rocked them in the green corridor like a cradle, and Toma lay awake doing the same arithmetic over and over, as if hunger were a lock he could pick by worrying at it.

Five days of food. Eight days to the Wreck. Even the numbers themselves were liars—he'd been estimating portions by eye, and eyes get generous when your stomach gets loud.

Near dawn he heard Ilsa move. She slid over the gunwale into the flooded cane without a splash, and when she came back ten minutes later she had six small fish strung on a reed, silver-bellied, gasping.

"Drowned forest breeds them," she said. "Where there's cover, there's feed."

They ate them raw because a fire was a beacon and beacons got you killed, and the flesh was cold and muddy-tasting and the best thing Toma had ever eaten. For a little while the boat was warm with something like fellowship. Sef laughed at something—Ilsa mimicking the way he'd shouted orders in the squall—and the sound surprised all of them, like a bird landing on a wire.

Then morning came, and the storm came back, and the arithmetic resumed.

It was midday, gray light seeping down through the canopy, when Toma said it again. He hadn't planned to. It rose in him the way bile rises.

"We should turn back."

This time Ilsa set down the map fragment and looked at him fully, and there was nothing friendly in her face.

"Say the rest of it."

"The rest of what?"

"The rest of why. Go on. We're all thinking it, so say it plain." Her voice was low and even, which from Ilsa was worse than shouting. "You want to turn back because you're scared. Fine. But there's more under it. Say it."

Toma's hands curled into fists on his knees. He stared at the floorboards, at the bilge water sloshing in the seams, and the truth came up his throat like a hook.

"Because I sold us." His voice cracked on the second word. "I sold the route to Harrow, and I told myself it was for my sister's debt, and maybe some of that was even true. But I've been paid by that man before. I worked his boats for two years. And I know how he treats people who bring him half a prize. If we turn back empty-handed, we're not running home—we're running to the one place he's guaranteed to find us, carrying nothing worth buying mercy with. And I'm the reason he knows our heading. Every mile we go backward is a mile deeper into what I did."

The silence afterward was total. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

"So it's not the food," Ilsa said.

"It's the food too."

"It's always the food too." She leaned back against the mast step. "But thank you for saying the rest. A crew can survive a liar if he tells the truth once."

Sef was looking at Toma with an expression he couldn't read—something between anger and pity, or maybe anger at himself for feeling the pity. "You understand that changes nothing."

"I know."

"If we press on and reach the Wreck, Harrow may already be there. Your route-selling may have killed us regardless."

"I know that too."

"And if we turn back—"

"Then I've spent everyone's lives to save my own skin twice." Toma finally raised his head. "Once selling the route. Once running from it. I'd rather die going forward than live going back. That's the whole of it. That's what's left of me after the hunger's been at it."

Mirabel opened her compass again. Just a crack, just enough to see the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, faithful as a heartbeat, pointing upriver at whatever waited there.

"Vote," she said.

* * *

It should have been simple. Four people, two options, majority rules. But nothing on this river had been simple since the radio went silent, and the vote curdled almost immediately.

Sef voted on. Mirabel voted on. Ilsa surprised no one by voting on—but she voted on with a condition, delivered flat as a ledger entry: "We eat half-rations starting now. Real ones. Measured. And anyone who steals from the stores answers to me, and I don't mean a talking-to."

Which left Toma, and Toma found that he could not say the word.

Back. He couldn't say it. Not because he'd changed his mind about the danger—every number still argued for retreat, and the numbers were honest in a way people weren't. But saying *back* aloud would make it real, would commit him to being the man who turned the boat, and he had discovered, somewhere in the last sleepless night, that he would rather be the man who starved going forward.

"On," he said. "Against my judgment. On."

And then Sef ruined it.

"Good," Sef said, and there was relief in it, naked relief, and something else too—something proprietary, a landowner's satisfaction—and Toma heard it and understood, all at once, with the clarity that only an empty stomach provides, that Sef Adama had never seriously considered turning back. Not at the fork. Not at the deadfall. Not now. Because Sef needed the Wreck the way a drowning man needs air, and every argument he'd ever made about chances and bargains had been scaffolding built around a decision he'd made before Callow's Landing.

"You never meant to ask us," Toma said. "Any of the times. The votes were theater."

Sef's face went carefully blank. "I asked."

"You announced."

"Toma." Mirabel's hand came down on his forearm, light but firm. "Don't."

"Don't what? Don't say it out loud?" He was shaking now, and he knew it was the hunger talking, the hunger and the fear and the shame all speaking through one mouth, and he couldn't stop it. "Fine. Then let's say everything out loud. Sef needs those deeds or he's finished—that's why double pay, that's why we push through a monsoon on half a sack of rotten meal. Ilsa's taking a cut of whatever we find, which makes her a claim-jumper with better manners than Harrow's. And Mirabel—" he caught himself, too late, the name already out, the accusation already forming, "—Mirabel's compass points to the Wreck and won't tell us why, and she lies badly whenever anyone asks about her family, and we're all pretending that's normal."

The boat went very still.

Mirabel looked at him. Not angry. Worse—measuring, the way she measured distances across open water, deciding whether a thing could be crossed.

"You're right," she said. "About all of it. About me most of all." She closed the compass and tucked it away. "And when we get where we're going, you'll understand why I lied. Or you won't, and you'll hate me, and either way you'll still be alive, which is more than the river was offering."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It's a promise. Those are different things."

Ilsa had picked up her pole during the exchange, casual as a woman picking up a broom, but Toma saw where her thumb rested—along the grain, near the ferrule, where the iron tip began. Watching. Ready. Not threatening, exactly. Just reminding the boat what tools were for.

"Knives stay sheathed," she said mildly. "Storm's got another day in it, maybe two. Nobody wins a fight in a leaking hull on an empty stomach except the fish." She nodded at the bilge. "Somebody bail."

Toma bailed. It was easier than arguing, and the work warmed him, and there was a strange mercy in having a task when your whole insides were at war. He scooped the gray water over the side and watched the rain stitch holes in the flood and thought about his sister in Callow's Landing, who didn't know if he was alive, who he'd sold these strangers to protect, and who would spit in his face if she ever learned the shape of what that protection cost.

Sef retreated to the bow and pretended to study charts. Ilsa ate one fish and gave the rest away without comment. Mirabel sat at the stern with her hood up, a shape rather than a person, keeping her own counsel the way she kept everything—close, dry, and out of the wind.

By evening the storm had settled into a long gray grind, and the boat rocked, and the hunger walked among them like a fifth passenger, patient, certain of arrival.

Toma dreamed of bread. In the dream it was the cheap dark loaf from the Landing markets, and it was stale, and it was the finest thing God had ever made, and when he woke his mouth was full of river water because the hull had shipped a wave in the night and nobody had called him.

He bailed again. The sky stayed the color of tin. And near noon, when the light through the canopy shifted in a way that made Ilsa go rigid at the pole, he understood that the storm was the least of what was coming.

"What," Sef said.

Ilsa tilted her head, listening to something below the hearing of the other three. When she answered, her voice had gone quiet and flat, the voice she used for facts that bite.

"Water's rising fast. Too fast for rain." She spat over the side. "That's not weather upstream. That's the crest. The monsoon's coming down the valley all at once, and this channel—" she looked at the cane walls closing them in, green and narrow and calm, "—this channel becomes a chute. In about an hour, maybe less."

"Can we outrun it downstream?"

"Nothing outruns a crest. You get out of the water or you ride it." She was already untying the mooring lines, movements quick and economical. "There's high ground a half-mile up this cut—an old levee road, if it holds. We pole for it and pray."

Toma grabbed his pole. His arms screamed. He didn't care. Behind them, far off but not far enough, the river made a sound he'd only heard once before, the day the flood took his grandmother's town—a sound like the whole world clearing its throat.

"Move," Ilsa said. "Now. Move like you mean it."

They drove the boat up the narrow cut against the first tug of the current, four starving people poling for their lives, and Toma thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace: whatever waits at the Wreck, at least we'll arrive knowing exactly what each of us is.

The water pulled. The cane walls blurred past. And somewhere behind them, low and gathering, the crest began to sing.
