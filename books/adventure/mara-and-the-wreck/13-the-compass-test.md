## 13. The Compass Test

The fire was small enough to hide with a cupped hand. That was the rule Ilsa had set on the first night of the dead water—no flame taller than a fist, no smoke that could be read from the treeline—and for three days we had kept it, huddled around a heat source the size of a secret.

We were beached in a cut of blackwater where the current had given up entirely. The surface lay flat as poured glass, and the drowned forest stood around us in ranks, trunks silvered and stripped, holding up nothing. The air tasted like a wet rag wrung out over old iron. Somewhere behind us, three days back now, the engine had died in Harrow's ambush, and Toma had gone into the reeds as a decoy and had not come back, and none of us had said his name since except once, at night, when Sef thought I was asleep.

I sat with my back against the hull and the compass open in my palm.

I had been sitting with it like that for an hour. Not using it. Looking at it.

"You're going to do it," Ilsa said. She was across the fire, mending a pole shoe with wire she'd pulled from the boat's ruined ignition. She didn't look up. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The one people get before they say something they've been chewing since Callow's Landing." She bit the wire, twisted it. "Go on. Better out here than out there."

Sef was at the bow, keeping watch the way he'd kept watch every night since the fork—with a rifle across his knees and a stillness that wasn't calm, just held. He turned at Ilsa's voice. In the firelight his face was hollowed out, the wound in his side pulling at him even after two weeks, and he looked older than he had on the dock at Callow's Landing, older by whole seasons.

"Mirabel," he said. Just my name. But it was a question.

I closed my hand over the compass. The brass was warm from my skin, which was ridiculous—it was brass, it took whatever temperature you gave it—but it felt warm anyway, the way a thing feels warm when you've finally decided to stop pretending about it.

"You both deserve to know what this is," I said. "Before we pole another day toward something I might not be leading you to honestly."

"Here it comes," Ilsa murmured.

"Shut up, Renn," Sef said, but without heat. He came aft and lowered himself onto the thwart across from me, careful of his side, and waited. Waiting was the thing Sef did best. It was why I'd taken his contract in the first place, back when I told myself it was only about the money. A man who could wait through silence without filling it was a man who understood what silence cost.

I opened the compass.

It's a plain instrument, if you don't know what you're seeing. Brass case, dinged to hell, a glass crystal cracked across the upper left quadrant so that everything north of northeast has always had a hairline scar through it. My grandfather's initials are engraved inside the lid: E.V., 1931. I'd been carrying it since I was nineteen, since the night the rest of my family's world went into a broker's strongbox, and I'd carried it through eleven years of desert work because it had never once failed me. Never pointed wrong. Never fogged. Never lied.

That was the problem. I just hadn't known it was the problem until this river.

"Watch the needle," I said.

They watched. The needle hung steady, trembling only with the tremor of my hand, pointing off to the northwest—at the drowned forest, at the black water, at nothing any map would confirm.

"That's not north," Sef said.

"No."

"Since when?"

"It's never pointed north," I said. "Not once. Not in eleven years."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, something moved—a gar rolling near the surface, maybe, or just the water settling—and I let the sound die before I went on, because once I started I wanted to get all the way through it.

"I figured it out when I was young. Maybe sixteen. I was working my first season on the flats, guiding salt-haulers, and I noticed the compass didn't agree with the sun. Every reading, off by degrees. Different degrees, depending on where I stood. I thought it was broken. I took it to an instrument man in Perdition Wells and he opened the case and looked at it for a long time and then closed it and gave it back and wouldn't take my money." I turned the compass so the firelight caught the cracked crystal. "He said, *Girl, this isn't a compass. This is a follower.* I didn't know what he meant. I didn't want to know. So I stopped asking, and I learned to navigate by sun and stars and landmarks, and I carried this thing because it was my grandfather's and because—" I stopped. Started again. "Because it made me feel found. Even when I was alone. Especially then."

"And now?" Sef asked.

"Now I know what it follows." I lifted the needle's bearing with my eye and extended the line out across the black water, northwest, into the drowned country. "It points there. To the Wreck. It's pointed at the Wreck my whole life. When I was standing in the salt flats four hundred miles south, it was pointing here, under all this water, at a barge my grandfather helped chart before the flood came and buried it."

Ilsa had stopped working on the pole shoe. She was watching the needle now with the flat attention she gave to water—the same look, I realized, she gave to everything she intended to read.

"Your grandfather," Sef said slowly. "Elias Voss."

"You knew the name."

"I knew the surveyor's name on the fragment. The drafting hand. The way he lettered his elevations—I've seen a thousand survey charts and nobody letters like that." He rubbed his jaw. "I hired a tracker named Voss to find a thief who stole a map drawn by a Voss. I told myself it was coincidence. There aren't many of that name left in the territory."

"There's one," I said. "There's exactly one, and she's sitting right here, and she's known for three weeks that the map in your strongbox and the compass in her pocket came out of the same head."

"Why didn't you say something?" Not angry. Worse—hurt, carefully folded away, the way Sef folded everything. "At the camp. On the boat. Any of the hundred nights—"

"Because saying it made it real." The words came out faster than I meant them to, and I let them; there was no point dressing them now. "You want to know what I am, Adama? I'm the granddaughter of a man who sold water rights he had no right to sell, and the debt my family's been drowning in for thirty years—that debt I told you I couldn't talk about—is the debt of what he did. And this compass is the proof. It's built off the same charts. Same hand. Same obsession. He didn't make a tool that finds north. He made a tool that finds *his* north. His claim. His Wreck. And then he died, and the flood came, and the thing he built ended up in the pocket of the last Voss, dragging her across a desert toward a hole in the water she swore she'd never go near."

I hadn't raised my voice. I heard how it sounded anyway—too loud in the dead air, too raw. I made myself stop. Made myself breathe.

"So that's the test," I said. "That's what I've been chewing since Callow's Landing, Renn. I can find the Wreck without maps, without charts, without any of Harrow's stolen paper. I *am* the map. And every mile we've poled, I've been asking myself whether I brought you here because you paid me, or because this thing in my pocket finally got what it wanted."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The fire burned down a fraction. Out in the flooded timber, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

Ilsa spoke first, which surprised me. I'd have bet on Sef.

"It's a key," she said.

Sef turned on her. "It's a leash."

"It's a key." She set down the wire and leaned forward into the firelight, and I saw that she wasn't being contrary—she was being precise, the way she was precise about channels. "Think about what you're actually saying, Adama. A compass that points at one place, always, no matter where you stand? You know what that means? It means the maker *wanted it found*. You don't build a pointer unless you want somebody to follow it. Elias Voss sealed those deeds in a barge and then built an instrument that would lead straight to them, and he put it in his family's hands. That's not a trap. That's an inheritance with instructions."

"He sold rights he had no right to sell," Sef said. "Her own words."

"People say that about every claim in this territory. Half the deeds on the books were signed by men who stole the ground fair and square." Ilsa shrugged. "I'm not saying he was clean. I'm saying a key doesn't care whether the lock is honest. It opens what it opens. What she does with what's inside—" she nodded at me, "—that part's hers."

"A leash," Sef repeated, quieter now, and I understood he wasn't arguing with Ilsa anymore. He was arguing with the version of events that let him keep trusting me. "Think it through, Renn. If the compass leads to the Wreck, and the compass belongs to a Voss, then whoever holds the Voss holds the route. Harrow doesn't need charts anymore. He needs *her*. He's probably known since Callow's Landing—he's had eyes on this crew since before we launched. Why do you think he let us run this far? Why do you think the ambush at the fork took our engine and not our lives?" He looked at me, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Because a dead guide is worth nothing, Mirabel. A leashed one is worth everything."

The words landed where he'd aimed them, which was the worst place: the exact fear I'd been carrying since the fork, since Toma walked into the reeds, since the radio ghost voice had said *the Voss girl* in a tone I couldn't stop hearing.

"I'm not Harrow's," I said.

"No," Sef agreed. "But your grandfather's instrument is doing half his work for him. It's been walking you toward that hold since you were nineteen years old. Eleven years, and it never once pointed anywhere else. Tell me that's freedom. Tell me you ever chose a direction it didn't already choose for you."

I looked down at the compass. The needle hung there, patient as water, pointing northwest through its cracked glass at the thing it had been pointing at my entire adult life.

And here was the truth underneath everything, the one I'd have to say aloud eventually, so I said it now, into the fire, where it was smallest:

"I checked. After Perdition Wells. One time, just to see." My thumb moved over the worn brass rim. "I walked out past the last marker on the flats, due east, away from anything, and I held the compass and watched it swing around and settle. And I thought—if I throw it in the salt, I'm free. Nobody finds the Wreck, the debt stays a story, the name dies with me. Clean."

"And?" Ilsa asked.

"And I put it back in my pocket." I laughed, but there was nothing funny in it. "Eleven years ago. And I told myself I kept it because it was the last thing of his, and because it had never failed me, and because a person ought to carry her own history instead of running from it. All true. None of it the truth. I kept it because as long as it pointed somewhere, I wasn't lost. Even if where it pointed was a debt."

The fire had burned to coals. I fed it a stick, small, fist-height, within the rules.

"So vote," I said. "That's what this crew does now, isn't it? We vote. Here's the question. I'm the map, and I'm also the liability, and I can't cut either thing out of myself. We're three days' poling from the Wreck, Harrow's ahead of us or behind us or both, and the monsoon crest is coming whether we like it or not. So somebody tell me: do I turn this boat around? Do I go on? Or do I give the compass to one of you and let somebody else decide what it's worth?"

* * *

Ilsa answered by picking the pole shoe back up and going back to work, wire between her teeth, which in her language was a full sentence: the question was stupid, the answer was obvious, we were going on.

Sef took longer.

He sat with his rifle across his knees and looked at the compass in my open palm for a long time—not at me, at it, the way you look at a stranger who claims to be kin. Then he reached out, and I thought for a second he was going to take it, and some animal part of me clenched hard enough to hurt.

He didn't take it. He turned it, gently, with one finger, so the lid faced him, and read the engraving inside. *E.V., 1931.*

"My father had a watch like this," he said. "Different trade, same idea. Something a dead man leaves you that keeps telling you what time it is long after his time ran out." He let the lid fall shut. "You should have told me in Callow's Landing."

"Yes."

"I'd have hired you anyway."

"I know," I said. "That's what scared me."

Something moved through his face then—too fast to name, gone before it finished arriving—and he nodded once, the nod he used to close a matter, and stood, careful of his side, and went forward to take the middle watch.

Ilsa spat out her wire and looked at me across the coals.

"For what it's worth," she said, "keys don't drag. Leashes drag. Keys just sit there until somebody decides to use them." She nodded at my pocket, where the compass now lived again, warm against my hip. "You've been deciding for eleven years, Voss. You just didn't like the decision."

She rolled into her bedroll. Within minutes her breathing went slow and even, the sleep of a woman who trusted the water to warn her before trouble arrived.

I stayed up. The dead water lay black and mirror-still, and the drowned trees stood in their thousands, and somewhere northwest, three days off, a hillside held a ship that held my grandfather's handwriting and my family's ruin and possibly, if Ilsa was right, a set of instructions I'd been carrying in my pocket since before I could read them.

Around midnight, far off in the direction the needle pointed, I saw a light.

One light. Low, steady, moving along a line no honest traveler would take—moving the way a man moves when he knows exactly where he's going and wants everyone else to think he doesn't.

Harrow. Ahead of us after all. And between us and him, three days of dead water, and a storm building somewhere over the sea that we could feel in the pressure behind our ears like a held breath.

I woke Ilsa with a hand on her shoulder.

"Light," I said. "Northwest. Moving."

She was up before I finished, no grogginess in her at all, squinting into the dark. Then she looked at me, and at my pocket, and something grim settled into her face.

"Well," she said. "Key's turning."
