## 14. Ambush at the Fork

Ilsa Renn had poled the drowned country for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned one law above every other: the water tells you when someone is watching. Not with sound. With stillness. A heron lifts when a boat comes, but it lifts *before* the boat, because birds feel engines through their feet on drowned branches half a mile out. Frogs go quiet in a moving band, like a curtain drawn across the channel. Even the midges change their drift.

That morning the frogs had gone quiet two hundred yards too early.

She stood at the bow with her pole planted and said nothing for a long moment, reading the green corridor ahead—the fork where Deadfall Channel split around a drowned granary, east branch wide and slow, west branch choked with reed islands. The water in the east branch lay flat as poured tin. Too flat. No wind anywhere else that morning, true, but there should have been current seams, little wrinkles where the flood pushed through. There were none.

"East branch," she said.

Sef looked up from the engine hatch, grease to his elbows. "You said west was faster."

"West is faster. East is alive." She kept her voice level. "Someone's sitting in the east branch, and they've been sitting long enough to drink their coffee."

Mirabel came forward from the stern, compass in her hand though she'd stopped pretending it pointed north days ago. "How many?"

Ilsa shrugged. "Frogs went quiet in a band about forty yards across. Could be six men. Could be twelve." She spat over the side, an old habit that meant she was thinking. "Harrow doesn't send six."

The boat drifted. Behind them the channel they'd come up stretched back gray and endless, and somewhere down that grayness Harrow's second boat was following their wake—had been since the ambush two nights ago that had cost them their fuel drum and nearly cost Sef his arm. They couldn't go back. That was the whole arithmetic of it. Going back meant meeting what followed; going forward meant meeting what waited. The only question left was which trap had been built by men who were tired, and which by men who were paid.

"Toma," Sef called. "Kill the engine. We'll pole."

Toma killed it, and the silence that came down was worse than the frogs' silence, because now Ilsa could hear everything: water dripping off the reeds, the creak of the hull taking sun, Mirabel's breathing, quick and controlled. And underneath it, far off but not far enough, the low diesel mutter of another boat.

"They're behind us too," Toma said quietly. He was at the stern rail, and he had the look he got when he was doing sums he didn't want finished. "They've been hanging back since the fuel. Keeping pace. Herding."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "That's how Harrow works. He doesn't chase you into the trap. He walks you into it and then closes the gate." She planted the pole and turned them toward the west branch anyway, because a trap you choose is better than a trap you're given, and the reed islands of the west branch were the only cover on this whole stretch of drowned country. "But he expects us to take the west. That's why the east is quiet. He wants us to see the empty branch and think we've fooled him."

"So which do we take?" Mirabel asked.

"The one he's not watching," Ilsa said. "Neither of them."

* * *

There was a third way, and it wasn't a way so much as a wound in the land. Halfway down the west branch, before the reed islands thickened into a maze, an old levee breach had cut a back-channel through what had once been somebody's orchard. Ilsa knew it because she'd run stolen goods through it in her Harrow years, before she'd understood what the goods were buying and whom they were drowning. The back-channel was narrow enough that the boat's gunwales brushed the drowned apple trees on both sides, deep enough to float them if the season was right, and blind as a well. It came out below the fork, past the trap, into open water—if it hadn't silted shut since last monsoon.

"If it has silted shut," Sef said, when she told him, "we're in a dead-end ditch with two boats behind us."

"Then we fight in a ditch," Ilsa said. "Better than fighting in a funnel."

He looked at her a long moment. She watched him decide to trust her—not fully, never fully, not after Toma's confession, not after learning she'd taken Harrow's coin too—but enough. Enough was all anybody got out here. She'd built her whole life on *enough*.

They went in slow. Mirabel stood at the bow calling depth from the lead line—six feet, five, four and a half—and the apple trees closed overhead, black branches dripping, fruit gone to mush and ghost-white pulp floating on the water like drowned eyes. The light went green and dim. Somewhere above them a bird complained and fell silent.

At four feet, the channel bent, and Ilsa felt the bottom come up soft under her pole—silt, feet of it, the kind you sink into and never come out of. She tried the other side. Same. Ahead, the surface ahead grew scummy, still, matted with a skin of algae that meant no current had moved through it in weeks.

"It's closing," Mirabel said. Not a question.

"It's closed." Ilsa pulled her pole and let the boat drift. Her heart was doing something strange and cold, because she had bet them all on a memory eleven years old, and memories out here were worth exactly what the drowned country paid for anything: nothing. "Back out. We'll take the west branch and the reed maze and pray Harrow's men are bad at close work."

They backed out into the light, and the light was wrong.

The diesel mutter was louder. Coming from both directions now—or rather, from behind and from ahead, because the sound carried strangely through the trees, and Ilsa realized with a lurch that the second boat hadn't been waiting at the fork at all. It had come around some other way, or it had been launched from a camp she didn't know, and it was already below them. Between them and open water.

"Harrow," Sef said. Just the name, flat, like setting down something heavy.

"No," Ilsa said, listening harder. Two engines, yes. But the near one was idling rough, missing, a sound she knew the way you know a voice. "That's not Harrow's big boat. That's a pusher skiff with a rebuilt engine, and the rebuild was done badly." She almost laughed, and it came out as nothing. "That's a broker patrol skiff. Harrow leases those to claim-jumpers. Whoever's below us isn't Harrow. It's scavengers running his colors without his license."

"Does that help us?" Mirabel asked.

"It means they're greedy instead of disciplined," Ilsa said. "Greedy men can be frightened. Disciplined men can't."

* * *

They took the west branch at speed, engine wide open, because stealth was dead now and noise was all they had left. The reed islands came at them like a crowd parting and closing. Ilsa steered by instinct and old sin, cutting channels she'd smuggled through in another life, and twice the hull kissed mud and shuddered free. Behind them the fork opened and closed out of sight, and the diesel mutter followed, patient, keeping their pace, not pressing. That patience scared her more than pursuit would have. Pursuit meant they wanted you panicked. Patience meant they wanted you tired.

It was Toma who saw the net first.

"Boom," he said, pointing. "Across the channel. Ahead."

A fallen tree, laid deliberate, its trunk riding just at the waterline, brush piled on it. Below it, half-hidden in the reeds, the glint of an aluminum skiff and the shapes of men who had stopped pretending to be quiet. Rifles. Broker colors tied to a pole—orange and black, Harrow's lease-flag, whether licensed or not.

And behind, the diesel swelled. The following boat came around the last bend and slowed, blocking the channel they'd come through, and Ilsa saw the man standing in its bow raise a hand—not a weapon, just a hand, the universal gesture of *nowhere to go*.

They were in a funnel after all. She'd promised them a ditch and delivered a funnel, and the thought landed in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Sef cut the engine. The boat drifted toward the boom. In the silence Ilsa could hear the men ahead talking, easy voices, men with time. Six of them ahead, she counted. Four behind. Ten against four, in open water, with a wounded man aboard and half a tank of fuel and a hold full of charts that ten men would kill for.

"We give them the charts," Toma said suddenly.

Everyone turned.

"We give them the map fragment, we tell them the Wreck's a story, we turn around, we go home." His voice cracked on *home*. "Nobody dies over paper."

"They won't let us turn around," Ilsa said gently. "You know that. You worked for them."

"I worked for the *office*," Toma said. "Not the field men. The field men are—" He stopped. Looked at the rifles. "They're paid per recovery. We're a recovery."

Mirabel had gone very still at the bow, compass closed in her fist, and Ilsa saw her looking not at the guns but at the water beside the boom—at the gap between the tree trunk and the eastern bank, maybe eight feet wide, maybe less, where the reeds grew thickest and the current, finally, showed itself: a thin seam of moving water threading between the boom's anchor point and a drowned fence post.

"You see it," Ilsa said quietly.

"I see it," Mirabel said. "Eight feet. At speed. If we hit the post we tear the hull open and everyone swims in reed water with men shooting at the splashes."

"And if we don't hit the post?"

"Then we're through, and they have to pole after us through the maze, and poling is your country, not theirs."

Sef was already at the engine, hand on the throttle, face gray with pain and decision. "The engine's been dying since the fuel raid. I can give you one burst. Maybe thirty seconds of full power. After that it's done—it'll seize, or it'll catch fire, I honestly don't know which."

"One burst is enough," Ilsa said. "If we spend it right."

Then Toma did the thing she would carry for the rest of her life, the thing she would replay at night on high ground and in river camps and in whatever years she had left: he looked at the gap, and he looked at the skiff behind the boom, and he did the arithmetic faster than any of them.

"They'll shoot at the boat," he said. "They'll shoot at the engine, because Sef told us the engine's the weak point and brokers read wrecks the same way we do. So the engine takes the fire." He was already moving, uncoiling the stern line, pulling the spare fuel bladder—their last—up onto the deck. "Unless there's something at the stern that looks more like an engine than an engine does."

"Toma," Sef said.

"A fuel bladder, wrapped in tarp, lashed to the stern rail." His hands were steady. That was the worst part, Ilsa thought later. His hands were completely steady. "From eighty yards, in reed shadow, that's an engine. They'll put their first volley into it. And a bladder full of our last fuel burns very, very bright, and while they're watching it burn, nobody's watching the gap."

"You don't come back from the stern," Mirabel said. It wasn't an order. It came out like a prayer said by someone who didn't believe.

"Somebody has to lash it," Toma said. "And somebody has to stay with it and make it look real. Wave arms. Shout. Be a target." He grinned, and it was the old deckhand grin, the one from the first day upriver, before the radio died, before the looted camp, before everything. "I'm the best liar on this boat. Ask anyone. I sold our route to Harrow, didn't I? Lying's my trade."

"Toma—" Sef started.

"You keep him anyway," Toma said. "That's what you decided. Traitor or not, you kept me. So let me be worth the keeping." He lashed the bladder fast, three turns and a hitch, professional hands. Then he looked at Ilsa. "Get me close to the boom. Close as you dare. Then hit the gap."

* * *

What happened next lived in Ilsa's memory afterward as a series of frames with the film burned out between them.

The boat surging forward, Sef giving the dying engine everything, the boom growing huge, the riflemen rising from the reeds shouting, the first shots cracking—into the stern, into the bladder, exactly as Toma had read them. The world going orange. Flame sheeting up the stern rail, and Toma silhouetted against it, arms spread, bellowing like a man on fire, drawing every eye on the water—

—and the gap. Eight feet of reed-shadow and moving seam. Ilsa threw her whole body against the pole, and Mirabel was beside her with the sweep oar, and the boat went through the way a needle goes through cloth, gunwales screaming along the drowned fence post, a sound like the country itself objecting—

—a rifle ball punching through the hull below the waterline, one, two—

—open water. Gray, wide, empty open water beyond the maze, and behind them the burning boat-light shrinking, and Toma's voice still carrying across the reeds, still shouting, still playing his lie—

—and then the engine, as Sef had promised, seized with a shriek like a living thing, and the boat coasted, and coasted, and slowed, and stopped.

Silence. Real silence, the honest kind, frogs and all.

They drifted. Ilsa stood with the pole shaking in her hands and listened for Toma. The shouting had stopped. The firing had stopped. Smoke stood up over the reed maze in a greasy column, and the diesel of the following boat was moving—moving away, toward the smoke, toward the decoy, toward the man who had made himself the whole target so the boat could be nothing.

"Toma," Mirabel called, cupping her hands. Nothing answered but birds starting up again, one by one, the way they do when the danger has passed—or moved.

"He'll swim out," Sef said. His voice was wrecked. "He grew up on the river. He'll swim out downstream and find us."

Ilsa said nothing. She had seen the column of smoke. She had done the arithmetic Toma had done, and she knew what a fuel bladder did when a bullet opened it at the rail of a wooden boat, and she knew that the shout had gone on exactly as long as a performance required and not one second longer, and she knew—she knew with the water's own certainty—that the price of the gap had been named and paid, and the name was Toma.

They poled. What else was there. They poled into the gray afternoon with a dead engine, a holed hull they stuffed with shirts, and a silence aboard that none of them could break, because breaking it would mean saying his name and saying his name would make it true.

Near dusk, drifting with the current seam, Ilsa found the stern line coiled on the deck where he'd left it—three turns and a hitch, professional hands—and she sat down beside it and held it and did not cry, because the drowned country taught you early that grief was a luxury you paid for with speed, and speed was the only thing keeping the rest of them alive.

But in the last light, far behind them, at the mouth of the maze, she saw a single skiff lantern come out and hang motionless on the dark water, searching. Searching the banks. Searching the reeds.

Either they were looking for a body.

Or they hadn't found one yet.

And either way, Ilsa thought, taking up the pole, the Wreck was two days further up the dead water—and now they owed a debt that no amount of burning paper would ever settle.
