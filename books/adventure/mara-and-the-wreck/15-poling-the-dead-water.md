## 15. Poling the Dead Water

The engine died a mile past the fork, and after that there was only the sound of poles in water that didn't want to be moved.

Dead water, Ilsa called it. Backwater cut off from the current since before Mirabel was born—flooded country that had drowned and then forgotten how to flow. The channels here were wide as lakes and shallow as dishes, choked with silt and standing timber and the pale skeletons of trees that had been dead for thirty years but still stood upright, bleached white as bone. The surface lay flat as poured glass. Nothing moved on it. Nothing lived in it. When you looked down over the gunwale you saw your own face looking back, and beneath it, faintly, the drowned roofs of a town.

Three days of poling. That was what Ilsa promised. Three days through the dead water and they'd come out below the ridge where the Wreck sat fused into the hillside like a ship that had decided to become geography.

Mirabel had stopped believing promises somewhere around the second hour.

* * *

They took turns. That was the first rule, and Ilsa made it law before they'd gone a hundred yards: two hours on the pole, four hours off, no exceptions, because the pole was not work you could do tired. The channel was shallow enough to walk in places, but walking meant wading into water that came up past your waist and held things—snags, wire, old fence line, the occasional soft shape that had been a body once and was now just an argument between the mud and the flood. Nobody walked. Everybody poled.

Sef went first. His wound had closed into a hard red seam along his ribs, and he poled with his whole body, jaw set, saying nothing, spending himself like a man who'd rather burn out than think. Then Toma. Then Ilsa, who poled the way other people prayed—rhythmic, wordless, her eyes fixed on some middle distance where the water told her things it wouldn't tell anyone else. Then Mirabel.

Two hours each. Six hours a cycle. The days didn't divide anymore; they just accumulated, one gray light bleeding into the next, the sun a smudge behind cloud so thick it might have been a ceiling.

By the end of the first day, Mirabel understood why Ilsa had made the rule about rest. The pole didn't take strength so much as it took everything else. Your shoulders went first, then your hands—you learned to grip with the heel of your palm because your fingers stopped answering—and then something deeper than muscle, some reserve you didn't know you had until you felt it draining. You pushed twelve feet of ash-wood against a riverbed of silt and the boat moved maybe its own length, and then you pulled the pole up dripping and planted it again, and again, and again, and the water gave back nothing. No current to fight, but none to help either. The dead water didn't resist. It just refused.

Rations: half a cup of rice per person per day, cooked once at midday over a smokeless fire Ilsa built on a sheet of tin. Water they drank straight from the channel, boiled, tasting of rust and rot and something mineral and old, like drinking the flood itself. Sef counted the food out with a dealer's precision, and nobody argued, because arguing cost breath and breath had become currency too.

That was the thing nobody warned you about dead water. You rationed air. The heat sat on the channels like a lid, and the rot underneath breathed up through it—a thick, sweetish stink of drowned wood and drowned earth and drowned years—and every lungful cost more than it paid. You learned to breathe shallow. You learned to talk in sentences of five words or fewer. By the second day, conversation had died entirely, and the boat traveled in silence broken only by the knock of poles against hull and the far-off complaint of some bird too stubborn to leave.

Mirabel found she liked the silence. That frightened her more than anything else—the discovery that under enough exhaustion, loneliness stopped being armor and became just skin.

* * *

The hallucinations started the second night.

Ilsa had said they would. She'd said it matter-of-factly, splicing a new tip onto her pole by lamplight: "Second night, you'll hear things. Third day, you'll see them. Don't answer the voices, don't follow the lights, and if you see someone standing on the water, it's a reflection off a root and nothing else." She'd tied off the splice. "And if you see someone you know who's dead, you look away. That's the important one."

"Who did you see?" Toma asked.

"Ilsa doesn't hallucinate," she said, and rolled into her blanket, and that was the end of the conversation.

Mirabel's came at moonrise, which wasn't really moonrise, just a thinning in the cloud cover that let down a little silver light. She was on watch, sitting in the bow with the compass open on her knee—not navigating, there was nothing to navigate toward, just holding it, the way you'd hold a hand. The needle pointed ahead into the dark, steady as ever, toward the Wreck, toward whatever her grandfather had built into it, toward the debt that had a shape now and her family's name on it.

She heard her mother's voice coming off the water.

Not calling. Just talking—the low, half-heard murmur of someone speaking in the next room, the tone without the words, and underneath it the particular rhythm Mirabel would have known anywhere, the way her mother's voice rose slightly at the ends of sentences, as if everything were a question she already knew the answer to. Mirabel sat very still. The voice drifted across the channel, closer, then farther, then closer, and she could almost make out words—her name, maybe, or the name of the town they'd lost, or—

*I know,* she thought. *I know what we owed.*

The voice faded. A root broke the surface fifty yards out, black and glistening, exactly the size and shape of a shoulder.

She looked away. She kept the watch. When Sef relieved her, she handed him the compass without a word and lay down in the bilge and slept like the drowned.

* * *

Day three, the water got strange.

It happened gradually, the way weather happens, and then all at once. The channels began to braid—splitting, rejoining, splitting again—until the boat was threading through a maze of narrow lanes between stands of dead timber, and the lanes all looked identical, and the light came down greenish through the canopy of bare branches so that everything underwater had the color of old glass bottles. Ilsa poled them through without hesitating, reading markers Mirabel couldn't see: a notch in a trunk, a certain lean to a snag, the way the silt clouded or didn't when the pole went down.

"How do you know?" Mirabel asked, five words, breath conserved.

"My father ran this water," Ilsa said. "Before the syndicate bought the charts. He knew it freehand. I'm just remembering him."

Then, twenty minutes later, without turning around: "There's a town under us. Was called Meridian. Had four hundred people and a church with a bell they never rang because the priest said it was cracked. Whole thing's down there, streets and all. On a clear day—" she paused, planting the pole, "—you can see the bell tower from here. Look down."

Mirabel looked down.

The water was clearer here, or the light was kinder, and forty feet below the hull lay a street. An actual street, running straight as a ruled line between foundations, with a stone hitching post still standing at the corner and a child's toy—a hoop, an iron hoop—resting against it as if set down a moment ago. The green light moved over it all in slow waves. It looked less like a drowned place than a sleeping one, a town lying down to wait out something long.

Nobody spoke for a while after that. There weren't words that fit, and anyway words cost breath.

But Mirabel thought: *this is what debt looks like.* Not the ledger kind. The other kind—the kind that sits on top of a place and holds it under, generation after generation, everyone downstream paying interest on a flood. Her grandfather had drafted the charts. Her family had borrowed against rights to water that had swallowed four hundred people and their iron hoops and their unringable bell. And somewhere ahead, fused into a hillside, the paper that proved it all waited in a sealed hold, dry as sin, intact as a lie.

She gripped the pole and pushed, and the boat slid over the sleeping street, and the street did not care.

* * *

The third day was hunger with a horizon.

The rice ran out at noon. Sef divided the last of it into four portions with a knife's edge of precision, and his hand shook doing it—not from weakness, Mirabel saw, but from the arithmetic. He was a man who measured things, and the measurement had come up short. "One more day," he said. "Maybe two. After that we're fishing with our shirts or eating bark."

"You can't eat this bark," Ilsa said. "It'll kill you slower than starving, which is worse."

"Thank you," Sef said, "for the precision."

Toma laughed—a short, cracked sound, the first laugh in two days—and then coughed, and then was quiet again. He hadn't been right since the fork. He took his turns at the pole and did them competently and said nothing, and when he slept he slept curled small, facing the hull, and Mirabel caught herself watching him and wondering what it cost a man to volunteer as a decoy and live. Whether living felt like a gift or a bill.

In the afternoon the heat pressed down until breathing felt like drinking, and Mirabel's turn at the pole blurred. She remembered fragments: the knock of the pole finding bottom. The green light. Her own reflection in the dead water, staring up at her from below like a woman trapped under glass, mouthing something. At some point she became aware that she'd been counting strokes—counting them the way her grandfather must have counted survey chains, the way her mother had counted coins into envelopes, the way debtors count, because counting is the prayer of people who owe—and she realized she was whispering the numbers aloud, and she couldn't remember starting.

*Four hundred and six. Four hundred and seven.*

"What are you counting?" Ilsa asked quietly, from the stern.

Mirabel looked at the number in her mouth. "Strokes."

"Liar," Ilsa said, not unkindly. "You're counting what you're carrying. Everyone does, out here. The water pulls it out of you." She planted the pole and held the boat against nothing, resting, and looked at Mirabel with those river-gray eyes that read water like scripture and people like tributaries. "What is it? Names?"

"Coins," Mirabel said. Before she could stop it. "My family's debt. I've been counting it since I was nine years old. Every coin, every month, every year of interest. I can tell you the exact figure to the coin."

"And if you get to the Wreck," Ilsa said, "and the deeds say what you think they say—what then? Does the number change?"

"I don't know."

"That's honest." Ilsa pushed off. The boat moved its own length. "For what it's worth, my father used to say the dead water has a use. Everything upstream gets carried down and dropped here, and the water goes still, and whatever you've been refusing to carry settles out and you can finally see it on the bottom." She nodded at Mirabel's reflection, upside-down beneath the hull, mouth moving. "Yours is showing."

* * *

Near dusk of the third day, the channels ended.

It happened without ceremony. One moment they were threading the braided lanes between dead timber, and the next the timber fell away behind them and the water opened into a vast brown lagoon ringed by drowned hills, and at the far side of the lagoon a ridge rose out of the flood—black rock, real rock, the first solid ground Mirabel had seen in three days—and something jutted from the hillside above the waterline.

Something long. Something with a spine.

Even at a distance, even through the heat shimmer, there was no mistaking it: a hull, enormous, driven into the slope of the hill at an angle, half-buried, furred with thirty years of moss and creeper, its stern rising sheer out of the green like the wall of a building. The pre-flood barge. The Wreck. It sat exactly where the stolen map fragment said it would sit, exactly where the compass needle pointed, exactly where her grandfather's drafting lines had led—every chart, every instrument, every road of her life converging on this one hillside like water finding a drain.

The compass lay open in Mirabel's hand. The needle didn't waver. It pointed at the Wreck the way it had always pointed, and for the first time she understood that it had never been pointing north at all—it had been pointing here, all along, waiting for her to arrive, patient as inheritance.

"There it is," Sef said hoarsely. He was standing, though the boat rocked, gripping the gunwale, and his eyes had the fever-brightness of a man seeing a payoff after years of loss. "Thirty years. It's real. It's actually—"

"Look," Toma said.

He was pointing at the base of the hill, where the water met the rock. Where the shore should have been empty.

Smoke stood up from behind a fold of the ridge. Thin, deliberate, contained—a cook fire, banked and tended, the smoke of people who expected to stay. And moored in the lee of the hillside, half-hidden in shadow, rode a boat Mirabel recognized with a sick drop in her stomach: a claim-jumper's craft, broker colors at the mast, patched and repainted but unmistakable.

Harrow's men. Here first. Or never left.

Ilsa brought the pole down and held the boat motionless in the middle of the lagoon, and the dead water went utterly still around them, and in that stillness Mirabel heard—faintly, across the whole width of the flood—a sound that did not belong to wind or water or any bird.

A voice. Calling out from the Wreck, across the evening air, in a tone of easy confidence, the tone of a man greeting guests he'd been expecting:

"Well," it called. "About time somebody showed up."
