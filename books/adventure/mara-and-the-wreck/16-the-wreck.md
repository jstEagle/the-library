## 16. The Wreck

They smelled it before they saw it—iron and old tar, a smell like the inside of a lock, rising off the water in the gray hour before dawn.

"Dead water ends here," Ilsa said. She had her pole planted and both hands on it, leaning into the current that wasn't a current but a slow pull, as if the whole channel were draining toward something. "Feel it? The water's going in. Been going in for years."

Mirabel felt it. The boat was being drawn forward without any of them poling, sliding through a corridor of drowned cypress whose trunks had gone black as stove iron. Ahead, the hillside rose out of the flood—a real hill, the first they'd seen since the salt flats, green with strangler fig and hung with vines that trailed into the water like rigging.

And set into the hill's flank, half-swallowed, was the barge.

It was bigger than she'd imagined. Bigger than the maps had made it look, and Sef's fragment had made it look enormous. A pre-flood river vessel, three hundred feet if it was an inch, driven into the slope by whatever wall of water had come down the valley all those years ago, and the hill had simply grown over it, accepted it, made it part of itself. Rust had gone to lace along the upper rail. Trees stood on the deck. But the hull lines were still true beneath the green, and the bow—or what had been the bow—thrust out of the hillside like the prow of a ship sailing into stone.

Nobody spoke. Even Toma, when he'd been aboard, would have had nothing to say to this.

"There," Sef said quietly. He pointed to a seam in the hillside below the waterline, where a rectangle of darker stone showed through the roots: a hatch cover, fused shut, its edges grown over but its shape unmistakable. "The hold. The surveyor's notes put the strongroom twenty meters aft of that. Sealed since the flood." His voice cracked on the last word and he didn't apologize for it.

Ilsa backed water and brought them alongside a fallen fig trunk that had bridged the gap between the boat and a ledge of rusted plating. The climb was ugly—wet iron, wet bark, vines that gave way like rotten rope—but Mirabel went up first because she always went up first, and because her hands needed something to grip that wasn't shaking.

She told herself it was the cold. It wasn't the cold.

* * *

The deck of the Wreck was a forest floor with a spine of steel running down its middle. They walked it single file, boots sinking in leaf mold two generations deep. Somewhere under their feet, sealed and dry, were papers that said who owned the water. All of it. Every channel, every seep, every well from the salt flats to the hills—deeds signed before the flood by men who'd known the water was coming and had priced it accordingly.

One of those men had signed with a drafting hand Mirabel knew better than her own heartbeat. Her grandfather had drawn these charts. Her grandfather had built her compass from the same plates. And her family's debt—the debt she'd carried since she was nineteen, the debt she couldn't name aloud even now—had been incurred buying a piece of what lay under this hill.

She stopped at the base of the superstructure, where a stairway climbed toward what had been the wheelhouse, and looked back at the others. Sef was gray-faced and feverish, his harpoon wound bound in Ilsa's spare shirt, leaning on the rail. Ilsa stood with her pole across her shoulders like a yoke, reading the deck the way she read water.

Then Mirabel saw the smoke.

A thin thread of it, rising from behind the wheelhouse. Woodsmoke. Fresh.

"Down," she breathed—but it was too late, because a voice came down through the trees, unhurried, almost friendly.

"You're late. I expected you two days ago."

* * *

Harrow stepped out from behind the wheelhouse the way a man steps out from behind his own front door.

He was smaller than Mirabel had pictured, and older—sixty maybe, with a broker's soft hands and a claim-jumper's eyes, dressed not in syndicate colors but in plain river cloth, as if he'd decided the Wreck belonged to him so thoroughly he didn't need to advertise it. Four men flanked him with crossbows held low. Not aimed. Aimed was a courtesy you offered strangers.

And beside him, wrists roped in front of him, face swollen on one side but unmistakably, impossibly alive—

"Toma," Sef said. It came out broken.

"He's alive," Harrow agreed pleasantly. "He's been very useful, actually. Toma, tell them how useful you've been."

Toma wouldn't look at them. He stared at the deck between his feet, and when he finally spoke his voice was flat as sheet tin. "I told him about the fork. I told him which channel you'd take. I'm sorry, Sef. I'm sorry, Mirabel. I told him everything."

"He sold your route twice," Harrow said. "Once to me, once to the advance camp's cook, who sold it to me again. A man with two buyers is a man with no loyalty, but he is a man with excellent information." He smiled at Mirabel, and the smile was the worst thing she'd seen since the delirious survivor in the gutted camp. "Mirabel Voss. Do you know, I've been looking forward to meeting you? Your grandfather and I never met, but I've spent eleven years buying up everything his estate ever touched. Charts. Correspondence. Debt paper." He let that word sit. "Especially debt paper."

Mirabel kept her voice level. "You sent four men to kill us in Deadfall Channel."

"I sent four men to slow you down. If I'd wanted you dead, you'd be dead. I need you alive, Miss Voss. You're the only person alive who can open that hold." He nodded past her, toward the dark seam in the hillside below the deck. "Your grandfather built the lock on that strongroom himself. Combination wheel, counterweighted tumblers, keyed to a sequence—not numbers. *Movements.* The way a compass needle moves." He paused, savoring it. "I have the charts. I have the sequence written down. What I don't have is the touch. The mechanism is temperamental. Wrong pressure, wrong rhythm, and it seizes permanently. I've lost two men learning that."

"So you want me to open it," Mirabel said, "and then?"

"And then we discuss your family's account." Harrow began to walk, slowly, and the crossbowmen drifted with him, angling until Mirabel, Sef, and Ilsa were bracketed against the rail. "Here are the terms, plainly, because I respect plainness. You open the hold. Inside are the deeds—water rights to this entire valley, predating the flood, valid under common law the moment they're filed. Your grandfather's firm bought a share of those rights eighty years ago. Your family has been paying interest on that purchase ever since, and losing, because the rights themselves sat underwater. Literally." He spread his soft hands. "Sign your share to me, and every coin your family owes is struck from the book. Today. You walk away owning nothing—which, I understand, is your preference—and owing nothing. That's more than most people get."

"And if she doesn't?" Ilsa asked.

Harrow looked at Toma.

Toma lifted his head at last, and Mirabel saw what was in his face—not fear, exactly. Something worse. Hope. "Take the deal," he said hoarsely. "Mirabel. Take it. It's just paper. It's just money. He'll let us all walk. Please."

Sef pushed off the rail and stood upright on his own, fever-bright, and his voice came out low and hard. "Those deeds belong to everyone whose grandparents drowned building nothing. You burn them, Voss. You know what they are."

"They're my inheritance," Mirabel said, and heard her own voice go strange.

"They're a leash," Sef said. "Same as that compass."

"The compass brought you here," Harrow observed mildly.

Everything hung. The monsoon wind had begun to move through the hilltop trees, and far downstream Mirabel could hear it coming—the deep, gathering roar of the crest, days early, the sound of a season arriving like an army. Water lapped higher against the hull than it had an hour ago. She could feel it through the deck, through her boots, a slow lift, as if the Wreck were taking its first breath in eighty years.

Harrow heard it too. His smile thinned by a degree. "Decide quickly, Miss Voss. The strongroom hatch is below. The water won't wait, and neither will I."

Mirabel looked at Toma—at the rope marks on his wrists, at the hope she couldn't afford to give him. She looked at Sef, who met her eyes and didn't blink. She put her hand in her pocket, around the cracked brass case of the compass, and felt the needle trembling there, pointing not north but *down*, toward the hold, toward the lock built by her grandfather's hands, waiting for hers.

"My grandfather," she said slowly, "built a lock only a Voss could open."

"He did," Harrow agreed. "Sentimental man. Terrible businessman."

"No," Mirabel said. "He knew exactly what he was doing." She pulled the compass out and held it up between them, and watched Harrow's face change as he understood—for the first time, visibly, unwelcome—that the thing he'd spent eleven years buying around had been in her pocket the whole time. "He wanted whoever came here to have a choice. So. Let's go open it."

She started down the stair toward the hatch, and behind her she heard the water rising, and Harrow's men falling in around her, and Toma calling her name once, twice, like a man watching someone walk into deep water on purpose.

The needle pulled. She followed it down.
