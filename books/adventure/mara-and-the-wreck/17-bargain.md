## 17. Bargain

Toma had imagined this moment a hundred times on the river—what he'd say when they finally reached the Wreck, how he'd stand tall and pretend he hadn't sold them all for a sack of coin and a promise of more. In his imaginings there was always a speech. Something clean. Something that made the betrayal sound like survival instead of what it was.

There was no speech now. There was a gun against the back of his skull and Harrow's voice saying, *Gently now,* and the smell of the old barge rising up around them like something drowned but not dead.

The hold of the Wreck smelled of iron and river-mud and, beneath it, something older—tar, maybe, or the ghost of cargo packed away before the flood came and turned the valley into a sea. Toma knelt where Harrow's men had put him, wrists bound in front with wire that bit when he moved. Through the open hatch above, gray monsoon light fell in slanted bars, and in those bars floated dust that had been paper once, and men once, and a whole world that had believed itself permanent.

Mirabel Voss stood ten feet away with a deed-box open at her feet.

Toma had watched her lift the lid. He'd watched her go still in a way he'd never seen a person go still—not frozen, not afraid, just *stopped*, like a clock someone had decided no longer needed to run. Inside the box were documents wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with wax stamped by a government that no longer existed, and every one of them carried a name Toma had heard her say only once, quietly, to the compass she carried like a wound: *Voss.*

"Careful," Harrow said. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, dressed in broker colors that tried too hard—deep green, gold thread—and he moved through the Wreck's hold like a man walking through a house he already owned. "Paper that old crumbles if you look at it wrong. Which is rather the point."

Sef Adama was on his knees beside Toma, hands wired behind him, blood from the harpoon wound at Deadfall Channel seeping fresh through the bandage Ilsa had tied two days ago. Sef wasn't looking at the deeds. He was looking at Mirabel, and his face had the particular set of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

Ilsa Renn stood apart, near the ladder, her pole still in her hand as if a weapon might matter here. It didn't. Four of Harrow's men held the deck above; two held the hold; one held Toma's hair in his fist whenever Toma breathed too loud. And Harrow held everything else—the exits, the boat, the season, the law, or what passed for it out here where the water had swallowed the courthouses whole.

"Well," Harrow said, and clapped his hands together softly, a rich man's applause. "Look at that. Intact. Sealed. Sixty years under the flood line and the wax never even sweated." He crouched by the box, close enough that Mirabel could have struck him, and didn't, which told Toma everything about the geometry of the room. "Do you know what these are, Miss Voss? Not rhetorically. Do you actually know?"

"Water rights," Mirabel said. Her voice was flat as the salt flats. "Pre-flood grants. Senior claims. Whoever holds these papers owns every drop that moves through this valley."

"Every drop," Harrow agreed, delighted. "And everyone who drinks it. The settlements downstream. Callow's Landing. The pumping stations. The ferry tolls. All of it, downstream of a signature." He straightened, brushed his knees though there was nothing on them. "Your grandfather drafted half of these himself. Lovely hand. Very precise. You can see where your own stubbornness comes from."

Toma flinched. He couldn't help it. He'd known—Ilsa had told him on the dead water, in a whisper, while Sef slept—that the compass pointed at the Wreck because the man who built it had helped make the map. But hearing it said aloud, casually, as leverage, made it real in a way whispers weren't.

Mirabel didn't flinch. That was the thing about her that Toma had never understood and had come, somewhere over the last hundred miles of drowning country, to be afraid of. She absorbed blows the way the salt flats absorbed rain: nothing showed, nothing ran off, and you suspected that underneath, something was being kept.

"So here is my offer," Harrow said. "And I want you to hear all of it before anyone starts performing principle."

He nodded to one of his men, who produced a leather folio and laid it on top of the deed-box with the reverence of an acolyte arranging relics.

"A bill of sale," Harrow said. "Assigning the rights in this box to me and my syndicate, in perpetuity, for consideration rendered. Standard language. My lawyers—it amuses me to call them that—drafted it in Callow's Landing before we ever launched." He smiled at Mirabel. "And in exchange: your family's debt. Erased. Every ledger entry, every marker, every name scratched into every book between here and the coast. Gone. Your grandfather borrowed against these rights sixty years ago, did you know that? Borrowed, and defaulted, and the debt has been sold and resold until it landed with me. It's mine to forgive. I'm offering to forgive it."

"You're offering to buy her," Sef said.

"I'm offering to *settle* her," Harrow said pleasantly. "There's a difference, and the difference is that she walks away free. Name clear. Hands empty. No syndicate, no colors, no obligations. Just a woman and a compass and a desert full of places to disappear." He spread his hands. "You of all people should appreciate that offer, Miss Voss. Isn't disappearing your whole profession?"

Toma looked at Mirabel's back—at the set of her shoulders, the way her weight sat slightly forward on her feet even standing still, a ranger's habit, always ready to move—and he thought: *she's going to refuse.* He knew it the way you know weather coming. And knowing it, he did the thing he'd sworn on the church steeple he wouldn't do again.

He opened his mouth.

"Take the deal."

Everyone looked at him. Harrow's man tightened his grip in Toma's hair, but Harrow raised a finger—*let him talk*—because Harrow, Toma realized with a sick lurch, had been counting on exactly this. Had probably picked Toma out on the dock at Callow's Landing months ago. A man who'd sell a route would sell an argument.

"Toma," Sef said quietly. Warning in it. Or pity. Toma honestly couldn't tell anymore, and that was worse.

"No. Listen." His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going anyway, because the words had been building in him since the dead water, since the hallucinations, since the day he'd watched the engine die at the fork and known that Toma-the-decoy was the only currency he had left to spend. "You don't know what that debt is. I do. I've worked for brokers my whole life. I've seen ledgers. Debts don't die, they *breed*. Interest, fees, transfer costs—you burn these papers and in ten years some other Harrow shows up holding your grandfather's signature and a number three times bigger, and there'll be no deeds left to trade for it. Take the deal. Walk away clean. It's the only door out of this room that doesn't have water behind it."

"The only door," Ilsa said from the ladder, and her voice was dry as her namesake, "that has *him* behind it." She tipped her chin at Harrow without looking at him, the way you'd indicate a hazard on the water. "I signed his folios once. Two years. You know how his settlements work? He forgives the debt and holds the forgiveness. Every year, a renewal. Every renewal, a favor. You're not buying freedom, girl. You're renting a leash and calling it a gift."

"I changed my mind about him," Toma said. "That's different."

"It is different," Ilsa agreed. "It cost you a boat and nearly a life. Ask yourself what it'll cost her."

Harrow laughed—a real laugh, warm, horrible. "Renn, you are wasted on a pole. Adama. Let's hear from the expedition chief. You came all this way. Spent a boat, a crew, most of your fuel, and I'd wager most of your savings. What's your philosophy? Burn them?"

Sef took his time. Toma had learned that about him on the river: the man treated silence like ballast, something you shipped deliberately to keep steady. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough from the wound and the fever that rode behind it.

"They're not ours to burn."

Toma blinked. Of all the answers—

"Not hers alone, not yours, Harrow, not mine. Those grants cover water that forty thousand people drink. Her grandfather drafted them; her grandfather also mortgaged them, which means the paper's been tangled in courts and ledgers since before the flood. There might be a dozen legitimate claimants. There might be none. Burning them doesn't settle anything—it just makes sure nobody ever settles it fairly. And signing them—" he looked at Mirabel's back, and something in his face went older, "—signing them makes forty thousand people drink on your permission for the rest of their lives. Both options are theft. One's just faster."

"Beautifully said," Harrow murmured. "Truly. You should write speeches for the common-law crowd downriver. They love that sort of thing right up until the pumps fail." He turned back to Mirabel, and his warmth dropped away like a coat taken off indoors, and what was underneath was just arithmetic. "But speeches aren't offers. Here's the rest of it, Miss Voss, so we're honest with each other. The crest hits tonight. The survey teams measured it at Callow's Landing yesterday—two meters over the '09 mark. This barge shifts. Everything loose in this valley shifts. If you walk out of here without signing, I take the box, I take the boat, and I leave the four of you on this hillside to negotiate with the water. If you sign, you walk out ahead of the crest with a clean name and my personal guarantee of passage." He paused. "And the boy keeps both hands."

The gun barrel shifted against Toma's skull, a small cold adjustment, and Toma understood that Harrow hadn't been threatening him at all. He'd been *pricing* him. A line item. Consideration rendered.

"That's not an offer," Sef said, and now there was steel under the fever. "That's a hostage note."

"It's a market," Harrow said. "Same thing, out here."

* * *

The light through the hatch was going the color of old tin. Somewhere above them, rain had started on the deck, a sound like static, like a radio dying mid-sentence. Toma's legs had gone numb under him and he was grateful, because numb legs couldn't carry him anywhere stupid.

He watched Mirabel kneel and lift one of the oilcloth packets out of the box. Harrow's men stirred; Harrow stilled them with a hand. She unwrapped it slowly, and Toma saw the top document: heavy stock, faded ink, a seal like a sunburst, and columns of fine drafting that even he could tell were beautiful. Her grandfather's hand. The same hand, he realized, that had etched the compass dial she carried—the same obsessive precision, the same small hooked capitals.

She read it for a long time. Nobody rushed her. Even the rain seemed to wait.

"My grandfather bought land with these," she said finally, not turning around. "A farm. On the high bank, before the flood. He mortgaged the rights to buy the seed and the stock, and then the water came, and the farm went under, and the debt stayed above the waterline. It's the only thing our family ever managed to keep." She set the packet down. "He used to say the debt was the truest thing he owned. Because everything else could flood."

"Sentimental," Harrow said. "Also irrelevant."

"He also wrote on the back of this one." She turned the document over, and Toma craned to see: a single line in that precise hand, smaller than the rest, almost hidden in the margin. He couldn't read it from where he knelt. But he saw Mirabel read it, and he saw her go still again, that stopped-clock stillness, and when she spoke her voice had changed—lower, like she was reading aloud to someone dead.

"*Water isn't property. Water is what property floats on. Any man who sells the second and calls it the first has drowned twice.*"

Silence. Even Harrow let it sit for a moment.

"Grandfathers," he said at last, gently, "are not legally binding."

"No," Mirabel said. "They're not."

She stood. She looked at Toma first—he'd remember that, later, that she looked at him first, the traitor, the hostage, the argument—and her eyes weren't angry. They were tired in a way that had nothing to do with the river. Then Sef. Then Ilsa. Then Harrow.

Then she reached down and closed the deed-box lid, and the sound of it was very small and very final, like a latch on a door in a house that was already underwater.

"Here's my counteroffer," Mirabel Voss said.

Outside, the rain stopped being static and became a roar, and the hillside beneath the Wreck gave a long, groaning shudder, like something rolling over in its sleep, and every man in the hold remembered at the same moment that the crest was coming tonight, and that no signature on earth was senior to the water.

Harrow's hand drifted toward the folio. Toward the gun at his belt. Toward whatever came next.

Mirabel smiled at him, and it was the loneliest thing Toma had ever seen.

"You'd better hear it fast," she said. "We're all about to be underwater."
