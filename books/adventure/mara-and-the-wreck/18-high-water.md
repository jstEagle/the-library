## 18. High Water

The water spoke first.

I'd been asleep against the sealed hold door, the deed-box's iron weight warm under my palm, when the sound came up through the hull—a long, low groan, like a whale dying in another language. Then a second sound beneath it: rushing, vast and organized, the sound of a river that had stopped being a river and become an argument.

"The crest," Ilsa said. She was already at the rail, poler planted, her face turned toward the channel we'd come up. "It's early."

"It's not early." Sef was limping toward us from the stern, his harpoon wound bound in strips of sailcloth gone brown at the edges. "It's exactly on time. We're the ones who were late."

The Wreck shuddered.

I'd spent three days learning its moods—the way it ticked as the sun heated the old steel, the way it sighed when the current leaned on it. This was neither. This was the whole structure moving, a hillside of fused barge and silt and sixty years of drowned forest shifting on whatever bones it rested on. Somewhere below us, rivets let go in sequence, a string of small explosions traveling down into the dark.

"How long?" I asked.

"Long enough," Ilsa said. "If we don't waste it."

* * *

Harrow came up the ladder with Toma in front of him like a shield, and I understood in one cold look that the negotiation was over before it started. He'd never intended to trade. Leverage doesn't get traded; it gets spent.

"Mirabel Voss." He said my name the way a broker reads a ledger line—no warmth, no malice, just valuation. "You have something of mine."

"You have something of mine too," I said.

"Toma isn't yours. He sold himself to me years ago. You've all been carrying his debt this whole time without knowing it." Harrow smiled at the flinch that went through Sef. "That's the thing about debts. They travel. They find people. Your grandfather understood that better than anyone—that's why he built the compass, why he buried the deeds here instead of spending them. A debt you can't see is a debt that owns you."

"And one you can see?" Ilsa asked, flat.

"Is a debt you can sell." Harrow extended his hand toward me, palm open, patient. "The box, Voss. The water comes either way. The only question is whether you drown rich or drown clean."

Behind him, Toma looked at me. Not pleading. That was the worst part—he wasn't begging. His eyes said what they'd been saying since the night he confessed: *I did this. Whatever it costs, it should cost me.*

The Wreck groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere deep in the hillside a section of hull tore free with a sound like a giant unzipping the world. Water geysered up through the deck planking ten yards aft of us, black and glittering, and fell back as rain.

"Mirabel," Sef said quietly. "Whatever you're going to do. Do it fast."

* * *

Here is what I want to be honest about, because honesty is the only inheritance I ever actually received: in that moment, I wanted to take the deal.

Not because I'm a coward. Because I'm tired. My whole life has been arithmetic—every meal weighed against every mile, every kindness priced against every risk, the debt riding on my back like a second spine. Harrow was offering to take it off. One signature. One box. And then I could walk out of the drowned country light, the way I'd walked into it, invisible again, owing nothing to anyone.

My grandfather had carried these deeds for forty years and never spent them. I used to think that was weakness. Standing there with the flood coming up through the floorboards, I finally understood it was the opposite. Some things you don't spend because spending them would make everything you are into a transaction. He'd kept them sealed so that his name would mean more than paper.

He'd also built a compass that lied about north. Nobody's clean.

"Harrow," I said. "Do you know what happens to a man who sells water rights during a monsoon?"

"He gets paid."

"He gets paid once." I shifted the box behind me, feeling its edges bite into my hip. "And then every year after, for the rest of his life, somebody downstream dies of thirst or drowns on land that used to be theirs, and their family learns your name. You won't own the water, Harrow. You'll own the reason they hate you. That's not wealth. That's a target with interest accruing."

His smile thinned. "Poetry. From a ranger."

"My grandfather wrote poetry too. Right on the margins of his charts. Little notes about where the channels would run after the flood." I took a step back, toward the hold door. "He was right about all of them. Which means he was right about this one too—the note on the back of the map fragment. Did you ever read it? *The water decides. We only decide what we carry.*"

"I read it," Harrow said. "I thought it was sentimental."

"It's hydrology."

The crest arrived.

* * *

There's no describing it honestly, so I'll describe it badly. The channel behind us went white—not with foam but with volume, a wall of brown water maybe four meters high, carrying entire trees, a dead cow, half a roof, moving at the speed of a galloping horse. The sound hit us before the water did, a roar that swallowed speech, and the Wreck lurched like a ship taking a wave broadside.

We all went down. I heard Sef shout something—I'll never know what—and then the world was noise and spray and the terrible grinding of the hillside-ship tearing loose from its moorings of mud.

I crawled. There's no heroism in the memory, only hands and knees and the iron box clutched to my chest like a child, and the hold door ahead of me swinging loose on its hinges. Behind me, Harrow was screaming orders at men who no longer existed—his crew had scattered or been swept at the fork, days ago; he'd come up the ladder alone except for Toma, and now even that arithmetic was failing him.

The water took the deck in sections. I watched it happen from the doorway of the hold: the stern going first, peeling away like bark, then the midsection tilting, and Harrow sliding, scrabbling, catching the base of the cargo crane—one hand, then both, the flood tearing at his legs, trying to strip him off the Wreck like a label.

"Voss!" he screamed. "VOSS! The box! Give me the box and I'll pull you out of this—you'll drown otherwise—you'll—"

I didn't answer. I was already inside.

* * *

The hold was dry. That was the miracle nobody had planned for—the crest had come so hard and so fast that it hadn't yet found the seams, and in the last sealed room of a sixty-year-old barge, lit by nothing but the gray light through a shattered vent, I stood among my grandfather's life's work.

Crates. Dozens of them, stenciled with the survey office mark, packed in waxed paper and pitch. Charts rolled in oilcloth tubes. And on a shelf bolted directly to the frame, wrapped in canvas gone stiff as bark, a row of deed-boxes—iron, small, each stamped with a number and a name.

I found ours third from the left. VOSS, M. Two initials. My grandfather's, and mine, and I don't believe in omens but my hands shook anyway.

The Wreck was dying around me. I could feel it through the soles of my boots, the whole structure torquing, and above the roar of the flood I heard something worse: voices. Sef, shouting my name. Ilsa, shouting Sef's. And underneath, thin and cracking, Toma.

I got the box open—it wasn't locked; it had never needed to be locked; it had only ever needed to be lost—and inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were the deeds. Sealed water rights, predating the flood, signed by officials whose titles no longer existed, granting the Voss family dominion over channels that were now indistinguishable from the rest of the drowning world. Paper worth a fortune to a broker. Paper worth nothing to the water.

Paper worth everything, if you believed names could own rivers.

I stuffed them inside my jacket, against my chest, next to the compass, and ran for the door.

* * *

The deck was gone. Not all of it—a spine of planking remained, running from the hold door to the crane assembly, and along that spine the flood was doing its work. Sef had lashed himself to the crane's base with rope, Ilsa beside him, both of them half-submerged in the surge that swept across the remaining deck. And twenty feet beyond them, clinging to the crane's arm itself, was Toma.

Below Toma, where the deck had been, there was only water—and in the water, going under for the second time, Harrow.

He surfaced. He caught the crane's footing. He reached up and grabbed Toma's ankle.

I saw it all in the space between two waves. Toma looking down at the man who owned him, the man who'd bought his silence and his route and his shame. Harrow looking up, mouth working—*pull me up, pull me up, boy, and I'll tear up your contract, I'll make you rich, I'll*—

Toma let go.

Not kicked. Let go. Opened his fingers the way you set down something heavy you've carried too far, and Harrow dropped back into the brown water, and the crest took him the way it takes everything—without malice, without ceremony, without ever learning his name.

Then Toma climbed. Hand over hand up the crane arm while the Wreck broke apart beneath him, and I understood what he was doing. He wasn't climbing to safety. The crane was the highest point left, and the highest point was the last thing to go, and Toma had decided—calmly, the way he did everything—to be the last thing to go with it.

"No," I said. Out loud. To nobody. "No, you don't."

* * *

Getting to him was the worst hundred feet of my life. The deck spine was underwater in places, surfacing in others, flexing like a living thing. Ilsa saw me coming and screamed something—I caught only *rope*—and threw me a coil end that I caught against my ribs hard enough to crack one. I tied it off at the hold doorframe, the last solid iron on the ship, and went out along my own line hand over hand while the flood tried to peel me off like a decal.

Sef was cutting himself free as I passed him. "The boat—" he shouted. "Ilsa's got the boat at the lee side—we can't hold here—"

"Toma!"

"I know!" And then, quieter, in the half-second between waves: "Mirabel. He made his choice. Don't make him watch you make a worse one."

I reached the crane. Toma was straddling the arm, arms wrapped around the cold iron, water breaking over him in sheets. Up close his face was calm in a way that frightened me more than any panic would have.

"You should be on the boat," he said.

"So should you."

"I sold the route, Mirabel. I sold all of you. Harrow's men knew every channel because I drew them a chart." He shook his head, and for a moment he looked sixteen, the age he must have been when Harrow first put a price on him. "Ilsa forgave me because Ilsa forgives. Sef kept me because Sef needs hands. But you—you tracked a thief across salt flats for a debt you wouldn't even say out loud. You know what owing is. So you know some debts don't get paid by walking away from them."

"This isn't payment. This is drowning."

"Same thing, sometimes." He looked past me, at the water, at the place where Harrow had gone under. "He's dead. Somebody has to stay with the paperwork."

The Wreck gave a final, catastrophic groan. The crane arm tilted—ten degrees, fifteen—and below us the last of the deck folded into the flood like wet bread.

"Toma." I got my hand on his wrist. Under the water and the wind, I said the thing I'd never said to anyone: "My grandfather bought those rights with money he borrowed from a broker. That's my debt. That's the debt I've been carrying since I was nine years old—his signature, his borrowing, his name on the paper. It was never mine. And I am not letting this family add your death to the principal. Do you hear me? We are not paying interest anymore."

Something moved in his face. Not hope. Recognition, maybe—the look of a man hearing a language he'd forgotten he spoke.

"The boat," he said finally. "Get to the boat."

"Not without you."

"Mirabel—"

"*Not without you.*"

The crane arm went.

* * *

We hit the water together. The flood closed over us like a fist, and for a long time there was no world, only tumbling brown chaos and the deeds hammering against my chest and the compass cord biting into my neck. I lost Toma's wrist. I lost up and down. I lost, briefly, any belief that I would surface at all.

Then the rope went taut.

Ilsa had held the line. Through all of it—the surge, the debris, the Wreck coming apart behind us like a sandcastle in a tidepool—Ilsa Renn had stood in her boat with my lifeline wrapped twice around her forearms, leaning back against the flood with her heels braced, and she had not let go.

We came up gasping. Her hand found my collar, then Toma's, and she hauled us over the gunwale one after the other like fish, and Sef collapsed across us both, laughing or sobbing, impossible to tell.

Behind us, the Wreck settled. Not sank—settled, the crest passing over it, the hillside-ship finding some deeper bone to rest on. The crane arm was gone. The hold door was gone. Everything we'd nearly died for was either in my jacket or in the water, and the rain came down on all of it equally.

Toma lay in the bilge, coughing river out of his lungs, alive. After a while he turned his head and looked at me.

"You said family," he croaked.

"I said what I said."

* * *

We rode the crest downstream through the drowned forest, Ilsa poling when the water allowed and steering with the current when it didn't, the five of us—four of us, I corrected myself, still counting a ghost—in a boat with a cracked hull and no engine and a cargo of one iron box full of paper I already knew I was going to burn.

But that decision belonged to high ground, and high ground was hours away, and the monsoon had more to say before it was done.

Because when the light came up gray through the rain, I opened my jacket to check the deeds, and found something else tucked among them—oilcloth, folded small, that hadn't been in the box when I'd opened it. A chart fragment. My grandfather's drafting, unmistakable, the little marginal notes crawling along the channel lines like ants.

And at the bottom, in fresh ink, in a hand I didn't recognize:

*One box was never enough. Ask Callow's Landing who else holds a key.*

Ilsa leaned over my shoulder. Read it. Looked at me.

"Well," she said. "Now we know why Harrow was smiling."
