## 19. Ash and Ink

Sef Adama had spent eleven years of his life chasing paper.

He'd chased it through flooded courthouses in the drowned country, through the archives of Callow's Landing where the clerks charged by the candle-hour, through the memories of old men who remembered what the river had been before it swallowed everything. He'd chased it with survey crews and bribed deckhands and a radio he'd paid too much for because it reached farther than any other radio on the water. He had told himself, all that time, that he was chasing justice—that the deeds sealed inside the Wreck belonged to the people whose names were written on them, and that whoever brought them up into daylight would be handing back something stolen.

He had never quite believed it. But he had said it often enough that it had worn a groove in him, the way water wears a groove in stone, and now, standing on the high ground above the flood line with his arm strapped to his chest and Harrow's blood still somewhere under his fingernails, he watched Mirabel Voss feed those papers into a fire one page at a time, and he understood that everything he had said for eleven years had been a kind of lie he'd needed to survive.

The fire was small. That was the obscene part of it. Eleven years of chasing, three dead crew members, Toma gone into the flood with a harpoon gun in his hands and a look on his face like a man finally paying off a debt of his own—and the whole edifice came down to a cookfire's worth of flame on a gravel bar that wouldn't exist again until the monsoon retreated.

Mirabel knelt beside it with her sleeves rolled up. She didn't hurry. She took each deed from the oilskin packet, held it a moment as if reading it—she wasn't reading it, Sef realized; she was letting it be seen, one last time, by someone—and then laid it on the coals. The vellum caught slow, curled, went black at the edges, and lifted off in flakes that the wind took east, toward the desert she'd come from.

"You could stop," Sef said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "You understand that. Right now, this minute, you could stop."

"I know." She didn't look up.

"That's not common law you're burning. That's not some clerk's copy. Those are the originals. Sealed signatures. Pre-flood registration marks. Whatever a court decides after this, there will be nothing to decide *from*." He heard himself speeding up, the way he did when a channel narrowed. "Harrow's syndicate falls apart without them, fine, I'll grant you that. But so does every claim in the valley. Every farmstead that's been paying lease on land they thought they owned. Every town that built its charter on those registrations. You're not freeing anyone, Voss. You're drowning them all over again."

"They've been drowning for thirty years," Mirabel said. "The paper just made it legal."

She fed another page to the fire.

Sef turned away. His shoulder throbbed where the harpoon had gone through—not deep, Ilsa's stitching was better than any field doctor's, but deep enough that every breath reminded him of Deadfall Channel, of the snare-line singing taut, of the half-second where he'd understood that Ilsa Renn had led them into a trap and then, impossibly, hadn't. He sat down on a slab of flood-scoured limestone and watched the water instead. The monsoon crest had passed in the night; the river below was running brown and fast and full of trees, whole islands of drowned forest sailing past like funeral barges. Somewhere downriver, Toma was in that water or under it. Sef couldn't make himself think the third option.

"You want to know the worst part?" he said, when he trusted his voice again.

"Tell me anyway."

"The worst part is that you're right." He laughed, and it hurt his ribs. "I've known since Charts in the Dark. Since you looked at your grandfather's drafting style and went white as salt. I knew a woman who found her own name in the middle of a fortune doesn't burn that fortune unless she's already decided what it costs. I just thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought if we got here first, if the deeds came up clean, I could argue you out of it. I'm good at arguing. It's most of what I am."

Mirabel looked at him then. Firelight moved on her face. Up close, in this light, she looked younger than she ever let herself look on the boat—the armor off, or at least unbuckled.

"Why did you really hire me?" she asked. "In Callow's Landing. Double pay for a thief. You never told me why you picked me out of every tracker on the landing."

Because your grandfather drew the charts, he almost said. Because I recognized the compass on your belt before I recognized your face. Because I'd been looking for a Voss for six years and there you were, selling your invisibility by the day.

"Because you were the only one who asked about the thief instead of the reward," he said.

It wasn't a lie. It just wasn't the truth either. He was getting good at that distinction, living around these two women.

* * *

Ilsa Renn came up the slope an hour later, poling a salvaged skiff against the current with the easy, insulting grace she brought to everything on water. She grounded the bow, stepped off, and stood watching the fire with her head tilted, the way she read channels—looking for the shape underneath.

"Harrow's men pulled out at dawn," she said. "Three boats, running downstream fast, no colors flying. Word'll reach the Landing inside a week that the syndicate's claims are ash. By the time anyone thinks to dispute it, the flood season will have redrawn half the boundaries anyway." She shrugged. "Water settles more arguments than courts do."

"You're very calm," Sef said, "for a woman who just watched a fortune burn."

"I never expected to see a fortune." Ilsa crouched by the fire, held her palms over it. Her hands were rope-scarred and steady. "I expected a cut. Then I expected to die in Deadfall Channel. Everything since has been extra." She glanced sideways at Mirabel. "Except one thing."

She reached into her jacket and drew out the map fragment—the stolen chart, the thing that had started all of it, water-stained and soft as cloth now from handling. Sef felt his chest tighten. After everything, the actual object. The reason for the expedition, the theft, the chase, the ambush, Toma—

"You're taking it," he said flatly. "Of course you are."

"I'm keeping it," Ilsa corrected. "Different thing." She turned the fragment over in her fingers. On the reverse, faint, in the same precise hand as the front—Voss hand, Sef knew now, Voss drafting, Voss ink—was a scatter of small annotations. Soundings. Dates. A private record of a river that no longer existed. "This isn't a deed. It's not power over anybody. It's a picture of where the water used to go. Somebody's grandmother walked these channels and wrote down what she saw." She folded it small and put it away. "That's worth keeping. The rest was just weight."

"And the cut?" Sef asked. "Your fee. You guided us through backwaters on a promise."

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face shifted—the broker's arithmetic going out of it, leaving something older and harder to price.

"Toma paid it," she said.

Nobody spoke after that. The fire cracked. Downriver, the brown water kept carrying the drowned forest toward the sea.

* * *

They burned the deeds until there was nothing left to burn. It took longer than Sef expected—there were more pages than the fragment had suggested, decades of registrations, amendments, transfers, each one signed and sealed by clerks who had probably drowned believing their ink meant something. Mirabel worked through the packet methodically, and Sef found he couldn't leave. He told himself it was because he intended to memorize the registry numbers, in case anything survived in duplicate, in case a court someday wanted testimony. He told himself a lot of things sitting on that limestone slab.

Near the end, she held up a single sheet and stopped.

"What is it?" he asked.

She didn't answer right away. The wind gusted, and he saw the page clearly for a second: a transfer instrument, old style, the signature block heavy with flourishes. And in the consideration clause, in language older than the flood, a debt recorded—a debt incurred against future water rights, secured by the family name.

*Voss.*

Her family's debt. The one she carried like a stone in her pocket, the one she'd never once named aloud in all the weeks he'd known her. The original of it. The proof that her grandfather had bought survival with the very rights sealed in the Wreck, and that the bill had come due generation after generation until it landed on a girl who tracked thieves across salt flats because she couldn't afford to stay anywhere.

"You could keep that one," Sef heard himself say. "Burn the rest. Keep that. It's evidence. It clears you—it shows the debt was secured against assets that no longer legally exist. Any arbiter in the Landing would void it."

"I know what it would do." Mirabel's voice was quiet. "That's why it goes in last."

"Sef." She said his name, and he realized she almost never did. "When I take jobs, I take them invisible. No name, no history, no debts named aloud. You hired a ghost because ghosts don't ask questions. But I'm not a ghost. I'm the last person holding a bill my family ran up thirty years ago, and every year I didn't pay it, somebody else paid interest on it. Farmers. Towns. People like Toma." Her jaw tightened. "If I keep this page, I keep the argument. There's always another arbiter, another buyer, another Sef Adama with a good reason. The only way out of a debt like this is to stop pretending it can be traded."

She laid the page on the coals.

It burned like all the others. The wind took the ash east.

Sef sat with the strange lightness that followed, and couldn't tell if it was grief or relief or both wearing the same coat. He had come upriver to recover a fortune and return it to its owners. The fortune was smoke. Its owners were a tracker who owned nothing but a broken compass and a name she was only now learning to say out loud, a poler who kept pictures instead of money, and himself—an expedition chief with a gutted boat, a dead crew, a ruined reputation, and, for the first time in eleven years, absolutely nothing left to lose.

It should have felt like failure. Instead it felt like setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"We should get downstream," he said at last. "Before the water drops and strands us up here with Harrow's ghosts."

"In a minute." Mirabel stirred the coals with a stick, making sure. Then she looked up at the sky, where the storm clouds were breaking apart into long white bars, the first clear weather in nine days. "Do you think it'll hold? The common law. After we're gone. Do you think the towns will actually get the water?"

Sef considered lying. He was, after all, good at it.

"No," he said. "Somebody else will build a new Harrow within five years. Somebody with new paper." He paused. "But it'll take them five years. And in five years, kids will grow up drinking water nobody charged them for. That's not nothing. That might be the most anything ever gets."

Mirabel nodded slowly, like a woman accepting terms.

Then Ilsa, who had gone down to check the skiff, called up the slope in a voice pitched low and sharp—the voice she used for water that had changed overnight.

"You both want to see this. Now."

* * *

At the waterline, Ilsa pointed downstream.

A boat was coming up against the current—small, battered, riding low. Not Harrow's colors. Not syndicate steel. A patched hull, a jury-rigged mast, and at the tiller a figure Sef's eyes refused to resolve and then, all at once, resolved completely: broad shoulders, one arm wrapped in filthy bandages, a face burned dark by sun and spray.

Toma.

Alive. Poling and sailing and hauling, alone, upriver toward them, toward the high ground, toward the fire's last smoke—as if he'd known exactly where to look.

"He's alive," Mirabel breathed.

"He's alive," Ilsa said, and for the first time since Sef had met her, Ilsa Renn smiled like the water hadn't taught her to.

Sef said nothing. He stood on the gravel with his ruined shoulder and his empty hands and watched the impossible boat come closer, and understood that the world had just handed him a bill of a different kind—one marked *paid in full*—and that he had no idea yet what it had cost Toma to carry it home.
