## 20. Settlement

The river gave them back the way it had taken everything else: slowly, and without apology.

Ilsa Renn stood at the stern of the crippled boat with a pole in her hands and blisters that had opened, healed, and opened again across both palms, watching Callow's Landing assemble itself out of the morning haze. First the smoke from the fish-drying racks, thin gray threads against a sky the color of wet slate. Then the crane at the fuel dock, its arm folded like a sleeping bird. Then the rooftops, the tin and tarpaper of them, and the long low shed where Harrow's men had once hung their broker colors like a flag over everything worth owning.

The colors were gone.

She noticed it before anyone else did—before Sef, who was below deck trying to keep their last working lantern dry, before Mirabel, who sat in the bow with her arms around her knees and her eyes fixed on nothing, the way she'd sat for three days since the high water took Toma's channel and nearly took the rest of them with it. The pole on the harbor office still flew the town's colors, mud-brown and river-green, but the black-and-gold of the Harrow syndicate—the banner that had flown from every mast, every gatepost, every debt notice nailed to a door between here and the salt flats—that was gone. Not lowered. Gone. Cut down, by the look of the frayed halyard swinging loose against the pole.

"Someone beat us to it," Ilsa said.

Mirabel didn't turn. "Or no one had anything left to hold it up with."

Ilsa tested the bottom with her pole. Six feet, then five, then the soft grab of silt that meant the channel into the landing. She'd read this water since she was nine years old, back when her mother poled tourists through the drowned orchards and told them stories about the flood as if it were a grandmother instead of a murderer. She knew every sandbar's mood, every snag's temper. And she knew what it meant when a town's colors came down without a fight: not liberation. Vacuum.

"Water rights revert," Sef said from the companionway, his voice rough with the fever he'd been fighting since the harpoon wound went bad and then, slowly, didn't. He climbed up into the gray light, moving like a man carrying his own body as cargo. "Common law. That's what the deeds said. That's what your grandfather wrote into them, Mirabel—reversion clause, automatic, if the claims were ever destroyed or abandoned. The old surveyor knew exactly what he was doing."

"He knew," Mirabel said.

It wasn't agreement. It was something flatter than that, and Ilsa watched her the way she'd learned to watch the river during ice-out, when the surface looked calm and the current underneath could pull a grown man under in four seconds. Mirabel Voss had burned those deeds on the high ground above the Wreck with a face like a woman reading her own eulogy. She hadn't cried. She hadn't smiled either. She'd fed the paper to the fire page by page, and when it was done she'd stood there until the ash stopped glowing, and then she'd walked down to the boat and asked if anyone wanted tea, as if they hadn't just unmade a hundred years of ownership with a box of matches.

Ilsa had decided, somewhere in the dead water, that she liked her for it. She'd also decided that liking Mirabel Voss was going to cost her. Everything decent did.

* * *

They came into the landing under pole power alone, the engine a fused lump of metal that Toma had died buying them time to lose, and the whole town turned out to watch them do it. Not a welcome. A witnessing. Fishermen on the dock, women on the seawall, children hanging off the crane's guy-wires. Nobody shouted. Nobody waved. They just watched the broken boat slide in, watched the tall sick man and the desert woman and the poler nobody in this town had seen in six years, and Ilsa felt their eyes doing arithmetic.

Three had gone upriver. Four came back, minus one, plus one. She could see them counting.

A boy of maybe twelve waded out to meet them, hip-deep, grabbing the gunwale with both hands. "Is it true?" he said. "About the Wreck? About the rights?"

"The rights are done," Ilsa said. "Burned."

"Burned." He said it like a foreign word, turning it over. "So who owns the water?"

"Everybody," Ilsa said. "That's the point."

He frowned at her, unconvinced, and let go of the boat, and Ilsa understood him completely. She'd grown up in a town like this one, downstream of a dozen like it, where water had been owned so long that freedom sounded like neglect. When everything belongs to somebody, common property feels like abandonment. It would take years before these people believed the river was theirs. Some of them would never believe it. Some of them would spend those years trying to become the next Harrow.

That was the personal cost nobody put on the ledger. You could burn the paper. You couldn't burn the appetite.

* * *

Sef Adama lasted two days on his feet before the fever won the argument, and then he was in the mission infirmary with a wet compress on his forehead and a doctor—a real one, sent up from the coast on the first packet boat since the syndicate's tolls came off the river—telling him he'd live if he stayed horizontal for a month. He did not intend to stay horizontal for a month. Ilsa heard him negotiating with the doctor through the door like a man haggling over fuel prices, and lost, and heard the silence afterward that meant he'd given in.

She went in and sat with him because there was nothing else to do and because sitting with the sick was a debt she owed forward, if not backward. The infirmary smelled of carbolic and river damp. Rain ticked on the tin roof. Sef lay with his eyes closed, looking ten years older than the man who'd hired her for a cut of whatever they found, and when he spoke he didn't open his eyes.

"I'm ruined," he said. Conversationally. Like reporting the weather.

"You're alive."

"I financed an expedition to recover stolen property worth more than every boat in this fleet combined. The property is ash. My backers—" He laughed, which turned into coughing. "My backers will be along shortly to explain my situation to me."

"Your backers are Harrow's creditors," Ilsa said. "Harrow's dead. His syndicate's a headless snake. Headless snakes don't collect."

"They find new heads." He opened his eyes then, and they were clear and tired and honest in a way she suspected was rare for him. "You know what I keep thinking about? Toma. I signed that boy onto my crew because he was cheap and he could swim. I never asked him what he needed. If I had—" He stopped. Started again. "If I had, would he have sold our route anyway?"

"Yes," Ilsa said. "But you'd have known why. Hunger makes liars of everyone. Knowing why doesn't stop the knife. It just lets you see it coming."

"That's not comfort, Renn."

"I'm not a comfort. I'm a poler."

He almost smiled. Then: "What will you do?"

Ilsa looked out the small window at the rain coming down on the landing, on the masts, on the harbor office with its naked flagpole, and she thought about the map fragment folded in her shirt pocket, soft as cloth now from handling. The piece of her grandfather's drafting that showed the back channels north of the drowned town. She'd kept it when Mirabel burned the rest—not for the paper, not for leverage, though she'd lied to herself about that for a day or two. She'd kept it because it was beautiful. Because some old man she'd never met had drawn the water she loved like it was scripture, and holding it felt like being handed a psalm.

"I'll pole," she said. "There's work now. Free water means free traffic. Farmers, traders, families moving back into the drowned country. Somebody has to get them through the snare channels without killing them."

"And the fragment?"

She should have lied. She was good at lying; she'd been paid by Harrow to lie with a boat hook and a smile. But Sef was a ruined man in a charity bed asking honest questions, and the rain made her soft.

"It's mine," she said. "Not to sell. To keep. There's a difference, and I spent most of my life not knowing it."

* * *

The reckoning came on the fourth day, and it came the way Ilsa had predicted: not as violence but as a meeting.

They held it in the fish market hall because it was the biggest roofed space in town, and half the landing crowded in to listen while a clerk with a coastal accent read the reversion clause aloud from a copy Sef had carried in his survey kit since before any of this started. *Upon destruction, loss, or voluntary surrender of recorded claim instruments, all appurtenant water rights within the surveyed district shall revert to common tenure under the old statute, inalienable, indivisible, held by the people of the district in perpetuity.*

The clerk finished. Silence. Then an old woman near the front—Ilsa recognized her, Marta Osei, who ran the net loft and had paid Harrow's tolls for eleven years without ever once lowering her eyes to him—stood up and said, "Read it again."

He read it again.

And this time, when he finished, the hall made a sound Ilsa had never heard in a room full of people who'd lived under a syndicate: not cheering, not weeping. A kind of collective exhale, like a rope parting under too much load. Somewhere in the back a child started laughing for no reason anyone could name, and the laugh spread, helpless and slightly hysterical, until even Marta Osei was wiping her face and pretending it was the dust.

Ilsa stood at the edge of it all and felt the strange grief that comes with repair. Because the world was slightly better now—she could feel it, the way you feel a channel running clean after years of silting—and yet the price of it sat in the ground outside the drowned town where they'd buried Toma under a cairn because the ground was too wet for digging deep. Nineteen years old. Sold their route for a promise of dry boots and a full belly, betrayed them, saved them, chose to stay behind holding a line against armed men so a boat full of strangers could live. Ilsa had voted to keep him aboard after his confession. She'd told herself it was mercy. Now she wondered if it had just been another transaction—his life for their forgiveness—and whether Toma had figured that out too, standing on the Wreck's deck with a harpoon gun in his hands, deciding that the only thing left he could buy outright was time for other people.

Mirabel hadn't spoken at the burial. She'd set her cracked compass on the cairn for one night, compass pointing at the stones, and taken it back at dawn. Ilsa had watched her do it and understood, finally, what the thing was. Not a tool. Not a leash. A gravestone small enough to carry.

* * *

On the sixth evening, the syndicate's remnants came to town, and Ilsa learned how a snake finds its new head—or fails to.

They arrived by packet boat, eight men in good coats flying no colors at all, which told her everything. Their leader was a soft-spoken accountant type named Verel whom Ilsa had seen once at Harrow's elbow, taking notes. He booked rooms at the inn, bought drinks, shook hands, and by nightfall had made offers to a dozen prominent people in the landing: quiet partnerships, favorable rates, a new syndicate with a friendlier face. By midnight, every offer had come back refused.

Ilsa heard about it secondhand, in the tavern, from the harbormaster's daughter, who was gleeful about it. "Marta Osei told him the water isn't for sale. He said everything's for sale. She said, 'Then buy a bucket and carry it yourself.' Whole tavern cheered. He left on the morning packet."

"Verel doesn't quit," Ilsa said. "He compounds."

"Let him compound," the girl said, with the magnificent confidence of someone who'd never been foreclosed on. "We've got the statute now. We've got the clause. And we've got her."

Her. Ilsa followed the girl's glance across the tavern to the corner table where Mirabel Voss sat alone with a cup of tea, listening to everything, promising nothing. Someone had already nailed a hand-lettered sign to the market hall door: *WATER COURT — FIRST SITTING — ALL DISPUTES HEARD — NO FEES.* And someone else had written underneath, in different ink: *ASK THE VOSS WOMAN. SHE KNOWS THE OLD SURVEY LINES.*

"She doesn't want it," Ilsa said.

"Doesn't matter," the girl said cheerfully. "Wanting's got nothing to do with it. You burn a hundred years of paper, people are going to ask you what to do with the ashes."

Ilsa carried her drink over to Mirabel's table and sat down without asking, because that was the arrangement between them—no permission sought, none required. Up close, Mirabel looked wrung out and steadier than she had any right to be, the desert tan finally fading toward something that matched the river country.

"You heard," Ilsa said.

"I hear everything. It's exhausting."

"They're going to make you a judge."

"They're going to try." Mirabel turned her cup a slow quarter-turn on the table. "I came here invisible. That was the whole discipline of my life. Stay small, stay unnamed, stay paid and gone. And now there's a sign on a door with my name on it, and a boy at the dock asking me about reversion clauses, and Sef telling anyone who'll listen that I'm the granddaughter of the man who drew the charts." She looked up, and there was something in her face Ilsa hadn't seen before—not peace, but the ground floor of it. "I owe a debt. Back home. Real coin, real people, and I've been carrying it like a stone for years without ever saying what it bought. I think it's time I went and paid it. Out loud. Coin by coin."

"And after?"

"After, I walk north." Mirabel almost smiled. "Sort of."

Ilsa nodded slowly and drank, and said nothing about the fact that north, sort of, was exactly the direction her own boat faced, or that the map fragment in her pocket showed channels up that way that no living poler had run since the flood—channels she'd been dreaming about since the dead water, since before that, maybe since she was nine years old in her mother's boat hearing about the drowned orchards. Some things you didn't say yet. Some things you let the river deliver in its own time.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Through the tavern window Ilsa could see the harbor office, the bare flagpole, and beyond it the dark line of the river running free—unowned, unmetered, unrecorded—doing what it had always done, which was go where it decided to go.

Morning packet came at dawn. Mirabel would be on it, heading south to settle her debts. Ilsa stood on the dock to see her off, and as the boat pulled away, Mirabel raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. More like a marker dropped in open water, something to steer by later.

Ilsa raised hers back, then turned to her boat, and began loading for the north.
