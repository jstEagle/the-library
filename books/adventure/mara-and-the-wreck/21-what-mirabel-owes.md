## 21. What Mirabel Owes

The debt house in Callow's Landing had once been a granary, and it still smelled like grain gone to rot—sweet and sour at once, the smell of something meant to feed people instead feeding on them. Mirabel climbed the outside stairs slowly, counting each step the way she'd counted everything for three weeks now. Steps. Coins. Days.

Forty-one steps to the ledger office.

She carried the money in a canvas sack against her hip, and it was heavier than she remembered debt being. Not because the sum had changed—it hadn't, not a copper of it—but because she had. Three weeks ago she would have found a way to make this lighter. A shortcut. A story. She'd spent her whole life being good at stories.

The clerk behind the desk was a narrow man named Pell with ink-stained fingers and the patient eyes of someone who watched people fail for a living. He looked up when she came in, looked at the sack, and then did something no clerk in a debt house should ever do: he smiled.

"Voss," he said. "We'd heard you were dead."

"Heard from who?"

"Doesn't matter." He pulled the ledger toward him, licked his thumb, turned pages. "Harrow's syndicate came apart two days ago. Half his paper is worthless now. Including—" his finger stopped on a line, tapped it twice, "—yours."

Mirabel set the sack on the desk anyway. It made a sound like a dropped stone.

"Then I'm early," she said. "Or late. Either way, I'm paying."

Pell stared at her. Then at the sack. Then at her again, longer this time, as if the second look might reveal the trick. Outside, through the granary's high windows, the river ran high and brown with monsoon runoff, carrying branches and roof shingles and one entire door past the town toward the sea. The flood was going down. Everything the water had taken was coming back changed.

"You understand what I'm telling you," Pell said carefully. "The claim's void. Harrow bought your family's note three years ago off a broker in Saltmere, and every instrument he held just reverted to common law. You could walk out that door owing nothing."

"I know what you're telling me."

"And you're paying anyway."

"I'm paying anyway."

He sat back. For a long moment the only sound was the river and the creak of the old granary beams settling, the building remembering what it was built for. Then Pell opened the sack and began to count, and Mirabel stood there while he did it, coin by coin, because that was the whole point. That was the entire point.

* * *

It had taken eleven days to gather it.

Not the earning—the earning had been simple, almost restful. She and Ilsa had run salvage on the drowned channels south of town, poling into the flooded orchards where the water had gone still and clear, hauling up fence wire and pump fittings and once an entire brass bedstead that Ilsa swore she could sell to a widow in the Landing for more than Mirabel's yearly wage. Ilsa took her cut without apology and Mirabel paid it without resentment. That was the shape of their friendship now: honest arithmetic.

No, the hard part had been the other debts, the small ones that turned out not to be small at all.

She'd owed Toma's mother in the Landing four days' wages Toma had sent home before the Wreck and never arrived. She'd paid it first, before anything, and the old woman had wept into her apron and made Mirabel drink tea she didn't want and tell her everything twice. The second telling was worse than the first. Some things didn't get easier with practice; they got truer.

She'd owed the ferryman at Deadfall Channel for the rope they'd cut loose during the ambush. She'd owed a doctor in the drowned quarter for stitching Sef's harpoon wound back when none of them had coin, a debt Sef had been carrying quietly ever since and which Mirabel had discovered by reading his expense book upside down over breakfast. She'd paid that one too, and Sef had been furious about it for exactly one evening, and then he'd bought her dinner and said nothing else, which between the two of them counted as forgiveness.

And she'd owed the truth. To herself, mostly, but also to anyone who asked, and lately people kept asking. The story of the Wreck had spread downriver faster than the floodwater receded. There were versions in which she was a hero and versions in which she was a thief and one particularly inventive version in which she'd wrestled Harrow hand to hand, which was a lie she privately enjoyed. In none of them was the truth, so she'd started telling it, badly at first, then less badly, standing in taverns and on docks with her hat in her hands like a woman at a funeral.

"My grandfather drew the charts," she'd say. "The ones the compass was built from. He surveyed the valley before the flood and he knew where the water rights were buried, and my family's debt—that debt I just paid—was incurred buying those rights in the first place. My grandfather bought paper instead of land. Bought promises instead of water. And the bill came due on me."

People wanted her to be angry about it. She wasn't, or not only. What she felt was closer to recognition. Her grandfather had done what everyone in that valley had done: tried to hold something against the coming water, and failed, and left the failure to his granddaughter like furniture nobody wanted.

* * *

Pell finished counting at midday. Forty-one coins short of nothing, the ledger closed, the line struck through with a single clean stroke of his pen.

"There," he said, and sounded almost disappointed. "You're free."

Mirabel looked at the struck-through line for a long time. She'd imagined this moment for years—in the salt flats, on the boat, in the black water under the Wreck—and in every imagining it had felt like setting down a weight. It didn't feel like that. It felt like taking one off a shelf and finding out it had been hollow all along, and the real weight was something else, something that didn't go in ledgers.

"Pell," she said. "The original instruments. My grandfather's deeds. Harrow's copies are burned, but there were filings, weren't there? Registry copies. Saltmere?"

He frowned. "There'd be duplicates, yes. Why? Those are void too. Common law took the whole valley back. Anyone can draw water from those channels now. That's the law."

"I know the law." She rolled the empty sack closed. "I want to see them anyway. I want to read what he signed."

"The registry's four days upriver."

"I know where it is." She almost smiled. "I have a compass."

* * *

Sef found her that evening on the dock behind the Broken Keel, where she was sitting with her boots off and her feet in the brown water, watching the flood go down inch by visible inch. He lowered himself onto the planks beside her with the careful stiffness of a man whose harpoon scar ached in damp weather, which was every weather now.

"Pell says you paid," he said. "All of it. With the syndicate dead, with the whole thing void. He says you insisted."

"I insisted."

"That's either the stupidest thing I've ever heard or the most honest, and I genuinely can't tell which."

"Neither can I." She splashed her foot a little. "But it's mine either way. That's the difference. Before, the debt owned me. Now I own having paid it. There's a word for that."

"There's several."

"Responsibility," she said. "That's the one I like."

They sat while the light went long and gold across the water. Somewhere upstream a barge horn sounded, low and mournful, and a flock of ibis rose off the drowned orchards in a white sheet and settled again.

"Toma's mother told me something," Mirabel said finally. "While I was paying her. She said her son always believed you get to choose what you carry. Not what happens to you—what you *carry*. She said he learned it from her, and she learned it from losing a farm to the flood, and she said it like it was scripture." She paused. "I used to think inheritance was what got left to you. The debt, the compass, the name. Things in a box with your face on the lid. But that's not it. Inheritance is what you agree to keep carrying. Everything else is just stuff that happened."

Sef was quiet a moment. "And what are you keeping?"

"The truth, mostly. It's lighter than I expected." She looked at him sideways. "My grandfather wasn't a villain, Sef. I've been telling people he was greedy, and that's a lie I let stand because it was easier than the real thing. He was scared. Same as everybody. He saw the water coming years before anyone believed him, and he couldn't save the valley, so he saved paper. He sealed the rights in the Wreck because he thought someday someone from his family would come back and use them to put things right." She shook her head. "He was wrong about how. But he wasn't wrong that someone would come. That part he got right."

"And burning them—"

"Burning them *was* putting things right. Just not the way he planned." She flexed her toes in the current. "You don't own water. Nobody owns water. He knew that too, deep down, or he wouldn't have hidden the deeds where it would take a flood and a death and a stolen compass to reach them. Maybe that was the test. Maybe the whole thing was a test, and the answer was always fire."

Sef snorted softly. "You've been talking to Ilsa. She says things like that."

"Ilsa says things like that and means them. It's very annoying."

* * *

Ilsa appeared at full dark, as she tended to, materializing at the end of the dock with her pole over her shoulder like a pilgrim's staff. She'd sold the brass bedstead, Mirabel learned, for nearly double the widow price, because the widow had a sister, and Ilsa had sold to both. Honest arithmetic, Ilsa-style.

"Pell tells me you're going upriver," she said, sitting down uninvited. "To the registry. Four days through channels that are half-drained and full of silt traps."

"Three and a half if the water keeps dropping."

"You'll need a poler who knows which channels lie."

"I'll need a poler who works for a cut of nothing," Mirabel said. "Because that's what's up there. Paper worth nothing. I checked."

Ilsa considered this with the gravity she gave all financial matters. "A cut of nothing," she said at last, "divided by two, is still nothing. But the company's better than nothing, and I hate traveling alone in drained season." She spat ceremonially into the river, which among her people constituted a signed contract. "Besides. I want to see the man's handwriting. Your grandfather's. The man who drew maps good enough to build a compass from. I want to know if he was as good as the thing he made."

"He was better," Mirabel said. "That was the problem."

* * *

She left at dawn, alone on the dock, the others still asleep. The river had fallen another hand's width overnight; the tops of fence posts stood out of the water now like a congregation waiting. In the east the desert beyond the flats was turning from grey to rose, and somewhere out there was the salt, and the boot prints that had started all of this, long since blown away.

She took the compass from her pocket and held it flat on her palm. It didn't point north. It never had. These days it pointed wherever she was already going, which was either because her grandfather had built it that way or because she'd finally become the kind of person a needle like that could trust. She suspected the second. She hoped it was both.

Four days upriver, in a registry drawer, her grandfather's signature waited on a page. She was going to read every word of what he'd signed—not to own it, not anymore, but to know it. To carry it honestly, the way you carry a grave you tend.

She put the compass away, picked up her pack, and started walking north along the levee road, sort of.

Behind her, unhurried, a second set of footsteps fell into rhythm with hers on the planks.
