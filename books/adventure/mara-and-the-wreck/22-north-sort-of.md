## 22. North, Sort Of

The desert took her back the way an old dog takes back someone who left without saying goodbye—sniffing once, deciding not to hold a grudge, and falling into step.

Mirabel Voss walked out of Callow's Landing at first light, before the town was fully awake, because goodbyes were a currency she'd never learned to spend well. She'd said what needed saying. Sef had shaken her hand like he was closing a contract and opening another one somewhere else, and Ilsa had pressed something into her palm on the dock—a river stone, flat and gray, worn smooth by water that no longer existed anywhere except in memory and flood season.

"For the pocket where the compass used to live," Ilsa had said. "So it doesn't rattle."

The compass hadn't left her pocket. But she'd taken the stone anyway, because refusing gifts from Ilsa Renn was a fight Mirabel didn't have the appetite for anymore.

Now the salt flats opened ahead of her, white and blinding under a sky scrubbed clean by last week's monsoon. The air smelled different than when she'd left—wetter, greener, carrying the ghost of drowned country on the wind. The world had been rearranged while she was gone. Harrow's syndicate had folded like wet paper without the deeds to prop it up. The water rights had reverted to common law, which meant they belonged to everyone and no one, which meant, if you asked Mirabel, they finally belonged to the right people: whoever was thirsty.

She'd settled her debt two days ago. Coin by coin, counted out on a scarred table in the back of a chandlery, while the man she owed watched with eyes that kept flicking to the door as if expecting Harrow's men to burst in and make everything simple again. No shortcuts. No name-dropping. No grandfather's forged compass held up as collateral or credential. Just money earned tracking a thief through drowned country, minus what it cost to keep four people alive doing it—which, as it turned out, was most of it.

She'd walked out of that chandlery owning forty-one coins, a bedroll, a knife, a canteen, a river stone, and a compass that pointed wherever she told it to.

It was, by any honest accounting, more than she'd ever owned before.

* * *

The cracked glass caught the light as she walked, throwing a small wobbling star across her knuckles. She'd stopped hiding it. That was the strange part—the thing she'd spent years keeping buried deep in her pocket, wrapped in cloth, treated like a wound that would bleed if exposed. Now it rode openly in her hand some mornings, catching sun, daring anyone to ask about it.

Nobody did. Out here there was nobody to ask.

Her grandfather had built it from stolen charts—that much she'd finally said out loud, in a room full of people who had every reason to hate her for it. *My family's debt was incurred buying those rights. My grandfather helped forge the map. The compass is made from the same work.* She'd waited for Sef's face to close, for Ilsa to spit, for Toma—

Toma wasn't there to hear it. That debt didn't settle in coin. It settled in the weight she carried now, walking, the way you carry a stone in your shoe until the shoe wears to fit around it and then you forget it's there except every fourth step.

She'd tried to give the compass away twice since the Wreck. Once to Sef, who'd looked at it like it was a snake and told her to keep her leash. Once to the chandlery man, who'd turned it over in his fingers, frowned at the needle swinging lazy circles that matched no north he knew, and handed it back with the expression of a man declining counterfeit money.

So it stayed. And somewhere between the burning of the deeds and the settling of the debt, she'd stopped thinking of it as an inheritance and started thinking of it as a tool. Her grandfather had built it to point at what he wanted. She could rebuild it to point at what she chose.

That was the whole difference, maybe, between a debt and a life. One pointed backward. The other you aimed yourself.

* * *

By midday the flats gave way to hardpan, and the hardpan to the low broken country where she'd grown up learning to be invisible. She moved the old ways—shading her eyes with a flat hand, reading wind for dust, choosing ridgelines that broke her silhouette against rock instead of sky. Habit older than memory. She'd worn invisibility like armor for so long she'd forgotten it was a choice.

Except now, walking, she kept catching herself checking behind her.

Not for claim-jumpers. Not for Harrow's ghosts—those were scattered to the winds, broker colors burned or buried. She checked because of the figure on the ridge behind her, half a mile back, moving when she moved, stopping when she stopped.

Ilsa Renn poling a boat was poetry. Ilsa Renn walking a desert was a joke the land was playing on itself—too much hip-swing, too much looking at the horizon like it owed her money, boots meant for deck planking complaining bitterly about gravel. But she was there. Had been there since the second morning out of Callow's Landing, always at the edge of sight, never closing the distance, never dropping away either.

Mirabel hadn't spoken to her yet. There was a protocol to these things, unwritten but iron-clad among people who lived alone on purpose. You didn't acknowledge the follower until the follower decided to be found. You especially didn't acknowledge them when you were secretly glad they were there, because gladness made you soft, and softness out here got people killed.

But it had been three days. And Mirabel was tired of pretending she didn't know.

* * *

She made camp early, in the lee of a fallen shelf of rock, and built a small fire because smoke was a message and she was done sending the message that said *stay away*. She brewed tea from leaves that grew along the drainage channels, bitter and restorative, and set a second cup on the stone beside her where anyone approaching would see it.

Then she sat and watched the ridgeline and waited.

The sun went down. The stars came out in their brutal multitudes. And eventually, inevitably, a silhouette detached itself from the dark against dark and came down the slope with the unhurried gait of someone who had known all along they'd been invited.

Ilsa dropped onto the rock across the fire like she'd been sitting there for years. She looked at the tea. She picked up the cup. She drank.

"You walk slow," Ilsa said.

"You follow loud."

"I'm a river woman. Everything I do sounds like water." She stretched her legs toward the fire, wincing as her boots—city boots, dock boots, wrong boots—finally got relief. "Your desert's terrible, by the way. Nothing to read. Water's honest. It tells you where it's been. This—" she gestured at the vast indifferent dark, "—this just sits there keeping secrets."

"That's why I like it."

"I know." Ilsa looked at her over the cup, firelight catching the sharp planes of her face. "That's the part I can't figure out how to feel about."

Mirabel let the silence sit. Out here silence was a language, and she was fluent, and she suspected Ilsa was too, in her own dialect.

"Why are you following me?" she finally asked.

"Following?" Ilsa snorted. "I'm traveling. Same direction. Coincidence."

"Three days of coincidence."

"Rivers run long, Voss. So do coincidences." But something shifted in her face, the sardonic mask slipping just enough to show the structure underneath. "Harrow's syndicate is dead. The Landing's going to eat itself for a year dividing up the scraps. Every job I take for the next while is going to be some fool wanting to know where the deeds went, and the answer is ash, and nobody wants to pay for ash." She turned the cup in her hands. "You're the only interesting thing left in that whole drowned country. You and that compass."

"The compass points wherever I tell it."

"Yeah." Ilsa's mouth curved. "Sef called it a leash. I called it a key. Turns out we were both wrong. It's a pen. You write with it." She drained the tea and set the cup down. "Anyway. I'm not following you. I'm walking north."

"North's that way." Mirabel nodded east, where the compass needle—her needle, her choice—had been resting all day.

Ilsa squinted. "That's east."

"Sort of."

A long pause. Then Ilsa laughed—not the barroom laugh, the other one, the rare one, the one Mirabel had heard exactly twice on the river and both times in the space between surviving and realizing you'd survived. "Sort of," Ilsa repeated, shaking her head. "Fine. I'm walking sort-of-north. Same as you."

They sat with the fire between them, and the desert kept its secrets, and for the first time in longer than Mirabel could measure, the silence she wore didn't feel like armor. It felt like a coat you take off when you come inside.

* * *

They walked north, sort of, for six days.

Ilsa learned the desert the way she learned everything—by insulting it until it revealed itself. She learned to read dust devils the way she'd read current, to find water in rock seams by smell, to sleep in the open without waking every hour reaching for a pole that wasn't there. In exchange she taught Mirabel things the desert couldn't: how to laugh at bad luck before it finished happening, how to cook river fish over a fire even when there was no river, how to talk about Toma without the name becoming a wound you both fell into.

They talked about him on the fifth night, sitting under a rock overhang while wind hissed across the flats outside. Ilsa spoke plainly, the way she did everything.

"He stayed because he wanted to matter," she said. "Not to us. To himself. He sold us out because he thought he didn't matter, and he died holding that line because for one hour he finally did." She poked the fire. "I think about that a lot. What people will pay to feel real."

"He was real to me before that," Mirabel said. "I should have told him so."

"Yeah. Well." Ilsa shrugged, gentle as a shove. "We're all illiterate until somebody reads us out loud."

Mirabel thought about that for a long time after Ilsa slept. About debts and inheritances and the difference between owing and belonging. Her grandfather had left her a forged compass and a buried shame and a debt that had shaped thirty years of her life like water shaping stone. She'd spent those years paying it down invisibly, believing the debt was the inheritance, that her whole legacy was what she owed.

But the deeds had burned. The debt was paid. And what remained—what she carried north, sort of, in her pocket and in her chest—wasn't an obligation. It was a name. Hers. The only thing she'd ever truly owned, and the only thing, it turned out, worth owning.

* * *

On the ninth day they reached the high broken country where the maps went thin and the horizon opened like a held breath. Mirabel stood on a rise at dawn and took out the compass and let it swing.

North, it said. East. South. Whatever she leaned toward, it agreed, eager as a dog, faithful as nothing else in her life had ever been. Her grandfather had built it to point at treasure. She'd rebuilt it, without touching it, to point at this: high ground, morning light, a road that belonged to no broker and no syndicate and no dead man's charts.

"What's out there?" Ilsa asked, coming up beside her, squinting at the empty distance.

"Nothing."

"Nothing's my favorite destination." Ilsa shaded her eyes. "How far?"

"Far enough that nobody's ever bothered to put it on a map."

"Perfect. We'll draw our own." She started down off the rise, then paused, looking back. "Well? You coming, ranger? Or you going to stand there being invisible at the scenery?"

Mirabel looked at the compass one more time. The needle swung lazily, freely, pointing nowhere and everywhere, waiting for her to decide.

She closed her hand around it—and around the smooth gray river stone beside it, worn by water, carried by choice—and followed Ilsa down into the unmapped country, walking north, sort of, visible as daylight, and no longer alone.
