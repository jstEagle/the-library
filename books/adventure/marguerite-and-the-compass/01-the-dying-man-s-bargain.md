## 1. The Dying Man's Bargain

The elk had led Theo Ashgrove two days into the high country before the weather turned, and by the time he crested the last ridge above Wren's Meadow, the light had gone the color of old pewter. Snow was coming. He could smell it — that flat, mineral stillness that settled over the range a day or two ahead of the first storm, like the mountain holding its breath.

He'd been guiding for twenty-two years, and this was to be his last season of it. Not by choice. The outfit in Alder Crossing had let him go in September, polite as a funeral, and the word had come down through every valley town since: *Ashgrove's done. Don't hire him.* Twenty years ago it had been a different word, a worse one, but the effect was the same. A man could survive a mountain. It was other men who buried you.

So he'd come up alone, hunting meat he didn't strictly need, because a man alone with a rifle was at least a man with a purpose.

The meadow opened below him like a held note — gold grass gone silver at the edges, a creek threading through it, and against all sense, cattle bones from some long-dead drive half-buried near the timberline. And in the middle of it, thirty yards out, a shape that was not a rock.

Theo went still. The rifle came off his shoulder without his deciding to raise it.

The shape moved. A hand lifted, weak as a moth's wing, and dropped.

He crossed the meadow fast but careful, eyes on the tree line, on the ridgelines, on the rocks where a man with a gun might wait. Nobody waited. Whatever had happened here had happened hours ago.

Elias Wren lay on his back in the crushed grass with both hands pressed to his belly, and even before Theo knelt, he knew what he was looking at. He'd seen gut wounds twice in his life, once on a man and once on a horse, and neither had ended well. This one had bled through the shirt, through the jacket, into the ground beneath him, darkening the gold grass in a spreading stain. The man's face was gray as the sky, lips gone the blue of deep ice, but his eyes were open and burning.

"Prospector," Theo said. "Who did this?"

Wren laughed, and the laugh cost him; his whole body seized around the wound. "You're the guide." His voice came out scraped thin, wind through a crack in a door. "Ashgrove. I know your face."

"Most people do," Theo said, and there was no pride in it. "Can you walk? There's a cabin—"

"I can't walk to the end of this sentence." Wren's hands were shaking now, and not from cold. "Water. Please."

Theo had a flask. He propped the man's head up and tipped it carefully, watching most of it run down his chin, and while he did, he took stock the way he took stock of everything — automatically, uselessly. Sixty-odd years old. Boots resoled three times. Hands like tree roots, stained with claim dirt under the nails. A man who had lived hard and high and alone for a very long time.

"They came yesterday," Wren said. "Three of them. Good coats. Good boots. Asked after my claim like they were buying, not taking." His breath whistled. "I told them nothing. One of them smiled and shot me anyway. Didn't even take the map. Said—" he coughed, wet and terrible— "said they'd let the mountain keep it. Let *me* keep it. Said no one would ever climb far enough to read it."

Theo's jaw tightened. Men in good coats. He knew the type better than he wanted to. He knew one in particular.

"You need a doctor," he said. "It's two days down. If I carry you—"

"If you carry me, we both die on the trail, and then nobody knows anything." Wren caught his wrist with shocking strength, fingers closing like a trap. "Listen to me. Listen. I've got maybe an hour and I've spent forty years earning this hour, so you'll give it your whole attention."

Theo gave it. There was something in the man's voice that demanded it — not fear, exactly. Fury, banked down to coals, hot enough to burn a hole through dying.

"The map," Wren said. "Inside my coat. Left pocket. Take it out."

"I'm not robbing a dying man."

"You're *inheriting* from one, if you've got the spine for it." Wren fumbled at his own jacket, too weak to work the buttons, and Theo — hating himself a little for it — reached in and drew out the packet.

It was oilcloth-wrapped, tied with waxed cord, worn soft as cloth at the corners from years of handling. Heavy for its size. He set it on the man's chest and did not untie it.

"There's a vein up there," Wren said. "Silver. Not a rumor, not a dream — a *vein*, wide as a wagon, running under the glacier shoulder below Sentinel Pass. Forty years I worked it out. Forty years of winters and empty pans and men laughing at the crazy prospector." His eyes fixed on Theo with terrible clarity. "And there's more than silver in that wrapping. But that comes later. First things first."

"First things," Theo said, "is getting you off this mountain."

"First things is my daughter."

The words landed strangely in the empty meadow. Theo said nothing.

"Marguerite." Wren said the name the way a man says a prayer he hasn't earned. "She'll be twenty-six now. Last I saw her she was fifteen, and I told her I'd be back by spring, and I wasn't, and I never wrote, and I never came, because—" his breath hitched— "because shame is a heavy load and I kept telling myself I'd climb out from under it next season. Next season. Forty seasons of next season." He turned his head and looked directly at Theo, and there was something awful in it, some recognition. "You know about leaving, guide. I can see it on you. You've got that look men get when they've done a thing they can't put down."

Theo stood. He walked three steps away, because he needed not to be looked at like that, and stared at the tree line, at the gray wall of coming weather.

"I'm blacklisted," he said finally. "You know what that means. Half the territory thinks I sold a party of climbers to their deaths, and the other half just believes whatever the syndicate prints. You want to hand your treasure and your daughter to a man with my name?"

"I want to hand them to a man who climbed up here alone in October to shoot an elk he didn't need," Wren said, "and whose first instinct was to ask who hurt me instead of what I was carrying." A pause, ragged. "That's rarer than silver, Ashgrove. Rarer than anything in that packet."

Theo turned back. The man had gotten the oilcloth open somehow, or had it mostly open, and inside — Theo could see it now — was a folded sheet of heavy paper, hand-drawn, dense with lines and notation, and beneath it something else, flatter, darker, that Wren's trembling thumb pressed closed again.

"Two promises," Wren said. "One: see it through. Take the route. All the way to the vein, all the way past it — there's a thing past it, and you'll know it when you find it, and when you find it you'll understand why they shot me. Two:" his grip found Theo's sleeve, pulled, insistent, "find Marguerite. She'll come looking. She always comes looking, every few years, letters to every town in the range. Tell her everything. Everything except—" he tapped the packet— "except you tell her yourself, on the trail, in your own time. Don't hand her this like a telegram. She's owed better than telegrams."

"And if I say no?" Theo asked. "If I walk away and let the mountain keep its secret?"

Wren smiled. It was the worst thing Theo had seen all day — worse than the blood, worse than the wound. It was peaceful.

"Then you'll be the second man today to leave me in a meadow," he said, "and you'll live with it longer than I will."

The wind came down off the ridge then, first cold breath of the storm, moving the grass in long silver waves. Somewhere above, a ptarmigan called once and went quiet. Theo crouched there with a stranger's blood on his knees and twenty years of distrust sitting on his shoulders like a pack he couldn't shrug off.

He thought about the outfit in Alder Crossing. About the polite letter. About the name he couldn't buy back at any price in any town. About how a man with nothing left to lose was either free or damned, and usually couldn't tell which until it was too late.

"Where does she look first?" he heard himself say.

Wren exhaled — relief or pain, impossible to tell. "Alder Crossing. General store. She posts notices." His eyes were losing focus now, going soft at the edges. "Tell her... tell her the meadow was gold. Tell her I thought of her every time the light hit it right. Tell her—" The sentence unraveled. His hand found Theo's wrist again, squeezed once, hard, and pressed the packet into Theo's hands with the last of his strength. "See it through. Both halves. The silver *and* the rest. Promise me, guide. Promise me on something you still believe in."

Theo looked down at the map, at the dense hand-drawn lines climbing toward Sentinel Pass, at the small neat signature in the corner — *E. Wren* — and felt the weight of it settle onto him like a yoke.

"I promise," he said. It came out rough. "On the mountain. That's the only thing I still believe in."

Something in Wren's face let go then, all at once, like a knot slipping. "Good," he whispered. "Good man. Watch the third switchback. Ice early this year." And his eyes drifted toward the ridgeline, toward the high white country, and stayed there.

Theo sat with him until there was nothing left to sit with. Then he built a cairn from the meadow stones, working fast before the snow could bury the man unmarked, and laid the empty flask at its base because it seemed wrong to carry it away full-handed.

By the time he finished, the first flakes were falling — fat, slow, deliberate, weeks ahead of any honest winter.

He stood at the edge of the meadow with the oilcloth packet inside his coat, against his chest, heavier than lead. Two days' walk to Alder Crossing. A town full of people who believed the worst about him. A daughter who would arrive asking questions he wasn't sure he could answer. And somewhere behind him, descending through the same gray weather, three men in good coats who thought the mountain had already kept its secret.

They were wrong. But so, Theo thought grimly, turning downhill into the snow, was anyone who believed this bargain would cost less than everything.

* * *

He made the lower trail by dark and pushed on by lantern light, because the storm was filling in behind him like water behind a dam, and because every hour he lingered was an hour closer between those good coats and Alder Crossing. The map rode against his ribs. Twice he stopped, struck the lantern, and nearly opened it — and twice he tied it tighter instead. *Everything except*, Wren had said. *In your own time.*

Near midnight, sheltering under a granite overhang with a small fire, he took out the decade-old letter Marguerite had once sent her father — Wren had carried it folded inside the map, soft as cloth from reading — and read it by firelight.

*I don't want your silver, Papa. I want to know why. Come home and tell me why, and I will forgive everything else.*

Theo fed the letter back into the packet with a care that surprised him, and stared into the flames a long time.

Somewhere down-valley, a girl was posting notices on store walls, asking after a ghost. And somewhere behind him, men in good coats were climbing.

Come morning, he intended to reach Alder Crossing first.
