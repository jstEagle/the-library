## 2. A Daughter Arrives

The letter had been folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth, and Marguerite Wren had read it so often she could recite it with her eyes shut — which was fortunate, because by the time the stage rattled into Copperline Flats, her eyes were doing anything but shutting.

*My dear girl. If you are reading this, I have gone ahead on a trail you cannot follow yet. There is something set aside for you in the mountains. Something that was always meant to be yours. When you are ready, come find me. Come find what I left.*

No signature but an *E.W.* pressed hard enough into the paper to tear it. No address. No apology. Ten years old, and still it managed to be both a promise and an insult.

She stepped down from the stage into a street that smelled of sawdust, horse dung, and woodsmoke, and stood a moment letting the cold of the high valley settle into her bones. The town clung to the mountainside like lichen to stone — two streets, a church with a crooked steeple, a saloon whose sign read THE LUCKY STRIKE in letters that had lost half their gilt. Above it all, the range rose white and indifferent, peaks stacked against a sky so blue it looked painted.

Somewhere up there, Elias Wren was alive. Or had been. The letter said *come find me*, and Marguerite Wren had never once in her life done as she was told, except for this.

She went first to the general store, because a woman traveling alone learned quickly that the storekeeper knew everything worth knowing in a town of four hundred souls.

"Elias Wren," she said, laying the letter on the counter between them like a warrant. "Prospector. Works the high country."

The storekeeper — a narrow man with flour on his apron and suspicion in his eyes — picked up the letter without touching it, read it upside down, and looked at her over his spectacles.

"You'd be the daughter, then."

"I'd be the daughter."

He set the letter down carefully, the way a man sets down something that might go off. "Wren comes through maybe twice a year. Sells his dust, buys flour and cartridges, drinks one whiskey at the Lucky Strike and no more. Keeps to himself." He hesitated. "Hasn't come down this season."

"When did he usually come down?"

"By now." The flour-dusted hands busied themselves straightening a row of tins that needed no straightening. "There's talk, mind. Talk isn't nothing. But there's a guide been asking after him too. Ashgrove. You might try him — he's got rooms above the livery."

Marguerite took back her letter. "Where's this Ashgrove now?"

"Where he always is when he's not on a mountain," the storekeeper said, nodding toward the saloon. "Drinking alone."

* * *

Theo Ashgrove sat at the corner table of the Lucky Strike with his back to the wall and a whiskey he wasn't drinking, and Marguerite marked him before the door had finished swinging shut behind her. Not because he stood out — though he did, a big weathered man with gray coming into his beard and a face like a cliff face, all planes and shadow — but because every other man in the room had looked up when she entered, and he hadn't. He was watching the mirror behind the bar instead, which showed him the door, which told her everything about the kind of life he led.

She crossed the floor and put her father's letter down in front of him.

"Storekeeper says you've been asking after Elias Wren."

Ashgrove looked at the letter. Then at her. His eyes were pale gray, the color of granite in fog, and they went over her face slowly, feature by feature, like a man comparing a map to the country itself.

"You have his jaw," he said at last.

"I have his name, which is more than he ever gave me." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat without being invited. "You've seen him. Recently. Don't waste my time telling me otherwise — a man doesn't ask questions about a stranger in a town this size unless he's got reason."

For a long moment he said nothing. The barman drifted near, decided against it, drifted away. Somewhere a piano player was murdering a waltz in the next room.

"Sit tight a minute," Ashgrove finally said, and rose, and spoke low to the barman, and paid for something, and came back with a second glass he set down in front of her untouched. Then he leaned forward, elbows on the table, and studied her the way she imagined he studied weather.

"How old are you, Miss Wren?"

"Twenty-four. And it's not *Miss* anything, it's just Wren."

"Twenty-four." Something moved behind his eyes — not quite pain, not quite arithmetic. "Then you were fourteen when he wrote that letter."

"Fourteen when he wrote it. Twenty-four when he bothered to send it." She heard her own voice sharpen and could do nothing about it; it had been sharpening for ten years and had cut everything it touched since. "A boy ran it out to our farm last month. Said a trapper gave it to him, said the trapper got it from a man heading into the high country. That's all anyone knows. So either my father has spent a decade dying slowly, or he's spent it hiding, and I've come three hundred miles to learn which."

Ashgrove turned the whiskey glass a quarter turn on the table. Turned it back.

"There's a version of this conversation where I tell you your father loved you," he said quietly. "I don't know if it's true. I only met him once, and he was bleeding out at the time."

The piano stumbled and stopped. Marguerite heard her own heartbeat instead, loud and stupid in her ears.

"Bleeding out," she repeated.

"Gut shot, in a meadow below the Kestrel Divide. I found him on my way down from a guiding season. He lived long enough to give me something and make me promise things." Ashgrove's voice stayed level, but his hands had gone still, and she understood suddenly that the stillness cost him. "I promised I'd see a thing through. And I promised I'd find his daughter."

"And the thing?"

"That's between me and a dead man."

The anger came up in her like floodwater — hot, instant, familiar, the oldest companion she had. It was easier than the other thing pressing underneath it, the thing with no name that had been sitting on her chest since the storekeeper said *hasn't come down this season*.

"Between you and a dead man," she said. "Convenient. My father dies with his secrets intact and hands them to a stranger, and his own daughter gets a ten-year-old note and a secondhand grave. What did he leave me, Mr. Ashgrove? Because the letter says he left me something. Something that was always mine. I'll have it, whatever it is, and I'll have it from your hand or I'll find another way up that mountain, but I will *have* it."

"He left you a map."

The words dropped between them like a stone into a well. She waited for the splash.

"A hand-drawn map," Ashgrove went on, "to a silver vein he found years ago and kept quiet. A fortune, if the drawings are honest. They usually aren't, but his were." He met her eyes. "That's what I can tell you. Where it leads is the part that stays between me and him. Not because I don't trust you — because everyone who knows where that vein sits ends up dead, and I watched enough men die this month to last my remaining ones."

"Everyone," she said. "Who's everyone?"

"The men who shot him, for a start."

The saloon noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. Marguerite became aware of the room again in pieces: the card players, the barman polishing a glass that was already clean, a man by the window who had stopped pretending to read his newspaper.

"Outside," she said.

Ashgrove followed her into the street without argument. Evening was coming down purple off the peaks, and the first lamps were being lit along the boardwalk. He walked until the buildings thinned and the river noise replaced the town noise, and then he stopped and faced her with his hands loose at his sides, a man accustomed to open ground.

"Your father didn't abandon you the way you think," he said. "I don't know the whole of it — he didn't have breath enough for the whole of it. But he talked about you while he was dying. Not the way guilty men talk, checking the exits. The way proud men talk. He said you'd come looking someday, and God help whoever was in your way."

"He had ten years to say it to my face."

"Yes," Ashgrove said simply. "He did. And that's a debt he can't pay now, and I won't pretend otherwise to make this easier. But the map is real, and the vein is real, and the men hunting both are real, and right now they're asking the same questions in this town that you asked me an hour ago. Respectable-looking men in good coats. I've seen their kind before. I buried friends of theirs' making twenty years ago."

"So take me with you."

"No."

"You don't get to say no. He's my father—"

"Which is exactly why I'm saying it." For the first time his voice roughened, and she saw the man under the granite. "I made a promise to keep that map moving and to find you. I never promised to drag his daughter into country that killed him. You want your inheritance? Wait in town. When it's done, if I'm alive, you'll have what's yours and answers besides. That's the best trade on the table."

"It's a terrible trade." She stepped closer, close enough to see the fatigue carved around his eyes, the week-old bloodstain on his coat cuff she'd missed indoors. "You think I've waited ten years on a farm outside Denver, reading that letter until it fell apart, to sit in a boarding house drinking tea while strangers decide my family's business? My mother died waiting for that man to come home. I buried her with his letters in her hands. I am not *waiting* anymore, Mr. Ashgrove. Whatever's up that mountain — the silver, the answers, the men in good coats — I'm going. With you or without you. Without you, I die on the first scree slope and it's on your conscience. With you, I might live, and you might sleep at night having kept both promises."

The river ran loud in the silence. High above, the evening light slid off the summits, and somewhere in that white distance a vein of silver lay under ice with her name on it, and her father's ghost, and men who killed for less than either.

Ashgrove looked at her for a long time. Whatever he was weighing, it wasn't her toughness — she could see he'd already priced that. He was weighing something older and heavier, some debt of his own that had nothing to do with her father.

"You can ride?" he said at last.

"Since I was six."

"Shoot?"

"Well enough to be dangerous. Mostly to myself."

"Do you have gear?"

"I have a letter and three dollars."

Something that was almost a smile moved through his beard and died young. "We leave tomorrow night. Dark. And Wren—" he paused, and the granite came back into his face, "—the men asking questions in town? If any of them speak to you, you know nothing about me, nothing about maps, and you're here about a dead mule. Understood?"

"Understood." She held out her hand. He took it; his grip was dry and hard as rope. "And Mr. Ashgrove — when this is done, you'll tell me everything he said at the end. Every word. That's my price for riding quiet."

"Every word," he agreed.

But as they walked back toward the lamplit street, Marguerite caught him glancing up at the darkening peaks, and she saw in his face something he hadn't shown her in the saloon — not doubt of her, but dread of the mountain, quick and involuntary, like a man flinching from an old scar.

She filed it away with everything else. Tomorrow night they would climb into the teeth of the place that had eaten her father, and she intended to arrive knowing more than her guide thought she did.

Behind them, unnoticed by either, a man in a good coat stepped out of the Lucky Strike's doorway and turned up the street toward the livery, walking unhurried, keeping to the shadows between the lamps.
