## 3. Men in Good Coats

They came into Copper Bend on a Tuesday, and Theo knew trouble had arrived before he saw a single face.

He knew it by the horses. Three of them tied at the livery rail where the town's own stock stood shaggy and half-starved — these were bay geldings with clean legs, deep chests, shoes fresh-set within the week. Nobody rode horses like that up from the low country for pleasure. Nobody rode them at all unless somebody was paying well to have them ridden.

Theo was shoeing a mule behind the farrier's shed when they passed him on the street, and he kept his head down over the hoof and let his hat brim do its work. But twenty years of reading men in bad country doesn't stop working just because you want it to. He heard three sets of boots on the boardwalk, unhurried, spaced like men who'd walked together long enough to stop thinking about it. He heard the creak of good leather. And he heard one of them say, in a voice pitched to carry no farther than its owner intended:

"—the girl first. The old man's guide after."

The voice was smooth as river stone. Theo's hands went still on the mule's fetlock, and the animal — sensible creature that she was — shifted her weight and blew out through her nostrils, as if she'd caught on the wind what he'd caught in his gut.

*The girl first.*

Marguerite Wren was staying at the Widow Haskett's boarding house, in the corner room with the crooked shutter, because it was the only room the widow had and because Marguerite had paid for a week in coin she clearly couldn't spare. She'd been in town four days now, and in those four days Theo had told her everything except the map's destination: how her father had died in the high meadow below Sawtooth Ridge, gut-shot by parties unknown; how he'd pressed a folded paper into Theo's hands with his last living strength; how his final words had been her name, spoken twice, once as a question and once as an apology. Marguerite had taken it standing, chin up, eyes dry in a way that Theo recognized as more dangerous than weeping. She wanted her inheritance. She wanted, though she hadn't said it outright, an apology that no man alive could give her. And she wanted to come along on whatever journey the map described, and Theo had told her no with such flat finality that she'd stopped speaking to him entirely, which suited him fine, because every hour he spent near her was an hour he spent remembering Elias Wren's blood soaking into the meadow grass.

Now three strangers were asking after her, and after him, and the mule was looking at him like it expected better judgment from a man of his years.

* * *

He found them at the Silver Dollar Saloon at sundown, and he did not go inside. He went to the window instead, to the gap between the whiskey barrels stacked against the wall, and he looked through the fly-blown glass at the three men seated at the corner table, and his stomach turned over slow and cold, the way it does when a body recognizes something it had prayed was dead.

Callum Reeve sat with his back to the wall, because Callum Reeve always sat with his back to the wall. Twenty years had put silver at his temples and softness around his jaw, but the face underneath was the same face Theo had last seen across a burning campfire on the Grey Ledge traverse, lit orange and utterly calm while everything Theo had built went up around him. Reeve wore a city coat now — dark wool, tailored, a watch chain across the vest — and he held a glass of the saloon's worst whiskey like he was doing it a favor. His two companions flanked him: a big flat-faced man with a broken nose who was drinking nothing, and a younger one with neat blond hair and spectacles who was writing something in a small book, quick and precise, like a clerk taking inventory.

Reeve said something and the big man laughed. Even through the glass, even across twenty years, the sound of it went through Theo like a splinter finding old wood.

Twenty years ago, Theo Ashgrove had been the best high-country guide on the western slope. He'd had a name that opened doors, contracts with two survey companies, and a partner he trusted with his life: Callum Reeve, who could read weather like scripture and lie like breathing. Together they'd signed on to lead a syndicate outfit into the northern range — silver country, disputed claims, money moving hands that nobody counted in public. And somewhere above the tree line, in a camp under the Grey Ledges, Reeve had sold the route maps to a rival outfit, taken the syndicate's advance besides, and when the resulting mess turned violent — when two of the syndicate's own men died in a "guiding accident" that Reeve arranged down to the loosened rope — it was Theo's name on the contract, Theo's rope, Theo's reputation left hanging from the cliff. The inquiry had found *negligence contributing to loss of life*. The companies had dropped him. The towns had closed their doors. He'd spent two decades guiding poachers and hermits and men who didn't ask questions, blacklisted in every office between here and the coast, while Callum Reeve climbed the syndicate's ladder rung by rung until he could afford city coats and fresh-shod horses.

And now Callum Reeve was sitting in the Silver Dollar asking questions about a dead prospector's daughter.

Theo stepped back from the window. His hands were shaking, and he looked at them with a kind of detached disgust, the way a man looks at a wound that has reopened. Fear, yes. But under the fear, something older and uglier: the memory of standing in that inquiry room with every eye on him, knowing the truth and unable to prove it, watching Reeve testify in a clear steady voice about ropes he'd never touched.

The past had teeth. It always had. He'd only ever pretended otherwise.

* * *

He went to the boarding house through the back alleys, moving the way he moved in country where being seen was being dead, and he knocked on Marguerite Wren's door with a pattern of raps he hoped she'd read as urgent rather than rude.

She opened it with a candle in one hand and a boning knife in the other, which improved his opinion of her considerably.

"They're here," he said. "Three of them. Asking after you."

"Who is?"

"The mining syndicate." He stepped past her before she invited him, because inviting took time and time was a currency running short. "The same outfit your father ran afoul of. The same outfit, I'd wager, that put a bullet in him."

Marguerite set the candle down. Her hand, he noticed, was not quite steady on the knife, but she didn't put the knife away either. "You know them."

"I know their leader." The words came out of him like teeth pulled. "Callum Reeve. Twenty years ago he destroyed my name and I could never prove it. Now he's in town wearing a good coat, asking the same questions you asked four days ago."

Something moved behind her eyes — anger, grief, the whole tangled knot she carried — and then hardened into decision. "Then we leave tonight."

"We?"

"You have a map. I have my father's notes — the letters, the marginalia, ten years of his handwriting telling me where he went and why. You think I'm staying in this town with *that* man downstairs?" Her voice stayed low, which was the only reason Theo understood it wasn't bravado. It was arithmetic. She'd done the sums faster than he had. "You promised my father."

"I promised your father I'd find you," Theo said. "Not bury you."

"Then don't." She was already pulling her satchel from under the bed, already stuffing it with the economy of someone who had packed and fled before, or dreamed of it often enough to practice. "But I'm going, Mr. Ashgrove, with you or without you, and if it's without you, I'll get lost and die and it will be on your conscience, which I understand is already crowded."

For a moment — one long, unwelcome moment — Theo saw Elias Wren in her face. The same stubborn set of the jaw. The same refusal to be managed. The dying man had known exactly what he was doing when he made Theo promise.

"Low trail," Theo said finally. "Through the forest, off the ridgelines. We move tonight, before they think to post riders."

"Tonight," she agreed, and blew out the candle.

* * *

They went out the boarding house kitchen door at midnight, past the widow's sleeping cat, down the woodshed alley where the mud was frozen hard enough to hold bootprints without filling them. Theo led, having spent the evening doing what he did best: reading ground. The syndicate men had stabled their horses at the livery, and the livery sat on the north end of town, which meant the south trail was open — for now. But Reeve hadn't built a career on carelessness. There would be a watcher somewhere, and there was.

Theo spotted him at the edge of the lamplight outside the assay office: the big flat-faced man, standing in a doorway with a cigarette, patient as furniture. Not watching the boarding house. Watching the trailhead.

Theo took Marguerite's elbow and drew her into the black mouth of the alley behind the assay office, and he put his lips close to her ear. "There's a man on the south road. We go over the fence behind the smithy, cut through the corral pasture, and pick up the creek bed a half mile down. You can walk a creek bed in the dark?"

"I grew up in a mining camp," she whispered back. "I can walk a creek bed blindfolded."

They went over the fence. The pasture grass was stiff with frost and it crackled under their boots like breaking ice, and every crackle sounded to Theo like a gunshot announcing them, though rationally he knew sound didn't carry that far in cold still air — irrationality being, in his experience, the natural condition of a hunted man. At the far fence line he paused and looked back once.

Copper Bend lay quiet under a fat yellow moon, all forty buildings of it, smoke standing straight up from a few chimneys. Somewhere in that town Callum Reeve was sleeping or pretending to sleep, and tomorrow morning he would learn that the daughter of Elias Wren and the disgraced guide Theo Ashgrove had vanished in the night, and he would smile — Theo knew he would smile, that particular unhurried smile — and send his men out after them, and follow himself, because Callum Reeve never trusted the important work to anyone else. Not since the Grey Ledges. Not since he'd learned that hired hands could be bought, but only he could be relied upon to sell.

"Mr. Ashgrove." Marguerite's whisper came from the fence beside him, sharp with impatience or fear or both. "Which way?"

He turned from the town. Ahead of them the creek glimmered down out of the black shoulder of the mountains, and beyond the mountains, somewhere in all that darkness, a dead man's map lay folded against Theo's chest, carrying them both toward whatever Elias Wren had died trying to finish.

"Creek bed," Theo said. "We walk water where they can't track us. And Marguerite—" he hesitated, hating the admission even as the words formed, "—we don't stop moving until the sun's high. Reeve travels fast when he's hunting."

"How fast?"

"Fast enough that twenty years ago, I made the mistake of believing I'd escaped him." He swung down over the fence into the frozen willows. "I won't make it twice."

Behind them, faint across the frost-silenced fields, a door banged in Copper Bend, and a dog began to bark.
