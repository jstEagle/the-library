## 5. What the Ledger Says

The cabin squatted in a fold of the timber like something the mountain had tried to swallow and failed. Its roof sagged under two decades of snow-load and regrowth, spruce boughs pressing down on shakes gone soft as bread, but the walls stood — hand-hewn logs notched tight, chinked with moss that had gone black and hard. Theo circled it once before he let Marguerite inside, the way he'd circle any stranger, checking for sign: no fresh tracks, no horse droppings, no smoke-stain at the stovepipe's elbow. Only then did he shoulder open a door that groaned on leather hinges.

"Trapper's," he said. "Long abandoned. Elias likely used it himself, years back."

Inside smelled of mouse nests and old pitch and the ghost of woodsmoke soaked so deep into the logs that no amount of weather could wash it out. A stone hearth filled one wall. A bunk frame stood against another, its canvas long rotted to lace. In the corner sat a table built from a single split slab, scarred by knife-points and candle wax, and above it, nailed to the log wall, hung a rack of antlers with a hat still perched between the tines — felt eaten to a gray halo, but a hat nonetheless, left behind by some man who had meant to come back.

Marguerite stood in the doorway while Theo knelt at the hearth, laying kindling with the economy of long habit. She watched his hands — quick, certain, unhesitating — and thought of her father's hands, which she remembered only as large and rough and smelling of tobacco, lifting her onto a fence rail when she was five. Everything else about him was fog. Twenty-three years of it.

"You knew him," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd been circling toward it all day, through the wet timber, through the ford where she'd nearly drowned and where this man had hauled her out by the collar without a word of blame or praise.

Theo struck a match. "I knew him eleven days."

"Eleven days." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "I had him nineteen years and got nothing. You get eleven days and he gives you—" She stopped herself before *the map*, before *everything*, but the shape of the words hung there anyway.

"He gave me a job to do." Theo fed the flame until it caught the splits of pine, and light climbed the walls. "That's all I know how to tell you. Sit down. We're staying the night — storm's coming off the divide, and I'd rather meet it behind walls."

She didn't sit. She took off her soaked boots instead, set them by the fire with the grim competence that had surprised him twice now, and unwrapped her feet, wincing. Then, because standing still was unbearable, she began to explore the cabin the way she explored everything — thoroughly, angrily, looking for something.

* * *

The map lived inside Theo's coat, against his chest, where it had lived since a dying man pressed it into his hands in a meadow going gold with evening. He'd shown Marguerite the route itself — the low trail, the river, the pass beyond — because she'd earned that much and more. But there were margins. There were always margins, and Elias Wren had written in them the way other men breathed.

Theo spread the map on the table now, weighting the corners with stones from the hearth, and bent close to read the marginalia by firelight. *Low water at the second ford after rain.* *Grizzly working the berry slope, give it room.* And smaller, cramped almost illegible: *If it comes to it, the chute below the pass is survivable on the north side. Barely. I barely did.*

Marguerite leaned over his shoulder. Her hair, drying, smelled of woodsmoke and pine, and Theo was abruptly aware of how young she was — younger than the anger made her seem.

"That one," she said, tapping the note about the chute. "He wrote that like a man leaving instructions for someone specific."

"He did." Theo traced the edge of the map with one finger. "For whoever finished what he started."

"And what did he start?" The question cracked out of her sharper than she meant it to. "Eleven days, Mr. Ashgrove. What does a man say in eleven days that makes him hand a stranger a fortune and a daughter?"

Theo didn't answer right away. His finger had stopped at the map's lower corner, where the paper was thicker than it should be. He'd noticed it days ago — a stiffness, a seam — and told himself it was mending, or paste, or nothing. Now, in this cabin, with the storm beginning to mutter against the roof and Marguerite's grief filling the room like rising water, he found he couldn't lie to himself anymore.

"There's something in here," he said quietly. "Something besides the map."

He worked the seam with his thumbnail, then with his knife-point, careful as a surgeon. The backing layer parted along a fold that had been glued flat with pine pitch, and from inside it slid a single sheet, folded twice more, yellowed and soft as cloth at the creases.

It wasn't part of the map at all.

* * *

Marguerite saw the page before Theo could unfold it fully, and something in his face — the way the firelight caught him going still — made her reach for it first.

Columns. Dates running down the left margin in her father's hand, the same hand from the letter she'd carried across half a territory, the letter dated ten years back that said only *I'm alive, don't look for me, I'm sorry*. Numbers beside the dates. Names beside the numbers. And in the far column, small and terrible in their plainness, sums of money.

"Payments," Theo said over her shoulder. His voice had changed — flattened, careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he already knows is thin. "Read the names."

She read them aloud, because reading them aloud was the only way to make them real. "Hollis, T. March 1889. Corrigan, P. June 1889. Voss, A. September 1889." Each name followed by an amount, each amount followed by a word or two in the margin: *settled.* *closed.* *handled.* "These are claims. Claim-jumpers' claims. Father wrote about them — men who jumped a claim and got run off, or worse. The syndicate bought them out cheap or—"

"Or paid someone else entirely." Theo took the page gently from her hands and held it to the light. "Look at who received the money. Not the claim holders. These are payments *out*. To the same three names, over and over. Reeve. Dane. Merrick." He set it down. "Callum Reeve. I'd bet my life these are the same men wearing good coats in that valley town."

Marguerite's eyes went back to the columns, faster now, hunting. And then they stopped.

The date was April 1891. The entry read: *Lachance, M. — claim on Miller's Fork. Refused offer. Settled otherwise.*

And beneath it, in writing pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the paper: *God forgive me. I signed the papers. I was there.*

"Marguerite." Theo's voice came from very far away. "What was your mother's name?"

The cabin went silent except for the fire and the wind finding the chinks in the walls. Marguerite heard herself answer from somewhere outside her own body.

"Marie. Marie Lachance. Before she married my father." Her finger hovered over the entry without touching it, the way you hover over a stove you know will burn. "She had a claim. She never talked about it. She said the mountains took things from people and gave back nothing." Her voice frayed. "She died when I was nine. They said it was fever. They said—"

"They said fever." Theo's jaw was rigid. "Elias was there. Whatever happened on Miller's Fork, he watched it happen, and he signed whatever they put in front of him, and then he ran and hid for twenty years and wrote *God forgive me* until the ink wore out his pen."

Marguerite stepped back from the table. The firelight moved on her face and she let it, because if she moved her face might break apart and she needed — she needed one more minute of being whole. Her mother, who had sung to her in French and taught her letters and died coughing blood into a rag while doctors shook their heads. Her mother, who had owned a piece of ground worth killing for, and been killed for it, and whose daughter had grown up poor and fatherless believing in fevers.

"She wasn't sick," Marguerite said. Each word laid down like a stone in a wall. "They killed her. My father helped them kill her, and then he spent twenty years drawing maps and writing notes in margins because he couldn't say it out loud."

"Yes."

"And the silver — the vein at the end of this map —"

"Is bait." Theo folded the ledger page with the same care he'd unfolded it. "Or it was. Elias dangled the one thing the syndicate wants more than anything — the vein he found and never reported — to draw them into the open. Or to buy passage for whoever carried this." He tapped the folded page. "This isn't a treasure map, Marguerite. It's testimony. It always was."

Outside, the wind rose, driving sleet against the shake roof in long rattling volleys. Inside, Marguerite Wren stood very straight in a trapper's ruined cabin, twenty-three years old, orphaned twice over in the space of a minute, and Theo Ashgrove — who had trusted no one for twenty years, who had agreed to carry a stranger's wish the way you carry a weight you expect to resent — watched grief turn over inside her like a blade being reforged.

When she spoke again, her voice was level. Colder than the storm.

"We're not turning back."

"I didn't think you would be."

"My mother's name goes in front of a judge. Not a grave. A judge." She looked up at him, and her eyes were dry and bright and terrible. "Whatever my father wanted done with that vein — the money, the fortune, all of it — I don't care. I want the men who received those payments. I want Callum Reeve, if that's really his name, standing in a courtroom hearing my mother's name read aloud."

Theo nodded slowly. Somewhere in his chest, twenty years of caution shifted and settled into something harder and more useful. Because he knew those three names too. Reeve. Dane. Merrick. He'd known them when they were younger and their crimes were smaller, and he'd lost everything to them once already — his name, his livelihood, his place in the world — and had spent two decades telling himself the past was past.

The past had teeth. He'd known that since the valley town. But now it had a face he owed a debt to, and a name that belonged to the woman standing in front of him with her dead mother's murder folded in her fist.

"Then we go higher," he said. "Over the pass, down the far slope to Fort Meridian. Two days' climb, two days' descent, ahead of the early snows if the weather holds — which it won't." He glanced at the window, where the dark had thickened to wool. "And Reeve's men are somewhere behind us on this trail."

As if the mountain had been waiting for the words, a sound came up through the trees from the valley below — faint, carried on the wind, unmistakable to a man who'd spent his life listening to country.

A gunshot. Then another.

Then, thinner and farther off, the high whinny of horses that were not theirs.
