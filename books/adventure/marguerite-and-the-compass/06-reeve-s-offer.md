## 6. Reeve's Offer

The river told them before Reeve did.

Theo heard it first as a change in the water's voice — a deeper note under the shallows they'd been wading all morning, the sound of a river gathering its shoulders. The ford where Elias's map crossed the Grayling was a wide gravel bar in August. In late September, with three days of warm rain on the high snowfields behind them, it was something else entirely: brown water moving fast and heavy, carrying whole pine trunks like matchsticks, hissing against the far bank forty yards of white-capped current away.

"Too high to cross," Marguerite said, standing at the edge with her boots already off, slung around her neck by their laces, her trousers rolled to the knee out of pure stubbornness. She looked at the water the way she'd learned to look at everything since the low trail — measuring it, arguing with it.

"It'll drop by morning if the rain holds off."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we go up." He nodded upstream, where the valley walls pinched together and the grayling gorge began, a slot of water between granite walls that no man could swim and no horse could climb beside. "There's a bridge of sorts. Elias marked it. Timber, old, maybe rotten. We'll see."

She made a face that said she'd heard *maybe rotten* before and hadn't enjoyed the experience, but she didn't argue. That was the thing about her that kept surprising him — she argued with him plenty, but never with the mountain.

They were pulling the packs off the horses when Theo felt it: that prickle at the back of the neck, older than memory, the one that had kept him alive through twenty years of guiding fools through country that killed them. He turned slowly, hand drifting toward the rifle slung across his bedroll.

Three riders had come down the south bank, single file, unhurried. They stopped a hundred yards off, close enough to make out faces, too far for a pistol to be worth anything. The man in front sat his horse like he'd been poured into the saddle and left there to set — long, lean, silver at the temples, wearing a wool coat too good for this country and a smile that had never once reached his eyes.

Callum Reeve.

Twenty years fell on Theo like a rockslide. The last time he'd seen that face, it had been lit by lantern light in a syndicate office in Meridian, smooth and reasonable, explaining why the survey reports had to be adjusted, why the claim boundaries had moved overnight, why Theo Ashgrove's signature on falsified assays was worth more to everyone concerned than Theo Ashgrove's honesty. And when Theo had refused — when he'd gone to the territorial recorder instead — it was Reeve who had stood up at the hearing with documents in his soft hands and buried him. Perjury, they'd called it. Fraud. His name struck from the guide rolls, his outfit seized, his reputation fed into the same mill that ground up every claim-jumper and small operator who wouldn't sell.

*Men in good coats,* Elias had called them, spitting blood. *They kill you with paper first.*

"Mr. Ashgrove!" Reeve called across the water, and his voice carried beautifully, trained for boardrooms and courtrooms, pitched now to sound like an old friend hailing another across a churchyard. "You're a hard man to find. I've been asking after you from here to the divide."

"You found me," Theo said. "Now ride on."

"I'd hoped for better manners." Reeve dismounted, unhurried, and handed his reins to one of the men behind him — big men, quiet men, the kind the syndicate hired when paper wasn't enough. He walked to the water's edge and stood there, boots dry, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring the view. "But then, you always did have a talent for holding grudges. Twenty years, isn't it? You must be tired, Theo. May I call you Theo? We were friends once."

"We were never friends. You were the knife and I was the hand that got blamed for the cutting."

Something flickered behind Reeve's pleasant expression — annoyance, quickly filed away. "Water under the bridge," he said lightly, gesturing at the torrent between them as though he'd arranged it for the metaphor. "Which brings me to my purpose. I'm not here for unpleasantness. I'm here to make you whole."

Marguerite had drifted closer to Theo, close enough that he could feel her breathing, quick and shallow. Her hand was inside her coat, and he knew without looking that it was wrapped around the ledger — Elias's ledger, folded inside the false back of the map case, the pages that named names and dates and payments. *Paid to H. Voss, for the Wren woman's claim, settled permanently.* Settled permanently. Four words that meant his mother had been murdered for a hillside of low-grade ore, and Marguerite had read them aloud in a dark cabin with a voice like flint striking steel.

"Whole," Theo repeated. "That's a word with a price tag on it."

"Everything has a price tag. That's not cynicism, that's arithmetic." Reeve smiled again, and God help him, Theo remembered how persuasive that smile had been to a younger, poorer man. "Here is my offer, plainly, because I respect plain dealing even when it's been denied me. The map. The old man's map, and whatever papers traveled with it. In exchange: five thousand dollars, deposited wherever you name. And—" he paused, savoring it, letting the words land like coins on a counter— "a full restoration. Public statement, signed by the company's counsel, withdrawing every allegation. Your name back on the guide rolls. Your outfit returned or its value paid. Everything they took from you, Theo. Everything you've spent twenty years being nothing without."

The river roared. Somewhere above them a hawk screamed once and went silent.

And Theo — God forgive him — Theo wanted it.

It rose in him like floodwater, sudden and filthy and irresistible. Twenty years. Twenty years of taking clients nobody else would guide, drunkards and dreamers and greenhorns who'd heard the rumors and hired him anyway because he was cheap. Twenty years of eating beans in a cabin with someone else's brand on the deed. Twenty years of watching men who'd done half what he'd done walk free and prosperous while his own name was a curse in every outfitter's office from Meridian to the coast. He had told himself he didn't care. He had built a whole life on not caring, the way a man builds a cabin on scree — and it had seemed solid enough until a dying prospector put a map in his hands and a daughter walked into his camp with fire in her eyes, and suddenly he cared about everything.

Five thousand dollars. His name clean. Walk away from the mountain, from the storm coming down early, from the girl whose father he'd promised—

He glanced at her. Just a glance. She was watching him, and her face gave him nothing, and that nothing was worse than anger. Because she knew. He could see that she knew exactly what that offer weighed, and she was waiting to see what kind of man held the scale.

"Theo," Reeve said softly, and the softness was the worst part, the reasonableness of it. "You're fifty years old. How many more seasons do you have? Take the money. Take your name. Let the dead bury their dead. The old man was a fool who wouldn't sell either — look where it got him, gut-shot in a meadow. Don't be a fool twice in one life."

"There it is," Theo said quietly. "I wondered when you'd confess to it."

Reeve's smile didn't move but his eyes went flat. "Confess to what? An unfortunate incident I had no part in? Elias Wren was robbed by parties unknown—"

"He wrote your name, Reeve. Dated it. Along with Voss and Aldous Crane and every payment ever made to bury a claim under a corpse. It's all written down, and copies exist in places you'll never find." That was a bluff, half of it — there was only one ledger — but Reeve couldn't know that, and the flicker in his face said the hook had set. "You can't buy paper back once it's loose."

For the first time, Callum Reeve stopped smiling.

"Then you understand my position," he said, and the boardroom warmth drained out of his voice like water out of a cracked cup, leaving something colder underneath. "I came to offer you a future. I can offer the alternative instead. There are two of you and three of us, Mr. Ashgrove, and my men are very good shots, and the territory is very large, and bodies stay lost in country like this for decades. Give me the map. This is the last time I ask nicely."

Theo's hand closed on the rifle sling. A hundred yards of open water between them; the current would slow any crossing by minutes, and minutes were lives with rifles involved. But the two hired men were already swinging down from their saddles, levering rounds into their magazines with the calm economy of professionals, and Theo had survived twenty years by knowing exactly when the arithmetic stopped working.

Then Marguerite stepped forward to the water's edge, boots still hanging around her neck, and her voice went out over the river clear and hard as breaking ice.

"My father spent ten years writing me letters he never sent. Ten years of being ashamed to face his own daughter. Do you know what he was ashamed of, Mr. Reeve? Not the mining. Not even the running. He was ashamed that he'd stayed silent while men like you buried people." She held up the map case, dangling it from two fingers, and for one insane moment Theo thought she meant to throw it in the river. "This isn't for sale. My mother's name isn't for sale. And his name—" she jerked her chin at Theo— "wasn't yours to take twenty years ago, and it won't be yours to give back now. He doesn't need your paper. He's going to watch you hang with the right paper."

"Marguerite," Theo started.

"No." She turned on him, and her eyes were wet and blazing at once. "No. You listen to me, Theo Ashgrove. I watched you look at that offer. I watched you *want* it. And I don't blame you — God knows you've earned a clean name ten times over. But if you take it, you take it knowing what it buys. Silence. Her silence—" her voice cracked and she drove through it— "*her* silence, bought twice. Once with a bullet and once with five thousand dollars. I will not carry that. And neither will you."

The river roared between them and the gunmen. Reeve stood very still on the far bank, and Theo saw him weigh something, and saw him decide against it — not mercy, never mercy, just arithmetic. Two rifles in the open against cover and distance and a man who knew this country like his own heartbeat. Not today. Today he'd mark the map's line and follow it higher with more men.

"Spring thaw," Reeve said at last, remounting, and the pleasant mask was back, which was somehow more frightening than the coldness. "I'll be in Meridian. So will the judge you're so fond of imagining. Think about who owns the courthouse, Theo. Think about who owns everything." He gathered his reins. "Enjoy the high country. It snows early this year."

They wheeled and rode south along the bank, unhurried, professional, gone into the trees like smoke drawn up a chimney.

* * *

Theo didn't speak until the last hoofbeat faded. Then he let out a breath he felt he'd been holding for twenty years.

"They'll circle ahead," he said. "Cut us off above the gorge. We've got maybe six hours."

"Then we'd better use them." But she was looking at him sideways, and he knew the question was still standing between them, the one she'd asked with her blazing eyes at the water's edge. *Would you have taken it?*

"Yes," he said, because she deserved the truth and because saying it out loud was the only way to drive a stake through it. "For about ten seconds, I'd have taken it. Sold your father's bones for a receipt with my name on it."

She was quiet a moment. "Ten seconds isn't twenty years," she said finally. "I can live with ten seconds." She swung her pack up. "Can you?"

He thought about it honestly, the way he thought about a route — hazards, exposure, odds. "Ask me at the top," he said.

They scattered Reeve's business where it lay — no, Theo corrected himself grimly, they scattered *their own* horses' rest, cutting the animals loose up-valley with a slap and a shout so they'd run for the open meadow and be days recapturing, buying speed on foot where speed mattered. Then they shouldered the packs and turned north, up the Grayling, toward the gorge and the maybe-rotten bridge and the tree line glittering white above it, where the early snow was already falling like a debt come due.
