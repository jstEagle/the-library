## 7. Above the Tree Line

The trees gave out the way a voice gives out at the end of a long speech — not all at once, but in a thinning, until there was nothing left to say. First the pines grew stunted and twisted, leaning east like men flinching from a blow. Then they were only shrubs. Then only krummholz, matted thickets you could walk on top of. And then, past eleven thousand feet, nothing green at all — just stone, gray and rust-red and bone-white, heaped to the horizon as if some god had emptied his pockets over the range and never bothered to pick them up.

Marguerite had climbed mountains before. She'd thought she had. The Adirondacks with her school friends, all of them laughing, carrying sandwiches and lemonade in a wicker basket. That was walking uphill. This was something else. This was a country that did not want her.

The air had been getting thinner for two days, but this morning it turned openly hostile. Each breath came short, rationed, as though the mountain were a miser counting out coins. Her pack straps bit into shoulders that had gone strangely numb. The sun was fierce and useless — it warmed nothing.

"Short steps," Theo said ahead of her, not turning. He'd said it a dozen times since dawn. "Don't fight the grade. Let the mountain set the pace."

"I know how to walk," she snapped, and then hated herself for it, because even speaking cost breath she didn't have.

He glanced back anyway. There was frost in the stubble of his beard and something in his eyes that wasn't quite patience and wasn't quite worry either. "Everyone knows how to walk down here," he said. "Up here, you relearn it."

They climbed through the morning on a spine of broken rock, scree sliding under their boots with every step, each footfall a small betrayal. Below them the valley where they'd slept was already a rumor, a fold of shadow. Above, the pass they needed was still hidden behind a shoulder of black stone, wearing its first snow like a warning delivered early. Elias's notes had said the crossing was good into October. It was barely the end of September, and the sky had the bruised, heavy look of a sky making plans.

By midday Marguerite noticed her own breathing had changed character. It wasn't just short anymore. It was wrong — a wet rattle at the bottom of each inhale, and a headache that had started behind her eyes at breakfast now sat behind everything, a dull iron band being tightened by degrees. The world had developed a shimmer at its edges.

She didn't say anything. She'd spent ten years learning not to say things — not to her father's memory, not to the aunts who'd raised her on silence and disapproval, not to this man who carried her father's map and half his secrets. Saying *I'm not well* felt like surrendering the one currency she had left: competence. She'd earned something on the low trail, wading that flooded ford with the ledger-page sewn inside her coat, reading Elias's cramped marginalia while Theo watched with new respect. She would not give it back on a slope.

But the mountain doesn't care what you won't admit.

It happened at the base of the black shoulder, where the route bent west into a bowl of loose stone. One moment she was stepping where Theo stepped; the next, the ground tilted gently, absurdly, like the deck of a riverboat, and the sky swung around to stand beside her instead of above. She heard herself sit down hard — heard it as if from another room.

Theo was back beside her in four strides, crouching, pulling off his glove, pressing the back of his hand to her cheek. His skin was cold; hers must have been colder, because something flickered across his face.

"How long?" he asked. "The headache. How long?"

"Since—" She stopped. Lied. He waited. "Since last night."

"And you didn't say."

"It would have passed."

"It's passing you instead." He was already unclipping her pack, shrugging it onto his own alongside his, so that he stood suddenly doubled, a man carrying two lives up a mountain. "Nausea?"

"A little."

"Dizzy when you stood?"

"Yes."

He nodded once, grim, a doctor confirming what he already knew. "Altitude sickness. You've been climbing too proud. Up here pride is a debt, and the mountain collects." He dug in his pocket and pressed two strips of dried meat into her hand. "Chew. Small sips from your flask. We need to get you lower or sheltered before—"

He didn't finish, because the wind chose that moment to arrive.

It came off the summit ridge like something released rather than blowing — a white wall of spindrift peeling off the cornices above, and behind it clouds that hadn't existed an hour ago boiling up from the far side of the range. The temperature fell so fast Marguerite could feel it through her coat, a hand closing around her.

"That's not forecast weather," Theo said quietly. "That's a storm arriving weeks early. Elias's notes said—"

"Elias's notes are twenty years old," Marguerite managed. "Maybe the mountain read them too."

For the first time since the meadow where her father died, Theo Ashgrove laughed — a short, startled bark of sound, gone almost before it came. Then he took her arm and hauled her upright. "There's a cave. Half a mile, maybe less. Elias marked it. Can you walk half a mile?"

She could not, and they both knew it, and she said, "Yes," because she was her father's daughter, whatever else that meant.

She made perhaps two hundred yards.

* * *

The whiteout took them between one step and the next. There had been rock, and wind, and the gray shape of Theo ahead of her, and then there was nothing — no up, no down, no horizon, just a whiteness so complete it seemed to press against her eyeballs. She staggered, found her knees, tasted blood where she'd bitten her lip.

Then arms were around her, and the world tilted, and she understood that Theo Ashgrove was lifting her — this man twice her age, carrying both packs, hauling her onto his back as if she weighed nothing more than the map that had started all of this.

"Put me down," she said into his collar. "I can—"

"You can't. I can. Hold on and don't talk. Save your breath for staying alive."

So she held on. She locked her arms around his neck and pressed her face against the wool of his coat and let him carry her through the end of the world. The wind screamed. Snow came sideways, then from below, then from everywhere, erasing even the memory of direction. She felt him stop, turn, stop again — navigating by something invisible, by memory or instinct or sheer stubborn refusal. Once he went down to his hip on a patch of ice and rose again without a word, adjusting her weight, climbing on. She could feel his heartbeat through his back, fast and steady as a pump handle, and she fixed on it the way a drowning woman fixes on light.

*I have been carried before,* she thought, drifting. *I don't remember it. Someone carried me once and then put me down and walked away for ten years.*

The thought should have made her angry. She reached for the anger and found it missing, like a pocket picked clean somewhere on the trail. What remained was smaller and heavier: the simple animal fact of being held, of someone's strength spent entirely on keeping her alive, asking nothing.

The cave, when they reached it, was barely a cave — a hollow under an overhang of granite, deep enough to sit in, shallow enough that the wind still clawed at their boots. But it was out of the worst of it, and Theo got the small stove going with hands that shook only a little, and melted snow, and made her drink it warm with a pinch of sugar from their dwindling store. Then he wrapped her in both blankets and sat her against the rock, and only then did he let himself sag, breathing hard, snow melting off his beard in little rivulets.

Outside, the storm howled like something with a grievance.

"Thank you," she said. It came out stiff. Gratitude had always sat awkwardly on her, a coat cut for someone else.

"Don't thank me yet. Storm could hold three days. We've food for two." He fed the stove another twig. "Shouldn't have pushed you today. My fault. I wanted the pass before the weather and I let the wanting do my thinking."

"You wanted it for the vein."

"I wanted it for the ledger." He said it flatly, looking into the flame. "The silver's just the bait the old man used on me. The ledger's the thing that matters. You know that now."

She did. They'd read it together in the trapper's cabin by lamplight — the dates, the payments, the names. *E. Wren, claim jumped, Meridian Fork. A. Delacroix, refused to sell, fire, no survivors.* And one line she had read four times before it would go through her: *M. Wren née Fontaine — syndicate men — saw too much — river accident, staged.* Her mother. Drowned in a river calm enough for church picnics, when Marguerite was six, and everyone had called it God's will.

"Theo." She used his name deliberately; she'd earned that much on the ford. "Tell me about the blacklist. All of it. Not the version you told Reeve's shadow-men in town. The real one."

The stove ticked. Wind pressed at the cave mouth like a beggar at a door.

"You won't like me better after."

"I'm not doing this because I'll like you better."

Something moved in his face — surprise, maybe, or relief. He pulled off his boots and set them by the stove to dry, and when he spoke, he spoke to the fire, which was easier for him, she could see, than speaking to her.

"Twenty-two years ago I guided for the Meridian Consolidated. Best routes man in the high country. Reeve was my partner then — not in business. In everything. We'd come up together, two poor boys who figured the mountains owed us a living." He turned a stick in the flames. "Syndicate found a rich strike on land that wasn't theirs. Needed it quiet. Needed a guide to move a party through in winter, over ground no honest party would cross, to jump a claim held by a widow and her boy. I said no. Reeve said yes. And when the widow's husband came looking for answers, somebody had to hang the crime on — and Reeve stood in front of the magistrate and swore he'd seen me take syndicate money to lead claim-jumpers myself. My word against his. Mine was worth nothing. I had no family, no property, no history anyone would vouch for. He had a good coat and a soft voice."

"So they took your name."

"They took my name and gave it to the crime." He finally looked at her. "Every guide in the territory dropped me. Every outfit. Twenty years I've worked the edges — packing for fools, running supply, taking whatever season work kept me near the high country. Because the high country was the only witness that ever believed me."

Marguerite sat with that. The storm filled the silence for her.

"My father knew," she said at last. "Didn't he. That's why he trusted you with the map. Two ruined names — he figured ruin recognizes ruin."

"He figured worse than that." Theo's voice dropped. "Your father was the syndicate's bookkeeper once, Marguerite. Before he ran. Before he buried himself in the hills with a pickaxe and guilt. Those ledger pages aren't stolen. They're *his*. He wrote the entries. He watched them kill your mother and he wrote it down in his neat little hand and he ran, and he spent twenty years deciding whether the truth was worth more than his daughter's safety. He mailed you that letter and couldn't sign it. He sent for you and died before you came." He paused. "He didn't abandon you. He was hiding you. From men who kill women who see too much."

The words went into her like a blade going into ice — slow, cracking her open along lines she hadn't known were there. Ten years of anger, of rehearsed speeches, of the letter she'd composed a thousand times in her head beginning *Dear Father, explain yourself* — and underneath it all, all along, a man hiding her the way you hide anything precious: badly, painfully, at a distance.

She didn't cry. She was too dehydrated and too stubborn and too far above the tree line for tears to come easily. But her breath hitched, and Theo, to his credit, looked away and tended the stove and pretended the wind was very interesting.

"There's a hole," she said finally, when she could speak evenly. "Where he should be. I've carried it so long I forgot it was a hole. I thought it was just... me."

"I know the hole," Theo said. "Mine's shaped like a name I can't wear. Twenty years, I introduced myself to people and flinched." He looked at her then, straight on, and the firelight made his eyes less flinty than usual. "You don't fill a hole like that, girl. You just learn to build around it. And sometimes — rarely — you meet someone building around the same shape, and you put your backs against the same wall."

Outside, the storm screamed and the temperature kept falling. Inside, Marguerite Wren shivered against the rock of a mountain that had tried to kill her, and found that for the first time in ten years the shivering felt less like fear and more like thawing.

"Two days of food," she said. "Three days of storm."

"Could be less. Could be more."

"Then we'd better hope the mountain gets bored." She pulled the blanket tighter and looked toward the cave mouth, where the whiteout churned like milk in a churn. "Because when it clears, we're finishing what my father started. Both halves of it."

Theo nodded slowly. Then his gaze sharpened on something past her shoulder — the cave mouth, the storm, and through a momentary tear in the white, far below and to the south, a line of dark figures on the ridge, roped together, climbing.

Torchlight, or lantern-glass, glinting once, and was gone.

"Reeve," Theo breathed. "He's not waiting for the storm to pass. He's climbing *through* it."
