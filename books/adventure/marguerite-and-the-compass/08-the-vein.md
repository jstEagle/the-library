## 8. The Vein

They came up out of the dark with the stars still nailed to the sky, Theo breaking trail through knee-deep powder while Marguerite followed in his prints, her breath ragged but steady, her hands bare and blue-knuckled on the ice axe. Elias Wren's notes had promised a shelf of granite below the eastern face, a place where the wind scoured the snow clean, and the notes had not lied. Theo had stopped doubting the dead man's hand three days ago.

"Light," Marguerite said, and her voice came out strange, flattened by altitude and awe, because the east had begun to burn.

It came up slow, the way it always did up here — first a bruised purple along the ridgelines, then a seam of copper, then the whole sky opening like a furnace door. And the light climbed down the mountain and found the face of the glacier below them and set it alight.

Theo stopped walking.

The seam ran diagonally across the ice wall like a vein in a wrist — a hand's width of silver-white metal threaded through blue ice, exposed where a summer's melt and a winter's shatter had split the glacier open. In the dawn light it did not gleam. It burned. It threw the sunrise back in fragments, and the fragments scattered across the snow around them like spilled coins, and Theo Ashgrove, who had spent twenty years learning not to want things, stood in the middle of a fortune and could not move.

"God," Marguerite whispered. "He found it. He actually found it."

"Your father found it twenty years ago," Theo said. "He just never told anyone where."

He worked the sample out with the cold chisel and the small hammer, and it took longer than it should have because his fingers would not obey him — not from the cold, he told himself, though that was a lie he'd carry a while. The ore came free in a chunk the size of a fist, argentite shot through with native silver, heavy as sin. He turned it in his gloved hand. He bit it, an old habit, and the soft metal took the mark of his teeth.

"High grade," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Higher than anything the syndicate ever pulled out of the Kestrel. This one seam, worked proper, is worth more than every claim I ever staked in my life. More than the outfit I lost. More than the name they took."

Marguerite stood close to the ice wall with the light on her face, and for a moment she looked so much like Elias Wren that Theo's chest tightened. Same jaw. Same way of looking at a thing like it owed her an explanation.

"This is what he wanted me to have," she said.

Theo said nothing. He was doing arithmetic, the cold merciless kind, the kind a man does with his back against a wall. He looked at the seam. He looked at the sky to the south, where the storm that had chased them for two days was gathering itself behind the peaks like a debt collector. He looked at the eastern slope, down two days of killer terrain to Fort Meridian, where a frontier judge named Corliss held court in a drafty hall and took testimony from anyone desperate enough to walk that far.

And he looked at the ledger.

It rode against his chest in an oilskin wrap, Elias Wren's folded pages of dates and payments and names — the names of claim-jumpers who had died in "accidents," the payments made to men who made accidents happen, and one entry, dated the spring Marguerite was nine years old, that Theo had read four times and could not unread. *L. Wren — widow's claim seized. No further payment required.*

Marguerite's mother. Murdered on paper in a neat column of figures, in a hand so small and tidy it might have been a grocer's inventory.

* * *

They made a cold camp in the lee of the granite shelf, no fire, because fire on the skyline was a signal and Theo had spent the night watching the glacier's lower tongue through the field glasses. He'd seen them at first light. Four men and a string of pack animals, tiny as lice against the white, moving up the route Reeve's scouts had cut through the icefall. Moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had bought their way up a mountain before.

"They'll reach the seam by tomorrow noon," Theo said. "Maybe sooner if the weather holds, and it won't."

Marguerite took the glasses and looked a long time. When she handed them back her face was composed, but her jaw was set the way it had been set the day she'd walked into his camp demanding her birthright.

"Four of them," she said. "And Reeve?"

"Reeve leads from behind. He'll be third or fourth up the rope. He was never brave. He was always careful. There's a difference, and I paid twenty years learning it."

She crouched by the sample of ore, still sitting on the canvas where he'd chiseled it free, and picked it up. Turned it. The dawn light had gone off it now, and in the flat grey morning it was just rock and metal, heavy and dumb.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever you're calculating. Say it out loud, because I've watched you do sums in your head since the ford, and I'm tired of it."

Theo pulled off a glove and worked feeling back into his fingers. "All right. Here's the sum. We take the ore. We take as much as we can carry — a hundred pounds, maybe, of the richest silver in the territory. We go down the far slope ahead of the storm, we reach the low country, and we sell. And with that money I buy back the guiding license they stripped from me, and I buy the outfit, and the horses, and the reputation, and you buy back your father's seized claim, and neither of us ever has to be hungry or hunted or small again. That's the sum. That's what's sitting in that ice."

"And the ledger?"

"The ledger's paper. Paper burns, gets lost, gets disbelieved. A judge in a frontier fort against the Meridian Consolidated's lawyers — you think Corliss can stand against that on paper alone? But silver buys witnesses. Silver buys lawyers. Silver buys back my name, and a man with a clean name and money can testify in a courtroom and be *believed*." He heard his own voice gathering heat and made himself stop. "That's the argument. I've been making it to myself since midnight."

Marguerite set the ore down. "And the other sum?"

"The other sum is that the ledger is the only thing on this mountain that can hang Callum Reeve. Not the silver. The silver just makes him richer. The ledger makes him *dead* — legally speaking. And every hour we spend chiseling ore out of that ice is an hour closer Reeve gets to taking both." He looked at the storm building in the south. "And the other sum is that Elias Wren didn't carry this map twenty years to buy his daughter a house. He carried it because it was the only proof left that they killed her mother. He just never had the stomach or the spine to deliver it. He was going to, at the end. He was gut-shot in a meadow trying."

The wind came up the face and pushed at them, and somewhere below, faint as a memory, came the sound of the icefall calving — the mountain clearing its throat.

"I never knew her," Marguerite said quietly. "My mother. I was nine. They told me she died of fever." She looked at the ledger in Theo's coat. "Fever. In a column. *No further payment required.*"

"I know."

"My father wrote me one letter in ten years. One. And in it he said, *there's something I owe you.* I thought he meant money. I thought he meant an apology." She shook her head slowly. "He meant a debt. He owed a debt, and he couldn't pay it, so he handed it to a stranger in a meadow."

"Not to a stranger," Theo said. "He asked me first if I'd been wronged by the Meridian. I said yes. He said, *then you'll understand the weight of it.* I thought he was a dying man talking. He wasn't. He was handing me exactly what I asked for."

Marguerite looked at the seam a long moment — the fortune in the ice, glittering dully now, patient as a creditor. Then she looked at Theo.

"So which is it? Is it an inheritance or is it bait?"

Theo got up. He walked to the ice wall, and he put his bare hand against the blue ice beside the seam, and he thought about the man he had been twenty years ago — young, hungry, certain that the mountain owed him a living and the world owed him a name. He thought about the night Callum Reeve sat across a campfire from him with a friendly face and sold the route of his own party to the syndicate's claim-jumpers, and how Theo had survived and been blamed, and how the word *blacklisted* had followed him through two decades like a dog. He had wanted, for twenty years, exactly what was glittering in front of him. He had wanted it so badly it had a shape and a smell.

And now it was here, and it was not enough. That was the thing no one told you. That the thing you wanted most, when it finally came, might arrive wearing the face of the thing that ruined you.

"Some inheritances are debts," he said. "This one's both. The silver's an inheritance. The ledger's the debt. And you don't get to keep an inheritance while the man who wrote the debt is climbing up the mountain to collect."

He took up the chisel — and drove it not into the seam but into the ice beside it, breaking loose a single fist-sized sample, nothing more. He wrapped it in canvas and stowed it in the pack.

"One piece," he said. "Enough to prove the vein exists, if Corliss needs convincing. Enough to show a court what the syndicate would kill for. Evidence, not wages." He met her eyes. "The rest stays in the ice. And when the warrants go out and the syndicate's claims are voided, you and I will come back up here in the summer with a legal right and a proper crew, and we'll take it out of the rock like honest people. Or we won't. But we won't take it like them."

Marguerite was quiet. Then she reached out and touched the seam herself, one fingertip against the metal, the way you'd touch a grave marker.

"Goodbye, Papa," she said. Not to the silver. To the whole mountain — to the marginalia, the notes, the twenty years of a man living up here with his guilt. "You can rest now. I've got it from here."

* * *

They broke camp fast and moved south along the shelf toward the pass, and they had made perhaps a mile when Theo stopped, turned, and put the field glasses on their back trail.

Reeve's party had reached the base of the glacier tongue. Even at this distance Theo could pick him out — the careful one, third up the rope, standing with his hands in his coat pockets while his hired men examined the icefall. And then Reeve did a thing that turned Theo's blood to ice water.

He stopped. He looked up. Not searching — *aiming*. As if he knew exactly which shelf, which route, which shelf of granite two specks of humanity had slept against. As if somewhere in his pocket there was a map drawn in the same hand.

"Marguerite," Theo said. "The notes. Your father's notes. Is there anything — anything at all — about a second copy of the map?"

She went white. "He said — in the meadow, he said, *the mountain knows the way now.* I thought he was raving."

"Or he sold a copy once, years back, to buy provisions. Or Reeve tortured the route out of someone." Theo slung the glasses and turned south, where the pass waited between two black peaks and the storm was already spilling over the ridge like smoke. "It doesn't matter. What matters is they're a half day behind us, and the only way down the far slope is the chute your father survived in 'ninety-one, and the storm's going to close the pass within the hour."

"Then we go now."

"We go now." Theo looked back once at the seam, burning faintly in the ice, and felt lighter than he had in twenty years — and more afraid. "And Marguerite — whatever happens in that chute, the ledger rides on you now. Not me. If I go down, you keep moving. Promise me."

She looked at him, and he saw her weigh it, and he saw her father's jaw set.

"Promise," she said.

Behind them, far below, a sound rolled up the glacier — the crack and boom of the icefall letting go, and beneath it, thin and human, a shout. Reeve's party had found the abandoned camp.

The storm took the pass.
