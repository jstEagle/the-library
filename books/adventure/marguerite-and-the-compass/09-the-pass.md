## 9. The Pass

The storm came down on them like a verdict.

Theo had watched it build all afternoon — a gray wall rolling off the western peaks, swallowing the ridgelines one by one, and he'd known before the first flake fell that they would not outrun it. The pass was a half mile ahead. Reeve's party was a quarter mile behind.

"Wind's going to funnel through that notch," he shouted over the rising howl. "When it does, everything loose on those slopes above comes down with it."

Marguerite stood beside him at the lip of the saddle, snow already crusting her shoulders, her father's notebook open against her chest inside an oilcloth wrap. She didn't ask what he was thinking. He could see it in her face — she was reading the same mountain he was.

"The cornice," she said.

He looked at her. Wind-whipped hair, cracked lips, eyes steady as surveyor's plumb lines. Three weeks ago she'd been a stranger with a dead man's letter and a chip on her shoulder the size of the range itself. Now she stood at nine thousand feet reading avalanche terrain like his own mother might have.

"You see it too."

"That overhang above the eastern approach." She pointed with her chin, hands buried deep in her sleeves. "It's been loading all day. One crack across the base and the whole shelf goes."

"It takes their route with it."

"And ours," she said quietly. "We'd be trapped up here."

Theo turned back toward the notch. Below them, small as ants in the whiteout's edge, four figures moved along the eastern approach — Reeve in front, driving his men upward through wind that bent them double. Coming for the map. Coming for the ledger. Coming, if it came to it, for them.

Twenty years ago, Theo Ashgrove had stood in a valley camp while Callum Reeve smiled at a mining magnate and let Theo's name take the weight of a collapsed tunnel and three dead men. Twenty years of towns that wouldn't hire him, outfits that crossed the street, a name spoken like something you scraped off your boot. And every year of it, some cold practical voice in him had whispered: *you could have been him. You could have learned to smile like that.*

He unslung his pack and took out the coil of rope and the iron pry bar he'd carried since the low trail, telling himself it was for emergencies.

"This is the emergency," Marguerite said.

* * *

The cornice base was thirty feet above the eastern approach, reachable by a traverse along the notch's southern wall. Theo went first, cutting steps into wind-hardened snow, the storm screaming sideways now so hard that each breath came in sideways too, half ice. Behind him Marguerite moved in his tracks, roped to his waist, her face a pale smudge in the murk.

At the cornice's underbelly he found what he'd hoped for and dreaded: a seam where the overhang met the slope, a dark line of weakness running forty feet along the base. He wedged the pry bar into it.

"Once this goes," he called back, "there's no undoing it. Not for them. Not for us."

Marguerite pressed close so he could hear her. Snow filled her eyelashes. "My father spent eleven years out here alone because men like Reeve made the valleys unsafe for honest people." Her voice didn't shake. "I'm not going to spend my life looking over my shoulder either. Cut it loose, Theo."

He thought of Elias Wren gut-shot in a high meadow, trading his last breath for a promise. He thought of the ledger hidden in the map's folds, full of names and dates and payments — proof that the syndicate had murdered claim-jumpers, that among them was a woman named Lise Wren, which meant Marguerite had spent ten years hating a father who'd been hiding from the men who killed her mother.

He set the bar and drove it with his whole body.

The crack ran like a rifle shot. For one heartbeat nothing happened — and then the cornice sighed, settled, and let go, forty tons of snow peeling off the mountainside in a slow white thunder. It took the eastern approach with it, a hundred yards of route erased, and the last thing Theo saw through the swirling murk was Reeve's party scattering like startled birds below the fall line.

Then the roar swallowed everything, and the powder cloud rose around them, and the world went white and silent and total.

* * *

They were trapped. He'd known they would be. Knowing it and standing in it were different things.

The descent route — the only one left — was the chute. Elias had marked it in the notebook with a single line Theo hadn't understood until now: *The throat. It killed Danner in '71. I went down it in '68 with God holding my collar. Do not try it twice.*

Below them the chute dropped eight hundred feet through a slot of blue ice and rotten rock, steep enough that falling meant sliding, and sliding meant the bottom.

"We can't stay," Marguerite said. They'd dug into the lee of a rock rib, backs against stone, sharing body heat while the storm tried to peel them off the mountain. "Snow's coming faster than we can burn it. By morning we're a pair of interesting shapes for next spring's climbers."

"I know." Theo flexed his right hand. It had taken the pry bar's recoil badly — something in the wrist was wrong, a grinding wrongness that flared white when he moved it. He didn't tell her yet. "But I can't lead a descent like that one-handed, and I won't lie to you about it."

She looked at his hand, then at his face, and he watched her do the arithmetic without flinching.

"Then I lead," she said.

"Marguerite—"

"My father wrote down every hold, every anchor point, every place the ice thinned. Eleven years of notes, Theo. You said yourself I read them like a second language." She pulled the notebook from its oilcloth and held it between them like a lantern. "You taught me the mountain. He taught me the words. Let me use both."

The wind screamed. Somewhere below, muffled by the storm, there came a sound like distant thunder that wasn't thunder — and Theo understood that Reeve's party was trying the debris field, or finding another way around, and time was bleeding out of the day faster than the light.

"All right," he said. "All right. But we go on my count, and if I say stop, you stop."

"And if you fall?"

"Then you keep going."

Her jaw set in a way that was pure Elias Wren, though she'd never known it. "No," she said. "That's not how this works anymore. We go down together or not at all. That's the bargain."

He almost laughed. A dying man's bargain in a meadow; a living woman's bargain on a mountainside. It seemed the Wrens only ever dealt in absolutes.

"Together," he agreed.

* * *

The first two hundred feet were the worst of Theo's life.

Marguerite led, calling holds from the notebook — *ice bridge left, trust it; rock horn right, don't* — her voice thin and fierce in the wind, and Theo followed with his broken wrist strapped tight against his chest, descending by boots and one arm, every movement a negotiation with pain. Twice the ice groaned beneath him. Twice he hung by Marguerite's rope, swinging over blackness, watching her lean back against his weight with her heels planted and her whole slight body become an anchor.

"Move!" she screamed the second time. "Theo, move, the anchor's cutting!"

He moved. His boots found the ledge her voice described, and he crawled onto it and lay there gasping while she lowered herself down beside him, shaking, snow-plastered, alive.

"Your father did this," he panted. "In '68."

"He wrote that the mountain forgives no debts." She wiped her face with a mittened hand. "I think he meant it keeps accounts. Come on. Two hundred feet left."

They were fifty feet from the bottom when the shout came.

It rolled down the chute from above — human, furious, echoing off the ice walls. Theo twisted to look up and saw them: figures on the chute's upper rim, tiny in the murk. Reeve had found another way around the slide. Of course he had. Men like Callum Reeve always found another way.

"They're coming down!" Marguerite cried.

"Then we're done being careful!"

They dropped the last fifty feet in a controlled fall, rope hissing through Theo's gloved grip, pain detonating up his arm with every yard, and hit the chute's mouth in a sprawl of snow and breath. Below them the slope gentled toward the tree line, a thousand vertical feet of scree and timber, and beyond it, two days' travel, Fort Meridian and Judge Corliss and the end of twenty years.

Behind them, Reeve descended fast and reckless, his men strung out above him, and even at this distance Theo could hear the rage in the man's voice. The map was ash — burned at the cave, the treasure half of it given to the fire. All Reeve had left to chase was the ledger, and the ledger was worth more than silver now, and he knew it.

"Go!" Theo shoved Marguerite ahead of him. "Tree line! Don't look back!"

They ran. Scree slid under their boots; the storm drove needles of ice into their faces; Theo's arm was a live coal strapped to his ribs. Marguerite pulled ahead, sure-footed as a goat, and he heard her shouting something back at him — encouragement or warning, lost in the wind.

Then the mountain spoke.

It wasn't loud. That was the terrible part. Just a soft whump beneath the wind, somewhere upslope — the sound of a loaded slope deciding, the sound Theo had heard twice in his life and never forgotten. He spun in time to see it: high on the chute's flank, where Reeve's party had cut across a wind-loaded slab, the whole slope shrugging loose.

Three figures scattered. Two made the rocks.

Reeve did not. Theo saw him turn, saw him run — downhill, the one direction that never saves anyone — and then the white water took him, silent from this distance, elegant, absolute. The slab carried him two hundred yards and folded him into the debris field below the cornice they'd cut, and the mountain closed over him the way a ledger closes over a final entry.

His men stood on the rocks staring. Then they looked downslope — at Theo, at Marguerite — and whatever they saw in the two figures standing in the storm made the decision for them. They turned and climbed away, east, toward distances that wanted nothing to do with any of this.

Marguerite reached Theo's side. Snow streamed off them both. Neither spoke. There was nothing to say that the mountain hadn't just said.

"He chose the chase," she said finally, her voice raw. "Right up until the end."

"The mountain doesn't choose anything," Theo said. "It just keeps the books."

They turned south, toward the tree line, toward the timber and the long descent and Fort Meridian waiting two days out with a judge who had never heard of either of them. The storm still raged, but it raged at their backs now.

Theo's arm was broken, the ledger was dry against his chest, and the road ahead was the hardest kind — the kind where you walk out of the mountains carrying the truth, and have to make someone in a warm room believe it.

Two days. One ledger. One chance.

They started down.
