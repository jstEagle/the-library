## 10. Measured in Miles

Fort Meridian smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, and Marguerite had never loved a smell more.

They came down off the mountain on the ninth day after the pass — Theo's arm splinted with a ski pole and her father's belt, both of them hollow-eyed and frost-burned, walking more on stubbornness than flesh. The far slope had been kinder than the killer chute, but only in the way that a slow knife is kinder than a fast one. She remembered little of it now except rhythm: step, breathe, brace, step. Theo falling once at the treeline and her hauling him up by his good arm, both of them laughing in that cracked, hysterical way people laugh when the alternative is weeping.

Now they stood in the doorway of Judge Corliss's chambers, dripping snowmelt onto a floor some clerk would curse them for, and Marguerite held the ledger out like something that might still bite.

It was smaller than she'd expected. That was the thing she couldn't get past. All those miles, all that blood — Reeve's party scattered somewhere behind them in the high country, one man gone into a crevasse with the mountain's own verdict — and it came down to forty pages of Elias Wren's cramped handwriting, wrapped in oilcloth and stained with her tears.

Judge Corliss was an old man with a face like a dried riverbed and eyes that missed nothing. He took the ledger without hurry, turned to the first page, and read.

The room went very quiet. Outside, a dray team rattled past; somewhere a soldier barked an order. Inside, there was only the scratch of the judge's thumb moving down columns of dates and sums and names — names Marguerite had memorized against her will in a trapper's cabin by lamplight: Hollis. Brandt. *M. Wren.* Her mother, entered in her father's hand like a debt he could never repay, dated eleven days before the "accident" at Miller's Flats that Marguerite had been told was a cave-in.

Corliss looked up. "Where did you get this?"

"From Elias Wren," Theo said. His voice was rough as gravel, but steady. "He carried it twenty years waiting for someone to carry it further."

"And you are?"

"Theo Ashgrove." A pause, the length of a held breath. "Late of this territory."

Something moved behind the judge's eyes — recognition, or its shadow. Every soul in the territory knew the name Ashgrove had carried for two decades: the guide who'd led the syndicate's survey party into the wrong valley, who'd cost them a season and three men, who'd been struck from every register from here to the coast. Marguerite had heard the story told twice on the trail down, once by Theo himself in the whiteout, once by him again in the cave, quieter, the version that was true. The route had never been wrong. He'd drawn it wrong on purpose, because he'd seen what the syndicate did to the valleys it wanted, and he'd chosen exile over complicity. Then he'd spent twenty years paying for the choice while men in good coats spent twenty years profiting from his silence.

Corliss set the ledger down and pressed a small brass bell. When the clerk appeared, he said only: "Warrants. And fetch the territorial register."

* * *

The warrants went out by rider within the hour — six names, six horses, six sets of papers that would reach Denver before the syndicate's telegrams could be unsent. Marguerite watched the first rider gallop out the fort gate and thought of her mother, who had died believing no paper would ever carry her name anywhere but a grave marker.

Then Corliss opened the register.

It was a fat ledger bound in green leather, and it sat on his desk the way Bibles sit on altars. He licked his thumb, turned pages back through years — 1879, 1878, 1877 — until he found what he was looking for. Marguerite leaned close enough to read it upside down.

*Ashgrove, T. — Guide, Class First. Struck from rolls by order of Territorial Commission, 14 March 1861. Cause: gross negligence, loss of life and property.*

The judge took out a pen. He drew a single line through the entry — not erasing it, Marguerite noticed, but striking through it, the way you strike through a debt paid — and beneath it wrote in a hand far steadier than his age suggested:

*Restored by order of Hon. A. Corliss, 3 November 1880. Cause: service rendered to the territory, proof given.*

"There," Corliss said, as if he'd done nothing more remarkable than sign for a delivery of flour. "Not law's grace, Mr. Ashgrove. Law's arithmetic. You brought me the sum; I've only written it down."

Theo stared at the page. Marguerite watched him — watched this man who had crossed a mountain range with a broken arm and never once made a sound about it — and saw his jaw work and his eyes go bright and wet, and understood that this was the moment the last twenty years finally ended. Not on the pass. Not with Reeve falling. Here, over ink and paper, in a warm room that smelled of coffee.

"Thank you," Theo managed.

"Don't thank me." Corliss closed the register. "Thank the dead man who trusted a stranger. And take care of his daughter. She reads marginalia better than any clerk I employ."

Marguerite blinked. "You—"

"I read everything, Miss Wren. It's my trade." The ghost of a smile crossed the riverbed face. "Your father's figures were elegant. Terrible business, murder. Sloppy bookkeeping. But elegant figures."

* * *

They stayed three days at the fort while Theo's arm was properly set and proper affidavits were taken. On the third day, a courier arrived from the south with news that traveled faster than winter: Callum Reeve had been taken at a rail junction outside Silver Bend, sitting in a first-class carriage with two trunks and a ticket under a false name, reading a newspaper as though the world were an ordinary place. Two of his agents had run. Four were in irons. The syndicate's Denver office had been sealed by the marshal, and already the newspapers were calling it the Wren Affair, which made Marguerite's skin crawl and her chest ache in equal measure.

Her father's name in the headlines at last. Twenty years too late, and still — not nothing. Never nothing.

She stood at the fort gate a long time that evening, watching the peaks go rose and then gray, holding the decade-old letter that had started all of it — the letter that had brought her storming into a valley town demanding inheritance and apology, certain she knew exactly what she was owed and exactly whom to blame.

She'd gotten neither, in the end. No vein. No apology from a living man. What she'd gotten instead was a ford that nearly drowned her, a cabin full of grief, a chute that nearly killed them both, and a partner who'd taught her that trust wasn't given like an inheritance — it was built, mile by mile, like a trail.

She folded the letter away and went inside.

* * *

In spring, when the passes opened, they rode north together to the trailhead where it had all begun — the high meadow where a dying man had traded a map for a promise.

Marguerite had asked for this stop, and Theo hadn't questioned it. Now she knelt in the new grass with a small tin box and unpacked it piece by piece: her father's letters, the ones he'd written and never sent, the ones she'd carried across a mountain range without ever quite finding the courage to read straight through. She'd read them all now, on the ride north, by firelight. They were clumsy and proud and full of love he'd never learned to say aloud. *When the work is done, girl. When I've made something worth your name.* He'd thought silver was the something. He'd never understood that the something had been her, all along, waiting in a town he was too ashamed to enter.

She dug a hole at the base of the meadow's lone pine, laid the letters in, and covered them with earth and stones.

"Not a grave," she said quietly. "A trailhead marker. So anyone who comes this way knows somebody finished the route."

Theo stood beside her with his hat in his hand, his arm healed straight, his face turned toward the peaks where the snow still clung blue-white above the tree line. When she rose and brushed off her knees, he said, "I've been thinking about a name for the outfit."

"Oh?"

"Ashgrove and Wren." He glanced sideways at her, and there was something almost shy in it, which on a man who'd faced down Reeve and a cornice and a killer chute was frankly absurd. "Alphabetical. It seemed fair."

"Wren comes first alphabetically," Marguerite said.

"So it does."

"*Wren and Ashgrove,* then." She stuck out her hand, and he shook it, solemn as a treaty. "Partners. Equal shares. And I do the bookkeeping, because we've both seen what happens when men keep sloppy books."

That earned the first real laugh she'd heard out of him since the pass.

They walked back down to where the horses waited, and the trail unspooled ahead of them through the meadow, over the creek, toward the low country and the towns and the clients who would pay good money next season to be led safely through country that had nearly killed them both. There would be hard miles. There would be storms nobody predicted and fords nobody trusted and fools who ignored every warning. There would be winters to provision and routes to scout and a hundred small repairs to a hundred pieces of gear.

Marguerite swung up into the saddle and looked at it all — the whole green length of the year ahead — and found she wasn't counting fortune anymore.

She was counting miles.

And for the first time in her life, she liked the number.
