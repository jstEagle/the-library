## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The monsoon came in from the sea like a debt collector, and Edmund Quintana had learned to work ahead of it.

He rolled off the gunwale of the *Marguerite* at first light, when the water was still the color of old pewter and the swell hadn't yet found its temper. Forty feet down, the wreck waited the way it always waited — patient, silted, pretending to be nothing. The charts called it an unnamed obstruction. The fishermen called it nothing at all, because you didn't name what you'd been told to forget. Only the navy's old salvage manifests, three decades out of print, gave it a hull number, and even those pages had been pulled from the archive before Edmund was old enough to read them.

His father had kept a copy. His father had kept a lot of things he shouldn't have.

Edmund descended hand over hand along his own shot line, equalizing against the pressure that pressed on his ears like a warning. The visibility was poor this morning — two meters, maybe less, the runoff from the hills turning the shallows into weak tea — but he didn't need to see far. He knew the wreck by touch now. The rail came up under his glove at the right depth, then the scoured deck plates, then the gash amidships where the transport had opened like a struck bell and let the sea take what it wanted.

What it wanted, apparently, was everything.

He worked the cargo hold the way he'd worked it for eleven days: slow, methodical, one sweep of the lamp per pass. Silt hung in the beam like smoke that refused to clear. Ribs of corroded steel arched overhead, furred with barnacles, dripping with the small lives of the reef. Somewhere aft, a school of glassfish parted and reformed around his fins without interest. The wreck had made its peace with the ocean. It was only the land above it that hadn't made peace with him.

The patrol boat had come again yesterday. Grey hull, no markings worth reading, running a slow circuit two miles offshore — too far out for fisheries work, too close in for anything honest. It had circled twice, then stood off to the south and sat there through the afternoon like a dog told to wait. Edmund had watched it through his binoculars while pretending to mend nets, and the watching had settled into him and stayed, a cold weight behind the sternum that no amount of sun could lift.

Today he was going down anyway. That was the thing about unease, he'd learned. You could feed it caution until it grew fat and lazy, or you could starve it with answers. He'd spent thirty-eight years feeding it. His father's death had changed the diet.

He found the strongbox on the twelfth pass, wedged beneath a collapsed section of decking where he'd already swept four times. The storm surge of the last week must have shifted it — the same weather that had cracked a seam in the *Marguerite*'s hull and cost him two days of patching. The box sat half-buried in silt, its edges too clean, too square, too deliberate for wreckage. Things that fall from shelves tumble. This had been placed.

Edmund knelt on the deck plate and worked it free with both hands, his breath rasping loud in the regulator. It was heavier than it looked. Steel, not iron, the surface pitted but sound, and around the lid ran a seal — lead, poured and stamped, the kind of closure used for government freight that needed to survive salt water and scrutiny alike.

He turned it toward his lamp.

The stamp was a shield inside a circle, with a serial number beneath in a typeface that had gone out of fashion before he was born. He knew it. Not from books, not from archives. He knew it from a wooden crate in his father's boathouse, the one the old man had never let him open, the one that had burned unopened on the night the boathouse fire took everything else — including, three weeks later, his father himself, alone at the tiller of a skiff that came back empty.

The mark had been on the manifest copy too. He remembered that now, standing on the bottom of the sea with forty feet of monsoon ocean pressing on his shoulders: the same shield, the same circle, inked beside the hull number of this very ship, annotated in his father's cramped hand with a single word Edmund had always taken for a joke or a code or a symptom of a dying man's paranoia.

*Return.*

His air gauge said he had time. His nerve said otherwise. He took the strongbox anyway.

* * *

Surfacing was like being born backwards — pressure easing, color returning, the grey sky assembling itself piece by piece above him. He broke the water fifty yards from the *Marguerite* and saw immediately that something was wrong.

The boat sat wrong in the water. She was listing to port, gently, the way a tired man leans against a doorframe, and there was a new sound underneath the idling of her engine — a low, rhythmic knocking, wood on wood, hull working against a sea she no longer trusted.

"Ahoy," Edmund called, and got no answer, because there was no one aboard to answer. He'd anchored alone, as he did every day, because hiring crew meant questions, and questions meant the wrong ears.

He hauled himself up the ladder with the strongbox slung in his mesh bag, and the moment his boots hit the deck he understood. The anchor line was taut and canted hard to starboard. A wake — a big one, a ship's wake, thrown broadside — had caught the *Marguerite* beam-on sometime during his dive and hammered her against her own anchor. He could picture it exactly: the grey hull making its lazy circuit, closer in today, indifferent to what its propellers did to smaller craft. An accident. Of course an accident. Patrol boats threw wakes; small boats took damage; nobody signed anything.

The crack ran along the port strake below the waterline, six feet of splintered planking weeping seawater through the temporary seal he'd patched last week. The patch had held. Barely. The bilge pump was churning away below decks with the stubborn rhythm of a heart that knew it was losing the argument.

Edmund stood in the cockpit, dripping, and looked at the strongbox in the mesh bag, and made the decision the way you make most decisions that matter — all at once, and then forever.

He should report the find. That was the law, and more than the law, it was the sensible thing. Wrecks in these waters belonged to the state; artifacts went to the survey office upcoast; the survey office sent receipts, and receipts kept men like Edmund Quintana out of trouble. There was a navy liaison who handled such things, a pleasant man with a pleasant office, and Edmund had already drafted the report in his head twice this week, and twice had stopped at the line where he would have to describe the wreck's location.

Because the patrol boat would come. Because the pleasant man would make a call. Because somewhere between the wreck and the archive, the strongbox would become a line item, and the line item would become a rumor, and the rumor would become a hole in the ground and a story about a diver who drowned doing something foolish in bad weather. His father had drafted reports too. His father had been careful his whole life, right up until the night the water took him, and the only thing carefulness had bought him was a closed coffin and a son who didn't believe a word of the official account.

Edmund carried the strongbox below, set it on the chart table, and cut the lead seal with a knife.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were documents. Shipping manifests, decades old, typed and countersigned. A ledger in a clerk's precise hand. And beneath them, flat and heavy in a way paper shouldn't be, a single gold bar the size of a soap dish, stamped with the same shield, the same circle — and a second mark, smaller, punched deep: a number that matched nothing on any manifest.

It matched, instead, a string of coastal villages whose names Edmund had grown up hearing spoken quietly, if at all. Villages that had emptied during the war years. Villages whose fishing grounds had been requisitioned, whose boats had been confiscated, whose people had been moved inland with promises of compensation that arrived, according to his father's letters, in the form of nothing at all.

The gold wasn't cargo. It was evidence. And someone, thirty years ago, had decided it should go back — and had hidden it where only the sea could keep the secret, and left a compass and a dead man's letters to finish the job.

Outside, the wind was backing around, dragging the first smell of rain across the water. The bilge pump knocked on. Edmund wrapped the documents, stowed the bar, and climbed topside to run the *Marguerite* for harbor before the sea finished what the patrol boat started.

He did not file a report. He did not radio the survey office. He steered his wounded boat toward home with the storm building behind him and the certainty building faster, and it was only when the harbor lights came up through the rain that he saw the figure waiting on the quay — a woman with their father's jaw and a lawyer's envelope in her hand, standing in the weather like she'd been born to it.

Marguerite. Eleven years since she'd spoken to him, and here she was, at the exact hour he had something worth taking half of.
