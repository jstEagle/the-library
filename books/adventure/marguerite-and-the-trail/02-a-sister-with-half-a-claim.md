## 2. A Sister with Half a Claim

The bus from the airport let Marguerite off at the top of the harbor road, and the first thing she smelled was the sea — brine and diesel and something older underneath, something like rot and rain that had been falling on the same coast for a thousand years. The second thing she smelled was the fish market, and the third was herself: airplane air, hotel soap, the faint chemical sweetness of a lawyer's waiting room she'd sat in for two hours yesterday while a man named Okonkwo explained, in the gentlest possible terms, that she was entitled to half of everything.

Half of a boat. Half of a debt. Half of a man who had never once called her on her birthday.

The monsoon was three days from landfall — you could feel it in the way the air sat on your skin like a wet cloth — and the harbor was working anyway, because harbors always worked. Longboats nosed through the chop. Men in yellow slickers hauled crates up the ramp. And at the end of the pier, riding low and ugly and unmistakably alive, sat the *Marguerite*.

Her father had named a boat after her and then given it to her brother. She had decided, somewhere over the Pacific, that this was either an apology or an insult, and she would know which by the time she left.

Edmund was on deck, coiling rope with the economy of a man who'd done nothing else for twenty years. He was thinner than she remembered. Grayer at the temples. When he saw her he went very still, the way he used to go still when their father's footsteps came down the hall.

"You're not supposed to be here," he said.

"Hello to you too." She climbed aboard without asking, because asking was for people who needed permission. "The boat's listing to port."

"The boat's always listing to port. It's called character." He set the coil down. Up close she could see the bruised look under his eyes, the salt-cracked hands. He'd been diving. He'd been diving a lot. "Who told you where to find me?"

"Nobody had to. You've been at this harbor for fifteen years, Edmund. You're the only Quintana in the registry." She held up the envelope. "Okonkwo, Voss & Associates. I had them draft it properly. You'll want to read it before you argue, because arguing before reading is a family trait I'm trying to break."

He didn't take it. "Half," he said. "You came all this way for half."

"I came all this way for what's mine. There's a difference, though I understand it's a subtle one." She set the envelope on the hatch cover between them, a border crossing in paper form. "And before you say the estate is nothing but a rusted hull and a stack of unpaid berthing fees — I know. I've seen the accounts. I'm not here for the boat."

Edmund's hand went, unconsciously, to his collar. To the cord around his neck.

Marguerite had been counting on that.

"The compass," she said.

His jaw tightened. "The compass isn't part of the estate."

"The compass was our father's. It's in the photographs. It's in the letters." She pulled the bundle from her bag — oilskin-wrapped, tied with the same kind of cord Edmund wore, and watched him register that. "Forty-one letters, Edmund. He wrote to me for nine years and never mailed a single one. Okonkwo found them in a storage unit in Colombo. Did you know about them?"

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man finding a room in a house he thought he knew completely.

"No," he said. Too fast.

"He mentions the compass in eleven of them. He mentions *you* in thirty-eight." She let that land. "He mentions the coast in every single one."

The harbor noise filled the silence between them — the slap of water against pilings, a winch somewhere, the gulls conducting their usual grievances. Edmund picked up the envelope finally, turned it over, didn't open it.

"You don't know what you're asking for," he said.

"I know exactly what I'm asking for. That's the whole point. I read the letters, Edmund. All of them. Twice." She stepped closer, and the boat rocked under her, and she let the motion be part of the argument. "He was here. Thirty years ago, on this coast, with that compass, doing something he never told either of us about. And then he came home and drank himself to death in a house with two children in it and never said one word about why. So yes. I want my half. And I want the compass. And I want to know what he was doing."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him decide — she could see the decision happening, the same stubborn machinery their father had built into both of them grinding through its gears — and then he said, "No," and turned back to his rope.

"You don't get to say no. It's a court order."

"It's a letter. Courts are months away, and by then—" He stopped himself.

"By then what?"

"Nothing." He coiled rope that was already coiled. "Go home, Marguerite. There's a storm coming, and whatever you think is buried in those letters, it's worse than you think, and it's mine to deal with. It was always mine to deal with. That's what half means to me — I took half so you wouldn't have to."

She laughed, and it came out sharp and ugly. "That's the story you tell yourself? That you took the boat and the compass and the silence *for* me?"

"I took them because someone had to."

"Someone had to *write* too. Someone had to call. Someone had to show up when—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, hated that after all these years and all this distance he could still reach the crack in her with one hand on a rope. She steadied. "I'm not leaving. I've booked a room at the Anjali Guest House for two weeks. I have a lawyer on retainer and a return ticket I fully intend to waste. So you can either read the letter, or you can read it in front of a judge."

Edmund set the rope down. He looked out at the water, at the gray-green line of the horizon where the monsoon was stacking itself up like a debt coming due, and when he spoke again his voice was different. Quieter. Almost careful.

"Three days ago," he said, "I pulled a strongbox out of a wreck that isn't on any chart. It had a government stamp on it. I've seen that stamp once before — on Dad's effects, in the box you're not supposed to open until the estate settles." He turned to face her. "Yesterday a patrol boat came close enough to crack my hull waking me. Nobody patrols that stretch of water. Nobody's patrolled it in thirty years. And now, suddenly, everyone does."

Marguerite felt the harbor tilt, or maybe just herself. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I was going to ignore it. Dive somewhere else, let it lie, let *him* keep lying. And then you show up with forty-one letters and a lawyer, asking about the compass." He finally opened the envelope, but he didn't read it; he held it like a man holding a depth gauge he didn't trust. "The compass doesn't point north, Marguerite. It hasn't since he died. I keep it because it's the only thing of his that ever told me the truth — which is that it's lying."

Before she could answer, footsteps hammered up the pier.

It was the boy from the chandlery — Tomas, she'd have learned his name later — soaked to the knees, waving a radio handset like it was on fire. "Edmund! Edmund, they're saying on the navy band—" He caught his breath, gulped air. "The survey team. The government team, upcoast at the old station. Six of them. Nobody's seen them since last night. The station's empty. Coffee still warm, radio dead, boats all accounted for. They just—" He made a gesture, helpless, opening his fingers like a man releasing a bird. "Gone."

The word went through Marguerite like cold water through a wetsuit.

Edmund had gone white. Not the white of fear, exactly. The white of a man whose map has just been redrawn. "Six men," he said. "Armed escort, satellite phone, the whole—"

"Gone," the boy said again, and then, lower: "My uncle says the patrol boat's been asking about divers. Asking who's been working the deep water."

The harbor went on around them — the winches, the gulls, the fish, the ordinary machinery of a coast that had swallowed things for centuries and would swallow more. Marguerite looked at her brother, at the cord around his neck, at the envelope in his hand, and felt the old resentment shift inside her like ballast, like something heavy rolling to a new side of the hull.

"Edmund," she said quietly. "Read the letters. Because I don't think either of us came here for half of a boat anymore."

He looked upcoast, where the rain was already coming down like a curtain being drawn, and nodded once.
