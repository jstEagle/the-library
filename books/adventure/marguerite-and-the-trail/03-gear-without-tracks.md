## 3. Gear Without Tracks

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and the whole coast smelled rinsed and raw, like something scoured. Edmund beached the dinghy in the lee of a black rock shelf while Marguerite held the painter without being asked, her sleeves rolled, her hands already salted white. Neither of them spoke about the night before. The argument about the compass was still sitting between them, unspent, and the news of the vanished survey team had pressed it flat under something heavier.

The navy camp sat on a shelf of ground above the tideline, half a mile upcoast from where the *Marguerite* swung at her mooring. From the water it looked like nothing — a scatter of olive canvas, a radio mast leaning at a tired angle. From the ridge above it, it looked wrong.

"Nobody's moving," Marguerite said.

"There'd be smoke," Edmund said. "Six men, monsoon season, there'd be smoke by now no matter what."

They came down through the wet grass with the caution of habit. Edmund went first because it was his coast, his wreck, his trouble, whatever Marguerite's lawyer said about shared inheritance. The grass soaked them to the waist. The camp resolved out of the gray morning one detail at a time, and each detail was worse than the last.

The mess table under its tarp still held breakfast. Tin mugs, three of them, tea gone cold and skinned over. A loaf under a cloth, half cut, the knife standing upright in the crumb. Rice in a pot with the lid cocked, dry as chalk — whoever cooked it had never come back to eat it, and the rain that should have flooded every open vessel had somehow missed this shelf of ground entirely, or been kept off it, or simply declined to fall here, which was a thought Edmund put away before it could grow teeth.

"Edmund." Marguerite's voice had changed. She was standing at the edge of the camp, very still, looking inland.

He followed her gaze and felt the skin of his arms tighten.

The gear was laid out in a line.

Not scattered, not stacked, not packed for transport. Laid out. The theodolite on its tripod at the near end, leveled, its telescope trained precisely inland along a bearing. Behind it, spaced with a regularity that made his stomach drop, the rest: the rangefinder case, the survey stakes bundled and squared, the waterproof log boxes in a row, their latches open and empty, then the entrenching tools crossed at exact right angles, then a coil of signal wire paid out straight as a plumb line, running up the slope into the treeline and out of sight. Every instrument facing the same direction. Every piece of equipment oriented along a single axis, as if the whole camp had been combed flat and pointed, like a compass needle the size of a clearing.

"No footprints," Marguerite said. "It rained all night. Where are the footprints?"

She was right. The ground around the line of gear was unbroken mud, smooth as poured wax. No boot prints leaving the camp toward the trees. No prints returning to it. No drag marks, no struggle, no blood. Six men had eaten breakfast here and then the rain had fallen on ground that remembered none of them.

"They didn't leave," Edmund said quietly. "Or they left in a way that doesn't print."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." He crouched at the edge of the cleared mud and didn't touch it. "Look at the spacing. Look at it."

Marguerite looked. He watched her get there — she was quick, always had been, quicker than him at seeing the shape of a thing once someone named its edges. Her frown deepened.

"It's deliberate. It's — arranged."

"It's a line-and-bearing drill." His voice came out flat, professional, because if he let anything else into it he'd have to admit what was crawling up his spine. "Divers use it. Salvage crews. You lay your guide line from your anchor point along a fixed bearing so anyone coming after you can find the working site blind — in silt, in dark, in current. You space your markers at set intervals. You orient everything along the bearing so even a man who's never seen the site can read it." He stood. "Somebody turned this camp into a guide line."

"For who?"

"For whoever was supposed to follow it." He looked down the axis of the arrangement, past the theodolite's glass eye, up the slope, into the green dark under the trees. "And they wanted it found by exactly one person. Because anybody else would've called this a crime scene and waited for the navy. Only a diver looks at this and reads it as instructions."

Marguerite was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its lawyerly edge. "You think they knew you were diving the wreck."

"I think somebody's been watching my boat for days. I think the patrol vessel that nearly rolled us wasn't on a routine pass. And I think six men ate breakfast two nights ago, walked into those trees or were carried into them, and left me a map." He pulled the compass on its cord out from under his shirt, almost without deciding to. The brass was warm from his skin. The needle lay steady, pointing — as it had pointed since he'd first handled it as a boy — at nothing in particular, a few degrees off north, patient as a cat.

As he faced up the slope, the needle swung.

Not much. Five degrees, maybe six. But it moved, deliberately, decisively, settling onto a new heading that ran dead parallel to the line of survey gear, straight into the trees.

Marguerite saw it too. He heard her breath catch.

"Your father's compass," she said. "That doesn't point north."

"It points at things," Edmund said. "I used to think it was just broken."

* * *

They followed the wire.

There was no discussion about it, which Edmund noted distantly as its own kind of alarm. Two days ago he'd have refused to take his estranged sister inland on a hunch and a broken compass. But the camp had rearranged the argument. Whatever claim Marguerite had on the estate, she was standing next to him in the mud with her jaw set, and the alternative to her company was doing this alone, and alone suddenly seemed like exactly what whoever built this trail wanted.

The wire ran through the treeline and along a game path grown soft with leaf mold. Here the rain had done its ordinary work — the path was churned, puddled, printed with the tracks of civets and a monitor lizard big enough to leave a groove like a tire. But no boots. No human prints at all, going in or coming out, though the wire itself had been strung through the brush at knee height with staples hammered into living wood, fresh-cut pale wounds in the bark.

"Somebody carried six men down this path without touching the ground," Marguerite said.

"Or the men walked it after the rain stopped, and the rain started again behind them." He didn't believe it. He said it anyway, because the alternative was a hole in the world he wasn't ready to look into.

The wire led them uphill for twenty minutes, switchbacking along the contour, always bearing the same direction — the direction the compass needle had chosen. Twice they passed survey stakes driven into the earth, each one painted with a number stenciled in government white. The numbers descended. Whatever the sequence counted down toward, it was ahead of them.

At the third stake, Marguerite stopped.

"This is a cataloguing operation," she said. "Look at the stakes. Numbered stations. This isn't a boundary survey or a road alignment. They were marking a route to something and recording what they found at each station." She glanced back down the slope, toward the invisible sea. "Toward the wreck?"

"Toward what the wreck was carrying." Edmund touched the stake. The paint was barely a week old. "My strongbox had a government mark. Their log boxes are empty. Somebody upstairs wanted an inventory before anyone else knew the cargo existed."

"And then somebody upstairs decided the inventory team knew too much."

The thought landed between them and neither picked it up. They walked on.

The trees thickened. The light went green and dim, and the air took on the mineral smell of standing water — they were approaching the swamp country that ran inland from the coast in a great braided tongue of mangrove and black channels. The wire ended at the last stake, number one, driven into the root buttress of a strangler fig. Beyond it, the ground dropped away into standing water and the mangroves began, a drowned forest running to the horizon.

Edmund checked the compass. The needle hadn't wavered. It pointed into the swamp, steady as a fact.

And there — half-submerged in the black water thirty meters out, where the mangrove roots made a screen — something pale. Cloth. A scrap of shirt snagged on a root fork, fluttering faintly in the breath of wind that moved through the drowned forest.

Navy khaki.

Beside it, caught in the mud below the waterline, a boot sole pressed into the silt. One print. The only human footprint either of them had seen since the camp. Pointing inland. Deliberate as a signature.

"Devi Raghunathan," Edmund read aloud from the tag stitched inside the collar when he'd waded out and lifted the scrap free. "That's the name on the manifest. The junior surveyor." He wrung the water from the cloth, heart hammering. "One man got far enough to leave a mark. One man wanted to be followed."

Marguerite had gone very still on the bank. "Edmund. If he left the shirt as a sign, he's close. And if the others didn't leave signs—"

"Then we're the only ones looking for him," Edmund said, "and the people who erased the rest will come back to finish the job. We find him first."

The compass needle trembled once against its glass, and held its bearing into the dark of the mangroves, waiting for them to move.
