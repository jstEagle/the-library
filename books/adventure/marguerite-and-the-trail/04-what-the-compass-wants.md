## 4. What the Compass Wants

The trail was a lie told in straight lines.

Marguerite had understood that within the first hour of walking it. The instruments at the abandoned camp — the theodolite, the ranging poles, the brass-cased surveyor's level — had been laid out with a precision that no fleeing man produces and no accident explains. Each item sat exactly one pace from the last, each bearing identical, all of it aimed inland like a finger pointing through the rain. Edmund had looked at the arrangement for a long time without touching anything, and then he'd said, in the flat voice he used when he didn't want her to hear what he was actually feeling: "This is a line-and-bearing drill. Divers use it. You lay a search line when you're looking for something underwater."

"And someone laid one on land," she'd said.

"Someone who wanted it found," he'd said. "By a diver."

Now they were following that line into the hills, and the monsoon had decided to make them earn every step.

The rain came in sheets that moved like living things across the valley, sweeping down the slopes and across the paddy terraces in gray curtains you could watch approaching. The path — if it deserved the name — was a farmer's track gone to soup, and twice Marguerite went down hard enough to taste mud. Edmund walked ahead because he always walked ahead, because thirty years of diving alone had welded the habit into his spine, and she let him, because watching him work out the trail's logic was like watching him read water.

Except.

She'd noticed it an hour ago and told herself it was nothing. Told herself again at forty minutes. By the time the rain eased to a steady drumming and the track climbed onto a ridge above a drowned green valley, she couldn't tell herself anything anymore, because the compass on its cord around Edmund's neck was doing something impossible, and he either knew or he didn't, and both possibilities were unbearable.

It was their father's compass. That was the first wound. She'd known it the moment she saw it hanging against his chest in the harbor light — the brass case worn smooth at the hinge, the glass scratched in a crescent where a wedding ring had clicked against it for years, the little stamped serial number on the back that she had memorized as a child by tracing it with a fingernail while he pretended not to notice. He'd worn it around his neck the way Edmund wore it now. He'd sworn to Marguerite, once, drunk on cheap brandy and sentiment, that it never left his body, and that someday it would be hers.

It had gone to Edmund instead. Everything had gone to Edmund. The boat named after her, even — that was the joke the estate had played, the boat called *Marguerite* moored in a harbor her brother worked alone, while the daughter got a lawyer's letter and a parcel of debts. So yes, she had come here angry. She had crossed an ocean angry. Anger was the luggage she traveled with.

But this was different.

"Edmund." She said it once, quietly. He didn't turn. "Edmund. Stop walking."

He stopped. Rain ran off the hood of his jacket and dripped from his chin. Behind them the valley smoked with mist; ahead the trail bent along the ridge toward a stand of ironwood trees black with wet.

"The compass," she said. "Look at it."

He looked down at it the way a man looks at a watch — automatically, without interest. And she watched his face do the thing she'd been dreading for an hour. Not confusion. Recognition.

The needle wasn't pointing north. It was pointing at him.

Not exactly — she was no instrument reader — but close enough that a child could see the wrongness. It swung as he shifted his weight, settling each time toward some bearing that had nothing to do with the pole, some private direction only it knew. When Edmund stepped left, the needle leaned after him like a plant toward light.

"How long," she said.

"Since the camp."

"Since the camp. Or since before the camp?"

His jaw worked. Rain filled the silence between them, drumming on leaves, filling the gullies, erasing everything except the two of them and the small brass lie swinging between their bodies.

"Since I pulled the strongbox up," he said finally. "Maybe since before. I thought it was the storm. Iron in the hull, the wreck itself, magnetic anomalies — wrecks are full of them, whole ships are anomalies, you get used to your instruments lying—"

"You don't get used to a compass lying." Her voice came out harder than she intended, and she let it. "You're a professional. You've been reading water for thirty years. You knew."

"I suspected."

"Suspected." She laughed, and there was nothing kind in it. "Do you know what I did to get here? I sold my apartment. I signed papers I didn't read. I came to take half of what was left of him, and the half I wanted — the only thing I wanted — is hanging around your neck, and it turns out it isn't a keepsake at all. It's a key. And you've been carrying it for weeks knowing it was a key."

"It was his," Edmund said. "That's all I knew. That it was his, and that he wore it into the water more times than I can count, and that when I dive with it I feel—" He stopped himself. She watched him stop himself, and that was worse than anything, that visible act of a man deciding how much truth he could afford.

"Feel what?"

"Like someone's checking my work."

* * *

They took shelter under the ironwoods, backs against trunks wide as doors, and ate cold rice and tinned fish while the rain kept up its interrogation of the forest. Marguerite turned the compass over in her hands — Edmund had surrendered it without a fight, which frightened her more than any argument would have — and read the serial number by touch, the way she had at seven years old, sitting on a dock with her feet in the harbor while their father mended nets and talked about the sea like it was a country he held citizenship in.

"He used it here," she said. "On this coast. Didn't he."

It wasn't a question, but Edmund answered it anyway, slowly, the way a man pays out anchor line. "There were letters. In the effects. I didn't show you because—" 

"Because showing me would mean sharing."

"Because showing me would mean admitting I didn't understand them." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it land harder. "There were charts. Old ones, war-era, with grid squares inked out. There was a letter from a salvage company that folded the year I was born. And there was a page in his handwriting — just fragments, notes to himself — about an operation. Thirty years ago. Before us. He was part of a crew that worked this coast. Recovering cargo from transports sunk in the war."

"What cargo?"

"That's the thing." Edmund stared out at the rain. "The letters never say. Pages cut out. Inked out. But there's one line that survived, in the margin, like he wrote it fast and couldn't bring himself to cross it out." He recited it the way you recite something you've read five hundred times trying to make it mean less than it does: "*We did not find it. We moved it.*"

Marguerite felt the words go into her like cold water into a sleeve. *We did not find it. We moved it.* A man writing that about a salvage operation means a man who knows where something is. A man who hides the pages means a man who knows what it costs to know.

"And the compass," she said. "It doesn't point north. It points—"

"Wherever he calibrated it to point." Edmund took it back from her gently, holding it flat on his palm, and the needle swung and settled, patient as a dog. "Dad wasn't sentimental about tools. If he modified it — weighted the card, hid a magnet in the case, whatever he did — he did it for a reason. This compass doesn't want north. It wants *something*. And ever since I brought up that strongbox, it wants it badly."

The strongbox. Stamped with a government mark she'd seen once before, Edmund had said, on their dead father's effects. She'd filed that away in the harbor, in the shock of arrival, and now it came back to her with interest. Their father. Government mark. Wreck nobody names. Survey team vanished overnight, leaving a trail only a diver could read. Contractors, Edmund had heard rumors, men paid to make evidence disappear.

Pieces. They were all pieces, and the shape they made when she pushed them together was a shape she didn't like.

"Thirty years ago he helped move something," she said. "Something taken from this coast. And then he spent the rest of his life diving wrecks alone, wearing a compass that pointed at his secret, and he gave it to you and not me because—"

"Because I dive," Edmund said. "Or because I was the son. Or because he trusted me with the guilt and didn't want you touched by it." His voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, just enough. "I don't know. I've had three weeks to hate him for it and I still don't know."

For the first time since the harbor, Marguerite looked at her brother and saw something other than the man who'd inherited everything. She saw a man standing in the rain holding a debt he hadn't chosen, trying to pay it alone, badly, the way their father had taught them both to do things — alone, badly, in bad weather.

The anger didn't leave her. She was too honest with herself to pretend it did. But it changed state, the way water changes state — same substance, different form. Betrayal cooling into something sharper and more useful. Curiosity. The need to know.

"Show me the letters tonight," she said. "All of them. Every fragment, including the parts you don't understand. Especially those."

Edmund nodded.

"And Edmund — if the needle moves again, you tell me before you decide what it means. We do this together or we don't do it at all. Those are my terms. Half of everything, remember? Including the truth."

"Half of everything," he agreed.

* * *

They made the ridge top at last light, and that was where the compass stopped pretending.

Marguerite saw it happen — saw the needle swing off its long-held bearing in one smooth arc, like a hand releasing a rope, and settle on a new heading, dead steady, quivering faintly with what she could only describe as eagerness. Below them, half-hidden in the monsoon gloom, lay a fold of coastal swamp: mangroves standing knee-deep in brown water, channels threading between them like veins.

And on the near edge of the swamp, half-submerged, half-concealed, something pale. A man's arm, motionless. Or a man waving — too weak to lift it high.

Edmund was moving before she finished saying his name.
