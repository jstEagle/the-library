## 5. The Man in the Mangroves

The trail ended where the land gave up and the water began.

Edmund stood at the edge of the mangrove swamp with the compass hanging against his sternum, its needle still trembling off true north like a dog straining at a leash. Behind them, the line of survey instruments stretched back across two kilometers of sodden ground — theodolite, range poles, the steel tape wound to its last meter — each piece placed with a precision that had stopped feeling like abandonment hours ago and started feeling like handwriting. Someone had written this message for one reader only. Edmund was increasingly afraid he knew which reader.

"Tell me again why we're walking into a swamp," Marguerite said. She'd rolled her trousers to the knee and tied her hair back with what looked like a strip torn from her lawyer's letter, and there was mud already to her ankles. She hadn't complained once since the camp. That frightened him more than anything else. His sister complained the way other people breathed.

"Because whoever laid that line wanted it followed," Edmund said. "And because the compass wants this way."

"The compass doesn't want anything. It's brass and bad bearings."

"It pointed my father down this coast thirty years ago." He said it flatly, watching her face. "It's pointing at something now."

She didn't answer. She'd gone quiet in a different way since the camp — not sulking, calculating. Whatever she'd come ashore wanting from him, half a boat, half an estate, half a grievance, the line of instruments had rearranged her priorities. Edmund understood. It had rearranged his too.

They went into the mangroves.

* * *

The swamp swallowed sound first. Their footsteps died in the peat, and the prop roots rose around them like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, black and dripping, hung with orchids that smelled of rot and honey. The tide was coming in. Edmund could feel it in the water rising past his shins, warm as blood, carrying the smell of the sea behind it. Somewhere out in the gray murk, thunder walked around on the horizon without ever quite arriving.

He led by the compass and by instinct, and after twenty minutes he admitted they were no longer the same thing. The needle had steadied on a bearing that took them along a channel barely wide enough for his shoulders, and the deeper they went, the more certain he became that the channel wasn't natural. The banks were too regular. Old pilings surfaced from the muck like knuckles — cut timber, tarred, decades soft. This had been a route once. A supply path, maybe, or an escape one.

"Edmund." Marguerite's voice came low and flat from behind him. "There's blood on that root."

There was. A smear, browned almost black, at shoulder height, as if someone had dragged themselves past using the roots for handholds. And below it, half-sunk in the peat, a boot print — a good boot, government-issue tread, filled with rainwater that hadn't yet been disturbed by crabs.

Edmund crouched. The print pointed deeper into the swamp. Away from the sea. Away from rescue.

"He's hurt," Marguerite said. Not a question. She'd seen the same things he had; whatever else divided them, they'd been raised by the same man, and their father had taught them both to read ground the way other people read newspapers. "Hurt, alone, moving inland. He knows someone might follow the line."

"Or he's bait," Edmund said. "Laid out the way those instruments were laid out."

"For whom?"

"For whoever the line was written for." He touched the compass through his shirt. "Us."

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment, water streaming off her chin, and then she did something Edmund hadn't seen her do since she was fourteen years old. She believed him. He saw it happen — saw the skepticism go out of her face like a tide withdrawing, leaving something rawer underneath.

"Then let's not keep him waiting," she said.

* * *

They found him forty minutes later, or rather Marguerite found him — a flicker of movement in the root tangle that Edmund would have walked straight past. A man wedged into the fork of a mangrove two meters above the waterline, legs dangling, one arm clamped around his own ribs. He'd wrapped himself in a survey tarp gone green with mold. He was maybe fifty, thin as a fishbone, with a graying beard and eyes that had been awake too long.

He didn't run. Edmund noted that first. Most men, surprised, would have bolted, wounded or not. This one just watched them come, and when Edmund stopped three meters away and raised empty hands, the man laughed — a dry, cracked sound with no humor in it.

"You followed the line," he said. His English carried the coast in it, vowels rounded by the fishing villages upshore. "I told them you would. I told them nobody else would read it right."

"Devi Raghunathan," Edmund said.

Something moved behind the man's eyes. "You know me."

"I know your name off the survey manifest. I pulled it from the harbor office before we came inland." Edmund kept his voice level. "Six men on that team. Five missing. You're either the luckiest man on this coast or the one who killed them."

"If I'd killed them," Devi said, "I'd be the one reporting it, wouldn't I. Instead of sitting in a tree bleeding." He shifted, and the tarp fell open, and Edmund saw the wound — a gash along the man's side, long and deep, crudely bound with strips of shirt that had soaked through twice. Not a knife wound. Something with edges. Boat propeller, maybe, or a machete swung in panic rather than purpose.

Marguerite waded forward. "We have a kit on the boat. Antiseptic, sutures—"

"No boat." Devi shook his head. "You can't go back to the harbor. That's the first thing you have to understand. There is no going back to the harbor." He said it without drama, the way a man reports weather, and it landed harder than any shout would have. "How did you get here? Whose boat?"

"The Marguerite," Edmund said. "Anchored in the lee off Kandapola Point."

Devi closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and Edmund couldn't tell if it was the pain or something else. "Then you have until high water tomorrow. Maybe less. They'll sweep the point when the storm breaks. They always work before weather — everyone's eyes are on the sky, nobody's on the water."

"They," Marguerite said. "Say who they are."

* * *

They got him down from the tree slowly, Edmund taking his weight while Marguerite cleared a dry hummock above the tide line and built a windbreak from palm fronds. Devi talked the whole time, and Edmund came to understand that talking was how the man kept himself upright — that if he stopped, the guilt underneath would rise like the tide and take him under.

"We weren't supposed to find it," he said, lying back against the fronds while Marguerite cut away the ruined bandage. He flinched but kept talking. "The contract said bathymetric survey. Sediment mapping for a coastal development consortium. Standard work. We ran the grids for eleven days." He nodded inland, toward the hills hidden behind the rain haze. "Then Vasanth's magnetometer started screaming. Anomaly readings off every scale we had. Not a wreck — the signature was wrong for a wreck. Too concentrated. Too deep. Inland."

"A cache," Edmund said.

"A cache." Devi laughed again, that terrible dry sound. "Gold. Crated gold, Edmund Quintana — I'll say the word plainly because I've spent six days saying nothing else to the crabs. War-era gold, packed in oilcloth and zinc, stacked in a flooded chamber under the limestone. And stamped on the crates—" his breath caught as Marguerite cleaned the wound, and he rode through it, jaw working, "—stamped with a requisition mark. Coastal levy. Nineteen forty-three. You know what that mark means?"

Edmund knew. Everyone on this coast knew, though nobody said it aloud. During the occupation years, the colonial administration had 'levied' the fishing villages — rice, boats, jewelry, temple offerings, anything portable and precious — in the name of war security. The villages never saw any of it again. Whole communities had been stripped bare and left to starve through the blockade winters. His own grandmother had traded a wedding bangle for cooking oil and told the story until the day she died, and even then she'd lowered her voice, as if the men who took it might still be listening.

"It means it was stolen from these villages," Edmund said quietly. "And never returned."

"Seven hundred kilos, by our estimate. Maybe more." Devi stared up through the fronds at the bruised sky. "Vasanth wanted to report it. Proper channels, ministry, heritage commission. But our contract wasn't with the ministry. It was with a shell company that doesn't exist, and when we transmitted the coordinates, the reply came back within the hour. Not instructions. A question. One question: *Who else has seen the data?*"

Marguerite's hands had gone still on the wound. "And you answered honestly."

"I answered honestly." Devi's voice cracked, and he hated himself for it; Edmund could see the hatred. "Two days later, Menon went into the surf at night and didn't come back. Then Prakash and little Tomas went upcoast for fuel and the fuel boat came back empty. Vasanth and I ran. We split up in the rain. They found him yesterday — I heard the shot from the ridge." He turned his head, and his eyes fixed on Edmund with a ferocity that had nothing left to lose in it. "Five men. I signed the same contracts they signed. I ate at the same table. And I hid in a tree while they erased my team one by one, because I thought if I stayed quiet, stayed small, they'd forget me."

"And instead you laid out the instruments," Edmund said.

"Instead I laid out the instruments." For the first time, something kindled in the man's face — faint, exhausted, but real. A coal refusing the rain. "A line-and-bearing drill. Every salvage diver learns it. You lay a course for a man who can read it, and only a man who can read it. I've watched this harbor thirty years, Quintana. I know whose son dives the forbidden wreck. I know about your father." He paused. "I know about the compass."

The swamp seemed to lean closer. Water lapped at the hummock. Somewhere far off, thunder finally committed to its arrival, rolling in long and slow.

"My father," Edmund said carefully, "died eight years ago."

"Your father," Devi said, "was on the original recovery team in nineteen sixty-eight, when the navy tried to raise that levy gold and lost two divers doing it. Your father was the one who got out. And your father was the one who told the inquiry it was all lost in the collapse." He held Edmund's gaze. "It wasn't lost, was it? Because the anomaly we found matches nothing on any chart — except the bearing your father logged in sixty-eight and then struck from the record himself."

Edmund's hand had gone to the compass without his deciding to. Through the wet shirt, the brass felt warm, absurdly warm, like something alive. His father — careful, silent, Sunday-quiet Edmund Senior, who'd taught him knots and currents and never once spoken of the war years or the wreck or why he drank coffee black at dawn staring at the sea — had carried a secret down thirty years and buried it in a boy's inheritance.

"That's why they haven't found you yet," Marguerite said suddenly. She was looking at Devi with the expression she wore when a contract clause finally made sense. "You're not hiding from them. You're hiding *for* us. You needed to reach the one person alive who could finish the reading."

"I needed to reach the person whose father started it," Devi corrected softly. "There's a difference. The first is luck. The second is duty." He pushed himself up on one elbow, wound and all, and the movement cost him visibly, and he paid it anyway. "Quintana. Listen to me. Those men coming up the coast — they aren't thieves. Thieves want the gold. These men want the gold *gone*, unrecorded, unwitnessed, so the families it was taken from stay robbed forever and the men who signed the orders stay clean. If they erase it, it stays erased. Nobody comes looking again for another fifty years, and everyone who remembers will be dead."

"So we get the evidence out," Edmund said. "Take you to a journalist, a magistrate—"

"With what?" Devi gestured at the swamp, the rain, the whole indifferent coast. "My testimony? A dead man's word against a company that legally does not exist? No." He shook his head. "Evidence is the crates themselves. The stamps. The provenance. You don't move seven hundred kilos of gold, Quintana. You document it. Photographs, video, the chamber itself, the bearing, everything — and you put it somewhere they can't reach before they reach you."

The needle pressed cold against Edmund's chest now, or he imagined it did, pulling its old pull toward the hills. His father's bearing. His father's lie, told to protect something — or to hide something — or, Edmund was beginning to suspect, to *keep* something, the way you keep a promise you can't yet pay.

"High water tomorrow," Edmund said. "That's our window."

"That's your window," Devi agreed. He lay back, spent, the coal in his face guttering but not out. "Rest now. Both of you. Because tomorrow you dive, and tomorrow they hunt, and only one of those things goes according to plan."

Marguerite caught Edmund's eye across the dying light. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say that the compass wasn't already saying, dragging its needle inland, patient as an inheritance, toward whatever their father had left them besides the boat and the grief.

Far up the channel, where the mangroves thickened into blackness, Edmund heard it — faint, rhythmic, wrong for the swamp.

An engine. More than one. Coming in with the tide.
