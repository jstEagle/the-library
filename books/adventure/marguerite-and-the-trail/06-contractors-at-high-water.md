## 6. Contractors at High Water

The rain came down the river valley like a second river, and we were running ahead of it.

Edmund had the Marguerite's patched hull groaning at six knots through the brown flood, Devi wrapped in a tarp in the wheelhouse, his wound dressed with the last of our gauze and half a bottle of arrack poured over it while he hissed through his teeth. I stood at the wheel because Edmund's hands were better used on the engine, and because I wanted something to hold that wasn't my own fear.

The radio had been static for two days. Then, an hour before dawn, it wasn't.

"—survey vessel *Kestrel* requesting all craft clear the lower channel, repeat, clear the lower channel—"

Edmund came up the ladder two rungs at a time. "That's not a survey vessel."

"You know it?"

"I know the name. Kestrel Group. They do security for mining concerns." He said it flatly, the way you say a diagnosis. "They don't survey. They erase."

The river bent north around a headland of black rock, and beyond it, far off, I saw them: three white hulls riding at anchor in the coastal roadstead, lights burning through the rain like a string of cold pearls. One of them was launching a boat. Even at this distance, even through the monsoon murk, I could see the wake it threw — hard, straight, purposeful. Not searching. Coming.

"They're already on the water," I said.

"Then we go the other way. Downriver, through the delta, out past the fishing grounds. There's a journalist anchored off Serampur Point — Devi gave me the frequency. We make contact, we put him on the record, and this stops being something they can bury."

Devi lifted his head from under the tarp. His face was grey, but his voice had iron in it. "They will have the delta mouth watched. It is the only deep channel. I told you — I helped write their operational plan. I know how they think."

"Then we don't take the deep channel," Edmund said. "We take the cut behind Karait Island. It's shallow. My draft is two meters."

"It is three meters at low water," Devi said.

Edmund looked at the patched hull, at the epoxy and canvas and the steel plate he'd bolted over the patrol boat's damage, and I watched him do the arithmetic and not like the answer. "Then we go through fast, at the top of the tide, and we don't stop."

The tide turned at eleven. We went through at eleven.

* * *

The cut behind Karait Island was a brown corridor between walls of mangrove, the water the color of milk tea and moving the wrong way, crosswise, in long slow surges that kept trying to lay us against the roots. I worked the wheel in short corrections, feeling the boat through my hands the way you feel a horse, and Edmund stood beside me calling the depth off the sounder in a voice that got flatter every time he said a number.

"Two point six. Two point four. Hold her centerline, Marguerite, there's a bar ahead—"

"I see the bar."

"You don't see anything, nobody sees anything in this soup—"

"Then hold the wheel with me and stop narrating."

He almost laughed. That was the thing about my brother; even with contractors behind us and the sea trying to eat us, some part of him was enjoying it, and some part of me hated that I knew that about him, and some part of me loved it.

The bar came up under us like a fist.

I felt it through the keel first — a grinding shudder, a long tearing scrape that went up my arms and into my teeth — and then the engine note changed, went hollow, and Edmund was down the ladder before I could say his name, into the bilge, and when he came back up his face told me everything.

"It's opened. The patch. The whole plate's gone." He wiped his hands on his trousers, leaving brown streaks. "We're making water faster than the pump can—"

The engine died. Just like that, a cough and a silence, and the Marguerite swung sideways into the current and began, with terrible dignity, to drift.

We got her in against the mangroves on the leeward edge of a channel marker, wedged her bow into the roots with lines to two trunks, and by then the water in the bilge was over the sole boards and Devi was standing in it, hip-deep, holding our one dry bag above his head like a man carrying a child out of a fire.

"She's done," Edmund said. "For now. She's done for now."

I looked at my brother standing in the flooding wheelhouse of a boat our father had named for me, and I understood — in a way I hadn't in the lawyer's office, hadn't in the survey camp, hadn't on the trail — exactly what we were losing. It wasn't a vessel. It was the last thing our father had touched that hadn't been a lie.

"There's a village," Devi said quietly. "Half a mile, on stilts, past the marker. Karait village. The people there—" He stopped. He looked at the water, at the mangroves, at the grey wall of rain coming down the valley behind us. "The people there lost everything to the cache. Their rice lands. Their boats. Their temple brass. It was all taken in the war years and sold, and the money went into that gold. I catalogued the ledger pages myself. Village by village. Karait is the third entry."

Edmund and I looked at each other over the rising water.

"So we're going to ask them," I said, "to hide us."

"We're going to ask them," Edmund said, "to help us. There's a difference. We're going to tell them the truth and let them decide." He picked up the dry bag. "It's their gold too. Maybe more theirs than anyone's."

* * *

The village stood on stilts over the flooded flats like a congregation of herons, forty houses on hardwood poles, walkways strung between them, and the rain came off its tin roofs in sheets that turned the world to white noise. Dogs announced us. Then people did — a dozen faces at the walkway rails, and then a man coming down the central walk with a machete in his hand and no hurry in his step at all, which was more frightening than if he'd run.

He was maybe seventy, small and straight-backed, with a white shirt gone translucent with rain. He looked at Devi first, at the bloodstained tarp, at Edmund's and my salt-crusted clothes, and then he said something in the local tongue to the faces above us, and the machete went away, and the faces came down the walkways instead.

His name was Anand Perera. He spoke English the way our father had, with the same old colonial school cadence, and when I heard it I had to look away for a moment.

"Devi Raghunathan," Anand said. "I know your name. You came here in the spring. You asked about the old takings. You wrote in your book while my cousin's son watched you from the trees."

Devi's shoulders went down. "I should have told you then what I was doing. I was a coward about it. I was paid to catalogue, not to warn."

"And now?"

"Now they want to kill me for it."

Anand looked at Devi for a long moment. Rain ran off his chin. Then he turned to us.

"And you two. Who are you, standing in my village with no boat and a bag you won't put down?"

Edmund set the bag on the walkway boards and opened it. He took out the brass compass, our father's letters, and the photographs Devi had salvaged from the camp — the ledger pages, the cache manifests, the shipping marks that matched the strongbox in Edmund's strongbox ashore.

"This gold was taken from this coast," Edmund said. "From villages like this one. It's been sitting in a cave for thirty years while the men who took it got old and respectable. My father helped hide it there. He couldn't bring it back himself. We can. But there are men coming behind us to make sure nobody ever does, and right now we have no boat, no radio, and no plan. We're asking for your help. Not offering you a share. Asking."

The walkways had filled with people. Old women with umbrellas of woven palm. Young men in oilskins. Children peering between legs. Anand looked at the compass for a long time.

"That mark," he said, touching the photograph with one dry finger. "My father wore that mark on a chain they took off his neck. My mother watched them load the temple brass onto a barge and told me about it every day of her life until the day she died. You are asking us to help you." He looked up. "No. You are asking us to finish something. There is a difference."

I heard Edmund's own words come back to him in an old man's voice, and I saw my brother understand, finally, that the inheritance was never a thing you could hold.

* * *

They hid Devi in the schoolhouse, in the space under the floor where the children practiced cyclone drills, and put a woman named Kamala — a nurse, retired from the coastal hospital — to keeping his wound clean. They gave Edmund and me a room in Anand's house, one bed, one lamp, rain hammering the tin an arm's length from our heads.

We sat up half the night with the letters spread between us, reading our father's hand by lamplight. He had written to the village headman of Karait in 1989 — Anand's father, it turned out, dead eleven years. The letter was a promise. *When it can be done safely, it will be done openly, and what was taken will be returned with interest owed.* It had never been sent. It was in the bag with the compass, unsent, thirty years, a promise kept in a drawer.

"He was going to come back," I said.

"He came back," Edmund said. "He dove this coast every year. He just never found the courage for the last part." He looked at me across the lamp. "Neither have I. Not quite."

"Then we'll split it between us."

He almost smiled. Outside, the rain found a new gear, a roar, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of engines on the water. More than one. Slow. Methodical. Working the channels the way a man works a field.

Anand appeared in the doorway without knocking, water streaming off him, and he didn't waste words.

"They are at the mouth of the cut," he said. "Six men in two boats, asking after a damaged vessel. The tide comes up at four. At high water, this village floods to the walkways, and every boat in Karait can float — including the one under my house that they took from my father and my father took back, and that I have been keeping oiled for thirty years for a night I did not know was coming." He looked at the compass on the table between us. "At four, we can put you on the water. But you should know — the man asking questions has a photograph. He is showing it to my neighbors down the channel. It is a photograph of the two of you, and it is a good one."

I looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at the compass, and then at me, and something in his face had gone very still and very calm, the way it went when he was thirty meters down and the light was failing.

"Then at four," he said, "we dive."
