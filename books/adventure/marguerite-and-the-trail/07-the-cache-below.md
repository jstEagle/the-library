## 7. The Cache Below

The letters told him where to look. The compass told him how.

Edmund sat in the stern of the Marguerite with the oilskin packet open on his knees, reading his father's handwriting for the third time since dawn, and for the third time the same line stopped him cold:

*The water keeps what men are too greedy to carry. I have given it back to the keeping. Let whoever finds this finish what I could not.*

Below that, a sketch — crude but precise, drawn by a man who'd spent thirty years thinking about it. A sinkhole inland of the sea cave, connected by a flooded passage at depth. A bearing to hold on descent. A depth: forty-one meters. And beneath the sketch, small and almost apologetic, the words *forgive me*.

He hadn't forgiven him yet. He wasn't sure he ever would. But forgiveness was a luxury, and the contractors were not.

"They're moving," Devi said from the bow, binoculars pressed to his eyes. His arm was splinted with a boat oar and duct tape, and he held it against his chest like something borrowed. "Two boats. Not patrol gray. Charter white."

Marguerite took the glasses from him and looked a long moment. "They'll reach the cave mouth within the hour."

"Then we don't use the cave mouth." Edmund folded the letters away. "My father didn't hide gold where any diver with a torch could swim into it. He hid it where you'd need the compass just to find the door."

"And if the compass is wrong?"

"Then we've lost nothing but air." He looked at her. She was soaked to the elbows, hair plastered flat, and she hadn't slept more than an hour in two days, and she was still here. "You could stay on the boat."

"I could," she said. "I'm not going to."

* * *

The sinkhole lay a half-mile inland, past the mangrove line, in a bowl of collapsed limestone the villagers called the Devil's Well. Local children were forbidden near it. Local fishermen wouldn't net it. The water inside was green-black and utterly still, holding the sky like a lens, and when Edmund stood at its rim and looked down, he felt the old pull in his chest — the one that had put him under wrecks for fifteen years, the one his father had put there first.

"Forty-one meters," Marguerite said, checking the sketch over his shoulder. "In a hole nobody's ever dived."

"In a hole everybody's been afraid of. That's better than a lock."

Devi kept watch at the treeline. Marguerite fed out the guideline while Edmund checked his gauges twice, then a third time, because his hands weren't quite steady and he wanted to know it was the cold and not anything else.

"You understand what you're looking for isn't treasure," Marguerite said quietly.

He looked up.

"It's him. Down there. Whatever's down there — it's him, deciding to trust us after everything." Her voice caught and steadied. "Don't come up until you've actually found it. Don't come up because it got dark and scary. He waited thirty years, Edmund. We can wait forty minutes."

It was the most honest thing anyone had said to him in years. He pulled his hood on.

The water took him the way deep water always did — all at once, then gradually, the world narrowing to the circle of his torchlight. Ten meters, twenty, the green going to black, the walls of the sinkhole falling away beneath him in fluted limestone columns like the pipes of some drowned organ. At thirty meters he felt the current. Not random — directional, steady, tugging west-northwest. The passage. Water moving through the system meant the sinkhole breathed with the tide, which meant it connected to the sea, which meant his father's sketch was true.

He held the compass up before his mask.

The needle swung off north — hard, deliberate, swinging until it pointed down and to the left, along the current, into a cleft in the rock no wider than a doorway. The compass that didn't point north. His father had re-magnetized it, or rigged it, or whatever a man does to make an instrument lie about everything except the one thing that matters. It had never been broken. It had been aimed.

Edmund went through the cleft.

* * *

The passage ran forty feet, ceiling low enough that his tank scraped stone, and then opened, and his torchlight fell across a sight he would carry for the rest of his life.

A chamber the size of a chapel. Air pocket above — a dome of trapped breath, black and ancient. And below, stacked with a care that hurt to look at, the cache.

Strongboxes. Dozens of them, the same government stamp as the one from the wreck, arranged in rows on a shelf of rock above the flood line. Gold bars wrapped in waxed canvas. Crates stenciled in a colonial shipping mark he recognized from museum photographs — the requisition ledgers of a war-era administration that had stripped the coast bare and called it protection. Coins in leather sacks gone stiff with age. Enough wealth to buy every boat in the harbor ten times over. Enough theft to do the same arithmetic in reverse.

And on top of the nearest strongbox, weighted down with a brass diving weight, a second oilskin packet.

His father's initials.

Edmund's hands shook as he worked the knot. Inside, dry after three decades in the sealed air of the chamber: a photograph of a young salvage crew, his father among them, barely older than Edmund was now. A page torn from a requisition ledger, listing villages by name — Kilancheri, Vettukad, Thaikkal — and tonnages taken, with the word *compensation* struck through and *requisitioned* written above it in different ink. And a letter, longer than the others, written in a hand that had aged between the first packet and this one:

*I helped take it. That is the truth I have carried. They told us it was enemy gold, war salvage, lawful prize. Then I saw the ledgers. These are not prizes. These are the dowries and temple funds and boat-money of people who are still alive, whose grandchildren still fish these waters. I could not return it alone — they would have killed me and moved it deeper. So I hid it where only someone who understood the water would find it, and I made sure my children would understand the water.*

*I am sorry I taught you silence instead of this. Finish it. Give it back. That is your inheritance — not the gold. The giving back.*

Edmund stayed in the water a long time, breathing slow, letting the torch beam rest on the letter until the paper blurred behind his mask and he couldn't tell the salt in his eyes from the sea around him.

Grief and pride, entwined so tightly he couldn't separate them. His father had been a thief. His father had also spent thirty years building a lock that only conscience could open — rigging a compass, writing letters, trusting the future with the one thing he couldn't fix himself. Every dive Edmund had ever made, every knot and bearing and breath-hold drill, had been training for this without either of them knowing it.

He hadn't inherited gold. He'd inherited the finishing.

He tucked the ledger page and the letter into his suit's waterproof pouch, took one last look at the rows of boxes — patient, patient, waiting exactly where a stubborn man had left them — and turned for the passage home.

* * *

He surfaced into voices.

Not Marguerite's. Not Devi's.

Edmund froze at the rim of the sinkhole, body still half-submerged, regulator hanging loose, and listened. Four men, maybe five, at the top of the bowl. Boots on wet limestone. The metallic snap of a rifle bolt carrying clearly across the still water.

"—line's still anchored. Someone's down there or was."

"Then we wait. Nobody surfaces fast from forty meters anyway."

Edmund sank slowly back beneath the surface, heart hammering against the cold, and hung in the black water at the mouth of the passage, watching the dim green ceiling of the world he'd just left ripple with the vibration of footsteps above.

Above him, at the rim, silhouetted against the grey monsoon light, stood the men who had erased a survey team and were here to erase everything else.

Between him and the open sea lay forty meters of flooded limestone, one narrow passage, and a chamber full of proof.

And somewhere beyond the mangroves, he could only pray, Marguerite and Devi had heard the voices too — and were already deciding what to do about it.
