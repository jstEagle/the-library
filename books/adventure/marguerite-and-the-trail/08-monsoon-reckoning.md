## 8. Monsoon Reckoning

The rain came sideways now, the way it did when the monsoon stopped pretending to be weather and became a siege. Marguerite crouched in the doorway of the stilt house, watching three figures come up the mud track from the river — not hurrying, not needing to. Men who moved like that had already decided how everything would end.

"Devi," she said quietly. "Inside."

He was already moving, but too slow. The wound in his side had slowed him for two days, and two days of fever had done the rest. He made it four steps down the ladder before the first man reached the base of the stilts and put a hand on the rail.

"Don't," the man said. Not loud. The rain made shouting pointless; he'd calibrated his voice to cut through it anyway. "Raghunathan. Come down or we take the whole village apart looking for you, and then we burn what's left. Your choice how much that word is worth."

Marguerite counted them without meaning to — an old habit from her years auditing other people's disasters. Three at the ladder. Two more at the treeline, rifles slung but hands near. A sixth somewhere she couldn't see, which meant he was the dangerous one.

Devi stood on the third rung, half in and half out of the rain, and looked at her. His face said what his mouth wouldn't: *run*. As if she were the kind of person who ran.

"You're the sister," the man at the rail said, tilting his head up at her. "Quintana. We know about the compass. We know your brother's already gone to the water." He smiled without warmth. "So it's just coordinates now. Give us the sinkhole, and everyone walks away. Even him." A nod at Devi. "Even you."

*Everyone walks away.* She'd spent eleven years in insurance litigation listening to men say things like that with straight faces. Nobody walked away. People were walked.

Below her, Devi shifted his weight off the bad leg, and she saw him calculating something — surrender, probably, trading himself to buy her time, the same arithmetic Edmund was running right now out there in the storm. Her family's talent for noble self-destruction, arriving on schedule.

"No," she said, and started down the ladder.

* * *

They bound Devi's wrists with zip ties and left him kneeling in the mud under an awning of woven palm, one contractor standing over him with the bored posture of a man minding luggage. Two others took Marguerite to the far end of the platform, out of his earshot, though they needn't have bothered. Devi knew everything she knew. That was the point of him.

The leader — Voss, the others called him, when they called him anything — spread a laminated chart on a crate and weighted the corners with shells. Rain drummed on it like fingers.

"The sinkhole," he said. "Your brother went into the cave system an hour ago. He'll come back up through the sinkhole mouth, because there's no other way out that isn't a drowning. When he does, we'll be standing there. All we need from you is confirmation of the chamber location so we don't waste time flooding half the system looking for it." He tapped the chart. "Or we can do this the inefficient way, and I start with the old man."

She looked at the chart. Looked at the rain hammering the lagoon, where the surge was already climbing past the second marker stake. Looked at Devi, kneeling, feverish, watching her with an expression that asked nothing and forgave everything.

Her father had worn that compass around his neck for thirty years and never once told either of his children what it pointed at. He had let them grow up believing he was a small man who'd wasted his life on a small coast. And all along he'd been holding a door shut with his body, waiting for someone strong enough to hold it after him.

She thought: *I am not brave. I am furious.* It turned out those could be the same thing.

"There's a bearing," she said. "The compass gives it. But it has to be read at the sinkhole rim itself — the needle won't settle anywhere else. My father built it that way. Deliberately." She let Voss see her swallow. "If you want the chamber, you take me to the rim. Both of us, if you want the old man alive long enough to confirm the survey data."

Voss studied her for a long moment. Rain ran off his jaw.

"You're negotiating," he said, almost admiring. "With me. In this."

"I'm an actuary's daughter," Marguerite said. "I price risk for a living. Right now you're carrying a hostage nobody's seen die, a witness who's worthless dead, and a treasure you can't find. I'm the cheapest thing on your ledger. Take the trade."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the professional's reluctance to admire the mark — and then he jerked his chin at his men. "Bring both. If she's lying, the swamp gets two bodies instead of one."

* * *

They moved upriver along the flooded trail, Devi limping between contractors who held him by the arms as much to keep him upright as captive. Marguerite walked behind, hands free, unbound — because Voss believed fear was a better restraint than rope, and because she had asked him, with a tremor she'd rehearsed in the boat's cabin mirror, whether he really wanted to explain to his employers why the only person who could read the compass arrived with broken hands.

The lie sat in her chest like swallowed glass. There was no reading at the rim. The compass worked fine anywhere. But Edmund needed minutes, and minutes were the only currency she had left, and she had learned in the last week that she would spend anything.

The storm surge reached them before the sinkhole did. It came up the creek bed all at once — not a wave but a rising, the sea shouldering inland through every channel at once, brown water climbing the mangrove roots in visible surges. One of the contractors swore. Voss checked a watch against the chart and said nothing, which frightened her more than swearing would have.

The sinkhole opened at the base of the limestone bluff like a blowhole, thirty feet across, already swallowing surge water in slow revolving gulps. The cave beneath it connected to the sea caves, Edmund had explained that morning with charcoal scratches on a plank — one system, one throat, and the tide breathing through it twice a day. Except today the tide wasn't breathing. Today it was screaming.

And there, at the rim, a coil of dive line staked in a pattern she recognized from the survey camp — Edmund's line-and-bearing drill, laid out in plain sight. A signal. *I'm below. I know they're here. Trust me.*

"He's down there," Voss said, reading the coil the same way. He posted two men at the rim, sent one back down the trail, and kept Devi close, pistol loose in his hand. Then he turned to Marguerite. "The bearing."

She knelt at the rim as if consulting something invisible, rain streaming off her, and bought another minute by pretending the needle wouldn't settle. Below the churning surface, forty feet down where no one else could see, she knew — she *knew*, the way you know things about family — that Edmund was working in the dark with a brace of demolition charges and their father's letters, rigging the limestone shelf above the cache chamber to come down.

He couldn't hear her. But she said it anyway, lips barely moving, words lost in the rain:

"Hold on, Eddie. I've got them exactly where we need them."

Because that was the truth she hadn't spoken aloud until this moment, kneeling at the edge of a drowning world: she wasn't trading herself for Devi. She was delivering them. Every contractor on this bluff, drawn to the rim, packed at the mouth of the one cave system the surge was about to turn into a closed fist. Edmund didn't need her to save him. He needed her to keep them *here*.

Devi caught her eye across the rim. Fever-bright, wrists tied, he still managed to look at her the way a professor looks at a student who has finally understood the lecture. She almost laughed.

"The needle," Voss demanded. "Now."

"It's settling," she said. "It's settling."

Above them the sky went the color of wet slate, and the surge climbed the bluff another foot, and somewhere below the water, in the dark, her brother began to work.

* * *

What happened next began with a sound none of them expected: a deep, subterranean groan, felt in the teeth before the ears, as if the island itself had cleared its throat.

The contractors froze. Marguerite did not. She threw herself sideways off the rim, grabbed Devi by the collar, and hauled him down the slope as the first crack opened along the limestone — not the collapse Edmund had planned, not yet, but the surge finding weaknesses faster than any charge could. Water geysered up through the blowhole in a column of spray and foam, and the cave system below inhaled, a vast wet gasp, pulling at everything near its mouth.

One of the men at the rim went down on the slick rock, slid, caught himself ten feet from the opening. The other fired wildly — at what, Marguerite never learned — and the shot cracked away into the rain.

"THE CHAMBER'S FLOODING!" she screamed at Voss, pointing at the geyser, selling it with everything she had. "Whatever's down there is going under — you want it, you go NOW!"

It was the one lie that cost her nothing, because it was almost true. The cache was going under. Everything was going under. The whole coast, the whole rotten secret, the gold and the guilt and the men who'd come to collect it — the monsoon was repossessing all of it at once.

Voss looked at the water. Looked at her. And for the first time since the stilt village, she saw doubt move across his face like a shadow of a bird.

Then the bluff groaned again, louder, and the rim began to give.

Marguerite dragged Devi into the mangroves as the world behind them folded inward — stone and surge and shouting, the cave system taking its first mouthfuls of the men who'd come to empty it. She didn't look back to count them. She was already counting something else: the seconds until the main charge blew, and the distance to high ground, and whether her brother — her stubborn, impossible brother — was still alive in the dark below it all.

The last thing she heard before the trees swallowed them was the water, rising, and rising, and rising.
