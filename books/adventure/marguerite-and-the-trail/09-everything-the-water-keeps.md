## 9. Everything the Water Keeps

The water in the sinkhole had gone from green to black, and Edmund knew what that meant: the sea was coming in through the cave system beneath him, filling the chamber from below, the way it filled everything eventually. He hung on the rope at the lip of the flooded shaft with the rain hammering his shoulders and listened to the storm make its argument.

Below him, forty feet down through churned water, the cache waited. His father's crates. The gold looted from villages whose names Edmund had only learned three days ago, when Devi Raghunathan said them one by one into Marguerite's recorder like a man paying off a debt.

Above him, at the rim of the sinkhole, four contractors were working their way down the slick limestone with headlamps and a climbing line, and they were moving faster than men should move in this weather, because men who are paid to erase things do not wait for convenient conditions.

* * *

It had taken him twenty minutes to find the valve.

His father's letters had described it obliquely — *the old drainage works, the iron wheel, the passage the engineers cut and then pretended they hadn't* — and Edmund had spent two dives confirming it: a rusted sluice gate set into the lower gallery of the cave, connected by a flooded tunnel to the sinkhole shaft. Open it, and the incoming tide would pour through the whole system, scouring the chambers, collapsing the silted galleries that were already failing. Close it, and the cache sat dry and catalogued and ready for whoever held the coordinates.

His father had built the mechanism to be opened by hand. By someone who knew. By someone who came down here intending to finish something.

Edmund had come down here intending exactly that, though an hour ago he'd imagined finishing it differently — hauling crates up, documenting everything, letting lawyers and journalists fight over the rest. That was before the headlamps appeared on the rim. Before he heard one of them shout down the shaft, voice flattened by rain: *"Quintana! We know you're in there!"*

They knew his name. Of course they did. They'd known it since the patrol vessel's wake cracked his hull, probably since before that.

He checked the rope. Checked the knife on his belt. Checked, absurdly, the compass hanging against his chest — needle swinging wild, useless down here, but warm from his skin, and he touched it the way you touch a railing in the dark.

Then he went down.

* * *

The water at thirty feet was a war. The tide was turning and the tunnel to the sea breathed in long surges, and Edmund swam them hand over hand along the guide line he'd rigged that morning, past the first gallery where the crates stood in ranks like a drowned regiment. Stenciled marks still legible under a skin of silt. Government property. Government theft. He'd have wept if his mask weren't already flooding with something.

The sluice wheel was worse than he remembered. Iron gone to lace, furred with barnacle, and when he put his weight on it, it moved one spoke and stopped as if the ocean itself had put a hand on it.

He thought about his father then — not the absent years, not the silence, but the man in the photographs Devi had described: young, sunburned, standing on this same coast with a survey crew and orders he must have understood even then were lies. His father had found this gold and could not return it and could not keep it, so he'd hidden it and carried the compass home like a splinter under the skin, and it had ruined him quietly for thirty years, and now it had brought his children back to finish the sentence.

Edmund braced both boots against the gate housing and pulled until his vision starred.

The wheel gave.

Not all at once — in a grinding, shrieking surrender, one quarter-turn and then another, and behind the gate he felt the change before he saw it: pressure shifting in the tunnel, a low groan through the rock, the water around him beginning to move with purpose. The tide was coming in, and now he'd shown it the door.

He kicked for the guide line and the surface and the rope up the shaft, and the last thing he saw below him was the first crate stirring in the current, tilting, shedding its silt like a man shrugging off a coat.

* * *

The climb up the shaft nearly killed him, which he supposed was fair, since he'd designed it to nearly kill everyone.

The surge hit the chamber while he was still ten feet below the rim, and the rope went slack and then taut and then slack again as the water column hammered upward, and Edmund rode it like a man clinging to a mast in a sea state no boat survives. It slammed him against the limestone twice. He tasted blood and rainwater. Above him, faintly, through the roar, he heard shouting — the contractors' voices, higher now, less certain.

He broke the surface at the shaft's throat and hauled himself onto a ledge, and looked up.

Headlamps at the rim, four of them, and one of the beams was swinging wildly because its owner was retreating, and another was gone entirely, and the sound coming down the shaft was not shouting anymore. It was the sound of the sinkhole drinking. The galleries below were collapsing the way old drowned places collapse — slowly, then all at once — and the rim itself was shedding stone into the maw, and the contractors were discovering that the treasure they'd been sent to secure had just become a hole in the world.

*"Quintana!"* One voice, raw with fury and fear. *"You're killing it — you're killing the whole site—"*

Yes, Edmund thought. That was rather the point.

* * *

Marguerite found him at the top, dragging him the last few meters over the lip and flat into the mud, and she was screaming something he couldn't hear over the storm, and then he could: *"—the recording, Edmund, it's transmitting, it's transmitting NOW—"*

She held up her phone, screen cracked, wrapped in plastic, and the little signal indicator pulsed green. Offshore, anchored beyond the surf line where the monsoon couldn't reach, the journalist's boat had been waiting since dawn. Devi's testimony — every name, every village, every order given by men above their pay grade — was riding the storm's interference out to sea in fragments and bursts, and Marguerite had kept the transmitter running for six hours with a battery pack and her own body between it and the rain.

"They'll have enough," she shouted. "Devi says they'll have enough by now."

"Then it's done." His voice came out broken, half water. "It's done, Marguerite."

She looked at him — at the blood, the rope burns, the wreck of him — and then past him, down into the shaft, where the glow of the headlamps had become one lamp, then none, and the sound from below was stone settling into stone, the ocean closing its accounts.

"The gold," she said. Not accusing. Just asking.

"Gone." He said it and felt it land somewhere deep and clean. "Back where it started. In the water."

* * *

They crawled away from the rim as the sinkhole finished its work, and from the tree line they watched the last of it: a section of the slope slumping inward, the shaft swallowing itself, brown water boiling up and then calming with terrible efficiency, as if the coast had simply exhaled after holding its breath for eighty years.

Two of the contractors made it out — Edmund saw their lights staggering along the ridge toward the road, and he did nothing about it, because there was nothing left to do. The other two had been at the bottom of the shaft when the galleries let go. He would carry that. He would carry it alongside everything else, and he would not pretend it weighed nothing. But he also knew what Devi would say, later, in the inquiry room, in his careful exhausted voice: *they came armed to a drowning coast to bury the truth, and the truth buried them.*

The evidence hadn't drowned, though. That was the joke of it, the beautiful bitter joke. The gold was gone; the proof remained. Devi's testimony, transmitted. The stenciled fragments that would wash ashore for months along this coast, government marks on rotted wood, washing up in front of witnesses, in front of cameras, in front of the coastal families who would walk the beaches after the storm and pick up the pieces of their own history and finally be believed.

Justice outside any courtroom. The water keeping what it keeps, and giving up what it gives.

* * *

Near dawn the storm began to spend itself, and the Marguerite — patched hull and all, God help her — came around the headland through gray light, and there were people aboard: villagers from the stilt village, and the journalist, thin and rain-soaked and holding a receiver like a reliquary.

"We got most of it," the journalist called across the water. "Enough. More than enough."

Edmund sat on the shingle with his sister's shoulder against his and watched the boat come in, and felt the compass against his chest, and thought about his father waiting thirty years in the dark for someone to open a rusted wheel.

"You could have died down there," Marguerite said quietly.

"I nearly did."

"And the gold's gone, and your half of the inheritance is gone, and mine too."

"Yes."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Good."

And Edmund laughed — a raw, salt-cracked laugh that hurt his ribs — because it was good, it was the only good thing left, and it was theirs.

Then the villager at the bow pointed inland, upcoast, toward the navy station, and said something in his own language that the journalist translated with a frown:

"He says there are boats at the survey camp. Navy boats. And men asking questions about *you two* — and about the survivor."

Devi was still in the mangroves. Still wounded. Still the only living witness who hadn't yet told his story to anyone who could protect him.

Edmund got to his feet.
