## 10. Marguerite and the Trail

**MARGUERITE**

The inquiry opened on a Tuesday, in a courthouse that smelled of mildew and old paper, and Marguerite arrived early enough to watch Devi Raghunathan climb the steps alone.

He'd put on weight since the mangroves. His arm had healed crooked — it always would — but he wore his sling like a man who had decided to stop apologizing for surviving. He paused at the bottom of the stairs, looked up at the columns, and said, to no one in particular: "I used to work in buildings like this. Before."

"Before they tried to erase you," Marguerite said.

He smiled without humor. "Before I let them pay me to look away." Then he squared his shoulders and went inside, and she followed.

The hearing room was half full. Journalists, mostly — the story had legs now, the way stories do once someone powerful is embarrassed. The recording of Devi's testimony had gone out over the storm-wrecked airwaves three weeks ago, transmitted from the Marguerite's battered radio to a journalist's boat anchored offshore while the monsoon ate the coastline behind them. Since then: resignations, arrests, a parliamentary committee, and a scramble of officials suddenly unable to recall who had authorized what.

The villages filed their claims first. Old men and old women in their best clothes, standing before the commission with documents older than the republic — deeds, tax receipts, a photograph of a temple that no longer existed. The gold looted from their grandparents during the war had sat in a flooded chamber beneath a sinkhole for thirty years, catalogued by men who called themselves surveyors, guarded by a dead man's compass and a dead man's patience.

Their father's patience.

Marguerite still didn't know how to feel about that. Edmund didn't either. They'd agreed, somewhere in the stilt village between the storm surge and the gunfire, not to decide yet. Some inheritances you carry before you understand them.

The commission chairman read the village claims into the record, one by one. A woman named Suriya spoke for her grandmother's generation, her voice steady as a keel. "We are not asking for charity," she said. "We are asking for what was taken. The water kept it safe. We only want it home."

Marguerite wrote that down in her notebook, though she wasn't sure why. It felt like something worth keeping.

**EDMUND**

Edmund spent the days of the inquiry at the boatyard, because grief was easier to hold when your hands were full.

The Marguerite looked worse than she had any right to. The patrol wake damage from that first day, the split hull from the river escape, the patched seams that had barely held through the monsoon peak — she was a floating argument for retirement. But boats weren't arguments. Boats were promises. And he had made this one a promise years ago, back when the name on her transom was the only part of his sister he allowed himself to keep.

She was sanding the port seam when he arrived, hair tied back, paint flecks on her forearms, arguing with the yard owner about marine plywood prices in a voice that brooked no negotiation. She'd been on the island nine weeks and already the harbor men treated her like weather — unavoidable, occasionally violent, ultimately necessary.

"You're late," she said, without looking up.

"The inquiry ran long. Suriya testified again."

"And?"

"They accepted the chain of custody. The cache contents go into escrow until the claims are adjudicated." He set down his bag. "All of it. Every bar."

Marguerite straightened, studied him. "You're all right with that."

It wasn't quite a question. He picked up a sander anyway, bought himself a moment in the noise of it. "I spent ten years pulling things out of wrecks and selling them to men who never asked where they came from," he said finally. "I told myself salvage law covered it. Clean hands." He looked at the hull, at the long scar where the sea had nearly taken them both. "It doesn't. It never did. Dad knew that. That's why he hid it instead of selling it. That's why he left the trail."

"He left a trail that got four people killed."

"He left a trail that got one person out alive," Edmund said. "And one truth above water. You count what counts."

Marguerite was quiet a moment. Then she tossed him the sanding block. "Port side's yours. I don't do fiberglass."

"You did it last week."

"And I hated it. Inheritance means division of labor, brother."

* * *

**MARGUERITE**

They worked through the afternoons, and the boat came back to them plank by plank.

It was strange, Marguerite thought, how repair worked. You didn't undo the damage. You couldn't. You just laid new material over the wound, sealed it, made it strong enough to hold water again — and the scar stayed underneath forever, doing its quiet work of reminding you.

That was what she and Edmund were doing too, she suspected. The lawyer's letter felt like a lifetime ago — *half of everything*, she'd demanded, standing on the dock with their father's letters in her bag and twenty years of resentment in her chest. She'd wanted the money then. Or she'd wanted the fight; it was hard to remember which. What she'd gotten instead was a drowned cave, a hostage exchange in rising floodwater, and a brother who had held her hand in the dark while the surge tried to peel the world apart.

The debt between them hadn't been paid. It had been dissolved — like salt in seawater, still present, no longer separate.

On the last Friday of the month, she brought the papers to the boatyard. Two sets. She'd had the harbor lawyer draw them up properly, because some things you did right or not at all.

Edmund read them sitting on the transom, feet dangling, the way he'd sat on docks since he was eight years old. His face did its usual thing — nothing, nothing, nothing — and then, at the second page, something.

"Co-owner," he said.

"Fifty-fifty. The boat, the business, the debts." She handed him a pen. "You can keep the name. I've decided I like being the namesake of something that refuses to sink."

He laughed — actually laughed, short and surprised, a sound she realized she'd heard maybe five times in her adult life. He signed. She signed. The yard owner witnessed it, unimpressed, and charged them for the ink.

"So," Edmund said, folding the papers into a waterproof pouch, because neither of them would ever trust paper to anything less again. "Partners."

"Partners." She looked out at the harbor, at the fishing boats coming home ahead of the evening squall, at the coast beyond it where the sinkhole mouth had been sealed by the government with concrete and bureaucracy. "Do you ever think about what he'd say? Dad. Seeing us do this."

"I think," Edmund said slowly, "he'd say we took the long way around. And then he'd check our knots."

* * *

**EDMUND**

The compass ceremony, if you could call it that, happened by accident.

They were fitting the new binnacle light in the wheelhouse, testing wiring by lamplight, when Marguerite said, "Put it back. The compass. It belongs here, not around your neck."

Edmund touched the brass case through his shirt. He'd worn it every day since the wreck — through the trail inland, through the swamp, through the dive into the flooded dark where he'd found the cache and, bolted to the chamber wall beside it, a waterproof cylinder containing his father's final letter. *If you're reading this, you found it the hard way. Good. The easy way would have meant the wrong people found it first.*

The letter had named the villages. Had named the theft. Had asked, in a hand gone shaky near the end, only one thing: *give it back, and give each other a chance.*

Edmund unhooked the lanyard. The compass sat heavy in his palm — brass worn smooth, glass clouded at the edges, the needle that had pulled off north for thirty years, dragging them inland toward the truth.

He mounted it in the binnacle, centered it, and stepped back.

The needle swung once, twice, settling — and stopped. Dead on north. Steady as a plumb line. As if whatever it had been waiting for was finally done, and it could rest.

Neither of them spoke for a long moment.

"Huh," Marguerite said at last, in the tone she used when she refused to be moved by something that had moved her.

"Yeah," Edmund said.

They stood there in the lamplight, two people and a small true instrument, while outside the rain began again — ordinary rain this time, the gentle kind, the kind that fed things instead of drowning them.

* * *

**MARGUERITE**

The Marguerite put out on the first clear morning of the dry season, co-owned, rebuilt, and bound for the coastal villages — not for salvage, but for something the lawyers were calling "community documentation" and Suriya called, more simply, "listening."

Devi came aboard as far as the river mouth, standing on the dock with his crooked arm and his clean conscience, waving them off like a man seeing off family. Which, Marguerite supposed, he was now. The inquiry had listed him as a protected witness; the siblings had listed him as crew, effective immediately, whether he liked it or not. He'd pretended to deliberate for exactly four seconds.

The coast slid past, green and gilded in the early light. Edmund had the helm. Marguerite stood at the bow with the wind in her face, watching the water change color over the reef, thinking about trails — the ones other people leave for you, and the ones you leave behind without knowing, and how the only real question is who picks them up.

"Course?" Edmund called from the wheelhouse.

She checked the binnacle. North, steady, true.

"North," she said. "Where else?"

Behind them, the harbor fell away. Ahead, the whole open coast waited — and in the wheelhouse, taped inside the cover of the logbook where only a diver would think to look, Edmund had tucked one page of their father's letter they'd never shown the inquiry. One line, underlined twice:

*There is a second cache. Smaller. Farther south. I could not reach it, and I pray no one else does — until you two are ready.*
