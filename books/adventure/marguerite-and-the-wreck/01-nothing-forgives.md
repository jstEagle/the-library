## 1. Nothing Forgives

The Redline Range doesn't forgive, and it doesn't forget. It just waits. Fifteen years I've been walking its ridgelines and dry washes, and the country has taken a wife, two brothers, most of my hearing in the left ear, and every friend who ever asked me to be somewhere on time. What it's left me is a badge, a rifle I've never fired at anything with a face, and a talent for reading ground that the government pays me for and nobody else wants.

So when the survey team went missing out of Fort Meridian — four people, no tracks, gear stacked like soldiers folding flags — they sent me. Because I'm the one who finds things the desert has decided to keep.

And because there was a thief.

Marguerite Voss. That name came down the wire three days ago attached to a warrant and a photograph that did her no favors. Stolen government documents, classified, worth more than the truck she'd abandoned outside Calico Flats. The file said she was dangerous. The file said a lot of things files say about people who embarrass agencies with acronyms.

What the file didn't say was that she walked like she owned the ground under her feet, which is a lie you can only keep up for so long in this country before it starts charging you rent.

I picked up her trail at dawn on the second day, where she'd crossed a pan of alkali flat and left prints sharp as coin edges. She traveled light and she traveled smart — cutting her own track with livestock trails, doubling back through rock gardens where a hundred years of flash floods had written the same story a thousand times. But everybody leaves a signature. Hers was economy. No wasted steps, no campfires, water discipline like a woman who'd grown up counting what she drank.

By noon I could have told you her height, her stride, how long since she'd slept. By midafternoon I could tell you something stranger: she wasn't running from anything. A runner cuts corners, drops weight, goes to ground. Marguerite Voss was moving with intent, straight-line across country that punished straight lines, like she had an appointment.

I followed her appointment.

* * *

The wash opened up around four o'clock, a dry riverbed of red sandstone winding down from the high country, walls twelve feet tall in places, the floor printed with the ghosts of every rain the last decade had failed to send. Good ambush country. Bad place to corner somebody. I came down the rim slow, rifle slung, hands loose, because fifteen years teaches you that the moment you reach for iron is the moment the story gets worse.

She was standing at the bend of the wash with her back to me, crouched over something at the base of the wall.

"Ma'am." My voice carries in canyon country; it's one of the few gifts the range gave back. "Ranger Blackwood. Let's keep this simple."

She didn't startle. That was the first thing wrong with her. Most people, caught, do something — run, freeze, talk too fast. She just finished whatever she was doing, stood, and turned around like I was a bus she'd been expecting.

"Casper Blackwood," she said. "The ranger who finds things."

"The ranger who found you."

"You're half right." She was smaller than the photograph, wiry, sunburned across the cheekbones, with eyes the color of wet slate that looked at me the way you look at weather — assessing, not afraid. Her pack sat against the wall behind her, and beside it, a crate.

A military surplus water crate. The kind the survey teams carried. Stenciled with a property number I recognized, because I'd inventoried its twins at the abandoned basecamp two days before the surveyors vanished.

"What's in the box, Ms. Voss?"

"Water." She stepped aside so I could see it plainly. Gallon jugs, maybe eight of them, packed in straw. "About nine gallons. It stays here."

"It stays—"

"For them." She said it simply, without performance, and that was what made my skin prickle. People lie with drama. Truth comes out flat. "There are people in these mountains who need water and can't carry enough themselves. I've been caching supplies along their route for eleven days."

"Route to where?"

"That's the question, isn't it, Ranger?" She tilted her head. "You found the survey camp. You found the gear stacked neat as church. Four people walk into the high country and leave no footprints. And your agency's response was to send one man with a rifle after a document thief instead of a search party after four lost scientists. Doesn't that arithmetic bother you?"

Everything about her bothered me, which is different from being wrong.

"Those documents you took," I said. "They were maps. Survey data."

"They were a route." Something moved behind her eyes then, quick as a fish under ice. "Through the glacier. And your government intends to make sure nobody else ever walks it."

I'd heard rumors. Sealed passes, restricted corridors, the north country slowly getting fenced off by men in offices who'd never been cold. Fifteen years I'd told myself the badge meant something anyway — that the law was a rough instrument but mine to hold level. I'd built a whole life on that shelf, and I felt it flex now, just slightly, under a weight I couldn't name yet.

"Turn yourself in," I said, and hated how tired it sounded. "Come down off this mountain, give a statement. I'll see you fed and I'll speak for you at the hearing. That's the best deal you're going to get out here."

"No," she said.

Just that. No fear in it, no bravado. She looked at the water crate, and her voice changed — dropped into something quieter, almost tender. "My brother is up there, Ranger. Elias Voss. He was on that survey team. Four days ago his radio went silent, and the agency that sent him sent nobody. So either I'm a thief stealing papers, or I'm the only person in the world looking for him, and you get to decide which story you carried up this mountain."

The wind came down the wash and moved the dust in little ropes. Somewhere above us a raven said something ugly to the sky.

I thought about fifteen years. About the wife the range ate, about my brother Danny who froze two miles off this same trail because the response time to the Redline was measured in days, not hours. About every night I'd spent alone in a line cabin telling myself the work was the point, the service was the point, that nothing forgives out here but the work keeps you honest.

And I thought about a radio still warm when the techs reached the survey camp. Warm means transmitting. Transmitting means somebody wanted to be found — or somebody wanted something else to find them first.

"Show me the route," I said.

Marguerite Voss smiled for the first time, and it wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile of a woman who'd bet everything on one card and just watched it land.

"Not showing," she said. "Following. There's a camp two valleys north. If you want your answers, Ranger, you'll have to walk into them with me."

Every instinct I owned said arrest her, cuff her to the water crate, and call it a career honestly served. Instead I looked at nine gallons of water left lovingly in a dead wash for people who might already be dead, and I felt something crack open in me that fifteen years of silence had sealed shut.

Curiosity. Worse than thirst. Nothing forgives it, either.

I shouldered my rifle and nodded north.

"After you," I said.
