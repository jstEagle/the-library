## 2. The Thief Who Left Water

There are two kinds of people who chase you across a desert for three days. The kind who want to hurt you, and the kind who want to understand you. I'd been running from the first kind my whole life, so when the second kind finally caught up to me at the dry wash — dusty, sunburned, and holding my stolen canteen like it was evidence in a trial — I didn't know what to do with him.

"You've been leaving water," Casper Blackwood said. Not a question. A ranger's accusation dressed up as an observation.

"I've been leaving water," I agreed.

"Along a route nobody travels."

"Somebody travels it."

He crouched at the edge of the cache — three sealed tins buried under a flat stone, marked with a cairn of red jasper that any fool could see if any fool were looking. He wasn't a fool. That was going to be a problem.

"This is surveyor's marking," he said. "Government pattern."

"Maybe I read about it in a book."

"Maybe." He turned one of the tins over in his hands. "Or maybe you're supplying someone who was trained by the Bureau of Land Survey, because you know exactly where they'll be and exactly what they'll need, and you're betting your life they're still alive to need it."

I should have run then. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody's keeping them. I had a twelve-minute head start on foot over broken ground, and I'd spent six years learning how desert country swallows a pursuer. But he said *still alive*, and something in my chest — the part I keep locked, the part with my brother's face in it — went very quiet and very still.

"How long have you known?" I asked.

"That you weren't just a thief?" He sat down on the rock like a man settling in for a long story, which annoyed me almost as much as being right. "Since the second cache. Thieves don't bury water. Thieves don't mark caches in survey code. And thieves—" he tapped the tin, "—don't use government-issue seals. Where did you get these?"

"Stole them. Obviously. It's sort of my whole brand."

"Marguerite Voss." He said my name the way other men said curse words. "Six years of warrants. Artifacts, documents, one memorable incident involving a Bureau helicopter. Every report says the same thing: takes only paper, never touches the payrolls, never hurts anyone. You know what we call that in the service?"

"A flattering profile?"

"A pattern." He looked up at me, and his eyes were the color of the range at dusk — all shadow and heat behind them. "You steal documents. Only documents. So either you're the world's most principled thief, or you're looking for something specific, and everything else is cover."

The wind came down off the Redline escarpment and moved the dust between us in a slow gray curtain. I thought about my brother. I thought about him a lot, but in that moment I thought about him the way you think about a wound when someone presses near it — involuntarily, entirely.

"His name was Tomas," I heard myself say. God help me. Six years of silence, undone by a man sitting on a rock. "Tomas Voss. Surveyor, third class. He walked into the Redline Range eleven months ago with a government team of nine, and the government says he never existed."

Blackwood didn't move. "That's not possible. Personnel files—"

"Were sealed. Along with the mission. Along with the route they were mapping." I pulled the folded papers from inside my jacket — the ones I'd bled for, the ones I'd spent three weeks copying in a records annex while a clerk with bad sinuses slept twenty feet away. "I stole these from the Regional Director's safe. Want to arrest me now? This seems like a good moment. I'll even come quietly. It's the least I can do after you solved my entire personality in one afternoon."

He took the pages. I watched him read them, watched his ranger's face do something I suspect it hadn't done in fifteen years of service, which was change.

*Glacier Route 9 — Northern Passage Survey. Calving window: six days per annum. Strategic access assessment: control of northern transit. Recommendation: permanent closure following data retrieval.*

"They're not mapping a trail," he said slowly.

"No. They're mapping a door. One that opens six days a year, and one they intend to weld shut once they own the only map of it. Whoever holds this survey holds every way north through the range. Smugglers, refugees, anyone the state doesn't feel like letting pass." I shrugged, and hated how much my hands wanted to shake. "Or that's the theory. My brother believed it enough to die for it. Or vanish for it. The distinction has lost some meaning lately."

"And the water caches?"

"Because the government says nine men vanished without tracks, and governments lie, and my brother taught me surveyor's marking before he taught me to ride. If any of them are alive out there, they'll move along the old lines. They'll look for caches. And they'll find them." I met his eyes. "I've been leaving water in the high country for eleven months, Ranger. Call it madness if you want. Everyone else does."

* * *

Here is what I expected: arrest. Cuffs, a hard ride to the regional post, a cell, a trial where the papers would be entered as evidence against me and locked away forever, which is the same as destroyed, which is the same as Tomas dying twice.

Here is what happened instead: Blackwood folded the documents, handed them back to me, and stood up.

"Camp Two was resupplied four months ago," he said. "Standard rotation. The supply detail reported the site abandoned — gear intact, no sign of struggle, no tracks. Command wrote it up as equipment loss and moved on." He whistled his mule — a mean-eyed animal named Deacon who had tried to bite me twice during the chase, and whom I already respected enormously — and started walking north. "It's two days' ride. You clearly know the way better than the official maps do."

I stood there like an idiot. "Are you arresting me?"

"Do I look like I'm arresting you?"

"You look like a man walking toward a place he shouldn't go, which in my experience is either an arrest or a funeral, and I've attended more of the latter."

He stopped and half-turned, and for a moment the wry set of his mouth fell away and I saw what was underneath — fifteen years of a badge worn over a hollow, a man who had given the wilderness everything except the one thing he had left, and wasn't sure anymore why he'd kept it.

"The radio log from Camp Two had a transmission on it," he said. "Forty seconds, sent the night the team vanished. Coordinates, repeated three times, then nothing. Command classified it. I filed the paperwork myself and told myself it was above my pay grade." He looked north, at the white shoulders of the range rising out of the haze. "I've been telling myself things are above my pay grade for fifteen years, Ms. Voss. I'd like to see what happens when I stop."

"You realize I'm a wanted criminal."

"You realize I'm supposed to bring you in."

"We could do both. Very efficiently. There's a post south of here—"

"Two days' ride," he repeated, and kept walking.

I followed him. Of course I followed him. Not because I trusted him — trust is a currency I stopped carrying years ago — but because he was going where Tomas had gone, and because he'd looked at the words *permanent closure* and his face had done the thing honest faces do when they meet a lie big enough to swallow a career.

Also, he had Deacon, and Deacon was carrying most of the food.

* * *

We made the first night's camp in the lee of a basalt shelf, and I learned three things about Casper Blackwood before the fire burned down. One: he cooked like a man feeding a crew of ten, out of habit, and ate like a man who usually ate alone. Two: he asked questions the way surgeons cut — precisely, minimally, only as deep as necessary. Three: he hadn't asked me a single question about the thefts themselves. Not the how, not the where, not the six years of it.

"You're wondering why I haven't asked," he said, catching me staring.

"The thought crossed my mind."

"Because I don't care how you stole them." He fed a stick to the fire. "I care that the Director keeps classified strategic assessments in a personal safe instead of a command archive. That's not procedure. That's ownership. Somebody owns that route, and it isn't the public." He glanced at me across the flames. "Fifteen years, I've hauled thieves to posts and written my reports and gone to sleep fine. First time I ever caught one I wished I'd caught sooner."

"That's the strangest compliment anyone's ever given me."

"Don't get comfortable. If those papers turn out to be nothing — if your brother's team just walked off the job and you've built a conspiracy out of grief — I'm still taking you in. Grief explains a lot of things. It doesn't excuse them."

"Fair," I said, and meant it, and lay back on my bedroll looking up at more stars than the lowlands ever show you.

But sleep didn't come easy, and around midnight I heard him shift, and knew he wasn't sleeping either.

"My brother used to say the desert doesn't hide anything," I said to the dark. "It just waits for you to be ready to see it. He said that about the range too. Said there were places up there the maps got wrong on purpose."

Blackwood was quiet a long time. Then: "My first year, my captain told me the wilderness is the only honest witness left. Everything else can be bought." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "I've been trying to decide for three days whether that makes the wilderness honest or just expensive."

"Both. Like everything worth trusting."

Another silence, softer this time. Somewhere out in the dark, a coyote ran its ragged scale across the flats, and neither of us reached for a weapon, and that felt like a treaty being signed somewhere below the level of words.

* * *

We reached the bowl below Camp Two at midmorning on the second day, and I knew something was wrong before I saw the camp, because Deacon refused to go forward. Ears flat, nostrils working, planting all four feet like a mule-sized declaration of independence.

"She smells smoke," Blackwood said. "Old smoke. Days old."

"Nobody's camped up here. Nobody comes up here."

"Right." He dismounted and drew his sidearm, and I noticed he checked the load with the bored fluency of a man who did it forty times a day, and that his eyes had gone flat and professional. "Stay behind me."

"Ranger, I've been dodging people with guns since you were writing reports about me. I'll stay wherever I please."

"Behind me," he said again, but there was no order in it. Something closer to a request. Something closer, even, to worry.

We came over the last rise together, and there it was: Camp Two, tucked into the cirque below the glacier's white shoulder, tents collapsed and weather-grayed, the supply sled half-buried in drift. And the gear — I saw it before he said a word, and my mouth went dry. Crates stacked in perfect ranks. Ropes coiled in regulation loops. Tools laid out on a canvas square with geometric precision, each item squared to the next, like soldiers standing at attention.

No tracks. No tracks anywhere, though snow had fallen and partly melted and fallen again — plenty of surface to record a departure, an arrival, anything. The camp looked less abandoned than *arranged*. Set-dressed. A stage where the actors had been lifted straight up out of the scene.

And above it all, on a mast lashed to a supply crate, a radio antenna stood against the sky.

"It's transmitting," Blackwood said quietly. I hadn't heard him approach the receiver, hadn't seen him lift the headset, but he had it pressed to one ear and his face had gone the color of the ice above us. "Coordinates. On a loop. Same transmission as the log entry — it's been repeating for eleven months." He lowered the headset slowly. "Somebody's broadcasting a position into empty air, Marguerite. Either as a signal..."

"Or as bait," I finished.

The wind came off the glacier and moved through the dead camp, rattling a loose guy-line, shifting the perfect stacks of gear by fractions of inches. And from somewhere beneath our feet — beneath the snowfield at the cirque's edge, beneath the drift piled against the rock — came a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be.

A cough. Human. Close.

Then a voice, cracked and rusted with disuse: "If that's the retrieval team, we're not coming out. If it's anyone else — please. Please be anyone else."
