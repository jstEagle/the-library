## 3. Gear Without Tracks

The last mile to the survey camp, Marguerite stopped talking.

I want that on the record, because in the two days since I'd caught her at the dry wash — since I'd found her water caches and made the worst professional decision of my career by not putting cuffs on her — she had talked nearly without pause. About her brother Elias. About the documents she'd lifted from a district office in Meridian with a coat hanger and what she described as "an unreasonable amount of charm." About the glacier route the government was so eager to map and then bury. She talked the way water talks coming down a canyon wall: constant, insistent, wearing grooves into stone.

Then we crested the ridge above the camp, and she went quiet, and I understood that some silences are louder than anything a person can say.

The camp sat in a bowl below us, half-swallowed by snow. Four tents, or what remained of them — three collapsed under weather, one still standing at an angle like a drunk leaning on a fence post. A solar array lay face-down in the drifts. And between the tents, arranged on a tarp spread over the snow, was the gear.

I've worked wrecks. Plane crashes, flash floods, a bus off the Kestrel Grade that I still don't talk about. Wrecks scatter. That's the first law of disaster — entropy gets its hands in everything, throws packs fifty yards, scatters boots across a hillside, leaves a boot here and its owner's dentures there. People panic, or they die mid-motion, and either way the world comes apart messy.

This wasn't a wreck. This was a quartermaster's dream.

Sleeping bags rolled and stacked in ranks of four. Stoves nested inside their pots, pots inside their stuff sacks. Ice axes laid parallel, points aligned north — I checked, because I didn't believe it, and they were. Boots paired at the foot of each bedroll, laces tucked in. Even the food crates were squared to the tarp's edge, labels facing out.

"Nobody does this," Marguerite said. Her voice had gone flat, careful. "Not civilians. Not scientists. Scientists are worse than civilians."

"Military does it," I said.

"You think soldiers walked out of here?"

"I don't know what I think." Which was true, and which I hated. Fifteen years of reading ground — every track, every broken twig, every place a body had pressed against a slope — and this ground was telling me nothing at all. "That's the problem."

We came down into the bowl slow, the way you approach anything that might be a crime scene or might be worse. The snow around the camp was unbroken. Not thin unbroken, not wind-healed unbroken — *pristine*. Six inches of fresh powder at least, maybe eight hours old, but beneath it the crust should have held the memory of every boot that had ever crossed it, and there was nothing. No tracks leaving. No tracks arriving. It was as if the four surveyors had been beamed up, and whoever did the beaming had tidied up first out of professional courtesy.

Except the radio was warm.

I found it in the standing tent, clipped to a folding table, transmitting. Red light steady, antenna up, and when I put my palm flat against the case it carried heat like a living thing. I keyed the mic. Static answered, then a tone, then static again. Somewhere out past the edge of hearing, something was receiving coordinates from a dead man's camp, and whatever it was, it hadn't gotten bored yet.

"Casper." Marguerite was at the tent flap, and her voice had changed. Lower. "You need to see this."

I need to see a lot of things, is what I thought. What I said was nothing, because I'd already seen it.

They were coming out of the snow.

At the base of the slope behind the camp, where a rock overhang met the drift, the snow itself opened — no, not opened, *unsealed*, a wall of packed blocks swinging aside on a hinge of canvas and ski poles — and two people stood up out of the mountain like the mountain had decided, after nine days, to give them back.

The first was a tall man with a graying beard crusted white and eyes that took in me, my badge, my sidearm, and Marguerite in about a second and a half, cataloguing all of it with the calm of someone who had recently stopped being afraid of anything smaller than the glacier. The second was a woman, small, dark-haired, moving with the deliberate economy of somebody conserving calories she couldn't spare. She had a med kit slung across her chest like a bandolier and frostbite silvering the tips of two fingers.

"Ranger," the tall man said. Not a question. He'd read the badge too. "Idris Okafor. Survey team lead. This is Sana Reyes, our medic. You're late."

"We weren't on the manifest," I said.

"No," Okafor agreed. "Nobody was. That was rather the point."

Sana Reyes crossed the camp without looking at any of the neat stacks, went straight to the food crate, and began counting cans with her lips moving. Priorities. I liked her immediately.

Marguerite hadn't moved. She was staring at Okafor the way you stare at a door you'd stopped believing would open. "Elias Voss," she said. "He was on your team."

Something shifted in Okafor's face — not grief exactly, more like grief being set aside for later, in a drawer he clearly kept well organized. "Yes."

"Where is he?"

Okafor looked at the stacked gear. At the unbroken snow. At the radio, still murmuring its coordinates to nobody. When he spoke again his voice was quieter and much harder.

"That's the question, isn't it. Nine days ago a helicopter landed here that wasn't ours. No flight plan, no markings, no engine noise until it was already on the ground — which, if you know helicopters, should interest you. Four men got out. They were very polite. They asked for the survey data. Elias told them the data was encrypted and the keys were distributed." He paused. "Elias was the most careful man I ever worked with. He'd have hidden those keys somewhere clever."

"And?" Marguerite's hands had closed into fists at her sides.

"And they took him and Tomás Brandt back up into that helicopter, and it left without a sound, and Sana and I crawled into the snow cave because Idris Okafor has been in enough government operations to know that polite men in unmarked helicopters come back for the rest of the inventory." He gestured at the tarp, the ranked sleeping bags, the aligned ice axes. "So we packed it. All of it. Neat. Because they're not going to believe survivors did that, and they're not going to look for survivors who'd leave gear like that. We let the camp tell the story they wanted to hear: extraction complete, site clean, nothing human left."

"The radio," I said.

"Sana's idea." For the first time, something almost like a smile touched his mouth, and it was not a comforting smile. "It transmits the camp's coordinates on a loop. Officially, it means the site is still active and monitored. Unofficially—" he nodded upward, at the gray sky, at the empty air "—it means anyone flying in to finish the job knows exactly where we are. And it means we always know how long we have before they decide to come look."

"How long do we have now?" Sana asked from the food crate, not looking up. She'd finished counting. "Because we're down to eleven days of rations for two, which is five and a half days for four, and I'm assuming you two didn't pack for a glacier crossing."

I looked at Marguerite. She looked at me. Two days ago I'd had her dead to rights at a dry wash, theft warrant in my pocket, fifteen years of procedure telling me exactly what came next. Procedure had nothing to say about this. There was no form for *the missing persons are hiding from the people who reported them missing*.

"The route opens in six days," Marguerite said quietly. "Calving season. Elias's notes said six days, and after that the pass seals for the winter."

"Elias's notes," Okafor repeated. His eyes came up and fixed on her, and I watched him do the arithmetic — the name Voss, the stolen documents, the thief standing in his ruined camp wearing a ranger's reluctant protection. "You're his sister."

"I'm the reason you're getting out of here," she said. "If you want to be."

Sana stood, brushed snow off her knees, and looked at each of us in turn with the flat appraisal of someone triaging a situation instead of patients. "For the record," she said, "I vote we go. Whatever 'go' means. Because I've spent nine days in a hole in the snow listening to that radio, and yesterday morning it changed."

I felt the cold walk up my spine ahead of her words.

"Changed how?"

"It stopped sending the camp coordinates." Sana nodded at the transmitter, red light pulsing, patient as a heartbeat. "Now it's sending coordinates for the far side of the glacier. Somebody reprogrammed our loop, Ranger. Somebody wants everybody at the calving fields — us, you, her, and whoever's listening. Six days from now, at the ice."

* * *

That night we broke camp in the dark, and I understood, packing the last of the neat stacks into four packs instead of four graves, that I had stopped being a ranger somewhere between the dry wash and the unbroken snow. What I was now was undecided. But the dread I'd carried since the first footprint-less drift had hardened into something with edges, something I could hold.

Resolve, maybe. Or just the certain knowledge that in six days, on the ice, we'd finally meet whatever had erased those tracks.

And it would be expecting us.
