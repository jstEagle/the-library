## 4. The Six Days

Idris Okafor made tea the way other men defused bombs — with total concentration and a kind of grim tenderness, as though the water might go off if he looked away. He'd scavenged a burner from the wrecked camp stove, found three chipped enamel cups in a crate marked *FRAGILE — OPTICS*, and set them out on a flat stone like an offering.

"Four cups," Sana said from the mouth of the snow cave. "There are four of us."

"I counted," Idris said. "I always count."

Marguerite sat on a supply crate with her wrists still remembering the weight of Casper's handcuffs, which he'd taken off two hours ago without apology or explanation, and which she had decided not to read too much into. Casper stood at the edge of the camp with his back to all of them, watching the valley the way a man watches a door he's not sure is locked.

She'd known brothers who talked too much and brothers who didn't talk enough, and Tomas had been both at once — silent for weeks, then pouring out everything in one flood when it finally broke loose. She wondered if Idris was like that. She wondered what it would take to break him loose.

The tea, when it came, tasted like pine needles and tin. It was the best thing she'd ever drunk.

* * *

"So," Idris said, settling across the fire from her with his cup balanced on his knee. "You want to know why they left."

"I want to know why you're alive."

"That's the same question." He smiled, but it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "We were six days into the survey when Sana found the anomaly. The glacier — the Meridian, we called it, though that's not its real name, the government never names things honestly — the Meridian calves every spring. Big calving season, ice shelf lets go, the pass through the range opens up for about a week. Maybe less. Six days, on average. Then it seals again until next year."

"A week," Marguerite said. "Out of a year."

"A week." Idris held up his hand, fingers spread. "Six days where the northern passage is open to anyone on foot. No roads, no checkpoints, no tolls. Just ice, and a window to cross it. For six days, the Redline Range has a door in it, and anyone can walk through."

Casper turned around slowly. "And the rest of the year?"

"The rest of the year it's a wall of ice three hundred meters thick. Impassable. Always has been." Idris sipped his tea. "Except our survey wasn't mapping the route. That's what they told us. Map the pass, chart the calving schedule, establish safe crossing windows for civilian use. That's what the briefing said." He reached into his coat and pulled out a data slate, cracked across one corner, wrapped in a wool sock like something newborn. "This is what the data actually says."

He set it on the stone between them.

"What does it say?" Casper asked.

"It says the calving isn't natural anymore." Idris's voice went very quiet, the way voices do when they're carrying something heavy. "There's a fault line under the eastern shelf — a compression fracture, running the whole length of the glacier. Natural. Old. Harmless, unless you know exactly where it is and exactly how to stress it. Our instruments mapped it to within two meters because that's what we were told to do. Chart the fracture. Measure the load tolerances. Establish detonation points." He laughed, short and awful. "They used the word *stabilization*. Eleven times in the mission brief. Stabilization points. You don't stabilize a glacier by drilling into its fault line, Ranger. You destabilize it."

Marguerite felt cold that had nothing to do with altitude. "They're going to collapse it."

"They're going to collapse it *permanently*." Idris nodded. "Seal the pass for good. No more calving season, no more six-day window, no more northern crossing on foot. Ever. And once the route's gone, there's exactly one way north — the government corridor, the toll road, the checkpoint chain. Everything moves through their gates or it doesn't move at all. Every trader, every refugee, every family trying to get across the range. They won't own the north. They'll just own every road to it."

The fire popped. Somewhere above the camp, wind moved through the scree with a sound like slow breathing.

"And the survey team," Casper said. His voice was flat, careful, a man walking on ice he didn't trust. "Your team. They finished the survey?"

"They finished it." Idris looked down at his cup. "Three days ago. We transmitted the final data package back to base, and then—" He stopped. Started again. "Then the orders changed. New directive, encrypted, priority black. We were instructed to remain in place. To await a retrieval team. Sana picked up the transmission traffic afterward — there were helicopters, Ranger. Military birds, running dark, circling the valley for two nights. Not looking for us. Looking for *witnesses*."

"They were going to kill you," Marguerite said. It wasn't a question.

"We hid in the cave." Sana spoke for the first time, her voice hoarse from cold and disuse. "Six of us went in. Four of us came out." She didn't elaborate, and nobody asked her to. "The others went back to camp to break down the equipment, make it look like we'd gone over the western ridge. We heard the shots from inside the ice."

Marguerite closed her eyes. Tomas. Her brother, who wrote letters full of terrible jokes and sent her pressed flowers from survey sites, who had joined the corps because he believed maps were a form of honesty. *Somebody has to draw the world true, Margot, or the liars win by default.*

He'd drawn this map true. And they'd killed him for it.

"There's a copy of the data," she said, opening her eyes. "On that slate. Is that the only one?"

"The master package went back to base before we understood what we'd done," Idris said. "But the raw files, the field notes, the fracture analysis — it's all here. And yes. As far as we know, it's the only copy that hasn't been sanitized."

"Then it's evidence," Casper said.

Everyone looked at him.

"It's evidence," he repeated, slower, testing each word like a rope bridge. "Chain of custody, sworn statements, a formal inquiry. You bring that to the territorial office—"

"To *your* office," Marguerite said. "The office that sent you after me. The office that dispatched military helicopters to a survey site and shot four cartographers. You'd walk that slate through the front door of the building that ordered it?" She shook her head. "Ranger. Be serious."

"I took an oath."

"You took a job." She stood, and the cold air rushed into the space where her anger had been sitting. "There's a difference. An oath is a promise you make to people. A job is a promise you make to a paycheck. And somebody signed off on those helicopters, and I promise you they collect the same paycheck you do."

Casper's jaw worked. He looked at the fire, at the slate, at the snow cave where two people had hidden for three days listening to their friends die. When he spoke again, his voice had lost some of its certainty, which was more than she'd expected from him.

"Then what's your plan, Voss? Burn it? Destroy the only proof?"

"No." She crouched by the stone, by the slate in its wool sock, and put her hand flat on top of it. Under her palm: her brother's last work. His instruments, his measurements, his careful honest numbers. "We carry it out ourselves. All of it. Through the pass."

Silence.

"You want to cross the Meridian," Casper said.

"I want to cross during the window. Six days, Okafor said. The calving opens the route, and while it's open, we take this data north — past the corridor, past the checkpoints, to the free press in Halvard. There are journalists there who've been asking questions about the northern corridor for a year. Give them this, and the collapse order dies in daylight. The government can't seal a pass the whole world is watching."

"And if the window closes early?" Sana asked quietly. "If the ice seals while we're on it?"

Marguerite met her eyes. "Then we'd better be fast."

* * *

They voted, because Idris insisted — said something about how after what they'd survived, nobody got ordered anywhere. Four people around a dying fire, voting on whether to walk into a glacier.

Sana voted yes first. "I'm a medic," she said. "I patch people the state breaks. This is the same work, bigger scale." She said it steadily, but her hands were shaking around her cup, and Marguerite wanted to hold them and didn't.

Idris voted yes second. "Tomas would have carried it himself," he said simply, and Marguerite's throat closed so hard she couldn't nod, so she just gripped his shoulder and he covered her hand with his own, briefly, like sealing a pact.

Then it was Casper.

He stood apart from the fire, badge catching the last light, fifteen years of service hanging off him like a wet coat. Marguerite watched him wrestle with it — watched the exact moment the argument about oaths and jobs lost, though she couldn't tell you what won. Duty, maybe. But duty pointed somewhere new now.

"Yes," he said. "But we do it my way on the ice. Roped teams, fixed pace, nobody wanders. Glacier travel kills amateurs, and I've pulled bodies out of crevasses for fifteen years." He looked at her, and something passed between them that wasn't trust yet but was at least its skeleton. "You follow my calls, Voss. On the ice, I'm in charge."

"On the ice," she agreed. "Everywhere else, we argue."

"That's already happening."

"Good. It keeps you honest."

Sana snorted. Idris almost smiled. The fire settled into embers, and above them the peaks went from gold to violet to a blue so deep it looked permanent, and somewhere beyond that blue lay a wall of ice with a door in it, opening soon, for six days only.

Marguerite lay awake that night in the snow cave with the slate against her chest, listening to the glacier groan in the dark — long, tectonic sounds, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Getting ready to let go.

*Get ready,* she told it silently. *We're coming. And we're bringing the truth with us.*

At dawn, Casper shook her shoulder, and his face in the grey light told her everything before he said a word.

"The window's open early," he said. "Ice is moving. We leave in an hour."
