## 5. White Silence

The Redline Range doesn't announce its weather. It just changes its mind.

We'd made camp at the tree line the night before, four people and a duffel of stolen government secrets, and by morning the sky had gone the color of old tin. I'd seen that color maybe two hundred times in fifteen years of rangering, and every time it had meant the same thing: whatever you were planning, unplan it.

"We go anyway," Marguerite said, tightening her bootlaces like she was strangling something small.

"Of course we do," I said. "I just wanted it on record that I objected."

Idris Okafor looked up from the survey maps he'd been studying by lamplight, his face hollowed out by three weeks of eating snow cave rations. "On record where? You're the only one keeping records anymore."

He had me there. The badge on my belt was the last official document left in this expedition, and I was starting to suspect it wouldn't survive the trip either.

Sana Reyes laughed — a small, cracked sound, but a laugh. Three weeks buried alive will do that to a person's sense of humor; it either dies or gets strange. Hers had gotten strange, and I found I liked it. "Ranger Blackwood objects," she said, mimicking my flat desert drawl. "Noted for the tribunal."

"There won't be a tribunal," I said.

"That's what worries me," said Idris, and folded the maps away.

* * *

The first day of the climb was honest work. Switchbacks through scree, then old snow packed hard enough to hold an edge, then the glacier's tongue reaching down between two shoulders of black rock like something the mountain had coughed up and regretted. Marguerite moved through it the way water moves — no wasted motion, no complaint, always half a step ahead as if the route were a rumor she'd heard first.

I kept the rear, which is where you put the person who knows the mountain and the person you don't entirely trust, and in my case those were the same job.

"You're staring at my back," she called over her shoulder at one point.

"I'm staring at the slope behind your back. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Fifteen years' worth."

She smiled at that, quick and crooked, and didn't turn around fast enough to hide it. I told myself the warmth in my chest was altitude. Fifteen years alone in the high country teaches you to misfile your own symptoms.

By afternoon the tin sky had curdled into actual weather. It came in sideways off the northern faces, not snow so much as the idea of snow — a white static that erased the world in horizontal bands. Within twenty minutes I couldn't see Sana ten feet ahead of me. Within forty I couldn't see anything except the rope in my hands and the ghost-shape of Idris ahead of her, and the small, stubborn rhythm of their boots that I had to trust because I could no longer verify it.

This is the part they don't put in the recruitment brochures: whiteout isn't darkness. Darkness at least has the decency to be honest. Whiteout lies to you constantly. It tells you the ground has ended. It tells you the ground is fine. It tells you the figure stumbling toward you is your friend when it might be a cornice edge, or nothing at all, or your own reflection in all that blind white, come back to ask what exactly you think you're doing up here at fifty-three with a dead career and a live thief.

I roped us tighter and kept counting steps. Four hundred. Six hundred. Somewhere past a thousand, the wind dropped without warning, the way it does, like a door closing, and the silence that followed was so total I could hear my own pulse ticking in my ears.

"Camp," Marguerite's voice came back down the line, muffled to almost nothing. "Wall of ice. We're digging in."

* * *

We dug into the lee of a serac wall, a blue cliff of ancient ice that hummed faintly if you pressed your ear against it, like a struck tuning fork nobody could hear. Sana got the stove going inside the windbreak while the rest of us cut blocks, and for one hour the world contracted to something almost bearable: four people, a flame, the tick of cooling ice.

It was Idris who broke it.

"The helicopter," he said. He wasn't looking at the sky. He was looking at his hands. "When they flew over the camp — after we went into the cave — it circled twice. Twice. Then it left."

"Search patterns are grid-based," I said. "Maybe—"

"I know search patterns, Ranger. I wrote half the protocols." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "That wasn't a search. That was a confirmation."

Nobody answered. The stove hissed. Sana reached over without looking and put her hand on top of his, and he turned his palm up to hold it, and I watched two people who had been abandoned by their own government perform the small mechanics of comfort anyway, because that's what people do, and something in my chest shifted a quarter-inch sideways and did not shift back.

Fifteen years, I'd believed the service was clumsy but decent. Slow, bureaucratic, occasionally cruel by accident — but never on purpose. You can work with clumsy. You can't work with on purpose.

I didn't believe it yet. Not fully. But the crack was there now, hairline, running through the foundation, and I could hear it if I listened.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, which in the Redlines is not good news — it means the storm is reloading. We moved early and moved fast, onto the glacier proper, following a line Marguerite had memorized from her brother's notes. Elias Voss. I still hadn't asked her much about him. Some doors you wait for the other person to open.

The crevasse field began an hour in, invisible under the season's new snow — bridges of white spanning gaps of blue-black nothing, holding until they didn't. I went first, probing with the axe, calling the safe line back over my shoulder. Left of the pressure ridge. Two meters right of the sagging bridge. The whole time, that white silence pressing down, broken only by our breathing and the occasional deep groan of the glacier shifting its weight, like an old man turning over in bed.

Sana was crossing a bridge I'd already tested when it decided it had lied to me.

The sound was wrong first — a soft whump, like a book falling shut — and then the snow under her simply stopped existing. She dropped to her waist, arms pinwheeling, and the rope snapped taut around my waist and hauled me two staggering steps toward the hole before I threw myself flat and drove the axe in.

"Anchor!" I shouted. "Marguerite — anchor!"

She was already moving. I saw her boot stamp the pick of her axe through the crust behind me, saw Idris belly-crawl to a second placement, and then it was just me and the rope burning across my forearms and Sana Reyes hanging in the dark below my boots, screaming once, then going quiet in a way that frightened me more than the screaming.

"Sana! Talk to me."

"I'm — okay. I'm okay. My harness—" A pause. A horrible pause. "My axe is gone. It's on the surface. I can see it. I can't reach it."

Below her boots was three hundred feet of blue dark. Above her was six inches of snow bridge that had already proven itself a liar.

"Blackwood." Marguerite's voice, close to my ear, stripped of everything wry. "Tell me what you need."

"Get Idris on the second anchor. You take my pack. I'm going down the rope to her."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly the protocol. Section nine. I wrote section nine." And then, because the crack in my faith ran both directions, I added: "Somebody should get some use out of it."

I fed myself down the rope hand over hand, boots against the crevasse wall, into the cold that pours upward out of the ice like the breath of something sleeping. Sana hung ten feet below me, gray-lipped, arms crossed tight, conserving heat the way medics are trained to. When I reached her she looked at me with eyes too calm for the situation.

"Took you long enough, Ranger."

"Had to finish my coffee."

I clipped her to me, took her weight on my harness, and shouted the haul signal up the rope. Then came the part no protocol covers: the long, stupid, human labor of rising. Marguerite and Idris hauling above, me walking my boots up the wall with another human being strapped to my chest, her breath fogging my chin, the whole rope system held together by knots I'd tied and axes I'd placed and two people I'd known for five days deciding, without discussion, not to let go.

We came over the lip like something being born backwards. Sana crawled clear, shook, laughed her strange laugh, and cried for about ninety seconds, and then stood up and checked everyone else for frostbite, because that was who she was.

Marguerite crouched beside me at the edge of the broken bridge, looking down into the gap where Sana had been. Her hands were shaking. She noticed me noticing and didn't bother hiding it.

"You went down the rope," she said.

"Somebody had to."

"You didn't hesitate."

"Fifteen years of practice hesitating. I've gotten fast at it."

She looked at me then — really looked, the way she had at the dry wash, back when she was a mystery instead of a co-conspirator — and whatever she saw made her nod slowly, like a verdict being entered.

* * *

It was mid-afternoon when we heard it.

The helicopter came up the valley behind us, low, riding the glacier's wind, and I knew the airframe before I knew anything else — government recovery, twin rotor, the same model that had lifted me out of the burn zone on Miller Ridge eight years ago when the service spent eleven hours hovering in worse weather than this to pull one injured ranger off a rock slab. Eleven hours. I'd lain there watching that aircraft fight the storm for me and thought: this is what the badge means. This is what it's for.

We waved. All four of us, standing in open snow, waving like idiots. Idris fired a flare that arced red against the gray.

The helicopter passed directly overhead. Close enough that the rotor wash flattened the snow around us in a white ring. Close enough that I could see the silhouette of someone in the cockpit turning their head — not toward us. Toward the route ahead. Mapping it.

Then it was gone up-valley, and the sound faded, and the white silence closed back over the four of us like water.

"They saw us," Sana said finally. Her voice was very small. "They had to have seen us."

"Yes," I said.

"Then why—"

"Because we're not the mission anymore." I heard myself say it flatly, the way I'd say a trail report, and hated how easily it came. "We're loose ends. They're confirming the route for the closure team. We were never on the list to be recovered. We were on the list to be *unaccounted for*."

Idris sat down heavily in the snow. Sana put her arm through his. Marguerite watched me, and I watched the valley the helicopter had gone up, and somewhere in my chest that hairline crack widened into something I could no longer pretend was structural soundness.

Fifteen years. Eleven hours in a storm for one man. And today, four living people waving in open snow, passed over at two hundred feet.

"Ranger," Marguerite said quietly. It wasn't mockery. It was almost gentle. "What are you going to do?"

And the honest answer — the answer I'd been circling since the dry wash, since the stacked gear, since the radio transmitting coordinates into empty air — was that I didn't know yet. But I knew which direction the question pointed, and it wasn't back down the mountain.

Ahead of us, the glacier climbed toward the calving fields, and the pass would open in three days, and the only copies of the survey that mattered were zipped into the bag on Marguerite's back.

Three days to decide what the badge was for.

Two, if the weather had opinions.
