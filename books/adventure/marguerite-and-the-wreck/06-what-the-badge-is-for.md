## 6. What the Badge Is For

The fire lookout had been abandoned so long that the Forest Service had forgotten to tear it down, which in my experience is the only way anything survives out here. It stood on a granite shoulder above the tree line like a lighthouse for a sea of dust, its windows fogged with forty years of grime, its stairs missing every third step as if the mountain itself were playing a practical joke on anyone desperate enough to climb.

We were that desperate.

"Mind the gap," I said, hopping the last broken stair. "And the one after it. And the general concept of infrastructure."

Behind me, Sana Reyes laughed — a small, cracked sound, the first laugh since the crevasse. She was moving better now, her frost-nipped hand wrapped in gauze Idris had scavenged from the camp's med kit two days and one lifetime ago. Casper came up last, the way he always did, watching our backtrail out of habit even though the storm had erased it so thoroughly that we could have been walking on the moon.

Inside, the lookout was a single room with a rusted Osborne fire finder squatting in the center like an altar to a dead religion. Somebody had left a cot frame, a wood stove, and a coffee can full of what I chose to believe was once pencils. Idris set his pack down, pulled out the survey drive and the battered field terminal he'd been cradling like a sick bird since the camp, and said, "I need three hours and nobody talking to me."

"You've had three days of nobody talking to you," Sana said.

"And I've enjoyed every minute," Idris said, already hunched over the screen. "But this encryption isn't standard. It's not survey-grade either. It's—" He stopped. His fingers kept moving, but his face did something complicated.

"What?" I asked.

"Nothing. Three hours."

That *nothing* had a shape I recognized. My brother Theo used to make that face over his field notes right before he found something the funding agencies would have preferred he hadn't. I let it go. There were more immediate problems, namely: cold, dark, and the man by the window who had spent four days deciding whether I was a suspect or a companion and still hadn't filed the paperwork either way.

* * *

Casper built a fire in the stove with the economy of a man who'd been warming himself on other people's mistakes for fifteen years. While it caught, he cleaned his sidearm, which I'd learned was less about maintenance and more about meditation. The badge came off his belt last — he polished it with his sleeve, absently, the way you'd touch a scar.

"That thing mean something to you?" I asked. "Or is it just jewelry?"

"It means I get to be where I am," he said. "Fifteen years, that badge has opened every door between here and the state line. Search-and-rescue calls. Warrants. A bed at every station house in the Redlines." He turned it over. "You'd be surprised how much of the world runs on a piece of tin."

"I wouldn't. I've spent my whole life on the other side of those doors."

"Then you know they lock from both directions."

I sat down across the stove from him, close enough to feel the heat, far enough to keep the argument honest. "Here's what I don't understand, Ranger. You cornered me at the dry wash with your hand on your cuffs. You had me. Stolen federal documents, confessed thief, case closed, promotion pending. And instead you walked into the wilderness with me chasing ghosts. So either you're the worst lawman in the territory, or—"

"Or?"

"Or the law stopped fitting somewhere along the way, and you haven't told anyone yet."

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried the old shutters like a dog nosing at a door. "The law isn't supposed to fit," he said finally. "That's the point of it. It fits everybody the same, or it fits nobody. You don't get to bend it because your brother—"

"My brother is *missing*, Casper. Along with six other people whose government sent them up a glacier to draw pretty lines, and then sent exactly one ranger after the documents instead of after them. Don't lecture me about the law until you can tell me what it was doing while they were dying."

He didn't answer. That was answer enough, and we both knew it, and the silence between us got heavy enough to have a temperature.

Sana broke it, because Sana always broke things gently before they shattered. "For what it's worth," she said from the cot, "the law fed me for six years too. EMT license, state certification, all of it. And when I radioed for evacuation from that camp, do you know what dispatch said?" Her voice stayed level in the way voices do when they're holding something down. "'Stand by.' Four days of stand by. So maybe the doors open both ways, Ranger, but somebody's holding them shut."

Idris cleared his throat from the terminal. "I'm through the outer layer."

* * *

What he found took him another hour to say out loud, and when he finally turned the screen around, none of us spoke for a long time.

"It's not a route survey," Idris said. "I mean, it is — there are elevation profiles, ice cores, calving projections, all legitimate science, all Theo's work. But it's nested inside a second dataset. Military classification headers. Look at the deliverables tab."

I looked. I wish I hadn't. *Structural vulnerability assessment. Controlled demolition feasibility. Post-collapse passage denial — northern corridor.* Line items. Budget codes. A schedule with our calving season circled in it like a target.

"They weren't mapping the pass," Sana whispered. "They were pricing out how to kill it."

"The calving season opens the route for six days a year," Idris said, in the flat voice of a man reading his own brother's autopsy. "Six days when anyone — refugees, smugglers, people fleeing the drought zones south of here — can cross north without going through a checkpoint. The survey gives them everything they need to drop the serac fields and seal it permanently. Permanently, and deniably. 'Natural calving event.' Nobody investigates weather."

My hands were shaking. Not with fear. With the specific fury of being proven right too late. "Theo knew. He must have found the second dataset partway through. That's why he hid copies in the water caches. That's why he marked the route for me." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "He trusted me to finish his homework and I nearly got arrested for it by the world's most conflicted park ranger."

"Margo." Idris reached over and put his hand over mine. His fingers were cold and steady. "Theo talked about you the whole expedition. 'My sister will come find this. My sister never leaves a door closed.'" His voice cracked on the last word. "He wasn't wrong."

I looked away because looking at Idris looking at me was more than I could carry and keep standing.

Which is why I was facing the window when Casper finally said it.

"I wasn't sent to rescue anyone."

The room went very quiet. Even the stove seemed to burn more carefully.

"Three days before I picked up your trail," he went on, staring at the badge in his hands like it had started talking, "my supervisor handed me a sealed directive. Priority one: recover the survey data. Priority two: locate and detain the civilian in possession of stolen materials." He didn't look at me. "There was no priority three. No mention of seven missing surveyors. When I asked, I was told search operations were, quote, 'being handled through other channels.'"

"Other channels," Sana repeated. "Like the helicopter."

"Like the helicopter," Casper said. "I told myself the recovery team would reach the camp before I did. I told myself I was following procedure. Then I found your caches, Marguerite, and I realized you were following the same trail I was, for the same reason, without a badge or backup or permission — and I kept telling myself stories all the way up the mountain." He set the badge on the table between us, face down. "I was sent to retrieve the maps and bury the question. And I helped bury it, every day I didn't say this out loud. I'm sorry. To all of you. That's the truth of what the badge was for. It was for carrying orders I should have burned."

The betrayal landed exactly where he'd aimed it — in me, in the place where I'd started, against every rule I had, to trust him. Four days of shared water and shared watches and the crevasse where he'd held the rope until his palms bled, and all of it built on a lie of omission so large you could have surveyed it.

I could have said any number of true and cutting things. *You lied. You're no better than them. You were going to arrest me.*

Instead I heard myself say: "Did you decide before the lookout, or during?"

He blinked. "What?"

"The arrest. Me, in cuffs, the data in your pack, case closed. At some point on this mountain you decided not to. I want to know when."

Casper was silent for a moment. "At the dry wash," he said. "When I saw the water caches. A thief doesn't leave water for strangers. I knew then that whatever I'd been sent to stop, it wasn't a crime." He exhaled. "I just didn't have the courage to say it until someone else decrypted it first."

"Well," I said, and my voice only shook a little, "welcome to the resistance. We meet irregularly, we're all officially dead or soon to be, and the dues are terrible."

Sana snorted. Idris smiled for the first time since the camp. And Casper — Casper looked at me across the stove with an expression I couldn't file under any charge I knew, and said, quietly, "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," I said. "You still owe me an apology with feeling. Preferably somewhere warm."

* * *

We slept in shifts. I took first watch by the window, watching stars wheel over the range, listening to the others breathe — really breathe, the way people breathe when they've stopped bracing. Somewhere below us, thirty miles of glacier waited with its six-day door cracked open, and somewhere behind us, men with budget codes were waiting for their demolition homework.

Near midnight, Idris sat up abruptly in the dark.

"Margo." His voice was strange. "The encrypted layer — there's a final file. Timestamped the night the team vanished. It's addressed to the project lead, but it's flagged... it's flagged *personal*." He swallowed. "It's from Theo. And Margo — it says they didn't vanish. It says they *ran*. All seven of them, together, the night they read the second dataset." He turned the screen toward me, and the glow lit his face like something holy and terrible.

"And there's a map. Theo drew a route off the far side of the glacier. One that isn't on any government chart." He met my eyes. "It ends at a place he labeled — in his own handwriting — '*the wreck.*'"

Down by the stove, Casper was already pulling his boots on.
