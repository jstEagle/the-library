## 7. The Calving Fields

The glacier announced itself before we saw it — a sound like a freight train clearing its throat somewhere under the world, deep enough that I felt it in my boot soles and my back teeth. Fifteen years in the Redline Range and I'd heard avalanches, rockfall, the groan of frozen lakes at midnight, but nothing that sounded like this. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"That's it," Marguerite said, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before. Not fear exactly. Recognition.

We'd come up out of the tree line two days ago, following Idris's decrypted coordinates through country that got meaner with every switchback. Sana had stopped limping on day one of the climb, which should have reassured me and didn't — she'd simply decided the mountain wasn't going to get an apology out of her, and she was going to pretend her ribs didn't matter until they did. Idris walked like a man reading a book only he could see, muttering elevations and bearings, and Marguerite moved the way water moves downhill, always finding the line of least resistance without seeming to look for it.

And me. I brought up the rear, because that's where rangers walk, and because from behind you can watch people lie to themselves about how tired they are.

* * *

The calving fields opened up below us like a broken mirror the size of a county. Ice, blue-white and ancient, fractured into towers and ridges and crevasses wide enough to swallow a truck. The seracs stood along the upper margins like a jury that hadn't reached a verdict — leaning blocks of ice the size of buildings, held up by nothing but habit and cold.

"Six days," Idris said, checking his watch against the sun. "Season opened this morning. We have until—" he did the math with his fingers, lips moving, "—four days, eleven hours, give or take."

"Give or take what?" Sana asked.

"The glacier's mood."

"Wonderful," she said. "A moody glacier."

Marguerite was already scanning the route down onto the ice, and I watched her do it — watched the way her whole body went quiet when she was working a problem. She'd been quiet a lot since the fire lookout, since Casper Blackwood confessed he'd been sent to fetch paper, not people. She hadn't forgiven me out loud. She'd just kept walking beside me, which I was learning was how she forgave things: by refusing to leave.

"We rope up," I said. "Thirty feet between us. Nobody steps where the person ahead didn't step. And nobody—"

"—stops to take pictures of the pretty ice," Sana finished. "You've mentioned. Twice."

"I'll mention it again if it keeps working."

Idris laughed — actually laughed, a short surprised bark of it — and for a second we were just four idiots standing on a ridge looking at a death trap, and it felt almost like something else. Almost like the survey crews I used to escort, back when my job was protecting people instead of retrieving documents. Almost like family, if family was something you assembled out of strays in the middle of nowhere.

I didn't say any of that out loud. Rangers don't. But I thought it, and thinking it scared me more than the seracs did, because things you care about are things you can lose.

* * *

The ice talked the whole way across.

Not metaphorically. It popped and ticked and groaned under our boots, meltwater running somewhere beneath us in channels we couldn't see, and every few minutes one of the big seracs would shift with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat. Idris called them by name as we passed — he'd helped map this route, after all, before he became a man who lived in snow caves. "That one's the Bishop. That one's the Widow. Don't ask why."

"Why is it called the Widow?" Sana asked.

"Because of what happened to the last three people who asked."

Marguerite snorted. Even that small sound did something to my chest I wasn't prepared to examine.

We made good time for three hours. Good enough that I let myself believe the worst part would be the walking. That's the mistake I always make — believing the worst part is behind me. Fifteen years of service and I still hadn't learned that the worst part is always the part you haven't met yet.

It came at the far side of the calving field, where the glacier narrowed between two walls of blue ice, and it came not from the mountain at all.

"Down," I said, and I don't know what told me — maybe movement in the rocks above the far lip, maybe just fifteen years of being hunted and hunting — but I said it and dropped flat and the others followed, and half a heartbeat later the crack of a rifle rolled down the glacier like a thrown stone.

The bullet hit the ice where Marguerite had been standing and threw up a geyser of chips.

"Retrieval team," Idris breathed. "They found us."

"They followed the radio," Marguerite said, and her voice had gone flat and cold, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic with lives. "Or they've been shadowing us since the lookout."

Both, probably. Government work is thorough even when it's evil. Especially when it's evil.

Another shot. Then a third, from a different angle, and I understood the shape of it: they weren't trying to kill us. They were herding us. Pushing us off the safe line and toward the narrow ground, the crevasse field, the place where the ice would do their work for them and leave no awkward bodies for the news.

"They want us in the fractures," I said.

"Then we don't go to the fractures." Marguerite lifted her head just enough to look. "There's a route left. Along the base of the Widow. It's exposed to the serac, but it's fast."

"It's exposed to the serac," I repeated.

"I heard myself."

The Widow chose that moment to groan — long and low, a sound with weight behind it, the sound of several thousand tons of ice reconsidering its position. Sana looked at it, then at the rifle cracks coming from the rocks, then at the four of us lying in the shadow of a pressure ridge, and did something I will remember for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be.

She stood up into the open.

"Go," she said, and pulled her sidearm — Idris's old survey flare gun, actually, but held like it meant something — and started firing flares up at the rocks above us, white stars bursting against the gray sky, screaming and waving her arms like the whole glacier was hers to command. "GO! I've got their eyes! MOVE!"

"Sana—" Idris was already reaching for her.

"She can't outrun them and neither can we, not all together!" Marguerite had Idris by the pack strap, hauling him left, toward the Widow. "Casper!"

Everything in me wanted to stay. Everything in me — the ranger, the man, whatever was left after both of those — screamed to go get the medic and carry her out over my shoulder. But I'd spent fifteen years learning the difference between brave and useful, and Sana Reyes had just chosen her job, same as I'd once chosen mine. Covering fire. Drawing eyes. Making herself the loudest thing on the ice so the rest of us could be fast.

So I ran.

We ran the base of the Widow while it groaned overhead, while flares burst white and red behind us, while Idris sobbed something in a language I didn't know and Marguerite pulled him forward by sheer fury of will. The ice under us went from solid to treacherous to solid again, and then we were around the serac's shoulder and into the lee of the far wall, and I turned back with my heart going like a jackhammer.

Sana was down.

Not dead — I could see her moving, rolling, scrambling for cover behind a pressure ridge — but down, and there was blood on the ice around her, shockingly red against all that blue. A rifleman had gotten past her flares, or through them. She was holding her side. She was also, God help her, still firing the flare gun one-handed, still making noise, still covering a retreat that no longer needed covering.

"We go back," I said.

"Yes," Marguerite said. No argument. No debate. She was already re-racking her plan, eyes flicking across the ice, finding the line. "Idris — the crevasse field, due east of her, thirty yards. There's a bridge. You said there was a bridge."

"There's a bridge," Idris said, wiping his face with a shaking hand. "If it hasn't calved."

"Then we're quick."

* * *

Getting her out took eleven minutes and cost me ten years.

The bridge held. Barely. It was old ice, gray with embedded rock, sagging in the middle like a tired hammock, and it groaned the whole way across while bullets chewed the far lip of the crevasse and Sana crawled toward us leaving a smear of red that I made myself stop looking at. Marguerite went out to meet her — of course she did, of course — and the two of them came back across that sagging gray spine of ice with Marguerite half-carrying the medic, both of them laughing, actually laughing, wild and gasping, when the bridge finally gave way behind them with a sound like the world sighing shut.

The retrieval team didn't follow onto the ice. They didn't need to. They'd done their math: a wounded woman slows a party to a crawl, and the glacier does the rest. We saw them once more, distant figures on the rocks above the far lip, watching us go. One of them raised a hand. Not a wave. A countdown.

Four days, eleven hours. Now less.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of an ice ridge, and I sat with Sana while Marguerite and Idris argued quietly over the maps. The wound was a graze — a long, ugly furrow along her ribs that she insisted on cleaning herself, waving me off until I refused to be waved off and she gave up with a grunt that might have been gratitude.

"You know," she said, wincing as she taped the dressing, "most people would've left me."

"Most people aren't us."

"And what are we?"

I looked at her — this sharp-tongued woman who'd stood up into gunfire for four strangers, this crew of thieves and deserters and witnesses who'd known each other barely a week and had already bled for each other more than most families do in a lifetime.

"Honestly?" I said. "I have no idea. But I'm not interested in finding out what happens when we split up."

Sana smiled, tired and crooked. "That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me, ranger."

"Don't tell Marguerite. She gets jealous."

From across the camp, without looking up from the maps: "I can hear you."

Idris laughed again, that same surprised bark, and then quieter, almost to himself: "Ezra would've liked you all."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Her brother. The reason for all of it. His name hung in the cold air like breath.

"He'd have hated the Widow," Sana said finally. "He hated ice with names."

"He named it," Idris said softly.

"Yeah," she said. "That was the problem."

The wind picked up outside the ridge, carrying with it the endless low conversation of the glacier — pop and tick and groan, the sound of something vast deciding. Above us, the seracs leaned into the dark like patient jurors, and somewhere beyond them, four days out, was the far lip and the choice waiting there, the thing Marguerite carried wrapped in oilcloth against her back like a second spine.

I took first watch. I always take first watch. And as the others settled into their bags — the thief, the scientist, the medic who'd stood up into a rifle line for people she'd met in a snow cave — I sat facing the dark with their sleeping sounds behind me and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that I'd already made my choice. Somewhere back on the ice, probably. Maybe at the dry wash, the first day, when I cornered a thief and found she'd been leaving water for ghosts.

The badge on my belt caught the starlight. I turned it face-down against my leg.

Behind us, far off in the black, the glacier calved — one great cracking boom rolling down the valley like a door slamming. And in the silence after, I heard something else, faint and wrong: voices. Human voices, on the wind, from the direction of the rocks.

They weren't falling back.

They were coming down onto the ice.
