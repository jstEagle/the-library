## 8. Marguerite's Choice

The far lip of the glacier looked, from a distance, like a promise. A clean white ramp rising to a saddle of blue rock, and beyond it — the map said — a descent into pine and running water and the end of the ice. From up close it looked like what it was: the last place on earth where anyone could ask me for anything.

We'd been climbing since before dawn. Sana rode Casper's back in a sling of rope and pack straps, her leg splinted with an ice axe shaft and a length of surveyor's tape, and she'd stopped apologizing for it somewhere around hour three, which I took as a good sign. Idris walked point with his compass held out like a man offering bread to a horse. And I carried the case.

It wasn't heavy. That was the joke of it. Sixty grams of drive, wrapped in foil and my spare socks, riding against my ribs where my brother used to ride me piggyback across the flats behind our mother's house. All that weight, and it weighed nothing at all.

"Wind's shifting," Casper said. He didn't look at me when he said things like that anymore. He just said them, to all of us, the way you'd talk to a crew. Somewhere between the crevasse and the fire lookout he had stopped being my captor and started being something else, and neither of us had named it yet. Naming things on a glacier is how you jinx them.

"How long do we have?" Idris asked.

Casper checked the sky the way other men check watches. "Before they regroup? An hour. Before the weather? Less."

* * *

They'd come at us in the calving fields two days ago — four men in government grey with no insignia, which is its own kind of signature — and Sana had bought us thirty seconds by shoving Idris into a melt channel and standing up in plain view with her flare gun like she was greeting a parade. The round caught the lead man across the visor. It didn't stop him so much as insult him, but insults buy time too, sometimes.

She'd taken a round through the thigh for her trouble. Through and through, she kept saying, through and through, as if repetition were anesthesia. Casper had carried her anyway, and she'd stopped arguing around kilometer six, which was how we knew it was bad.

Now she lay propped against a serac while Idris changed her dressing, and I stood at the lip of the glacier with the whole sky leaning on me, and I thought about my brother's handwriting.

That's the thing nobody tells you about grief. It doesn't live in the big moments. It lives in the margins. Theo wrote his field notes left-handed because his right hand was always holding something else, and his sevens had this little crossbar like they were trying to be nines, and for eleven years I could pick his work out of any pile of paper in any office in the country. The survey data on this drive was full of those sevens. He'd built half the algorithms himself. His last work. His last everything.

And if I burned it, every seven went with it.

"You're doing the thing," Sana said.

I turned. She was watching me from under the serac, gray-faced, hands steady while Idris taped the bandage down. Medic's hands. "What thing?"

"The martyr math." She winced as Idris cinched the tape. "Where you stand there looking noble and calculate exactly how much of yourself you can spend. I've seen it in triage lines. It never ends well."

"I'm not—"

"You're holding the only copy of a dead man's life's work," she said, "and you're deciding whether his sister gets to exist again or whether his principles get to win. That's not martyr math, actually. That's worse. That's grief with a lighter."

Idris sat back on his heels. He didn't say anything. He'd barely spoken since the ambush, and I understood why. Every file he decrypted was another page of proof that the mission he'd signed onto had been a lie with his name in the footer. He'd been mapping a route so it could be sealed. He'd been making himself obsolete with great precision.

"Marguerite." Casper came up beside me at the lip. Below us the glacier fell away in blue steps toward the retrieval team's last known position, a white nothing with men in it somewhere. "Talk to me."

"About what?"

"About whatever you've been not saying since the lookout."

I almost laughed. Almost. "You want the inventory? Fine. Here's the inventory. If I hand this over, I walk. Officially. There's a man in Meridian — a prosecutor who owes me — and this data proves the survey was weaponized, proves the cover-up, proves everything, and I trade it for my name back. Marguerite Voss, acquitted. Marguerite Voss, free to buy coffee under her own face."

"And?"

"And it goes to the government, Casper. Even him. Even honest, it goes into a system that buried four people in a snow cave and called it weather. It becomes evidence, then it becomes archived, then it becomes classified, and in ten years some junior analyst rediscovers it and the route gets sealed anyway, except now they'll have done it legally, with paperwork, with my signature on the surrender form."

"So we don't surrender it."

"So we don't surrender it," I agreed. "So we burn it. And the route stays open because nobody knows how to close it. And Theo's sevens turn to ash. And I stay a thief forever, which — fine, I earned that, mostly — but he stays a line item. Survey team member T. Voss. Lost. No remains recovered. A footnote in somebody's budget hearing."

The wind came up the face and tried to take the words out of my mouth. I let it have them.

"That's the real choice," I said. "Not my name. His work. Nobody tells you that burning a thing burns everyone who made it."

* * *

Casper was quiet for a long time. Fifteen years of ranger quiet, the kind that has weather in it.

"When I was young," he finally said, "my captain told me the badge doesn't make you right. It makes you responsible. You carry the weight of every call you make, and you don't put it down, and you don't pass it to someone else." He looked out at the ice. "I've been putting mine on other people for two weeks. Sent you ahead at the lookout. Let Idris take the decryption alone. Kept telling myself duty would tell me what to do when the moment came."

"Has it?"

"No." He turned to me then, and his eyes were doing something I hadn't seen in them before — not softness exactly. Recognition. "Duty says give it up. Justice says burn it. And neither one of them mentions your brother, because institutions don't count the dead, they count outcomes." He held out his hand. Not for the case. Just open, palm up, in the wind. "So I'm not going to decide for you. But I'll tell you what I see. I see a man who spent his last months building a map beautiful enough that even the people trying to steal it couldn't help but admire it. And I think a map like that isn't the route. It's the argument. And arguments don't die when the paper does — they die when everybody stops remembering what they were."

Sana called weakly from the serac: "For the record, I vote burn. I got shot for this data. I'd like it to be worth more than a plea deal."

"For the record," Idris said quietly, "Theo would burn it. He argued with me about it for three weeks before the expedition. He said a route that can be owned isn't a route, it's a leash." He looked at his hands. "I told him he was being dramatic. I'd like to go on record as having lost that argument."

I stood there with the wind pushing me toward the saddle and the grief pulling me back toward the ice, and I understood, finally, what the hesitation was. It wasn't fear. It wasn't even principle, not really. It was that burning the drive was the last act of Theo's story I would ever get to participate in, and after it, he would be finished — truly finished, no drafts, no margins, no crossbarred sevens anywhere in the world.

But stories aren't meant to be kept. They're meant to be told. And the telling was us. Four people on a glacier, alive because of his maps, carrying his argument in our legs and lungs instead of sixty grams of foil-wrapped silicon.

Below us, movement. Grey figures coming up the blue steps, small and patient as ants.

"Cas," I said. "Company."

He didn't reach for his rifle. He reached for me. "Whatever you choose," he said, "choose it fast."

I pulled the drive from my coat. Held it once against my ribs, where a boy used to ride. Said goodbye to the sevens.

Then the mountain spoke first — a crack like the world's spine giving way, deep under our feet, and the whole far slope of the glacier leaned, and groaned, and began to move.
