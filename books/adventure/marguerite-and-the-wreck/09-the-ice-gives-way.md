## 9. The Ice Gives Way

The glacier told us it was going to break before it did. That's the thing about ice — it's honest in a way the government never was. It groans, it cracks, it whispers through its seams like an old man complaining about his knees. You just have to be arrogant enough to listen.

I was listening. I'd been listening for two hours, ever since the light came up gray and mean over the calving fields and Idris pointed his mitten at a serac the size of a courthouse and said, "That one's leaning wrong."

"Leaning wrong," Sana repeated from the sled. Her shoulder was strapped and splinted where the retrieval team's ambush had caught her yesterday, but she'd refused the morphine because, in her words, "someone has to keep you three idiots alive." She was pale as the ice under her, though, and every bump of the sled cost her something she didn't show.

"It's overhanging the route," Idris said. "It's been overhanging since the last survey photos. The question is whether it's had six days to think about falling or whether it decides today."

Marguerite stood beside me at the lip of the field, the survey case slung across her back like a coffin she'd volunteered to carry. Wind pulled at her hood. Below us, the pass opened up — a corridor of blue-gray ice maybe four hundred meters long, walled by seracs on both sides, the only way across before the season sealed it shut.

"The helicopter's been circling since dawn," she said.

"I know."

"They're waiting for us to commit."

"I know that too."

She looked at me then, and there was frost in her lashes and something else underneath, something that had been growing between us since a dry wash in the high desert when I should have put cuffs on her and instead asked her why she'd left water out for ghosts. "Casper. If they come down into that corridor while we're in it—"

"Then we run faster." I checked the anchor line for the third time even though I'd already checked it twice. Fifteen years of rangering teaches you rituals. It doesn't teach you what to do when the ritual is all that's left of your oath. "Idris leads. Sana on the sled behind him. You next. I'm anchor."

"You're always anchor," Marguerite said.

"Somebody's got to hold the rope."

* * *

We went down onto the ice at 0700 by my dead reckoning, which was the only clock I trusted anymore. The glacier spoke to us the whole way — long sighs, sharp reports like rifles, a deep tectonic moan that I felt in my molars. Idris moved well for a man who'd spent weeks in a snow cave eating melted snow and hope. He kept us off the blue spots, read the surface like Marguerite read lock tumblers, and called the crevasses before we could see them.

Sana didn't complain once. I kept glancing back at her on the sled and finding her watching me with those clinical eyes of hers, cataloguing my tells. At the halfway mark she said, quietly, "Your jaw's doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing it did before you anchored us out of the crevasse. The thing it does when you've decided something and haven't told anyone yet."

I hadn't decided anything. That was the lie I was holding onto, and Sana saw straight through it, because medics always do. Because I *had* decided, sometime in the night, lying awake listening to the glacier count down. I'd decided what fifteen years of duty actually weighed, measured against four people I'd known for less than two weeks who had become — God help me — the closest thing to family I'd had since the wilderness took everything else.

The radio crackled. Not ours — theirs. A voice, flat and official, bouncing off the serac walls: *"Blackwood. This is Retrieval One. We have you visual. Set down the case and step away from the civilians. You are directed to secure government property."*

Secure government property. Four people. Two of them half-dead. That was the arithmetic of the badge I'd worn for fifteen years.

Marguerite stopped. Idris stopped. The wind filled the silence.

"Casper," Idris said carefully, "that radio's keyed to your unit code."

It was. Which meant they weren't talking past us. They were talking *to* me, ranger to ranger, one professional to another, offering me the exit ramp. Deliver the maps, walk out with your pension and your name intact, let the thief and the surveyors vanish into the machinery like everyone else the Redline Range had swallowed.

I unclipped the radio from my pack and held it up so they could see me do it. Then I dropped it into a crevasse and listened until it stopped hitting things.

"That answer clear enough?" I said, to nobody, to everybody, to the helicopter shadow sliding over the ice.

* * *

It happened at the far quarter of the crossing, because glaciers have no respect for narrative timing.

The crack came first — not a whisper this time but a gunshot, then a hundred gunshots, the whole eastern wall announcing itself. I looked up and saw the leaning serac shrug off its centuries. Time went strange. I remember thinking, absurdly, that it fell the way debt falls — slow, then all at once.

"RUN!" Idris screamed, and we ran.

Four hundred meters became three hundred, became two hundred, the ice heaving under our boots, fissures opening ahead of us like the range itself was trying to swallow the evidence. Behind us, the serac came down in a white avalanche roar, and with it — I heard it, we all heard it — the shriek of metal. The retrieval team had committed after all. They'd come down into the corridor right behind us, and the mountain had made its own ruling on their warrant.

Dust and ice-fog boiled up. Through it, on our side of the pass, I could see figures — two, maybe three, alive, cut off now by a fresh wall of collapsed ice where the corridor used to be. And beyond them, wedged against the new rubble, a sledge. On the sledge, a hard case. Their copy of the survey. They'd brought a duplicate — of course they had; bureaucracies always make copies — and now they were digging it out, and the far lip of the glacier was still a hundred meters of broken ice away, and Marguerite was beside me with our copy on her back, and I understood, all at once and completely, how this ended.

If either copy survived, the route got sealed, weaponized, controlled. If both burned, four dead people stayed dead and free.

"Give it here," I said.

Marguerite stared at me. "What?"

"The case. Give it here." I held out my hand. "You carried it far enough. You don't get to carry this part. That's not a kindness, Marguerite. It's a sentence, and I'm the one serving it."

Behind us, through the fog, a voice: *"Blackwood! The case! Secure the—"*

Ten seconds. That's what the ice gave me. I counted them the way you count anything that matters.

One: I took the case from her shoulders and her fingers brushed mine and neither of us said anything because everything had already been said in a fire lookout and a dry wash and a hundred small hours on the rope.

Two: I popped the latches. Government engineering — smooth as sin.

Three through five: I pulled out the maps, the drives, the photographs of her brother's careful handwriting mapping a route that was never meant to save anyone, and I lit them with the emergency flare I'd been carrying since the crevasse, and they caught all at once, hungry, orange against all that blue.

Six: I dropped the burning case into the widest fissure and watched the fire go down into the glacier like a secret being buried alive.

Seven: I turned to face the retrieval team across the collapsing ice, raised my voice over the roar, and gave them my name, rank, and confession, loud enough to carry. "The survey is destroyed. I destroyed it. Casper Blackwood, Ranger, fifteen years of service, and I'd do it again tomorrow. Write that down."

Eight: I shoved Marguerite toward the far lip. "Go. All of you. Now."

Nine: the second serac, the one Idris had been eyeing all morning, began to lean.

Ten: I planted my axe in the ridge of ice between them and the collapse and held the route open with my body, my weight, my fifteen years, screaming at them to cross while the world came apart behind me.

They crossed. Sana first, cursing magnificently. Idris hauling the sled. Marguerite last — of course Marguerite last — turning at the lip with the firelight still reflected in her eyes, reaching back for me with a hand that shook.

And I let go of the ice and ran, and the glacier reached for my heels like a creditor, and I jumped.

* * *

I landed hard enough to see stars in broad daylight. Hands found me — Idris's grip on my collar, Sana's good arm, Marguerite's arms around me, all of them hauling me up the slope away from the edge while the calving fields roared themselves empty behind us. When I finally rolled over and looked back, there was nothing to look back to. The corridor was gone. The retrieval team was gone, or buried, or retreating — the fog hid which, and I found I didn't care. The survey was ash in the belly of the ice. My badge was ash in everything else.

Marguerite knelt over me, checking me for damage with hands that wouldn't stop moving. "You idiot," she said, and her voice broke on it. "You absolute — you didn't have to take the blame, you didn't have to—"

"Yeah, I did." I sat up. Everything hurt, which meant everything worked. "Fifteen years, Voss. Somebody ought to get something out of them."

Idris laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound. Sana pressed her forehead briefly to my shoulder, medic's benediction. And below us, far below, the glacier settled into its new shape, sealing the pass, closing the door on the old world with a sound like a vault.

Ahead lay the northern slopes, unmapped now by anyone, wide open and officially nonexistent.

"So," I said, spitting out ice. "Anybody know the way down?"
