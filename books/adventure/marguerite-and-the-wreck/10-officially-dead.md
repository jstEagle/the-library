## 10. Officially Dead

The far side of the glacier looked like nothing at all. That was the point of it.

We came down off the ice in a gray drizzle that couldn't decide whether it wanted to be snow or rain and settled, in classic government fashion, for doing both badly. Below us the valley opened up — brown tundra, a braided river running silver through it, and no roads, no towers, no fences. Just land that had never been asked to be anything for anybody.

Sana walked with her arm in a sling Idris had rigged from survey tape and stubbornness, and every hundred yards she'd stop and announce she was fine, which is how we all knew she wasn't. Casper carried most of the gear. He hadn't asked anyone's permission to do this; he'd simply started doing it somewhere around the calving fields and nobody had been foolish enough to object. Fifteen years of carrying a badge apparently trains you well for carrying everything else.

My brother's maps were ash by then. All of them. Every coordinate, every depth sounding, every careful line of Elias's handwriting reduced to a smell of smoke that still lived in my jacket if I held the collar close enough. I'd done the burning myself, back on the ice, with Casper standing next to me saying *it was my hand, not yours* — a lie we'd agreed to tell the wind, since there was no one else left to tell it to.

The wind hadn't believed him either.

* * *

We made camp that first night in the lee of a moraine, four people and one battered stove, and it was Sana who said it first, because medics are constitutionally incapable of letting a wound go undressed even when the wound is metaphorical.

"We should talk about names," she said.

Idris looked up from the fire. "I like my name."

"You liked your funding too," Sana said, "and look how that went."

She wasn't wrong. The news had reached us two days earlier, crackling out of the little radio Idris had salvaged from the wrecked camp — the same radio that had been transmitting coordinates into empty air while its owners vanished. The broadcast was brief and beautifully written, in the way official grief always is. *A four-person government survey team is presumed lost in the Redline Range after severe weather. Recovery efforts have been suspended.* No mention of a ranger. No mention of a thief. No mention of a glacier route worth sealing, or a retrieval team that had gone home empty-handed and presumably been told to keep going home.

Officially, my brother was dead. Officially, so were Idris Okafor and Sana Reyes. And officially, so — as of roughly ten seconds of falling ice and one very dramatic act of arson committed by a man who used to arrest people for exactly that — was Casper Blackwood.

"It's not the worst thing," I said, "being dead. The paperwork's already done. You get a kind of freedom."

"Easy for you to say," Idris muttered. "You were dead before you got here. I had a pension."

"You had a pension?"

"Eleven years of contributions." He poked the fire with real bitterness. "Gone. Vaporized along with the permafrost data."

Sana put her good hand on his shoulder. "Idris. Sweetheart. We watched a mountain try to eat us. Your pension is the fourth saddest thing that happened this week."

He considered this. "Third," he said finally. "Casper's badge is third."

We all looked at Casper. He was sitting slightly apart from the fire, the way he always did, as though fifteen years of habit had taught him that warmth was something you observed rather than participated in. But he'd been sitting closer each night. Nobody mentioned it. Everybody noticed.

"The badge stays buried," he said. "That was the deal."

"I know," Idris said. "I just want it noted for the record that it was a very nice badge."

"Noted," Casper said, and there was — I want to be precise about this, because precision was Elias's religion and I caught it from him like a fever — there was the ghost of a smile on the man's face. The first one I'd ever seen there. It made him look ten years younger and about forty percent more dangerous, which I hadn't previously thought possible.

* * *

We buried things the next morning, in ground that was more gravel than soil, beside a stream of snowmelt running fast and cold off the glacier's skirt.

Casper went first. He knelt and dug with his hands, no ceremony, and laid the badge down in the little hollow he'd made. Fifteen years of service, pressed into tin. He didn't say anything over it. I don't think there was anything to say that the hole itself didn't say better: *here lies the version of me that belonged to them.*

Then Idris lowered in a waterproof case containing the last physical copy of anything — his field notes, Sana's medical logs, the decryption keys, the whole paper skeleton of the mission that had never been scientific. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing I'd carried three thousand miles and across a living glacier to deliver into the ground:

Elias's notebook.

Not the survey. The survey was gone, burned, unrecoverable, and good riddance to what it would have built. This was the other thing. The personal one. Sketches of lichens. A running joke about Idris's coffee. A page where he'd drawn the aurora and written, in his terrible sideways handwriting, *if anything happens to me, tell Marguerite the stars out here argue back.*

I'd read it eleven times. I'd memorized it. And then I put it in the ground anyway, because a message kept in a pocket is a weight, and a message buried with honor is a gift.

"He'd have hated this," I said.

"He'd have loved it," Sana said. "He hated being useful to them. This is the only way he gets to stop."

Casper filled the grave. Then, because he was Casper, he marked it — not with a cross or a cairn but with a flat stone set upright, the way rangers mark trail junctions. A fork in the path. Choose.

We stood there a while. Four people who did not exist, mourning a fifth who also did not exist, beside water that had been ice a week ago and would be ocean eventually. The sun came through the clouds for maybe ninety seconds. It felt less like a blessing than a receipt.

"So," Idris said. "Names."

* * *

It took longer than you'd think, choosing to be someone new. Not because the options were scarce but because every name carries luggage, and we'd all sworn off luggage.

Sana went first and shocked everyone by keeping her name. "They think I'm dead," she said with a shrug. "Best cover identity I'll ever have. Besides, my mother spent nine months picking it. I'm not wasting her effort."

Idris chose "Moses," which nobody questioned because Idris had earned the right to be as strange as he pleased. He said it meant he'd finally get to lead someone somewhere without a government telling him where.

And Casper —

Casper took the longest. He stood apart again, working his jaw like the words were a knot in rope, and finally said, "Blackwood was my mother's name. Before that it was nobody's. I'll keep it. It's mine now, not theirs."

"That's not how names work," I said.

"It's how mine works," he said, and looked at me, and I understood that some part of the argument was already over and had been for days, possibly since the crevasse, possibly since the fire lookout, possibly since the dry wash where a thief had been leaving water caches for a brother who was already gone.

Which left me.

Marguerite Voss. Thief. Smuggler of documents. Sister. The name had a warrant attached to it in three jurisdictions, none of which were looking anymore, because you can't issue a manhunt for a woman listed among the lost. I turned it over in my mouth like a stone.

"Voss stays," I said. "Marguerite stays. If they're not looking for her, she's the safest person in the world to be."

"And if they start looking?" Idris asked.

"Then she'll leave water caches," Casper said, "and disappear."

* * *

We walked north after that. There was nothing north — that was the whole appeal — but there was a homesteading clause in land nobody wanted, an old trapping cabin Idris swore he could make weatherproof by October, and a river full of fish that had never once been surveyed, mapped, catalogued, or weaponized.

Sana's arm healed crooked. She said it gave her character. Idris built a stove that smoked terribly and refused all criticism of it. Casper learned to laugh — slowly, reluctantly, like a man relearning a language he'd spoken as a child — and I learned to sleep without checking the treeline, which took longer than learning to trust him, and I trusted him somewhere around the second day of knowing him, which tells you everything about how broken I was when we met.

Some nights I still take out the memory of Elias's notebook and read it by firelight, the way you'd visit a grave. *The stars out here argue back.* They do. I've checked. I've argued with several.

On the last night of September, the radio — kept now for music and weather, nothing else — carried the tail end of a northern news bulletin. Something about a review board. Something about irregularities in a suspended survey program. Something about documents that could not be located and questions that would be asked in the spring.

Idris turned it off. Sana poured tea. Casper put his hand over mine on the table, rough palm, steady weight, no explanation needed.

Outside, the first true snow of the season was coming down over the cabin, the river, the unmarked stone by the meltwater, and the four graves we'd dug for ourselves and gotten to climb out of.

Nobody was looking for us.

We intended to keep it that way — and if anyone came asking come spring, well. The pass wouldn't open for six days, and by then we'd be ghosts with a garden.
