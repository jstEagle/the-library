## 1. The Wreck They Don't Name

The contract came to Ada Thackeray the way all bad ideas come to drowning women: wrapped in money and delivered by a man who wouldn't look her in the eye.

She was three weeks behind on her mooring fees at Sallow Quay, and eleven months behind on everything else, when Vetch Corrigan's runner found her scraping barnacles off her own hull to sell as chum. The boy wouldn't come down the ladder — he stood on the dock with his nose wrinkled against the rot-smell of her boat, the *Kestrel*, and held out an envelope sealed with grey wax.

"Harbor boss says you're to be at Pier Nine by dawn tomorrow," he said. "Survey work. Navy-adjacent."

"Navy doesn't have adjacents."

"Navy has this one." He dropped the envelope and left before she could ask anything else, which told her more than the envelope did.

Inside: one page, no letterhead, no signature. Coordinates upriver past the last charted bend of the Vell. A fee that would clear her debt twice over and buy the *Kestrel* a new engine besides. And three lines of terms, typed in a font so clean it looked ashamed of itself:

*Survey only. No recovery. No salvage rights. Nothing recovered is to be raised, retained, or described.*

*Described,* she read again. Not *discussed*. Described. As if even words could be contraband.

Ada sat on her hull in the dying light and let the river breathe under her. She thought about Vetch Corrigan's smile, which had too many teeth in it for a man who lent money, and about the way his collectors had started leaving chalk marks on her mast instead of knocking — marks that meant *next time we don't knock.* She thought about Mirabel, who'd stayed with her through two wrecked seasons and one wrecked engagement, sleeping in the wheelhouse because there wasn't a bunk worth the name.

Then she thought about the number on the page, and hunger did what hunger does.

"We're going upriver," she told Mirabel that night, over tinned fish and rain drumming the deck. "Survey job. Easy money."

Mirabel looked at her the way Mirabel always looked at her when Ada lied badly — patient, unfooled, fond anyway. "Nobody pays easy money for easy work."

"This one does."

"And you signed it?"

"I signed it."

Mirabel sighed and passed her the tin. "Then I'll get the lines ready."

* * *

They ran upstream two days through country that thinned out around them like hair going grey. The villages stopped. Then the fishing stakes stopped. Then the channel markers stopped, and after that the river belonged to nobody, or to somebody who didn't want company. On the second evening they rounded a bluff of black stone and found the coordinates waiting for them like a trap already sprung.

The water there was wrong. Ada felt it before she saw it — a coldness rising through the hull planks, a stillness that had nothing to do with calm. The river ran fast everywhere else, brown and muscular, but in the bowl beneath the bluff it went slow and green and deep, deep enough that the sounder gave up counting fathoms and just blinked.

And forty feet down, where the green went black, something long lay across the bottom.

"Not a barge," Mirabel said, face lit blue by the sonar screen. "Too narrow. Too straight." A pause. "Ada, that's a hull. That's a ship."

"It's a survey target," Ada said. "That's all it is. That's all it gets to be."

But she was already pulling on her suit.

The terms said solo dive, one diver, no support craft on the water during descent. Whoever wrote them knew exactly how alone they wanted her. She checked her tanks twice anyway, and Mirabel checked them a third time without being asked, and neither of them said the thing they were both thinking, which was that the fee was too high and the rules were too strange and the smart move was to turn the *Kestrel* around and run.

Debt makes cowards of careful women. Ada spat into her mask, rinsed it, and went over the side.

* * *

Down, the world changed its mind about light.

At ten meters the green turned to iron. At twenty, her lamp found silt hanging in the water like breath held too long. At thirty-five, the hull rose up out of the dark to meet her — and Ada Thackeray, who had touched drowned things on three coasts and never once flinched, flinched.

It was a ship, but built like no ship she knew. The lines were too fine, the stem raked like a blade, and the wood — she pressed a gloved hand to it — was dense and dark and grainless, some timber she had no name for. No fouling. No teredo worms. As if the river itself had agreed not to touch it.

She swam the length of it slowly, running her lamp along seams tight as closed lips. There should have been cargo. Survey meant cargo — that was the whole dirty joke of these contracts; they sent divers down to inventory what somebody else wanted stolen later. But the hold, when she found the breach and slipped inside, was empty except for silt and one thing.

One thing, sitting amidships on the keel timbers, upright, deliberate, placed.

A chest.

Iron-banded, rope-handled, small enough to carry and heavy enough to matter. And on its lid, stamped into a plate of dull metal, a crest: a bridge spanning water, with a star above it and a wreath around both.

Ada knew every flag on the river. She knew the harbor bosses' marks and the navy's ensigns and the smugglers' signs scratched on pilings from the delta to the gorge. She had never seen this crest. But she knew what it meant anyway, the way you know a face is dead — by the stillness of it. Nations that live have flags that fly and fray and get patched. This had been stamped once, perfectly, and then the whole world had agreed to stop stamping it.

Her orders said *no recovery.* Her orders said *nothing raised, retained, or described.* Her hands, which had not consulted her orders, were already working the chest free of the silt that half-buried it, testing the weight, finding the lift point.

Here is what Ada told herself, forty feet down in water that didn't want her: *I'm just looking. I'm just confirming the target. This is due diligence.*

Here is what was true: the fee would clear the debt, but the debt wasn't the hunger. The hunger was older than the debt. It was the same thing that had made her a diver in the first place — the conviction, bone-deep and unreasonable, that somewhere below every ordinary surface there was a thing worth having, and that it would be hers, hers, if she just went deep enough.

She clipped the lift line to the chest. She cut it loose.

The moment the knot parted, the river moved. Not much. A shudder through the hull, a sigh of silt off the decks, the slow green water folding inward around her like a door closing. Her lamp flickered once. Somewhere above, muffled by fathoms, she thought she heard an engine that hadn't been there before.

Ada kicked for the surface and did not look back at the ship, because she was afraid that if she did, she would see it watching her go.

* * *

She broke the surface into evening, and the evening was wrong.

The *Kestrel* rode at anchor where she'd left it, Mirabel a silhouette at the rail. But beyond the boat, out where the channel bent toward the bluff, a shape sat low and grey against the last light — angular, engine-idling, a searchlight already warming from amber toward white.

Naval grey. Naval lines. A patrol boat, riding quiet in a stretch of river that appeared on no chart, guarding a wreck that officially did not exist, waiting — Ada understood this with a cold that had nothing to do with the water — for her.

Not for the survey. For whatever came up.

The chest broke the surface beside her, buoyed on the lift bag, dripping, the crest catching the last of the light. Ada got one hand on the rope and hung there in the current, treading water between her boat and theirs, between the debt behind her and the dread ahead, and made the fastest calculation of her life.

The searchlight snapped to white and swept the river, and it was coming for her.

Ada hauled the chest toward the *Kestrel* and began to swim.
