## 2. What the River Owes

The patrol boat came alongside before Ada's head had fully cleared the water, and Mirabel understood in the space of one breath that they were already caught.

She'd been coiling rope on the *Kestrel*'s stern when she saw it: grey hull, grey tarpaulin over its deck gun, twin engines idling with the patience of something that had never once needed to hurry. The ensign at its stern was navy blue, and the men on deck wore no insignia at all, which was worse. Uniforms could be appealed to. Men without them answered to nothing Mirabel could name.

"Ada," she said quietly, and held out a hand.

Her captain surfaced spitting river water, hair plastered black across her face, and Mirabel watched her take in the patrol boat the way she took in everything — fast, cold, calculating odds. Then Ada's eyes went to the crate lashed to the dive line, still thirty feet down, and Mirabel saw her make a decision she hadn't been asked to make.

"Winch," Ada said.

"You're supposed to leave it," Mirabel said. "They said survey only. Mark the position, log the—"

"I know what they said." Ada's hand closed over the winch crank. "Turn it."

Mirabel turned it. That was the whole of her life, some days — turning what Ada told her to turn, holding what Ada told her to hold, asking questions only after the asking couldn't hurt anyone. The cable groaned. The chest broke the surface dripping, dark wood banded in corroded iron, and even half-blind with river silt Mirabel could see the crest stamped into its lid: a broken tower above three wavy lines, struck through with a single diagonal bar.

She knew that crest. Everyone past forty knew it, from the old coins that turned up in market stalls and got confiscated, from the faded maps in the harbor office where a country used to be printed across the upper valley until someone inked it out so thoroughly the paper wore thin. Vessia. Except nobody said the name anymore. You just said *upriver*, and let the silence do the work.

"Ma'am." A voice through a speaking trumpet, flat as slate. "Step away from the salvage."

*Salvage.* As if the chest were driftwood. As if they hadn't been waiting here since before Ada went down, which meant they'd known exactly what she'd find.

Ada stepped toward the chest instead.

What happened next happened fast. A launch dropped from the patrol boat's side with four men and a boarding hook. Ada cut the mooring line with her belt knife — Mirabel was already at the wheel, because she was always already at the wheel, because Ada trusted her with the engine and nothing else and both of those facts meant something. The *Kestrel* swung her stern downstream and opened her throttle, and the delta took them.

Behind them, the patrol boat did not immediately pursue. That frightened Mirabel more than a chase would have. It simply sat there, growing smaller, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

They ran the channels for two hours, threading reed banks and sunken pilings that Mirabel knew from ten years of running cargo nobody asked about. Ada crouched over the chest with a pry bar and didn't speak. The seal around the lid wasn't wax; it was lead, poured and stamped, the kind of closure governments used when they wanted a thing sealed *forever*, not merely shut.

"We should open it," Mirabel said finally.

"No."

"They'll board us eventually. I'd rather know what we're dying for."

Ada looked up, and for a moment Mirabel saw past the hunger in her — the debt to Corvo Harl that ate every salvage like this one, the interest compounding like floodwater — and saw something underneath it that looked almost like fear. "If we open it, we can't un-open it. There's a version of this where we hand it back and say we found nothing."

"There isn't," Mirabel said. "You cut it loose against orders. They watched you do it."

Ada exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh. "There is that." She set the pry bar down but didn't move away. "After dark. We'll be through the narrows by then."

They weren't through the narrows by then, because that was when the woman in the skiff appeared out of the reeds.

* * *

She came alongside at dusk without hailing, without lights, a narrow black skiff driven by someone who knew the water better than Mirabel did, which Mirabel would not have believed possible an hour earlier. The woman stood amidships with a boathook and a face like a topographic map of some violent country — scar tissue pulling from her jaw up across her left cheekbone to her ear, old burns glossing the skin there smooth.

"Vessian," the woman said. Not a question. She was looking at the crest on the chest, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word like ice underfoot.

"Never heard of it," Ada said.

The woman smiled, and the scar made the smile terrible. "No one has. That's rather the point." She hooked the skiff to the *Kestrel*'s rail. "My name is Suri. I've spent nineteen years looking for that chest, and I will follow you to the sea or the grave, whichever comes first, so you may as well hear me out while we're all moving the same direction."

"The navy's behind us," Mirabel said from the wheel.

"The navy is behind everyone," Suri said. "It's the one thing they're consistently good at." She climbed aboard uninvited, moving with a limp she disguised well, and put her hand flat on the chest's lid the way other people touched relics or children. "In the last year of Vessia, my people lived in nine towns along the middle reaches. Farmers, ferrymen, archive clerks. When the war ended — the war nobody names either — the new government declared the valley flooded for a hydroelectric scheme. Dams upstream. They gave the towns eleven days to evacuate."

"And?" Ada said.

"And the sluice gates opened in four." Suri's voice didn't rise at all, which was somehow louder than shouting. "Nine towns. Eleven thousand people. The official record says the evacuation succeeded. The official record says Vessia never existed, that the maps were redrawn for administrative convenience, that anyone who says otherwise is a malcontent or a ghost." Her burned cheek caught the last light. "That chest came off a government dispatch boat that sank the night before the flood. My grandfather helped load it. It holds the orders, Captain. The real ones. Signed, dated, sealed. Proof that the drowning wasn't engineering. It was policy."

The river ran dark around them. Somewhere behind, far back, a searchlight swept the reeds in long slow strokes, looking.

"Why should we believe you?" Ada asked.

"You shouldn't. Believe the chest." Suri sat back on her heels. "Open it. If the documents say what the navy's lie says, throw them overboard and I'll bother you no further. But if they say what I know in my bones they say—" she looked at Mirabel, not Ada, and Mirabel couldn't have said why that landed harder— "then you two are already involved. You've been involved since you cut it loose. The only question left is whose side of history you're standing on when it surfaces."

* * *

They anchored at midnight in a dead channel choked with hyacinth, engines cold, the searchlights miles astern. Ada paced the deck for a long time. Then she brought the pry bar to the chest and knelt, and Mirabel knelt beside her, and Suri stood apart with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral.

The lead seal split. The iron bands had rusted to lace and gave without protest. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay bundles of paper — hundreds of pages, typed and handwritten, carbon copies and originals, bound with brass clips that had fused green. And on the topmost page, under a letterhead bearing the broken tower and the struck-through waves:

**DIRECTIVE 44 — RE: SETTLEMENT CLEARANCE, MIDDLE REACHES.**

Mirabel read. She read the sentence beneath the heading, and then she read it again, because the first time her eyes had refused it.

*Evacuation timeline is hereby reduced at the Ministry's discretion. Full inundation authorized effective immediately. No provision for resettlement of remaining population is required or anticipated.*

Signed. Stamped. Countersigned twice more. Beneath it, memos about sluice gate schedules. About "residual populations." About tonnage figures that were not tonnage at all. Page after page after page, in tidy bureaucratic hands, of eleven thousand people being converted into an engineering footnote.

"Oh," Mirabel said. It was a very small word for what it held.

Ada hadn't moved. She was staring at the signature block on Directive 44, and Mirabel realized her captain recognized the name — a name that now belonged to a ministry, a dynasty, someone still powerful, still *signing things*.

"This clears my debt ten times over," Ada whispered. "Corvo would pay. The navy would pay more. This is worth more than any wreck I'll ever—"

"Ada." Mirabel heard her own voice come out strange, steady, someone else's voice. "Eleven thousand people."

Ada closed her eyes.

And that was when the searchlight found them — not distant now, not sweeping the reeds, but blazing full upon the *Kestrel*'s deck, white and total, and through a loudspeaker crackling across the water came a voice Mirabel already dreaded:

*"Vessel in the dead channel. You are boarded. Do not touch the salvage."*

Suri was already moving for the skiff. "Captain," she said, low and urgent, "the archive at the source can authenticate every page. Monsoon seals it in six days. After that, the truth drowns a second time — forever."

Ada opened her eyes, looked at the light, looked at the chest, and grabbed the wheel beside Mirabel.

"Hold on," she said, and rammed the throttle forward into the dark.
