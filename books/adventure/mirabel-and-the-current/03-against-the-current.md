## 3. Against the Current

**Ada**

The delta let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has decided not to crush — slowly, and with the promise of trying again.

Ada ran the *Kestrel* hard through the braided channels for two days, sleeping in forty-minute shifts, Mirabel at the wheel more than she was off it. Behind them the navy's patrol boats fell away at the salt line; whatever orders their commander carried, they did not include being seen chasing a salvage scow into open water. Ahead of them the river narrowed, browned, and began — against all sense — to run the wrong way.

That was the thing about the Vell that no chart told you. Past the last ferry town it turned its back on the sea. Every mile upstream was a mile fought for, throttle wide open just to stand still, the current dragging at the hull like a hand on the ankle.

"Monsoon's four days out," Mirabel said, squinting at the sky, which had gone the color of old pewter. "Maybe five."

"Archive's three days up if the river behaves."

"The river doesn't behave."

"No," Ada agreed. "It doesn't."

In the hold, lashed under tarpaulin and chain, the chest rode like ballast. Ada had stopped opening it after the first night. Not because she couldn't bear what was inside — she could, barely — but because every time she looked at those water-stained pages she saw prices instead of names. What a naval commander would pay to make them vanish. What a newspaper might scrape together. What Suri's people had already paid, decades ago, in a way that never appeared on any ledger.

Suri sat at the stern rail now, wrapped in a blanket that had once been navy issue, watching the banks slide past. She hadn't slept much either. When Ada came aft, the exile spoke without turning.

"You're doing the arithmetic again. I can hear it."

"I'm plotting our course."

"You're pricing my dead." Suri turned then, and her scarred face held no anger, which was worse. "It's all right. Everyone does. The ones who drowned us did it on a ledger too."

Ada didn't answer. Upstream, thunderheads were stacking themselves against the mountains like freight waiting to be loaded.

* * *

**Mirabel**

The gorge closed over them on the second day like a door.

Mirabel felt it before she saw it — the current stiffening, the walls leaning in, the light going from gray to green as the cliffs shouldered out the sky. The Vell funneled between rock faces close enough to spit across, and the water in the narrows moved fast enough to stand a spoon in.

Which was why the gunboat sitting mid-channel, anchor down, smoke rising lazy from its stack, made her stomach drop through the deck plates.

"Ada," she said quietly. "Company."

They came around the bend into full view and there was nowhere to go. The gunboat mounted a bow gun that wasn't even pointed at them, which was its own kind of message: *we don't need to aim yet.* A figure stood at the rail — an officer, whites immaculate despite the spray, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring scenery.

A loudhailer crackled. "Vessel *Kestrel*. This is Commander Voss, Riverine Command. You are carrying stolen naval property. Heave to and prepare to be boarded."

Ada stood at the wheel, and Mirabel watched her do the math again — fuel, distance, the gorge walls, the monsoon stacking up behind them like a debt collector. There was no running. There was no fighting. There was only the narrow slot of water to the left where the current tore white around a submerged spur, a passage a fool would try and a corpse would fail.

Then a third voice joined the conversation, from the cliff shadow above the gunboat, and it belonged to neither of them.

"Heave to, Commander. That's a lovely gunboat. It would be a shame if it developed a leak."

Mirabel looked up. On the ledge above the narrows, half-hidden in the rocks, men crouched behind rail-mounted launchers — homemade things, scavenged, but aimed downhill at an anchored hull. And standing front and center, arms crossed, wearing a coat worth more than the *Kestrel*, was a broad man with a silver ring in one ear and the patient smile of someone who owned every bad decision on this stretch of river.

"Ossian Vane," Ada breathed.

"Smuggler lord," said Suri, coming forward. "King of the gorge. Half the navy is on his payroll and the other half is afraid of him."

Below, Voss's loudhailer crackled again, angrier now. "Vane, this is a naval matter—"

"And this is my river, Commander. We've had this discussion." Vane spread his hands, magnanimous. "But I'm a reasonable man. Leave the vessel. Take your crew. Walk home. Nobody drowns today."

Voss's silence lasted ten seconds. Then the gunboat's engine turned over, and it backed out of the narrows with the unhurried dignity of a predator that intended to be waiting downstream.

Vane looked up at the *Kestrel* and beckoned.

* * *

**Ada**

Ossian Vane received them in a cave that had been furnished like a parlor — rugs on stone, lamps on brackets, a table set with real porcelain and a bottle of wine older than the republic whose seal was stamped on the chest sitting between them.

He'd had them bring the chest up themselves. Of course he had. He'd wanted to look at it while he talked.

"A nation that isn't on maps," Vane mused, turning his glass. "Do you know how much I love things that aren't on maps, Captain Thackeray? They're the only inventory worth holding. Everything else depreciates." He tapped the lid once, lightly, almost fondly. "I'll take it off your hands. In exchange: safe passage through the gorge, my boats running interference with Voss all the way to the source, fuel, food, and my personal guarantee that no one shoots at you for three days. That's a generous market rate."

"And if we say no?"

"Then you can try the narrows on your own, with the monsoon coming and Voss anchored below like a bill come due." He smiled. "But we both know you didn't climb up here to say no. You climbed up here to find out the price. So here it is."

He set down the glass.

"The woman. Suri. She stays with me when you leave."

The cave went very quiet. Mirabel, beside her, had stopped breathing. Across the table Suri sat perfectly still, watching Ada with that terrible calm, as if she'd known the shape of this moment since the delta.

"Why?" Ada asked, and hated how level her voice came out.

"Because she's the witness, Captain. Papers burn. People testify. If the chest ever comes to light, I want the living proof of it somewhere I control — not running loose making speeches." He shrugged. "She eats well. She's treated well. Better than the river would treat her. But she stays."

Ada looked at the chest. At the wine, the rugs, the man who priced everything. Three days of passage. The archive. The truth, authenticated, delivered — bought with a person.

And then she looked at Mirabel, and understood that Vane had chosen wrong. He thought loyalty was a resource with one seam. He thought she'd bargain with the stranger because the stranger was worth less to her.

"Suri stays aboard," Ada said. "Take me instead."

Vane's eyebrows rose.

"I'm the captain. I'm the one the navy wants. Hold me for three days — collateral until they've passed the gorge and the chest is authenticated at the source. My deckhand runs the boat, the exile rides along, and if anyone defaults, you still have the valuable one." She leaned forward. "You don't want a witness, Vane. Witnesses are trouble. You want leverage. I'm offering you better leverage than the woman everyone's sympathetic to."

Silence. Then Vane laughed, a great delighted bark, and refilled her glass.

"Oh, I *like* you. Terrible instinct for self-preservation, magnificent instinct for theater." He raised the glass. "Done. Three days. Your people go through the gorge tonight. You stay here, drinking my wine, until my runners confirm they've cleared the source road." His eyes glittered. "And Captain — if they default, I sell you to Voss by weight."

That night Ada stood on the ledge above the narrows and watched the *Kestrel* fight upstream into the dark, Mirabel at the wheel, Suri beside her, her whole life receding into the gorge with the current dragging at its hull.

Three days, she told herself. She'd bought them three days.

Behind her, deep in the cave, Ossian Vane was already sending a runner downriver toward Commander Voss's anchorage — and Ada, watching the water, never saw him go.
