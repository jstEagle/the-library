## 4. The Source

The river ended the way all lies end — quietly, in a place no one bothered to look.

Ada had imagined a spring gushing from rock, something dramatic. Instead the current simply thinned and spread into a high green valley where the water came up through the earth itself, seeping from a hillside that had swallowed a city two generations ago. Black rooftops broke the surface like drowned men's knuckles. A bell tower leaned at an angle that defied sense, its bronze bell still hanging, furred white with mineral bloom. The water was so clear it looked like absence — you could see forty feet down to streets, doorways, a fountain with a stone bird perched on its rim.

"The archive," Suri said, standing at the bow of the *Magpie* with her scarred hands folded. "Under the chapel. My grandmother said they flooded it on purpose. Sealed the records under sixty feet of water and called it progress."

"Everything about this river is on purpose," Ada said.

Mirabel was at the tiller, easing them in past a submerged wall, and she didn't look back when she said, "Then let's finish what they started."

* * *

The dive was hers alone. That had been decided without discussion; Ada Thackeray dove alone, always had, and this last time she wanted it that way.

She went down in the grey morning light with a cutting torch and a salvage bag and nothing else worth losing. The cold came up through her suit like a hand closing. She swam down the bell tower's spine, using it as a guide-line, past windows glazed with silt, until the chapel roof opened beneath her — caved long ago, a black doorway into the dark.

Inside, the archive was exactly what the chest had promised: shelves of lead-sealed cylinders racked in rows, a library built to be forgotten. The water had been kind to them. Lead doesn't rot. She pried one free, then another, working by torchlight while her breath hissed loud as surf in her own ears. Each cylinder bore the crest she'd first seen stamped on that chest weeks ago — the twin rivers, the crowned star — the mark of a nation that existed now only in documents like these, and in the memory of people like Suri.

One cylinder was heavier than the rest, its seal unbroken. She cracked it against a shelf edge and the contents spilled into her gloved hands: orders, signed. Dates. Numbers of boats. Numbers of people. A colonel's neat handwriting describing how the valley would be inundated "with the population advised to relocate north," followed by a second memo admitting the population had not been advised, could not be, because relocation would invite questions, and questions were more dangerous than drowning.

Ada read it twice in the torchlight, floating in a dead woman's church, and felt the hunger that had driven her whole life turn over and die somewhere in her chest. This wasn't salvage. It never had been. She'd been hired to help bury a grave, and she'd nearly sold the shovel.

She filled the bag. Eleven cylinders. She kicked for the surface and did not look back at the shelves, though she felt them watching.

* * *

The navy commander was waiting for her when she surfaced.

Not a patrol boat this time — the gunboat itself, grey and patient, anchored in the narrows below the valley with its deck gun trained casually on the *Magpie*. Commander Vess stood on Ada's own deck when she climbed the ladder, dripping, the salvage bag slung over her shoulder. Two sailors flanked him. Mirabel stood at the rail with her jaw set. Suri sat on the hatch coaming, very still, like a blade at rest.

Vess was younger than Ada expected, with careful eyes and a voice pitched for reasonableness.

"Ms. Thackeray." He gestured at the bag. "Before you say anything — I've read your file. I know what you owe Harrow Quay. I know what he does to people who can't pay." He smiled thinly. "I also have authority to discharge debts incurred against crown-chartered operations. All of it. Every cent. You walk away clean. Boat, name, future."

"And the bag?"

"Goes home with me. It's classified material recovered from a restricted site. There are protocols."

"There's a massacre in there," Ada said. "That's the protocol."

His smile didn't move but something behind it did. "There's history," he said. "History is complicated. Drowning is simple. I'd hate to see you make it complicated for yourself." He glanced at Mirabel, then Suri, letting the arithmetic show. "Three of you. One gunboat. Do the sums, Ms. Thackeray. You're good at sums — it's why you're in debt."

Ada looked at the water, clear and green and full of dead men's rooftops. She thought about the colonel's tidy handwriting. *Questions were more dangerous than drowning.* Vess was just the same sentence wearing a cleaner uniform.

She shrugged the bag off her shoulder and held it out — and at the last moment turned, and pressed it into Suri's arms instead.

"Debt's mine," she told Vess. "Truth isn't yours to buy."

For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then Vess exhaled through his nose, almost professional in his disappointment, and said, "Board the vessel. Take the bag."

And Ada walked to the stern of her own boat, the *Magpie*, eleven years of her life welded into every plate of her, and opened the sea-cock.

Mirabel understood first — Mirabel always understood first — and hauled Suri toward the skiff without a word. Water came up through the bilge fast and cold and honest. Ada stepped over the rail into the skiff as the *Magpie* began to settle, and Vess shouted orders that were already too late, because the narrows were narrow, and a sinking hull drifts where the current tells it to.

They watched from the shallows as the *Magpie* went down across the channel, stern-first, her mast catching on the submerged wall and canting into a barrier no gunboat captain would risk his keel on. The grey ship backed water, horned, furious, held.

"She was a good boat," Mirabel said quietly.

"She was a debt," Ada said. "Now I'm out of both."

Suri clutched the bag to her chest like a child, and looked at Ada with an expression Ada couldn't name and didn't try to. Downriver, the gunboat's launch was being lowered — slow, bureaucratic, too late. Upstream, beyond the drowned city, the mountains waited, and beyond them the border, and beyond that the world that had agreed to forget.

"They'll want to see us," Suri said. "The ones who survived. They'll want to hear it from the diver who found it."

Ada looked at her empty hands, her empty harbor account, her whole life sitting on the riverbed behind her. It should have felt like ruin. It felt like breathing after a long dive.

"Then we'd better get moving," she said, "before monsoon seals the pass. I hear the walking's free."

Mirabel laughed — wet, disbelieving, alive — and took the first oar stroke toward the far shore, toward everything the river owed and was finally, at last, paying back.
