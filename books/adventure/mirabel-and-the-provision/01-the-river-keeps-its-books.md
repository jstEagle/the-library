## 1. The River Keeps Its Books

The Greywater woke before Astrid did, the way it always did, muttering against the hull of the *Kestrel's Debt* like a creditor who never slept. He lay in his bunk beneath the wheelhouse and listened to it work through its complaints — the slap of current against the bow, the groan of the mooring line, the long wet sigh of water finding its way around every obstacle a river could invent. Other men needed roosters. Astrid needed the river telling him what kind of day it intended to be.

This morning it sounded sullen. Low-voiced. The kind of murmur that meant the water was thinking about something.

He dressed in the dark out of habit, though there was no crew left to wake quietly for. That habit had outlived its purpose by two years now, and he kept it anyway, the way a man keeps a coat that no longer fits because throwing it away would mean admitting something had ended. His boots found the deck boards he knew by heart — the one that canted left at the companionway, the one with the split near the cargo hatch that would take skin if you crossed it barefoot in summer. He'd meant to fix that split for three seasons. A captain who couldn't be bothered to mend his own deck had either given up or made peace, and Astrid had never been sure which he'd done.

Fog lay on the river like a held breath. He stood at the rail with coffee going cold in his tin cup and read the water the way other men read ledgers — and the Greywater was nothing if not a ledger, every eddy an entry, every sandbar a debt marked *pending*, every drowned tree below the surface a note come due without warning. Most captains ran the Greywater by memory. Memory was how they died. The river changed its mind nightly, moving its bars in the dark like a merchant shuffling accounts, and the only honest book was the one written fresh each morning on the surface itself.

Astrid read it now. A boil of current off the eastern point where no boil belonged — new bar forming, maybe, or an old one grown ambitious. A slick tongue of smooth water hugging the far bank that said *deep channel here, trust me*, and Astrid didn't, because the Greywater lied in exactly that voice. A line of foam stitching downstream in a pattern too regular, marking a sunken snag with its teeth just under the surface, patient as a bailiff.

He finished the coffee. He cast off alone, which took twice as long as it should have and half as long as it used to, depending on how you counted, and took the *Kestrel's Debt* out into the fog with the engine turning over slow and sure beneath his feet.

* * *

The run downriver to Millbrook Landing was forty miles of arithmetic. Cargo today was salt pork, nails, and four bolts of dyed wool consigned to Hetta Brandt's dry goods store, plus a crate of lamp oil that rode badly and made him nervous. Ordinary freight. Honest weight. He stood at the wheel with one hand and let the other hang loose, ready, reading the channel as it unspooled ahead of him.

There were men on this river who called what Astrid did a gift. He'd heard them say it in dockside taverns, watching him thread the narrows above Corvane's Bridge where the current ran crooked and vindictive. *Gift*, they said, as if the river had chosen him for favor. Astrid knew better. The river hadn't chosen anything. It simply punished carelessness, consistently and without malice, and Astrid had spent thirty years learning to be careful in a way that looked like instinct from the outside. It wasn't love between him and the water. It was respect, formal and total, the way you'd respect a magistrate or a wolf.

The fog burned off by midmorning and the sun came up hard and white on the water, and Astrid ran the narrows with his eyes moving in their old pattern — bank, channel, bank, surface, sky — and came through without scraping paint. Below the bridge the valley opened up and the farms started, little holdings stitched along the banks, and he passed the first of the landing stages where a boy waved both arms at him and Astrid lifted two fingers off the wheel in answer. That was the whole conversation. It was enough. Words cost more than he liked to pay.

At Tarrow Bend he slowed the boat without deciding to, some older part of him doing the math before his mind caught up. There was a rock shelf under that bend, and when the water ran low in late summer it showed its edge like a scar, and three years ago — no. Four. Four years ago he'd come through here with a full crew and a full hold and Josiah Fenner at the stern calling depths, and they'd cleared the shelf by the width of a prayer, laughing about it after, and Josiah had said, *Captain, you read that water like your own handwriting,* and Astrid had said something modest and true, and that had been a good day, one of the last ones.

Josiah didn't call depths anymore. Nobody did.

Astrid brought his attention back to the present water and held it there, the way you'd hold a dog that wanted to run toward a grave.

* * *

Millbrook Landing smelled of sawdust and pig and the particular sourness of a town that owed more than it earned. He warped alongside the dock and began the unloading alone, hand over hand, the crate of lamp oil last because he distrusted it most. Hetta Brandt came down to count her wool and stayed to talk, because Hetta was one of perhaps four people on the entire Greywater whom Astrid tolerated at conversational length.

"You look thin," she said.

"I look the same."

"That's what thin looks like on you now." She signed his manifest with a stub of pencil, pressing hard. "You hear the office is raising the tolls again? Quarter-share on all commercial tonnage, effective next month."

"I heard."

"You heard because Corvane's clerk posted it on your own mast two weeks back, like he was nailing a notice to a dead tree." Hetta folded her arms. "Quarter-share, Astrid. On top of the inspection fees, the mooring levies, the 'channel maintenance' charge for a channel Corvane's dredges haven't touched since he took office. You know what he calls it?"

"I expect you'll tell me."

"*The Provision.*" She spat the word into the sawdust. "Provision for the river's upkeep, he says. Provision for the towns' protection. Man taxes the whole Greywater and calls it a gift to us." Her mouth went bitter. "My grandmother told me about the old legend, you know. The real Provision. Sea cave below Widow's Cliff, opens when the tide runs strange, full of everything a body needs — gold, grain, a place at the fire. Belonging, she called it. Whoever claims the Provision belongs somewhere forever." She snorted. "Corvane named his tax after a fairy story so it'd sound like charity."

"Everything on this river sounds like charity," Astrid said, "right up until you pay it."

Hetta looked at him a moment too long, the way women did when they were deciding whether to say the next thing. "You could fight it. The merchants would stand behind you. Half these landings would."

"The merchants stood behind me two years ago," Astrid said, "when Corvane's inspectors boarded my boat and found contraband in my own hold that I'd never seen in my life. Where were they then?" He kept his voice level, which took work. "They stood behind me all the way to the courthouse steps and then discovered urgent business elsewhere. I paid the fine. I lost my cargo license for a season. And my crew — " He stopped. Started again, flatter. "My crew scattered the way crews do when a boat stops being safe to stand aboard. Can't blame them. Corvane made it clear that anyone signing with me was signing onto a list."

"And you never found out who planted it."

"Oh, I found out." He'd spent one bad winter finding out, tracing the inspector's payments back through two intermediaries to an account in the magistrate's office, and then he'd sat on the knowledge like a man sitting on a lit stove, because knowing and proving were different countries and there was no ferry between them. "Knowing isn't worth anything on this river unless you can afford the interest."

Hetta pressed her lips together and let it go, which was a kindness. She took her wool and her opinions up the dock, and Astrid finished the unloading in silence, and the silence fit him the way the loneliness fit him: not well, but without gaps for anything else to get in.

That was the thing nobody understood about being alone on the water. It wasn't that he preferred it, exactly. It was that he'd arranged it, deliberately, plank by plank, after the inspection broke his crew apart — because people were the one cargo the Greywater couldn't teach him to read. Water lied, but it lied in patterns, and he knew them all. People lied in new shapes every time. Josiah had been the exception, and the exception had proven the rule by leaving with the rest, and Astrid had concluded that the safest number of people to trust was a number he could count on one hand with fingers to spare.

One, mostly. Himself. And himself only conditionally.

* * *

He took on return freight at dusk — furs, dried apples, a small strongbox of mill scrip — and cast off into the evening with the light dying copper behind the western bluffs. The river at night was a different document entirely, and he read it by ear as much as eye, the engine throttled low, listening for the change in sound where deep water went shallow, the hollow knock of current over a bar, the way the air moved differently above open channel than above drowned timber.

It was coming downriver past Widow's Cliff that he felt it.

Not saw it — felt it, in the hull first, a wrongness in the way the water carried him. The current had shifted. Not strengthened, not slackened — *shifted*, the whole river leaning toward the north bank like a crowd craning to see something. Astrid throttled down and drifted, and the moon came out from behind cloud, and he looked at the base of the cliffs and felt the hair rise on his forearms.

The tide was out. Farther out than he had ever seen it on the Greywater, which shouldn't have tides at all this far inland — and yet every old pilot swore the lower river breathed with the sea, faint and strange, and tonight it was exhaling like something dying. The water had fallen back from the cliff foot farther than any drought had managed, exposing a long gullet of black mud and stone — and at the end of it, a gap in the cliff face. A cave mouth, tall as a barn door, breathing cold air out at him across the exposed flats.

Astrid stood at the wheel a long time, engine idling, and thought about legends and about Hetta's grandmother and about the word *belonging*, which had always struck him as the most expensive word in any language.

Every book he trusted told him to log the anomaly, mark the chart, and move on. The river punishes arrogance; curiosity was just arrogance wearing a lantern.

But the tide was still falling. And the cave was still open. And in thirty years the Greywater had never once shown him anything it didn't intend him to see.

Astrid Dravenmoor turned his boat toward the cliff, dropped anchor in the last honest depth, and reached for his lantern.
