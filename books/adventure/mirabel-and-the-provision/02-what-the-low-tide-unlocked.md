## 2. What the Low Tide Unlocked

The Greywater had never once done Astrid Dravenmoor a favor, and he trusted it precisely because of that.

He knew its moods the way other men knew their wives' tempers — by the set of the current against the pilings, by the color of the foam, by the particular groan the *Kestrel's Debt* made in her timbers when the river ran shallow over the gravel bars below Widow's Cliff. Twenty-two years he'd run this water. He'd learned her alphabet in bruises and near-drownings, and she had never lied to him. People lied. The river only killed you honestly.

So when he came around the bend above Widow's Cliff on a grey October morning and saw the water standing four feet lower than any tide chart in his cabin said it should be, he did not celebrate the extra depth beneath his keel. He went cold all the way down his spine.

Rivers didn't do that. Not the Greywater. Not without something upstream holding the water back — a slide, a dam of fallen timber, ice — or something downstream pulling it away. And neither explanation left him comfortable, because both meant the river was about to change her mind, and when the Greywater changed her mind she did not send invitations.

He brought the boat in slow, past the drowned willows that usually showed only their crowns, now bare to the roots like drowned men standing upright. The cliff itself rose sheer and black on the eastern bank, streaked with mineral weep, crowned with wind-bent pines. At its base, where the water always chewed white and hungry, there was exposed now a long shelf of wet black stone — and cut into that stone, a mouth.

A cave mouth. Tall as a barn door, dark as a closed eye.

Astrid stood at the rail with his coffee going cold in his hand and felt the old stories climb up out of his memory like damp. Every child on the Greywater grew up on the tale of the Provision. Smugglers' gold, some said. A hoard laid down by the first free-traders when the magistrate's grandfather began taxing every barge on the river — supplies enough to outfit an army, hidden where no tax man would ever think to look. Belonging to whoever found it. That was the promise that made men stupid. *Whoever claims it.* Whole crews had wrecked themselves on Widow's Cliff over the generations, drunk on the legend, probing the base of the rock at high water for a door that wasn't there.

It was there now. The river had opened it like a hand unclenching.

"Shouldn't be possible," he muttered, and drank the cold coffee anyway, because waste was a sin worse than superstition.

He told himself he was only going to look. He told himself a lot of things that morning.

* * *

He took the dinghy in alone, which was no hardship, since alone was how he did most things these days. Two years since the rigged inspection, since Magistrate Corvane's men had found "irregularities" in his manifests — records Corvane's own clerks had written — and fined him into selling half his cargo rights, and his crew had scattered like startled birds. Rennick went first, then the Voss brothers, then old Marta who'd cooked for him eleven years. He couldn't blame them. A captain who couldn't pay couldn't feed them, and hunger doesn't wait on loyalty. But he'd stopped expecting anyone aboard besides himself, and the boat had grown quiet in the way a house grows quiet after a funeral.

The dinghy scraped onto the wet stone shelf with a sound like a knuckle cracking. He stepped out into air that smelled of river silt and something older underneath — cold stone, and faintly, impossibly, tallow. Candle smoke. Sealed places didn't smell of candle smoke unless someone had been burning candles in them recently.

His hand went to the boathook out of habit rather than decision. He stood at the mouth of the cave a long moment, listening. Water dripped somewhere inside, patient as a clock. Beyond that, nothing.

"Anybody home," he called, and the cave ate his voice and gave back nothing.

He went in anyway. Curiosity was the one debt he'd never managed to pay off.

* * *

Inside, the passage widened after twenty yards into a chamber tall enough to stand a mast in, and Astrid stopped dead, because the legends hadn't been lying — they'd just been wrong about the shape of the treasure.

Crates. Dozens of them, stacked with smuggler's neatness along the walls: salt pork in wax-sealed barrels, flour, dried apples, lamp oil, wool blankets wrapped in oilcloth, rope coils hung from iron pegs driven into the stone. A cache, provisioned with care and maintained — the oilcloth was fresh, the wax seals unbroken. Someone had been restocking this place within the last season. Along one wall sat a flat-bottomed skiff, upside down on trestles, varnished, oiled, ready to launch through a low channel at the chamber's rear that gurgled with the sound of moving water. Whoever built this room had planned for escape, not just storage.

And on a crate near the center, burned into the wood with a branding iron, a mark he recognized from thirty-year-old hulls rotting in the backwaters upriver: the old free-trade sigil. A keel and a rising sun. The mark of the charter folk — the families who'd held the Greywater trade before Corvane's office had strangled it with fees and forged debts until every last one of them lost their boats.

Astrid walked the rows slowly, touching nothing, reading everything. This was not treasure. This was somebody's insurance policy. Somebody who expected to need it soon.

He was crouched at the skiff, running a thumb along her gunwale, when the light behind him changed.

Not dimmed — *blocked*. A shadow fell across the crate-stacks, small and sharp-edged, and Astrid came up off his heels turning, boathook coming around in a wide arc that stopped six inches short of splitting open the skull of a girl.

She hadn't flinched. That was the first thing he noticed. Fifteen years old, maybe, thin as a pike pole, hair cut short and ragged as if she'd done it herself with a knife and no mirror. She stood at the mouth of the passage with a boat hook of her own leveled at his chest, two-handed, elbows locked, stance ugly but committed — the stance of someone who had decided that if she was going to die here, she was going to make it expensive.

"Don't," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle of the word and she hated it; he could see her hating it.

"Wouldn't dream of it," Astrid said. He let the arc finish slow and lowered the hook until its head rested on the stone. "You're standing between me and the only exit, though, so I'll point out we're at an impasse with poor prospects."

"I'm not afraid of you."

"Good. Fear makes people swing wild." He kept his hands visible, easy, the way you'd approach a spooked animal on a dock. "I'm Astrid Dravenmoor. I run cargo. My boat's moored maybe two hundred yards off your front door. I came in because the tide did something it's never done in twenty-two years, and I wanted to know why."

"The tide didn't do it."

"No?"

"They blew the sill." She jerked her chin toward the rear channel. "Upstream. Three days ago. Blasted a logjam they'd built to hold the water back, so the level would drop and the cave would open, and whoever was hiding in here would have to come out or drown when they let the river go again." She said it flatly, reciting facts she'd turned over so many times they'd gone smooth. "They're coming back. Maybe today. Maybe tomorrow. When the water rises, this whole chamber floods to the ceiling."

Astrid looked at the crates. At the skiff. At the girl with the boat hook and the eyes of someone who had already counted the cost of everything twice.

"You're the cargo," he said slowly.

"I'm nobody's cargo." The hook didn't waver. "My name is Mirabel."

"And what are you doing in a smuggler's cellar, Mirabel, waiting on a flood?"

For a moment something moved behind her face — grief, huge and badly packed, shifting under the floorboards of her composure. Then it was gone, shuttered, and what remained was harder and colder than anything he'd seen in adult eyes in years.

"My father's ledger," she said. She touched the breast of her coat, where a book-shaped weight rode against her ribs. "And staying alive long enough to deliver it."

"To who?"

"Anyone who isn't bought."

Astrid Dravenmoor had spent two years learning exactly how much of the Greywater was bought. He thought about Corvane's clerks writing lies in triplicate. He thought about Marta packing her pots in silence. He thought about the word *irregularities*, and the fine, and the quiet way a man's livelihood could be taken apart by paper while everyone watched and no one objected.

He thought about the fact that the river had dropped four feet to show him this door, and that he had never once believed in coincidence on this water.

Behind the girl, far down the rear channel, the water made a new sound — a low, rising note, like the cave clearing its throat. The river upstream, released, beginning to come home.

Mirabel heard it too. Her grip shifted on the hook.

"That's early," she whispered. "They weren't supposed to let it go till dusk."

Astrid looked at the skiff on her trestles, at the channel running fast now at the chamber's rear, at the girl who had been ready to fight a grown man to the death over a book.

"How many can that skiff carry?" he asked.

"Two," she said. "Three, if nobody minds getting wet."

"Then we'd best mind getting wet," Astrid said, and reached for the bowline.
