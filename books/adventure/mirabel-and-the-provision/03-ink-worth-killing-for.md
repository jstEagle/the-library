## 3. Ink Worth Killing For

The boat hook was heavier than it looked. I'd learned that the hard way, holding it for three days while the tide stayed out and my arms shook and I told myself that if anyone came through that cave mouth, I would swing until they stopped moving or I did.

So when the man with the river-weathered face came around the stacked crates, I swung.

He caught the haft one-handed — *caught* it, like he'd been catching falling cargo his whole life — and looked at me over the top of it with eyes the grey of the Greywater in November.

"Easy," he said. "I'm not the one you're afraid of."

"You don't know who I'm afraid of."

"I know who owns this river." He let go of the hook slowly, showing me his empty hands, palms scarred from rope. "Astrid Dravenmoor. Captain of the *Lark's Mercy*. And you're living in a cave that's supposed to be a legend."

I didn't lower the hook all the way. "The legend says there's treasure."

"The legend says a lot of things." He glanced at the crates behind me — flour gone hard as brick, salt pork, lamp oil, coils of rope, a canvas-wrapped skiff frame no bigger than a coffin. "This isn't treasure. This is somebody's escape kit."

"It's mine," I said, which was half true. My father had built it into the cliff two summers ago, back when he still believed the magistrate might come for him someday and wanted a door out of the world. He'd never told me what it was for. He'd only said, *Mirabel, if anything happens to me, the tide will show you where to go.* I'd thought he was being poetic. Fathers are allowed to be poetic right up until they drown.

* * *

He made a fire small enough to hide behind a boulder, and we sat across it, and I told him.

Not all at once. It came out in pieces, the way water comes through a cracked hull — first a trickle, then everything.

"My father was Tam Wrenlow," I said. "You've heard the name."

His jaw tightened. Everyone on the river had heard the name. Tam Wrenlow had held the trade charter for the middle Greywater for twenty years — the paper that said his barges could run grain and timber and salt between the towns without paying Corvane's tolls. The charter was why Halfway Landing had a market instead of a graveyard. The charter was why my father owned two boats and a house with a blue door and could afford to teach his daughter letters and knots and how to read water.

"Corvane wanted it," I said. "He's wanted it since before I can remember. But charters don't just get taken. They have to be forfeited. So he built a forfeiture."

I pulled the ledger out of its oilcloth wrapping and set it between us. It was not an impressive thing to look at — a fat book bound in scuffed leather, its corners worn round, my father's initials burned into the cover. But Astrid Dravenmoor went very still when he saw it, the way men go still around things that might go off.

"Every entry in here is in my father's hand," I said. "Every debt, every payment, every toll he ever collected or paid. Corvane's clerks keep copies of everything — that's the law, that's how they audit the charter holders. Two years ago the audit came. Corvane's men brought their copy of the ledger and compared it against ours, and theirs said my father owed the magistrate's office four thousand crowns in unpaid duties. Four thousand. My father nearly laughed. Then he nearly wept. Because the entries were *beautiful*, Captain. Same ink, same hand, same dates, woven right in among the true ones. Someone had copied my father's writing so well that even my father couldn't prove it wasn't his."

"He contested it."

"He contested it for eight months. Petitions, witnesses, a hired examiner from Kestrel Ford who took one look at Corvane's seal on the summons and withdrew. You don't beat the man who writes the law with the law. In the end the charter was stripped for default, and the debts passed to the estate, and we lost the boats, and then—" My voice did something I hated. I made it stop. "Then last spring, my father went out to check his fish traps in weather no sane man would launch in. They found him snagged under Widow's Cliff. His skiff was found upright and dry, tied off neat as church. A drowned man doesn't tie off his own boat."

Astrid stared into the fire. When he spoke, his voice was low and flat, the voice of a man reading a river he already knew was going to kill someone. "Who told you it was arranged?"

"Nobody told me. That's the point. Nobody *had* to." I turned the ledger open to the back pages, where my father had kept his private notes, the ones he thought no auditor would ever see because they were written in our family cipher — a child's cipher, really, the one he'd invented when I was seven and proud of knowing secrets. "But he knew. Look."

The entries were short. Dates and sums, mostly. And then, six weeks before his death:

*Corvane's clerk offered me 300 to 'correct' my copy. Refused. He smiled. Men smile before they drown you.*

And below that, in a shakier hand:

*If I am taken by the water, it was not the water.*

Astrid read it twice. Then he closed the book with a gentleness that surprised me, like closing a coffin lid.

"This is the only true copy?"

"The only one that exists. Corvane thinks he destroyed it when he seized the estate. His men searched our house for three days. They missed it because my father hid it inside a hymnal, and Corvane's men don't strike me as churchgoing." I wrapped it back in the oilcloth. "One book, Captain. One book with forged entries and a dead man's handwriting and a confession in cipher. That's the whole case. That's the whole *war*. Without it, I'm a runaway orphan girl telling stories. With it—"

"With it you're dangerous."

"With it I'm *proof*."

* * *

He asked me where I meant to go, and I told him: Kestrel Ford, over the mountains, to the free-town assembly. Every autumn after the harvest fair, the free towns sent delegates to hear grievances and settle disputes the magistrate's courts wouldn't touch. It was weeks away and getting colder every day, and I had a skiff frame, a boat hook, and fourteen coppers.

Astrid looked at me for a long time. I got the feeling he was doing arithmetic — the kind where you count what a thing will cost you, not in coin.

"You understand what happens if Corvane learns you're alive," he said. "You're not a runaway to him. You're a loose thread. He'll pull until you unravel."

"I understand it better than you do. I've been unraveling since spring."

Before he could answer, I heard it.

Hooves.

Not close — the shale slopes above the cliff carry sound strangely, and what I heard was more echo than hoofbeat, a hollow drumming somewhere up along the ridge road. But I knew it the way I knew my father's footsteps on the dock. Four horses, maybe five, moving at a walk. Patrol pace. Patient pace. The pace of men being paid whether they find anything or not.

Astrid was already moving. He killed the fire with a sweep of sand, no wasted motion, and pressed two fingers to his lips, and pointed toward the dark mouth of the cave where the skiff frame waited.

We worked without speaking. He assembled the frame while I fed the skin hull over it — my father had designed it, and even grieving I remembered the order, because he'd made me practice it a dozen times, saying *a boat you can't build in the dark is a boat you'll die waiting for.* By the time the drumming above grew distinct enough to count riders, we were carrying the little craft down the slick stones toward the waterline, the ledger strapped flat against my chest beneath my coat, riding over my heart like a second heartbeat.

The tide was coming back in. Of course it was. The river keeps its books too, and it had lent us the cave and now it wanted the loan repaid.

"There," Astrid breathed.

The riders had stopped on the ridge. Torchlight bloomed against the low clouds, orange and wrong, and a voice called down into the dark — a bored voice, a voice reading from a paper: *"By order of Magistrate Corvane, any person with knowledge of the whereabouts of Mirabel Wrenlow will report to the office of the river court. Reward posted. Concealment is a crime against the charter."*

*Against the charter,* I thought. *His charter. The one he stole.*

They began to descend. Slowly, torches swinging, boots grinding on wet stone. Five of them. I counted torches the way other girls counted stars.

Astrid pushed the skiff into the black water and held it steady against the current. "In. Sit low. Touch nothing."

"What about you?"

"I'm steering." He hesitated, and in the torchlight I saw something cross his face — not fear exactly, but the look of a man taking on weight he'd been trying to put down for years. "Girl, whatever you think I am, I'm not a hero. I run cargo. I owe that office money myself. There is no version of tonight where I come out ahead."

"Then why are you pushing the boat?"

He didn't answer. He shoved us off the stones and swung aboard as the current took us, and the cave mouth slid past, and the torches shrank to sparks, and the Greywater folded its dark water over us like a page turning.

We ran without lights. Astrid steered by sound — by the different voices the river makes over gravel and clay and stone — and once, when the current shouldered us toward a line of white foam that meant rocks underneath, he leaned the whole skiff into the turn with his body, and we slipped through a gap I never even saw.

Above us, faint and fading, the torches moved along the cliff. Looking for a cave that was already closing its mouth under the rising tide.

I lay in the bottom of the skiff with my father's ledger against my chest and listened to the water, and I made it a promise, the way you make promises to rivers: *You took him. I'm going to take back everything else.*

Ahead, far downstream, the lights of the first river town glimmered — and on the dock road beyond them, small and moving, more torches. Waiting. As if someone had already guessed which way a fugitive would run.

Astrid saw them the same moment I did. His jaw set.

"Hold on," he said. "It's going to be a long night."
