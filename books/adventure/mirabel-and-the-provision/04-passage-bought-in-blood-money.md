## 4. Passage Bought in Blood Money

The notice went up at dawn, and by the time the mist burned off the Greywater, every dockhand in Saltmere Landing had read it twice.

Astrid heard about it before he saw it — heard it in the way conversation died when he walked the plank road toward the chandlery, in the way men found sudden business elsewhere, in the way a boy selling smoked eel gathered his tray and left without finishing his sales. The river had taught him to read water: eddies, pressure lines, the pale seam where current fought current. It had not taught him to read silence like this.

The notice was nailed to the customs post beside the mooring fees board, and it was written large enough that even a man squinting against morning glare could take its measure from ten feet off.

*By order of Magistrate Corvane, Lord of the Greywater Reaches:* two hundred crowns for information leading to the recovery of one Mirabel Voss, fugitive, wanted in connection with theft of official records and the death of her father, Aldous Voss, charter-holder, deceased. *And fifty crowns additional* — and here the ink seemed to lean closer, the way a knife leans — *for the apprehension of Astrid Dravenmoor, riverboat captain, named accomplice to the above.*

Fifty crowns. He almost laughed. He'd once hauled a season's worth of iron ore down from Kestrel Ford for sixty, and half of that had gone straight back into Corvane's coffers as "inspection fees" levied by men who'd never touched a rope in their lives. Fifty crowns was what his life was worth to the magistrate. Fifty crowns was an insult dressed in wax seal.

But it wasn't the number that made his hands go cold around the mooring line. It was the word *accomplice.* Corvane hadn't waited for rumor to reach him. He knew. Which meant someone had talked, or someone had watched the cave mouth below Widow's Cliff, or — likeliest of all — the magistrate had simply decided that whoever pulled that girl out of the Provision's hiding place was guilty of something, because guilt was a tool Corvane kept sharpened for exactly this kind of weather.

Astrid stood there long enough that the gulls started landing on his rail behind him, bold as tax collectors.

Then he went to find his crew.

* * *

He should have known better than to call them crew anymore. They hadn't been crew since the rigged inspection three years back — since Corvane's men had boarded the *Grey Heron*, planted contraband in her hold, and stripped Astrid's papers until he paid a fine that ate two seasons' profit. The men who'd signed on with him after that were fair-weather hands, hired run by run, paid by the mile. They steered well enough. They didn't owe him their hearts, and he'd never asked for them.

Still, when he came down the dock at midmorning and saw Tam Wicker and the brothers Oll and Fen Marsh standing at the *Heron*'s gangplank with their gear already bundled, something in his chest went tight and sour, like bilge water rising.

Tam saw him coming and didn't have the grace to look away. That was the thing about Tam — honest to a fault, which on the Greywater was less a virtue than a liability.

"Astrid." He shifted his bundle from one shoulder to the other. "You've seen the post."

"I've seen the post."

"Fifty crowns." Oll said it like a prayer. Fen elbowed him, but not hard.

"It's not about the money," Tam said quickly. "It's — look. I've got a wife. You know Marta's got the little one coming in the thaw. I can't be named in a magistrate's writ. If they come asking who ran you upstream—"

"You don't have to explain, Tam."

"I do, though. I want to." The big man's jaw worked. "You pulled me out of the weir flood at Cutter's Bend. I haven't forgotten it. But I can't drown with you, either. There's a difference between owing a man and dying for him."

"There is," Astrid said. "I taught most of this river that lesson myself."

The Marsh brothers were already backing away, and Tam lingered one more moment, eyes going past Astrid's shoulder to the cabin where a lamp still burned behind fogged glass — where a fifteen-year-old girl sat with her father's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat.

"She's just a girl," Tam said quietly.

"That's what I keep telling myself."

Tam nodded once, the way men nod over graves, and walked up the dock without looking back. By noon the *Grey Heron* was empty except for Astrid and the smell of the tar he'd been laying into her seams since before sunrise. By dusk, three separate men had come by to ask — casually, too casually — whether the captain might be needing to sell her, seeing as how she'd be sitting idle, seeing as how a man in his position might want liquid funds.

By dark, Astrid understood the shape of it completely. The reward wasn't meant to catch him. It was meant to isolate him — to peel away every hand that might help, every dock that would let him tie off, every ear that would listen without calculating fifty crowns against the cost of listening. Corvane didn't need riders yet. He only needed patience. A man alone on a river dries up like a beached boat.

* * *

Mirabel came out onto the deck after full dark, moving quiet the way she always did, though there was nowhere left to hide aboard a hundred-foot hull with one soul aboard. She'd heard everything through the cabin wall. Of course she had. Walls on a riverboat were thinner than excuses.

"They'll burn her," she said. It wasn't a question. She was looking at the *Heron* the way Astrid had seen sailors look at drowned friends — knowing, already, what rivers took.

"Who will?"

"The magistrate. When he takes you. Or when he decides taking you is more trouble than burning you." She turned to face him, and in the lamplight her face was all sharp angles and older griefs than fifteen years should carry. "That's what he did to my father's warehouse. Didn't even wait for the court ruling. Just — fire, and then paperwork saying the fire proved guilt."

Astrid coiled a line slowly, giving his hands work so his mouth wouldn't say anything stupid. "You're telling me to give you up."

"I'm telling you what it costs." Her voice didn't waver, and that was the worst part. She'd done this arithmetic already, alone, in the dark, while he slept. "Two hundred crowns is a new keel. New rigging. Your debts to his office cleared twice over. You could sail anywhere after. Up the Marrow, down to the delta. Somewhere his writ doesn't reach."

"And leave you on his dock?"

"On his *mercy*, maybe. Maybe he'd only take the ledger and let me go to the workhouse. Maybe." She hugged the ledger bundle tighter. "I did the sums, Captain. I'm cargo. Bad cargo. You should jettison me."

For a long moment the only sound was the river working against the pilings, patient as a debt collector, and somewhere far off the low moan of a night bird hunting the shallows.

"You know what the first thing my father ever taught me about passengers was?" Astrid said finally.

"To pay up front?"

Despite everything, his mouth twitched. "That if a man goes overboard, you turn the boat. Doesn't matter if he's a stranger, doesn't matter if stopping costs you the run, doesn't matter if the current's against you. You turn the boat. Because the day you don't turn it, you're not a captain anymore. You're just a man steering cargo." He set the coil down. "I lost my papers once to Corvane's inspectors. Lost my savings, my reputation, three years of runs. You know what I never lost? The right to look at myself in the mirror of a morning. I'm not rich, girl, but I'm not poor enough to sell you."

Mirabel stared at him. Then, quietly, in a voice stripped of all its weaponized grief: "My father used to say almost the same thing. About cargo. About people being cargo you're responsible for." Her chin trembled once and stilled. "He'd have liked you."

"He'd have hated my rates."

She laughed — a small, cracked sound, surprised out of her — and it was the first time he'd heard her laugh, and he thought: *there she is. Underneath all that ash. Somebody's daughter.*

"We go upstream, then," she said. "Kestrel crossing."

"Kestrel crossing." He said it flatly, because saying it any other way would mean admitting what it was: eleven days of river against current, then the high pass, then snow country, in a season when the snows were coming early. "First light. We take the skiff up the Marrow channel where the customs cutters can't follow, and we abandon the *Heron* at Halfway Landing."

"Abandon her?" Mirabel's voice caught. "You love this boat. I've watched you talk to her planks."

"I love her planks, yes." Astrid looked down the length of his deck — the scarred rail, the wheel he'd gripped through ten thousand miles of grey water, the lantern swinging its slow arc like a lighthouse for a harbor that no longer existed. "Which is why I'm not going to watch them take her. Better she sits empty at a smuggler's dock than burns as evidence in Corvane's justice."

He turned for the cabin, then stopped.

"One thing, Mirabel. Before we cast off and there's no turning back. Once we're upstream, there's no surrendering you without becoming the kind of man who surrenders people. Understand? This isn't a fare you can pay off at the next landing. You're buying passage with trouble, and trouble is the only coin I've got room for."

She lifted her chin, and for a moment he saw her father in her face — some ghost of the charter-holder Aldous Voss must have worn when men told him the magistrate's terms were non-negotiable.

"Then we understand each other," she said.

* * *

They cast off in the blue hour before dawn, when the mist lay on the Greywater thick as wool and the customs post was nothing but a smudge of darker dark. Astrid took the *Heron* downriver first — south, toward the open lower reaches, running lights lit, deck lanterns glowing, a decoy of ordinary commerce for any watcher on the banks. He let her drift on the current for six miles, easy as Sunday, while the fog swallowed them whole.

At the Marrow fork he brought her alongside the old rotting pier at Deadman's Slough, transferred two weeks of provisions, the skiff, and one stubborn girl into the shallows, and set the *Heron* loose again with her tiller lashed true and her lamps burning — a ghost ship sailing itself south into the mist, bound for nowhere, fooling nobody for long but fooling somebody for long enough.

Mirabel watched the lanterns fade. "She looks alive out there."

"She always does." Astrid shipped the oars and pointed the skiff's nose into the Marrow channel, where the current ran cold and quick against them and the banks closed in dark and whispering. "Now row, passenger. We've bought ourselves a head start, and head starts are like mercy on this river — they don't last unless you make them last."

Behind them, far downstream, a bell began to ring in Saltmere Landing — alarm cadence, three-and-one, the signal for a fugitive sighted. Too late, and too far, and aimed at a boat that was already learning to be a legend.

Ahead, the Marrow ran north into the hills, toward Halfway Landing, toward Odessa Vane and whatever price the smuggler queen would ask, toward the Kestrel Range where the first snows were already falling on heights no rider could climb and no river could follow.

Astrid pulled against the current, and the current pulled back, and between the two of them they made way.
