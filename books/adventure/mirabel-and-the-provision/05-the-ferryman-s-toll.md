## 5. The Ferryman's Toll

Halfway Landing was not so much a town as a wound the river had made and never bothered to heal.

We came down to it in the last grey light of evening, Astrid leading our one exhausted mule along a deer trail that smelled of pine rot and cold stone, and I saw it first as a scatter of lamps strung along a mudbank like coins dropped in a gutter. Then the shapes resolved: stilt-houses leaning over black water, rope bridges sagging between them, a long timber wharf where boats with no names and no flags lay rafted three deep. Somewhere a fiddle played, badly, and stopped. Smoke from cook-fires hung in the still air like something drowned.

"Rules," Astrid said, without turning around. His voice had been getting shorter the higher we climbed away from the river, as though words cost him something he couldn't spare. "Halfway Landing belongs to Odessa Vane. Whatever she asks you, you answer careful. Whatever she offers you, you count twice."

"What did she offer *you*?" I asked.

He didn't answer. He adjusted the mule's lead and kept walking, and I learned that about him too: silence was not absence with Astrid Dravenmoor. It was a wall he built quickly, and I was learning to read what stood behind it by what leaked over the top.

I carried the ledger against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth beneath my coat, bound there with a strap my father had used for his charts. Three days on the trail and I no longer noticed its weight until I took it off to sleep, and then my ribs felt wrong, hollowed, as if I'd been carrying part of myself. Papa's handwriting lived inside that oilcloth. His careful, patient numbers. The true accounts of the Greywater Trade Charter — the ones Corvane's forgers had buried under their beautiful lies before they put my father in the river.

*You don't hand truth to strangers,* Papa used to say, mending nets on our dock at Tarnmouth while I sat among the floats. *Truth is the only cargo worth drowning for.*

He'd said it lightly then. He hadn't known how literally the river would take him up on it.

* * *

Odessa Vane received us in a houseboat moored mid-channel, which I understood later was less vanity than policy: a woman who could cut her moorings and be gone in a minute was a woman who slept well. We crossed to her by a swaying plank walk, past two guards who looked at Astrid the way wolves look at another wolf crossing a scent line, and into a cabin lit by a single hanging lantern and lined floor to ceiling with ledgers.

Not gold. Not weapons. *Ledgers.* Hundreds of them, spines cracked, corners soft with handling, stacked in crates and shelves and drifts on the floor like snowfall made of paper. The smell of ink and tallow was so thick I could taste it.

"Well," said the woman behind the desk, "the river sends me a drowned man and a dead girl."

She was smaller than I expected. That was the first trick of her, I think — everyone expects a smuggler queen to be large, loud, armored in rings. Odessa Vane was slight and silver-streaked, with a face like a folded map and hands that never quite stopped moving, turning a pen, squaring papers, touching things as if counting them even in conversation. Her eyes were quick and brown and missed nothing, and they went first to my chest, where the ledger rode, and stayed there a half-second longer than politeness allowed.

"Astrid Dravenmoor," she said. "Three years since you ran cargo past my tollgate without paying. You owe me for that, incidentally."

"You'll bill me," Astrid said. He lowered himself onto the offered stool without being asked twice; I realized he was more tired than he let anyone see. "And this is Mirabel. She needs to reach Kestrel Ford before the pass closes."

"Everyone needs to reach Kestrel Ford before the pass closes." Odessa leaned back. "The difference is most of them can pay, and the ones who can't are already working my decks. So. What do you have?"

Astrid named a figure. She laughed at it. He raised it. She shook her head slowly, enjoying herself the way a cat enjoys a cornered thing, and I understood we were not negotiating passage. We were being measured.

Then she stopped enjoying herself, and it was frightening how fast her face could go from amusement to arithmetic.

"But here's my real price," she said, "and it isn't coin. The girl's ledger. The true charter accounts out of Tarnmouth. Everyone on this river knows Corvane's riders are hunting a runaway and a boat captain, and everyone with sense knows a runaway doesn't make a magistrate spend this much blood unless she's carrying something that can hang him. I want it. Give it to me, and you both cross the Kestrels in my own caravans, warm, fed, ahead of the snow. Refuse—" she spread her small hands, an apology, a shrug, a door closing all at once— "and you're welcome to try the pass alone, with winter coming down early and riders behind you. I hear Corvane doubled the reward. Dead or alive, and alive only if convenient."

The cabin was very quiet. The lantern flame leaned and righted itself. Out on the water, someone laughed far away, and the sound came across the river thin as wire.

I felt Astrid shift beside me — felt it rather than saw it, the way you feel weather change — and I knew, with a sick drop in my stomach, that he was considering it. Why wouldn't he? A ledger was paper. Paper could be copied, quoted, sworn to later. Odessa wanted leverage over Corvane, not to burn the truth; she had every reason to keep it safe. And Astrid had already lost his crew, his name, his river to this fight. He owed me nothing. He'd said so himself, the second night, when I thanked him and he told me not to: *I'm not your ally, girl. I'm just the fool who found you.*

My hands closed on the strap at my chest.

"No," I said.

Both of them looked at me.

"It's my father's," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "His last true thing. Corvane killed him to bury these numbers, and I watched them lower the coffin, and I ran through the marshes for six days with his blood drying on my sleeve to keep this book out of that man's hands. It goes to the assembly at Kestrel Ford. Read aloud. In front of everyone. That's the whole point of it — that's why Papa wrote it true instead of writing it safe. If I give it to you, it stops being testimony and starts being *leverage*, and leverage gets traded, Mrs. Vane. Leverage gets spent. My father doesn't get spent."

Odessa regarded me for a long moment. There was no anger in her face. That was somehow worse.

"You're fifteen," she said.

"I'm the only witness left."

"And you'd trade a warm caravan and your life for a book."

"I'd trade yours too, apparently," I said, because I was frightened and fifteen and it came out before I could stop it. "Yes. I would. I'm sorry. But yes."

Silence. The lantern hissed. And then Astrid spoke, and what he said rearranged something in my chest that I am still trying to name.

"She speaks for both of us," he said.

Odessa's eyebrows rose. "You're giving up the pass? Your neck's the one with a price on it, Captain. Hers at least has the mercy of youth."

"The river taught me one rule I've never broken," Astrid said. "You don't leave passengers overboard. She says the book stays with her, the book stays with her. Find another way or we find it ourselves." He stood, joints cracking, and put coin on the desk — not enough for passage, only enough, I understood, for courtesy. "For the tea we didn't drink."

For a moment I thought Odessa Vane would laugh again. Instead she looked at Astrid with something old and complicated moving behind her eyes, some debt or grief or memory I had no map for, and then she looked at me, and whatever she saw made her sigh like a woman closing a shop at the end of a long day.

"Get off my boat," she said, almost gently. "Before I change my mind and take the book anyway."

* * *

The plank walk back seemed longer than the walk out. Neither of us spoke until we reached the mudbank and the mule, and the town's noise swallowed us — fiddle and argument and the lap of water against pilings.

"You backed me," I said finally. It sounded stupid the moment I said it, but I didn't know how else to say the size of it. "In there. You gave up the pass. For a book."

"For *your* book." Astrid checked the mule's girth with rough, absent hands. "Don't make it larger than it was."

"Why?" The word tore loose before I could weigh it. "You said you weren't my ally. You said—"

"I know what I said." He was quiet a moment. Above us the sky had gone the color of unpolished pewter, and the first smell of snow was in the air, sharp and mineral, coming down off the Kestrels like a warning sent ahead of the army. "When they took my crew — rigged inspection, Corvane's men, confiscated everything and licensed them into nothing — every one of them had a chance to speak for me. To say the inspection was false. Every one of them stayed quiet and walked away whole." He tightened the girth strap one hard notch. "I decided people were cargo that sinks. Then I pulled you out of a cave, and now I'm finding out I decide things too fast." He glanced at me, and for once there was no wall in his face at all, just tiredness and something underneath the tiredness. "You wouldn't sell your father. I won't sell you. That's the whole arithmetic."

I didn't cry. I had used up all my crying somewhere in the marshes, and besides, I was learning that some feelings are better carried than spilled. But I put my hand flat against the oilcloth over my heart, where Papa's numbers slept, and I thought: *he would have liked this stubborn, ruined captain. He would have trusted him with the boat.*

"We have no guide," I said. "No caravan. No warm anything."

"We have the shale canyons," Astrid said, looking north, where the last light was dying on ridgelines already going white. "There's a route through them to the high country. Harder. Slower. Riders use the canyon roads too, which means we'll want to be where roads aren't." He swung up onto the mule and held down a hand for me. "It also means nobody's between us and the pass but weather."

I took his hand. It was colder than mine, and rougher, and it did not let go a moment before it had to.

Behind us, Halfway Landing's lamps burned on, and somewhere in that paper-lined houseboat Odessa Vane was surely already writing letters about us — selling our description, maybe, or keeping it, or weighing one truth against another the way she weighed everything. Ahead of us the mountains stood up black against the last of the sky, and the wind coming down off them had teeth in it now.

We rode north, into the canyons, into the dark, with the snow a day behind us and the riders, I would learn by morning, barely a day behind that.
