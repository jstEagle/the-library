## 6. Riders on the Shale

The shale canyons began where the river's mercy ended.

Astrid had told Mirabel as much that morning, pointing at the grey slopes rising east of the trail like the ribs of some drowned leviathan. *Stone that talks*, he'd said. Shale announced every footfall for a mile in either direction — a whispering, sliding chatter that carried on the wind the way sound carried over water. A boatman learned to read water because water lied; shale was worse. Shale told the truth about everyone, including you.

They had one mule between them, a sour-tempered bay gelding named Turnip that Odessa Vane had thrown into their bargain when she'd washed her hands of them at Halfway Landing. Turnip carried the food, the oilskin, and the bedroll. Mirabel carried the ledger against her chest, wrapped in waxed canvas, strapped tight beneath her coat. Astrid carried the boat hook he'd taken from the cave skiff, its iron head ground to a point that would do poor man's work as a spear.

Three days out from the Landing, the canyon narrowed to a throat.

He heard them before he saw them — not hooves, not yet, but the shale itself, chattering somewhere above and behind, a sound like a thousand small stones clearing their throats. He had spent twenty years listening to rivers announce what was coming downstream. This was the same grammar. Something heavy, moving fast, taking the slope too quickly because it did not care who heard it.

"Mirabel." He said her name quietly, the way he'd say *rocks ahead* on a dark reach. "Off the trail. Now."

She didn't argue. That was the thing about her he'd stopped being surprised by — the girl had a boatman's instincts without ever having touched an oar. She hauled Turnip off the path and into the shadow of an overhang while Astrid climbed three yards up the scree to look back the way they'd come.

They came around the bend below like water finding a channel: six riders, dun cloaks dusted grey, riding two abreast with the easy spacing of men who had done this before. Corvane's colors were hidden, but nothing else about them needed saying. The lead rider raised a hand, and the column fanned out across the canyon floor like ink dropped in still water.

"Magistrate's road warrant!" the lead rider called, his voice bouncing off the walls and arriving twice, once from each side. "Astrid Dravenmoor! You're named on paper. Come down and answer it, and the girl walks free!"

The lie was so smooth it was almost beautiful. Astrid had watched the Greywater drown men with exactly that voice — calm, reasonable, promising the far bank while the current took your feet.

"You hear that?" he called down to Mirabel, not loudly enough for the riders to catch the words. "He says she walks free."

"I heard," she whispered back, and there was something in her voice that wasn't fear anymore. It was arithmetic. Grief turned to sums. *Six riders. One mule. One hook. One gorge.* "What's the plan, Captain?"

"The plan," Astrid said, "is that I finally get to use twenty years of reading water on something that isn't water."

* * *

The gorge came half a mile on, where the canyon floor dropped away into a slot cut by snowmelt — forty feet deep, twelve wide, and running brown and violent with early meltwater that should have been a trickle this time of year but wasn't. The whole range was drinking wrong this season. The snows were coming early, and everything above them was melting fast to make room.

Astrid had clocked the sound of it a mile back and filed it away the way he filed everything: *flooded gorge, narrow, fast, cold enough to stop a heart in ten minutes.* Most men would have called it an obstacle. Astrid looked at it and saw the only road in the canyon that six riders could not follow.

But first they had to live long enough to reach it.

The ambush came at the narrows, where the trail pinched between a fallen slab and the canyon wall. Two riders had cut ahead along the high shelf — he never saw exactly how they'd gotten up there, only heard the shale sing and then they were simply *there*, above, and the world went to noise.

"Down!" He caught Mirabel's collar and pulled her flat as a crossbow bolt cracked off the slab where her head had been. The quarrel buried itself in the stone with a sound like a knuckle rapping a hull. Turnip screamed — a horrible, human sound — and bolted, and the mule was gone down the trail with their food and their blankets and twenty years of Astrid's careful planning strapped to his back.

"No!" Mirabel lunged after him. Astrid grabbed her arm. "Leave him."

"He has everything—"

"He has everything, and they want him more than they want us right now, which means he's buying us time." It was brutal arithmetic and he hated himself for it, but the river had taught him that too: sometimes you cut loose what's dragging so the rest of the hull floats. "Gorge. Run."

They ran.

The second bolt took him in the shoulder.

He felt it before he understood it — a punch, hot and deep, just below the joint, spinning him half around and putting him on one knee in the scree. Not a shaft, thank whatever god kept books on boatmen. A crossbow quarrel, short and heavy, punched through the muscle at the top of his arm and out the back. Blood ran down inside his coat sleeve, shockingly warm, then shockingly cold.

"Astrid!" Mirabel was beside him, hauling at him with both hands, and she was stronger than she looked, or frightened enough to be. "Get *up*—"

"I'm up." He was. He didn't remember standing. They ran together, her shoulder under his good arm, down the last stretch of trail while the riders below crashed through the narrows behind them and the ones above scrambled along the shelf, shouting coordinates to each other in clipped voices. *Trail's blocked. They're making for the gorge. Cut them off at the crossing.*

There was no crossing. There hadn't been a crossing in that gorge since the melt started, and no rider from these dry hills would know that. But Astrid knew it the way he knew the Greywater's every sandbar and snag — and knowing it was going to have to be enough.

The gorge opened before them like a wound.

Twelve feet across, forty down, and the water in it was not water so much as moving earth — brown, thick with silt, hauling whole branches and slabs of ice past at a speed that made his eyes water to track it. The roar filled the canyon and ate every other sound. Spray hung in the air like rain that couldn't decide which way to fall.

Mirabel stared down into it and went white. "You can't be serious."

"I'm serious the way I've been serious since the cave." He tore a strip from his shirt with his teeth, one-handed, and bound the shoulder as tight as his arm would let him. The pain made the world go silver at the edges. "Listen to me. This is a read, and I'm the only one here who can make it. See that bend downstream? The water piles on the outside wall and goes slack on the inside — there's an eddy under that overhang, maybe thirty yards down. We go in at the throat, we angle hard left, we let the eddy catch us. If we miss it, the next slack water is four miles down and we'll be dead of cold long before."

"If we miss it—"

"We won't miss it." He said it with a certainty he absolutely did not feel, because he had learned long ago that fear is contagious and so is confidence, and a captain gets to choose which one he passes to his crew. His crew. One girl, fifteen years old, holding a ledger worth more than his boat had ever been. "Take a breath. Big one. Fill yourself up like a cask."

Shouting above. The riders on the shelf had found their line; he could see one of them working down through the rocks toward the rim, crossbow leveled, picking his footing.

"Now or never, Mirabel."

She looked at the flood. She looked at him. And then she did the thing that told him everything he'd ever need to know about her: she cinched the ledger strap tighter against her chest, checked it twice, and only then nodded.

"Together?" she shouted over the roar.

"Together. On my count. One—"

The crossbow spoke from the rim. The quarrel skipped off a stone between them and vanished into the gorge.

"—two. GO."

* * *

The cold hit him like a hull hitting a dock.

It went through skin and meat and bone in a single instant, a white detonation that emptied his mind of everything except the single oldest truth he knew: *the water is in charge. Read it. Obey it. Fight it and die.*

The flood took them instantly, completely, tumbling them apart and together and apart again. He got one glimpse of Mirabel — a pale face, dark hair streaming, arms locked over the ledger at her chest just as he'd drilled her at Halfway Landing's millpond in the one hour of practice they'd allowed themselves — and then the water folded over them both.

He swam by feel. Angle left. Hard left. The current fought him with hands of wet stone, dragging him toward the outside wall where the water stood up in a brown ridge and thundered. His wounded shoulder screamed. His fingers went from pain to nothing. He kicked anyway, hooked the water with his good arm, and counted — *ten strokes, fifteen, twenty* — the way he'd count fathoms in the dark.

The eddy took them like a mother's arms.

One moment the world was violence; the next it was a slow brown whirlpool under the overhang, turning them gently in circles, and Astrid's boots found bottom and his head broke surface and he was coughing half the Greywater's mountain cousins out of his lungs. Mirabel surfaced beside him, gasping, clawing for the rock, and he got a fist in her collar and hauled her against the stone shelf and held on.

For a long time neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say over the roar, and nothing that needed saying besides.

Above them, faint through the gorge's throat, he heard the riders' voices — angry, confused, working along the rim, finding no way down. Someone fired a shot that hit nothing. Then distance swallowed even that.

Mirabel got her back against the rock, breath heaving, lips already blue-tinged, and looked at him. At his shoulder, where the blood was diluting pink in the water sheeting off them both. At his face.

"You knew," she shouted. "You *knew* there was an eddy there. How?"

"Because I asked the water," he shouted back, and then, because the truth deserved better than a joke: "Because I've spent my whole life learning how floods behave, and today it finally paid."

She laughed then — a wild, cracked, half-frozen laugh with terror still in it, but underneath the terror something new. Something fierce. She put her hand flat against his chest, over the ledger, over both their heartbeats, and shouted the words like an oath:

"Then you're stuck with me, Captain. All the way over the pass."

Astrid Dravenmoor, who had spent nine years keeping every soul on the Greywater at oar's length, looked at this soaked, shivering, stubborn girl who refused to drown and refused to let him, and found that the old loneliness had quietly gone overboard somewhere in the rapids.

"Kestrel Ford's five days north," he said. "And the first snows are coming down to meet us."

Above the gorge, fat and silent, the first flakes were already falling.
