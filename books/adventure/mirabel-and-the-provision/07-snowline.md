## 7. Snowline

The trees gave up before we did.

I had never thought of timber as a living thing that could surrender, but watching the last stunted pines fall away behind us — bending, whitening, then gone altogether, as if the mountain had simply decided it would permit no more green — I understood that the Kestrel Range kept its own laws, and they were older and colder than any magistrate's. Above the treeline there was only rock and sky and a wind that came off the high snowfields with the sound of a crowd whispering your name.

Astrid walked ahead of me, favoring his left leg where the shale had opened him up two days ago. He didn't complain. He didn't talk at all, most hours, except to point out where the path doubled back on itself or where the scree would slide underfoot like a river deciding to run uphill. I had learned to read his silences the way he read water: there was the silence of *save your breath*, and the silence of *I'm listening*, and once, memorably, the silence of *you are about to do something foolish, girl, and I will let you learn from it*.

The snow caught us on the third morning above the trees.

It did not fall so much as arrive — one moment the air was clear and knife-bright, and the next the whole world had gone soft and white and silent, flakes big as moths drifting down without hurry, as though they had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Within an hour my boots were white. Within three I could no longer tell path from cliff-edge, and Astrid roped us together with the coil from his pack, tying the knots with fingers gone clumsy and gray at the tips.

"Walk where I walk," he said. "Step in my prints. The mountain lies less than the river does, but it lies more often than people."

"That doesn't make sense."

"No," he agreed, and kept walking.

We made camp that night in the lee of a granite shoulder, a hollow barely deep enough for two, and ate the last of the smoked fish Odessa's people had pressed on us at Halfway Landing. It was not enough. I could feel it not being enough, a hollow ache below my ribs that had nothing to do with grief, though grief lived in the same neighborhood. My hands wouldn't warm. I put them under my arms and watched my breath hang in the fireless dark like small ghosts, and thought about how my father used to say that cold was just the world telling you the truth about how small you were.

I hated that he'd been right.

* * *

Sometime in the black middle of the night, I woke to find Astrid sitting up, awake, staring down the slope we'd climbed. Far below — so far it might have been imagination — something flickered. A light. Then another.

"Torchlight," he said quietly. "Or I'm dreaming of torchlight."

"How far?"

"A day behind us. Maybe less, if they climb better than I think they do." He turned and looked at me, and even in the dark I could see the arithmetic happening behind his eyes: food, distance, my frostbitten fingers, his leg, the pass still two days off if the weather held, which weather never does. "We leave before first light. We move slow and steady and we don't stop again until the saddle."

I nodded. And then — because the dark makes honest creatures of us all, and because the light below had scared something loose in me that I had been holding shut since Halfway Landing — I heard myself say:

"I almost sold it."

Astrid went very still. "Sold what?"

"The ledger." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "That night at Halfway Landing, after we turned Odessa down. While you slept. There was a man in the common room — a factor's clerk, fat rings on his fingers, said he was traveling east. He bought my wine. He asked questions the way Odessa asks questions, soft, like he already knew the answers. And he offered me forty crowns for whatever papers I was carrying south. Forty crowns, Astrid. That's a boat. That's a new life anywhere Corvane's writ doesn't run."

The wind moved through the rocks above us, sifting snow into our hollow like flour through a sieve.

"He didn't know what he was offering for," Astrid said at last. "You could have taken his money and given him a bundle of laundry lists."

"I know." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "That's what I told myself. That's what I *kept* telling myself, lying there listening to you breathe. But here's the truth of it, captain: I sat up half the night doing sums. Not whether to sell it — whether he'd pay more. Whether my father's whole life, everything he built and everything they took from him, was worth forty crowns or fifty. I was haggling over him. In my head. With a stranger who smelled of lamp oil and lies."

Astrid said nothing for a long time. Below us, the lights had gone out, or hidden themselves, or never been.

"Why are you telling me this?" he finally asked. Not cruel. Genuinely asking, the way he asked the river things.

"Because you trusted me with your boat. Your whole life is that boat, and you pointed it upstream for me knowing what it would cost. And because—" My throat tightened. "Because if I don't say it out loud, it stays inside me growing teeth. Out loud it's just a thing I almost did and didn't. That's all. Almost-and-didn't. I need someone else to have heard the *didn't*."

"You want a witness."

"I want absolution," I said, "but I'll take a witness."

He was quiet again, and I braced myself for the speech — the lecture about weakness, about how a courier carrying evidence worth killing for can't afford midnight temptations, about how trust is cargo and damaged cargo gets jettisoned. I knew all of it. I'd been saying it to myself since the common room.

Instead he pulled his blanket tighter around his shoulders and said, "In Odessa's cabin, while she was pouring the third cup, I looked at her strongbox."

I stared at him.

"She keeps it under the chart table. Iron-bound, cheap lock — I could've had it open between heartbeats. Inside would be coin, papers, maybe letters of passage. Enough to buy us across the Kestrel without her help and without owing her anything." He flexed his ruined fingers toward the faint stars. "I stood there doing sums too. What the ledger was worth against what my freedom was worth. Whether one passenger, however brave, outweighed a boat and a river and twenty years of my life."

"What stopped you?"

"The same thing that stopped you, probably." His voice dropped low, rough as river gravel. "My father drowned when I was nine. Ferry accident, bad crossing, the Greywater took him and four others. And every soul on that bank stood there and *watched*, and not one of them went in after him, because the water was high and the current was murder and going in meant dying too. I've spent my whole life learning that river so well that no water could ever make me stand on a bank again." He exhaled, long and slow, breath ghosting silver. "Corvane's men drowned your father same as mine, Mirabel. Different river, same current. And I'll be damned — damned by the Greywater herself, which is the only damnation I believe in — if I stand on a bank and watch."

The wind gusted. Snow hissed against the granite above us like sand poured on a drum.

"So we're both thieves," I said.

"We're both honest," he corrected. "There's a difference, though the courts won't see it. A thief takes what isn't his. An honest man knows exactly what he wants and says so out loud, at least once, to at least one person, before he chooses better." He glanced sideways at me. "You chose better. So did I. Now go to sleep, and tomorrow we climb, and somewhere past that saddle there's a town full of people who'll hear your father's name spoken the way it deserves."

I lay back against the cold stone. My hands still ached. My ribs still felt hollow. But something had shifted — some load redistributed, some rope drawn tight between us and knotted off. That was the thing about honesty, I realized as sleep came for me at last: it worked like rigging. One line alone snaps under load. Two lines, crossed and bound, hold.

* * *

We climbed out of the hollow before first light, as promised, and the mountain punished us for it beautifully.

The storm that had been flirting with us all night committed fully by midmorning. The snow thickened until the world shrank to a circle ten feet across, with Astrid at the center of it, a dark shape leaning into the white, rope trailing back to me like an umbilical cord. We couldn't see the pass. We couldn't see the valley. We could barely see each other. We walked by compass and stubbornness, and twice the mountain tried to lie to us — once with a cornice that wasn't ground, which Astrid tested with an ice axe and swore at creatively, and once with a false trail that would have funneled us into a bowl with no exit.

"Talk to me," he shouted over the wind at one point, when I'd gone quiet too long. "Frostbite starts in the mind before the fingers. Keep talking."

So I talked. I told him about my father teaching me to write numbers in ledger-hand, how he'd made me copy columns until my penmanship was cleaner than the clerks'. I told him about the day the riders came to repossess the warehouse, how my father stood in the doorway with the charter in his hand and said, *This paper belongs to the river towns, and rivers don't recognize your signature.* I told him about finding the true ledger hidden in the false bottom of mother's hope chest, and how I'd understood, holding it, that he'd known for months he might not survive to use it.

And somewhere in the telling, the storm eased. Or we climbed above it. Hard to say which. By late afternoon the clouds tore open below us like wet cloth, and there it was: the saddle, a notch of blue-shadowed snow between two black crags, and beyond it — impossibly far, impossibly real — the green descent toward Kestrel Ford, smoke rising thin and domestic from its chimneys.

Astrid stopped at the top of the saddle and stood looking down, chest heaving, blood seeping fresh through the bandage on his leg. Then he turned and looked back the way we'd come.

I followed his gaze. Far below, crawling up the trail like ants on a white tablecloth, came a line of dark figures. Six of them. Maybe seven. Moving fast, moving steady, closing the gap we'd spent three days building.

"They'll reach the saddle by nightfall," Astrid said. His voice was calm in the way water is calm right before it goes over the falls. "Maybe sooner. They're fresher than we are."

"Then we keep walking."

"Yes." He unslung his pack and looked at it, and then at me, and then at the figures below, and I watched him begin — visibly, the way a helmsman reads a coming squall — to calculate something terrible. "But not together, and not both of us carrying weight. Listen to me, Mirabel. The descent on the far side forks at the shrine of the Small Mercies. The left fork drops straight to the Ford — a hard day's walk, downhill all the way. You take the ledger and you take that fork, and you don't stop, and you don't wait, and you don't look back no matter what you hear."

"And what will I hear?"

He smiled, and it was the saddest thing I'd seen since my father's funeral. "Wind," he said. "You'll hear wind. Now come here and let me show you the fork, because the light's going, and I mean to have you over this saddle and gone before those riders ever see your face."
