## 8. The Pass of Small Mercies

The mountain did not want them on it. Astrid had known that from the first switchback, the way a man knows a river's mood before he sees the first whitecap — in the set of the wind, in the wrongness of the silence, in the small telltales that most travelers ignore and boatmen cannot afford to. The Kestrel Range spoke a language he only half understood, but he understood enough: the clouds stacking themselves against the western peaks like cargo loaded by a drunk stevedore, the air gone thin and metallic, the birds flown down-valley two days early. Snow was coming. Not the polite, decorative snow of the lowlands. The kind that closed passes and opened graves.

"Keep your eyes on the trail, not the view," he said, without turning. Mirabel had stopped again three steps behind him, her breath ragged, her gaze fixed on the drop where the trail narrowed to a shelf barely wider than a coffin laid lengthwise. Below them the Greywater was a silver thread stitching together dark country, so far down it looked like something remembered rather than seen.

"I'm not looking at the view," she said. "I'm looking at how far we'd fall."

"That's worse." He shifted the pack higher on his shoulders and kept climbing. "Falling's quick. Looking is what kills you."

They had been on the mountain four days. Four days since the gorge, since the flooded run that should have killed them both and hadn't, since Astrid had stitched his own forearm by firelight with Mirabel holding the needle steady because his left hand wouldn't stop shaking. The wound had gone hot and angry at the edges, and every step upward now cost him something he pretended he could afford. He climbed the way he poled a boat through dead water — slow, deliberate, refusing to acknowledge the current dragging at him.

The ledger rode against Mirabel's chest in its oilskin wrapping, tied with the same cord she'd used since the cave. She touched it constantly, the way other people touched amulets. Her father's last truth, beating under her coat like a second heart.

By midday the wind arrived ahead of the storm, moaning through the saddles between peaks with a sound like a drowned man trying to whistle. By early afternoon the light had turned the color of old pewter, and the first flakes came down — fat, lazy, almost gentle, the way the first lie in a long con is always the kindest one.

Astrid stopped at a bend where the trail widened around a cairn some forgotten traveler had stacked, and looked back the way they'd come.

He saw them immediately. Three points of movement far below, near the tree line they'd left that morning, moving fast along the shale. Riders. Corvane's riders had found the mule's carcass two days back; now they'd found their trail.

"They've got horses on this?" Mirabel asked, coming up beside him. Her lips were cracked, her cheeks scoured raw by wind, but her eyes were clear. They'd been getting clearer all week, he'd noticed. Grief burning off like morning fog.

"Horses below the shale line, maybe. Above it they'll walk them or leave them." He calculated distances the way he read water — depth, speed, obstruction. "We've got maybe two hours. Less if the snow slows us more than it slows them."

"Then we keep walking."

"We keep walking." But he didn't move yet. He stood there in the thickening snowfall, and he let himself do the thing he'd been refusing to do for four days: he thought about the *Wren*.

His riverboat lay moored at the last landing below the pass — the little waystation at Cold Larder Bend, where he'd paid a wintering fee to a half-blind keeper who asked no questions because Astrid overpaid him not to. He'd told himself it was practicality. A captain doesn't abandon his vessel; you winter her, you come back for her when the thaw comes, when the charter is restored and the Greywater belongs to honest men again. That was the story. But the truth, which Astrid admitted to no one, least of all himself, was that he could not bear to leave her. Forty years of Dravenmoors had worked boats on the Greywater, and the *Wren* was the finest thing any of them had ever built — black-hulled, shallow-drafted, fast as gossip, built plank by plank with his own hands after the rigged inspection took his crew and nearly took his license. She was the last thing in the world that had never lied to him.

And she was moored directly on the riders' path.

The realization settled into him cold as the snow melting down his collar. If the riders reached Cold Larder Bend, they'd find the *Wren*, and they'd know exactly whose boat it was, and they'd know exactly where the fugitives were headed, because there was only one pass and only one trail and only one place ahead worth reaching. Worse — Corvane's men would find the keeper, and the keeper would talk, because half-blind men who take wintering fees will also take thumbscrews.

But if the *Wren* burned—

The thought arrived whole, the way a flood arrives, and he hated it instantly and completely.

"Astrid?" Mirabel had turned back. "What are you looking at?"

"The weather," he lied, and started climbing.

* * *

They made the shrine at dusk. It was nothing — a lean-to of split stone against the cliff face, old enough that the pilgrims who'd raised it had been forgotten even by the priests who claimed their order. Inside, someone had left a rusted lantern, a stack of dry juniper somebody's grandmother had carried up decades ago, and a carved wooden fish, worn smooth by centuries of worried hands. Travelers' offerings. Small mercies, the highland folk called such places. Places where the mountain permitted you to stop pretending you weren't afraid.

Astrid got a fire going in the lee of the stones while Mirabel chipped ice into the dented kettle. Neither of them spoke much anymore. They'd passed through talk days ago into something quieter and stronger, the wordless coordination of people who have bled together. She handed him things before he asked. He moved his body to block the wind before she felt it. It was, he thought, the closest thing to crew he'd had in years, and the realization sat in his chest like a swallowed hook.

He watched her across the small flame — fifteen years old, frostbitten at two fingertips, stubborn past all sense, carrying a dead man's ledger through a blizzard because the truth deserved better than a drawer — and he made his decision the way he made all his decisions: alone, quickly, and without asking anyone's permission, including hers.

Especially hers.

"The trail forks a mile above here," he said. "You remember? The cairn with the iron cross."

She looked up. "The high route and the low route."

"The low route drops to Cold Larder Bend and climbs out the far side. Longer, but sheltered, and there's a keeper's hut." He fed a stick to the fire. "The high route goes straight over the shoulder of the peak. Exposed. No shelter for six hours. In this weather, it's a gamble."

"So we take the low route."

"You take the low route."

The kettle stopped rattling. Mirabel went very still, the way she did when she was working something out, and he watched her work it out — saw the moment she understood, saw her face go through surprise and anger and land somewhere worse, on fear.

"No," she said.

"The riders are behind us and the *Wren* is on their path. When they find her, they'll know I'm close, and they'll assume I'm with you, and they'll follow the low route hunting for two sets of tracks." He kept his voice level, the voice he used for passengers during rough water. Calm is contagious; so is panic, and a captain chooses which one to spread. "Unless they find something at the bend that tells them different."

"You're going to burn your boat."

"I'm going to burn my boat," he agreed.

"Absolutely not." She was on her feet now, and the firelight caught the fury in her face. "That boat is everything. You told me — you built her yourself, plank by plank, after they took—"

"I know what I told you."

"—and you're going to hand it to the fire so that some magistrate's dogs chase smoke instead of you?" Her voice cracked on the last word. "No. We both take the low route, we both—"

"There won't be time for both of us to reach the bend and climb out before they arrive. You know it and I know it. I'm faster downhill than you are right now, and I'm the only one who can handle her lines and get her burning right — oil in the hold, fire in the cabin, done properly so it reads true." He looked at her steadily. "Mirabel. Listen to me. Your father died so that ledger would survive. I lost a crew to Corvane's paper tricks, and I swore I'd never lose anything else to him. But a boat is wood and pitch and iron. What you're carrying is the only thing on this mountain that can't be rebuilt."

"It can be rebuilt," she whispered. "*You* can rebuild it. You said in spring—"

"In spring I'll build another one." And here was the strange thing, the thing he would turn over in his mind for years afterward: he discovered, saying it, that he meant it. Somewhere between the cave and the shale canyons and the frozen gorge, the *Wren* had stopped being the last thing he belonged to. "I'm a boatman, girl. Boats are what I do. But nobody else can carry that ledger over this pass, and nobody else can be trusted to make those riders believe a lie. That's not sacrifice. That's just seamanship. You steer the cargo safe and write off what the river takes."

"The river didn't take her. *You're* taking her."

"Yes," he said simply. "I am."

For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the storm pressed against the shrine like something listening. Then Mirabel did something he didn't expect. She pulled the oilskin packet from inside her coat, untied the cord, and held out the ledger.

"When it's done," she said, "when Corvane falls — you build the new boat, and I name it. And I'm naming it after this. After tonight."

Astrid looked at the ledger in her outstretched hands and shook his head slowly. "Keep your father's book. I've got my own cargo to declare." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Name her whatever you like. Just make sure you're standing in the free towns when you do it."

She threw her arms around him then, quick and fierce, her cold cheek against his cold jaw, and he held on for exactly as long as a captain allows himself and no longer. Then he stepped back, shouldered the smaller pack — food, rope, the lamp oil he'd been hoarding against just this arithmetic — and pointed her up the trail toward the fork and the high route beyond.

"Straight over the shoulder. Don't stop for anything short of the far saddle. There's a waystation shrine at the top — same kind as this one. Light the juniper if you make it, so I know."

"And if you don't come?"

He paused at the edge of the firelight. Snow was filling his footprints already, erasing him from the mountain inch by inch.

"Then you read that ledger so loud they hear it in the capital," he said. "Go with the water, Mirabel."

* * *

Cold Larder Bend at midnight was a black mirror — the lake still and glassy beneath the falling snow, the *Wren* riding at her moorings like a sleeping bird, snow gathering white along her gunwales. Astrid came down out of the trees at a limping run, breath tearing, wound screaming, and stood on the dock for one long moment just looking at her.

"Hello, old girl," he said quietly.

Then he went to work, because grief is a luxury and captains schedule it for later. Oil from the hold into the bilge. Lamp oil through the cabin, over the bunks, across the chart table where he'd plotted ten thousand miles of river. He saved the wheelhouse for last, and there he allowed himself thirty seconds — thirty seconds with his hand flat on the wheel his grandfather had turned, the brass worn bright by three generations of Dravenmoor palms.

"You were the best thing I ever made," he told her. "Time somebody made something better."

He struck the flint on his way out the stern, and the *Wren* caught with a soft whump that rolled across the lake like a sigh.

Fire is loud in the mountains. Fire is visible for miles. By the time Astrid reached the treeline above the bend, the whole hollow was lit orange, flames standing forty feet off her deck, sparks streaming downwind like a school of startled fish, and the heat reached him even at two hundred yards, pushing against his face like a living thing. Any rider within five miles would see it and come running — and what they'd find was a burning riverboat registered to Astrid Dravenmoor, accomplice of the fugitive Mirabel, evidence that the pair had been cornered at the lake and had fled on foot into the trackless country west of the water, away from the pass entirely. Let them waste a day on that. Let them waste two.

He stood in the snow with his arm pressed against his ruined side and watched her burn. The wheelhouse went first, then the mast, and when the hull finally gave, the *Wren* settled into the black water with a hiss heard clear across the lake, steam rising off her grave in great ghostly columns.

"Small mercies," Astrid said to the storm, because his grandfather had taught him the old custom — you thank the mountain for what it permits, and you don't curse it for what it takes.

Then he turned uphill, into the wind and the dark, and began to climb.

Above him, miles up through the driving snow, a single point of light flickered to life at the far saddle — small, unsteady, defiant. Juniper burning in a waystation shrine.

She'd made the fork. She was over the shoulder, or nearly, and she was waiting, and the ledger was warm against her heart, and somewhere below him three riders were spurring their exhausted horses toward a bonfire that meant nothing and cost him everything.

Astrid smiled — a crooked, exhausted, unmistakably hopeful thing — and put his head down against the storm.

One more ridge. One more night. Then justice, outside any court, and a new keel waiting on the far side of winter.
