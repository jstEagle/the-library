## 9. Justice Outside Any Court

The pass let me go the way a fist lets go of something it has been crushing.

One moment I was stumbling through wind that screamed like a scalded gull, snow filling my boots, my fingers so far past pain they had become someone else's fingers — and then the trail bent south, dropped below the ridgeline, and the Kestrel Range exhaled. The wind fell to a mutter. The pines came back, black and patient, dusted white like old men who had seen everything and forgiven nothing.

I did not look back for Astrid. I made myself not look back. He had put the ledger inside my coat, against my chest, and tied it there with a length of tarred cord, and he had said — in that flat river voice of his, the one that never wasted a word on comfort — *"You carry it. I make noise. We meet at Kestrel Ford."* Then he had turned back down the slope toward the smoke of his own burning boat, walking into the riders' torchlight like a man stepping off a dock into deep water.

So I walked. Down through the pines, down through the alders, down until the snow gave way to frozen mud and the mud gave way to a road, and the road ran beside a river I did not know, and I understood with a strange, floating clarity that this was the first water in my life that owed me nothing.

Kestrel Ford announced itself before I saw it: woodsmoke hanging in the valley air like a held breath, the clang of a smithy, dogs barking in relay from farmstead to farmstead as if passing my name along. By the time I reached the outskirts my legs were shaking and my lips were cracked and bleeding, but the ledger rode warm against my sternum, warmed by my own body now, my father's ink pressed to my father's daughter's heart, and I thought: *not yet. Hold together. Not yet.*

The free-town assembly met in the tithe barn, because Kestrel Ford was too proud and too poor for anything grander. I could hear it from the square — dozens of voices rising and falling through the plank walls, the particular argumentative music of people who governed themselves and enjoyed saying so. Lantern light spilled yellow across the trampled snow of the yard. Sledges stood in rows. Farmers in sheepskin, traders in city wool, a knot of riverfolk near the doors with rope-burned hands and sharp eyes.

I stood at the edge of the lantern light, and fear finally caught up with me.

Not fear of the cold or the climb — I had left those behind on the mountain. This was an older fear, my father's fear, the one he never spoke of but carried in his shoulders every time a magistrate's barge passed his dock: *the law is theirs. The paper is theirs. The court is theirs.* Corvane had killed him with documents, drowned him in paperwork as surely as in the Greywater, and what was one half-frozen girl with a stolen book against a magistrate's seal?

Then I heard a voice inside the barn say, "—and the Greywater towns will simply have to accept the magistrate's terms, there's no alternative—" and something in my chest went hard and cold and clean, like river ice.

There was an alternative. I had carried it over a mountain. My father had died so it could be read aloud.

I pushed open the barn door.

* * *

The heat hit me first — a hundred bodies, a roaring fire, tallow smoke — and then the silence, spreading outward from the doors like a stone dropped in still water. A hundred faces turned. Somewhere a baby fussed and was hushed.

At the far end, on a platform of nail kegs and planks, sat the moderator: a broad woman with iron-grey braids and a farmer's forearms, a gavel lying unused beside her elbow. Behind her, seated apart from everyone else in seats a little too fine, a little too new, sat three men in grey coats cut in the magistrate's style. Corvane's agents. I knew them the way you know a snake's track in mud — by shape alone. One of them was already rising, already reaching into his coat, and I understood with terrible certainty that if I stopped moving, even for a breath, I would be taken out the back and no one in this room would ever learn what happened to me.

So I didn't stop.

"Moderator," I said, and my voice cracked like a boy's, and I hated it, and I kept going anyway, louder. "Moderator. I have business before this assembly. It concerns Magistrate Corvane of the Greywater towns, and it cannot wait, because these men are here to see that it never gets said."

The grey-coated man found his smile. "Forgive the interruption, Moderator. This child is distressed. She wandered away from her guardians on the pass — we've been searching for her since yesterday." He took a step toward me, hand extended, voice dripping kindness. "Come along now. You're safe."

"I'm safe," I agreed, "because there are two hundred witnesses in this room."

That got a laugh — short, hard, the laugh of self-governing people who distrusted exactly this kind of official tenderness. The moderator raised one forearm and the barn went quiet again.

"Girl," she said. "State your name and your business. Quick."

"Mirabel Voss. Daughter of Edran Voss, charter-holder of the Greywater trade, who was drowned last spring between Millbrook and Thorn's Reach." I pulled the cord loose and drew the ledger out of my coat, and I saw the grey coats go very still, all three of them, stillness like a held knife. "And my business is this book."

I climbed onto the nearest bench so they could all see me, small and filthy and frostbitten as I was, and I opened the ledger to the ribbon my father had tied there himself, months ago, in another life.

"These are the true accounts of the Greywater Trade Charter," I said. "Every entry in Magistrate Corvane's office copy is forged. Every debt, every default, every forfeiture he used to strip my father's charter and seize our barges — invented. Here are the real figures, in my father's hand, witnessed and sealed." I turned the book around, holding it high. "Corvane wrote my family into ruin with a pen. When my father wouldn't sign the surrender, Corvane arranged his drowning and called it an accident. And when I ran with the only true copy, he sent riders to kill me. They burned a riverboat two nights ago trying. Ask your own eyes what kind of man hunts a fifteen-year-old girl across the Kestrel Range."

"The book is a forgery," the second agent said smoothly, standing. "A clever fabrication. The magistrate's seal on those pages is—"

"Is genuine," said a voice from the doorway, "because I stole it off his own desk."

Odessa Vane filled the barn door with her shoulders and her presence, snow melting off her leathers, a dozen of her smugglers crowding in behind her with the easy menace of people who had never once asked permission to enter anywhere. The assembly murmured. The moderator's hand moved to her gavel and stayed there.

"Odessa Vane," the moderator said flatly. "Halfway Landing's finest. Come to testify? Or come to steal?"

"Both, historically." Odessa strode down the aisle, and the crowd parted for her the way water parts for a hull. She set a document case on the platform with a click. "Seized records, Moderator. Corvane's duplicate ledgers, correspondence with his factors, and letters instructing his riders regarding 'the recovery of certain property, by any means.' Property meaning the girl. Means meaning whatever they liked." She looked back at me, and for one unguarded moment something almost gentle crossed her face. "I offered to buy this book from her on the mountain road. She refused. Cost us both dearly." She turned to the assembly. "I'm telling you that so you understand what you're looking at. Everyone in this valley would have sold it. She didn't."

The moderator looked at me for a long moment. Then she picked up her gavel — and instead of striking it, she held it out to me, handle first.

"You've the floor, Mirabel Voss. Read it. All of it. We're farmers and we're stubborn and we've got nowhere else to be tonight."

* * *

I read until my voice went raw.

I read the true tonnage figures and the false ones side by side. I read the forged debt entries, dated and initialed, each one matched against my father's actual receipts. I read the letter where Corvane's factor wrote, in careful clerk's script, that *the charter-holder remains obstinate; perhaps a season without him would loosen the family's grip.* I read until the fire burned low and someone fed it without being asked, until the grey-coated agents' protests wore themselves out against the moderator's gavel, until the oldest trader in the room — a woman with knuckles like walnuts — stood up and said, "I dealt with Edran Voss twenty years. Those are his numbers. I'd know them drunk or dead."

When I reached the final entry — my father's last page, written the week before he died, a note in the margin that said only *If anything happens to me, the truth is in this book* — my voice finally broke. I stood there on the bench with a hundred faces watching me cry, and I found I wasn't ashamed. Grief, Astrid had told me once on the river, isn't weakness. It's ballast. It keeps you upright in rough water.

The vote, when the moderator finally called it, wasn't close. Kestrel Ford issued warrants under free-town law — warrants the Greywater towns would honor, because half their traders were sitting in this barn, and because Corvane's charter existed only on paper the assembly had just declared fraudulent. His agents tried to leave quietly somewhere around midnight. Odessa's people escorted them to the town line with great courtesy and took their dispatch case with greater courtesy still.

I came out of the barn into the blue hour before dawn, and the whole valley smelled like woodsmoke and coming morning. Odessa stood in the yard waiting, arms crossed against the cold.

"He'll fight the warrants," she said. "Men like Corvane don't drown easy. But it's done — the paper's turned on him, and paper was always his weapon." She studied me. "Your captain. The big one, half-dead, who burned his own boat on the mountain. My scouts found him limping down the north trail an hour ago. He'll reach the ford by midday."

I was running for the north road before she finished speaking.

The sun came up gold behind me as I ran, and ahead, small and slow and unmistakable against the brightening snow, a broad-shouldered figure was walking down out of the mountains — and beside the road, where the river widened into the ford, lay a half-built frame of green timber that hadn't been there yesterday, its fresh-cut keel timber gleaming like a promise.

Someone had already started building him a new boat.
