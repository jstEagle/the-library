## 10. New Keel, Old River

The Greywater ran high and green with snowmelt the morning Astrid Dravenmoor walked down to the mudflats below Halfway Landing and drove the first stake into the earth.

He had chosen the spot the way he chose everything on the river — by reading it. The flats there were firm gravel under a hand's depth of silt, drained south toward the deep channel, close enough to the water to slide a hull on greased timbers when she was ready, far enough above the spring floods that she wouldn't drown in her cradle. He had walked it three times in the last week of winter, when the ice still held the banks like a clenched fist, and once more the day the ice let go with a sound like distant cannon fire and the whole river shrugged itself awake.

The stake went in crooked. Astrid straightened it, tamped the gravel around its base, and stood back.

One stake. That was all. A single ironwood post driven into the mud of the Greywater, and already his hands were shaking — not from cold, though the morning wind off the meltwater still had teeth in it, but from something older and stranger. He had built boats before. He had laid keels in shipyards from Kestrel Ford down to the delta towns, back when he was young enough to think the river owed him something. But he had never built one *here*, never built one where people could see him do it, never built one that anyone but himself would ever care about.

He heard footsteps on the frozen mud behind him and did not turn around.

"You've got her stern too far upriver," said a voice he knew. "You'll fight the current every time you launch."

"I'll fight the current anyway," Astrid said. "It keeps me honest."

Odessa Vane came around beside him, wrapped in a smuggler's grey wool coat that had seen more border crossings than most customs men, her breath steaming. She looked at the stake, at the flattened patch of gravel where a keel would lie, and then at Astrid, and her sharp face did something complicated — the look of a woman deciding whether to say a kind thing or a true one, and knowing they might not be the same thing.

"Corvane's warrant came through Kestrel Ford two days ago," she said. "Sealed with the assembly's own stamp. They're sending riders down the whole length of the Greywater to read it aloud at every landing." She paused. "Your name's in it. As injured party. They want you to come witness the seizure of his assets."

"I don't want anything from Corvane's estate," Astrid said. "I want his ledgers burned and his docks given to the families he bled. That's all."

"That's not all," Odessa said, and smiled thinly. "But it's a start."

* * *

They came the next day, and the day after that, and Astrid learned what it meant to be claimed by a river town.

It started with timber. He had planned to buy oak for the frames from the mill at Two Rivers, and he had counted out the coin for it twice, because coin counting was a habit from the years when every piece of silver had to answer for itself against the magistrate's fees. But on the third morning of the build, he came down to the flats and found a stack of seasoned planks already there, squared and stacked under oilcloth, with no note and no name. The fourth morning there were more. By the end of the week there was enough white oak to frame a boat twice the size of the *Grey Wren* — his lost boat, his burned boat, the decoy fire that had bought Mirabel her pass over the mountains — and Astrid stood among the stacks in the grey light feeling like a man who had been robbed in reverse.

He asked around, of course. Nobody would tell him. Old Marta at the ferry house said she'd seen cart tracks from the direction of the cooperage. The cooper said he'd seen nothing. A boy mending nets on the dock said, with the gravity of a child delivering state secrets, that his father had said the river gives back what it takes, if you wait long enough and don't ask how.

Astrid stopped asking.

They came to work, too. Not all at once — Astrid would have refused them all at once, and everyone seemed to know it, so they came in ones and twos, with excuses. Tam Broadhurst, who had run cargo opposite Astrid for eleven years and hadn't spoken to him since the rigged inspection that took Astrid's crew, appeared with an adze over his shoulder and said he needed to practice his framing on something and might as well be useful. Marta sent her grandsons to steam the ribs. The cooper's wife brought bread and left without a word. A woman Astrid had never seen before — riverfolk, by her hands, by the way she walked the mudflats like a deck — spent an entire afternoon silently re-hanging the door of his tool shed, which had not been on his list of problems and now, mysteriously, no longer was.

By the second week, there were a dozen people on the flats on any given morning, and Astrid had stopped flinching when he heard voices behind him.

It should have been harder than this. He kept waiting for it to be harder. For twenty years he had built his life like a bulkhead — tight-fitted, caulked, holding the world on the other side of the planking. He had told himself it was discipline. It was only now, standing knee-deep in sawdust while Tam Broadhurst argued cheerfully about the angle of the stempost, that Astrid understood it had been fear. A man alone can't be abandoned. A man alone can't watch his crew dragged off by corrupt inspectors, can't stand at a dock and hear his own name posted as an outlaw, can't lose a boat that is also, though he'd have denied it under oath, a family.

A man alone just sinks quietly, and the river keeps its books, and nobody reads them.

Well. The books had been read now. Read aloud, in fact, in front of the free-town assembly at Kestrel Ford, in a fifteen-year-old girl's voice that didn't shake until the very last page — and even then, Astrid had thought, watching from the back of the hall, that the shaking looked less like fear than like a dam finally being allowed to crack.

* * *

Mirabel came to the flats on a Sunday, when the work had gone quiet and the keel lay bare and pale along its bed of gravel like the spine of some enormous sleeping animal.

She wore riverfolk clothes now — someone had fitted her out, Marta probably, or the cooper's wife, or all of them in conspiracy — a good wool coat and boots that fit, her hair braided back in the practical style the women of the landings favored. She had grown somehow in the months since the pass, or perhaps Astrid had simply gotten used to seeing her small and frostbitten and furious, and now had to adjust to seeing her tall and fed and calm. The fury was still there. It had just gone quiet, the way a river goes quiet when it stops fighting its banks and starts cutting its canyon.

"It needs a name," she said, standing at the foot of the keel.

"It has a name."

"I know." She crouched and put her palm flat on the white oak, the way you'd check a horse. "I just wanted to hear you say it."

Astrid looked down the length of the keel, thirty feet of oak that would become a hull that would become a home, and said: "*Provision.*"

The word sat on the air between them. Behind it lay everything it had come to mean — not the hoard of legend, not gold in a sea cave, but a girl with a ledger and a boat hook, a father's truth carried over a mountain, the thing you provisioned a journey with when the journey was justice and the road was closed. The thing a river gives you when you stop asking it for treasure and start asking it for passage.

"She'll be a cargo boat," Astrid said. "Not fast. Not pretty. Deep-bellied, for heavy loads. I'm putting a proper cabin aft this time — two bunks."

Mirabel looked up at him. "Two?"

"Riverfolk look after their own," Astrid said. It was the closest he could come, and from the way her mouth twitched, she knew it, and forgave him for it, and would keep letting him practice saying things properly for years.

"I sail with Odessa in three weeks," she said. "First run up the Kestrel tributary. She says I'll learn charts before I learn ropes, because I'm better with ink than with knots." She stood, brushing gravel from her knees. "She says it like it's a compliment. From Odessa Vane, I think it is."

"It is," Astrid said. "From her, everything's a transaction. If she's spending compliments on you, you're worth more than you know."

Mirabel laughed — really laughed, the sound bright and unguarded over the running water — and then grew serious, and did something Astrid did not expect. She took off the cord at her neck, the one that had held her father's ledger through a sea cave and a skiff flight and a mountain pass, and on it hung a small brass fitting, green with age, salvaged from the burned wreck of the *Grey Wren*. She had gone down to the burn site in the thaw, she said. Everyone had known she would.

"For the stemhead," she said, pressing it into his hand. "So she carries something of the old boat. And something of mine. Fair trade."

Astrid closed his fist around the brass. It was warm from her skin. He found, to his considerable irritation, that his eyes were stinging, and turned to look upriver as if checking the weather.

"Fair trade," he managed.

* * *

They launched her at the end of May, on a high bright tide, with half the town on the banks and the other half in whatever floated.

The greased timbers sang as the hull took the water. *Provision* slid off the ways and into the Greywater like a word sliding into a sentence that had been waiting years for it, and the crowd made a sound Astrid had never heard directed at him before — not applause exactly, something rougher and warmer, the sound people make when a thing that should have happened has finally happened. Marta was crying. Tam Broadhurst slapped Astrid on the shoulder hard enough to bruise. And Mirabel stood at the new boat's rail in Odessa's colors, apprenticed and claimed and grinning like the sun off the water, and threw the mooring line ashore to Astrid with perfect, deliberate insolence, making him catch his own boat like any common dockhand.

He caught it. Of course he caught it.

He took her out alone that first evening, just a short run upriver and back, to learn her voice. Every boat had one — a particular groan and shudder in the chop, a way of answering the helm, a temperament. *Provision* spoke in a low, steady register, patient and strong, forgiving of sloppy helmsmanship in a way the *Grey Wren* had never been. She felt, Astrid thought, like a boat built by many hands. Because she was.

At the bend below Widow's Cliff, where the low tide had once unlocked a cave mouth and changed the shape of his whole life, he brought her head to the current and let her drift, and looked up at the cliff face going gold in the evening light.

"You don't leave passengers overboard," he said aloud, to the river, to the cliff, to the dead man whose daughter had taught a lonely captain the difference between cargo and company. "That's the whole trade. That's all of it."

The river said nothing, the way rivers do. But it ran high and kind beneath him, carrying him home, and when he brought *Provision* in to the landing at dusk the lamps were lit along the dock, and people were waiting, and for the first time in twenty years on the Greywater, Astrid Dravenmoor did not count the cost of belonging — he simply stepped ashore, and was counted among them.
