## 1. The Dying Trade

The storm came off the headlands like something with a grudge, and Dominic Hartwell had just enough time to get the *Kestrel* behind the breakwater at Cutter's Reach before the sky opened its throat.

She'd been running the river eleven years now—long enough that the boat felt less like a vessel than a second skin, long enough that she no longer flinched when someone called her Captain. Nobody on this stretch of water knew her as anything but Dom. That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of her life: a name worn down to two syllables, a hull kept patched, a past kept bailed overboard one bucket at a time.

The rain hit sideways. She tied off at the fuel dock with double lines, checked them twice, and went below to wait it out with a pot of coffee going bitter on the stove.

She heard him before she saw him—a dragging footstep on the dock planks, then a fist against her hull, weak as a bird hitting glass.

"Anyone aboard? Anyone at all?"

Dominic stood in the companionway with the lamplight behind her and looked down at the man clinging to her rail. He was sixty if he was a day, soaked through to the marrow, his beard crusted with salt and something darker. His eyes had the fever-bright look of a man who had been arguing with death for days and losing ground.

"You're not my problem," she said. "Harbor office is two hundred yards down."

"The harbor office," he said, and laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent him nearly double. "Closed for the storm. Everything's closed. You're what's left."

He wasn't wrong. The Reach emptied out when weather like this rolled in; even Juno had gone upriver to see her sister. Dominic weighed it—the way you weigh anything, quickly, with your hand already moving—and stepped aside.

"Deck cabin's dry. Don't bleed on anything."

"I don't bleed," he said, hauling himself aboard. "I leak."

His name was Edda. He told her that much between shivers, wrapped in her spare blanket with a mug of coffee he held in both hands like a candle in wind. He was a prospector—or had been, once, before prospecting became diving and diving became obsession. He talked in loops, circling back on himself, and Dominic learned early not to follow too closely. She'd sat with dying men before. They all circled.

Outside, the storm worked itself into a fury. The *Kestrel* groaned against her lines, and rain hammered the deck overhead like a crowd demanding entry.

"You're the captain that runs the night channel," Edda said suddenly. It wasn't a question. "Cutter's Reach down to Grayling Bar. Nobody else will take it."

"Nobody else is stupid enough."

"Or good enough." He set the mug down. His hands were trembling, but his voice had gone steady, and that steadiness frightened her more than the shaking had. "I've been asking after you for three weeks, Captain. Three weeks of bad coffee and worse company, waiting for you to come through."

Dominic's spine cooled. "Then you've wasted your time. I don't carry passengers this season."

"I'm not buying passage." He reached inside his coat—she tensed, hand drifting toward the boathook by the door—but what he drew out was oilcloth, wrapped tight and tied with waxed cord, soft with handling. He unwrapped it on the table between them with the reverence of a priest unveiling a relic.

A map. Hand-drawn, ink gone brown with age, on paper that had been folded and refolded so many times the creases were wearing through. It showed the river from Grayling Bar down to the sea mouth, and there, in a narrow channel marked with careful soundings, a small precise cross and two words in a cramped hand: *the Mirabel.*

Dominic didn't touch it. She made herself not touch it.

"There's no wreck at Grayling Bar," she said. "That channel's been dredged twice since I've run it."

"Dredged above it." Edda's smile was terrible. "The Mirabel went down in '09. Forty-one souls. They say she struck the old shoal in fog. They say a lot of things." He tapped the map with one cracked fingernail. "I found her four years ago. Forty feet down, sitting upright, cargo hold sealed like a tomb. And Captain—I know what's in that hold."

"Gold," Dominic said flatly. There was always gold. In every drowning story, somewhere under the silt, gold.

"Gold," he agreed. "And papers. Manifests, correspondence, insurance filings. Things certain people have spent fifteen years believing were dissolved in salt water." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the fever in his face. "There's a salvage fleet fitting out at Port Merrow right now. Six vessels. Voss's fleet. Cassian Voss has been hunting this wreck longer than I have, and he doesn't want the gold for the gold. He wants it because of what's *with* the gold. And when he finds it, he'll raise it quietly, melt it down quietly, and burn whatever else comes up with it. Quietly."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I can't dive anymore." He said it simply, the way a man admits a leg is gone. "My heart. The doctor at Port Merrow gave me a month, and the sea took most of that back this morning. I need someone to beat Voss there. Someone fast, someone who knows these waters, someone who—" he paused, choosing, "—someone who has reasons of their own to want the truth on the surface."

"I have no reasons," Dominic said. "I have a boat and a schedule and neither of them includes treasure hunts or salvage wars. Take your map to the harbor authority."

"The harbor authority takes Voss's money at the fuel dock. Everyone takes Voss's money." He pushed the map across the table anyway, slow, deliberate, until it rested against her knuckles. The paper was cold. "I'm not selling it, Captain. I'm trading it. For a promise."

"I don't make promises."

"No," Edda said softly. "I imagine you don't. Not anymore."

The rain filled the silence. Dominic looked at the map without looking at it—the way you avoid a mirror when you're not sure who'll be in it. The Mirabel. Forty-one souls. She'd been a child when it happened, mostly, or young enough that the scandal had come to her secondhand, through slammed doors and her father's white face at dinner. She had spent a decade making sure no current carried her anywhere near that history.

"Everyone makes promises," Edda said. "The question is only which ones you keep." He coughed again, longer this time, and when it passed he was gray around the mouth. "I promised a woman named Ada Corrigan I'd bring her husband's watch home. Thirty years ago, when we were both young and stupid and the Mirabel was just a story divers told. She's dead now. Her granddaughter isn't. There are people on that manifest, Captain. Children of children. They think their people died in an accident."

"Maybe they did."

"They didn't." He said it with such flat certainty that the lamp flame seemed to gutter. "And I can prove it. Or I could, once. Now I can prove nothing except where to look."

Dominic stood. "You should sleep. There's a berth aft."

Edda caught her wrist. His grip was astonishing—dry, light bones, and underneath them the grip of a man who had hauled himself up anchor chains for forty years. "Promise me you'll think about it. That's all. Think about it while I sleep. That's a promise you can afford."

It was such a small thing. Such a cheap thing. She heard herself say, "All right. I'll think about it."

He smiled and let go, and she got him into the aft berth, and within the hour he was sleeping the thin, rattling sleep of the nearly finished.

* * *

He lasted two days.

The storm blew itself out against the headlands, and the sun came up obscene and golden over a washed world, and Edda slept through most of it, waking only to drink water and ask, each time, in the same mild voice: "Have you thought about it yet?"

By the second evening the rattle in his breathing had deepened into something tidal. Dominic sat beside the berth because there was nowhere else to be, the boat rocking gently at anchor in the calm after, and held his hand because he asked, and because his hand was cold.

"You remind me of someone," he murmured. "A man I sailed with. Broke every promise he ever made and called it pragmatism. Died rich and alone and nobody came." His eyes found hers, unfocused, seeing something further off than the cabin wall. "Don't be pragmatic, Captain. It's the coldest way to drown."

"Edda—"

"My cache boat," he said. Words coming slower now, spaced like stones across a crossing. "Moored in the reeds past Grayling Bar. Green hull, half-sunk, looks like junk. It isn't junk. Gear inside. A journal. Everything you'll need to understand why the promise matters." His fingers tightened. "Six tides, Captain. Voss sails on the third. You sail tomorrow, or you don't sail at all."

"I haven't said yes."

"You said you'd think about it." A ghost of his earlier smile. "Think faster."

His breathing slowed. Between one tide of breath and the next, he said, "Ada. I tried," and then he said nothing more at all, though his hand stayed warm in hers for a long while after, and Dominic sat in the lamplight holding it, unwilling to be the one who let go first.

* * *

She buried him at the Reach in the morning, in the little churchyard above the fuel dock, paying the groundskeeper out of her own pocket and giving the name Edda and nothing else, because it was all he'd given her. The groundskeeper didn't ask questions. Small harbors knew better than to ask.

Then she went back to the boat and sat at the table where the map still lay, and finally allowed herself to look at it properly.

It was beautiful work, she had to admit that. Every sounding noted, every current marked, the hidden channel past Grayling Bar drawn in with the confidence of a man who had run it a hundred times. And there in the margin, in that same cramped hand, pressed so hard the nib had nearly torn the paper:

*They didn't sink on their own.*

Dominic read it five times. Then she rolled the map closed, tied it with its cord, and set it on the shelf above her chart table, where it sat like a splinter working its way toward the heart.

She told herself she hadn't promised anything. She'd promised to think about it, and thinking wasn't doing, and a dead man couldn't collect. She told herself the salvage fleet was Voss's problem, the gold was Edda's ghost's problem, and the truth—if there was any—could stay forty feet down where it had kept quiet for fifteen years.

But she caught herself, that afternoon, standing at the wheel with the tide tables open, counting.

Six tides. Voss sailed on the third.

And somewhere downriver, in the reeds past Grayling Bar, a green hull waited in the shallows with a dead man's journal inside—and Dominic Hartwell, who had spent ten years being no one, felt the current begin, gently and without mercy, to pull.
