## 2. Six Tides

Dominic worked the tide tables the way other people prayed: alone, at night, with a pencil worn to a stub and no expectation of being answered.

She spread them across the chart table in the wheelhouse of the *Kestrel's Wake*, weighting the corners with her coffee mug, a brass compass, and the dead man's map. Outside, the harbor breathed. Buoys clanked their slow iron bells. Somewhere across the water a generator coughed itself awake, and the smell of diesel and brackish mud came through the cracked window the way it did every morning, the smell she had told herself for ten years was freedom.

The tide tables didn't lie. That was the trouble with them.

She ran the numbers twice, then a third time because she wanted them to be wrong. The Mirabel lay off the Serrano Reach — Edda's map put her in ninety feet of green water past the old light, close enough to shore that the wreck had spent decades hiding in plain sight beneath a shipping lane nobody bothered to dredge anymore. To work a wreck that deep you needed slack water, the two short windows a day when the current quit fighting and let a diver hang on a line like a fly in amber. Six usable tides before the spring tides turned the whole reach into a washing machine and shut everyone out for a week.

Six tides. And the salvage fleet had a head start measured in days.

Dominic sat back and looked at what her pencil had done. The fleet would steam down from Corvane at eight knots, fueling once at Marsh Landing, arriving with time to spare for the fourth tide. Her own boat, if she left tomorrow on the morning water and took the honest route around Cape Ferrow, would make the reach in time for the fifth. Maybe. If the weather held, which it wouldn't; there was a bruise-colored line already building along the western horizon like a rumor.

The fifth tide. Voss's divers would have been down twice by then. Whatever the Mirabel still held — gold, papers, bones — would be coming up on his crane lines while she was still tying off.

Unless she cut through the shallows.

Her eyes went there without her permission, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. The Serrano Flats. Forty miles of unmarked water between the cape and the reach, a drowned maze of sandbars and oyster reefs where the charts gave up and printed only a single word: *unsurveyed*. Local boats didn't cross it. Insurance wouldn't cover it. Ten years ago, before everything, her father's barges had skirted its northern edge, and even then the old pilots had called it the Devil's Tablecloth, because it would look smooth all day and fold up under you without warning.

Running it at night, on a rising tide, with a hand-drawn map as her only guide — that wasn't seamanship. That was a wager with the river itself, and the river always collected.

"You're doing the math."

Juno stood in the wheelhouse doorway with two mugs of coffee, and she set one down next to the tide tables without being asked, which was Juno's way of saying she'd already read the situation from the galley. She was twenty-six, built like a capstan, with a shaved head gone stubbly and a permanent squint from too many years looking into sun on open water. She'd been with Dominic four years now, long enough to know when the captain went quiet it meant either weather or trouble, and usually both.

"I'm doing arithmetic," Dominic said. "Math implies I like the answer."

"Voss's people are at Marsh Landing." Juno leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed. "Talked to Remy this morning, before you were up. His cousin runs the fuel dock down there. Two of Voss's scouts came through yesterday, paid cash, topped three tenders and a supply barge. Asked questions, too."

"What kind of questions."

"The kind you ask when your boss already knows where he's going and just wants to know who else does." Juno picked up the map's corner, careful not to smudge Edda's faded ink, and studied it the way she studied everything — patiently, thoroughly, like she was waiting for it to confess. "They showed a picture. Old woman, white braid, missing two fingers on her left hand. Asking if anyone'd seen her come downriver."

Dominic's coffee suddenly tasted like the bottom of the pot. "And?"

"And Remy's cousin said no, because Remy's cousin owes me money." Juno set the map down. "But it won't hold, Dom. Cash talks louder than favors. Give it a day, maybe two, and somebody down there remembers an old prospector on a beat-up riverboat heading for the reach. Then Voss doesn't need scouts. He'll know exactly whose wake to follow."

Dominic turned back to the chart. The pencil marks stared up at her, six little tides marching toward the end of the month, and above them the flats waited like a held breath.

"She died holding my hand," Dominic said. It came out flatter than she meant it to, so she said the rest of it too, because Juno deserved more than flat. "Edda. Right there on my deck, in the rain. She gave me that map and made me promise I'd get to the Mirabel before the fleet did. And I said yes. I looked a dying woman in the eye and said yes, and then she stopped breathing, and now it turns out keeping that promise means either losing the race or running water that kills boats for a living."

"Or," Juno said, "both."

Dominic almost smiled. "Both."

* * *

She spent the middle of the day pretending to do maintenance, which meant she scraped barnacles off the transom ladder while actually staring at the map, and by late afternoon she'd given up the pretense and carried it below to the cabin, where the light was bad but the walls were thick enough to keep the world out.

The map was beautiful, in the way old things are beautiful — every line drawn by a hand that had known exactly what it knew and admitted freely what it didn't. Edda had marked the channel buoys along the honest route with small confident crosses. But the flats were different. There the ink grew hesitant, exploratory, full of soundings penciled and re-penciled, depths corrected over what must have been years. *12 ft at low water, keep the reef on your port shoulder.* *Sand shifts after nor'easters — trust the color of the water, not the chart.* Edda had run these waters herself, dozens of times, hunting the Mirabel the way other people hunt God: faithfully, obsessively, alone.

Dominic traced the route with one finger. It could be done. That was the terrible thing — it could be done, at night, on a specific tide, following the dark water and the pale water the way Edda's notes described, reading the flats by the shade of the shallows and the texture of the chop. She had grown up on a river. She could read water the way Juno read faces. But reading water in daylight and trusting your life to it blind, in the dark, in a place where the sand moved after every storm —

Her father used to say the river keeps what it's owed. He'd said it the way men say comfortable things, over cards, over brandy, back when the Hartwell name opened doors instead of closing them. She wondered what he'd say now, knowing his daughter was considering the Devil's Tablecloth to chase a dead woman's promise.

She wondered, and then she stopped wondering, because she saw it.

A marginal note, low on the map's eastern edge, written smaller than anything else, in ink that had gone brown with age or salt or something else entirely. She'd read the map a dozen times since Edda died and never seen it — it was tucked into the margin the way a man tucks a confession into a coat pocket, hoping it will be found only by whoever looks hard enough.

Four words:

*They didn't sink on their own.*

Dominic sat very still. The cabin lamp buzzed. Water slapped the hull in its patient rhythm, the same rhythm it had kept all her life, indifferent and eternal.

They didn't sink on their own. Not *she* — not the ship, not the storm, not bad luck or bad ballast. *They.* The crew. The Mirabel hadn't gone down; the Mirabel had been *put* down, with thirty-one souls aboard, and Edda had known it, had spent her whole last decade circling that knowledge in the dark, and had handed it to a stranger on a rainy dock because her hands couldn't hold it anymore.

And something cold slid down Dominic's spine, colder than the note itself, colder than the flats at midnight. Because she knew those waters. Knew them from childhood charts pinned above her father's desk, from company ledgers she'd been forbidden to touch, from the summer the Hartwell Shipping offices burned and her father's face, when he thought no one was watching, looked less like grief than like a man doing sums.

The Mirabel had gone down in Hartwell waters. On a route Hartwell barges had worked. In the year the company's books needed, more than anything, for a certain insured cargo to disappear.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty cabin, to the lamp, to the ghost in the margins. "No, that's — coincidence. Rivers are rivers. Routes are routes."

But her voice sounded like a woman bailing a hull with a teacup, and she heard it, and hated it.

* * *

She came up on deck at dusk and found Juno coiling line on the foredeck, working the rope through her hands in long practiced pulls, not looking up.

"Flats?" Juno asked.

"Flats," Dominic said.

"On the night tide?"

"On the night tide. Day after tomorrow, if we fuel tomorrow morning." Dominic leaned on the rail and watched the last light go copper on the water. "There's a note on the map. Edda wrote that the Mirabel didn't sink on her own."

Juno's hands went still on the coil. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the harbor filled the silence with its small sounds — halyards ticking against masts, a dog barking somewhere ashore, the tide turning with a sigh against the pilings.

"That why she wanted the fleet beaten there?" Juno said finally. "Not the gold?"

"I don't know. Maybe. Maybe she wanted the gold and the truth both, and figured nobody gets one without the other." Dominic turned the map over in her hands, feeling its weight, much heavier than paper had any right to be. "I keep telling myself I can throw this thing over the side and be done with it. Take the loss. Let Voss have whatever's down there and stay small and quiet and alive, the way I've been doing for ten years."

"And?"

"And I promised a dying woman." She looked east, toward the reach, toward the ninety feet of green water and whatever waited in it. "And somebody on that ship was murdered, Juno. Thirty-one somebodies. And the note is in my hand now, whether I want it or not."

Juno finished the coil, set it down, and wiped her palms on her thighs. When she spoke, her voice was steady as a depth sounding. "Then we fuel at first light. And Dom — if we're running the Tablecloth, I want to know one thing before we commit."

"Name it."

"Whose name were you about to say, back in the cabin? Because you've got a look like a woman reading her own death warrant, and I've seen that look exactly once before." Juno met her eyes across the darkening deck. "Whose name is on this, Dominic?"

The river slapped the hull. Once. Twice.

"Hartwell," Dominic said, and the word came out of her like a tooth coming free — bloody, relieving, ten years late.
