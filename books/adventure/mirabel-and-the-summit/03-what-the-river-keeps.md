## 3. What the River Keeps

The channel after dark was a different animal entirely.

Dominic ran the *Pelican* at half-throttle through the narrows, one hand on the wheel, the other cupped around the compass light so its glow wouldn't bleed past the windows. Running lights off. That was illegal in any jurisdiction that mattered, and it was also the only way to move at night without announcing yourself to every scout and salvage tender between here and the coast. The river didn't care about legality. The river cared about whether you knew it or you didn't.

She knew it.

Ten years of knowing it. Ten years of learning which bends silted up after spring flood, which pilings had rotted to stubs just under the surface, where the current ran lazy and where it grabbed your hull like a hand closing around your ankle. She could read the water the way some people read scripture — not word by word but whole passages at a glance, the meaning arriving before the letters did. A seam of glassy calm meant dead water over a snag. A long V of ripples pointed downstream like an arrow toward the deep channel. And where the surface went suddenly rough over nothing at all, there was rock underneath, patient and unforgiving, waiting for someone who thought they knew better.

The moon was three days from full and hung low enough to gild the tops of the swells. Ahead, the channel split around Graveyard Island, and Dominic eased the wheel starboard, taking the eastern passage because the western one had a sandbar that moved every season and she hadn't checked it since August.

Behind her, Juno slept in the cabin, curled under a wool blanket with his boots still on, the way he always slept when he trusted the watch. He'd argued for two hours about running tonight instead of waiting for morning. She'd won the argument the way captains win arguments, by being the captain, but his last words had followed her out onto the deck like a stray dog.

*You're not just running a river, Dom. You're running from something. I can tell by how quiet you get.*

He was twenty-three and too observant for his own good. She'd have to fire him someday, if she wanted to stay invisible properly. She knew she never would.

* * *

Ten years ago, she hadn't been invisible at all. She'd been the most visible thing in the harbor.

Dominic Hartwell, daughter of Aldous Hartwell, whose barges hauled freight on this same river, whose name was painted forty feet tall on the warehouse at Pier Nine, whose handshake opened doors from the fuel docks to the county courthouse. She'd been nineteen and certain the world was built on solid ground, because her father stood on it and he was the most solid man she knew.

Then came the winter the company died.

It started small, the way rot starts small — a reporter asking questions at a chamber dinner, an auditor's letter that Aldous burned in the office stove instead of answering, a barge crew whispering about cargo manifests that didn't match what they'd loaded. Dominic remembered standing in the doorway of her father's study while he sat with a ledger open in front of him and a glass of whiskey untouched beside it, staring at numbers as though they were a confession written in a language he'd forgotten he spoke.

*Papa,* she'd said. *What's happening?*

And he'd looked up at her with an expression she had spent ten years trying to forget, because it wasn't guilt exactly, and it wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. He was calculating something, right then, looking at his own daughter's face, and whatever sum he arrived at decided everything that came after.

Three weeks later the scandal broke in full. Insurance fraud, the papers said. Overinsured cargo, scuttled hulls, claims paid out on losses that had been manufactured in boardrooms rather than suffered at sea. Two of the company's own vessels had gone down in "accidents" over five years, and the payouts had kept the company breathing while it rotted from inside. Aldous Hartwell's signature was on the paperwork. Of course it was. His signature was on everything.

He didn't live to see the trial. Heart failure, the coroner said, though Dominic had always suspected the man's heart had simply done the honorable thing his conscience couldn't manage and quit.

What she remembered most clearly — more clearly than the reporters camped on the lawn, more clearly than the creditors stripping the warehouse, more clearly than her mother's face going gray and permanent — was the night she left. She'd walked down to Pier Nine in the rain, stood under the sign with her father's name peeling off it letter by letter, and made herself a promise: no one would ever again be able to find her by following a name. She took the first name of a woman who'd once been kind to her at a church bake sale, and the surname off a rusted channel marker, and she became Dominic Sandoval before dawn.

The river kept the rest. The river kept everything.

* * *

Now she killed the engine and let the *Pelican* drift through the slack stretch below Graveyard Island, listening. Water slapping the hull. Wind in the pines on the island's spine. Somewhere far off, the groan of a buoy bell, keeping its lonely time.

She unrolled Edda's map across the chart table and weighted the corners with brass fittings, then laid her own charts over it and held both up to the compass light.

The coordinates sat there, inked in a dying woman's careful hand, and Dominic felt the cold come up through the deck plates.

She'd known roughly where the *Mirabel* had gone down — everyone on the river knew the story, the excursion steamer lost with all hands in the storm of '31, a tragedy told in bars and at dockside until it wore smooth as sea glass. But knowing roughly and seeing precisely were different things. The wreck lay in a basin east of Cutter Point, and Cutter Point was—

Her father's barges had worked those waters.

Not occasionally. Every week, for years. The Hartwell route ran coal and timber upriver past Cutter Point twice a week, and young Dominic had ridden along in summers, sitting on the barge rail with her feet dangling over thirty fathoms of black water, eating an orange her father had bought her at the fuel dock, watching him wave to the lighthouse keeper as they passed.

Thirty fathoms beneath her dangling feet, maybe, the *Mirabel* and everyone aboard it, lying in the dark while her father's profitable little fleet steamed overhead.

*They didn't sink on their own.* Edda's marginal note, cramped and urgent, the pen pressed hard enough to tear the paper in places.

Dominic set the maps down and gripped the edge of the chart table until her knuckles ached. Coincidence. It could be coincidence. The river was only so wide; everything on it brushed against everything else eventually. Her father had been a thief and possibly worse, but that didn't mean he'd had anything to do with a passenger steamer going down with women and children aboard. Fraud against insurance companies was one kind of sin. Murder dressed as weather was another order of darkness entirely, and she would not — she refused — to stand in her father's study doorway again, watching him do arithmetic on her face.

But the note was there. *They didn't sink on their own.* Edda had spent forty years diving that basin, according to the rumors, and dying men didn't write lies into their own maps. Whatever Edda had found down there, she'd found it in waters her father had sailed with oranges and smiles.

The past didn't stay sunk. That was the thing nobody told you. You could ballast it, you could pump it, you could paint over the waterline and call the boat by a new name, and still it came up through the seams, inch by inch, until you were standing ankle-deep in your own history wondering why you'd ever believed the bilge pumps would hold.

* * *

A sound woke her from the memory: the particular hollow knock of driftwood against the hull, close aboard. Dominic straightened, scanned the water, and saw the log sliding past off the port bow, pale and blind in the moonlight. Nothing. Just the river doing what the river did, carrying its debris down to the sea, keeping what it wanted to keep and giving back the rest when it pleased.

She rang the engine back to life and brought the *Pelican* up onto plane, gentle, easy, the hull barely kissing the surface. Cutter Point lay two hours ahead. The tide would turn at 3:40, and she wanted to be anchored in the lee of the point before the current reversed, hidden from anyone coming upriver behind them.

Anyone like Voss's scouts. Anyone like Voss himself.

Juno appeared at the cabin door, hair flattened on one side, eyes already sharp. He didn't ask if she'd slept. He looked at the map spread on the table, then at her face, and whatever he saw there made him pour coffee from the thermos and hand her the first cup without a word.

"Cutter Point," she said finally. "That's where we're headed."

His eyebrows went up. "That's old Hartwell water."

So he knew. Of course he knew; he'd grown up on this river too, and the Hartwell name hadn't washed out of it in ten years any more than hers had. She'd been a fool to think choosing a new name would scrub the geography.

"It is," she said.

"You think that's a coincidence? The *Mirabel*, and your father's route?"

"I think," Dominic said, looking out at the black water sliding past, "that the river keeps things, Juno. And I think it's about to give some of them back."

Ahead, around the next bend, a single white light blinked twice and went dark. Not a buoy. Buoys didn't blink twice. Someone was out there in the night ahead of them, signaling to someone else, and every instinct Dominic owned stood up at once and said: *you are not alone on this water anymore.*

She cut the lights and reached for the throttle.
