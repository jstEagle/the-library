## 4. Voss at the Fuel Dock

The fuel dock at Grayling Bend sat where the river widened and forgot itself, a sprawl of rusted pilings and sun-bleached plywood that had been "temporary" for thirty years. Dominic brought the *Kestrel* in on the morning tide with her hat low and her collar up, the way she'd done a hundred times, and told herself this stop was no different from any other. Diesel, ice, coffee, gone before noon.

Juno took the lines without being asked. "You want me to handle the pump?"

"I can pump my own fuel, Juno."

"You can. But you don't have to like it." The kid's eyes flicked downriver, toward the gray smear of hulls that had been gaining on them since dawn. Three vessels, riding heavy, working lights still burning even in daylight. Salvage fleet. They'd been a rumor two days ago; now they were geometry, closing angles on a chart only Dominic could see.

"Keep your head down anyway," she said.

The dock attendant was half asleep behind a counter built from an old hatch cover, and Dominic paid cash, always cash, and signed nothing but a fuel slip with initials that weren't hers. Old habits. Ten years of old habits, worn smooth as river stone. She was coiling the hose when the voice came across the planking, warm as spilled whiskey.

"Captain Hartwell."

Every muscle in her back locked before her mind caught up. *Not the name. Not here.* But when she turned, the man approaching wasn't looking at her like he knew her. He was reading the name stenciled on her transom — *Hartwell*, because she'd been arrogant or tired or both the day she'd lettered it, telling herself it was common enough, forgettable enough. A thousand Hartwells on the water. Just not this one.

He was broad without being soft, silver at the temples, wearing a canvas jacket too clean for the river and boots too expensive for the dock. Behind him, one of his crew — a woman with a clipboard and the flat stare of someone who counted things for a living — waited by a black-hulled salvage tender with *Voss Marine Recovery* painted along its flank in letters you could read from orbit.

"Cassian Voss," he said, extending a hand. His grip was dry and precise, calibrated to communicate exactly how much strength he wanted you to feel. "I couldn't help noticing your boat came up from the shallows side of the bend. Most captains give that stretch a wide berth this time of year."

"Lucky," Dominic said. "Or stupid. Hard to tell the difference some mornings."

He laughed, and it was a good laugh, practiced until it sounded real. "That's what I keep hearing about you. Lucky." He gestured toward the dock's one concession to civilization — a plank counter under an awning where somebody sold coffee and, if you knew to ask, something stronger. "Let me buy you a drink, Captain. Call it professional courtesy between people who make their living off the same river."

"I've got a schedule."

"So do I. Mine has six tides left on it." He said it lightly, watching her face the way a card player watches a hand he hasn't seen yet. "Yours might too, if I'm guessing right."

* * *

She should have walked away. She knew it the way you know weather coming — pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper. But there was a part of her, the part she kept fed on small lies and smaller vanities, that wanted to stand in front of Cassian Voss and be underestimated. Wanted to look the wolf in the eye from inside the sheep pen and feel nothing.

So she sat on the stool beneath the awning while Voss ordered two coffees and, with a glance at the attendant, upgraded them both without asking. The bottle came out from under the counter. Whiskey went into the coffee like it belonged there.

"To the river," Voss said, raising his cup. "May she give up what she owes us."

"That's a strange toast."

"It's an honest one." He drank, set the cup down, and turned on his stool so he faced her fully — the whole attention of him, deliberate as a searchlight. "Here's my situation, Captain. I'm running a recovery operation. Wreck of the *Mirabel*, lost eleven years back with all hands, somewhere in the middle reach. Insurance paid out, families grieved, everyone moved on. Except now there's reason to believe the cargo manifest didn't match what actually went down with her. And what actually went down is worth more than every boat on this dock combined."

Dominic sipped her coffee. "Sounds like a job for the insurance company."

"The insurance company settled. Closed the file." He smiled. "I'm the one who reopens files."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you run these waters alone, quiet, and fast, and you came through the shallows like you've got the middle reach memorized down to the gravel. Because triple rate buys a lot of local knowledge, and I pay triple rate." He let the number sit there between them, fat and patient. "Channels. Currents. Where the silt's shifted since the sinking. What the old-timers say when they're drunk enough. That's all I'm asking for. No risk to you. Just talk."

"Just talk." She laughed softly into her cup. "In my experience, Mr. Voss, talk is the most expensive thing on the river."

"Then name your price for silence instead. Same money either way." He said it easily, generously, a man who genuinely did not care which she chose, because both choices ended with him holding what he wanted.

And that was the thing about Cassian Voss that she understood immediately, the way you understand a current by the way debris moves over it: he wasn't threatening her. Threats were for amateurs. He was making himself inevitable.

"I don't know anything about the *Mirabel*," she said. "Wreck's a ghost story down here. Every bar on the river's got a different set of coordinates and none of them match."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "They don't. Edda Kroy's matched, though."

The name landed in her chest like a hook finding flesh. She kept her face still — kept it moving, even, reaching for her coffee, frowning mildly — and thanked whatever gods watched over liars that the first rule held: never flinch on the name, flinch on the drink. She set the cup down a half-second too hard, a small irritation, the gesture of a busy captain whose time was being wasted.

"Edda Kroy," she repeated. "Prospector? Half-mad old woman who used to trade fish stories at the Grayling landing?"

"Prospector, yes. Thief, definitely." Voss's voice cooled by a single degree, and the temperature drop was more frightening than any heat would have been. "She stole something from me three years ago. A survey. Coordinates, soundings, ten years of my company's money sunk into that wreck before she walked off with the results and vanished." He turned his cup slowly on the counter, watching the coffee turn. "She surfaced last week. Bought passage on a mail boat, got off at a landing upriver. Sick, they said. Dying, maybe."

"River's full of dying people this season."

"Mm. And full of people who helped them die comfortable." He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were pale gray and absolutely without malice, which made them worse. "If she gave anyone anything — a map, a story, a debt — I'd want to know. Not to punish. Edda's beyond punishment now, wherever she is. But paper doesn't die, Captain. Paper just changes hands. And I always find out whose hands."

There it was. Not a question, not an accusation. A net laid gently over the water, waiting for her to swim into it on her own.

Dominic stretched, rolled her shoulders like a woman with a long day ahead, and lied.

"Last I saw Edda Kroy was four years ago, begging diesel off me at the Cutler landing. Paid me in smoked fish and a story about a Spanish galleon up the Sable fork." She shook her head, rueful. "If she had coordinates worth stealing, she spent them on whiskey years ago. You're chasing a dead woman's daydreams, Mr. Voss."

It came out smooth. It came out easy. It came out with exactly the right weariness in it, the right boredom, the right grain of truth — because it *was* true, mostly, every lie she'd ever told was built on a spine of true things — and that was what sickened her. Not that she could lie to him. That she could lie this well. That ten years of hiding had honed the skill instead of rusting it, and somewhere beneath the woman who wanted to do right by a dead prospector was the daughter of Gerald Hartwell, who had learned at his knee how to smile at a man while signing away everything he had.

Her father would have liked Cassian Voss. That thought arrived unbidden and ugly, and she had to take a slow breath around it.

Voss studied her for a long moment. Then he smiled, and the warmth came back into his face like sun after cloud, total and convincing. "Well. Can't blame a man for asking." He stood, dropped bills on the counter that covered their drinks twice over, and put out his hand again. "If the ghost story ever turns solid, Captain — if you hear coordinates in a bar, or a map washes up in a net — my number travels. Triple rate stands. For knowledge, or for silence. Either one keeps you rich and invisible."

*Invisible.* He'd chosen the word like he'd chosen the whiskey — knowing exactly what it was worth to her.

She shook his hand. His grip lingered a half-second past courtesy, and his pale eyes did one slow pass over her — not her face, her *boat*, the *Kestrel*'s lines, her draft marks, the wear pattern on her hull — cataloguing, filing, the way he'd probably already catalogued her name off her transom.

"Hartwell," he said, releasing her. "Common name on this river."

"Common enough," she said.

"Not so common as it was ten years ago." He tipped an imaginary hat, turned, and walked back down the dock toward his black tender, whistling something tuneless and cheerful, and the woman with the clipboard fell in beside him without ever having looked up once.

* * *

Dominic didn't move until the tender's wake reached the pilings. Then she stood, walked to the edge of the dock, and gripped the rail with both hands until the knuckles went white, staring down at the brown water churning around the posts.

"He knows," Juno said quietly. The kid had materialized at her shoulder, fuel receipt in hand, unread. "About the boat. About the name."

"He suspects. There's a difference, and it's the width of this river." She straightened. Her hands were shaking now that there was no one to see, and she hated them for it. "He mentioned Edda. Called her a thief. Which means he's been hunting her longer than we've known she existed, which means—"

"Which means he knows what she had."

"He knows what she *took*." Dominic turned and looked upriver, toward the middle reach, toward the forbidden shallows and the hidden cache and the journal wedged in its waterproof wrapping below Juno's bunk, and felt the whole shape of the next six tides come clear and terrible. Voss hadn't bought her a drink. He'd measured her. And somewhere in that pale gray arithmetic, she'd come out interesting.

"We run tonight," she said. "Before his scouts finish buying fuel."

Juno pocketed the receipt. "Through the shallows? At night?"

"Through the shallows at night." Dominic pushed off the rail and headed for the *Kestrel*, and found, to her surprise, that the shaking had stopped — that underneath the fear there was something harder taking shape, something with edges. "He offered me money to stay invisible, Juno. Triple rate."

"What'd you tell him?"

"Nothing. That was the problem." She stepped aboard and put her hand flat against the wheelhouse door, cold steel under her palm. "Ten years ago I'd have taken the money. Today I stood there lying to his face and all I could think about was Edda's hand going cold in mine." She looked back at the boy — the young woman — whoever Juno was becoming out here on the water with her. "Get the charts. We're going to read that journal again, every page, before dark. If Voss wants the *Mirabel*, he can have the mud around her. Everything else belongs to the drowned."

Downriver, faint as a bruise on the horizon, the black tender was already turning to follow the current — heading not downriver at all, but up. Toward the narrows. Toward the route they'd need to run tonight.

Voss hadn't needed her knowledge after all. He'd only needed to know whether she'd lie to him.

And now he knew.
