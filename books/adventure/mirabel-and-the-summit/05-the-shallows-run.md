## 5. The Shallows Run

Juno had learned to read Dominic Hartwell the way other people read weather, and by the time the sun went down on the fourth day, the forecast was grim.

It wasn't anything she said. It was what she stopped saying. All afternoon she'd been quiet in that particular way of hers—not the easy quiet of a woman at peace with her boat, but the coiled quiet of someone doing arithmetic she didn't like. She stood at the wheel with Edda's map spread across the chart table behind her, weighted down with a coffee mug and a wrench, and every few minutes she'd glance at it the way you'd glance at a dog you suspected was going to bite.

"We're really doing this," Juno said. Not a question.

Dominic didn't answer right away. She watched the water ahead—the broad, brown-gold expanse where the river widened before it split into the delta country, all those braided channels threading toward the coast like veins under skin. Somewhere out there, past the shallows and the sandbars and the drowned forests, lay the Mirabel. And somewhere behind them, closing fast, was Voss's fleet.

"Voss's scouts bought fuel at Marsh Landing two days ago," Dominic said finally. "Juno, they're not following the shipping lane. They don't have to. They've got draft to spare and charts we don't." She turned, and in the last copper light of evening her face looked carved out of something older than she was. "We go through the Sedge Cut or we lose."

"The Sedge Cut isn't a channel. It's a rumor."

"It's a channel if you believe hard enough."

"That's not how boats work."

A ghost of a smile crossed Dominic's mouth and died there. "Get the running lights off. We go dark."

* * *

They entered the Cut an hour after full dark, and Juno understood within ten minutes why no chartmaker had ever bothered to name it.

The river broke apart around them into a maze of shifting sandbars and deadfall, channels that opened invitingly and then closed like traps. The water here didn't move so much as seethe—currents fighting currents, the outgoing tide dragging against the river's own push, stacking up standing waves where no wave had any business standing. There were no markers. Of course there were no markers. Nobody sane came through here at night, and nobody sane came through here at all except smugglers, poachers, and fools.

Juno stood at the bow with the spotlight off, using instead the old deckhand's trick: reading the water itself. Dark water was deep water—usually. Water that shimmered faintly green-black under the stars might be three feet or thirty. But a smooth, oily patch where the current seemed to hesitate, where driftwood spun lazily instead of shooting past—that was a bar rising up to meet them. A line of whitecaps meant wind over shallow water. A sudden flattening, a glassy tongue in the middle of chop, meant something big and solid just under the surface, holding the current still above it.

"Hard to starboard," she called back. "There's a hump dead ahead—I can hear it."

She could hear it. That was the part nobody believed until they'd done it: you could hear shallow water, a low rushing whisper where the bottom squeezed the current through too small a space. The river talked constantly if you listened, and tonight it was saying nothing good.

The engine note changed behind her. Dominic had heard it too.

They slid past the hump so close that Juno could have leaned down and trailed her fingers through the boil of sand it kicked up. Then the channel bent left, then right, then split into three threads, and Dominic chose the middle one without slowing, without asking, as if she'd run this water a hundred times before—as if the map weren't even open anymore.

"You've been through here," Juno said, when she made her way back to the wheelhouse.

"No."

"Then how—"

"I grew up on barges, Juno. My father ran everything from the head of navigation to the sea, legal and otherwise, and he never once paid for a pilot." Dominic's eyes stayed forward. "You learn the grammar of it. Every river speaks the same language. It's just got different accents."

That was the most she'd said about her family in the four years Juno had worked for her, and Juno knew better than to spend it all at once. She filed it away and went back to the bow.

They grounded the first time forty minutes later.

It wasn't Dominic's fault—or it was, but only in the way that all groundings in unmarked water are partly fate. A log raft, half-sunk and black, had shifted since whoever had last run this channel, and it masked the deep slot behind it perfectly. Juno saw the smooth water, called it clear, and the keel kissed mud with a grinding shudder that went up through the hull and into her teeth.

"Port! Port and give her everything!"

Dominic gave her everything. The engine roared, the stern swung, and for one long, horrible moment they sat there with the river trying to suck them onto the bar while the propeller chewed brown water into foam. Then the hull slewed free with a groan, and they were moving again, and neither of them spoke for a full minute.

"Your call was right," Dominic said at last. "The log lied."

"The log lied," Juno agreed, and her hands only shook a little.

The second grounding was worse, because it happened in the narrows where the banks closed in and the current ran like a freight train. They came around a bend and the whole character of the channel changed in fifty yards—the flood tide meeting the river head-on, piling the water up into short, vicious chop—and the depth reader, which had been lying to them all night anyway, blinked out entirely.

"Juno—"

"I see it!" She did see it: a pale ribbon of broken water ahead, the kind of water that meant rock or wreck or bar, and no room to dodge because the banks were walls of cypress and black mud. "Left side! Hug the left!"

"Hugging!"

They hugged. The port runner touched once—a sickening scrape, brief as a scream—and then the current took the stern and swung them through a gap Juno would swear afterward was narrower than the boat, and then they were through, into open lagoon water, and the moon was coming up over the drowned trees like a coin at the bottom of a well.

Juno realized she'd been holding her breath for the better part of a minute. She let it out slowly.

"Dominic."

"Yeah."

"How the hell do you know where you are?"

Dominic was quiet for a moment. Ahead of them, the lagoon opened into a long corridor of water between two ridges of marsh, and somewhere down that corridor, according to a dying man's pencil lines, waited a boat that Edda Vale had hidden from the world.

"Because I've been running from this river for ten years," Dominic said, "and you can't outrun a place you can't read. So I read it. Every day. Every mile." She glanced sideways, and in the moonlight her expression was almost naked. "I told myself it was just habit. Turns out it was rehearsal."

* * *

They found the cache boat at slack water, tucked into a cut behind a screen of dead reeds exactly where the map's final annotation promised: a little X drawn in a shaking hand beside the words *where the heron stands.*

The heron was there. That was the thing about Edda Vale—even dying, even broke, he'd had a showman's touch. A great blue heron stood in the shallows beside the cut like a hired sentry, and it regarded their approach with one baleful yellow eye before lifting off on slow wings, offended.

The cache boat was an old aluminum skiff, camouflaged with faded paint and half sunk under its load, tied off to a cypress knee with a knot Juno recognized as riverman's work. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed against the damp with a care that said *this matters*, they found the treasure.

Not gold. Dive gear—two sets of tanks, regulators, weights, a dive light still in its box. A waterproof case. And inside the case, a journal.

Juno held the light while Dominic unwrapped it. The journal was cheap, spiral-bound, swollen with river damp, its cover soft as cloth. Edda's handwriting filled it in a crabbed, determined scrawl, the letters of a man who'd taught himself to write late and never quite made his peace with it.

"He kept records," Juno murmured. "Prospectors keep records."

"This isn't prospecting records." Dominic turned pages slowly. Tide tables, yes, and salvage notes, coordinates, sketches of the Mirabel's hull based on old survey drawings—but threaded through all of it, page after page, was something else. Names. Dates. Cargo manifests copied out in careful block letters. Insurance filings. A ledger of payments, amounts and initials, spanning months before the sinking.

And in the margins, in fresher ink, notes Edda had added later, after years of digging: *Asked R. Calloway about the manifest discrepancy. He laughed at me.* *Found the second copy of the survey. Depth readings falsified.* *They didn't sink on their own.*

Dominic's jaw was tight. "He spent twenty years building a case nobody would hear."

"People hear things now," Juno said. "Inquiries happen. Papers print things."

"Do they?" Dominic's voice was flat. "For a river crew? For drowned deckhands with no families worth bribing—" She stopped. Turned another page.

Juno watched her stop. Watched her go absolutely still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"What is it?"

Dominic didn't answer. She turned the journal around and held it into the light, one page, one entry, and put her finger under a single line.

*Payment authorized through Hartwell Shipping. Signed: A. Hartwell. He knew what the cargo was. He signed anyway.*

The name sat there in Edda's crabbed capitals, spelled out plain as a brand: HARTWELL.

Juno looked up. Dominic's face in the dive light was the color of the paper, and her eyes had gone somewhere far away—to a house Juno had never seen, a company Juno had only heard rumors of, a scandal ten years old that a captain named Dominic Hartwell had run from so thoroughly she'd repainted her whole life over it.

"Dominic," Juno said carefully. "Is that—"

"My father." The words came out sanded flat. "Augustin Hartwell. That's his signature style—he pressed too hard, always tore the paper." She closed the journal with a gentleness that frightened Juno more than any rage would have. "Edda knew. He must have known who I was the whole time. That's why he picked me. That's why he made me promise."

The river lapped against the hull. Out in the marsh, something splashed and went silent.

"So what do we do?" Juno asked. "We could burn it. Right now. No one would ever know we found it. You could stay invisible."

Dominic looked at the journal for a long time. Then she looked at the water—at the dark corridor leading east, toward the coast, toward the wreck, toward whatever was waiting there—and when she spoke, her voice was quiet and terrible and certain.

"Start the engine, Juno. We've got one tide left, and I'm done hiding in my father's shadow." She tucked the journal inside her coat, against her heart, like a coal she intended to carry into a fire. "It's time somebody dragged the Hartwell name into the light. Even if it has to be me."
