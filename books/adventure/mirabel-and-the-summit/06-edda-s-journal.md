## 6. Edda's Journal

The cache boat's cabin smelled of kerosene and old paper and something faintly sweet—tobacco, maybe, cured long ago in a tin that had rusted shut. Dominic sat at the little fold-down table with the journal open before her, and for a long time she did not touch it.

Outside, dawn was doing its slow gray work on the water. Juno had built a fire on the bank and put coffee on, and the smell of it drifted through the cracked porthole, ordinary and warm, an anchor to a world where things were still simple. Dominic envied him that. She sat with her hands flat on either side of the journal like it might bolt, like it was a live thing she'd trapped under a bucket.

Edda's handwriting filled the first pages in a slanting, impatient script—the hand of someone writing against time even when there wasn't any. *Bought this book in Kestrel Harbor, 4 bits, paper too thin, ink bleeds.* Complaints about weather. A sketch of a heron. A recipe for hardtack crossed out with the annotation *lies, all lies.*

Then, forty pages in, the Mirabel.

Dominic read the way a diver descends—one fathom at a time, telling herself she could still turn back.

*Everyone says she hit the shoal off Cutter Point. Everyone is wrong. I've talked to three men who worked her decks and every one of them tells the same story sideways, like they're afraid of saying it straight. The Mirabel went down with her sea cocks opened. Deliberate. Slow. She settled on the bottom like a woman lying down to sleep because she was told to.*

Dominic's breath fogged the page. She wiped it with her sleeve without looking away from the words.

*Why? Insurance, plain as rot. The owners had over-insured her cargo—gold bullion, or so the manifest said, though half of what went into that hold was lead bar wrapped in gilt paper. The company was drowning in debt. Sink the ship, collect on the gold, and no one counts bars at the bottom of a river. But there were witnesses. Crew who'd seen the loading. A purser who kept his own books. So the cocks were opened at night, and eleven souls went down with a lie, and the lie collected its payout inside the month.*

Eleven. Dominic knew the number. Everyone on the river knew the number. Eleven names carved into the memorial stone at Harrow Bend, worn smooth by thirty years of thumbs.

She turned the page.

*And who signed the survey? Who certified the loss as "act of God, shoal contact, no foul play"?*

There was a name there. Of course there was a name. Edda had written it out in careful block letters, as if she'd wanted it legible to anyone who ever found this book, as if she'd known it would matter more than anything else she'd ever set down.

*HARTWELL. R. Hartwell & Sons Marine Survey, licensed and bonded. His signature is on the loss report dated three days after the sinking. He took payment—I've seen the ledger, or the ghost of it—and he looked the other way while eleven people drowned.*

The fire crackled outside. Coffee boiled over somewhere and Juno swore softly at it, and the world went on being ordinary, and Dominic sat very still in the cold cabin and felt the floor tilt under her though the boat wasn't moving.

Her father. Her father's signature. The same steady, schoolmaster's hand she'd watched sign birthday cards, shipping manifests, the back of her mother's photograph when he'd sent it away for framing. *With love, R.H.* She could draw that H from memory. She had drawn it, a hundred times, in the margins of her schoolbooks, practicing being somebody's daughter.

*No,* she said aloud, to the empty cabin, to Edda, to the dead man who couldn't argue. *No, he wouldn't have. He was careless with money, not with people. He was—*

But even in her own head the defense collapsed under its own weight, because she remembered. She remembered the autumn the company fell apart, the way her father had gone silent at dinner, the way he'd burned papers in the yard barrel one night when he thought everyone was asleep, standing over the flames in his shirtsleeves with his face lit orange and terrible. She'd been fourteen. She'd thought he was destroying evidence of debt. She'd thought that was the worst of it.

She'd run from the scandal. From the name. Ten years on the river under a shortened name, a changed spelling, a woman made of omissions. And all that time she'd believed she was hiding from shame that belonged to her father's failures—bad investments, bad luck, the ordinary rot of a business gone under. Not this. Never this.

He hadn't just let a company die. He had signed his name beneath eleven coffins and cashed the check.

Dominic stood up so fast the table scraped and the journal slid, and she caught it by the spine without meaning to, gripping it like a throat. Her vision had gone narrow and hot. There was a lantern on the shelf above the bunk and she didn't remember lighting it but it was lit, throwing her shadow huge against the bulkhead, a stranger's shape with her fury in it.

* * *

She took the map out of her coat.

Edda's hand-drawn chart of the wreck site, the coordinates, the tide notes, and across the bottom margin in that same thin script: *They didn't sink on their own.* She carried it up onto the bank where the fire was burning down to coals, and Juno looked up from the coffee pot and saw her face and set the pot down slowly, the way you set things down when a animal has come into camp that might be dangerous.

"Captain?"

She held the map over the coals. The heat came up against her palm, gentle, almost kind. All she had to do was let go. Let the river keep its dead and their secrets; let Voss have his gold and his salvage fleet and his dirty ledger-closing; let the past stay sunk, which was where she'd spent ten years pretending it lived. One open hand and she'd never have to carry the name Hartwell anywhere but inside her own skin, where no surveyor's ink could reach it.

"Dominic." Juno was on his feet now. "Captain. What did the book say?"

"It said my father murdered them." Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else reporting a weather event. "Not with his hands. With his pen. Eleven people, Juno. They opened the sea cocks at night and my father wrote *no foul play* at the bottom of it and got paid."

Juno said nothing for a moment. The coals ticked. Downriver, early light was coming up the channel the color of pewter.

"So burn it," he said finally.

She stared at him.

"Burn the map," he said. "Burn the journal too, if you want. I'll help. I'll stand here and watch it go and neither of us will ever speak of it again, and we'll run cargo to Miller's Landing like always, and you'll be nobody from nowhere for the rest of your life." He stepped closer, close enough that she could see the fire reflected twice in his eyes. "That's on the table. It's been on the table since Edda died. You want to know why I haven't touched that map? Because it's yours to burn. But you listen to me first."

"I don't need a lecture—"

"You need the truth, and you're too busy drowning to swim, so I'll say it plain." His voice stayed low, even, the voice he used on frightened passengers in rough water. "You didn't sink that ship. Your father signed a paper, and that's a sin with his name on it, not yours. You've been paying his debts your whole adult life—running, hiding, answering to half a name—and it hasn't bought you one hour of peace. I've watched you flinch when harbormasters ask for identification. I've watched you take the long route around towns where somebody might've known your family. Ten years, Captain. And those eleven people—" he nodded at the map trembling over the coals, "—they've got grandchildren. Maybe great-grandchildren. Kids who grew up hearing their people died in an accident, that it was bad luck and a shoal, and some of them probably believe the river took their kin fair and square. That's the theft here. Not the gold. The truth. Somebody stole the truth and your father helped bury it, and you're holding the only shovel left."

The map dipped toward the coals. The corner began to brown.

"And if you burn it," Juno said quietly, "you're signing the same kind of paper he did. Different ink. Same hand."

Dominic snatched the map back.

She hadn't decided to. Her body decided, some part older than argument, and then she was standing there with the scorched corner smoking between her fingers and her chest heaving like she'd hauled it up from deep water. The burned edge curled, black and fragile, a small crescent missing from the world.

Juno picked up the coffee pot and poured two tin cups. He handed her one. She drank it scalding and didn't care.

"There's a family," she said at last. "The purser Edda mentions—Okafor. First mate Okafor, on the memorial stone. There'll be people carrying that name."

"Harrows Bend registry will know," Juno said. "Small towns remember."

"The inquiry would reopen it. If the journal surfaced. If the loss report was pulled and compared." She was thinking aloud now, the captain's habit, plotting a channel through impossible water. "Voss isn't after truth. He's after bullion. If he raises that wreck first, he'll strip it clean and the evidence goes into his strongroom and stays there, bought and paid for, same as it was thirty years ago. Same hands, different gloves."

"So we get there first."

"We get there first." She looked down at the map, at the small burned wound in its corner, and found she didn't regret it. Some things should show their scars. "Two tides left before the narrows clear. Three before the fleet makes Cutter Point, if Voss doesn't push harder, and he will. We'll be running on fumes and nerve."

Juno grinned, tired and crooked. "Our usual ballast."

But Dominic wasn't smiling. She was looking at the journal where it lay on the ground beside her pack, Edda's block letters facing the sky: *HARTWELL.* Her name. Her father's crime. Somewhere south, Cassian Voss was buying fuel and buying silence, and he already knew Edda's name, which meant sooner or later he'd learn about the cache boat, and the journal, and the woman who'd taken both.

She crouched and tucked the journal into the oilskin wrap, careful as handling a relic, because that's what it was now. Testimony. Cargo more precious than gold and heavier than lead.

"Get the lines in," she said. "We're running the fifth tide through the narrows, and Voss will be waiting for us there."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's what I'd do," Dominic said, and felt the last of the old invisibility tear loose and drift away downstream. "And I taught myself to think like the man I'm hiding from. Time I started thinking like the woman who's going to stop him."

* * *

They cast off into the morning current with the fire drowned behind them and the journal lashed under Dominic's arm like a second heartbeat. Ahead, the river bent south toward the narrows, dark water sliding fast over hidden stone, and somewhere down that channel, riding at anchor or already moving, Cassian Voss was waiting with his divers and his lawyers and his price for her silence.

Dominic stood at the wheel with Edda's map spread on the chart table and her father's name burning a hole through every thought, and rang for full ahead.
