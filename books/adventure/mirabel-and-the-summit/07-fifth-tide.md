## 7. Fifth Tide

The narrows came at them out of the fog like a held breath.

Dominic had run this stretch of river a hundred times in the dark, and she knew it the way she knew her own pulse—the way the current leaned left below the old quarry wall, the way the channel pinched to barely two boat-widths between the drowned pilings of the cannery dock. It was the kind of water where you either committed or died hesitating. She was committing. There was no other way through, not with the fifth tide already turning and Voss's fleet somewhere behind them, fat and fast and certain.

"Engine's running hot," Juno called from below. Her voice carried up through the deck plates, thin with something that wasn't quite fear but lived next door to it.

"Keep her under the red line."

"She wants to go over it."

"She'll have to want a lot of things today." Dominic stood at the wheel, one hand on the spokes, watching the fog peel off the water in rags. The Mirabel's Wake—still wearing her old name then, still anonymous, still *nobody's boat*—pushed upriver against the last of the ebb, and every minute cost them fuel they couldn't spare and time they didn't have.

The journal lay open on the chart table beside her, weighted down with Edda's map. She'd read it three times now. *Hartwell.* The name sat in her chest like swallowed glass. Her father's signature on a scuttling order, her family's money built on thirty-one drowned men and women, and somewhere ahead of them, at the bottom of the sixth tide pool, the proof of it all waiting in a strongbox bolted to a dead ship's frame.

She should have burned it. She'd almost done it, that first night, standing over the galley stove with the pages curled toward the flame—and Juno had put her hand flat on the cover and said, *Not yours to burn, Captain.*

Not hers to burn. Not hers to bury. That was the whole trouble with truth. It didn't care whose throat it came out of.

"Fog's lifting," Juno said, coming up the companionway. She had a coil of line over one shoulder and her knife at her hip, which meant she'd been thinking about the same thing Dominic had been thinking about for the last hour. "That's not good, is it."

"No."

"Because if we can see them—"

"They can see us." Dominic checked the mirror mounted above the wheel, the old brass one her father had installed when this boat was his, back when it had another name entirely. The channel astern was empty gray water and lifting mist. Empty, and getting less empty by the minute.

Then the sound reached them: engines. Not one. Three, maybe four, running heavy and low, the way salvage tugs ran when they weren't pretending to be anything else.

Juno went still. "How did they get ahead of us?"

"They didn't get ahead." Dominic's mouth went dry. "They were waiting. He knew the narrows was the only door." She thought of the fuel dock, of Voss's easy smile and the drink he'd bought her, of how he'd said *Edda* like he was tasting an old grudge. He'd never needed her local knowledge. He'd needed to know whether she'd run—and she'd answered him by running.

The lead vessel came out of the fog bow-first, a black-hulled tug with a crane arm folded flat along its deck and a name painted over in fresh white: *Reclamation*. Behind it, two more shapes resolved, holding position across the channel like a hand closing.

There was no room to turn. There never had been.

"Hold on," Dominic said, and put the wheel hard over.

* * *

They came alongside with a grinding crunch that threw Juno against the rail. Grappling hooks arced across the gap—three, four, five of them, iron claws biting into Dominic's gunwale—and men began hauling before the lines even went taut. The *Reclamation*'s deck looped and swayed beside them, close enough to step across, and Cassian Voss stood at its rail with his hands clasped behind his back like a man watching a delivery arrive.

"Captain Hartwell," he called across the water. "Or should I say Captain *whoever you are this week*. Your papers list a name I don't believe is yours. Interesting problem, paperwork."

"Cut us loose," Dominic said, "and I'll forget your face."

"You won't. You're not built for forgetting—that's your whole tragedy." He gestured, almost courteously, and four of his men swung aboard.

What happened next happened fast and ugly. Juno met the first man with the boathook and took his legs out; the second caught her across the temple with a gaff handle and she dropped to one knee, blood sheeting bright down the side of her face. Dominic got between them and the wheel, and she fought the way she'd learned to fight ten years ago—close, mean, and without any interest in winning points. She broke a nose. Someone broke a bottle over the rail and she felt it kiss her forearm, opening a line of fire from wrist to elbow. A boot caught her ribs and the world went white at the edges.

And through all of it, Voss watched. Never moved. Never raised his voice.

"That's enough," he said finally, and his men stepped back as if a switch had been thrown.

Dominic stood over Juno, breathing hard, arm dripping onto the deck boards. Below them, faintly, she heard the sound of tools in her engine room—metal on metal, deliberate and unhurried.

"What are they doing to my engine?"

"Making sure you arrive when I decide you arrive." Voss stepped across the gap onto her deck, boots landing soft, and looked around the wheelhouse with the frank appraisal of a buyer at auction. His gaze settled on the chart table, on the journal, and Dominic moved to stand over it the way a dog stands over a bone.

"I could take it," Voss said pleasantly. "I could take everything on this boat and drop you both on the quarry bank, and by tomorrow there wouldn't be a harbor master from here to the delta who'd ever heard of you. Ghosts don't file complaints." He smiled. "But I'm offering you something better. A last out, Captain. Walk away. Go back to whatever name you were using last month. Keep your record clean, keep your little life invisible. In exchange—" he tapped the chart table once, lightly, "—you leave the Mirabel to professionals."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I take your papers. All of them. Registration, insurance, survey documents, the works. This boat becomes mine by administrative reality—you'll be operating an undocumented vessel, and the coast authority impounds what I hand them. You'll spend the sixth tide explaining yourself to clerks while my divers strip that wreck to the keel." He spread his hands. "You keep your name clean. That's the offer. It's the only one."

Dominic looked at him for a long moment. Blood ticked off her fingertips. Juno was trying to rise behind her, one hand pressed to her scalp, and Dominic put her arm out—not to hold her back, exactly. Just to feel her there.

Ten years ago, she would have taken the deal. Ten years ago she'd taken exactly that deal, walked away from her father's collapsing company with clean hands and a new name, and told herself survival was the same thing as innocence. She'd spent a decade learning what that choice cost, and here was Cassian Voss, offering to sell her the same lie at triple rate.

"You think I want my name clean," she said. "You've got it backwards. My name's the only thing I've got left that's worth anything, and I'm done hiding it."

Something flickered across Voss's face—disappointment, maybe, or respect, or just the recalculation of a man who'd priced her wrong. "Your father said something like that once. Right before he signed."

"Don't," Dominic said quietly, "say his name to me."

Voss studied her a moment longer, then nodded to his men. They withdrew across the gap in good order, taking with them a canvas bag Juno realized too late held their spare fuel filters, their flare kit, and—Dominic discovered with a cold drop in her stomach—the folder from beneath the wheelhouse bench where she kept the boat's papers. Everything. He'd known where to look. He'd known this boat, or boats like it, down to the drawer.

The grappling lines fell away. The *Reclamation* backed off into the fog, and Voss's voice came trailing across the water, almost gentle:

"Sixth tide, Captain. Bring your testimony. I'll bring my lawyers."

* * *

The engine turned over on the third try and ran like a man with a stone in his shoe—missing, shuddering, losing power every time Juno pushed past half throttle. She came up from the engine room wiping her hands on a rag streaked black, and there was a butterfly bandage over her eyebrow and a bruise blooming purple along her jaw.

"They pulled the governor linkage and scored something in the fuel system. I can nurse it. Maybe eight knots, maybe nine." She sat down heavily on the chart bench and pressed the rag to her scalp again. "He could've sunk us, Dom. He had us dead to rights and he let us go."

"He doesn't want us sunk." Dominic wrapped a strip of cloth around her forearm, tying it one-handed, teeth gritted. "A sunken witness is a rumor. A beaten witness is a warning. He wants me limping into that wreck site just late enough to watch, so the next captain who thinks about crossing him knows exactly what it costs."

"It's working," Juno said. "I'm terrified."

Dominic almost laughed. Then she looked at the chart, at the distance remaining, at the tide tables she'd memorized until they swam behind her eyes, and the laugh died somewhere in her chest.

The fifth tide was turning now. The sixth—the last window, the only window before Voss's full fleet arrived with cranes and lawyers and divers enough to strip a cathedral—would begin in nine hours. Nine hours to cover what should have taken six, on an engine that coughed blood, with a wounded crew of two.

"We can't make it," Juno said. It wasn't despair. It was arithmetic.

Dominic stared at the chart, at the wriggling blue thread of the river, and her eyes kept snagging on a place she'd been avoiding since Gray Harbor—a place marked on no chart she trusted, a cutoff the old pilots called the Throat, where the river tore straight through the marsh instead of bending politely around it. Unmarked. Unrun in living memory, except by fools and by Edda, whose pencil note beside it read simply: *I've done it twice. Once drunk.*

Eight knots through the long bend: eleven hours. Whatever was hidden in the Throat, if it could be survived at all: four.

Below them the engine knocked and stumbled, keeping its own grim time. Above them the fog was burning off, and the sky over the delta had gone the color of hammered tin.

"Get below," Dominic said. "Bolt down everything that isn't welded. And Juno—" she paused, hand on the throttle, feeling the wounded heartbeat of the engine come up through the floorboards, "—if I don't ask you to trust me in the next hour, it's because I'm asking you to trust the river instead."

Juno looked at her, bloodied and steady, and nodded once.

Dominic eased the throttle forward, and the boat that wasn't yet the *Mirabel's Wake* turned its scarred bow toward the Throat.
