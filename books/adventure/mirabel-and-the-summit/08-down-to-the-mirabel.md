## 8. Down to the Mirabel

The tide gave her four hours. Maybe five, if the weather held its breath.

Dominic stood at the rail of her own boat and looked down at the water over the Mirabel, and the water looked back the way deep water always does—patient, indifferent, keeping what it keeps. The chart said forty-one feet to the deck at low slack. Forty-one feet of green-gray cold, of silt and shadow and ten years of river settling over a wound nobody had been allowed to clean.

Juno had wanted to come. Juno had argued, actually—first time in six years the kid had raised his voice on her deck—and she'd shut it down with the flat captain's voice she saved for mutinies and mercy. One diver was a ghost. Two divers was a search party. If Voss's scouts were watching the narrows, and they were, then whatever happened down there needed to happen alone.

"You come up breathing," Juno had said, not quite a question.

"I always come up," she'd said, which wasn't quite an answer, and they'd both let it stand because the alternative was saying something true.

Now she checked her gear a third time anyway. Weight belt. Mask, spit-rinsed, no fog. The knife on her calf—not for the wreck, for the lines. Old wrecks grew fishing line like coral, monofilament strung across doorways by decades of men who'd never thought about who might swim through after them. She'd seen free-divers die on thirty feet of nylon. She didn't intend to join them.

The journal rode in a waterproof bag clipped inside her suit, against her sternum. Edda's cramped handwriting, the names, the dates, the ledger numbers that didn't add up because they'd never been meant to add up. *Hartwell* in her father's hand on the survey certification, dated nine days before the Mirabel went down with eleven souls and a cargo hold full of insured gold that had, according to every honest number Edda could find, already been quietly sold twice.

She'd slept maybe two hours. Every time she closed her eyes she saw the shoe.

No. Not yet. She hadn't seen it yet. That was ahead of her, down in the dark, waiting like everything else that had ever caught up with Dominic Hartwell.

She rolled her shoulders, set the mask, and went off the rail without ceremony—a flat dive, arms extended, cutting the surface so cleanly she barely heard herself enter.

* * *

The cold hit first, as it always did, a fist closing around her chest, and she let it. Panic was just breath spent badly. She exhaled a thin stream, watched it climb toward a sky gone wobbly and silver above her, and pulled herself down the anchor line hand over hand, equalizing every few feet, ears popping their small complaints.

The visibility was worse than she'd hoped. Six feet, maybe eight near the bottom where the silt hung suspended like something drowned mid-thought. Her light cut a cone through it and the cone kept finding nothing—water, particulate, the occasional startled perch flashing silver and gone. She counted her kicks. Kept her breathing long and slow, the way you stretch a lungful of air the way you stretch a dollar in a bad month. Ten years of running boats had taught her body things her mind preferred not to discuss, and one of them was how to be calm forty feet under a river with no tank and no partner and no name anyone would miss.

The hull rose out of the murk like a decision she couldn't take back.

The Mirabel lay heeled hard to port, settled into the mud up to her scuppers, and even furred with a decade of silt and zebra mussel she was beautiful. A coastal freighter, two hundred feet of riveted steel, her superstructure leaning like a tired man against a wall. Dominic's light traveled along her flank and found the name still legible beneath the growth, letters her father's company had painted and her father's company had condemned: M-I-R-A-B-E-L, the R half-eaten by rust, holding on anyway.

"Hello, old girl," she said, and the words came out as a burst of bubbles, and that felt right. Some things you shouldn't say in air.

She swam the length of the cargo hold's port side, following Edda's map in her head—the waystation marked with an X that wasn't X enough, the notation in the margin: *strongbox welded aft of the pump room, below the waterline plates. They put it where the water would guard it.* Whoever *they* were. Her father, probably. Her father's money, certainly. Men who trusted steel and depth more than they trusted each other.

The pump room hatch gaped where the explosion had peeled it back like a tin lid. She'd read the accident report a hundred times in another life, when she was still the daughter of a shipping dynasty instead of a woman with a borrowed name and calloused hands. Boiler failure, the report said. Tragic, the report said. Eleven dead, the report said, and paid out the insurance, and closed the file, and her father had signed the condolence letters himself—she remembered that, remembered being fourteen and watching him write them at the dining table, his pen moving in that careful clerk's hand, and how strange it had seemed even then that he never once looked sad.

Her light swept into the hold and the silt swallowed it.

She went in.

* * *

Inside, the wreck became a country with its own laws. Up was wherever your bubbles said it was. Time was however much air you had left. She moved through corridors of collapsed decking and hanging cable, past a galley where a pot still sat on a stove top furred white with mold, past a doorway frame with a coat hook still screwed in, empty, patient, waiting for a man who had been dead since she was a child.

The strongbox was where Edda said it would be, welded to a frame beside the pump room bulkhead, and the sight of it stopped her heart for one full beat. It was real. After all the maps and margins and dying men's promises, it was real—a steel box the size of a sea chest, its lock plate green with age, sitting in the dark exactly where greedy men had hidden it from other greedy men, guarded only by water and time.

And wedged between the box and the bulkhead, where the current had pushed it sometime in ten years and nothing had ever moved it again—

A shoe.

Small. Leather, once brown, gone gray-green now, the sole curled, a strap with a brass buckle gone dull. A child's shoe, sized for a foot maybe five years old, lying on its side in the silt as if its owner had stepped out of it a moment ago and would be right back.

Dominic hung in the black water and the cold stopped mattering.

Because crew didn't bring children aboard. Company regulation, her father's own signature on the policy—no dependents on working runs, insurance liability, standard clause. Which meant someone had broken the rule. Someone had brought a daughter or a son along on what should have been a routine coastal run, maybe because the child was sick and the run ended near good doctors, maybe because there was no one else to leave her with, maybe just because it was summer and the Mirabel was a fine ship and a father wanted his kid to see the water from the wheelhouse.

And her father—or his partners, or his insurers, or whoever's name was on the ledger Edda had carried twenty years through mountains and rivers to bring to this moment—had lit the boiler anyway.

Eleven souls, the report said. It had never said twelve. The child had never been on the manifest. The child had never been anywhere except the bottom of this hold, uncounted, unmourned, unwitnessed, for ten years while men signed papers and cashed checks and her family's name got polished a little brighter by the shine of everyone else's grief.

Dominic's air was burning low. She knew that distantly, the way you know a train is coming while you stand on the tracks looking at something else. Her vision pulsed at the edges. And she found that she wasn't afraid, and that frightened her more than anything, because what filled the space where fear should have been was something vast and quiet and absolutely certain.

She'd come down here for evidence. For leverage. For a way to save her boat and Juno's skin and her own invisible neck. She'd thought the mission was the gold, or the journal, or beating Voss to the prize.

It wasn't. It had never been. The gold was just the bait the truth had used to get her into the water.

She reached out and picked up the shoe with both hands, gentle, the way you lift anything that used to belong to someone, and tucked it inside her suit against the waterproof bag, against her heart, where it weighed almost nothing and more than the whole wreck.

Then she took the pry bar from her belt, set it against the strongbox's hasp, and went to work, because testimony needs its exhibits and she intended to deliver all of them.

* * *

She surfaced behind her own boat's hull, gasping, hauling the line-bagged strongbox up on the rope she'd rigged, and the world above was wrong before she understood why.

Too many engines. Too many lights, far off downriver but closing, arranged in the deliberate spacing of vessels under orders rather than the scattered laziness of fishermen. And closer—much closer—a tender boat riding at anchor a quarter mile north, dark, running no lights, with a wake pattern around it that said people had recently gone into the water.

Voss's divers. Already down. Already swimming toward a wreck whose secret was currently sitting in the bottom of Dominic's boat.

She got her mask off, lungs heaving, and Juno was suddenly there at the rail, hauling the box over the side, his face pale in the last gray light. "Dom—the narrows—they're coming through, I can see their running lights, we've got maybe—"

"How long?"

"Twenty minutes. Fifteen if they push it."

Dominic looked at the strongbox dripping on her deck. At the shoe. At the south horizon where the coast authority's station sat dark and indifferent, forty miles of river away.

Fifteen minutes to decide what kind of Hartwell she was going to be.

"We're not running," she said, and started uncoiling the tow cable.
