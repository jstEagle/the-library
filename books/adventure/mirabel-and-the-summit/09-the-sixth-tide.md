## 9. The Sixth Tide

The water let her go the way it always did—reluctantly, like a creditor releasing a debtor for one more day.

Dominic broke the surface into gray light and the smell of diesel, and the first thing she saw was the net.

It hung from Voss's salvage barge in a glittering curtain of chain-link and cork floats, stretched across the flooded cargo deck of the Mirabel like a fisherman's patience made steel. She'd felt it before she'd seen it—a vibration through the water, the thrum of a generator somewhere above, and then the shadow of the barge's hull blotting out what little light the overcast morning allowed. Her head came up. Her lungs burned with more than air hunger.

Voss stood at the rail.

He'd changed since the fuel dock—out of his pressed shore clothes and into oilskins that fit him too well, the kind of gear bought by a man who expected to be paid back for every dollar he spent. His hands were folded on the rail in front of him, patient as a banker's. Behind him, two of his divers were already suited up, helmets under their arms, waiting for the order to drop.

"Captain Hartwell," he said, and the name landed on the water between them like a stone.

She treaded water, treading carefully around the word itself. He knew. Of course he knew. Edda's journal had named names, and Voss had been buying information down this coast longer than Dominic had been alive on it. The question was only how much, and what he meant to do about it.

"You went down alone," he said. "That was stupid."

"I've been called worse."

"I'm sure you have." He crouched at the rail, bringing his face closer to hers across four feet of cold harbor water. Up close, the charm was gone—or rather, it had been packed away, like a tool no longer needed for this particular job. What remained was simpler and more honest: appetite. "You have something in your dive bag. I watched you work it loose from the strongbox. You're very good, by the way. Better than my people. That's not a compliment I give lightly."

"It's not one I asked for."

"No." He smiled without warmth. "But here's what you're going to do with it anyway. You're going to hand it up, and then you're going to climb aboard, and we're going to have a conversation about salvage rights, finder's percentages, and the wisdom of a woman with no registered name making claims on anything at all. Because that's the thing, Hartwell. A ghost can't own gold. A ghost can't testify. A ghost can't even file a complaint when someone takes her boat's papers and burns them." He tilted his head. "You've spent ten years being nobody. It shows. And nobodies don't get to win."

The tide was turning. She could feel it in the way the current had begun to tug at her ankles, gentle now, insistent—the Mirabel exhaling after six hours of held breath. Below her, forty feet down, the wreck shifted on its bed of silt as it did with every tide, groaning through its broken spine, ballast stones rolling in the flooded hold like bones in a sack. She'd felt it move while she worked. Felt the whole dead ship lean.

And she'd noticed something else, down there in the dark, that Voss's charts wouldn't show him. Something Edda must have known, because Edda had marked it on the map in that cramped marginal hand: *the old girl rolls with the tide—mind the pilings.*

The mooring pilings of the derelict cannery wharf stood thirty yards off the wreck's port side. Rotten at the waterline, but rooted deep below in concrete poured eighty years ago. When the tide turned hard—and it turned hard in this narrows; she'd run these waters long enough to know—the current funneled between the pilings and the hull like water through a throat.

"The journal," Voss said, when she didn't answer. "Edda kept a journal. I know because I've been looking for it for six years. Names, dates, the insurance filings, the whole rotten architecture. If you have it, you're holding the only document on this coast that can put my operation in prison and make three generations of respectable families answer questions they've paid good money never to answer." His voice dropped, almost gentle. "Or you can give it to me, take your percentage, buy a new boat, and go back to being no one. Safely. Permanently. I'll even return your papers."

*He has Juno,* she thought suddenly, and her stomach dropped. *Where is Juno?*

As if he heard the thought, Voss glanced over his shoulder, toward the far side of the barge, where a small figure sat bound to a stanchion with zip ties, wet to the waist, a bruise blooming along one jaw. Juno's eyes found hers across the water. No fear in them. Just a fierce, furious attention, and a small, deliberate shake of the head: *don't.*

Dominic looked back at Voss. And something settled in her—not calm, exactly. Nothing so kind as calm. More like the moment just before a storm makes landfall, when the pressure drops and every nail in the boat knows what's coming.

"You're right," she said. "A ghost can't testify."

Something flickered in his face—satisfaction, beginning its slow bloom.

"But I stopped being a ghost an hour ago," she said. "I radioed the coast authority from the bottom of the Mirabel. Underwater transmission, low band, patched through the emergency buoy channel. They've got my voice, my name, my position, and a summary of what's in that journal. Dominic Hartwell. Say it again if you like. Get used to it." She watched the satisfaction die. "You're not negotiating with a ghost anymore, Cassian. You're negotiating with a witness."

His face went through several weathers in the space of two seconds—disbelief, recalculation, and then something cold and decisive settling over it like frost on glass. "Then there's nothing left to talk about," he said, and nodded to his divers. "Take her bag. Take the journal. Put her in the net until we're done loading."

The first diver hit the water before Dominic finished drawing breath.

* * *

She went under on purpose.

It was the only direction they weren't watching. The diver plunged past her in a churn of bubbles, grabbing at where she'd been, and Dominic kicked sideways and down, into the green murk along the Mirabel's flank, dragging the dive bag against her chest like a shield. Above her, the surface boiled with searchlights and thrashing limbs. Below her, the wreck waited with its cargo of the drowned.

She pulled herself hand over hand along the hull plates, rust flaking away beneath her gloves like dried blood, and came to the gap in the cargo deck where the tide had torn the plating open decades ago. She slipped inside.

The flooded hold was cathedral-dark, lit only by the wavering gray glow from the breach. Water filled it to within ten feet of the overhead beams, and the ballast lay heaped along the keel line—hundreds of tons of river stone loaded into her belly the day she sank, the day men decided a ship full of evidence should stay on the bottom. As the tide turned, that stone shifted. Dominic could hear it: a deep, grinding whisper, the sound of a mountain deciding which way to lean.

She wedged herself into the shadow of a collapsed bulkhead and watched the light at the breach. The diver came through thirty seconds later, helmet lamp sweeping, clumsy in the confined dark, fighting buoyancy in a space built for cargo, not combat. Dominic waited until he passed beneath her, then dropped onto his back from above, wrapped her arm across his faceplate, and drove him down into the ballast pile. The lamp spun away, casting wild shadows. He thrashed; she held; the current, strengthening now, helped her press him against the stone until his panic exhausted him and his hands came up open, surrendering. She stripped his knife, cut his tether line, and shoved him toward the breach and the surface beyond. Let him explain himself topside.

Through the breach she could hear Voss shouting. Orders fraying at the edges. The generator note changing pitch.

The second diver didn't come in. Smart man. Instead the barge itself began to move—engines backing, repositioning directly over the wreck, and Dominic understood with a lurch what he intended. The crane. The grapple. He was going to tear the strongbox out of the hold with machinery, evidence and all, and crush anything—anyone—in the way.

She swam for the forward section, lungs screaming, and hauled herself up through a cargo hatch into the air pocket trapped beneath the main deck. There she hung in the black half-light, chest heaving, and listened to the ship speak.

The tide was in full turn now. She could feel the Mirabel rolling beneath her, slow and immense, the ballast grinding from port toward starboard as the current funneled through the narrows and pushed against the exposed flank. Edda's note echoed in her skull. *Mind the pilings.*

The barge anchored. Chains rattled down. The crane arm swung out over the breach, cable singing.

Dominic climbed.

She went up through the wreck the way water goes up a drainpipe—through the broken stairwell, along the tilting corridor, out through a scuttle onto the main deck, where the gray morning hit her like a slap and the salvage barge loomed twenty feet away, its crane swinging its grapple out over the open hold. Voss stood at the controls. Juno still lashed to the stanchion, shouting something she couldn't hear over the engines.

The grapple dropped.

Dominic ran the length of the deck as the cables paid out, and instead of fleeing, she did the thing no sane person does: she grabbed the trailing safety line hanging from the barge's stern and hauled herself up it, hand over hand, out of the water and onto the barge's aft deck, dripping, weaponless, furious.

Voss saw her and abandoned the crane controls—which was, she would think later, the only mercy the morning offered him. He came at her with a pry bar.

What followed was not graceful. It was two people fighting on a pitching deck in the rain, slipping on wet steel, trading blows that mostly missed and hurt enormously when they didn't. He was stronger. She was angrier, and anger, she had learned over ten years of running, was a fuel that burned hot and fast and left nothing behind. He caught her across the shoulder; she caught him across the jaw with her elbow; they went down together behind the crane housing, and while they struggled, the Mirabel rolled.

She felt it through the barge's hull before she heard it—a deep shudder, a groan of shifting tonnage, the whole drowned ship leaning into the turn of the tide. The ballast sliding home to starboard. The hull rolling, slowly, ponderously, exactly the way it had rolled for sixty years, twice a day, faithful as scripture.

Toward the pilings.

"Voss," she gasped, pinned beneath him, his forearm across her throat. "Your anchor line. Check your anchor line."

He didn't check it. But he heard the grinding—the enormous, subterranean grind of a hundred-ton hull coming to life—and he looked up, and in that half second of attention stolen from her, Dominic wrenched free, scrambled to the crane controls, and slammed the winch into full reverse.

The grapple, buried in the Mirabel's hold, jerked upward—and jammed. Hooked on the deck plating, on the shifting ballast, on the wreck itself, which chose that moment to roll its full weight against the barge's anchor line. The cable snapped taut. The barge lurched. The anchor, set too close to the pilings by a captain in a hurry, dragged, caught, held—and the Mirabel leaned into it like a wrestler taking a hold, and pulled.

Steel shrieked. The barge swung, broadside, into the current funneling between the pilings and the hull. The rotten piling took the impact at the waterline and held—concrete doesn't rot—but the barge's hull buckled along its seam with a sound like a door slamming in a giant house. Water began to find its way in. Not fast. But finding it.

Voss stared at his dying rig, and then at her, and there was nothing left in his face but arithmetic. "You'll drown us both," he said. "You'll lose everything. The gold, the journal, the boat—"

"The gold was never mine," Dominic said. "Neither was the silence. I'm done carrying other people's cargo."

She wrenched the throttle of the crane one final time, dumping the grapple and cable into the flood between the vessels, severing the last connection, and then she went over the side with the dive bag strapped to her chest, swimming hard for the gap between the pilings as the current took her.

Behind her, Voss's crew scrambled for the life raft. Behind her, the barge settled by the bow against the piling, wedged fast, a wounded animal pinning itself to a fence. Behind her, the Mirabel rolled back onto her keel, satisfied, keeping her dead and her secrets another hour.

Dominic surfaced in the lee of the cannery wharf, spat brackish water, and hauled herself onto the rotted planking. Juno was already there—cut free, half-carrying a younger crewman of Voss's who'd chosen surrender over drowning, both of them soaked and shaking and alive.

"The radio," Dominic said. Her voice came out scraped raw. "The skiff. Now."

They ran. The skiff bobbed at the wharf's end where they'd hidden it before dawn. Dominic keyed the handset with bleeding fingers, and when the coast authority answered, she gave her name clearly, twice, spelling it out letter by letter like a woman signing a ledger she'd avoided for a decade. Position. Evidence. Salvage vessel aground and taking water, crew evacuating. Request immediate response.

Then she sat back against the thwart, the journal heavy and salt-stained against her ribs, and watched the sixth tide carry the last of the cover of darkness out of the narrows.

Juno slumped beside her, pressing a shaking hand against her bruised jaw. "They'll come for the gold," Juno said. "Coast authority, press, everyone. Once they know what's down there, you can't control it. You gave up any say."

"Yeah," Dominic said. She was watching the Mirabel's mast tip break the surface in the distance, black against the brightening sky, and thinking of a child's shoe wedged beside a strongbox, forty feet down in the dark. "Some things shouldn't belong to anybody. Some things just need to be said out loud."

The first government boat appeared at the mouth of the narrows fifteen minutes later, running lights flashing, and behind it—smaller, slower, flying no flag at all—a second hull that made Dominic sit bolt upright.

She knew that hull. She'd grown up watching it work her father's docks.

Someone else had heard the broadcast. Someone who shared her name.
