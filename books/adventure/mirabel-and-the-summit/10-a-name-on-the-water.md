## 10. A Name on the Water

The inquiry room smelled of old varnish and colder coffee, and Dominic Hartwell sat in the third row with her hands folded like a woman waiting for a verdict she had already written herself.

Three weeks had passed since the sixth tide. Three weeks since the coast authority's cutter had come grinding through the fog to find Voss's salvage rig pinned against the piling, his divers hauled half-drowned from the water, and a riverboat captain standing on the flooded cargo deck of the *Mirabel* with a waterlogged journal held above her head like something rescued from a fire. The gold had still been down there then, forty feet under green water, in a strongbox that had waited eleven years for someone to open it for the right reasons.

She had not opened it. That was the part the newspapers couldn't stop printing, as if it were a miracle instead of simply the only thing that had ever made sense. She'd given the coordinates to the authority along with everything else—the journal, Edda's map with its careful marginal note, the manifest pages that proved the *Mirabel* had gone down heavy with insured cargo that had never been aboard. The strongbox was raised by government divers two days later, photographed, catalogued, sealed. Not one coin touched her hands.

Her father's signature was on the fourth page of the journal's appendix. She had known it would be before she saw it. She had felt it coming for six tides, the way you feel weather in an old scar.

Now the inquiry officer, a dry woman named Prentiss with reading glasses on a chain, looked up from the transcript and said, "State your name for the record."

And there it was. The moment she had been running from for ten years, arrived at last wearing a plain gray suit and a court reporter's patient pen.

Dominic had rehearsed this. On the boat, at night, saying it to the dark water over the rail. *Dominic Hartwell.* Just words. Just air shaped by a throat. But her mouth went dry anyway, because names are not words—they are doors, and every door she had closed behind her a decade ago came back at once: the shipping company collapsing in a storm of subpoenas, her father's face going gray across a boardroom table, the reporters on the lawn, the way she had walked out of her own life with a duffel bag and bought a riverboat under a name that wasn't quite a lie—*Dominic* was true enough; it was only the *Hartwell* she had drowned.

"Captain," Prentiss said gently. "Whenever you're ready."

"I'm Dominic Hartwell." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Daughter of Aldous Hartwell, whose company signed the insurance certification on the cargo of the *Mirabel*. I'm here to testify about what he did, and what was done to cover it."

The court reporter's keys clattered. Somewhere in the gallery a man shifted, and Dominic recognized him without turning—Voss's lawyer, or maybe Voss himself, she hadn't checked, didn't need to. He had taken her boat's papers hostage in the narrows and lost them to a coast authority boarding party three days later, along with his salvage license, pending review. He had offered her one last out on that flooded deck, right up until the tide turned against him. *Walk away, Hartwell. Keep your name clean.*

Her name had never been the dirty part. That was the thing she understood now, standing in front of the inquiry with the whole ugly ledger spread out in evidence bags: the shame had never belonged to her. She had carried it ten years like ballast, and ballast is only useful if you're trying to stay sunk.

She testified for four hours. She told them about Edda dying in the wheelhouse with her hand going cold around Dominic's fingers, about the promise that stuck like a splinter, about the shallows run and the cache boat and Juno reading the depth sounder by lantern light while grounding loomed twice out of the dark. She told them about the child's shoe wedged beside the strongbox, small enough to fit in her palm, and how it had changed everything—how treasure became testimony somewhere between the surface and the seabed, and she had come up breathing a different person.

When Prentiss asked her why she'd refused Voss's money—not once but twice, triple rate, then whatever she wanted to name—Dominic looked at her own hands for a long moment.

"Because I've spent ten years being no one," she said, "and I know exactly what it costs. The crew of the *Mirabel* spent one night being no one, forever. Somebody owed them their names back. It might as well be me."

* * *

The ruling came down on a Tuesday, delivered to the harbor office in a folder thick as a brick. Juno brought it aboard waving it over her head like a trophy fish.

"They cleared them," she said, dropping into the wheelhouse where Dominic was re-caulking a window seam. "All nine crew. Negligence findings vacated. The inquiry found the *Mirabel* was overloaded on paper only—phantom cargo, inflated manifests—and the sinking was deliberate, to bury the fraud evidence when the audit got close. They named your father. They named two underwriters. They named the harbormaster who looked the other way." Juno dropped onto the bench, grinning, eyes bright. "Posthumous commendations for the crew. There's going to be a ceremony."

"A ceremony," Dominic repeated. The word sat strangely in her mouth.

"On the water. Families coming in from three provinces. The descendants get the gold—there was a fight about it, some claimant tried to argue salvage rights, but the ruling was explicit. Wreck found through evidence of criminal concealment; salvage law doesn't apply. It goes to the estates." Juno turned the folder over in her hands, suddenly quieter. "There's a letter in here for you. From one of the families."

Dominic set down her caulking iron. Her hands weren't quite steady, and she let Juno pretend not to notice.

The letter was written in blue ink on lined paper, in a careful old-fashioned hand. A woman named Alma Reyes, daughter of the *Mirabel*'s second engineer, Tomás Reyes, who had been twenty-nine years old when the ship went down with his daughter's shoe stowed in his sea chest. *They told us for eleven years my father was careless,* the letter said. *They told us he overloaded his own ship. My mother died believing it. I want you to know that what you pulled out of that water was worth more than the gold, though we'll take the gold too, and gladly. Come to the ceremony. Come as family. You're the only one who went down and came back to tell us.*

Dominic read it twice. Then she folded it along its creases, very precisely, and put it in the drawer beneath the chart table where she kept the things she could not throw away—which, these days, was nearly everything.

* * *

She painted the stern on a Thursday morning, when the light came flat and silver off the harbor and the tide stood slack, holding its breath between movements.

The old name had been sanded off years ago anyway—she'd bought the boat with the transom already blank, which should have told her something about herself even then. A woman who buys a nameless boat is a woman planning to disappear. For ten years the bare wood had served her fine. Boats without names don't get remembered. Captains without names don't get asked questions.

Juno showed up with two brushes and a can of enamel the color of deep water, uninvited and unrepentant about it.

"You could at least knock," Dominic said.

"It's a boat."

"You could at least announce yourself."

"I'm announcing myself now." Juno popped the can and stirred. "So. What are we calling her? And don't say nothing, because I already primed the letters' outlines last night while you were sleeping, and you can sand them off yourself if you hate them."

Dominic looked at the faint pencil ghosts on the transom. Juno's handwriting, translated into primer: neat, confident, already certain.

"*Mirabel's Wake*," Dominic said. It was the first time she'd said it aloud. She'd chosen it weeks ago, in the dark, the way you choose anything true—without letting yourself examine it too closely, because examination is where cowardice gets its foothold.

Juno nodded slowly, like a judge accepting a plea. "Wake as in the trail on the water. Or wake as in the funeral kind?"

"Both." Dominic took the second brush. "That's the point. Everything she left behind us, and everyone we left behind her. You paint the M. I'll do the rest."

They worked through the morning, Juno chattering about the ceremony logistics—the descendant families arriving on a chartered ferry, the authority sending a cutter with honors, some deputy minister who wanted a photograph standing next to the strongbox—and Dominic mostly listening, answering in single syllables, letting the rhythm of brushstrokes and tide-talk carry her. The letters went on black against the pale hull, and each one felt less like labeling and more like unveiling. By noon the transom read *MIRABEL'S WAKE*, home port line beneath it, and Dominic stepped back onto the dock to look at the whole of it, and found her throat tight and her eyes hot and did not mind either one.

A boat with a name can be found. That was the whole terror of it, laid plain in the sunlight: a named boat gets remembered, gets sought, gets boarded by its own history. She had spent a decade being unfindable and had told herself it was freedom. It had been a hold full of ballast, nothing more.

"Well?" Juno said.

"She's beautiful," Dominic said, and meant the boat, and meant more than the boat.

* * *

The harbor registry office occupied a narrow brick building near the fuel dock, the same fuel dock where Cassian Voss had once bought her a drink she never touched and offered her triple rate for her silence. She passed it on the way, and the memory rose and fell away again like a swell, leaving no residue. Voss himself was gone from the river—license suspended, boats impounded, facing charges that would take years to crawl through the courts. She had testified against him too, calmly, completely, and discovered that telling the truth about a bad man was far easier than lying to protect a dead one.

Inside, the registry clerk—a young man with ink-stained fingers and a window's worth of afternoon light—slid the renewal form across the counter.

"Vessel name?"

"*Mirabel's Wake*."

He wrote it down. "Owner-operator?"

And there it was again, the small door, the ordinary door, the one she'd walked past a thousand times in a thousand forms: *Name of captain.*

Dominic picked up the pen. Ten years ago she had filled in a false surname on a bill of sale in a river town two hundred miles upstream, and the lie had been so easy it frightened her—that was the thing she remembered most, not the fear afterward but the ease itself, how smoothly the old skills had come back to her hand, how naturally a Hartwell could make paperwork say anything. Her father's gift. Her inheritance, the bad half of it.

She wrote *Dominic Hartwell* in clear block letters, and the pen did not hesitate, and the world did not end. The clerk stamped the page without looking up. That was all it was. Ink on a line. A door opening onto an ordinary room.

"That's everything," the clerk said. "Congratulations on the new registration, Captain Hartwell."

"Thank you," she said, and walked out into the light carrying her own name like a boat carries its keel—not decoration, but the thing that keeps you upright when the water turns against you.

Outside, the harbor lay gold and slack-watered, and at the pier the chartered ferry was already coming in with the families aboard, flags at the rails, and Juno was waving both arms from the deck of the *Mirabel's Wake*, and somewhere out past the breakwater the tide had turned and was running fair.

Dominic Hartwell squared her shoulders, and went down the dock to meet them.
