## 1. Cargo and Rumors

The fog came off the water at four in the morning like something with intent, and by six it had swallowed the whole coast. Ada Voss stood on the dock at Kestrel Point with a cup of coffee going cold in her hand and watched her floatplane disappear into the gray ten feet from where she stood — just the sound of it, the idle burble of the engine, and the ghost of her own reflection in the water where the plane should have been.

She'd been flying this coast for eleven years and she still didn't trust fog. Wind she could work with. Rain, sure. Even ice, if you respected it. But fog was the sea reaching up to take back what it had lent you, and every time she climbed into it she felt like a borrower who'd overstayed.

"Voss." Ruth Okafor's voice carried across the dock before Ruth did, which was how it always worked. The harbor master moved like a woman who'd made peace with her own weight years ago and saw no reason to hurry it anywhere. "You're not flying in that."

"I'm flying over it. Ceiling opens up at eight hundred feet past the headland. I checked twice."

"You checked twice last month too."

"Last month I was right."

Ruth stopped beside her and looked out at the white nothing where the plane sat. She was holding an envelope, Ada noticed. A thick one, the kind that came from offices with letterheads.

"Medicine run?" Ruth asked.

"Insulin for Miller Inlet, antibiotics for the schoolhouse at Tern, and somebody's heart medication that the pharmacy swore they shipped two weeks ago." Ada drained the coffee and set the cup on a piling. "And whatever's in that envelope you're pretending isn't for me."

Ruth handed it over without ceremony. "Came on the mail float yesterday. Figured you'd want to burn it somewhere private."

Ada weighed it in her hand. She already knew what it was. There were only three kinds of thick envelopes in her life: invoices, legal paper, and the particular species of both that arrived when a bank in Vancouver decided it had been patient long enough. She tore the corner open right there on the dock, because Ruth had earned better than pretense, and because tearing it open in public hurt less than reading it alone.

*Final notice of default,* said the letterhead. *Re: aircraft registration CF-VOS. Absent payment of $41,300 within thirty days, seizure proceedings will commence.*

Thirty days. The number landed in her chest and sat there, heavy as ballast.

"Bank?" Ruth asked, though she knew.

"Bank."

"Your mechanic say anything about the engine overhaul? Because if they're coming for the plane—"

"They're not coming for anything yet." Ada folded the letter along its original creases, precise, deliberate, the way her father had taught her to fold charts so they'd survive salt air. "I've got the Miller Inlet contract, the Tern contract, and the medevac retainer. That's three thousand a month against eleven hundred in fuel and maintenance. I can dig out."

"Three thousand a month against forty-one thousand," Ruth said. "That's fourteen months of perfect luck."

"Fourteen months of flying," Ada corrected. "Luck's got nothing to do with it."

Ruth looked at her the way she always did at this point in the conversation — that long, level look that said she'd heard the arithmetic and wasn't fooled by it, but also wasn't going to kick a woman while she was standing on a dock at dawn. "Coffee's fresh in the office," she said instead. "Come argue with me indoors where it's warm."

* * *

The fog held until nine, which meant Ada spent ninety minutes in the harbor master's office drinking bad coffee and listening to the radio traffic of a coast that couldn't see itself. It was there, half-listening to a fisherman complain about his depth sounder, that she first heard the name again.

"—told you, the tide tables are wrong, that's all I'm saying. Lowest water anyone's seen since what, '09? My grandfather used to say a tide like that comes maybe once a decade, and when it does, the bay gives back what it took."

"That old story?" A second voice, younger, amused. "The Meridian?"

"You laugh. My grandfather hauled salvage out of Graywater Sound for thirty years and he never laughed about the Meridian. Said the hull went down in the deep channel with a full hold and nobody ever found so much as a brass fitting. Said the man who was piloting her in—" a pause, the particular pause people put around certain names on this coast, "—said he knew exactly where he put her."

Ada kept her eyes on the tide table she was marking up and let the conversation wash over her. She was good at this. Eleven years of small harbors and smaller towns had taught her how to be furniture — present, useful, unremarkable. People talked in front of pilots the way they talked in front of dogs.

"Pilot error," the young voice said. "Everybody knows it. He came in hot in weather he shouldn't have been in and put a hundred tons of ship and somebody else's cargo on the bottom. Only reason anybody remembers the name is the money that was aboard."

"The money and the shame," the fisherman agreed. "Both of them still down there, far as anyone knows."

Ada drew a line through a tide height and wrote the correct one above it. Her hand was steady. She noticed herself noticing that — checking her own hand for steadiness the way some people checked a wound.

Her father's name was Elias Voss, and for twenty-two years it had been the answer to a question nobody on this coast asked politely anymore. *Whatever happened to the man who lost the Meridian?* He'd died when she was nineteen — heart, in his sleep, in a bunkhouse at a logging camp two hundred miles north where he'd gone to fly supplies and, she suspected, to be somewhere his name meant nothing. She'd inherited his debts, his logbooks, his spare propeller, and a brass compass that he'd worn on a lanyard around his neck for as long as she could remember, and which now hung around hers like an apology she hadn't figured out how to stop making.

The compass was the strange part. It was a good instrument — old, naval, the kind with a gimbal and a mother-of-pearl face — but it had one flaw she'd never been able to fix or explain. Most days it pointed north like any honest needle. But sometimes, in certain places along this coast, it swung. Not wandered — swung, deliberate, like a dog hitting the end of its lead toward something specific. She'd learned to ignore it. She'd learned to wear it anyway, because taking it off felt like a second abandonment, and she'd already decided at nineteen that she wasn't going to be the daughter who threw away the last thing her father had trusted her with.

Even if the last thing he'd trusted her with might have been a lie.

* * *

The ceiling lifted at nine-forty and Ada flew.

This was the part of the day that belonged to her, whatever the banks thought. The fog burned off in ragged sheets below her and the coast came up to meet the light — black rock, green timber, the white threads of creeks running down to inlets the color of hammered tin. She flew the way other people prayed: alone, low enough to smell the salt cedar, hands moving through the routine without needing her. Medicine in the nose compartment, forty pounds of insulin packed in gel ice, and beneath it the quiet hum of an engine that was due for an overhaul she could not afford, carrying her over water that had taken everything her father had and most of what she had too.

Miller Inlet's strip was a gravel bar that appeared and disappeared with the rain. She touched down light, taxied to the dock where old Sam Teller waited with his truck, and helped him carry the cooler up the ramp while he talked at her the way lonely men talk at anyone who lands.

"Heard you were grounded," he said. "Heard the bank's got papers on your plane."

"Heard that too," Ada said. "Heard wrong."

Sam grinned, toothless on one side. "That's the spirit. You know what my grandmother used to say about debt? She said debt's just a tide. Comes in, goes out. You just got to keep your feet on ground that doesn't flood."

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have made a terrible banker."

"She drowned," Sam said cheerfully, "so probably yes."

He signed for the insulin. She signed his carbon copy. And then, because the wind had come around and given her twenty minutes before she needed to be airborne, she sat on the tailgate of his truck and drank the coffee he poured from a thermos and listened, because Sam Teller was the best-informed man on fifty miles of coast and everyone knew it.

"Tide's going to be something tomorrow," he said. "Lowest in ten years, the oceanography fellas say. Some lunar thing. Whole bay at Miller's going to drain out past the point. You'll see bottom nobody's seen since before you were born."

"Anybody worried?"

"Worried!" Sam laughed. "Half the town's planning to walk out on the flats and gawk. Other half says leave it alone." He lowered his voice, though there was no one within a mile to hear. "You know what's under that water out past the point? Cave. Sea cave, big as a church, mouth of it underwater except at the lowest lows. My grandfather dove it once, back when he was young and stupid. Said it goes back farther than his lamp would reach. Said there was old timbers inside."

"Timbers."

"Ship timbers. Or so he claimed, and my grandfather claimed a lot of things." Sam shrugged. "Point is, with a tide like tomorrow's, that cave mouth's going to stand open in broad daylight for the first time in a decade. You watch. Every crackpot with a boat and a flashlight will be out there."

Ada said nothing. But her hand had gone, without her permission, to the compass hanging under her jacket, and she felt the brass warm against her palm, and she thought — absurdly, uselessly — *not now.*

Because here was the thing she had never told anyone, not Ruth, not anyone: the compass swung in exactly one place on this entire coast. One bay. And the bay it pointed toward, every single time she'd flown over it or anchored in it or cursed it in bad weather, was the bay whose cave Sam Teller was talking about. She'd told herself for fifteen years it was a coincidence of iron in the rock. Magnetic anomaly. Geology. Anything but the alternative, which was that her father's compass knew something about that water that she didn't, and that the man who'd lost the Meridian had worn this instrument around his neck until the day he died.

"Ada?" Sam was looking at her. "You went somewhere."

"Just working the schedule." She stood, brushed gravel off her jeans, and managed a smile she'd practiced into reliability. "Thanks for the coffee, Sam. Keep the insulin cold and tell Doc Reyes I'll be back Thursday."

* * *

She flew the leg to Tern in hard sunlight, and it was there, circling down toward the schoolhouse dock, that the phone in the panel mount lit up with the number she'd been dodging all week.

Meridian Recovery Partners. Third call today. She let it ring out, watched the name blink and die, and felt the small vicious satisfaction of a woman winning an argument she was losing everywhere else.

They'd started calling in March. A salvage outfit, the voicemails said, conducting historical research along the coast. They had questions about her father. They had questions, specifically, about the Meridian, and about whether any of Elias Voss's effects — logs, charts, instruments — had passed to his next of kin. The early messages had been polite. The recent ones had developed a new texture, a patience that was worse than rudeness, the tone of men who had already decided how things would go and were simply waiting for her to catch up.

She'd called them back exactly once, four weeks ago, mostly to make them stop. A smooth voice named Crane — Silas Crane — had thanked her warmly for returning the call and asked, in the manner of a man discussing weather, whether she happened to still have her father's compass.

She'd said no.

She'd lied to a stranger on the phone and then sat in the plane afterward with her heart hammering, wondering why a question about a sixty-dollar piece of brass had made her lie.

Now, on final approach to Tern, she caught herself doing the arithmetic she hated: forty-one thousand dollars owed, three thousand a month coming in, and a salvage company that wanted something she owned and seemed willing, eventually, to pay for it. She hated the arithmetic because it kept arriving at the same sum. The compass was worth nothing. The compass was worth everything. Both numbers were true, and only one of them would keep her flying.

She set the plane down on Tern's dock, delivered the antibiotics to a teacher with a head cold and a classroom full of kids who swarmed the floats asking about the wheels, and took off again into the long gold afternoon with the coast unrolling ahead of her like a chart someone had left unfinished.

* * *

It was nearly dark when she got back to Kestrel Point, and the harbor master's office light was still on. She almost walked past. Then she saw Ruth standing out on the end of the dock, looking west at the water, and something in the set of her shoulders stopped Ada's feet.

"Long day?" Ada asked.

Ruth didn't turn around. "Boat came in an hour ago. Charter out of the south, big one, riding high and burning money. Six vessels total, working up the coast in a loose formation. Professional." She turned then, and her face in the dock light was carefully blank, which on Ruth Okafor was louder than shouting. "They're calling themselves Corvus Salvage. Their advance man booked every moorage slip in the harbor through the end of the season. Paid cash."

"Salvage for what? There's nothing to salvage on this stretch."

"That's what I asked." Ruth reached into her coat and pulled out a business card, held it between two fingers like something dead. "Their man asked me one question before he asked about slips. Asked if I knew whether Elias Voss had any family still in the area."

The tide lapped against the pilings. Somewhere across the harbor a generator coughed and settled. Ada stood very still, and under her jacket, against her skin, the compass hung silent and warm, pointing — as it always did here, as it always did in this one bay — toward the water.

"Tomorrow's the big tide," Ruth said quietly. "Lowest in ten years. And a fleet full of strangers just paid cash to sit on top of it."

Ada looked west, where the sun had gone down behind the headland that sheltered the bay, and where, beneath forty feet of cold green water, a cave mouth waited in the dark for morning.

"I know," she said.
