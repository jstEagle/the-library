## 3. Debt Collectors

Ruth Okafor had run the harbor at Kestrel Point for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned to read the water the way other people read faces. The bay told her when a storm was coming two days before the weather service did. It told her which fishermen were lying about their catch. And on the morning after the lowest tide in a decade, it told her — through the wake pattern of a black-hulled survey vessel idling off the breakwater with its transducers down — that trouble had arrived wearing a corporate paint job.

She stood at her office window with a mug of coffee going cold and counted the strangers.

Three boats she didn't know, moored where her regulars should have been. The *Alder* was gone from slip fourteen. The *Marta B*, gone. The *Pelican*, gone — and old Sam Pelikan hadn't missed a salmon opener since his wife died. Every working hull in Kestrel Point had been chartered out, and every one of them was flying or carrying some version of the same flag: a black bird on gray field, wings folded, like something waiting.

Corvus Marine Salvage. She'd heard the name before, years back, attached to a wreck job in the Aleutians that had ended in litigation and a drowned deckhand nobody talked about. She'd hoped never to see them this far north.

"Morning, Ruth." Danny Aqpaluk came in shaking rain off his jacket, holding up a fuel receipt like a grievance. "You know what those Corvus people are paying? Double rate. Cash. Sam took it before I could even talk to him. Said he's got grandkids now."

"I heard."

"They asked me too." Danny set the receipt on her counter as if filing a complaint. "I said no. But Ruth — they're not asking about fish. They're asking about the cave."

Ruth turned from the window. "What cave?"

"The one everybody saw yesterday. Tide went out past anything on the charts, and there's this opening under the headland that half the town swears wasn't there before. Kids were down on the flats taking pictures of it before their mothers called them home." He rubbed his jaw. "The Corvus man — the one running it, name of Crane — he's got a chart table set up in the harbor house they rented, and he's paying for stories. Fifty dollars a story about the Meridian. A hundred if you can point at anything on a map."

The coffee in Ruth's hand had stopped being something she wanted. She set it down.

"And what are they saying," she asked carefully, "about Elias Voss?"

Danny looked at her the way people look at a door they're not sure is locked. "That's the thing. Crane himself came by my boat yesterday evening. Polite fella. Soft voice. Asked me if I knew Ada Voss, and whether her father ever talked about salvage work. I said Elias Voss has been dead eleven years and his daughter keeps to herself. And Crane smiled at me like I'd confirmed something instead of nothing, and gave me fifty dollars anyway, and said—" Danny paused, and Ruth watched him decide whether to say it. "He said, 'Her father left something unfinished out here. I've come to finish it.'"

* * *

By noon Ruth had walked the docks twice, and by one o'clock she had heard enough versions of Silas Crane to fill a ledger. He wore a gray coat. He spoke quietly. He tipped well. He had a photograph of a young man in a floatplane jacket, and he showed it around without explaining who it was until someone recognized the face — and then he watched, very closely, how they reacted.

It was Elias Voss's face. Younger than Ruth remembered him, but unmistakable: that long jaw, the wind-burned cheekbones, the particular way of standing slightly forward on his feet like the ground might move.

Ruth had known Elias for thirty years. She had drunk bad coffee with him in this office while he waited out fog, and she had been one of maybe four people in Kestrel Point who never joined the chorus about the Meridian. Let the rest of the coast say what they liked — that Elias Voss had been drunk at the wheel of the tug, that he'd grounded the Meridian on an uncharted rock out of carelessness, that eleven men would have lived if he'd been sober. Ruth had flown with him once, in the co-pilot seat of the old Cessna, and she had seen the man check his instruments four times in ten minutes. Careless people don't do that. Scared people do. Or careful people carrying something heavy.

When the inquiry board stripped his license, Elias hadn't argued. That was the part that had always bothered Ruth most. An innocent man argues. He fights. He spends everything he has on lawyers and appeals. Elias had just signed the paper, put his compass in his pocket, and started hauling freight for cash, and he'd carried whatever it was silently until his heart quit on him over the Kuskokwim flats.

Now here was a stranger in a gray coat asking questions about him, and here was Ada Voss due back on the strip this afternoon with a load of insulin and a phone full of creditors, and Ruth Okafor had spent nineteen years learning exactly one skill that mattered: knowing which storms you could outrun and which ones you had to batten down for.

This one, you batted down.

* * *

She found Silas Crane at four o'clock, because he found her first.

She was coming up the ramp from the fuel dock when a tall man detached himself from the shadow of the harbor house porch and fell into step beside her, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back like a man touring a garden.

"Harbormaster Okafor." His voice was mild, almost apologetic. "Silas Crane. I'd have made an appointment, but your office hours seem to be whenever the water says so."

"That's right." She kept walking. He kept pace.

"You run a tidy harbor. I'll say that. Nineteen years, I'm told. People trust you." He let that sit a moment. "Trust is a currency, Ms. Okafor. Most people don't realize they're spending it."

"I find most people who talk like that are broke in every other kind."

Crane laughed — a short, genuine-sounding sound, which somehow made it worse. "I wanted to introduce myself before you heard about me secondhand. My company has leased the harbor house through the season. We're conducting a survey operation. Historical recovery." He gestured vaguely at the bay, at the headland, at the whole gray sweep of it. "There's a wreck in these waters. The *Meridian*. You may have heard the stories."

"I've heard the stories."

"Then you've heard the name Elias Voss attached to them." Crane stopped walking, and Ruth, out of some instinct older than courtesy, stopped too. Up close his eyes were pale and very still, like water over deep rock. "I want to be honest with you, harbormaster, because I understand honesty buys cooperation faster than money does. Eleven men died on the Meridian. Their families have waited a long time for answers. I intend to provide them. Whatever that requires."

"Whatever it requires," Ruth repeated.

"There's a daughter," Crane said. "Ada Voss. She flies out of your strip. I understand she's been... struggling financially. Debts are heavy things. If she were to come into information — documents, effects of her father's — my company compensates fairly for provenance. Generously, even." He smiled, and it reached all the way to his eyes, which stayed cold. "Her father left something unfinished out here. I've come to finish it. I'd consider it a professional courtesy if you'd pass that along."

"She's busy," Ruth said. "Flying medicine. Real work."

"Of course." Crane inclined his head, gracious as a diplomat. "One more thing, and then I'll leave you to your harbor." He leaned in slightly, and dropped his voice to something almost intimate. "The tide tables. Yours are the official record for this district. I'll need certified copies for the next six cycles. All six. Can you arrange that?"

Ruth looked at him for a long moment. Six tides. Not seven, not the season — six, precise as a countdown.

"I can arrange that," she said.

"Wonderful." He shook her hand, brief and dry. "You have a good evening, Ms. Okafor. This is a fine community. I do hope we won't have to disrupt it."

She watched him walk back toward the harbor house, gray coat moving through gray light, and thought: *he already knows about the tides. He knows about the cave. He knows more than anyone in this town knows, and he's buying our tables just to confirm what he already has.*

Then she went to find a telephone number she'd sworn she'd only use in emergencies, and decided this qualified.

* * *

Ada landed at 5:40, wheels kissing gravel with the softness of someone who'd done it ten thousand times, and was unloading insulin coolers onto a hand truck before the propeller stopped. Ruth watched her work from the edge of the strip — small woman, efficient, dark hair tied back, moving with the economy of somebody who couldn't afford wasted motion in any sense of the word. Twenty-nine years old and carrying a dead man's name, a dead man's compass, and forty-one thousand dollars of somebody else's money.

"Ruth." Ada nodded, not slowing. "If you're here about the tie-down fees, I get paid Thursday."

"I'm not here about tie-down fees."

That stopped her. Ada straightened, wiped her hands on her jeans, and looked at Ruth with the flat directness of a woman who had learned to take bad news standing up. "Okay."

"Walk with me."

They walked the length of the strip, past the fuel tanks, out to where the gravel ended and the drop to the tideline began. Below them the bay lay exposed and glistening, still drained from yesterday's anomaly, and Ruth saw Ada's eyes go to the headland — to the black slot of the cave mouth — and away again, quick as a card trick.

"You were down there," Ruth said.

"I flew over it."

"You were down there." Ruth said it gently. "Somebody saw a floatplane on the flats yesterday morning. Blue Cessna, tail number ending in four-seven. I've known that plane since before you could reach the pedals."

Ada said nothing. She stood looking at the water, jaw tight, and Ruth let the silence do its work. It took nearly a minute.

"There were initials carved inside," Ada finally said, very quietly. "E.V., and a date. 1989. Before I was born." Her hand went to her collar, where a cord hung, and Ruth understood without being shown that the compass was on it. "My father's initials, Ruth. In a cave that isn't supposed to exist, under a wreck that isn't supposed to exist."

"Is that why the needle swings?"

Ada's head came around sharply. "How do you—"

"Because a man in a gray coat asked me for tide tables today. Certified copies, six tidal cycles, no more. And he asked about you. And he asked about your father. And he's chartered every boat in my harbor at double cash rate, including Sam Pelikan, who wouldn't charter his own coffin." Ruth turned to face her fully. "His name is Silas Crane. Corvus Marine Salvage. And Ada — he showed people a photograph of Elias. Young Elias. He carries it around like a warrant."

The color went out of Ada's face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"What does he want?"

"He says your father left something unfinished and he's come to finish it. He says he wants to buy anything of Elias's you might have. Documents. Effects." Ruth hesitated, then finished it, because Ada deserved the whole weight of it. "He used the word *compass*. Not to me — to Danny Aqpaluk. Asked whether you still carry it."

Ada's hand closed over the cord at her neck, knuckles white. For a moment she looked younger than twenty-nine, younger than the debt, younger than the graveyard of reputation she'd inherited — just a girl whose father had been taken from her twice, once by the sea and once by the story.

"He can't have it," she said. Flat. Final. The voice of bedrock.

"No," Ruth agreed. "He can't."

They stood together at the edge of the strip while the light failed, and below them the first thin sheet of the returning tide slid across the flats toward the cave mouth, patient as everything the sea does.

"Here's what I know," Ruth said at last. "Crane has money, boats, lawyers, and time. You have a compass, a cave, and maybe six chances before the tides close that door for another decade. You cannot do this alone. Alone, you lose — to him or to the water, doesn't matter which. So I'm going to make you an offer, and I want you to hear all of it before you answer."

Ada turned. Waiting.

"My skiff. The *Kestrel*. Thirty-foot aluminum, twin outboards, sound as the day I bought her. She's yours for as long as this takes — fuel, gear, the works. Teo Marsh will dive for you; he owes me and he owes the water. And I'll stand on the dock and tell this town whatever you need told." Ruth held up one finger. "In exchange for one thing. One condition, non-negotiable."

"Which is?"

"Whatever you find down there — gold, papers, bones, truth — this town hears it. Not after Crane buries it. Not after the lawyers file it. *First.* Kestrel Point kept your father's memory alive when the whole coast was spitting on his name. Half these people still believe he was innocent, and they believed it when believing cost them something." Ruth's voice didn't rise; it lowered, which was worse. "I'm not selling you a boat, Ada. I'm buying you a family. And families don't keep secrets that size."

Ada looked at her for a long time. Out past the breakwater, the black survey vessel had lit its deck floods, and the glare lay across the water like a scar.

"If what I find makes him look worse," Ada said slowly, "not better — if my father really did sink that ship on purpose, or for pay, or—" She stopped. Started again. "You still want the town to hear it?"

"I want the town to hear *true*," Ruth said. "We've had eleven years of the other thing. True is the only thing left worth having."

Ada's eyes went to the cave mouth one more time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — steadier, colder, the voice Ruth imagined she used on radios in weather that killed other pilots.

"Then I need to tell you something else first. Because if Crane's asking about my father, and Junie's calling me twice a day and hanging up—"

She stopped. Ruth watched the thought land on her face like a gull on a rail.

"—then somebody in my own family has already been talking to that man," Ada said. "And I think I know who."

Ruth said nothing. The tide crept up the flats below them, inch by inch, closing the distance to the cave like a hand closing around a secret — and somewhere out in the dark, six tides had already begun to count themselves down.
