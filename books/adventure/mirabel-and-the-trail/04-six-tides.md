## 4. Six Tides

The tide tables came off the printer at the harbormaster's office still warm, and Ada read them the way other people read death sentences.

Ruth Okafor stood beside her, arms crossed, watching the numbers do their work. "Say it out loud," she said. "I want to hear how bad it is."

Ada ran her finger down the column. "Spring tides end in nine days. After that the range drops below what we need to clear the throat of that cave by more than a foot. Maybe two." She looked up. "We get six windows. Six low tides where the entrance is passable and the surge channel isn't trying to kill us."

"Six."

"Six."

Ruth exhaled through her nose, slow, the way she did when she was converting bad news into logistics. She'd been harbor master for nineteen years and Ada had never once seen her panic. She'd seen her reorganize, though — the set of her jaw shifting like cargo being lashed down before weather.

"Six tides," Ruth said. "And Crane's fleet has been in port four days already."

"I know."

"So whatever we're going to do, we do it with what's on this dock right now."

Ada folded the tide tables into quarters and put them in her jacket pocket, next to the compass. The compass had been quiet since the morning of the lowest tide — needle steady, almost smug, pointing at nothing now that it had shown her what it wanted to show her. Her father's compass. The one thing of his she'd kept after the maritime board stripped his license posthumously and half the coast decided Elias Voss had drowned the *Meridian* through negligence or worse.

She'd gone back to the cave mouth twice since that first morning, both times at safe distances, both times standing on the rocks above the drained bay with binoculars, watching the water fall away from black stone. Both times she'd found fresh boot prints in the tidal silt that weren't hers.

Someone else was counting tides too.

* * *

Teo Marsh worked out of a shed behind the fuel dock that smelled of neoprene and diesel, and when Ada pushed the door open he didn't look up from the regulator he was rebuilding.

"If you're here about the compressor," he said, "it's fixed. If you're here about the money I owe Ruth, tell her Thursday."

"I'm not here about either."

That got his head up. Teo was thirty-four and looked fifty around the eyes, the way working divers did, skin creased from years of squinting through masks at things twenty fathoms down. He had hands that never fully stopped moving, even now, turning the regulator over and over like worry beads.

"Voss," he said. "Heard you found something in the bay."

Word traveled fast in a town of four hundred people. Word traveled faster when it was about money.

"Who told you?"

"Doesn't matter." He set the regulator down. "What matters is whether it's true."

Ada closed the door behind her. Outside, gulls were screaming over the fish plant, and somewhere down the dock someone's radio played a country song with more static than melody.

"There's a cave under the point," she said. "It only opens at extreme low water. Inside there's wreckage. Mast timber, iron fittings, maybe more. The compass my father carried points at it like it's got a string tied to it."

Teo was very still. Divers learned stillness; it was the difference between living long and not. "You're saying the *Meridian*."

"I'm saying I don't know yet. I'm saying I need someone who can go down past the mast and find out. And I'm saying the Corvus fleet is sitting in our harbor with three dive boats and a captain who's been asking questions about my father, which means if it *is* the *Meridian*, we've got six tides before they take it out from under us."

Teo looked at his hands. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone careful. "You know why I can't say no."

"I know you're in debt."

"Everyone's in debt. That's not—" He stopped, started again. "My brother dove for Corvus, up north. Three seasons. Came home with the bends so bad they had to fly him to Vancouver, and Corvus's insurance called it pre-existing. He's been in a chair two years. Their lawyers buried him in paper until he signed a release for eleven thousand dollars." His jaw worked. "Eleven thousand. For legs."

Ada said nothing. There wasn't anything to say that wasn't an insult.

"So yeah," Teo said. "I'll dive your cave. But I want it understood — if we find that wreck, and there's anything on it that says what Corvus did to my brother, I'm not selling that for a share. I'm using it."

"Understood."

"And Voss." He picked the regulator back up, thumbs moving again. "Your father. People talk. You know what they say."

"I know exactly what they say."

"You believe them?"

She thought about the compass, warm against her ribs. About the carved initials in the cave wall — E.V., cut deep and confident, by a hand she'd have known anywhere. A man planning something doesn't carve his initials like a signature. A man hiding something might.

"I believe he was there," she said. "The rest we'll find out."

* * *

Junie Voss lived forty minutes up the coast road in a rented house with a rusted swing set in the yard, and Ada sat in her floatplane at the government dock for twenty minutes before she worked up the nerve to call her.

They hadn't spoken properly in three years. Not since the funeral, when Junie had stood at the grave and said, too quietly for anyone but Ada to hear, that Elias Voss had gotten exactly what he deserved, and Ada had said something back that she'd spent three years not quite regretting.

The phone rang twice.

"I saw your plane land," Junie said, instead of hello. Her voice was flat, tired, older than Ada remembered. "Figured you'd either come see me or fly away again. You always were better at flying away."

"Junie—"

"The girl's asleep. Come in or don't."

The house smelled of coffee and children's medicine. Photographs crowded the refrigerator door, and most of them were of a small girl with Junie's dark hair and a smile that had been surgically corrected twice already and needed it a third time. Ada knew this because Ruth told her, because Ruth told everyone everything eventually, gently, as harbor masters do. The surgery was in Seattle. The surgery cost more than the house.

Junie poured two coffees without asking and sat across the table, and for a moment the cousins just looked at each other across three years of silence.

"You found something," Junie said. "In the bay. Ruth called me."

"Of course she did."

"She thought I should hear it from family before the town made it ugly." Junie wrapped both hands around her mug. "Is it true? The cave? The wreck?"

"It's true there's a wreck. Whether it's the *Meridian*, I won't know until someone dives it."

"And you came to me because—"

"Because you're a Voss. Because Dad left you letters I've never seen, and you left the funeral before I could ask. And because—" Ada stopped. Started over. "Because I need crew, Junie. Six tides. That's all the sea's giving me. And I'd rather do this with family than without it, even family that hates me."

Something moved behind Junie's eyes — not softening exactly. Recalculating.

"I don't hate you," she said finally. "I hated him. There's a difference." She stared into her coffee. "You want to know why I said what I said at the funeral? Why I wouldn't look at his casket?"

"Yes."

"Because three months before he died, he sent me a letter. Told me if anything happened to him, I should watch the water at the point. Those exact words. *Watch the water at the point.*" She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "He knew, Ada. Whatever happened to the *Meridian*, he knew it was coming. And he let it happen anyway, and then he died before anyone could ask him why, and I have spent three years wondering if my own uncle was a coward or a criminal or both."

The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs the little girl coughed in her sleep.

"He was my dad," Ada said quietly.

"He was mine too. He taught me to read charts before he taught you." Junie wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, like swatting a fly. "So yes. I'll crew for you. On one condition."

"Name it."

"When we find out what he did — and we will — you don't flinch. You don't defend him before the evidence is even in the boat. You look at it straight, same as me."

Ada reached into her jacket and touched the compass without meaning to. *Watch the water at the point.* He'd known. He'd pointed her here from beyond his own grave, and she still didn't know if it was a confession or an inheritance.

"Straight," she agreed.

* * *

They met that night in the back room of the harbormaster's office, the four of them around a chart table under a single buzzing fluorescent tube: Ada, Ruth, Teo, Junie. Rain ticked against the window. On the table, the tide tables lay open, six dates circled in red grease pencil, and beside them the harbor chart with the point marked and the cave's approximate position penciled in from Ada's observations.

Ruth laid it out like the officer she'd once been, before the coast turned her into paperwork.

"Crane's got three vessels and a support barge. We've got one skiff, one floatplane, one diver, and whatever the four of us can carry." She tapped the circled dates. "First window opens day after tomorrow, 0540. Each window gives us roughly ninety minutes of workable access, less if the swell's up. We run the entrance on tide one, survey on tide two, and if there's salvage worth taking, we work tides three through five and are clear before the season closes on six."

"And if Crane figures out where we're going?" Junie asked.

"He already knows *something*," Ada said. "There were boot prints at the cave. Fresh ones. Someone's been watching the water."

"Then we assume he knows everything," Ruth said, "and plan accordingly. Which means speed, silence, and no radio chatter except emergency channels." She looked around the table. "One more thing, and I want it said plain. Whatever's down there — gold, papers, cargo — it comes back to this town first. That's the price of my skiff. Crane's people have been buying up harbor debt for two years, and every month another business in this town gets a letter from a holding company nobody's heard of. If there's something in that wreck that explains where that money came from, this town hears it before any lawyer does."

Silence, except the rain.

"That's a heavy condition," Teo said.

"It's the only one I've got."

Ada looked at the faces around the table. Ruth, who was betting nineteen years of standing on a rumor in a cave. Teo, who wanted justice for his brother and would settle for leverage. Junie, who wanted the truth about a dead man even if it burned the memory down. And herself — she wasn't sure anymore what she wanted. Her debt forgiven. Her father's name clean. Or just to stop wearing the compass like an apology.

None of them were there for the same reason. All of them needed the *Meridian*. It occurred to her that this was either the definition of a crew or the definition of a disaster, and the sea would decide which.

"Vote," she said. "All in, or walk now and no hard feelings."

Nobody walked.

"All in," Ruth said.

"All in," said Teo.

Junie was last. She looked at the chart, at the red-circled dates, at Ada. "All in," she said. "But Ada — those boot prints. Next time you check that cave, check them close. Because if somebody else has been counting these same six tides—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to.

Ada gathered the tide tables, folded them into her jacket against the compass, and felt the weight of all of it: six windows, ninety minutes each, one skiff, and a rival fleet sleeping three hundred yards down the dock.

Outside, the rain was easing, and far out past the breakwater she could see the riding lights of the Corvus vessels, strung across the dark harbor like a row of patient, unblinking eyes.

Five hundred hours to the first tide.
