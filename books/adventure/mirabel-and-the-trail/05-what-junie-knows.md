## 5. What Junie Knows

Junie Voss had rehearsed this conversation for eleven years, and in all her rehearsals, Ada never once looked at her the way she was looking at her now.

They sat in the back of Ruth Okafor's harbormaster office, at a scarred table meant for chart work, with a kettle going on a hot plate that smelled faintly of burnt coffee and diesel. Rain ticked against the single window. Outside, the Corvus fleet sat at anchor in the roadstead like a hand of black cards, and somewhere out there, past the point, past the shoals, the bay was draining toward the second of six tides.

Ada had called her two days ago. Two words, mostly: *Come home.* Junie had come. She'd left her daughter, Sophie, with Mrs. Ansel in Ketchikan, kissed the small warm forehead, lied about how long she'd be gone, and flown north on a mail plane with a canvas satchel heavy enough to change everything.

The satchel was on the table between them now, unopened.

"You said you had something to show me," Ada said. "You've been sitting on it since the floatplane landed."

"I'm working up to it."

"Don't." Ada's voice wasn't cruel. That was the worst part. It was patient, the way she got when she was bracing herself. "Whatever it is, just say it. I've spent my whole life waiting for the other shoe. I'd rather it drop than hover."

Junie looked at her hands. Her daughter's hands, her mother's hands — broad-knuckled, nail-bitten, practical. Voss hands. She thought about how many times she'd picked up the phone over the years and dialed Ada's number and hung up before the first ring finished. Eleven years of that. Eleven years of letters in a fireproof box under her bed, letters she'd read so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth.

She unzipped the satchel.

* * *

The letters had come to her by accident, which was how most true things arrived, in Junie's experience. Fourteen months after the funeral, when their grandmother's house on Wrangell Island finally sold and Junie drove out to clear the attic alone because Ada couldn't bear to set foot in it — that was the story everyone told, anyway. The truth was Ada had offered to come, and Junie had told her not to bother. She'd wanted the attic to herself. She'd wanted, God help her, to find something worth money, because by then the first of Sophie's medical bills had started arriving like weather off the Pacific, front after front after front.

What she found instead was a lockbox bolted under the floorboards beneath the cedar chest, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as a sermon after thirty years.

Inside: papers. A marriage certificate nobody had ever mentioned. A photograph of their father at twenty-two, standing on the deck of a ship Junie didn't recognize, squinting into a sun that made him look almost happy. And letters. Nine of them. Business correspondence, mostly, typed on onionskin, signed with initials instead of names — but three of them were handwritten, and those three were the reason she'd carried them across two hundred miles of grey water and hadn't slept properly since.

She laid them on the chart table one at a time, squared like a dealer, and watched her cousin's face.

"This is his handwriting," Ada said immediately. Of course it was. She'd grown up reading it on fuel logs and grocery lists and the margins of navigation charts. "Where did you get these?"

"Grandma's attic. Under the floor."

"Under the—" Ada's jaw worked. "I helped clear that attic. I carried the cedar chest down those stairs myself."

"I know," Junie said. "I'm sorry. I told you not to come. That's on me."

Ada didn't answer that. She pulled the first letter closer and read it the way she read weather — fast, then again slow, hunting the system inside it. The rain kept ticking. The kettle climbed toward a whistle nobody moved to stop.

The first letter was dated eleven weeks before the Meridian went down. It was typed, unsigned except for a block-letter initial — **C** — and it offered their father, Elias Voss, master of the coastal freighter *Meridian*, a sum of money that made Junie's eyes skip off the page even now, the eleventh time she'd read it. In exchange: he would take aboard a cargo manifest that did not match his official manifest, and he would deliver the ship somewhere other than where the shipping board believed he was delivering it.

"Paid to move it," Ada said flatly. "Or hide it. That's what you're telling me."

"I'm telling you what it says."

"He scuttled her in the Narrows. Forty-one years the whole coast has said Elias Voss ran his ship aground drunk or asleep or both, and drowned four men doing it." Ada's voice stayed level, which frightened Junie more than shouting would have. "You're holding a piece of paper that says he took money to do it."

"The third letter is in his hand," Junie said quietly. "Read it yourself. Don't take my word. You never do."

Ada read it. Junie watched her lips move slightly around the words, the way she used to as a girl, reading by flashlight under blankets. Their father's handwriting slanted hard to starboard, cramped, urgent, the pen pressed so deep it had nearly torn the paper in places. He wrote about the cargo. He wrote about men who came to the dock at night and paid him in banded bills and told him the less he knew the longer he'd live. He wrote — and this was the sentence Junie had underlined in pencil fourteen years ago and regretted underlining ever since — *I am doing this for my girls whether it damns me or not.*

"For us," Ada whispered. "He wrote *for my girls*."

"And then the ship went down with four men aboard, and every inquiry on this coast pinned it on him, and the money — if there ever was money — never reached anyone named Voss." Junie spread her hands. "That's the part I can't make fit, Ada. If he was paid, where'd it go? We grew up eating government cheese. Either he never took it, or—"

"Or he hid it," Ada said. "With the ship."

The kettle screamed. Ruth appeared from the front office, killed the burner without a word, poured three cups of something dark, and withdrew again with the tact of a woman who'd spent thirty years pretending not to hear things in her own harbor. Junie wrapped both hands around her cup and didn't drink. Ada didn't touch hers.

* * *

For a long moment there was only the rain and the two of them breathing, and the distance between them across the chart table, which was maybe three feet wide and eleven years deep.

"You knew," Ada said finally. "You've known since the attic. Fourteen years, June. You let me carry it. Every dock, every bar, every time some old man spat at his name — you let me carry it alone because you were sitting on proof."

"Proof of what?" Junie heard her own voice crack and hated it. "Proof he took dirty money? Proof he was mixed up with people who sank ships? I didn't know what it proved, Ada. I still don't. That's why I never showed anybody. Because half of me hoped I'd dreamed the whole box up, and the other half knew that the day I put these letters on a table in front of you, you'd look at me exactly the way you're looking at me right now."

"How am I looking at you?"

"Like I'm the one who sank the ship."

Ada stood up. She walked to the window, arms crossed, and stared out at the Corvus fleet through the rain-streaked glass, and Junie understood suddenly that her cousin wasn't angry at her — or wasn't only angry at her. She was doing arithmetic. Junie could see it happening behind her eyes, the same arithmetic she'd been doing for fourteen years: *if the letters are real, then he chose it; if he chose it, then he chose it over us; if he chose it over us, then everything I built my life on top of is standing on water.*

"I don't believe they're real," Ada said to the window.

"They're in his hand."

"His hand can be copied. His signature was on a logbook page too, supposedly, down in that cave — did you know that? Teo brought up a page with Dad's signature on it. Everyone keeps finding Dad's name in places it shouldn't be, and nobody asks the obvious question." She turned. Her eyes were wet and furious and Junie loved her so much it felt like an injury. "Who benefits, June? Who benefits from Elias Voss being a criminal instead of a drunk? Think about it. A drunk is an accident. A criminal is a *conspiracy*, and conspiracies have heirs."

"C," Junie said. "The letters are signed C."

Something moved through Ada's face then — quick, involuntary, like a fish turning under ice. Crane. Silas Crane, whose grandfather's name was all over the salvage registries of that era, whose fleet was sitting right out there in the rain, whose captain had already been asking questions about their father before any of them knew there was a cave.

"You think I don't know that?" Junie said. "Why do you think I'm here? Why do you think I finally dug the box out from under my bed after fourteen years? Because three weeks ago a man in a good coat came to my apartment in Ketchikan and asked me, very politely, whether Elias Voss had any living relatives who might have inherited papers. He knew things, Ada. Dates. Manifest numbers. Things that aren't public." She swallowed. "I told him nothing. But I stopped sleeping that night, and I haven't really slept since, and when your call came I was already packed."

Ada came back to the table slowly. She put her palm flat on the third letter, the one in their father's hand, and Junie watched her cousin's thumb tremble against the paper — against the ink of a dead man's confession, or a dead man's frame-up, and no way on this green earth yet to know which.

"If he did it," Ada said, barely audible, "he did it for us. That's what it says. For my girls. He took dirty money to keep us fed, and it bought us nothing, and it cost him his name and four men their lives, and I'm supposed to feel *what*, exactly? Grateful?"

"I don't know what you're supposed to feel. I've had fourteen years and I still don't know what I feel. Mostly I feel like a coward."

"You are a coward," Ada said, and it landed like a slap, and then, worse, she kept going, quiet and precise: "But you're here now. With the letters. On the record, in front of Ruth, before we run the next tide. So either you grew a spine in the last forty-eight hours or you're scared of something bigger than me."

"Both," Junie said. "Both, obviously. Have you met me?"

And that — God help them both — that got the ghost of a laugh out of Ada, one sharp exhale through the nose, gone as fast as it came. She sat back down. She squared the letters. She looked at Junie across the table with an expression Junie recognized from childhood, from the summer their fathers' boat burned and the two girls sat on a cold dock dividing up what was left: grief and grit in equal parts, jaw set against crying.

"Here's what happens now," Ada said. "These letters stay in this office tonight. Tomorrow they go in Ruth's safe. Nobody else sees them — not Teo, not anyone — until I decide what they mean. And June?" She held up one finger. "If you ever sit on something like this again, anything, for even a day, I will put you on a plane myself and you won't come back. Are we clear?"

"We're clear."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it. That's the only reason it works."

Ada gathered the letters, and her hand paused on the last one — the handwritten one — and for a second Junie thought she might say something soft, something sisterly, something that would stitch the eleven-year gap closed. Instead Ada folded the letters into her jacket, inner pocket, over her heart, next to where the compass hung on its chain, and said:

"There's a tide tomorrow at 0440. Teo thinks he can reach the strongroom. If Dad moved that ship on purpose — if he *hid* it — then whatever's down there isn't just treasure. It's evidence. Maybe it's the whole story." She stood, shouldered her bag, and paused at the door with the rain roaring beyond it. "One more thing. The man in the good coat. Did he give you a name?"

"He didn't have to," Junie said. "He had a card. Black card, silver bird on it. Corvus Salvage and Recovery."

Ada nodded once, like a woman confirming a diagnosis she'd already dreaded, and stepped out into the rain — and Junie sat alone at the chart table with two cold cups of coffee and the terrible, weightless feeling of a truth finally delivered, eleven years late, to exactly the wrong moment.

Outside, far out past the point, the sea was already beginning to pull back from the land, draining toward the cave, toward the wreck, toward whatever their father had buried at the bottom of his silence.

The sixth tide was coming. And now, so was Crane.
