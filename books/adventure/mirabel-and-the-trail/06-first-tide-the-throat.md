## 6. First Tide: The Throat

The tide table said we had ninety minutes.

I'd checked it four times the night before, then a fifth time at three in the morning when I gave up on sleep and sat in the skiff at Ruth's dock with my father's compass open in my palm, watching the needle hold its stubborn bearing toward the bay like it was pointing at something only it could see. The tide tables didn't care what I wanted. They said slack water at the cave mouth would come at 0640 and last — if the swell stayed down — maybe forty minutes before the throat started breathing again. After that, the next window belonged to tomorrow's tide, and tomorrow's tide would be six inches higher, and the one after that higher still, until the season closed its door for another decade.

So: 0640. No arguing with it.

We loaded in the dark. Ruth brought coffee in a dented thermos and a boat hook she'd owned longer than I'd been alive. Teo Marsh arrived smelling faintly of diesel and toothpaste, his dive gear bagged and slung over one shoulder like he carried it to work every day, which, in a way, he did. Junie came last, hood up against the drizzle, saying nothing, taking the stern seat as far from me as the skiff allowed. Four days since the letters. She hadn't mentioned them again and neither had I, which meant we were both thinking about nothing else.

"Fuel's topped," Ruth said, handing me the line. "Pumps primed. You want to take her out or you want me to?"

"I'll take her."

"You always do." But there was no edge in it. Ruth Okafor had run this harbor through two storms that had names and one that didn't, and she'd learned somewhere along the way that some hands need to be on their own tiller. She settled into the bow with her coffee and her boat hook and became, instantly, cargo.

Teo crouched by the console beside me, unfolding a chart he'd marked up in grease pencil. "Channel's changed since your dad's day," he said. "Sandbar's migrated east maybe forty meters. If the charts in that compass box are old—"

"They're old."

"Then don't trust them. Trust the water. The surge channel runs along the north wall of the cave mouth — you'll see it as a seam where the swells stand up taller than everywhere else. We go in south of the seam, wide, then cut hard left once we're past the lip. If we get caught in the seam—" He made a small, apologetic gesture, like a man describing weather.

"How bad?"

"The channel narrows to about the width of this skiff and funnels everything the ocean's feeling straight into the rock. It's not deep. It's just fast. Boat goes sideways in there, the water decides what happens next, and the water has no imagination. It does the same thing every time."

"Which is?"

"Puts you on the rocks."

Junie spoke from the stern without lifting her chin off her folded arms. "Ada's flown worse than a channel."

"I have," I said, "but I've never been underwater while doing it."

"Same physics," Teo said. "Worse consequences. Water doesn't forgive a stall."

I pushed us off the dock at first gray light, the harbor still blue-dark and smelling of kelp and cold iron, and swung the skiff's nose out past the breakwater into a sea that couldn't decide whether it was calm or thinking about it. The forecast said the swell would build by afternoon. The forecast also said a lot of things. Out here you took the water as it came and kept your opinions to yourself.

* * *

The bay opened up around the point like a held breath let go, and there it was.

I'd seen the cave twice from the air — once the morning after the lowest tide, banking my Cub low enough to make gulls complain, and once more yesterday on a supply run I'd stretched twenty minutes past honest. From above, the cave mouth was a dark comma in the cliff, unremarkable unless you knew to look. From the water, at skiff level, with the tide drained out of it like a sink losing its bathwater, it was something else entirely. It was a doorway. Forty feet of black arch rising out of a shelf of wet stone, dripping, breathing out a smell of ancient salt and cold rock, big enough that the ocean sloshed around inside it with a sound like a giant turning over in bed.

Nobody spoke. Even Junie sat up.

The compass in my jacket pocket felt warm against my chest, which was impossible, and I knew it was impossible, and I felt it anyway.

"There," Teo said quietly, and pointed.

The seam. North side of the entrance, exactly where he'd said: a line in the water where the swells stood up a foot taller than their neighbors and folded over themselves in long, silent curls, like the sea was showing us its teeth without bothering to growl. South of it, the water was choppy but honest. You could read it. You could work with it.

"South of the seam," I said. "Wide approach, cut left past the lip."

"And whatever happens," Ruth said, turning around in the bow, "nobody stops the boat in the throat. You keep driving until you're through. Stopping is how people die in places like this."

"Copy," I said, and put the throttle down.

* * *

For about four minutes, it went perfectly.

The skiff planed across the bay's shoulder, spray coming off the bow in sheets, the cave mouth growing from an arch into a country. I throttled back at two hundred meters to bring the hull down off its plane — you don't enter a cave fast, Teo had drilled into us, because fast means no options — and we slid in toward the entrance on the last of our momentum, engine idling, the four of us suddenly very aware of how loud even an idling outboard sounds when the walls are close.

The lip of the entrance passed overhead. I mean that literally — the arch was so tall that looking up, I saw barnacled rock and a fringe of dried kelp hanging thirty feet above my head, marking the high-tide line, marking how much room the sea usually took for itself here and how little it was leaving us today. The water inside was darker than the water outside, green-black, and cold rolled off it like an open freezer door.

And then the throat.

The passage narrowed the way Teo's chart promised, walls closing in on both sides until I could have touched them with the boat hook, the ceiling dropping, the channel bending left around a shoulder of rock furred with mussels. The current ran against us — outgoing tide still draining the cave — and the skiff slowed like it was wading through honey. I fed it throttle. The engine answered. The walls slid past. Ruth stood in the bow with her boat hook ready, fending us off the rock by inches, calling depths in a voice flat as a depth sounder: "Three feet. Two and a half. Two — bear right, bear right—"

I bore right.

That was the mistake. Or maybe it wasn't a mistake; maybe it was the water changing its mind. Because the moment I corrected, the whole character of the channel shifted under us — a long, low swell from outside finally reaching into the cave, stacking itself up in the constriction the way Teo had warned it would, and the seam we'd avoided at the entrance found us anyway, deeper in, where nobody had thought to look for it.

The water stood up.

Not gradually. It stood up the way a fist stands up. One second the skiff floated in two feet of churning green; the next, a wall of water lifted the stern and threw it sideways toward the north wall, and the channel — the real channel, the funnel — took hold of us.

I've been flying long enough to know what a wind shear feels like: the sky suddenly deciding your aircraft belongs somewhere else. This was that, in water. The skiff went broadside. The wall came at us. Ruth slammed her boat hook against rock and shoved, all hundred and sixty pounds of her leaning into it, buying us maybe a foot. Teo had the spare paddle out and was doing the same on the other side, useless and necessary at once. And through the middle of it all ran the sound — a deep, hydraulic roar from behind us, from the throat of the throat, where the funnel narrowed to its final width and the ocean poured through it like water through a fire hose.

"We're going to get sucked back through," Teo shouted. "If we go in backwards—"

"Then we don't go in backwards." I jammed the throttle forward and swung the wheel hard over, turning the skiff's nose back into the current, into the roar, choosing the fight I could steer in over the one I couldn't. The bow rose. The engine screamed. For one full, endless second we hung there pointed upstream at a wall of white water, making no headway at all, a moth pinned against a fan —

— and then the swell passed. Just like that, the way swells do, the mountain of water moved on into the cave's interior and the channel dropped back to angry-but-navigable, and the skiff lunged forward ten feet, twenty, and the walls fell away and the ceiling lifted and we shot out of the throat into the body of the cave like a cork out of a bottle.

Silence, after. That enormous, ringing silence of underground water, broken only by drips and the idle of my abused outboard and Ruth, standing in the bow, slowly putting her boat hook down.

"Well," she said.

"Everybody breathe," I said. My own voice came out about an octave higher than intended. My hands on the wheel were shaking, and I watched them shake with a kind of clinical interest, the way you watch someone else's hands.

Junie was gripping the gunwale with both hands, knuckles white, hair plastered to her face. She looked at me across the length of the skiff, and for the first time since the letters, since the harbor, since any of it, she laughed — one short, cracked, involuntary bark of a laugh.

"Dad would've loved that," she said.

And God help me, I laughed too.

* * *

The cave opened into a chamber bigger than the church hall back in town, bigger than my hangar, bigger than anything a person expects to find hollowed out of a coastline. Our running lights carved a cone through the dark and the darkness ate most of it, but the beam found walls slicked with flowstone, ledges stacked with old bird nests, and — when I swept the light low — water. A lagoon of it filling the chamber's floor, black and utterly still except for the slow lift and fall of the tide breathing through the throat behind us.

"Depth?" Ruth asked.

Teo dropped a weighted line over the side and paid it out. "Fathom. Fathom and a half here. Deeper toward the back — feel that current? There's an opening on the far side. Water's moving through this whole system."

"An outlet?"

"Or another entrance. Caves like this usually have more than one mouth. Tide comes in one, goes out the other." He frowned at the black water. "Which means somewhere in between, there's a connection to open sea. Which means—"

He stopped. We all heard it at the same moment, or rather felt it — a change in the echo, a hollowness. I swung the light across the far end of the chamber, past the lagoon, to where the cave wall should have been smooth stone.

It wasn't smooth. It was wood.

A mast, raked at an angle out of the water like the arm of a drowning man, thick as a silo, crusted with a century of barnacle and weed, its top swallowed by shadow near the vaulted ceiling. As the light traveled down it, the mast disappeared beneath the surface of the lagoon and kept going, a pale vertical blur fading into black, attached — presumably — to something below.

"That's not driftwood," Ruth said unnecessarily.

"That's a ship's mast." Teo was already moving, pulling his dive bag open with hands that had stopped shaking the moment there was work to do. "Lower mast, probably — mainmast of something in the hundred-and-fifty-foot class. It's stepped into a hull. It's standing in a hull, which means the hull is down there, upright, on the bottom of this lagoon, and—"

"Teo." Ruth's voice. Gentle, but with a hand on it.

He stopped. Breathed. Looked at me.

"It's her," he said. "It's the Meridian. Or part of her."

I brought the skiff closer, slow, the outboard barely ticking over, and held position ten feet off the mast. Up close it was even more impossible — iron-hooped, tar-black under the crust, and scarred. Not storm-scarred. Tool-scarred. Someone had worked this spar with adzes and axes, and near the waterline, where the barnacles thinned, the light caught something carved into the wood, old letters filled with a century of green:

MERIDIAN

My father's compass sat against my chest, and I swear the needle was trembling.

Except.

Except something was wrong, and it took my brain a moment to climb over the wonder and see it. I'd grown up on salvage stories the way other kids grew up on bedtime ones. I knew what a wreck looked like — or I knew what wreck stories looked like, and they all agreed on one thing: ships die messy. They hit rocks, they burn, they roll over, they break their backs on a bar and scatter themselves along a mile of coastline. Nobody sinks a ship neatly. Nobody steps a mast, squares the yards, and lowers a vessel gently, upright, into the bottom of a sealed sea cave a mile from open water.

"This isn't where she sank," I heard myself say.

Teo looked up from his dive bag. "What?"

"She was moved." I swept the light along the mast, up to the shadows, around the chamber — the smooth walls, the single throat of an entrance that even at the lowest tide in a decade could barely pass a skiff. "Look at this place. Nothing over thirty feet could get in here, ever. The Meridian was a hundred and fifty feet if she was an inch. She didn't sail in. She didn't wash in. Somebody brought her here in pieces, or—" I stopped, because the other possibility was worse and it arrived fully formed, the way the worst ones do. "Or somebody scuttled her somewhere else and towed her in and sank her again. Deliberately. Upright. Like parking a car in a garage."

The silence had a different texture now. Before, it had been awe. Now it was the silence of four people realizing they might be standing inside someone's intention.

Ruth said, quietly, "Your father's initials were carved at the entrance."

"I know."

"And his compass points here."

"I know, Ruth."

"I'm not accusing anyone of anything. I'm saying this cave has fingerprints on it, and we should ask ourselves whose."

Junie hadn't spoken since her laugh. Now she stood up in the stern, one hand on the mast — actually touching it, her fingers resting on the carved name like she was taking a pulse. "There's a reason he never told anyone," she said. "Whatever he did, however she got here — there's a reason it stayed secret for thirty years. And we're the first people to walk into the reason."

"Are we?" Teo asked.

The question hung there. I turned the light back toward the throat we'd come through, back toward the channel, and let myself think about the approach with a pilot's cold eye instead of a treasure hunter's hot one. The sandbar Teo had flagged — migrated forty meters east, he'd said, since my father's day. The seam that had nearly killed us sitting exactly where an incoming stranger wouldn't expect it. The throat passable, today, at precisely this tide, in precisely this window — a window that existed maybe six times before the season shut.

Six tides. Six chances. Enough time for a crew to get in, if they were good. Enough time for word to travel, if it traveled.

Enough time, if someone wanted it to, for exactly this.

"Get your gear on," I told Teo. "One dive. Reconnaissance only — look, don't touch, and watch your bottom time. I want to know what's under that mast before the tide turns." Then I raised the light and played it slowly along the chamber walls, high up, where the flowstone gave way to dry rock, and said the thing I'd been circling since the throat spat us through:

"Because either we're the luckiest people on this coast — or somebody cleared the path for us, and I want to know who's standing at the end of it."

Teo clicked his regulator into place, swung his legs over the side, and tipped backward into the black water without a sound.

The ripples spread out toward the mast, touched the carved name, and flattened. Somewhere below us, in the dark, the Meridian waited — and the tide clock in my head started counting down the minutes until the throat began to breathe again, and this door, like every door my father ever left behind, started swinging shut.
