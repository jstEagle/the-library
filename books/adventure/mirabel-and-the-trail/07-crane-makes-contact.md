## 7. Crane Makes Contact

Silas Crane had learned long ago that the most valuable information in the salvage trade never came from charts or sonar. It came from waiting. From standing in the right place with a cup of bad coffee and letting other people's habits do the work.

The fuel dock at Kestrel Point was the right place. Every bush pilot on this coast came through eventually — there were exactly two pumps between here and the interior inlets, and Ada Voss's De Havilland burned through avgas like a rumor burns through a small town. He'd been waiting three days. He drank his coffee and watched the water and let his crew run their own quiet errands: a man photographing tide tables pinned to the harbormaster's door, a woman buying drinks for dockhands who talked too much when they were paid in beer, a boy of nineteen with soft hands copying down tail numbers.

By Thursday morning he knew three things worth knowing.

First: Voss had flown into the interior four times since the anomalous tide, each time carrying less cargo than her manifest claimed, which meant she was hauling something weightless and precious — hope, usually, or secrecy.

Second: Ruth Okafor had signed out her late husband's skiff, the *Pelican*, twice after dark, and harbor masters did not take pleasure cruises at midnight.

Third: someone aboard that skiff had been diving. Teo Marsh had bought oxygen and a fresh O-ring kit with cash, and Teo Marsh never used cash unless he didn't want the purchase traced, and Teo Marsh only had one reason to dive untraced in a season like this one.

Crane turned his cup slowly on the rail and watched the fog burn off the channel. Somewhere out past the point, under forty feet of green water, the Meridian was giving up its secrets one tide at a time. His grandfather had spent thirty years and most of the family fortune looking for that ship. Crane intended to finish the job in six tides, and he preferred to do it without firing a shot, because shots attracted coroners, and coroners attracted journalists, and journalists asked questions about 1931 that Crane had spent his whole career keeping buried.

He heard the plane before he saw it — the familiar stutter of a radial engine clearing its throat over the water, then the long glide, then the hull kissing the channel with a sound like a hand slapping a table. The Beaver taxied toward the fuel dock with the unhurried confidence of a machine whose pilot trusted it completely.

Ada Voss cut the engine and swung out onto the float to tie off, and Crane took a moment to study her the way he'd study a chart before committing to a passage. Mid-thirties. Lean in the way of people who worked outdoors and ate irregularly. She moved around her aircraft the way some people moved around animals they loved — automatic, affectionate, half-asleep. Her father's daughter in every way that mattered, including the compass hanging on a lanyard against her sternum. He could see it glinting brass through her open jacket.

Elias Voss's compass. Crane had photographs of it. He had a photograph of Elias wearing it, taken at the Meridian's commissioning dinner in 1929, back when Elias was still a hero and not yet a cautionary tale. He had a photograph of the same compass sitting on a bar in Port Anselm three weeks after the sinking, while its owner drank himself blind and everyone in the room pretended not to know why.

He knew more about that compass than Ada Voss did. He suspected he knew more about her father than she did, too. That was the advantage of being the villain of a story nobody had finished telling yet: you got to read ahead.

"Miss Voss." He pitched his voice to carry across the water, friendly, unhurried. "Mind if I buy your fuel?"

She turned, and he watched her place him — a flicker, a half-second where recognition assembled itself behind her eyes. She'd seen his picture in the trade papers. *Corvus Marine Salvage announces Pacific expansion.* He'd made sure those announcements ran everywhere; a reputation was cheaper than a gun.

"You're a long way from your fleet," she said.

"My fleet is exactly where I left it." He smiled. "I'm a long way from my office, which is different. May I?"

She hesitated, then gestured at the dock ladder with the grudging courtesy of someone who understood that refusing a man on a public dock created more talk than admitting him. He climbed up, offered his hand. She took it. Her grip was dry and strong and brief.

"Silas Crane."

"I know who you are."

"Most people do by now. It saves introductions." He nodded at the pump attendant, a kid named Denny who was already scurrying to hook up the hose, and slipped the boy a folded bill. "Fill her up. On Corvus."

"That's not necessary."

"It's polite. There's a difference, but I find people confuse them constantly." He leaned against the rail beside her, leaving a careful arm's length of space. Intimidation was a matter of geometry as much as tone; get too close and men got stupid and brave, get too far and they relaxed. Arm's length said *I could touch you* without ever saying it. "Rough crossing this morning? Fog was thick past the islands."

"I've flown worse."

"I'm sure you have. Your father used to say fog was just weather with an opinion."

That landed. He watched it land — a small tightening around her jaw, the way her weight shifted back onto her heels. Good. He hadn't come to threaten her. Threats were for later, if later was needed, and threats were also crude. What he'd come to do was show her the edges of what he knew, the way you showed a dog the fence line. Dogs learned faster than people.

"You knew my father," she said carefully.

"I knew *of* him. Everyone in this business did. Elias Voss was the best pilot on the coast and then he was the worst kind of legend, and both of those things happened because of one ship." Crane looked out at the channel, letting the silence do some work. "The Meridian."

"Funny thing about that ship," Ada said. "Half the coast thinks it's a myth."

"The coast is wrong about a great many things. It thinks the tide comes in evenly. It thinks harbormasters are honest. It thinks salvage is about treasure." He turned back to her. "Salvage is about paperwork, Miss Voss. Whoever holds the documents owns the wreck. Gold is just how the paperwork proves it was right."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, controlled, gone. But he'd seen it. She had documents. Or she had a diver down somewhere finding them. His information was holding together nicely.

"Is there something you want, Captain Crane? Because I've got a medicine run at noon and a fuel bill I didn't ask for."

"What I want is a conversation. A short one." He reached inside his coat — slowly, deliberately, letting her watch his hand the whole way — and produced not a weapon but a checkbook. Leather-bound, heavy stock, the kind of object that did its own talking. "Corvus would like to acquire your father's compass."

The pause before she answered lasted maybe two seconds, and in those two seconds Crane learned everything he needed about how this was going to go.

"No."

"You haven't heard the number."

"It doesn't matter what the number is."

"It might. My accountants would faint if they heard me say this, but I'll say it anyway: name a figure. Any figure. If it's within reason, we have a deal before your fuel is even pumped." He clicked the pen. "It's a family piece, Miss Voss. It belongs with the rest of his mistakes."

There it was. He watched the sentence go into her like a fishhook going into deep water — no splash, no fight, just a slow terrible understanding of what had just been set beneath her skin. *His mistakes.* Plural. Said by a stranger, on a public dock, in front of Denny the fuel kid, who was suddenly very interested in the hose fittings.

"My father made one mistake," Ada said quietly, "and it wasn't the one you think it was."

"And what mistake do you think I think it was?" Crane kept his voice light, almost bored. This was the part he enjoyed, though he'd have shot anyone who accused him of enjoying it. "Go on. Say it out loud. See how it sounds."

"He sank the Meridian. That's the story. Bad weather, bad judgment, a shortcut through the Narrows with a full hold, and eleven men went into the water." She recited it flatly, the way people recite things they've had carved into them young. "Except the Meridian didn't sink in the Narrows. The wreck board proved the hull came apart miles south of where he claimed, and no one's ever explained that, and he never tried to."

"No," Crane agreed. "He never did. Which has always struck me as the interesting part." He closed the checkbook and put it away, unhurried. "You know what I've found, in thirty years of pulling ships off the bottom? Wrecks don't lie, but survivors do. And dead men lie best of all, because they can't correct the record." He glanced at the compass at her throat. "That needle has been pointing at the truth since 1931. Your father wore it every day until the day he died, and I'd wager my fleet you never once asked yourself why a man keeps a thing that ruined him."

"Maybe he liked the reminder."

"Nobody likes the reminder." Crane smiled again, softer this time, and that was the worst thing he'd done all morning. "They keep the reminder because they're waiting for someone to ask the right question. And then they die waiting, and the question gets handed down like the compass does, and here we are."

Denny called out that the tanks were full. The moment broke like ice underfoot. Ada stepped back toward her plane, putting the hull between them, and Crane noted that too — she was already thinking in terms of cover and distance. Good instincts. Her father's instincts.

"Whatever you think you're doing out here," she said, "whatever you think my father did — the answer isn't in a compass. And it isn't for sale."

"Everything's for sale. That's not cynicism, it's arithmetic." He descended the ladder to the dock and stood below her, looking up, which he hated, so he made it look casual by checking his watch. "One more thing, and then I'll let you make your medicine run. Six tides. That's what the tables give you before the cave closes for the season. I know because my people have the same tables you do, and mine have annotations." He looked up at her then, and let the smile fall all the way off his face, and spoke plainly for the first time since he'd boarded. "I'm not asking you to stop, Miss Voss. Asking is for people with time. I'm telling you how it ends. You can bring me what's down there and be rich, or you can bring me nothing and be a footnote, but either way the Meridian comes up under my flag, because my family paid for that ship once already — in money, in blood, and in things I will happily explain to a courtroom if it comes to that. Your father knew that. Ask yourself why he never told you."

He tipped an imaginary hat. "Safe flying. Watch the fog past the islands — it's thicker than it looks, and it hides more than weather."

He walked down the dock without looking back, because looking back was for men who weren't sure, and Silas Crane had never once in his life been unsure. Behind him he heard the Beaver's engine catch, cough, catch again — her hands weren't quite steady, and he catalogued that with satisfaction — then settle into its working drone as she taxied out.

At the end of the dock, his driver held the car door. Crane paused, took out a notebook, and wrote three lines in pencil:

*Voss: hostile, informed, protecting something. Documents likely already recovered — confirm via Marsh.*
*Compass refusal = sentimental leverage available.*
*Tide three. Move the fleet to the outer anchorage tonight.*

He underlined the last line twice.

Then he looked out at the channel, where the Beaver was climbing through the fog shelf into hard blue sunlight, getting smaller, getting away — for now. Somewhere under that green water lay a strongroom with his family's name written all over its contents, and a dead man's signature on papers that could hang the whole Corvus fortune from a gallows of its own making.

Six tides, he'd told her. He'd lied. He gave her five.

* * *

That evening, in the charthouse of the *Corvus Star*, Crane spread the old survey charts across the table and weighted their corners with brass instruments his grandfather had carried. His first mate, Halloran, stood waiting with the fleet's morning orders.

"She won't sell," Crane said. "Which means she'll dig. Which means by tide three she'll have the strongroom open, and by tide four she'll understand what's actually in it."

"And if she goes public with it?"

Crane considered the chart, the little penciled cross marking a bay that appeared on no modern survey, the bay his grandfather had died believing in. "Then we stop being buyers," he said, "and start being creditors. Pull the file on the harbor charter. Every debt note Okafor's town owes, every holder behind them. I want the whole chain on my desk by Friday."

Halloran hesitated. "That's a lot of people's livelihoods, sir."

"Yes," Crane said pleasantly. "That's rather the point. Nobody fights for gold, Halloran. They fight for home. So we make sure that by the sixth tide, home is what they stand to lose."

Outside, the fog was coming back in off the islands, thickening around the anchored fleet, swallowing the lights of Kestrel Point one by one. Crane rolled up the charts and thought about a brass compass riding against a stubborn woman's heart, pointing at everything he'd spent his life burying.

Five tides, he corrected himself. Four, if she was smart.

He hoped she wasn't.
