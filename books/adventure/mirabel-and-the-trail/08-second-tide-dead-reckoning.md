## 8. Second Tide: Dead Reckoning

Teo Marsh had a rule about diving that he'd never once kept: don't go past the point where the light stops being your friend. It was a rule his first instructor had carved into him back when he was nineteen and broke and hungry for anything that paid, and it was a rule that had kept him alive through two decades of salvage work in water that most people only ever saw from the deck of a boat.

He broke it on the second tide.

Not all at once. That was the thing about breaking rules like that one — you didn't decide to break them. You just kept deciding to go a little farther, a little deeper, a little longer, each decision small enough to live with, until you looked up from the bottom of the arithmetic and realized you were forty meters down in a flooded sea cave with no direct line to open water and a dive computer that had started talking to him in a language he didn't like.

"Teo, talk to me." Ada's voice came through the hardline, tinny and close, like she was inside his hood. "You've been down eleven minutes."

The line went taut as he fed it out. Above him — far above, a pale coin of gray-green light — the skiff sat at anchor inside the cave's throat, riding the slack between tides. The Meridian's mast rose out of the dark below him like something planted, furred with anemones, tilted maybe fifteen degrees off true. It had been moved. Everyone knew that now. The question was where the hull had gone, and Teo was pretty sure he was looking at the answer.

Beyond the mast, the cave floor shelved downward into a trench the charts had never bothered to name. The sonar Ruth had borrowed from a crab boat showed a depression running back under the headland, maybe sixty meters long, with a return at the bottom that could have been rock or could have been something with straight lines. Straight lines were the whole game. The ocean didn't make straight lines. People did.

"I'm on the mast," he said. "Going over the edge."

A pause. He could hear her breathing, and behind her breathing, faintly, Junie's voice saying something he couldn't make out, and behind that the slap of water against the skiff's hull. His crew. His problem. His debts.

"Bottom time," Ada said. Not a question. A reminder.

"I know my numbers."

"That's what worries me."

He smiled inside his mask, because there was nobody down here to see it, and went over the edge.

* * *

The trench swallowed the light fast. At ten meters down its throat, the gray-green coin above him shrank to a rumor. He ran his light along the walls — basalt, slick, ribbed like the inside of a throat, which was exactly what it was. The cave breathed twice a day, and today it was exhaling, and he was swimming down its windpipe against the current of everything sensible.

His computer ticked. He made himself look at it. Depth thirty-eight meters, and climbing by the meter as he descended. No-deco limit at this depth: nine minutes if he pushed it, and he'd already spent four getting here. The math was simple and ugly. He had maybe five minutes at the bottom before every minute became a debt he'd pay off in a chamber, in nitrogen bubbles, in pain.

Five minutes. He'd done worse for less money.

The return on the sonar resolved out of the dark at forty-one meters, and it wasn't rock.

It was a hull. Or what was left of one — a ship's side lying on its side, copper sheathing gone green-black, timbers sprung but still holding their shape, as if the Meridian had lain down here to sleep and hadn't fully decided to come apart yet. Teo swept his light along her length and felt the old hunger wake up in his chest, the one that had gotten him into debt in the first place, the one that whispered *just a little farther, just a little longer.* A wreck this intact, this deep, this hidden — Crane would burn the town down for it. Crane probably intended to.

And then he saw the strongroom door.

It was set into the hull below what would have been the main cabin, a square of iron in the timber, riveted, with a wheel-lock like a bank vault's smaller cousin. Someone had sealed it deliberately — not with panic, not in a storm, but with care. The rivets were even. The wheel was greased, or had been; the grease was long since sea-slime, but the mechanism turned when he put his hands on it, stiff, grudging, alive after however many years.

He checked his computer. Three minutes left, and he needed two of them to get up the trench.

"Ada." His voice came out flatter than he wanted. "I found it. Hull's in the trench under the headland. There's a sealed compartment — iron door, wheel-lock. Strongroom, or something built like one."

Silence on the line. Then Ada, very controlled: "Can you open it?"

"Not today. Today I can tell you it's there and get out alive." He got his fingers under the wheel anyway, just to feel it, just to know. "There's something else."

"What?"

"There's paper." Wedged in the jamb of the door, half-dissolved, a wad of something fibrous that had been jammed there on purpose — packing, sealing, someone making sure the door shut tight against salt water. And caught under the lowest hinge plate, protected from the worst of the current, a single sheet, laminated in its own rot, edges gone soft as wet bread.

His computer ticked again. Two minutes. One minute of bottom time, and then the bill came due whether he liked it or not.

He took the page.

* * *

The ascent was supposed to be the easy part. He'd done a thousand ascents. You go up slower than you want to, you do your stops, you let the sea have its tax. He'd planned the stops on the slate strapped to his forearm: three meters a minute to fifteen, hang at fifteen for three, hang at nine for six, hang at six for twelve. Textbook. He'd written it out himself that morning while Junie watched him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't want to.

But the trench exhaled while he was in it.

He felt it before he understood it — a pressure shift, a soft concussion through the water, like the cave clearing its throat somewhere far above. Then the silt came down. Not up from the bottom, which he'd have expected, but down from the ceiling, a brown snowfall that erased his light beam to arm's length and erased the coin of daylight entirely. The line went slack in his hand. Slack line meant the skiff had moved, or the anchor had dragged, or —

"Ada." Nothing. "Ada, I'm blind. Silt-out. Talk to me."

Static. Then her voice, chopped, coming apart: "—current — anchor held — Teo, give me your heading—"

He gave it. She talked him up the trench by dead reckoning — compass bearing, depth, time, the oldest navigation there is, the kind you do with no eyes and no help and nothing but numbers and nerve. Dead reckoning. He thought about the name of it, how honest it was. You reckoned, and if your reckoning was dead wrong, so were you.

He came up the last five meters with his computer screaming and his vision narrowing to a tunnel, and broke the surface into the skiff's shadow with Ada's hand fisted in his BC harness and Ruth hauling on the line, and the first thing he said, before he even got his regulator out, was:

"Got it. Got the page."

Junie pulled his mask off. Her face was white and furious and scared, all three at once, and she said, "You idiot," in a voice that cracked in the middle, and he thought, distantly, that he should probably be worried about how much that mattered to him.

Then his right shoulder started to itch.

* * *

Decompression sickness doesn't hurt, at first. That's the trap of it. It starts as an itch, a deep-tissue prickle, like a limb waking up wrong. Teo knew the signs the way a drunk knows the shape of a bottle. He'd put a deckhand in a chamber outside Ketchikan eight years ago who'd ignored the itch for six hours and come out of the chamber with a limp he'd keep for life.

"Itch in my right shoulder," he told Ruth, flat, professional, while she cinched the oxygen mask they kept in the skiff's ditch kit over his face. "Started two minutes ago. Could be a skin bend, could be worse."

"Could be you're a damn fool," Ruth said. Her voice was steady but her hands weren't quite. "Nine-minute limit. You were down twenty-two."

"The silt cost me the stops."

"The silt didn't cost you anything. Greed cost you." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "Oxygen until we get you to the chamber in Port Anselm. Ada — get us moving."

Ada was already at the outboard, and the skiff swung toward the cave mouth, and Teo lay back against the gunwale with the mask hissing against his face and the logbook page sealed in a ziplock bag that Junie had pressed into his hand the moment he surfaced, because apparently that was the kind of person Junie was — furious at him and protecting the evidence in the same breath.

Through the mask's plastic window, the sky over the inlet was doing something ugly. Clouds stacking up over the headland in gray plates. The tail end of the season's weather, arriving early, or the beginning of something worse.

He flexed his right hand. The fingers answered, mostly. The itch spread down toward his elbow like slow lightning.

"How bad?" Ada asked. She'd dropped down beside him, one hand on the gunwale, the plane of her face sharp with wind and worry she was trying not to show.

"Ask me in an hour." He worked the bag out from under his thigh and held it up between them. "But read this first. Whatever happens to me, read this first."

She took it. Turned it to the light. And Teo, watching her face through the fog of the oxygen mask, saw her expression change the way a shoreline changes when the tide goes out — slowly, and then all at once, and nothing you can do about either.

The page was a fragment of a ship's log, water-stained, the ink faded to the color of dried blood but legible where it had been pressed against the iron of the door. A date. A position. And at the bottom, in a hand Teo recognized from the framed chart that hung in Ada's office — the same slanted capitals, the same impatient crossbar on the T — a signature.

E. VOSS, MASTER.

Her father. The man whose name had been mud in every harbor from here to the strait for fifteen years. The man who, according to everyone who told the story, had drunk the Meridian onto the rocks through negligence and cowardice and gotten away with his own life while the cargo and three of his crew went into the sea.

Except the entry wasn't about rocks. Teo had read it three times during the run out of the cave, holding the bag up against the gray light, and the words were short and terrible and clear:

*Cargo secured below per instructions. Door sealed on my order. No one is to know what she carries. If asked, we lost her in the passage. Let them think what they will of me.*

Let them think what they will of me.

Fifteen years of harborside contempt, of Ada growing up with her father's name as a cautionary tale, of creditors calling and doors closing — and the man had signed his own ruin on purpose, in ink, and sealed it in an iron room at the bottom of a hole in the world.

"Ada," Teo said, and the mask made his voice sound like someone else's. "That's not a confession. That's a man burying himself alive to keep a secret."

She didn't answer. She was reading it again, lips moving, and her hand had gone to the compass hanging at her neck, gripping it like a rail.

The itch reached his elbow. The tunnel of his vision narrowed another degree. Somewhere behind them, back in the drowned dark of the trench, the strongroom waited with its wheel-lock and its secrets, and somewhere ahead of them, Port Anselm's chamber waited with its steel tube and its questions, and between the two, Silas Crane's fleet was somewhere on the water, and Teo Marsh — lying in the bottom of a borrowed skiff, paying in flesh for five minutes of ambition — understood suddenly and completely that the page in Ada's hand was worth more than all the gold in the strongroom, and that Crane would kill every one of them to keep it from seeing daylight.

"Port Anselm's an hour," Ruth called from the helm. "Weather's turning. We'll make it or we won't."

"We'll make it," Ada said. She folded the page into her jacket, over her heart, next to the compass. Her eyes when she looked down at Teo were lit with something he'd never seen in them before — not grief, exactly, and not hope, but the place where the two meet and catch fire.

"Hold on, Teo," she said. "You're not allowed to die before you hear what my father actually did."

The first drop of rain hit the skiff's deck like a warning shot.
