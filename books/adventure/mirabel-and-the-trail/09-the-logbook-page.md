## 9. The Logbook Page

The hospital in Kasaan smelled like antiseptic and old coffee, and Ada Voss hated every minute of the four hours she spent in its waiting room, watching the clock and thinking about a piece of paper sealed inside a plastic bag on her lap.

Teo was stable. The dive doc had flown him out on the medevac float, recompressed him in the hyperbaric chamber until his joints stopped screaming, and pronounced him lucky. *Lucky* meant he'd walk with a limp for a month and dive again by the fourth tide if nobody stopped him. Nobody would stop him. That was the problem with Teo Marsh — he treated his body like a boat he'd already decided to sink.

Ada sat under fluorescent light that made everyone look drowned, turning the plastic bag over in her hands.

One page. Torn from a logbook, water-stained at the edges, the ink faded to the color of weak tea but legible where it mattered. She'd read it eleven times since Ruth had driven her up from the dock. She could recite it now, and still it refused to mean what she wanted it to mean.

*Meridian, 14 June. Cargo manifest amended per instruction. We do not carry what they say we carry. I have signed for what I will not deliver.*

Her father's signature beneath it. Elias Voss, master's hand, steady as a keel.

She had grown up on that handwriting. It was on the flyleaf of every book in his cabin, on the grocery lists he wrote in beautiful cursive because he said a man who couldn't make a shopping list beautiful had no business making anything else. It was on the letter he'd left her, the one she kept in the same box as the compass, the one that said *I'm sorry about the way it ended, kiddo* without ever saying what *it* was.

For twenty years she had built her life on a single sentence: *My father lost the Meridian through negligence.* Everyone in the coastal interior knew the story. Elias Voss, the young captain who ran a salvage steamer onto the rocks of the Sounding in a fog he should never have entered, drowning three men and a fortune, spending the rest of his short life as a cautionary tale told over harbor bars. He drank himself dead within five years. The compass came to Ada along with the shame, and she wore both.

But negligence didn't write *per instruction*. Negligence didn't sign for cargo it refused to deliver. Negligence didn't amend a manifest in a steady hand at two in the morning while the fog rolled in off the Sounding like something summoned.

Deliberate. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed hook.

"Voss." A nurse stood in the doorway. "You can see him now. Five minutes."

Teo lay propped in the narrow bed looking like a man who'd been run through a wringer, gray-faced, one wrist taped where the IV had been. His eyes found hers and there was something new in them — not the hungry shine she'd seen at the dock when he talked about salvage percentages, but something rawer. Fear, maybe. Or the beginning of shame.

"You're supposed to be resting," Ada said.

"I'm supposed to be rich." His voice came out scraped. "Did you read it?"

She held up the bag.

"Don't open it here," Teo said quickly. "I mean—" He exhaled, winced at whatever the motion cost his ribs. "I read enough of it down there to know it's not just your father's page. There are more pages in that strongroom, Voss. A whole ledger. And some of them aren't signed by him."

"What does that mean?"

"It means the Meridian wasn't just carrying gold." He stared at the ceiling. "It was carrying orders. And somebody gave them."

* * *

Ruth Okafor drove her back down the coast road in the harbormaster's truck, windows down, salt air pushing through the cab. Ruth drove the way she did everything — efficiently, silently, with an attention that made you feel audited. Ada watched the water flash between the trees and turned the page's words over and over like stones in a tumbler.

"He scuttled her," Ada finally said. "That's what this says. Not lost her. Scuttled her. On purpose."

"Or it says he wanted people to think so."

"Why would he want that?"

Ruth took the curve above Miller's Reach without slowing. "Why does anyone write something down and sink it? So it survives them. Your father wasn't a careless man, Ada. Whatever else he was."

"That's not what the town thinks."

"The town thinks what's easiest." Ruth glanced at her. "Including about you."

They rode in silence for a while. Then Ada said, "Crane's fleet. Corvus. How long has it been running?"

"Three generations. Old man Crane's grandfather — Marcus Crane — started it as a privateering outfit during the last border troubles. Letters of marque, prize crews, the whole romance. When the government shut it down, he pivoted to salvage. Same ships, same crews, same appetite. Just paperwork instead of cannon." Ruth's jaw tightened. "Half the foreclosures in this coast come out of Corvus money. They don't take towns. They buy them."

"And my father was running cargo for them."

"We don't know that."

"The page says *per instruction*, Ruth. Whose instruction? Who amends a manifest on a salvage steamer except the owner?" Ada pressed the bag flat against her knee. "He was carrying their freight. And then he sank it rather than deliver it."

Ruth was quiet for a long mile. "Then ask yourself what was in those crates that a man would drown three of his own crew to keep from delivering it."

"There were no bodies recovered. You know that. The three men who died were never found."

"I know what wasn't found." Ruth's voice dropped. "I also know what the old-timers say, which is that Elias Voss put those men ashore at Miller's Reach the night before and told them to disappear. And that they did."

Ada turned to look at her. "You've known this? All this time?"

"I've known rumors. Rumors are free. Believing them costs." Ruth pulled into the harbor lot and killed the engine, then turned in her seat, and her eyes were kind in a way that was almost worse than pity. "Your father may have been a hero, Ada. Or he may have been a thief covering his tracks with a noble story. The strongroom knows. That's why Crane wants it sealed or emptied before anyone else reads it. And it's why you have to decide, right now, whether you're diving for gold or for truth. Because you can't steer for both."

* * *

That night Ada sat in the floatplane's cabin at the fuel dock with the dome light on and the page spread on the chart table, weighted at the corners with a spark plug, a roll of electrical tape, and her father's compass.

The compass needle swung lazily, pointing southeast, toward the cave, toward the wreck, toward whatever her father had done in the dark on the fourteenth of June all those years ago.

She laid the page beside the letter from the box. Side by side, the handwriting matched perfectly. Of course it did. She'd hoped, stupidly, for a forgery — some explanation that let her keep the story she'd been carrying since she was nine years old. But the hand was his. The *E* with its long crossbar. The *ss* like twin gulls.

*I have signed for what I will not deliver.*

She thought about him drunk at the kitchen table in the last years, when she was small, when the whole coast called him the man who lost the Meridian. She remembered asking him once, standing on a chair to reach his shoulder, *Daddy, did you crash the boat?* And she remembered — God, she had buried this memory so deep it took twenty years and a torn page to dig it up — she remembered him going very still, and very sober-seeming, and saying:

*"I crashed her exactly where I meant to, kiddo."*

And then he'd laughed, the awful hollow laugh, and poured another drink, and she'd filed it away as drunken nonsense. A joke. The rambling of a ruined man.

It hadn't been a joke. It had been a confession delivered to the only person too young to understand it, because she was the only person he could trust with it.

Ada put her head down on the chart table and cried for the first time since his funeral — not the hot grief of loss but something stranger, grief reorganizing itself, grief changing shape the way water changes shape when the tide goes out and shows you what was underneath all along. Her father hadn't failed. He had chosen. He had carried the shame of a wreck on purpose so that something in those crates would never reach the hands that paid for the ship. Three generations of Cranes had been hunting this wreck, and her father had spent his life and his name keeping it from them.

And the coast had spat on him for it. And she had inherited the spit.

When she lifted her head, her face was wet and her jaw was set, and the crying was finished, and what remained in its place was not comfort. It was purpose, cold and load-bearing, like a new keel laid under an old hull.

Because here was the arithmetic that mattered: if her father had scuttled the Meridian to keep stolen relief funds — and Teo's fragments suggested exactly that, funds skimmed from coastal aid shipments, money meant for towns like Kasaan and Miller's Reach and the harbor Ruth kept alive on stubbornness and duct tape — then the gold in that strongroom wasn't treasure. It was evidence. And evidence in Silas Crane's hands was worth more than gold, because it could be destroyed.

Crane didn't want the Meridian's cargo. He wanted the Meridian's silence. His family fortune had been built on the theft; the ledger in that strongroom was the one document on this coast that could burn it down. Which meant Crane would spend anything — boats, bribes, blood — to reach it first.

Six tides. They'd used two. Four left.

Ada gathered the page, the letter, the compass. She clipped the compass to her jacket where it always hung, and felt its weight differently now — not an apology anymore. An assignment.

Outside, fog was coming down the channel in long white fingers, and far out in the black water she could see the running lights of the Corvus fleet, three vessels holding station off the point like patient sharks. One of them flashed a light twice. Deliberate. Directed. At her.

Someone was telling her they knew she had the page.

Ada killed the dome light and stood in the dark of her own airplane, heart hammering, and understood that the race had just changed shape. It was no longer about who reached the wreck first.

It was about who could be trusted aboard her own skiff — because somewhere between the second tide and tonight, someone had told Silas Crane exactly what Teo Marsh had brought up from the deep.
