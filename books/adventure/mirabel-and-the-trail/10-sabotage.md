## 10. Sabotage

Ruth Okafor had been harbormaster of Kestrel Point for eleven years, and in that time she'd learned that the water told you everything if you got up early enough to listen. The tide came in with its gossip. The boats knocked against their moorings like old men complaining about their knees. And the dock itself — her dock, the one she'd rebuilt plank by plank after the storm of '09 — creaked in a language she could read better than her own handwriting.

So when she walked down at five-forty in the morning and the language was wrong, she knew it before she knew why.

The skiff sat too low on its lines. That was the first thing. Ada Voss's old boat — Ruth's old boat, technically, until she'd signed it over two nights ago in a handshake that had felt more binding than any contract she'd ever notarized — sat listing maybe four degrees to port, and the morning fog lay against its hull in a way that said *empty*, that said *taking on water*, that said *nobody checked on me*.

Ruth took the last thirty feet of dock at a jog.

She swung aboard and pulled the bilge cover before she even looked at the engine. Six inches of gray water sloshed over her boots. She found the pump switch, thumbed it, and heard nothing but the click-click-click of a dead battery. Fine. Manual then. She grabbed the hand bailer wedged under the thwart and started throwing water over the side while her eyes did the work her hands couldn't.

The fuel line hung loose where it should have been clamped to the carburetor intake.

Not worn. Not chafed. Cut. A clean slice through the rubber, one pass, someone who knew exactly what they were doing and hadn't wanted to spend more than three seconds doing it. The fuel that should have been in the line was in the bilge instead, and the fumes alone would have been enough to make the whole skiff a bomb if anyone had gone to start it cold without checking.

Ruth set down the bailer very carefully and stood in the flooded bottom of her own boat and felt something cold settle into her chest that had nothing to do with the fog.

Somebody had tried to burn the Meridian run down before it ever left the dock. And they'd done it quietly, in the night, on *her* dock.

* * *

By seven, half the town knew. By eight, the other half wished they did.

Kestrel Point worked like that — news moved through the harbor faster than through any radio band, because the harbor was where everyone's livelihood lived, and people guarded their livelihoods the way they guarded their children. Ruth stood at the top of the ramp with her arms crossed and watched the crowd assemble: gillnetters coming off the early run, the two women who ran the chandlery, old Anders from the fuel float, a knot of younger men whose faces had that particular hungry look she'd been seeing all week — the look of people who'd spent years telling themselves the Meridian was a bar story and were now furious that it wasn't.

Ada arrived at eight-twenty, still in her flight jacket, hair pressed flat by the headset she'd clearly just pulled off. She came down the ramp fast, saw the crowd around her skiff, and stopped like she'd hit glass.

"Fuel line's cut," Ruth said, meeting her halfway. "Clean cut. Someone came out here after midnight and gutted your boat."

Ada looked past her at the skiff, at the bilge pump running off the borrowed battery Anders had already hooked up, at the slice in the fuel line that three men were now examining with the reverence of priests reading entrails.

"When?"

"Sometime between my last walk-through at eleven and first light. I checked the dock myself." Ruth kept her voice level, official, the voice she used for incident reports. "Whoever it was knew my schedule."

"That's not a long list."

"No," Ruth agreed. "It isn't."

And that was the problem, wasn't it. The list was short. The dock gate had been locked, and Ruth had the only key — except for the spare she kept in the harbormaster's office, and except for the fact that half the town had watched her hand that spare to Junie Voss two days ago, when the crew had started staging gear.

Ruth watched the realization move through the crowd like weather. She watched it arrive on face after face, and she hated every second of it, because she'd grown up in a place where accusations traveled faster than facts, and she'd sworn, moving to Kestrel Point, that she would never let this town become that place.

"Junie was seen down here last night," said Tom Brandt, one of the gillnetters, not loudly, but not quietly either. He said it the way men say things they want repeated. "Around ten. My wife saw her from the kitchen window. Standing right about where the skiff's tied."

The crowd made a sound. Not words. Just a sound, low and collective, the sound a jury makes before it's decided anything.

Ada turned around.

Ruth had known Ada Voss since Ada was nine years old and falling out of a dinghy off the north float, and in all those years Ruth had never seen her look the way she looked right then — not angry, exactly. Something colder than anger. Something with its sleeves rolled up.

"Say that again," Ada said.

"I'm just saying what—"

"You're saying my cousin sabotaged a boat she's crewing on. A boat she needs as badly as the rest of us. You're saying she cut a fuel line on a boat full of gasoline fumes that could have killed whoever started it — which, if you've thought it through, would most likely have been *her*, since she's the one who does the engine checks." Ada's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. It stayed flat and precise, like a depth sounding. "That's what you're saying."

Brandt shifted his weight. "People are saying—"

"People are always saying." Ada looked at the whole crowd now, sweeping them, and Ruth saw the exact moment the town realized that Ada Voss was going to make this a fight instead of a wound. "My father carried a name this town spat on for twenty years, and every one of you said *people are saying* right up until it cost him everything. So no. We're not doing that. Not today, not on my dock, not about my family."

"It's not your dock," someone said from the back. Ruth didn't see who. She didn't need to. The sentence did its work anyway.

Ada went very still.

Ruth stepped in then, because that was her job — not just pilings and moorage fees, but the space between people when it got too narrow. "It's my dock," she said, loud enough to reach the back row, "and the harbormaster's office says nobody came through that gate after ten p.m. except on foot, and the tide log shows a skiff crossed the outer channel at eleven-forty running dark. I haven't filed anything yet because I don't guess. I count." She let that sit. "What I can tell you is that the Corvus fleet has been in this harbor asking questions for six days, and Silas Crane doesn't charter every boat in port because he likes the company. If somebody wanted this run dead, ask yourself who profits."

"And ask yourself," Ada added, quieter, "why Junie would torch her own daughter's future. Because that's what the Meridian money is. That's what everybody in this crew is out there for. You think she doesn't know that better than any of us?"

Nobody answered. Nobody apologized, either. The crowd broke apart the way crowds do — slowly, unwillingly, carrying the accusation home with them like a splinter.

Ruth watched Ada stand alone on the ramp for a long moment afterward, watching her skiff drain, and Ruth understood that something had happened here that wouldn't be fixed by facts. Ada had chosen. Publicly. Loudly. She'd put herself between her cousin and the town, and the town had noticed, and the town kept score.

The price of loyalty, Ruth thought, is that you pay it in front of witnesses.

* * *

They found Junie at the diner, in the back booth, drinking coffee she wasn't tasting.

Ruth slid in across from her without asking. Ada stayed standing, arms folded, looking out the window at the harbor like she could hold it together by watching.

"They're saying it was me." Junie said it flatly, to the table. "Marnie told me in the parking lot. Half of them won't even look at me. The other half look too long."

"The fuel line was cut by someone who knew boats," Ruth said. "The cut was clean, single pass, right angle. Whoever did it has handled marine fuel systems before or been shown exactly where to cut. It also wasn't panic work — no hesitation marks. That's either a professional or someone calm enough to be one."

"So it could be me."

"It could be anybody with hands," Ruth said. "But it wasn't you, because you were on the phone with the surgeon's office in Port Ellison at eleven-fifteen. Forty minutes. I know because I called them an hour ago and asked."

Junie's head came up. For the first time since Ruth had sat down, her eyes weren't flat. "You called—"

"I'm the harbormaster. I investigate things. That's the job." Ruth folded her hands on the table. "Here's what I don't understand yet, Junie, and I'd like to. The gate log. Somebody came through on foot at ten-oh-six and left at ten-thirty-one. That was you — Marnie's window puts you there, and the timing fits. What were you doing on my dock for twenty-five minutes?"

The silence stretched. Ada turned from the window.

"I was sitting on the end of the pier," Junie said finally. "Looking at the water."

"Twenty-five minutes."

"My daughter has a hole in her heart, Ruth. Do you know what I do at night? I sit places and I do math. Boat shares, salvage percentages, what Crane would actually pay an informant, what a surgery costs, what a life costs. I sit and I do math until I can breathe again. Last night the only place I could sit was the end of your pier, because my house has ears — my neighbor watches everything I do and reports it to anyone who'll listen, which as of this morning apparently includes the entire town." Her voice cracked on the last part and she hauled it back down. "I did not touch that skiff. But I will tell you something worse, and you're not going to like it."

"Say it anyway," Ada said.

"If I *had* wanted to stop this run — if I'd wanted to take Crane's money and walk away — I wouldn't have needed to cut anything. I already gave him everything he needs. Where the cave is. When the tides open it. Who's on the crew." Junie looked directly at her cousin, and Ruth watched two women hold each other's gaze across a table with a wreck and a fortune and a dead man's name lying on it between them. "I told you that weeks ago. I'm telling the room now. Whatever happens next, that's on the table, and pretending otherwise is how people like Crane win."

Ruth nodded slowly. Confession, freely given, in front of a witness. In her experience that bought more credibility than innocence ever did.

"Then we're past that," Ruth said. "Which leaves the real question. If it wasn't Junie, and it wasn't any of us—" she looked at Ada "—then someone on this crew, or near it, is working for Corvus. And they didn't just cost us a fuel line. They cost us a tide."

Ada's jaw tightened. Ruth could see her doing the arithmetic — the same arithmetic, Ruth suspected, that Junie did on the end of the pier. Five usable tides left. A skiff with a severed fuel line. A parts order that would take a day to come up from Port Ellison unless someone drove it, and every hour on the road was an hour Crane's fleet spent positioning.

"We can fix the line ourselves," Ada said. "Teo keeps splicing stock aboard his own boat. Clamps, hose, the works. We lose the morning, not the tide." She paused. "Assuming Teo's splicing stock is still aboard *his* boat, and hasn't conveniently vanished overnight too."

The three of them considered that in silence.

"There's one more thing," Ruth said, and lowered her voice even though the booth was empty and the nearest table was fifteen feet away. "Before I came in here, I walked the dock again. Slow. Looking for anything the first pass missed." She reached into her jacket pocket and set a small object on the table between them. A cigarette butt, stamped out against a piling, rain-damp but intact. "I don't smoke. Junie doesn't smoke. Neither does Teo, or Ada. But these are Corvus brand — Crane ships them up from the south, his crews smoke nothing else, you see the butts all over his charter docks. This one was fresh. Under the covered section. Where it wouldn't have washed away."

Ada stared at it. "Someone from Corvus was on the dock last night."

"Or someone who smokes what Corvus smokes." Ruth picked the butt back up and sealed it in a plastic evidence bag she'd brought for exactly this purpose. "Either way, Ada — the person who cut your fuel line stood close enough to your skiff to hear the bilge water, and they took their time about leaving. That's not a saboteur in a hurry. That's a message. *We can reach your boat. We can reach it whenever we want.*"

Outside the diner window, the fog was burning off the harbor, and out beyond the point, faint as pencil marks, Ruth could see them — the masts of the Corvus fleet, riding at anchor where they had no business anchoring, close inshore, patient as gulls behind a fishing boat.

Ada followed her gaze and went quiet for a long moment.

"Fourth tide tomorrow," she said at last. "We run it regardless. And Ruth — when we come back in, I want a watch on this dock every night. Rotating. Crew members only."

"That'll mean someone standing guard instead of sleeping, every night, until the sixth tide."

"I know."

"And it'll mean the town talks even more. About all of you. About you especially, now that you've made defending Junie a public position."

Ada picked up her coffee cup, found it cold, and drank it anyway. "Let them talk," she said. "My father spent twenty years letting this town talk while the truth sat underwater. I'm done paying interest on that loan."

She stood, dropped bills on the table, and headed for the door — and stopped with her hand on it, because Junie had spoken without lifting her head.

"Ada. Why?" Junie's voice was small in a way Ruth had never heard from her. "After everything. After what the letters say about him. Why are you spending whatever's left of your name on me?"

Ada stood in the doorway with the harbor light behind her, and Ruth saw her choose her answer the way you choose footing on scree.

"Because you're what I have left of him," Ada said. "Both versions of him. The one in the letters and the one who taught me to fly. I'm not giving up either one without a fight."

The door swung shut behind her.

Ruth looked at Junie. Junie looked at the table. And out on the water, the Corvus fleet began, one by one, to raise anchor.
