## 11. Third Tide: The Strongroom

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and by 4:20 I was awake anyway, lying in my bunk at Ruth's listening to the water slap the pilings like it had something to tell me.

I gave up pretending to sleep at five and made coffee in the dark. The skiff rocked against the dock below, loaded since midnight: dive bottles charged, Teo's gear bagged against the spray, crowbars and a battery-powered grinder wrapped in oilcloth because salt water eats tools alive if you let it. Three days since Teo came up too fast out of forty meters with a logbook page zipped inside his suit and his left arm already going numb. The clinic in Kessel put him on oxygen for six hours and told him exactly what would happen to him if he did it again. He'd nodded politely and then spent the drive home reading decompression tables like a man studying scripture he intended to break.

He was on the dock when I came down, hood up against the drizzle, looking gray but steady.

"You're supposed to be taking it easy," I said.

"I'm supposed to be rich," he said. "One of those is happening today."

Ruth arrived next with a thermos of soup and the expression she wore instead of good luck wishes — jaw set, eyes doing a final inventory of everything that could go wrong. Junie was last, which surprised me. She'd barely spoken to me since the night she'd laid her father's letters on my table and let me see what was inside them. But she came down the ramp in rubber boots with her hair tied back and said only, "I'll run the winch," and I decided that was as much peace as either of us could afford before the tide came back.

We ran the Throat at slack water, the same way we had the first time, engine idling through the surge channel while the cave walls closed in on both sides close enough to touch. The needle on my father's compass didn't swing this time. It just pointed, steady as a plumb line, straight into the dark.

* * *

Inside, the cavern opened up the way it always did — that cathedral hush, water gone black-green and still, our headlamps throwing long trembling ropes of light across rock that hadn't seen weather in ten thousand years. The Meridian's mast still lay where we'd found it, half-buried in silt, its rigging fused into a single rusted braid. Beyond it, the floor of the cave dropped away into the trench where Teo had found the strongroom hatch.

Teo suited up on the skiff's bow while I held his fins. Up close I could see the fine tremor in his hands that hadn't been there two weeks ago. He caught me looking.

"It's a shallower dive," he said. "Thirty-one meters. Fifteen minutes bottom time. I've got a table written on my forearm." He showed me. He really had written it on his forearm, in waterproof ink, in his own careful diver's hand.

"Teo."

"Ada." He looked at me over the rim of his mask, and for a second the flippancy fell off him entirely. "You want to know why I'm doing this? It's not the gold. Well. It's partly the gold." A breath. "My brother's boat has a lien on it older than my nephew. If we come up empty, I go back to hauling crab pots for a man who owns my winter. If we don't—" He shrugged. "Fifteen minutes. I can hold my breath longer than that."

I couldn't argue with him without arguing with myself, so I didn't. I clipped the lift bag to his harness, checked his regulator twice, and watched him roll backward off the bow into water so still it barely splashed.

Then it was waiting. Waiting is the part nobody writes songs about. Ruth kept the engine ticking over against the current. Junie fed line off the winch spool and wouldn't look at me, and finally I moved down the skiff and sat across from her, close enough that our knees almost touched.

"The letters," I said. "The ones about Dad being paid to move the ship."

"What about them."

"I keep turning them over. Looking for another way to read them." I watched the bubbles rise off to our left, silver strings climbing toward a ceiling they'd never reach. "There isn't one, is there."

Junie was quiet a long moment. "No," she said. "But Ada — a letter tells you what somebody wrote. Not why. You'll want to remember that when we get down there."

"When we get down there we're going to find gold, and none of it will matter."

"That's what I'm afraid of," she said, and went back to the winch line, and I understood that we were not reconciled, only armed.

* * *

At eleven minutes the lift bag broke the surface twenty feet off the bow, and Ruth gunned us over to it, and then Teo came up slow and controlled along the shot line, exactly the way the tables on his arm told him to, and climbed aboard looking like a drowned man who'd won an argument with the sea.

"Hatch is free," he rasped, pulling his mask. "Hinges are shot but the lock's already broken — has been for years. Somebody beat us to the door but never opened it wide enough to get inside. There's a gap maybe eight inches. Everything behind it is dry." His eyes were bright, feverish or triumphant, probably both. "Ada. It's full. It's *full.*"

We rigged the lift bags to the hatch itself — three of them, chained through the hinge mounts — and flooded them until the old iron groaned and tore loose from its frame with a sound I felt in my teeth more than heard, a groan rolling up through the water like the cave clearing its throat. Then it was crowbars and the grinder and forty minutes of prying at a gap designed by someone who never wanted it pried, while the tide clock in my head counted down without mercy. Slack water would end. The Throat would start breathing. We had maybe three hours, and we used every minute of them.

The first thing out was a canvas-wrapped case the size of a footlocker, hauled up on the winch with all four of us leaning on the crank. Teo cut the bindings with a dive knife. Inside, packed in rows like eggs, were gold bars — not coin, not nuggets, but stamped ingots, each one the length of my forearm, each one stamped with a mint mark and a serial number. Dozens of them. The lamplight lay on them and they drank it and gave it back warmer.

Nobody spoke. Junie made a sound low in her throat, half laugh, half prayer. Ruth took off her hat, which from Ruth was the equivalent of falling to her knees.

"That's the debt," Teo said quietly. "That's the whole debt. That's the strip and the plane and ten years of your life, right there in a box."

It should have been the moment. I'd rehearsed it a hundred times in the air, alone above the fog line — the weight of it, the release, my father's ghost finally paid off. Instead I was looking past the gold at the second thing in the case, wedged beneath the false bottom Teo's knife had found: a steel document tube, wax-sealed, stenciled with lettering that time had softened but not erased.

**MERIDIAN — MANIFEST & CORRESPONDENCE — COASTAL RELIEF ADMINISTRATION.**

Coastal Relief Administration. I knew that name. Every town on this coast knew that name. It was the fund that rebuilt the cannery at Port Ansel after the fire. It was the schoolhouse at Little Kessel. It was the money Washington had voted after the '29 collapse to keep a hundred miles of fishing villages from starving — money that had vanished somewhere between the capital and the coast, officially lost to "maritime misadventure," unofficially the reason three towns went dark and their people scattered inland.

I cracked the seal with my thumb.

* * *

The documents were dry. That was the uncanny part — the cave had kept them like a reliquary, cold and airless, and the ink sat on the page as sharp as the day it dried. There was a manifest, signed and countersigned. There was a bill of lading. And there were letters, a whole correspondence, and I read them by headlamp while the others sorted gold bars around me, and the cave got colder with every page.

The Meridian had carried $340,000 of relief funds north in the spring of 1931. Not smuggled — carried openly, under contract, the standard arrangement. What the letters showed was what happened between the loading dock and the sea. The funds had been diverted before the ship ever sailed. Bought. Reassigned on paper to a private salvage concern — Corvus Marine Recovery, predecessor to Corvus Salvage, incorporated by one Ambrose Crane — and the Meridian herself was to be scuttled with a token cargo so the loss could be written off and the theft buried under forty fathoms.

The final letter was dated three days before the sinking. It was instructions, typed, unsigned except by initials at the bottom — **A.C.** — and it acknowledged receipt of "the Voss proposal." My father's name appeared twice more in the file, once as master of the vessel, once in a handwritten note in a different, shakier script: *Voss demands the crew be paid off and landed safe before the job. Will not proceed otherwise.*

"He didn't sink it," I said. My voice came out strange, flat, like someone else's. "They sank it. He was the captain they hired to steer it down. He insisted the crew live."

Ruth looked up from the manifest. "Ada—"

"And then he spent thirty years letting everyone believe he'd been drunk, or careless, or bought — because the alternative was telling people the money was gone and watching them tear each other apart looking for it." I was holding the letter hard enough to crease it. "Or maybe because whoever ran Corvus made sure he understood what happened to men who talked."

The gold bars suddenly looked different in the lamplight. They'd looked like freedom ten minutes ago. Now they looked like a schoolhouse that never got built, a cannery town that emptied out, Junie's mother leaving for the interior with two kids and nothing — my aunt's letters had said that too, though I'd never let myself connect it. This wasn't treasure. It was evidence. It was the corpse of something stolen, and every bar of it was stamped with a serial number that some clerk in 1931 had entered into a ledger against money meant for towns exactly like ours.

Teo sat back on his heels beside the open case. "So we can't sell it."

"We can't *touch* it," Ruth said. "Not one bar. The moment any of this surfaces, provenance attaches. Salvage law, admiralty court, the government's claim on relief funds — we'd be litigating until we're dead, and Crane's lawyers would eat us alive in the meantime." She put her hat back on, and her voice went rough. "This isn't a fortune. It's a bomb."

"It's proof," I said.

"It's proof that ruins us," Junie said. She'd gone pale. "Ada, think. If this comes out, it doesn't just clear Dad's name. It says the money our own family lived on afterward — the insurance payout, the settlement — it says all of it came from this. From them. Everyone on this coast who ever took a dollar of that era's compensation—"

"Was paid hush money," I finished. "Yeah. I know."

The silence that followed had weight, like the water outside the cave. Somewhere far off, deep in the rock, I could hear the tide beginning to turn — a low shifting groan, the Throat waking up.

Teo broke it, because Teo always broke silences eventually. "Okay. So we take the documents and leave the gold. Seal the case back up, let the cave keep it." He glanced at the bars, and I watched him physically wrestle something down — a man walking away from a year of his brother's debts. "The gold was never the point anyway. Right?"

Right. Except it had been the point, for all of us, and we all knew it, and pretending otherwise was just the first lie of whatever came next.

We repacked the case. We left the gold in the dark where it had lain for ninety years, guarding nothing now but its own guilt. I took the documents — all of them, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped inside my jacket against my ribs, where I could feel them with every breath like a second heartbeat. Ruth set the hatch back over the strongroom mouth as best we could. It wouldn't stop anyone determined. It would only have to slow them.

Because here was the thing I hadn't said out loud yet, the thing sitting in my chest heavier than any ingot: the documents named Corvus. Named Ambrose Crane. And Silas Crane had chartered every boat in the harbor, and asked questions about my father, and wanted my father's compass because — his words — *it belongs with the rest of his mistakes.*

He hadn't been hunting gold. Gold he could buy lawyers to claim later. He'd been hunting paper. And if he learned what we'd pulled out of that strongroom tonight, he wouldn't send lawyers.

The current had picked up by the time we ran the Throat outbound, the channel breathing hard now, white water stitching across the entrance. Ruth took us through with her jaw tight and her hands easy on the wheel, and the open sea received us gray and choppy under a sky the color of dishwater.

Junie sat beside me in the stern with the wind pulling her hair loose. She was staring at the bulge of documents under my jacket.

"There's something else you need to know," she said. "Before we get back to the harbor. Before anyone sees us come in together." Her face was terrible in the flat light. "Ada, I have to tell you what I did."

The compass on its cord swung against my chest as the skiff rose and fell, and for the first time since the lowest tide drained the bay, it pointed at nothing at all.
