## 12. Junie Confesses

Junie Voss had rehearsed the confession eleven times on the walk over, and every version had ended with her running.

She stood at the door of Ada's hangar now, rain beading on the tin roof above her like a slow drumroll, and understood that the twelfth version — the one where she told the truth and stayed to hear what it cost — was the only one that counted. Her daughter's name was stitched into the reason. *Poppy.* Everything came back to Poppy lately, the way water comes back to the sea.

Inside, lamplight moved across the floatplane's flank. Ada was under the engine cowling with a torque wrench, sleeves shoved up, a smear of oil along one forearm like war paint. She didn't look up when the door scraped open.

"If you're here about the skiff," Ada said, "Ruth already read me the riot act. Twice. I'm rebuilding the fuel line from scratch tonight."

"I know who cut it."

The wrench stopped.

Ada straightened slowly, and in the yellow light her face did something complicated — hope and dread fighting for the same ground. "You were seen at the dock, Junie."

"I was seen at the dock because I go to the dock. I've gone to that dock my whole life." Junie's voice cracked on the last word and she hated herself for it. She'd wanted to sound steady. Steady was the whole point of coming here. "But I didn't cut your line, Ada. I swear it on Poppy."

"Then why does everyone in this town think you did?"

"Because everyone in this town is right about everything else I've done."

* * *

They sat in the hangar's back room, where their grandfather had once kept charts of waters no chart company would print. A kettle ticked on a hot plate. Rain worked the roof. Ada poured two cups of coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration and set one in front of her cousin without ceremony, which was somehow worse than an interrogation lamp.

Junie wrapped both hands around the cup. Warmth first. Then words.

"Three weeks ago," she said, "a man came to the house. Not Crane himself — a lawyer type, soft shoes, soft voice. He knew Poppy's diagnosis before I said it. Knew the surgeon's name in Vancouver. Knew what the waitlist looked like, which is to say he knew we were drowning." She stared into the coffee. "He said Mr. Crane admired our family. Said there was a consulting fee available for someone with local knowledge of the interior coast. Historical knowledge."

"Junie—"

"He asked about Papa." Her throat closed around the name. "About the Meridian. About whether Papa ever talked, ever wrote, ever left anything behind. And I told him yes."

The rain filled the silence for a long moment.

"I told him Papa kept letters. That there were papers. I didn't give them to him — I couldn't find them then, and thank God — but I told him they existed. And I drew him the bay. The cave mouth. Where the tide goes out farthest." Junie finally lifted her eyes. "I sold our father, Ada. For a down payment on a surgery. And the check cleared, so I can't even pretend it didn't happen."

Ada set her cup down with exaggerated care, the way she did when turbulence hit and she needed her hands busy. "How much?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters because I want to know exactly what you think our father's name costs per pound."

"Forty thousand." The number came out flat, dead, like something netted. "Half up front. Half when Crane has the wreck."

Ada laughed once — a short, airless sound with nothing in it. She got up, walked to the window, watched the rain come off the harbor in gray sheets. Junie watched her sister-cousin's shoulders and waited for the verdict.

When Ada turned around, her eyes were wet but her jaw was set, and that combination terrified Junie more than shouting ever could.

"The night the tide went out," Ada said quietly, "I thought the compass was calling me. I thought it was Papa reaching across ten years to hand me something true." She shook her head. "And the whole time, Crane was following a map you drew him. We weren't called to that cave, Junie. We were *led*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"I know." Junie's voice was barely there. "I've known since the day you flew me over the channel and you looked so — sure. Like you trusted the water. I've been waiting three weeks for you to find out and hate me."

"And instead you let Ruth's town nearly string you up for sabotage you didn't commit."

"That wasn't strategy," Junie said, and something raw surfaced in her. "That was cowardice. If they believed I cut the fuel line, at least it was a crime I could almost live with. Cutting a fuel line is just vandalism. What I did—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum. "What I did was sell the only true thing our family ever had."

* * *

Ada refilled both cups. It gave them both somewhere to look.

"You said half up front," she said at last. "Where's the other half contingent? On Crane raising gold? On documents?"

"On the wreck being secured. His term was 'exclusive salvage position.'" Junie grimaced. "Lawyer language. But here's the thing I keep turning over — the timing doesn't work. I made contact three weeks ago. Crane chartered every boat in port within days, fine. But the sabotage?" She shook her head. "Ada, if Crane wanted us dead in the water, he had a hundred cleaner ways than slashing a fuel line on a skiff tied up at a dock full of witnesses. Slashing a line is loud. Sloppy. It makes the town pick sides, and picking sides is the last thing a man like Crane wants while his charter leverage is still quiet."

Ada frowned. "Then who?"

"Somebody close enough to touch the skiff without being seen. Somebody who knew which boat mattered." Junie leaned forward. "Think about it. Who knew we were running the tides? Who knew the schedule? Who had access to the dock after hours?"

"Ruth runs the dock."

"Ruth offered us the skiff. Why would she break it?"

"Teo's been diving every tide—"

"Teo's been in the water or in decompression since the second tide. When would he even—" Junie stopped. Something crossed her face, quick as a fish shadow. "Ada. Who else knows the tide schedule by heart? Who else has been 'helping' with logistics, running errands, keeping the books?"

Ada went very still. "Nobody else is on the crew."

"Exactly." Junie spread her hands. "So either Crane has a diver we don't know about slipping into that cave on off-tides, or—" she swallowed, "—or somebody inside this crew is being paid the same way I was paid. Soft-shoe money. Half now, half when it's done."

The kettle ticked. Somewhere out on the water, a gull complained about the weather.

"There's another possibility," Ada said slowly. "Crane could have a man on his own fleet doing dock work. Corvus crews have been all over the harbor."

"Maybe. But the cut on that fuel line was clean. One pass, sharp blade, angled to look like chafe damage." Junie held up her hands, mimicking the motion. "That's not a fleet deckhand in the dark. That's somebody who knew exactly what they were cutting and wanted it to look like wear. Somebody patient."

"Or somebody who'd done it before."

"Or that."

Ada rubbed her face with both hands. When she lowered them, she looked older, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Okay. Say you're right. Say there's a mole. We can't go to Ruth with a theory built on your confession — the moment the town hears you took Crane's money, every decision we make gets second-guessed through the lens of 'the Voss women are compromised.'"

"So we say nothing."

"We say nothing. For now." Ada's eyes narrowed. "But we watch. Every person who touches that skiff, every errand, every question anybody asks about the schedule. You're going to help me, Junie. You're going to earn back — I won't say trust. Trust takes longer. You're going to earn back *usefulness*. Starting tonight."

"What do you need?"

"Your memory. Every detail of those meetings with the soft-shoe man. What he wore, what he drove, what he smelled like, every phrase he used. Lawyers repeat themselves. Phrases are fingerprints."

Junie nodded, and then, because honesty was the only currency she had left, she spent the rest of it: "There's one more thing. The man — he said something at the door as he left. I didn't understand it until this week." She made herself hold Ada's gaze. "He said, 'Tell your cousin the compass points both ways.' I thought it was nonsense. Poetry. Now I think it means Crane believes your compass isn't just pointing at the wreck. He thinks it points at something he wants. Or someone."

Ada's hand went, without her noticing, to the compass hanging at her throat.

Outside, the rain thickened, and beneath the drum of it on the tin roof, both women heard it at the same instant — the low, diesel growl of a heavy boat running dark into the harbor, engines throttled down to a whisper, moving like something that didn't want to be counted.

Ada was at the window in three strides.

"That's no fishing boat," she murmured. "Fishing boats announce themselves."

The growl faded toward the north docks — toward the fuel shed, toward the row of moored skiffs, toward the one boat in the harbor that mattered.

Junie rose, her coffee forgotten, cold fear washing warm through her chest. "Ada. If there's a mole, and Crane's people are moving tonight—"

"Then whatever they're after," Ada said, pulling her slicker off its hook, "they think we're not watching."

She killed the lamp, and the two of them slipped out into the rain together, toward the docks, toward the dark, toward whoever among them had already been bought.
