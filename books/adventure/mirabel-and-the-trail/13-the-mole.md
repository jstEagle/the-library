## 13. The Mole

Ruth Okafor had run the harbor at Kestrel Point for eleven years, and in that time she had learned one rule above all others: when something went wrong on her dock, the answer was never in the water. It was in the ledger.

She sat in her office above the fuel shed with the door cracked to the smell of diesel and brine, a cup of coffee gone cold beside her elbow, and two sets of papers spread out on the desk like a patient she was trying to diagnose. On the left, a photocopy of pages from Silas Crane's fleet accounts — Junie had photographed them weeks ago with shaking hands and hadn't known what half of them meant. On the right, Ruth's own harbor records: moorage fees, fuel chits, repair invoices, the small honest arithmetic of a small honest town.

The sabotage had happened sometime between midnight and four. She'd found the cut fuel line herself at dawn, kneeling on wet planking with a flashlight in her teeth, running her thumb along the blade mark. Clean cut. One pass. Done by someone who knew exactly where the line ran and didn't need light to find it.

Junie Voss had been seen near the dock. That was the story moving through town like weather — at the Anchor, at the co-op, in the line at the post office. Junie, who'd already confessed to tipping off Crane. Junie, who needed money for her daughter's surgery so badly she'd sold out her own cousin's father's memory. Easy math. The town had done it before breakfast.

But Ruth had watched Junie Voss lie, three nights ago in this same office, and she'd watched her tell the truth about it afterward, and those were different animals entirely. A liar's hands go still. Junie's hands had never stopped moving. And more to the point: Junie had come forward. Saboteurs don't confess to the lesser crime to cover the greater one. They stay quiet and let the tide do the work.

No. Ruth turned the page of Crane's ledger and felt the cold settle into her shoulders before she even understood why.

The payments were there. Weekly, irregular amounts, coded as "survey retainers" against a vessel designation that didn't exist in any registry she knew: *Cormorant*. She'd seen that word before. Not in Crane's papers — in her own. In the moorage records from last spring, when Teo Marsh had been three months behind on his slip fees and had finally paid everything off in one lump sum, cash, no explanation, grinning like a man who'd found money on the beach.

She pulled the file. March: $1,400. April: $1,600. May: nothing — the lump sum, $4,200, clearing every debt he owed in the harbor book including the engine rebuild he'd been putting off for a year. And then June, July, August: smaller amounts, steady, like a retainer. Like a salary.

She laid Crane's ledger beside it. "Survey retainer — Cormorant." March: $1,400. April: $1,600. The dates matched within a day of each other. Every single entry.

Ruth sat back and looked at the ceiling for a long time.

Teo Marsh. The best diver on this coast between here and Port Hardy. The man who'd held Ada Voss's hand over the radio when her engine quit over the Sound five years ago and talked her down onto the water like it was nothing. The man who'd cried at her father's memorial service, actually cried, big unashamed tears, because Elias Voss had taught him to dive when he was sixteen and too broke to buy his own gear.

And the man who had surfaced from the strongroom dive with the bends and a logbook page in his mesh bag, and who had been alone on the bottom for forty minutes longer than his dive plan allowed.

*He could have copied anything down there,* Ruth thought. *He could have flagged anything. Marked the location for Crane with a transponder buoy while he was "decompressing." We've been feeding our route to the Corvus fleet one tide at a time.*

She got up and poured the cold coffee out the window into the gravel, mostly because she needed to do something with her hands.

* * *

The problem — and Ruth made herself look at it straight, because sentiment was how harbors got lost — the problem was that they still needed him.

Four tides left. Maybe three if the storm front coming down from the Gulf pushed the schedule around. The captain's cabin of the Meridian sat under sixty feet of cold green water at the end of a surge channel that would kill an amateur in ninety seconds, and Teo Marsh was the only person in Kestrel Point who could get there and back. Ada couldn't dive it. Ruth certainly couldn't. Junie had a PADI card and the nerve of a woman who'd already spent it.

So you couldn't cut him loose. You couldn't confront him, either — not yet. A cornered man tells his handler everything, and Crane would know their whole plan by nightfall, and the sixth tide would belong to the Corvus fleet before they ever launched. Whatever Ruth did with what she knew, she had to do it without Teo knowing she knew it.

Which meant the crew kept diving with a traitor. Which meant every piece of the plan now had to be built twice: once for the truth, and once for whatever version of the truth reached Crane's ears.

Ruth had been a harbormaster long enough to know that most betrayals weren't evil. They were arithmetic. Teo owed money somewhere worse than a slip fee — she'd heard the collection calls through the walls of the bunkhouse last winter, a voice flat as a fish knife saying words like *principal* and *final notice*. Crane hadn't bought Teo's soul. He'd bought his debt, which was cheaper and held its value better.

That didn't make it forgivable. It just made it survivable to sit across a table from him tonight and pass him the potatoes.

* * *

They gathered at Ada's place above the strip — the old Voss house with the wind sock turning lazy circles in the yard and the floatplane tied down at the edge of the grass like a sleeping gull. Rain had started around four, the fine sideways kind that wasn't weather so much as atmosphere. Inside, the woodstove ticked and the map of the cave system lay across the kitchen table weighted with coffee mugs and a tin of gunpowder tea nobody drank.

Ada stood at the head of the table with her sleeves pushed up, and Ruth watched her and thought: *she doesn't know yet.* Or maybe she suspected and couldn't afford to say it, the way Ruth couldn't afford to say it. Two women keeping the same secret for the same reason, each waiting for the other to crack first.

"Fourth tide is tomorrow, 0640," Ada said. "Window's two hours, maybe less if the swell picks up. Captain's cabin is aft, below the main deck, which means we go in past the mast, down through the cargo hold breach, and along the portside corridor. Teo leads. I'm second. Ruth runs the skiff and the surface interval."

"And me?" Junie asked from the corner, arms crossed, still wearing the town's suspicion like a coat she refused to take off.

"You're on the radio," Ada said. "You watch the channel traffic. If Corvus moves, we hear it before we see it."

"Because I did such a good job the first time."

"Because you're the only one of us Crane doesn't bother lying to," Ada said, and there was something in her voice that wasn't forgiveness yet but had stopped being anger. "He'll talk around you. He always does. Men like Crane can't help performing."

Ruth let the planning wash over her and studied Teo instead. He sat with his forearms on the table, nodding along, asking good questions — *what's my bottom time, what's the current read at the breach* — all the right questions, a professional's questions. His hands were steady. His eyes went to the map and stayed there, competent and calm.

Only once did he look up, and it was at Ada, and it lasted maybe two seconds, and Ruth saw in it something that wasn't calculation. It was guilt. The specific, unbearable guilt of a man sitting in a warm room full of people who trust him, doing long division in his head about what their trust will cost them.

*Or I'm wrong,* Ruth told herself. *Or the ledger means something else. Or somebody's playing both of us.*

But she'd matched eight entries across two independent books, and in eleven years of harbor work she had never once seen coincidence keep books that neatly.

* * *

She caught him outside, after. He was checking his gear in the lean-to off Ada's garage, tanks and regs laid out on a tarp with the neatness of a man whose discipline was the only thing he fully owned. Rain drummed the tin roof. Ruth stepped under the eave and stood beside him, not looking at him, looking at the dark yard where the wind sock turned.

"Slip fees came clear last spring," she said. "Cash. Four thousand two hundred, plus the engine work. I remember because I had to count it twice."

Teo's hands paused on a regulator hose. Just a pause. Half a second. Then they resumed, steady as ever.

"Good year," he said.

"Mm." Ruth nodded at the rain. "Crane's fleet books show survey retainers going to a vessel called the Cormorant. Same amounts. Same dates. Right down to the week."

The rain filled the silence. Down the hill, the harbor lights smeared yellow through the wet, and somewhere out there the Meridian waited under its fathom of green water with a dead man's orders in the captain's cabin.

"I don't know what you think you've got," Teo said quietly, "but—"

"I've got your debts, Teo. I've had your debts since winter. I know who holds them, and I know what men like that do when you miss a payment, and I am not telling you this because I want a confession." She turned and looked at him then, and let him see that she wasn't angry, which was worse than anger. "I'm telling you because tomorrow you're going to be alone on the bottom of that channel with our lives hanging off your regulator, and I need to know which way you'll swim."

His jaw worked. The rain came down harder. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough as beach gravel.

"If I say it wasn't me—"

"The fuel line says otherwise or it doesn't. I haven't decided."

"It wasn't the fuel line." He said it fast, like ripping tape. "I never touched the skiff. I swear it on Elias. But yes. Yes, okay? Crane bought my paper last fall. Every week he slides money into an account and every week I don't drown myself, and part of what he bought was reports. What we found. Where we're going." He stared at the tarp, at his own neat rows of equipment. "I gave him the strongroom location. That's on me. The logbook page I brought back real — I wouldn't fake that, not to Ada — but he knows everything else. Everything up to yesterday."

Ruth exhaled slowly. Part of her had hoped she was wrong. Most of her, though — most of her had already moved past hope to logistics.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "You don't quit. You don't confess to Ada, not yet, and you don't stop reporting to Crane. As far as he knows, nothing's changed."

Teo's head came up. "You want me to keep spying on us?"

"I want you to spy on him. You're the only double agent on this coast who comes pre-installed." She let that land. "Tomorrow, when you report, you give him the fourth-tide plan — the real one, times and all. Because the plan Ada's going to announce inside the next hour is going to change. Quietly. After you leave this yard. And what you feed Crane tonight will be true enough to check and wrong enough to matter."

"That's—" He shook his head, half laugh, half horror. "Ruth, if he figures it out, he'll take my debt to the wall. He'll take everything."

"He might anyway. Once the broadcast goes out, his paper's worth nothing and he knows it." Ruth put a hand briefly on his shoulder, heavy as a mooring line. "You want to buy yourself back, Teo? This is the price. You dive the captain's cabin tomorrow like your life depends on it, because ours does. And you feed Silas Crane a map that walks his fleet into the surge channel at slack water minus twenty minutes, when the current there will chew anything under thirty feet."

Teo looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way men nod when they've stopped negotiating with themselves.

"One condition," he said. "When this is done — whatever's done — Ada hears it from me. Not from you. From me."

"She will," Ruth said. "If we're all still floating."

She walked back down toward the harbor lights through the rain, and behind her she heard Teo's truck start, heading for wherever a man goes to make a phone call that betrays his friends on purpose.

At the office she opened her ledger again and, beneath the moorage records and the fuel chits, wrote a single line in pencil, dated, unsigned: *Cormorant — flipped. Verify at fourth tide.*

Then she picked up the radio and called Ada, and told her the plan had changed.
