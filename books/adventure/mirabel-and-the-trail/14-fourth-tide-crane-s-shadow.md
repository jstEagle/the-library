## 14. Fourth Tide: Crane's Shadow

The fog came off the water at dawn like a held breath finally released, and Ada watched it from the skiff's tiller with the compass open in her palm, the needle steady as a struck bell.

Fourth tide. Two left after this. She'd stopped counting the hours and started counting the tides, because hours lied to her — they stretched when she was afraid and collapsed when she was working — but tides were honest. The moon pulled, the water rose, the cave opened or it didn't. There was something restful about an enemy that kept a schedule.

"Contact," Teo said quietly.

He was at the bow, binoculars up, and his voice had gone flat in the way it did when he was trying not to feel anything about what he was seeing. Ada didn't need him to say more. She'd heard the engines twenty minutes ago — three of them, deep-throated diesels running at half power, the way boats run when they want to be heard late rather than seen early.

She raised her own glasses through the thinning fog and found them: the Corvus fleet, strung out in echelon across the mouth of the channel like a hand opening. The *Corvus Prime* in the lead, all black hull and white superstructure, too clean for these waters. Behind her two workboats, crane-armed and ugly, and behind those a tender riding low with equipment. They weren't searching. Searching boats wander. These boats were holding formation, holding position, holding *ground* — and their ground was directly between the skiff and the channel entrance.

"They've been waiting," Ruth said. She'd come up from below with a thermos and the particular expression she wore when she'd been doing arithmetic she didn't like. "That's not a patrol pattern. That's an ambush geometry."

"He knows the tide table too," Ada said. "Same tables, same numbers. I keep forgetting he can read."

"He can read more than that." Ruth poured coffee into the cap and handed it back to Ada without looking at her. "Harbor office picked up fleet traffic on sixteen last night. Encrypted bursts, short ones. He's coordinating with somebody ashore."

Junie sat near the transom with her arms around her knees, saying nothing, which was how Junie said most things now. Since her confession Ada hadn't decided what to do with her — send her home, forgive her, put her ashore at the fuel dock and let the town's judgment do what judgment does — so instead she'd done nothing, and Junie had repaid the nothing by being useful in small silent ways: coiling lines, checking the bilge, keeping watch on the radio without being asked. It wasn't trust. It was a truce, and both of them knew truces could be spent like money.

Ada drank the coffee. It was terrible. Ruth made coffee the way she ran the harbor — strong enough to strip paint, indifferent to complaints.

"Options," Ada said.

"We wait them out," Ruth said. "They can't hold station forever. Fuel costs money even for Crane."

"They don't have to hold forever. They have to hold for ninety minutes. Tide window's seventy-five." Ada looked at the water, gray-green and flat as hammered tin, then at the channel mouth two miles north where the headlands pinched together and the current ran hard over the shoal. "He doesn't need to catch us. He just needs to be *there* when we come out. Or better — he needs to make us decide we can't go in at all."

Teo lowered the binoculars. "There's another thing." He turned the glasses toward the eastern headland, where the fog was burning off in ragged sheets. A floatplane sat at a mooring buoy in the lee, rocking gently, its corrugated skin catching the light. White fuselage. Red wingtips. Ada knew that airplane the way you know a face from a bad dream.

"That's a survey bird," she said. "Camera port in the belly. He's photographing the channel."

"He's mapping your approach," Ruth said. "Every rock, every depth reading he can buy or steal. He's going to know that channel better than you do by tonight."

The coffee had gone bitter in Ada's mouth, or maybe that was something else. Her father had flown survey runs in a plane just like that one, back before the debts and the disgrace, back when the Voss name meant competent instead of cursed. She'd learned to read water from the air sitting in the right-hand seat of a plane like that, age eleven, her father's hand tapping the windshield: *See how the current bends around the bar? The water tells you where it's shallow. You just have to look.*

The water tells you where it's shallow.

Ada set down the cup.

"Ruth. How long to run the channel on the flood, skiff only?"

"Forty minutes if we're careful. Twenty-five if we're stupid."

"And Crane thinks we'll run it when?"

"When the window opens. Eleven-forty." Ruth's eyes narrowed. "Ada. What are you doing."

Ada was already looking up, past the mast, past the fog line, to where the sky over the eastern ridges had gone the color of a bruise — deep purple stacked on slate, with a greenish tint at the leading edge that every bush pilot learns to fear and respect in equal measure. The front had been forecast to hit by afternoon. The forecasters had been optimistic.

"I'm going to look at the water," she said, "from where the water can't lie."

* * *

They argued about it for exactly four minutes, which by this crew's standards was a war council.

"You can't fly into that," Ruth said, jabbing a finger at the bruised sky. "That's a squall line with a grudge. You go up in that, you're not doing reconnaissance, you're doing a séance."

"The front will hold off until early afternoon. The meteogram says so."

"The meteogram also said light winds yesterday, and I lost two crab pots off my own dock."

"It's forty minutes," Ada said. "Up the coast, over the channel, map his positions, back down before the line arrives. I've flown worse in worse."

"You flew worse alone," Junie said, and everyone went quiet, because it was true, and because it was Junie who'd said it — Junie who owed her, Junie who'd betrayed her, standing there with her jaw set like she had the right to care. "You always flew alone. That's why everything fell apart last time. Let someone sit in the other seat."

Ada looked at her cousin and felt the old anger rise and, unexpectedly, fail to find purchase. There was no room for it anymore; it had been crowded out by tides and gold and the specific exhaustion of loving someone who'd hurt you for reasons you understood. Her daughter needed surgery. Ada would have burned the world down for less.

"Fine," she said. "You're in the right seat. Touch nothing, talk when I tell you to talk, and if I say *out*, you punch out the door handle and swim, because I am not explaining to your kid why her mother drowned in a plane I was flying."

Something moved across Junie's face — relief, or grief, or the strange hybrid of the two that families specialize in. She nodded once.

Teo helped them push the floatplane off the mooring. His hands were shaking — Ada had noticed it yesterday, noticed the tremor he hid in his pockets, the sweat at his temples that wasn't exertion. The bends had gotten into him deeper than he admitted, a gift from the second tide that kept giving. She'd confront it later. Everything got confronted later; later was the richest account she had and it was badly overdrawn.

The engine caught on the third try. The floats kissed the swell, lifted, and the bay fell away beneath them, and Ada felt the transformation happen the way it always did — the fear draining out through the stick and into the machine, the machine converting it to altitude, altitude converting it to sight. Up here she wasn't a debtor. She wasn't a disgraced man's daughter. She was eyes, and speed, and judgment, and the sky didn't care who her father was.

"Course?" Junie asked, clipped, professional, terrified.

"North along the shore, then east over the point. We'll come downwind over the channel at twelve hundred feet, high enough to see, low enough to count deck plates." Ada banked onto heading and the coast unrolled beneath them — black rock, white sand beaches, the green smear of the timber cuts. "Then we get home before the sky eats us."

The first fifteen minutes were clean. Then they weren't.

* * *

The squall line had moved faster than anyone's forecast, because squall lines answer to no one, and Ada saw it now filling the eastern horizon wall to wall — a blue-black rampart with lightning walking inside it like a prisoner pacing a cell. Between her and it lay the channel, and between the channel and safety lay forty miles of coastline she'd have to retrace with the wind building behind her.

One pass. She'd get one pass.

"There." Junie pointed. "Boats."

Ada tipped into a shallow dive, throttled back, and let the plane settle into a slow orbit above the channel mouth, and below her the whole board laid itself out like a diagram in a book she suddenly knew how to read.

The *Corvus Prime* anchored square in the middle of the fairway, dead center, deliberately — not hiding, *announcing*, a floating gate across the channel. One workboat stood off the northern shoal with a diver platform rigged, close enough to foul anyone's prop who tried the inside passage. The second workboat sat on the southern side, and Ada's breath caught, because it wasn't anchored at all. It was slowly dragging its anchor across the channel bottom in long deliberate arcs, and behind it, on the surface, floated a line of small orange buoys, spaced evenly, sinking out of sight.

"They're laying something," Junie said.

"Nets. Or cable." Ada's mind was moving fast now, cold and clear, the way it did at altitude when there was no time to be anything but right. "A barrier under the surface. You run the channel at periscope depth in a skiff, you hit it. You hit it at speed, it takes your lower unit off, maybe swamps you." She keyed the thought tighter. "It's not to stop us. It's to *catch* us. He wants the boat intact. He wants us alive and talking."

"Why not just board us?"

"Because boarding us makes him a pirate in front of witnesses. This way, we wreck ourselves in open water, he renders assistance, salvages the boat, and holds everything aboard as salvage rights. Legal. Clean. *His*." She laughed once, without humor. "My father used to say the dangerous men aren't the ones who break rules. It's the ones who know exactly where the rules end."

She rolled into a wider orbit to check the headlands, and that was when she saw the third thing, the thing that changed the shape of the morning entirely.

At the inner bend of the channel, tucked against the cliff where radar from seaward couldn't touch it, sat a fourth vessel. Small, dark, low in the water. No lights, no markings visible at this range, no reason to be there except that it was exactly where a boat would want to be if it wanted to follow someone *into* the cave without being seen from outside.

"He's got a shadow boat inside the channel," she said. "He already knows the entrance."

Junie went very still beside her. "Then why is he stopping us at all?"

And Ada, watching the lightning walk closer, felt the answer assemble itself with the ugly click of a lock turning. "Because he doesn't want to go in first. He wants *us* to go in first. Find it, open it, mark it — and then he follows our wake and takes it, and if the cave collapses or the tide turns, it's our bodies that explain what happened." She shook her head. "No. Worse. He wants us to lead him in so that whatever happens in there happens to *us*, and he keeps his hands clean and his claim intact."

The radio crackled. Channel sixteen, the hailing frequency, the frequency every mariner in the world keeps open.

"*Unidentified aircraft over the channel.*" The voice was smooth as oiled teak, unhurried, a voice that had never once needed to raise itself. Silas Crane. "*You are conducting low-altitude maneuvers over a restricted salvage operation. Be advised we have documented your flight. Be advised further that obstruction of a lawful salvage claim carries civil penalties. I'd hate to see a licensed pilot add aviation violations to her financial difficulties.*"

Ada's thumb hovered over the transmit button. Every instinct said stay silent, don't confirm, don't engage. But silence was information too, and Crane collected information the way the tide collected everything eventually.

She keyed the mic. "*This is Voss. Restricted according to whose claim? The Meridian's cargo belongs to the coastal towns it was stolen from. Your paperwork's about to become kindling.*"

A pause. When Crane answered, there was something new in his voice — not anger. Interest. The interest of a man revising an estimate upward.

"*There it is,*" he said softly. "*The famous Voss certainty. Your father had it too, right up until the end. Do give my regards to the cave, Miss Voss. And do hurry. Weather's coming, and the sixth tide waits for no one — isn't that what your father used to say?*" A click. The channel went quiet.

Junie stared at her. "How does he know Dad's sayings?"

"I don't know," Ada said, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. "But I'm going to find out."

She pushed the nose north for home, and the squall line chose that moment to arrive.

* * *

It came sideways, the way coastal fronts do — no gradual build, just a sudden wall of rain and wind that took the visibility from five miles to fifty yards in the time it took Junie to say a word that wasn't a word. The plane bucked. Rain hammered the windshield like thrown gravel. Somewhere behind them thunder detonated, close enough to feel in the airframe.

Ada flew.

Not bravely, not heroically — *competently*, which was harder and mattered more. Instruments. Attitude. Heading. The storm tried to take the wings and she gave it nothing to take, feeding in corrections small and constant, flying the airplane the way her father had taught her: *the plane doesn't care how scared you are, Ada-girl. It only cares what your hands do. So make your hands boring.*

*Make your hands boring.* She almost laughed. Thirty years old, drowning in debt, racing a salvage baron for a dead ship's stolen gold, and the best advice she owned was still a dead man telling her to be boring.

"Compass is swinging," Junie shouted over the engine noise.

"Trust the heading indicator. Compass lies near lightning." Ada's eyes flicked across the panel, calm, methodical. Wind correction. Drift. The coastline should be coming up on the left in — there. A black smudge resolving through the rain, and she corrected ten degrees, and the smudge became the ridge above the strip, and the ridge became trees, and the trees became the world again.

She dropped through a gap in the clouds she'd have sworn didn't exist thirty seconds earlier, leveled over the bay, and saw the skiff waiting at the mooring, tiny and faithful, and Ruth standing on the dock with her arms crossed and her face doing a poor job of pretending she hadn't been afraid.

The landing was textbook. It usually was, when it mattered most; her hands saved their poetry for the ground.

By the time she'd tied off and climbed down onto the float, Ruth was there, gripping her forearm hard enough to leave marks, saying nothing, which from Ruth was a speech.

"Did you get it?" Teo called from the skiff.

Ada pulled the notebook from her jacket — dry, somehow, in the way of things carried against the body — and flipped it open to the page where, during the one calm pass, she'd drawn the channel in fast sure strokes: the *Prime* anchored mid-fairway, the net-laying workboat's track marked in dotted arcs, the shoal boat, and in the inner bend, circled twice, the dark shape of the shadow vessel.

"Every position," she said. "Anchorage, nets, timing on their buoy line, and a boat hidden inside the channel that means Crane's known the entrance since before we did." She looked around at the three of them — the harbor master, the broken diver, the cousin who'd sold her out and shown up anyway — and felt something settle in her chest that wasn't quite fear anymore. Fear was for things you couldn't act on. This she could act on.

"So here's what we know that he doesn't know we know," she said. "He's guarding the front door of a house he's never been inside. He's spending a fortune to stop us from doing his exploring for him. Which means—" she tapped the circled shadow boat, "—he needs us alive, cooperative, and *first*. And that, ladies and gentlemen, is a leash, and a leash pulls from both ends."

Ruth studied the chart over her shoulder. "The fifth tide's tomorrow, 0620. Dawn window. Fog likely."

"Fog's not a problem." Ada smiled, and it surprised her how little effort the smile took. "Fog's a tool. We go in on the fifth tide through the fog, quiet, using the inner channel he doesn't think we've mapped. We take the captain's cabin evidence and we're out before his buoy-watchers finish their coffee. And tomorrow night—" she looked up at the bruise-colored sky, already rebuilding itself for the next performance, "—we make copies of everything, because the sixth tide isn't a salvage run anymore."

"No?" Teo asked.

"It's a broadcast." She closed the notebook. "Crane wants to control the story. Tomorrow, the whole coast hears it at once, from us, on an open channel, and there's nothing his lawyers or his fleet or his family name can do about a truth that's already loose in the world."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The rain ticked on the water. Out in the bay, faint through the weather, the diesels of the Corvus fleet idled at their stations, patient as creditors.

Then Junie stepped forward and held out her hand — not for shaking, but open, offering what was in it: a water-stained envelope, soft with handling, sealed with tape gone amber with age.

"What's that?"

"Found it going through Dad's letters last night, looking for anything about Crane's family." Junie's voice was steady, but her eyes weren't. "It's addressed to you. It's dated three days before the Meridian went down. And Ada—" she swallowed, "—it's not in his handwriting."

Ada took the envelope. The paper was cold and damp against her palm, and somewhere beyond the headlands, the sea kept its own counsel, holding its secrets one tide longer.

She broke the seal.
