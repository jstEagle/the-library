## 15. Fifth Tide: The Captain's Cabin

Teo Marsh had a rule he'd never broken in eleven years of diving: when the numbers on the computer said stop, you stopped. The rule had kept his blood from fizzing like warm beer, kept his joints from screaming, kept him alive through two hundred cold, dark descents into the channels around Mirabel. It was the one piece of discipline he'd never traded away, not for money, not for pride, not even the winter his daughter was born and the work was thin and the Corvus fleet was paying triple rate for men willing to push.

He broke it on the fifth tide.

* * *

The morning came in gray and mean, wind out of the southeast with a smell of iron in it. Ada stood at the skiff's wheel with her father's compass open on the chart box beside her, and she didn't look at Teo once while they ran the channel. That was its own kind of message. Since Ruth's ledger and the payments that matched his debts to the dollar, nobody had accused him of anything. They'd just stopped talking freely around him, the way you do with a man who might be wearing a wire.

Only Junie spoke to him normally, and only because she didn't know. He almost preferred the silence.

"Bottom time's the problem," Ada said finally, eyes on the water ahead. "The strongroom's at forty-two feet. You did thirty-eight minutes down there last tide and came up shaking."

"I came up fine."

"You came up gray." She cut the throttle and let the skiff drift. "Today we're not going to the strongroom. We're going deeper — the captain's cabin, aft, under the collapsed section. Ruth found the deck plans in the maritime archive. There's a sealed compartment below the waterline, and if Elias Voss's orders exist anywhere, they're in there."

"Below the waterline means sixty feet or better," Teo said. "With the surge running, call it a working depth of seventy. I get maybe twenty minutes at that depth before the tables start lying to me."

"The tables are conservative."

"The tables are how I'm still walking." He looked at her, and she looked back, and for the first time since the fuel line got cut she let him see how tired she was. "Ada. If I go past my limits today, I might come up bent. Or I might not come up."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking because Crane's fleet moved overnight. Ruth counted four boats inside the outer markers at dawn. He doesn't need the documents anymore — he needs them buried, and us with them if it comes to that. Whatever's in that cabin is the only thing left that can hurt him worse than he can hurt us." She turned back to the wheel. "I won't order you. I'm asking."

It would have been easy, right then, to say no. To say the safe thing, the smart thing, the thing the old Teo would have said while texting coordinates to a man who paid him in cash and contempt. Nobody in that boat knew for certain what he'd done. He could dive shallow, come up empty, shrug.

Instead he heard himself say: "Give me twenty-five minutes. Not a second more. And have oxygen on the surface, mixed, ready to breathe. If I come up hot, you get me into it fast."

Ada nodded once. Junie reached over and squeezed his shoulder, and he pretended not to feel how much that cost him.

* * *

The cave swallowed the skiff the way it always did — a throat of green light narrowing to black, the walls sweating, the sound of their own engine coming back at them doubled and wrong. On the fifth tide the water inside sat lower than any previous run, exposing ribs of the Meridian's hull that had been hidden all week, and Teo saw Ada stare at them as they passed. Her father's ship. Her father's grave, maybe, in every way that mattered.

They anchored over the stern section. Teo kitted up in silence, checking his gauges twice, three times, the way a man counts money he already knows is short.

"Twenty-five minutes," Ada said.

"From the moment I leave the surface."

He rolled backward into water so cold it felt like a hand closing around his chest.

* * *

Down was easy. Down was always easy. The hard part was the dark between thirty feet and fifty, where the collapse had dumped the stern section across the seabed like a house falling over, and Teo had to find the way in by feel and memory of the deck plans Ada had drawn on his slate. Portside passage. Past the galley bulkhead. Through the gap where the hull plates had peeled back "like a tin can," Ruth had said, and Ruth had been optimistic.

The gap was barely wide enough for his tanks. He went through on an exhale, dragging himself sideways, feeling the rusted metal kiss his suit, hearing his own breathing loud and ragged in his ears. His light found silt and dead men's shoes and a brass door handle polished bright by eighty years of current.

The captain's cabin.

He went in low, under the fallen shelf that had been somebody's desk, and there it was: a strongbox bolted to the deck beneath the bunk frame, small, iron, sealed with a padlock that had surrendered to the sea long ago. The lid lifted without resistance.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had mostly held, were papers. Real paper, dense and dry where the cloth had protected it, soft as wet leaves everywhere else. And on top, folded twice, a document with a wax seal still intact — broken, but legible — pressed with a crest Teo recognized from the harbor master's wall and from Crane's letterhead both: the Corvus.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. He checked his gauge: nine minutes gone, sixteen left, and the swim back through the gap would eat five of those if the surge was kind. He tucked the oilcloth bundle inside his buoyancy vest, zipped it flat against his chest, and took one last look around the cabin — at the bunk, the desk, the small brass lamp hanging from a hook like it was waiting for someone to light it.

Somebody had lived down here. Somebody had taken orders, sealed or unsealed, and carried them into this bay, and died for them or near them. Teo thought about debts, about the clean arithmetic of money owed and money paid, about how Silas Crane had looked at him across a fuel dock table two winters ago and said, *Everyone sells, Marsh. Some just take longer to name their price.*

He'd named his price. He'd taken the payments. And now he was stealing from Crane for free, which was either redemption or just a different kind of selling, and he didn't have the air left to figure out which.

He turned for the gap.

That was when the surge hit.

It came through the hull breach behind him like a train, a wall of displaced water that had been building somewhere out in the channel, and it picked him up and drove him into the fallen shelf. Pain bloomed along his left side, bright and total, and his regulator knocked loose from his mouth and spun away on its hose. He caught it on the second grab, jammed it back between his teeth, breathed salt and rust. His gauge read eleven minutes remaining. The gap was six feet away and the surge was filling the cabin, slamming him against the shelf again, and something in his side gave with a sound he felt more than heard.

Rib. Maybe two. He'd cracked one off Kodiak in '19 and knew the geography of the pain intimately.

He stopped fighting the water. That was the trick, the one old divers knew and young ones learned too late: don't wrestle the sea, wait for it to finish its sentence. He curled around the bundle in his vest, let the surge press him flat against the deck, and rode it out, counting seconds, tasting blood he couldn't locate.

When the water slackened, he moved. Not fast — deliberate. Arm over arm along the shelf edge to the gap. Sideways through it, the rusted plate scoring his tank, his hip, the same wound twice. Then up, following his bubbles into the green, watching his gauge lie to him about how much time he had left, knowing the numbers were already past the point where the tables would forgive him.

At twenty feet he saw the skiff's hull above him, silver in the broken light, and he kicked for it with a side that screamed.

* * *

They hauled him over the gunwale by both arms, Ada and Junie together, and he came up the ladder like cargo, which was fair. The first breath of surface air tore at whatever was wrong inside him and he made a sound he was ashamed of.

"Oxygen," Ada snapped, and Junie was already there with the bottle, pressing the mask to his face. Cool, clean, the good stuff. He pulled it in like a drowning man, and the gray edges of the world came back a little.

"You're bleeding," Junie said. "Your side—"

"Suit held. Mostly." He pulled the mask away long enough to speak. "Bundle. Inside my vest. Don't lose it."

Ada's hands were on the zipper before he finished. She drew out the oilcloth package with the reverence of a woman handling a live shell, and unwrapped it right there on the deck, shielding it from the spray with her body. Teo watched her face through the fog of the mask as she read.

He'd imagined this moment a hundred ways. Shouting. Crying. Silence. What he got was worse: Ada going very still, the way water goes still right before it freezes.

"Read it," she said, and handed it to Ruth, and Ruth read it aloud in her harbormaster voice, the flat official tone that made everything sound true:

*"By authority of the Maritime Salvage Commission and under bond to the Crown, Captain E. Voss is directed to take possession of the vessel Meridian and her cargo, said cargo having been determined stolen goods — relief funds diverted from coastal reconstruction accounts by agents of the Corvus Shipping concern — and to secure said vessel against recovery by said concern until such time as proper inquiry can be convened."*

Ruth lowered the page. Nobody said anything. The seal at the bottom was the Corvus crest, inverted, struck through with the Commission's stamp — a document authorizing exactly what Ada's father had done, signed by the very institution whose descendants now wanted it erased.

"He didn't sink her," Junie whispered. "He was ordered to hide her. From *them*. From Crane's grandfather."

"And Crane's whole fortune," Ruth said quietly, "is built on the money he stole. The relief funds. Our money. Every town on this coast has been paying interest on that theft for eighty years." She looked at Teo, and her expression was complicated — gratitude and suspicion sharing the same face. "You went past your limits for this."

"I went past my limits to stop owing," Teo said. It came out rougher than he meant. He pulled the mask back on and breathed.

Ada crouched beside him. Up close he could see she was crying and furious about it. "You could have died down there."

"Yeah."

"For this."

"For this." He met her eyes. "Your father was clean, Ada. Cleaner than any of us. I figured somebody on this boat should be able to say they know what that costs."

She put her hand on his shoulder, the good one, and didn't say thank you, and didn't need to.

* * *

They ran for home ahead of the weather, the sky going the color of a bruise behind them. Teo lay in the stern with the oxygen mask on and his cracked ribs strapped, and watched Silas Crane's fleet standing off the headland in a neat, patient line, four dark shapes against the dying light, not approaching, not leaving. Waiting.

Crane knew they'd been down. Crane always knew.

Teo pulled the mask aside. "Ada. When we make port — broadcast it. Tonight, before he can move. Marine band, open frequency, every town on the coast. Don't wait for the sixth tide."

"That's the plan nobody likes," Ada said. "We vote when we're ashore."

"Then vote fast." He settled back against the gunwale, ribs throbbing in time with the engine, and watched the dark shapes hold their station behind them like a verdict deferred. "Because that man just lost the last thing protecting him. And men like Crane don't grieve. They escalate."

Ahead, the harbor lights of Mirabel came up through the rain, and behind them, patient as debt, the Corvus fleet began to move.
