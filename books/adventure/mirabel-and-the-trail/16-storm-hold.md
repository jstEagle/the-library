## 16. Storm Hold

The storm came in off the Gulf like something with a grudge.

Junie Voss felt it before she saw it — a pressure change in her ears, a restlessness in the gulls, and then Ruth Okafor standing in the doorway of the harbor office at six in the morning saying, "Nobody's going anywhere for two days," in the flat voice of a woman who had already argued with the sky and lost.

The barometer had fallen forty points overnight. The marine forecast read like a threat: southeast gales gusting to fifty, seas to five meters, small craft advised — not warned, *advised*, as if the sea were doing them a courtesy by mentioning it at all. The fifth tide would come and go without them. The sixth was still out there, two days past the storm, but it felt suddenly fragile, a coin balanced on its edge.

So they waited. That was the thing nobody told you about salvage — that half of it was weathering, and weathering meant sitting still while everything you wanted sat under thirty fathoms of gray water, getting no closer.

* * *

Ruth's house above the harbor smelled of woodsmoke and old coffee. Teo took the couch without being asked, still moving carefully from the dive on the second tide, his left arm wrapped where the decompression had settled into the muscle like a debt of its own. Ada claimed the chair by the window and didn't sit in it so much as occupy it, upright, watchful, her father's compass open in her palm like she was reading tea leaves.

Junie stood in the kitchen doorway with her phone pressed to her ear, listening to the hospital in Ketchikan explain, again, that the surgery fund needed another four thousand dollars before they'd put her daughter's name on the schedule, and that no, the storm delay did not extend the deadline, because deadlines were the one thing in this world that never got weathered out.

She hung up and found Ada watching her.

"I heard," Ada said quietly. "The other night. I wasn't asleep."

Junie's jaw tightened. "Then you know why I did what I did."

"I know why you think you did." Ada set the compass down on the windowsill, face up, needle trembling toward the bay. "There's a difference."

"Ada—"

"Two days," Ada said. "We're stuck here for two days, and I'm not spending them circling each other like dogs on a chain. So either we talk now or we don't, but I'm done pretending this family only knows how to be silent at each other."

It was the most words Junie had heard her cousin string together about feelings since Uncle Elias's funeral, seven years ago, when they'd stood on opposite sides of the grave and hadn't spoken since except through lawyers and harbor gossip.

"Fine," Junie said, and went to get the letters.

* * *

She'd carried them everywhere since she'd come back to Mirabel. A rubber-banded bundle in a ziplock bag, because that was how you protected paper in a fishing town — against rain, against bilge water, against the general damp hostility of the coast. She'd taken them from their father's storage unit in Cordova three years ago, after the bank sold it out from under the estate, and she'd read them maybe a hundred times, and every reading had made the same argument: *Elias Voss knew what he was doing.*

She laid them out on Ruth's kitchen table like a losing hand of cards.

Ada didn't touch them at first. She stood over them the way you stand over a body, hands at her sides, breathing carefully.

"That's his handwriting," she said finally. It wasn't a question. It was grief looking for somewhere to land.

"The letter's dated eleven days before the Meridian went down." Junie kept her voice level, clinical, because if she let any feeling into it she'd start shouting and they'd both been shouting enough for one lifetime. "Read the third paragraph."

Ada read. Junie watched her lips move slightly, an old habit from when they were kids sharing a flashlight under blankets, taking turns with the good parts.

"'They've offered me money to move her,'" Ada read aloud, and her voice cracked on *money*. "'Not salvage — move. There are men in this who don't want her found where she lies, and I've decided I'd rather be paid than be honest about why.'" She stopped. Put the page down. Picked it up again, as if the words might have rearranged themselves. "He wrote this? He wrote this and kept it?"

"He kept everything. You know that. Boxes of it. The man filed his life like taxes."

"Why didn't you show me these before?"

"Because you wouldn't have read them." Junie said it plainly, without apology, because apology was a currency she couldn't afford right now. "You'd have called them forgeries. You'd have said Crane planted them. You'd have said anything rather than believe Dad took money to scuttle a ship, because your whole — " she gestured at Ada, at the compass, at the floatplane visible through the window riding hard at its mooring, " — your whole architecture is built on him being wronged. Wronged by the board, wronged by the fleet, wronged by the town. And he was wronged, Ada. But he also took the money. Both things are true, and you can't live in just one of them anymore."

The fire popped. Out in the harbor, something loose banged against a mast in the rising wind, a slow irregular knocking like someone asking to be let in.

"You tipped off Crane," Ada said. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the table next to the letters, letting them sit together.

"Yes."

"For Nell's surgery."

"Yes."

"And the fuel line on the skiff?"

"No." Junie met her eyes and held them. "That wasn't me. I've done enough. But I didn't cut that line, and I need you to believe me, because if you don't, there's no version of these two days where we come out the other side still family."

Ada looked at her for a long time. Outside, the first real gusts hit the harbor, and the whole building shuddered like a dog shaking off water.

"I believe you," Ada said. "I don't forgive you yet. But I believe you."

Junie exhaled. It came out shakier than she wanted. "I'll take believing."

* * *

They spent the morning reading the letters together, all of them, in order, and it was worse and better than either of them expected.

Worse, because Elias Voss had been paid — three payments, documented in his own careful hand, from an account Junie traced to a shipping concern whose registered agent shared a name with Silas Crane's grandfather. Better, because buried in the last letter, written the week before the Meridian sailed for the last time, was a line neither of them had understood until now:

*"If I do this, I do it my way. The cargo goes down where I put it, not where they tell me. Let them spend twenty years looking in the wrong water."*

"He hid it deeper than they asked," Ada said slowly. "He moved the wreck himself — off the coordinates they gave him. That's why the mast is where it is and the hull isn't. He wasn't covering up a crime. He was burying evidence."

"Against the Cranes."

"Against the Cranes." Ada laughed, and it came out wet and strange. "He sank a ship to build a case. And then he died before he could testify with it."

Junie turned the last letter over. On the back, in pencil, faint almost to invisibility: a set of bearings, corrected twice, and a single word — *strongroom*.

"He meant for someone to find it," she said. "Eventually. When it was safe."

"He meant for us to find it." Ada picked up the compass from the windowsill and held it flat between them, and the needle swung once, twice, and settled — pointing not north, not toward the cave, but at the space between their hands, as if the instrument itself had finally gotten where it was going. "Maybe that's what it's been doing this whole time. Not pointing at the wreck. Pointing at whoever was supposed to finish his sentence."

Junie looked at her cousin — really looked, for the first time in seven years — and saw that Ada's eyes were red-rimmed and steady, and that she wasn't holding the compass like an apology anymore. She was holding it like a tool.

"So we finish it," Junie said. "The sixth tide. We take the documents, and we tell the truth — all of it. What he did and why. Not the clean version."

"Not the clean version," Ada agreed. "He deserves better than a clean memory. He deserves a true one."

* * *

The storm peaked at dusk and held through the night like a note that wouldn't end. They lost the power around ten o'clock and sat by the woodstove while the wind tried to peel the roof off, and somewhere in the dark hours Junie fell asleep in the chair across from Ada, and woke near dawn to find her cousin had draped a blanket over her without waking her, and that seemed somehow like more forgiveness than any words.

Teo slept through all of it, which Ruth said was the best medicine for him anyway. Ruth herself stood watches at the window like a lighthouse with opinions, muttering about charter deadlines and men named Crane.

On the second afternoon, the wind backed around to the northwest and began, grudgingly, to die. By evening the barometer was climbing. Ruth spread the tide tables on the kitchen table and tapped the final column with one blunt finger.

"Sixth tide," she said. "Day after tomorrow, 0440. Low water minus 2.1. After that the cave closes for the season and doesn't reopen till spring — if ever. This is it."

Silence, except the tick of the cooling stove.

"There's something else," Junie said. She'd been turning it over all day, and it wouldn't turn anymore. "Crane hasn't contacted us since the ultimatum. Two days of the worst weather on the coast, and his fleet is still anchored outside the harbor bar — I checked with the binoculars this morning. All four boats. Riding it out. He's not waiting for the tide."

Ada frowned. "What else would he be waiting for?"

"That's what scares me." Junie gathered the letters back into their bundle, then stopped, and held them out to her cousin instead. "You keep these. I carried them long enough."

Ada took them. Her hands weren't quite steady.

And then Ruth, who had gone to answer the knock at the door that none of them had heard over the dying wind, came back into the room holding a sealed envelope, salt-stained, delivered by some boat that had come and gone in the night.

"It's addressed to all three of us," Ruth said. "And it smells like a lawyer."

Ada opened it, read it once, and went very still.

"What is it?" Junie asked.

Ada looked up, and Junie saw the storm wasn't over after all — it had just moved indoors. "Crane's moved the deadline," she said. "The sixth tide isn't day after tomorrow anymore. His lawyers filed an emergency salvage injunction this morning. If we enter that cave, we're not salvors. We're criminals."

Ruth swore softly, fluently, in two languages.

"He's not trying to beat us to the wreck," Junie said, understanding arriving cold. "He's trying to make sure nobody gets there legally at all — and then he takes it whenever he wants."

"Then we've got one shot," Ada said, "and it has to be perfect."

Outside, the last of the storm dragged itself out to sea, and the water in the harbor lay flat and waiting, like a held breath.
