## 17. Crane's Ultimatum

Silas Crane had learned long ago that the sea gave up nothing to men who hurried. Patience was the whole trade. You waited out the weather, waited out the regulators, waited out the sentimental attachments of small towns that believed a harbor was theirs because their grandfathers had built it. Everything belonged to whoever held the paper, and Silas Crane had spent thirty years making sure he held the paper.

He stood at the rail of the *Corvus Star* and watched Ruth Okafor's skiff bobbing at its mooring in Merrow Bay, gray light leaking through a sky like wet wool. The storm had pinned everyone ashore for two days now — his fleet as well as hers, which galled him less than it might have, because the delay had given him time to work. Time was a currency too, and he was richer in it than any of them understood.

"Mr. Crane." His first mate, Duvall, came up the companionway with a clipboard and the particular stiffness of a man delivering news he'd rather not own. "The skiff. The harbormaster filed a complaint with the port authority this morning."

"Of course she did." Crane didn't turn. "Ruth Okafor files complaints the way other people breathe. What did the authority say?"

"That they'd review it after the season closes."

Crane smiled. The port authority's charter renewal was scheduled for the end of the month, and three of the five board members owed their positions to financing Crane's holding company had arranged when the harbor dredging went sideways four years back. He didn't own the town. He owned something better: the town's ability to continue existing. "Then the complaint will be reviewed by people who understand how things actually work. Good. Anything else?"

"The Voss woman's plane hasn't moved since yesterday afternoon. But there were lights at the Okafor house until two in the morning."

Lights meant meetings. Meetings meant plans. Crane found he wasn't troubled. Let them plan. Plans made under pressure were always plans with seams, and he had spent his career finding seams. He turned from the rail. "Get me the tide tables again."

Duvall handed them over without comment. Crane ran his finger down the column. The sixth tide — the last one deep enough to float anything substantial through the cave mouth before the season closed and the sandbar rebuilt itself for another decade — was forty-one hours out. After that, the Meridian would be sealed behind ten feet of water and a throat of rock that had already eaten one of his divers' nerves and very nearly a Zodiac.

Forty-one hours. Long enough for an ultimatum to ripen properly. An ultimatum delivered too early looked like panic; delivered too late, it was just noise. There was an art to the thing. You let your opponent exhaust themselves on hope first. You let them believe, right up until the moment you spoke, that they might still win. Then you showed them the ledger.

* * *

The Okafor woman met him on the fuel dock at noon, because he'd asked her to and because refusing would have cost her something she couldn't afford. She came alone, hands jammed in the pockets of a canvas coat gone soft with salt, and she stopped a full arm's length away — close enough to talk, far enough to swing.

"You've got nerve," she said, "coming to my dock."

"It's not your dock." Crane kept his voice pleasant, almost warm. That was the trick most men never learned: menace worked best when it sounded like courtesy. Shouting told people where the edges were. Softness let them walk off them. "It belongs to the port authority, which is chartered through Meridian Coastal Holdings, which I control. But I'm not here about the dock."

"No?"

"I'm here about your skiff." He nodded toward the mooring, where the little boat rocked in the chop. Thirty years old, patched twice, engine held together with wire and stubbornness. It was worth perhaps four thousand dollars and it was worth everything, and Crane knew both numbers perfectly. "I'm taking possession of it. Effective this morning. Consider it collateral."

Ruth's jaw tightened. "Collateral against what? We don't owe you a cent."

"Not you personally." He let the pause stretch. "But debts are rarely personal, Ms. Okafor. They're structural. Your harbor carries obligations — the dredging loan, the breakwater repair, the pension shortfall your predecessor papered over in 'nineteen. All of it consolidated, all of it held, directly or indirectly, by interests I represent. A skiff is a small thing. But it makes the point cleanly, and I do appreciate clean points."

"You're stealing a boat."

"I'm securing an asset." He said it mildly, the way a teacher corrects vocabulary. "And while I have you, I'll pass along a message for Ms. Voss. She's been difficult to reach. She doesn't answer her radio, she doesn't come to the fuel dock anymore, and her cousin Junie has been remarkably unhelpful despite our arrangement." Something flickered across Ruth's face at that — quick, quickly buried, but Crane caught it, and he filed it. So the girl hadn't told them everything either. Wonderful. Fractured crews were pliable crews. "Tell Ada that I know what Teo Marsh brought up from the captain's cabin. Tell her I know about the sealed orders. And tell her this: she has until the sixth tide to deliver those documents to me, intact, along with whatever else the wreck has produced. If she does, we part ways amicably. If she doesn't—"

"If she doesn't, what? You'll take her skiff too?" Ruth's voice cracked with contempt. "You've got every boat in the port except mine, and now mine. What's left to squeeze?"

Crane looked at her for a long moment. He genuinely liked this about coastal people — the way they always assumed the fight was about boats.

"Ms. Okafor," he said, "do you know what happens to a harbor charter when its debt holders call their notes? The authority defaults. The state appoints a receiver. The receiver is usually someone cooperative, because cooperative receivers get reappointed. Berth fees triple. Fuel contracts go to whoever the receiver chooses. Trawler licenses lapse for nonpayment. Within a season, Merrow Bay isn't a harbor anymore. It's a scenic disappointment with a rusting breakwater." He watched the color leave her face and felt nothing about it, or told himself he felt nothing, which amounted to the same discipline. "I don't want that. Honestly, I don't. Harbors are useful. Towns are useful. I'm not a vandal, Ms. Okafor — vandalism is for people who can't afford better tools. All I want is what the Meridian carried, and the papers that keep certain history where it belongs. In exchange, the notes stay quiet, the charter stays live, and everyone goes on believing the world works the way they were taught it does."

"And if Ada says no?"

"Then on the seventh day, the calls start going out. To the note holders. To the state maritime board. To the insurance syndicate that insured the original cargo, which has some very old questions about how the *Meridian* came to sink in the first place." He paused, letting the name hang between them without saying it — *Voss* — because he'd learned that unsaid names weighed more than spoken ones. "Ada Voss thinks she's protecting her father's memory. I'm offering her the chance to protect a hundred families instead. I'm curious which she'll choose. People are rarely who their eulogies say they are. I suspect she knows that better than anyone."

* * *

That evening, in the *Corvus Star*'s chart room, Crane laid it all out flat on the table where he could see it: the tide tables, the debt schedule, the single page of the salvage logbook his man had photographed before Teo Marsh ever surfaced with the rest. His grandfather's handwriting stared up at him from the old orders — orders his family had spent three generations ensuring stayed lost. *Transfer cargo to alternate berth. No manifest. No witnesses.*

No witnesses. Except one, apparently. A young pilot named Voss who had signed the transfer receipt and then, when the syndicate's collectors came asking why the relief funds had vanished into private accounts, scuttled the ship rather than hand over the proof. The official record called it negligence. The official record had been written by Crane's grandfather's lawyers. And somewhere in that cave, in a strongroom behind a moved mast, sat the documents that could unwrite sixty years of careful paper.

Crane poured two fingers of whiskey and didn't drink it. People assumed men like him wanted money, and money was fine, money was oxygen, but what he actually wanted — what he had crossed oceans and ruined partners to protect — was the version of events. Gold could be replaced. A story, once loose, could never be recalled. His family's fortune rested on a story, and Ada Voss was holding the receipts.

Duvall appeared in the doorway. "Radio traffic. Encrypted burst from the Okafor house, short. And the Voss woman's plane engines turned over twenty minutes ago — just a run-up, no flight plan filed."

"They're deciding." Crane set down the glass. "Good. Deciding means they've understood the stakes. Nobody deliberates over a boat."

"And if they don't fold? If she broadcasts those documents like—" Duvall hesitated. "There's chatter on the marine band. Fishermen speculating. Someone's been talking."

Crane felt the first cold thread of genuine unease and cut it off, the way you'd cut a fouled line. Panic was a luxury. "If she broadcasts, she burns her father's name to save a town that will forget her inside a year. Sentimental people always overestimate how long gratitude lasts." He straightened the tide table so its edge ran parallel to the table's edge, a small violence of order. "But just in case sentiment wins — I want the *Corvus Star* positioned off the cave mouth at slack water tomorrow. Both tenders fueled. And find out exactly where that skiff's fuel line runs, Duvall. Not to cut it. Just to know."

Duvall lingered. "One more thing. Marsh. He dove past his limits today — recon dive, shallow pass along the channel. Came up fast. If he's planning something desperate on the sixth tide—"

"He's planning whatever pays him," Crane said. "His account got its deposit Tuesday. Men like Marsh don't change sides; they just renegotiate. Keep the payments coming and keep watching him." He picked up the whiskey finally and drank it down, and the burn settled in his chest like ballast. Outside, the storm had broken apart into ragged stars, and the bay lay black and patient, keeping its secret one more night.

Forty hours to the sixth tide. Somewhere across the water, in a drafty harbormaster's house, Ada Voss was weighing her father against her neighbors, and Crane intended to make sure that whichever way she chose, she'd carry the weight of it for the rest of her life.

He switched on the chart room lamp and began, with a jeweler's patience, to draft the letter that would arrive with the morning tide — the one that would tell her precisely, in language a court could read aloud, what her silence was worth and what her defiance would cost.

* * *

The letter reached the strip at dawn, weighted to the tarmac under a stone, addressed in a hand so neat it looked typeset:

*Ms. Voss —*

*By now you know the terms. Sixth tide. Documents, complete, or the charter dies and the town learns what its loyalty purchased. I should add one clarification, since harbormasters are prone to editorializing: the gold is negotiable. The truth is not. Keep the treasure, if it pleases you — it's worthless next to what the papers prove. Deliver the papers, and I will even let you keep the compass. It seems only fair that you hold onto something of his.*

*Consider carefully. The tide won't wait, and neither, this time, will I.*

*— S.C.*

Ada read it twice, folded it once, and put it in her jacket pocket over her heart, where the compass rode. Then she walked to the fuel shed where the others waited — Ruth with her arms crossed, Junie pale and sleepless, Teo leaning against the wall with fresh bruising along his collarbone from the bends chamber — and she read it to them aloud, voice level, while the morning fog burned off the water and the sixth tide began, somewhere beyond the point, its long slow pull toward shore.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Ruth said quietly, "He gave us the plan himself. He told us exactly what he's afraid of."

Ada touched the compass through the fabric. "Then let's not disappoint him."
