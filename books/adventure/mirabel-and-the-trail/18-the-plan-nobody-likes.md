## 18. The Plan Nobody Likes

The storm had spent itself by morning, and the harbor lay flat and gray as sheet metal, still shivering from what had passed over it. I stood at the window of Ruth's office above the fuel dock with a cup of coffee I'd forgotten to drink, watching Crane's fleet ride at anchor in a perfect line, like teeth.

Ruth's skiff was still tied where they'd left it — but there was a man aboard it now. A Corvus man, sitting on the gunwale with his boots dangling, reading something. Keeping it company. Keeping *us* off it.

"They're not even pretending," Ruth said behind me.

"No."

She came to stand beside me. She was holding the captain's sealed orders in a freezer bag, because Teo had insisted that's how you handle paper that old, and because Teo, who had nearly died twice for that paper, got to be right about things now. Through the plastic the ink looked fresh. My father's name sat at the bottom of the authorization like a signature on a verdict: *E. Voss, Master, under Admiralty writ 44-C.* And above it, the order itself — scuttle the Meridian, cargo intact, position sealed, in exchange for immunity and silence. Silence purchased by men whose descendants ran salvage fleets out of the same harbors, wearing the same family crest on their letterhead.

Silas Crane's fortune hadn't been built on finding wrecks. It was built on making them.

"You've been quiet since dawn," Ruth said. "That usually means you're about to say something we'll all hate."

I set down the coffee. "I need everyone in one room."

* * *

We took the back room of the Netmender, the bar that opened at eleven for fishermen and stayed open until the fishermen stopped being customers and started being furniture. Junie brought her letters in a biscuit tin. Teo came in moving carefully, his walk still carrying the sea's argument with his body, and sat with his back to the wall where he could see the door. Ruth locked it behind us, which in the Netmender was less a security measure than a statement of intent.

I laid everything out on the table between the salt shakers and an ashtray nobody had used since the smoking ban: the orders, the logbook page, the strongroom documents photographed page by page on my phone, the ledger pages Ruth had pulled from her own records showing Crane's payments flowing into accounts that matched Teo's debts, and — last — the copy of the harbor charter with Crane's financing clauses highlighted in yellow.

"Here's where we are," I said. "In two days, the sixth tide opens the cave for the last time this season. After that, the Meridian is sealed behind rock and water until the next anomaly tide, if there ever is one. Crane knows it too. That's why he took the skiff. That's why he gave us the ultimatum. He doesn't need to beat us to the wreck anymore. He just needs us tired enough to trade."

"Then let's not be tired," Teo said.

"We can't beat him to the wreck either." I turned the photographs face up. "Even if we could get past his fleet, even if the cave didn't kill us, we can't move that gold. There are four of us and a skiff he already owns. The math is insulting. So I'm done doing math that insults me."

Junie leaned forward. "Ada. What are you saying?"

"I'm saying we give him what he asked for." I slid the folder toward the middle of the table. "The documents. All of them. On the sixth tide, exactly like he demanded."

The silence lasted three full seconds before Ruth said, flatly, "No."

"Wait—"

"Ada. Those papers are the only thing standing between Crane and everything else. You hand them over, he burns them, and by Friday my harbor charter is a footnote and your father is a liar again, permanently, with documentation."

"Those would be copies."

Ruth stopped. Teo's eyebrows went up slowly, the way they did when he was underwater and something moved in the edge of his vision.

"Copies," I said again. "Good ones. I can age paper. I grew up with a forger's library and nothing but time — Dad thought document work was a survival skill, same as knots. Crane gets his folder, he takes it back to his ship, he verifies it against whatever his grandfather's records tell him to expect, and while he's doing that—" I picked up my phone, then set it down and put my hand flat on the table instead, because some things you should say looking at people. "While he's doing that, we broadcast the originals. Marine band sixteen. Open channel. Every boat from here to Ketchikan hears the whole thing read aloud — the writ, the scuttling order, the payments, the theft of relief funds meant for towns exactly like this one. Every coastal town that's been paying Crane's moorage fees and buying his fuel and losing their charters to his debt clauses hears it in his own ancestors' handwriting. Before he knows what's happening. Before he can bury it."

Junie made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "You want to read our family's worst secret to the entire coast."

"I want to read *his* family's worst secret to the entire coast. Ours comes with it. That's the price, and I'm telling you now so nobody can say I hid it." I looked at her and made myself hold it. "Dad scuttled that ship, Junie. Under orders, legally, but the towns that lost that relief money don't care about writs. They'll hear 'Voss sank the Meridian' and that's the part they'll keep. We clear his name with the maritime board and maybe half the coast believes us. Maybe half."

"And the other half?" Junie asked quietly.

"The other half keeps calling him what they've always called him. But Crane's finished either way. Because the money — the stolen relief funds, the fraud, the debt scheme — that part isn't ambiguous. That part has numbers."

Ruth had gone very still, in the way she did when she was running harbor law through her head at speed. "Marine band sixteen is monitored by the Coast Guard."

"Yes."

"So the moment you start reading, every cutter in the region knows where you are, and so does Crane, and he will come for you with everything that floats."

"That's why it happens on the sixth tide, inside the channel, when his fleet has to bunch up to follow us in. Narrow water. His big boats are useless in there. And by the time he understands what we're doing, it's already been said. You can't unread a broadcast. You can't unhear it in three hundred wheelhouses."

Teo whistled softly through his teeth. "It's insane."

"It's insane," I agreed.

"It might also work." He rubbed his jaw, where the bruising from the bends had finally faded to yellow. "Crane's whole play is control. He buys boats, buys debts, buys charters, buys silence. He has never once in his life operated in a room he couldn't lock. You're proposing we do this in the one room on the coast that belongs to everybody."

"That's the idea."

"And if he catches us before we finish reading?" Junie said. "If he boards us mid-broadcast?"

"Then we're four people in a skiff against a salvage fleet, and I won't lie to you about those odds." I looked around the table. "But here's what I know. He offered to buy my father's compass because, in his words, it belonged with the rest of his mistakes. He thinks my father was a mistake. An error he's still cleaning up, forty years later, because somewhere in his grandfather's papers there's a loose end with a Voss signature on it. Men like Crane don't chase loose ends unless the end matters. And if it matters to him, it matters to every town he's ever squeezed. This isn't just about clearing Dad. It's about the fact that the money in that strongroom was *ours*. Was *everybody's*. Hospitals and schools and harbor repairs, paid for by coastal towns, stolen before it ever arrived, and the theft dressed up as a shipwreck legend so nobody would go looking." I heard my own voice rise and made myself bring it down. "He gets his copies. The coast gets the truth. And the gold stays where it is, because the moment we touch it, we're salvagers, and salvagers can be bought, sued, and buried in court for a decade. But a story told out loud on an open channel? That belongs to whoever heard it."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Outside, gulls were working the tide line, screaming about something.

"It's the plan nobody likes," Ruth said finally.

"I know."

"No, I mean — write that down. Name it that. Because when it goes wrong, and parts of it will go wrong, I want it on record that we knew." She pushed back her chair and stood, and for a second I thought she was going to leave. Instead she put her hand flat on the folder of copies-to-be. "I've kept this harbor alive for twenty-two years by never gambling the whole harbor on anything. One charter, one season, one storm at a time. And I'm about to vote to gamble all of it." She looked at each of us in turn. "Before I do, I want it said plainly: Ada, if this fails, Crane takes the charter, the town loses its moorage, half these families lose their livelihoods, and it will have been your plan. Not ours. Yours. Can you carry that?"

The question sat on the table like a weight. I thought about my father's compass, pointing at nothing now, or at everything. I thought about the way the needle had swung toward the cave like it was called home, and how home, it turned out, was a grave with a fortune in it that was never ours to take.

"Yeah," I said. "I can carry that. I've been carrying worse for years. It's called his name."

Junie reached across the table and put her hand over mine. Her daughter's surgery fund was part of this too — the towns' fund, the one Ruth dreamed up, which needed the story to survive in order to exist. "I vote yes," she said. "Not because it's smart. Because Crane's had my fear for a month now, and I want it back."

Teo exhaled. "My vote doesn't count as much as my lungs do, and my lungs say no. But—" he glanced at the door, at the town beyond it, at whatever arithmetic a man runs when he's spent his soul and is shopping for a new one, "—I've worked for Crane. I know what winning looks like on his side of the table. It looks like everyone else losing quietly, forever. I vote yes."

Ruth closed her eyes. When she opened them, she said, "Aye. God help us. Aye."

Three to nothing, with mine uncounted, because I'd learned from Ruth long ago that the person proposing the gamble shouldn't get to vote on it.

"Then we've got two days," I said, gathering the papers. "Junie, you're on the copies — your handwriting's better than mine and steadier under pressure. Teo, I need the channel depths from memory, every rock between the mouth and the mast, because we're going in on the sixth tide with Crane's whole fleet breathing down our necks. Ruth—"

"I know what I am," she said grimly. "I'm the one who tells the town. If we're going to burn the truth across the airwaves, this town hears it first, from me, tonight, before Crane's dock-walkers hear it from a radio. Loyalty that arrives late isn't loyalty."

She unlocked the door, and the noise of the Netmender's lunch crowd poured in — laughter, the clatter of plates, a jukebox playing something about ships that never come home. Ordinary sounds. The sound of everything we were about to bet.

I was halfway through the door when Ruth caught my arm.

"One more thing," she said, low. "The man watching the skiff this morning. He wasn't reading. I got close enough to see. He was sketching — the plane. Your floatplane. Registration number and all."

I felt the cold arrive in my stomach ahead of the understanding. Crane didn't just want the documents. He wanted to make sure that when the sixth tide came, there was only one way any of us could leave that cave — and it wasn't through the sky.

"Get the copies done fast," Ruth said. "I think he's planning to ground you."
