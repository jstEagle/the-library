## 19. Sixth Tide: The Wreck

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and we were already in the water.

I'd flown the last reconnaissance run at midnight — a stupid, beautiful flight through a sky full of ragged cloud, the compass on its lanyard around my neck swinging steady as a heartbeat toward the cave mouth below. Crane's fleet sat anchored in a line across the outer bay like a jury waiting for a verdict. Three vessels, running lights burning, generators humming. He wasn't hiding anymore. Hiding was over.

"Skiff's ready," Ruth said when I waded back to the dock. She was loading the document case into the bilge with the care of a woman handling something that could detonate. Which, in a way, it could. "Teo's checked his gear twice. Junie's in the bow pretending she isn't terrified."

"She's good at that."

"We're all good at that." Ruth looked at me across the gray water. "Last chance to not do this."

"The tide doesn't wait, and neither does he."

"No," she said. "But I want it on record that I offered."

I took the case from her. Inside were the copies — pages I'd spent two nights aging with tea and salt air until they looked like they'd spent forty years in a strongroom, my father's signature forged by my own hand so many times I could have signed his name on a bank loan. The originals were already gone. At 3 a.m., while the others slept, I'd keyed the marine band and read every word of the captain's sealed orders and the relief-fund manifests out to the coast, voice steady, letting the whole dark water hear what Silas Crane's grandfather had done. Somewhere out there, a hundred radios had crackled awake. By now the story was loose in the world like a bird you can't call back.

That was the plan nobody liked. Give Crane the bait, let him think he'd won, and make sure that whatever he did next, he did it in front of witnesses.

Now all we had to do was survive him.

* * *

The Throat swallowed us at first light.

I ran the channel the way Teo had taught me — throttle high through the surge, then cut hard left where the rock shelf dropped away, riding the back of the swell like it was a living thing under us. Junie whooped once, involuntary, and then went silent as the walls closed in. The cave opened around us, black water going green, going gold, the light from the entrance shrinking behind us to a coin.

And there it was. The Meridian.

Not the mast this time — the hull, exposed by six days of our work and the lowest tide in a decade, her ribs rising out of the shallows like the carcass of some enormous animal. We'd spent three tides hauling silt and stone away from the strongroom hatch. It stood open now. Teo had rigged lights down inside. The gold was still there — I could see it from the skiff, dull yellow stacks in the glow, more money than our whole town would earn in a generation.

It looked exactly like what it was. Stolen relief funds. Money meant for medicine and harbors and schoolhouses, sitting in the dark for forty years while towns like ours scraped by.

"Crane's coming," Junie said. She was watching the entrance, binoculars up. "Two skiffs. Maybe eight men."

"Right on schedule." I killed the engine. "Everyone remember the vote?"

Nobody answered, which meant yes. The vote had been four to nothing, but the nothing had been loud.

We waited. That was the hardest part — sitting in the green silence with the tide clock running in my head, listening to the echo of oars and outboards grow louder in the throat of the cave. Ruth checked the flare gun twice. Teo sat with his hands flat on his knees, breathing slow, the way he breathed before a dive past safe limits. His face was still wrong from the bends — a slackness on one side that might never fully leave. He'd bought back his soul with blood, and the interest was still accruing.

The first skiff rounded the inner shelf, and there he was.

Silas Crane stood in the bow like a man arriving at his own coronation. Gray coat, dry despite the spray, hands folded. Behind him, his men fanned out along the rocks with the bored competence of people who'd done ugly things before and expected to do them again.

"Miss Voss." His voice carried off the stone. "You're punctual. Your father never was."

"He kept different hours than thieves."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger. Appetite. "The documents."

I held up the case. "The harbor charter first. Signed release. Then you get these, and we walk out, and everyone goes home rich or at least alive."

He gestured, and one of his men produced a paper. Ruth read it by flashlight, twice, then nodded at me. Crane watched me the whole time with the patience of a man who had already counted the money.

I tossed the case. He caught it against his chest, and for one unguarded moment his composure cracked open and I saw underneath — forty years of family hunger, a fortune built on a theft, an heir who'd spent his whole life trying to finish a crime his grandfather started. He opened the case right there in the bow, wet fingers on old paper, reading.

I watched him read. I watched him get to the third page, where the ink ran just slightly wrong, where the paper was too new under its tea-stain, where a man who'd studied these documents his whole life would finally see what everyone else had missed.

His head came up slowly.

"These are copies."

"They're very good copies," I said. "I made them myself."

"You—" He stopped. Recalibrated. And then he smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen in six tides, worse than the surge channel, worse than the storm. Because it was calm. "You broadcast them. Last night. On the band." He said it like a man confirming a diagnosis. "Yes. My radio operator heard. Of course you did." He folded the forgery closed with terrible gentleness. "Well. It doesn't matter what the coast hears. Documents can be disputed. Stories can be buried. What matters is possession." He looked past me, at the strongroom, at the gold glowing in the dark. "And I'm standing on it."

"Tide turns in twenty minutes," Ruth said flatly. "You'll drown trying to haul it out."

"I've drowned better men for less."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do.

Crane's men moved — not toward us, but toward the entrance. Toward the throat. And I understood one second too late what he'd brought the second skiff's cargo for: cases of demolition charges, salvage explosives, the kind of shaped charge used to break apart wrecks. Not to blast the strongroom open.

To blast the throat shut.

"You can't seal a cave with us in it," I shouted. "There are witnesses—"

"There's no one in here but four salvagers caught in an unstable passage when the tide came in early," Crane said. "A tragedy. A Voss tragedy, fittingly. The sea keeps what it takes." He nodded to his man. "Set them at the narrows. Low charge. Just enough rock."

They worked quickly, professionals, wedging charges into the seams of the passage where the cave pinched down before the entrance. The tide was already pushing in behind them, water climbing the walls, and I did the math I didn't want to do: even if the charges only partially collapsed the throat, the surge would finish it. We'd be sealed in a tomb with forty years of stolen gold and six hours of air.

Ruth fired the flare — not at Crane, at the roof, a red starburst that lit the whole cavern like judgment. It wouldn't bring anyone. There was no one to bring. The nearest boat that might have heard the story on the band was hours out, and Crane knew it, and I knew he knew it.

Then Teo stood up.

He didn't say anything dramatic. He just picked up the spare charge case — the one Crane's men hadn't claimed — and walked along the ledge toward the narrows, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who has already decided how he dies if it comes to that.

"Teo," I said.

"Rock speaks to you after a while," he said, not turning around. "Forty years underwater, it gets chatty. This ceiling—" he touched the stone above the narrows, above the charges Crane's men had set, "—it wants to come down anyway. All I'm doing is choosing which way."

"You'll bring half the cave with it."

"Half the cave lands on their charges." He glanced back at me, and the slack side of his face almost smiled. "Get everyone behind the strongroom wall. It's granite. It'll hold."

"It's your soul, Teo. You bought it back."

"Yeah," he said. "Time to spend it."

* * *

I got Ruth and Junie behind the strongroom wall because arguing with Teo Marsh about rock was like arguing with the tide about direction. From cover, I watched him work — placing the counter-charge high, in the seam he'd been studying since the first tide, muttering to the stone like an old friend he owed money to. Crane's men were still setting theirs low in the narrows, and for thirty seconds neither side seemed to understand what the other was doing.

Crane understood first.

"Stop him," he said, and his men turned, and by then it was too late for that too.

Teo blew the ceiling.

The sound was enormous — not a bang but a compression, the whole cave inhaling, and then the rock came down exactly the way he'd promised: a curtain of stone across the narrows, burying Crane's charges unfired, sealing the passage from the inside. Dust filled the green water white. The skiff rocked hard. Somewhere in the roar I heard Crane screaming orders that no longer mattered, because his exit was gone, and his gold was behind a wall of collapsed granite that no salvage crew in the world would shift before the season closed — maybe ever. The Meridian's cargo sank back into the dark it had kept for forty years, and this time it would keep it.

The dust settled. The tide kept rising, patient as arithmetic.

Through the thinning haze I could see Crane in the failing light of his own lamps, standing on rock that used to be a doorway, staring at the wall where his fortune had just died. His men were shouting about the water level. The cave had other mouths — old channels, air pockets — but none a skiff could use, and Crane knew it, and for the first time since the fuel dock, Silas Crane looked small.

"Miss Voss," he called across the water, and his voice had lost its polish entirely. "You realize we're all entombed together."

"The locals know this cave," I called back. "Fishermen's families have known it for a hundred years. There's a chimney on the north wall. Tight, but it breathes. We'll be drinking coffee on Ruth's dock by noon." I let that land. "You're welcome to climb it with us. Or stay with your gold. Your choice. You've always been so good at choices."

Behind me, Junie laughed — a shaky, disbelieving sound, the laugh of a woman who'd tipped off a criminal to save her daughter and had just watched the criminal lose everything anyway. Ruth put her hand on my shoulder and squeezed once, which from Ruth was a parade.

Teo climbed back over the rubble toward us, gray-faced, ears bleeding, grinning with half his mouth.

"Told you," he said. "Rock talks."

* * *

We went up the chimney single file, Ruth first, then Junie, then me, then Teo, hauling ourselves hand over hand through a crack in the earth that smelled of salt and ancient air. Below us, faintly, we could hear Crane's men arguing, and Crane's voice cutting through them, and then — this was the part I'd remember — the sound of a man beginning to bargain with the sea itself, offering it things it had never wanted.

At the top, we spilled out onto the cliff grass in the full morning light, gasping, filthy, alive. The bay spread out below us, and Crane's fleet sat at anchor, and already I could see movement on the decks — radio masts being worked, flags signaling, the first tremors of a fleet learning that its captain's claim had just been buried under a hundred tons of granite along with the proof of everything he'd stolen.

Junie sat down hard in the grass and put her face in her hands. Ruth stood watch at the cliff edge like she didn't trust the morning not to betray us too. Teo lay flat on his back, bleeding gently from both ears, laughing at the sky.

I stood there with the wind coming off the water and felt the weight lift and the weight settle, both at once. The gold was gone. The debt was still real. My father's name was out there now in a hundred galleys and wheelhouses, being argued over by people who'd never met him — scuttler, hero, thief, guardian, all of it true, none of it clean.

And down the coast road, small in the distance, a truck was coming toward the strip. Fast. With a light bar.

Ruth saw it the same moment I did. "That's the maritime board's vehicle," she said. "Somebody filed something."

"Somebody did," I said. "Three weeks ago. A dead man's real record. Time somebody official finally read it."

The truck kicked up dust all the way to the fence line, and I started walking down to meet it.
