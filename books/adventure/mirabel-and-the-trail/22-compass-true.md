## 22. Compass True

The paint came off in long gray ribbons, curling away from the aluminum like old skin.

Ada worked the heat gun in slow passes along the fuselage of the *Kestrel*, her father's floatplane, her floatplane now — had been hers for eleven years, though she'd never once touched the paint. The letters on the tail read VOSS AIR CARGO in a font that hadn't existed since before she was born, and beneath them, smaller, half worn to ghost: EST. 1974. Her father had bought the plane two years after the Meridian went down. She'd always assumed he'd chosen the name himself. It wasn't until Ruth pulled the harbor registry last month that Ada learned the truth: he'd bought it from the estate of a man who'd drowned in the inlet, and kept the name because the dead man's widow asked him to.

Her father had been full of things like that. Small kindnesses nobody saw, buried under the one large failure everybody did.

The heat gun hissed. A ribbon of gray peeled free and spiraled down into the grass, and Ada watched it go with something that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite relief and was probably both wearing each other's clothes.

"You're going to burn through to bare metal if you keep daydreaming."

Junie stood at the edge of the strip with two paper cups of coffee and Maren on her hip, the girl's surgical scar still pink at her collarbone, three weeks old and already forgotten by its owner, who was more interested in trying to eat a dragonfly. Ada killed the heat gun and took the coffee.

"I'm not daydreaming. I'm deciding."

"On what?"

"What color."

Junie looked at the plane — stripped now to raw aluminum from nose to tail, gleaming dully under a sky so clean it looked rinsed. "It's kind of beautiful like that."

"It is." Ada ran a hand along the skin. It was warm from the gun and warmer from the sun. "But bare metal shows every dent. Every repair. There's a patch over the left wing spar where Dad landed on a gravel bar in '89 that I'm pretty sure was not a landing so much as a controlled crash."

"So paint it white," Junie said. "Let them see."

Ada looked at her cousin. Six weeks ago Junie had stood on this same strip and told her that their father had been paid to move the Meridian, and Ada had called her a liar, and the space between them had been wide enough to fly a plane through sideways. Now Junie stood close enough to hand over coffee, Maren's small weight balanced on her hip like it belonged there, like everything belonged where it belonged, finally.

"White," Ada said. "Yeah. Okay."

* * *

The maritime board office in Ketchikan occupied the ground floor of a building that smelled like diesel and old carpet, and the clerk behind the counter had the particular patience of a man who processed other people's histories for a living and had stopped being moved by any of them sometime in the previous century.

"Amendment to record?" he said. "Vessel name?"

"Not a vessel. A pilot. Elias Voss. Incident report 88-114, the loss of the freighter Meridian, October 1976." Ada set a document wallet on the counter. "I'm filing supplementary evidence."

The clerk looked at the wallet, then at her, then at the wallet again. "Supplementary evidence is reviewed by the board. Could take months."

"I know."

"There's a fee for—"

"I know that too." She'd sold the second fuel drum to cover it. She didn't mention that.

He opened the wallet. Inside were photocopies — the originals were already in the hands of three different newspapers and the state attorney general, because Ada Voss had learned something about single points of failure — of the captain's sealed orders recovered from the Meridian's cabin, Teo's blood-bought prize from the fifth tide. Elias Voss's signature on the receipt line. The authorization above it, signed by the shipping ministry, ordering him to scuttle the vessel rather than let its cargo — stolen relief funds, money skimmed from every coastal town from here to the border — fall into the hands of the Meridian's own owners. The Crane family. Four generations of fortune built on money meant for schoolhouses and medicine and winter fuel, and one bush pilot who'd been paid to hide the evidence and had instead put it at the bottom of the sea and carried the blame for forty years without saying a word, because the people who could have cleared him were the people who would have destroyed the towns he flew for.

The clerk read for a long time. When he looked up, his century-old patience had cracked somewhere around the middle.

"This says your father—"

"Sank his own ship on purpose," Ada said. "To keep them from getting the money back and burying the theft. Yes."

"And he never—" The clerk gestured vaguely, helplessly. "Forty years. He never filed anything?"

"He couldn't. The orders were sealed; producing them would have ended his license and probably his life, given whose money it was. And if he'd gone public, the Cranes' lawyers would have buried the towns in litigation until the relief funds were a rounding error." She shrugged. "So he flew cargo instead. Quietly. For towns like mine. I used to think it was penance."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was the mission." She tapped the wallet. "He couldn't save the money. So he saved the truth, and he waited for someone to come find it."

The clerk stamped the filing. The sound was very small for what it did.

* * *

They painted the Kestrel white on a Tuesday, all four of them — Ada, Junie, Ruth Okafor in a harbormaster's polo she claimed was formal wear, and Teo Marsh, who showed up with a roller and a cane and a grin that said the doctors had cleared him for light duty and he intended to interpret *light* as generously as possible.

"White's a mistake," Teo announced, dipping his roller. "Every bird within fifty miles is going to think you're an egg."

"Every creditor within fifty miles is going to think I've got nothing to hide," Ada said. "Which, for the first time in my adult life, is true."

Ruth rolled a stripe along the tail — she'd insisted on doing the tail herself, and nobody argued with Ruth about anything anymore, not after the charter hearing, not after she'd stood up in front of the port authority with the marine-band transcript in her fist and read Crane's own voice back to him in a room full of the towns he'd tried to foreclose on. The financing had collapsed inside a week. The debt holders had withdrawn their claims against Ada's strip with a speed that suggested they'd never really wanted the strip, only the silence of the woman who held it.

"You heard from him?" Ruth asked, casual in the way that meant not casual at all.

"Crane? Once. A letter." Ada worked her roller up over the wing root. "No return address. One sentence."

"What'd it say?"

"*You should have taken the gold.*"

Teo snorted. "Charming man."

"He's not wrong," Ada said. "There's a fortune under that rock face. Enough to buy this strip ten times over, pay off every pilot on the coast, fund Maren's surgery and her kids' surgeries too." She stepped back and looked at the wet white spreading across the fuselage, clean as new snow. "And it's staying there. Not because I'm noble. Because the moment I went back for it, everything I said on that broadcast becomes negotiable. He'd have won anyway. Just slower."

Ruth nodded slowly. "Your dad would've gone back for it."

"No," Ada said, and surprised herself with how sure she was. "My dad spent forty years proving he wouldn't. That's the whole point of him. That's the part I got wrong my entire life." She rolled another pass of white. "I thought he was a coward who ran from his mistake. He was a man who chose the harder thing and shut up about it. Those look identical from the outside. They are not the same."

Nobody said anything for a while. The rollers made their soft wet sounds. Maren chased a raven across the grass, laughing, and the raven tolerated it with the weary dignity of a bird who had seen many children come and go.

When the last coat was on and they stood back, the Kestrel sat on the strip blazing white against the green, and on the tail, in letters Junie had stenciled by hand, it said simply:

**KESTREL**

No company name. No establishment date. No inheritance.

Just the bird.

* * *

The compass hung on a nail above Ada's workbench for six days while the paint cured, and every morning she looked at it and every morning it did nothing.

That was the strange part. For her whole life — for her whole *life*, since she was nine years old and her father closed her small fingers around it and said *this will point you home when nothing else does* — the needle had behaved like something with an opinion. It had swung toward the cave mouth on the lowest tide like a dog hearing its name. It had trembled on her wrist in the storm flight over Crane's fleet. She had worn it like an apology, like a sentence, like a sonar ping from a man thirty years drowned, and she had believed — right up until the sixth tide closed over the cave and took the gold and the wreck and the whole impossible story back into the dark — that it was pointing at something.

On the seventh morning she took it off the nail and held it level in the kitchen light.

The needle swung once, twice, settled. Pointed north. Just north. No pull, no tremble, no opinion at all. It pointed north the way a hammer points at nails: because that was its job, and it was done waiting for her to figure out hers.

She laughed out loud, alone in the kitchen, and it came out closer to a sob than she'd have admitted to anyone.

"You old liar," she said to her father, to the compass, to both. "It was never pointing anywhere. You just wanted me to follow it far enough to find out what I'd do when I got there."

She put it in her shirt pocket, over her heart, where it had always lived. Some things you don't retire. You just stop asking them questions they can't answer.

* * *

Clearance came through on a Friday. The amended record — accepted, expedited, the board citing, in bureaucratese that barely concealed its own excitement, *newly surfaced documentary evidence materially exonerating the pilot of record*. Forty years of *negligent loss of vessel* struck through and replaced with *scuttled under ministerial authority to prevent misappropriation of public funds*. Recommended posthumous commendation, pending.

Pending. Fine. Her father had waited forty years; he could wait through one more committee.

She loaded the Kestrel at dawn: medicine for the upper inlets, mail, a crate of engine parts for the Widow Halloway's generator, and — tucked behind the seat where it couldn't bother anybody — a thick envelope of letters that Junie had finally handed over, all of them, their father's real handwriting, unburned, unsanitized. Ada had read them twice. She'd cry about the third one later, on some ridge or other, alone, the way he would have hated and understood.

Ruth was on the dock when she taxied out, coffee in hand, Maren's raven — it had apparently adopted the harbor — sulking on a piling behind her.

"Upper inlets?" Ruth called over the wind.

"Medicine and rumors," Ada called back, and grinned. "Mostly rumors. The medicine's just ballast."

"Bring me some good ones!"

"When have I ever brought you anything else?"

She ran up the checks, eased the throttle forward, and the Kestrel lifted off the water clean and light, white hull flashing, the whole bay unrolling beneath her like a chart with no marks on it yet. The fog was gone. It had been gone for weeks — the meteorologists blamed a high-pressure ridge, but Ada knew better. Fog was just weather with something to hide, and the coast had finally told the truth.

At five hundred feet she leveled off and checked the compass out of habit.

North. Just north. Steady as a heartbeat, pointing at nothing but the horizon, which was, when you thought about it, the same thing.

Behind her: the town, the harbor, the cave under the water holding its secret like a held breath, the whole story settled at last into the shape it was always going to take. Ahead of her: three hundred miles of inlets, a dozen strips, a hundred people who'd learn by tonight that Elias Voss's daughter was flying again — flying *clean*, no debts, no ghosts in the tail number, nothing owed to anyone but the weather.

Ada Voss banked south first, because the upper inlets were south, and the compass needle swung obediently to follow, and she felt it happen — the moment the instrument stopped being an oracle and became what it had always been. A tool. A good one. Made by people, carried by people, trusted by choice.

She keyed the radio out of habit, then hesitated, then keyed it again.

"Any station, any station, this is Kestrel, five miles south of Port Leland, climbing through eight hundred. Clear skies all the way to the border." A pause. She smiled. "If anyone's listening — the water's fine. Come on out."

She clicked off, pushed the nose toward the sun, and flew north anyway.
