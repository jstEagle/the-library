## 1. The Debt Collector Smiles

The storm came in off the Serrano Shelf like something with an opinion about Gideon Quintana specifically.

She'd been reading its mood since dawn — the green-black bruise on the horizon, the way the gulls went quiet and inland all at once, the barometer aboard the *Patient Answer* falling so fast it seemed to be trying to escape through the bottom of the dial. A front like this one didn't just blow through. It rearranged things. It pried loose what the sea had been holding, dragged moorings off their pins, cracked hulls against breakwaters, and scattered cargo across the shallows like a drunk emptying his pockets.

To everyone else in Port Calloway, that was a reason to batten down and pray.

To Gideon, it was payday.

"Front's making twelve knots," she said, one hand on the wheel, the other holding a mug of coffee that had gone cold an hour ago and tasted like it knew it. "It'll hit the Vireo shoals around dusk. Anything loose out there is going to end up on the sandbars by morning."

Okafor stood at the rail beside her, a mountain of a man wearing a rain slicker two sizes too small because he refused to spend money on clothes when there were engine parts to buy. He looked at the sky the way other men looked at open graves.

"That's not weather," he said. "That's a debt coming due."

"Everything's a debt coming due." She drained the coffee anyway. "Might as well be the kind that pays us."

He grunted — Okafor's full range of disagreement, deployed only when he knew he'd already lost the argument — and went aft to check the winch lines. The *Patient Answer* was forty years old if she was a day, a salvage trawler with a hull like a scarred knuckle and an engine that ran on prayer and diesel in roughly equal measure. Gideon had bought her at nineteen with money she hadn't had, from a man who'd smiled while he counted her inexperience, and she'd been paying for that smile ever since.

The harbor fell away behind them. Ahead, the water changed color first — that strange bruised olive where the front's outriders touched the surface — and then the wind arrived, shoving the trawler's stern around until Gideon corrected, feeling the old girl lean into it like a tired horse remembering it once loved to run.

This was the part she loved. She'd admit it to no one, but this was the part. The moment before the chaos, when the air went electric and the whole gray world narrowed down to a line of black cloud and the sound of her own engine answering it. Storm chasers were a rare breed on the Calloway coast, and most of them were idiots with cameras. Gideon chased for the morning after — for the flotsam, the grounded wrecks, the containers split open on the bars with their contents glinting up through six feet of green water. Salvage fees. Wreck rights. Enough to keep the Syndicate's collectors at arm's length for another month.

Enough to keep running.

The thought arrived uninvited, the way it always did when the wind picked up, and she shoved it down the way she always did. There'd be time for arithmetic later. Right now there was only the front, and the front was beautiful, and beauty on this coast always meant somebody somewhere was having the worst day of their life.

"Dusk," she said to the wheel, to the wind, to nobody. "We work the Vireos at first light."

The first drops hit the deck like thrown gravel.

* * *

They never made the shoals.

The front caught them twenty minutes out — faster than the forecast, meaner than the charts suggested, because the sea had spent three days cooking under a heat haze and now intended to settle accounts. Rain came sideways. Lightning walked across the water on white legs, close enough that Gideon could smell the ozone mixing with salt spray, and the *Patient Answer* climbed swells that kept growing after they should have stopped, the way bad news does.

Gideon flew her. That was the only word for it. Hands light on the wheel, eyes reading the water three seconds before it happened, taking the waves at the angle that turned violence into choreography. Okafor lashed himself to the rail and watched her do it with the expression of a man watching someone juggle knives who has long since stopped being able to look away.

"We should run for cover!" he shouted over the wind.

"And lose our position on the shoals!"

"You're going to drown us for a position!"

"I'm going to *feed us* for a position! There's a difference! It's mostly timing!"

Lightning struck the water close enough to turn the world white, and in that white flash, Gideon saw something that made her blood go cold in a way the storm never could.

A boat. Running lights burning steady through the rain, dead ahead, sitting in the teeth of the front like it had every right to be there. No sane vessel anchored itself in the path of a line squall. No sane vessel sat broadside to the swell with its engines idling, waiting.

Waiting for them.

She knew the silhouette before the next flash confirmed it. Sleek, black-hulled, a motor yacht built less for sailing than for arriving — the kind of boat that cost more than the entire Calloway fishing fleet combined. The kind of boat that belonged to people whose business was conducted in boardrooms and concluded in harbors.

The kind of boat that belonged to the Vane Syndicate.

"Oh, you have got to be joking," Gideon said.

"What?" Okafor followed her gaze. His face did something complicated. "No. No, no. Not here. Not *now*."

"They've been looking for me for six weeks."

"So lose them! You're the best helm on this coast!"

She was, and they both knew it, and it wouldn't matter. A trawler doing eight knots in a gale could not outrun a yacht doing twenty in the same gale, and the Syndicate's captains didn't lose quarry because losing quarry reflected poorly on their continued employment. Besides — and this was the thing that sat in her chest like swallowed ice — the yacht wasn't chasing. It was simply *there*, positioned on the only sensible line to the Vireo shoals, patient as a toll gate.

Mr. Lark had found her again. Mr. Lark always found her again.

* * *

They came alongside in the lee of the front's trailing edge, where the rain had thinned to a miserable drizzle and the swell still rolled long and heavy. A gangway plank swung across the gap between vessels with practiced ease, and Gideon stood at her own rail with her arms crossed and her jaw set, watching the man come down it.

Mr. Lark crossed a heaving deck the way other men crossed a ballroom. He was tall and silver-templed and dressed in a charcoal coat that probably cost more than the *Patient Answer*'s engine, and he wore the expression of a man perpetually delighted to see you — genuinely delighted, or so perfectly performed that the difference stopped mattering years ago. He carried a leather folio under one arm and an umbrella he hadn't opened, because getting rained on, for Lark, was apparently a form of networking.

"Captain Quintana." He said her name like a toast. "You know, I've been chasing storms my whole career, and I've never once enjoyed the weather. And yet here you are, sailing *toward* it. It's inspiring. It really is."

"Lark."

"Gideon. May I?" He gestured at her wheelhouse without waiting for permission, climbing the steps with the agility of a much younger man. He looked around the cramped cabin — the charts, the cold stove, the coffee mugs, the barometer with its needle pinned low — with the fond appraisal of a man touring a museum exhibit titled *Poverty, Maritime*. "She's held together beautifully. I've always said so. There's character in a ship like this. Character and, I'd wager, about eleven more months of hull life if the welding doesn't get attention."

"Why are you here?"

"Can't a friend drop by?"

"You're not my friend. My friends don't charge interest."

Lark laughed — a real laugh, warm, unguarded, which was somehow worse than a fake one. He settled against the chart table as though he owned it, opened the folio, and produced a single sheet of paper, which he laid flat with both hands, smoothing the corners like a man setting a table for an honored guest.

"Forty thousand marks," he said gently. "Principal and accrued. As of this morning."

The number landed in the room and sat there. Gideon had heard it before, many times, in many tones — shouted through a closed office door, murmured over a bar top, printed in letters that got redder each month. But Lark said it the way a doctor says a diagnosis. Kindly. Factly. With the terrible intimacy of a man who has delivered it enough times to stop pretending it might change.

"The Vane Syndicate values its relationships," Lark went on. "That's not a platitude, Gideon, it's policy. We would rather have partners than corpses. Corpses don't repay. Partners—" he tapped the paper, once, softly, "—partners build fleets."

"I'm working on it. The season's turning. There'll be salvage—"

"There's always salvage. There's always next month. I've heard 'next month' from you for fourteen consecutive months, and do you know what fascinates me? Every single time, I believed you meant it." He smiled, and the smile reached his eyes, and that was the horror of Mr. Lark: everything reached his eyes. "You're not a liar, Captain. You're an optimist. It's your best quality and it's going to be the death of you."

Outside, the last of the front grumbled away toward the shoals, taking Gideon's payday with it. She could see the black clouds retreating, lit from within, and she felt the loss physically — a whole night's wreck-fees dissolving into the dark while a debt collector stood in her wheelhouse drinking nothing and wanting nothing and owning everything.

"What do you want, Lark?"

"An extension." He let that word hang, watching her face. "One final extension. Sixty days. In exchange, the Syndicate asks for a small consideration."

"There it is."

"There it is," he agreed pleasantly. "Nothing dramatic. We simply ask that you make yourself findable. No more changing berths at odd hours. No more selling fuel rights to third parties to obscure your position. No more—" he consulted the folio with theatrical care, "—hiring your cousin's boy to answer your radio and claim you're 'out past the shelf, back never.' Which, incidentally, was quite funny. I liked the boy."

Gideon's hands had curled into fists without her permission. She forced them open. Lark noticed — he noticed everything — and his smile softened into something almost apologetic.

"You understand my position," he said. "I'm fond of you. Professionally speaking. You're the most interesting account on my ledger. But the ledger doesn't care what I'm fond of, and above me there are men who read numbers the way you read weather fronts, and right now your number reads *unsecured*. Forty thousand marks, secured against a forty-year-old trawler worth perhaps nine, captained by a woman with no family assets, no property, no—" he paused, delicately, "—no known next of kin worth mentioning."

Something moved behind Gideon's ribs at that. *No known next of kin.* Tomas would laugh himself sick, wherever he was. Or maybe he wouldn't. Maybe he'd gone quiet too.

"So this is the threat," she said. "Be findable, or—"

"No, no. You misunderstand me completely, and I take that as my failure, because I pride myself on clarity." Lark straightened, closed the folio, and for a moment — just a moment — the delight drained out of his face, and what remained underneath was older and tireder and far more frightening. "There's no threat tonight, Gideon. There's a courtesy. The men above me have begun to discuss you in the plural. Do you know what that means? When they say your name, other names follow. *Alternatives.* I have bought you sixty days because I am very good at my job and slightly better at liking you. But I can't buy you a second sixty days. Nobody can. After that, the conversation stops being about debt."

The rain ticked against the wheelhouse glass. Somewhere below, Okafor's boots creaked on the deck plates, staying deliberately within earshot.

"Sixty days," Gideon said.

"Sixty days. Findable. Working. Visible." Lark tucked the folio under his arm and moved toward the door, then paused on the threshold, silhouetted against the wet gray light, and turned back with the warmth fully restored to his face, like a lamp relit. "For what it's worth — and I know it isn't worth anything, that's rather the problem — I hope you pull it off. I hope some storm hands you a fortune. You chase them hard enough. Statistically, the sea owes you."

He went down the gangway, crossed the black yacht's deck, and was gone, and the yacht itself drew away with obscene smoothness, unhurried, as though the ocean had been asked politely to cooperate and had agreed.

Gideon stood at the wheel a long time after it vanished into the murk.

* * *

They worked the Vireo shoals at first light anyway, because the alternative was sitting in harbor counting days she no longer had. The storm had done its work overnight — a fishing skiff lay gutted on the second sandbar, and a container barge had shed half its deck load across the shallows, and by noon the *Patient Answer*'s hold held enough recoverable cargo to buy maybe three weeks instead of one.

Three weeks out of sixty. The math sat in Gideon's stomach like ballast.

Okafor found her at dusk, standing at the stern rail watching the flood tide roll in, brown and swollen and fast, carrying branches and fence posts and one incongruous garden chair out of the drowned river country toward the open sea.

"You're doing the face," he said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're calculating how far a person can run on three weeks of diesel."

"It's a good face. It's kept me alive."

"It's kept you *moving*," Okafor said. "Different thing." He leaned on the rail beside her, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the river kept giving up its debris, and the light went copper and then gone.

"He said the sea owes me," Gideon said finally. "Statistically."

"Statistically, the sea collects."

"Yeah." She watched the brown water slide past, faster than it should have been, the whole flooded watershed draining seaward in one great exhale. Something about the speed of it snagged in her mind — a flood tide that strong would drag things off the riverbed that hadn't moved in years. Things buried. Things sunk and forgotten. Her eyes tracked downstream toward the delta bars where the current would drop whatever it was carrying, sorting the river's secrets by weight, the way it always did, the way it had done since before there were maps.

Sixty days. Findable. Visible. Working.

Or a miracle.

Gideon looked at the roiling brown line where the river met the sea, and for the first time in fourteen months of running, she found herself hoping the water was hiding something worth finding.

"Okafor," she said slowly. "How's the dredge rig?"

He gave her a long look. "Why?"

"Because tomorrow," she said, "we're going to work the delta bars."
