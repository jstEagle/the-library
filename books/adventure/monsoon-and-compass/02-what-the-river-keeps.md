## 2. What the River Keeps

The flood gave back what it took, but never in the same order.

Gideon Quintana had learned that lesson at nine years old, standing on her mother's shoulders in a flooded street, watching a piano float past a bakery. The bakery was gone. The piano was fine. The river didn't distinguish between treasures and trash; it just rearranged. And three days after the water dropped, when the mud began to crack and steam in the sun, the whole drowned valley lay open like a purse turned out on a table—and every scavenger from the coast came to pick through it.

She brought the *Patient Answer* upriver at first light, running the trawler's engine low and easy through water the color of strong tea. Okafor stood at the bow with a boathook, pushing floating debris aside—fence posts, a dead pig swollen to the size of a rowboat, half a roof with its shingles still attached, spinning slowly in an eddy like it couldn't decide where it lived anymore.

"You smell that?" he called back.

"River."

"River's wrong." He wrinkled his broad nose. "River smells like something died and then died again."

"That's just Tuesday on this stretch." But she smelled it too—under the mud and the rot, something sweeter, almost metallic, like coins left too long in a dead man's pocket.

The flood had crested eleven days ago, higher than anything in living memory, and the salvage fees were already being posted at the harbor office: so much per recovered head of cattle, so much per body, so much per sealed container of unknown origin. Gideon owed the Vane Syndicate forty thousand marks, and Mr. Lark's smile from two weeks ago was still sitting behind her eyes like a splinter she couldn't work loose. A good dredging week could clear eight hundred marks after fuel and Okafor's cut. Forty thousand marks was fifty good weeks, if nothing broke, if no one got sick, if the Syndicate's interest—which Lark had explained with the patience of a schoolteacher—didn't grow faster than she could dig.

So she dredged. It was all she knew how to do that paid.

The winch cable sang out over the stern at midmorning, the drag bucket biting into the soft new silt where the old ferry landing used to be. Gideon worked the controls barefoot, feeling the engine's mood through the deck plates, and Okafor hauled the first loads up: mud, more mud, a mattress with its springs burst out like entrails, a child's bicycle fused to a sewing machine by rust and catastrophe.

"Any bodies?" she asked, because someone always asked, and better her than strangers.

"No bodies." Okafor tipped the bucket. "River keeps those for itself. River's greedy that way."

They worked the grid she'd marked on Sela's chart—the cartographer had drawn them a map of the floodplain with the obsessive precision of a woman who alphabetized her spice rack—and by noon they'd pulled up enough scrap iron to cover fuel and a little besides. Not enough. Never enough. Gideon was doing the arithmetic in her head again, the ugly kind, the kind where you divide your life into weekly installments and watch the remainder shrink toward zero, when the winch stopped.

Not jammed. Stopped. The drum locked with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the whole stern dipped, and the cable went taut as a harp string.

"Gideon." Okafor's voice had changed. He'd been hauling lines since he was six years old, and there was a tone men like him saved for cables under load that shouldn't be. "That's not mud weight."

"I feel it." She throttled down and eased tension into the line, testing. Whatever was down there held on. "Snagged on something?"

"On nothing. There's nothing down there to snag on. I walked this bottom when I was a boy. Mud and old pilings, that's all." He squinted over the side at the brown water. "This is heavy. This is *ship* heavy."

"Then we've found a ship."

"We've found a coffin, maybe." He said it lightly, but he crossed himself anyway, quick and embarrassed, the way he did whenever death came up uninvited. "Flood could've rolled something off the bank. Old wreck, old grave, either way—" He looked at her, and his face did the thing it did when he was about to say the sensible thing and already knew she wasn't going to do it. "Either way, we cut loose and move on. That's the smart play. You know it's the smart play."

The cable groaned. Somewhere below them, in the tea-dark water, whatever they'd hooked shifted, and Gideon felt the vibration come up through the deck and into her bare feet, a deep subsonic hum like the river clearing its throat.

"Bring it up," she said.

"Gideon—"

"Bring it up slow. If it's a coffin, we'll give it a respectful burial and bill the county. If it's not—"

"If it's not, then it's trouble wearing a shape we haven't met yet." But he was already moving to the winch, because that was the arrangement between them: she made the bad decisions, and he made sure they survived them. Eleven years he'd been her deckhand, and in all that time he had never once let her drown, even when she'd earned it.

The winch took the strain. The *Patient Answer* listed hard to port, gunwales kissing the water, and Gideon threw her weight against the starboard ballast valve while Okafor worked the drum in short, grudging pulls. The engine howled. Steam curled off the cooling lines. And then, with a sucking kiss of mud releasing its grip, the thing came free of the bottom and rose.

It broke the surface at 12:40 in the afternoon, and the first thing Gideon saw was the lead.

Lead lining, dull gray, sheeted over wood gone black with age—but the wood hadn't rotted. Lead doesn't rot, and whatever was inside had been kept dry by the gray metal the way a body is kept by a coffin. The chest was the size of a sea chest, banded in bronze gone green, and stamped on its lid, deep enough to have been struck with a die rather than hammered by hand, was a seal.

A sun, rising or setting—it was impossible to tell which—half-swallowed by a curling wave.

Okafor went very still.

"Gideon," he said quietly. "Throw it back."

"It's salvage."

"It's *Meridia*." He said the name the way sailors say a storm's name after it's killed someone they loved. "You know what that is. You know what that means. Every fleet from here to the Corvus Deep has spent sixty years looking for Meridian wreckage, and you want to haul it onto our deck like it's a mattress?"

"It's a chest."

"It's a *chest with a dead nation's seal on it*, and dead nations don't leave their mail lying around unless somebody meant for it to be found—or unless somebody else is going to come looking for it, and they're going to ask us where we found it, and they're going to ask with guns." He wiped his palms on his trousers, leaving muddy streaks. "I'm telling you as your friend and not your crew: throw it back. Let the river keep it. The river's kept it this long. The river knows how."

Gideon looked at the chest dripping on their deck, and she felt the pull she always felt, the one that had put her in debt and put her in storms and put her in front of Mr. Lark's smile. Curiosity, her mother had called it once—not kindly. *You look at locked doors the way other people look at open ones, Gideon. One day a door is going to look back.*

Water ran off the lead in thin brown threads. The sun-and-wave seal caught the afternoon light, and for a moment—she would swear this later, and no one would believe her—the wet metal seemed to brighten, the way a cat's eye does when it turns toward you.

"Help me get the lid," she said.

Okafor closed his eyes briefly, the way a man prays without believing anyone's listening. Then he got the pry bar.

* * *

The bronze bands were crusted but sound. The lock had failed decades ago—some earlier flood, some earlier finder who'd gotten scared and shut it again?—and the lid came up with a groan of lead hinges and a breath of air from inside that smelled, impossibly, of cedar and cold. Cold, in the middle of a steaming floodplain, as though the chest had carried winter up from wherever it had waited.

Inside, packed in tarred felt gone stiff as bark:

A bundle of papers, wax-sealed, the seals stamped with the same sun-and-wave.

A brass instrument like a sextant crossed with an abacus, its arms etched with numbers in a script Gideon didn't recognize—dense, angular, written right to left.

And beneath everything, a single bound volume, its cover cracked leather, its pages swollen with damp but intact. She lifted it out. A tide table. She knew tide tables the way she knew her own hands. But this one was wrong—columns of figures in that same angular script, annotations in a second hand, in trade-common, cramped and urgent, margins crowded with corrections.

On the flyleaf, in faded ink, someone had written a single line:

*For the one the river chooses.*

Gideon read it twice. Her mouth had gone dry.

"That's addressed to somebody," Okafor said. His voice had dropped, the way voices drop in churches and around accidents. "That's addressed to whoever opens it. That's you, Gideon. Somebody wrote that knowing—"

"Somebody writes that on every chest. It's a sales trick. Salvage superstition. You stamp a pretty seal on a box and suddenly every fool who finds it thinks the ocean loves him personally." She was talking fast, and she heard herself talking fast, and she couldn't stop. "It's worth money. That's all it is. Papers, instruments, a nation's junk—collectors pay for Meridian junk. We sell it quiet, through Tomas maybe, no names, and we clear forty thousand and we're done. We're *done*, Okafor. Do you hear me? One chest and we're done."

He looked at her for a long moment. The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere upstream, a crane called, harsh and lonely.

"And if it's not junk," he said finally. "If it's real. If it's what I think it might be." He tapped the tide table with one thick finger, careful, like it might bite. "Then forty thousand marks is what you'd pay for the *paper it's wrapped in*. And the people who know that are people who don't send Mr. Lark with his smile. They send people whose smiles we never see coming."

Gideon looked down at the flyleaf. *For the one the river chooses.* Her mother's handwriting.

She knew it instantly, completely, the way you know a voice in the dark—every loop and slant of it, the way Elena Quintana crossed her sevens, the little hook she put on her capitals. Gideon had spent twenty years trying to forget that handwriting and had failed at it daily.

The river chose. Of course it had. Her mother had never believed in luck; she'd believed in preparation, in leaving things where the current would carry them, in planting seeds in floods. Gideon stood on her own deck, barefoot, holding a dead nation's tide table in her mother's handwriting, and understood with terrible clarity that staying invisible had just stopped being possible—that somewhere, sometime, someone had planned this moment the way you plan a mooring, counting the tides, waiting for the water to rise.

"Okafor," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Get us home before dark."

He was already turning the boat. But he paused at the wheel, and he didn't look at her when he said it:

"Whatever's in that book, Gideon—whatever your mama left you—we should read it behind a locked door. Because the minute anybody learns what you're holding, this ain't salvage anymore." He swallowed. "It's a hunt. And we just became the foxes."

Behind them, the river closed over the hole where the chest had slept, smooth and brown and patient, keeping everything else it kept.
