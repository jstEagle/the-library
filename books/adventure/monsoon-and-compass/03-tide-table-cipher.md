## 3. Tide-Table Cipher

Sela Voss had spent eleven years learning to read the ocean the way priests read scripture—slowly, reverently, and with the constant suspicion that the text was lying to her.

So when Gideon Quintana came banging on her door at an hour when honest people were asleep and cartographers were dreaming in contour lines, Sela was already awake, already ink-stained, already irritated. She opened the door with a lamp in one hand and a drafting pen behind her ear like a duelist's blade.

"This had better be about the Kestrel Shoals survey," she said. "I told you I'd have it by Thursday."

"It's not about the shoals." Gideon shouldered past her into the cramped room that served as archive, workshop, bedroom, and shrine to the science of measurement. She was carrying something wrapped in oilcloth, and she was carrying it wrong—not like salvage, which Gideon handled with the casual contempt of a woman who'd hauled a thousand drowned things out of the water, but like something that might bite.

Behind her came Okafor, filling the doorway the way he always did, a mountain of a man doing his best impression of a man who wanted to be anywhere else. He had river mud to the elbows and an expression Sela recognized from funerals.

"She found a chest," Okafor said, as if announcing a death.

"I found a chest," Gideon agreed, setting it on Sela's drafting table with a care that made Sela's stomach drop. "Lead-lined. Sealed. And stamped with this."

She peeled back the oilcloth.

Sela had seen the sun-and-wave before. Everyone on this coast had, eventually—it turned up on corroded coins sold to tourists, on the prows of museum wrecks, in the margins of charts drawn two centuries ago by hands that had never seen the nation they were mapping. A golden disc half-swallowed by a curling wave. The seal of Meridia.

The country that wasn't anymore.

"No," Sela said.

"You haven't even looked at—"

"No." Sela set down her lamp. "Gideon. I know what you're going to ask me, and the answer is no. Do you know what happens to people who find Meridian things? The Meridian *thing*? There are men in this harbor who would cut your throat for a Meridian spoon. Real ones. Not harbor-rats—men with ships and lawyers and guns. You dredge up a chest with that seal on it, you don't have treasure. You have a target painted on your hull."

"I haven't asked you anything yet."

"You brought it to my house at three in the morning. That *is* the asking."

Gideon had the decency to look slightly abashed, which for Gideon was roughly equivalent to another woman weeping. She leaned against the drafting table, arms crossed, and Sela watched her decide how much truth to spend.

"Lark came back," Gideon said quietly. "The Syndicate. They gave me until the end of the month. Forty thousand marks, Sela. I've got a boat older than Okafor and a winch held together with prayer. I can't run fast enough to outrun arithmetic." She tapped the chest once, lightly, like knocking on a stranger's door. "And then the river hands me *this*. I'm not saying it means anything. I'm saying I need to know what it is before I decide what to be afraid of."

Sela looked at Okafor. Okafor looked at the ceiling with the expression of a man who had already lost this argument in his head and was simply waiting for his body to catch up.

"Open it," she said finally. "But if there's gold in there, I want no part of it. Gold has a smell. Everything that follows it smells the same."

* * *

There was no gold.

There was, instead, a bundle of thin metal sheets—lead-sheathed tin, each one the size of a prayer book—wrapped in waxed canvas gone stiff with age. And beneath them, nested in a bed of tarred wool, a brass instrument that made Sela forget how to breathe.

It was a tide-rule. Not the common kind, the sliding brass sticks every harbor pilot carried. This was a *computing* rule—a cylindrical device of nested drums, each drum engraved with fine spiral scales, capped with a knurled selector ring worn bright by use. Meridian workmanship. Pre-Fall workmanship, which meant it had been built by people at the absolute summit of the craft, before the sea took their workshops and their guilds and their language.

"Oh," Sela said. It came out very small.

"What?" said Gideon. "What is it?"

"Give me a moment." Sela cleared her drafting table of everything else—the Kestrel Shoals survey, three weeks of work, swept aside without ceremony—and laid the metal sheets flat under her lamp.

They were covered in writing. Or what looked like writing: dense columns of characters she almost recognized and didn't, arranged in grids, annotated with symbols that repeated with a rhythm her eye caught before her mind did. Tides rose and fell in patterns; anything recording tides would carry that pattern in its bones, even encrypted. She'd taught herself to spot it years ago, reading intercepted naval tables during the harbor war, back when knowing where the water would be and when had been worth more than knowing where the enemy slept.

"This is a tide-table," she said slowly. "A naval one. Full tidal predictions—heights, slack-water windows, current sets, probably seasonal corrections. Encoded."

"Encoded how?" Gideon had come around the table without seeming to move, the way she did, silent as gull-shadow. "Can you read it?"

"It's Meridian shorthand. The naval cipher—the Admiralty used a compressed glyph system so a whole season's predictions could fit on a single sheet a signalman could memorize. Most of it died with them. But—" Sela pulled a folio from her shelf, one of forty-seven, and flipped to a page she'd copied twenty years ago from a wrecked Meridian patrol boat off Cape Anselm, when she'd been young enough to think knowledge was free. "But dead languages leave cousins. The merchant variant survived longer. If the naval glyphs follow the same root structure—and they will, ciphers are lazy, they reuse what works—I can reconstruct maybe seventy percent. Eighty, given time."

"And the other thirty?"

"Is where the interesting things hide." Sela set the tide-rule beside the sheets. "This will do most of the mechanical work. These drums—" she rotated the selector ring, and the nested cylinders clicked through positions with a sound like a lock turning, "—are keyed to specific reference stations. Harbor stations. You set the date, the station, and the rule computes the tide. Whoever owned this could generate accurate predictions anywhere in Meridian waters without carrying a single table."

Okafor shifted. "So it's a fancy clock."

"It's a fancy clock that tells you when the sea opens its doors." Sela looked up at him. "Every wreck, every smuggler's run, every naval approach in history has been timed to tides. This isn't a curiosity, Okafor. This is a keyring."

* * *

She worked while they watched, because she couldn't help herself, because the sheets were singing and she had never in her life left a song unfinished.

The first sheet broke in under an hour. The grid structure gave her the grammar—rows for days, columns for hours, the recurring glyph clusters marking high water, low water, slack. The Meridian numerals she already knew from her Cape Anselm copies. By lamplight, with Gideon hovering and Okafor pretending not to hover, Sela translated the header of the second sheet and felt the intellectual thrill arrive exactly on schedule, that hot clean rush she'd chased since she was nine years old copying maps by candlelight:

*Station reference: Vhal-mer-Ath. Seasonal table, spring series.*

"That's not a port I know," Gideon said.

"It wouldn't be. It's pre-Fall nomenclature." Sela's pen moved fast now, cross-referencing, her old charts spread open around her like shed skins. "Vhal-mer-Ath. *Vhal* is deep water. *Mer* is the possessive—of Meridia. *Ath*..." She stopped.

"*Ath* what?" Gideon said.

"*Ath* is fleet. Fleet anchorage. Deep-water fleet anchorage of Meridia." Sela sat back. Her mouth had gone dry, and she noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather. "Gideon, this is a tide-table for a naval anchorage. A major one. These aren't harbor approaches for fishing boats. These are approach timings for warships."

"So there's a base out there."

"There *was* a base out there. And these coordinates—" she plotted them quickly, her hand steadier than her voice, "—put it here." Her pen came down on a stretch of open water sixty miles offshore, a region her own charts marked with nothing but depth soundings and a laconic note: *unsurveyed; bad ground.*

Sixty miles of open ocean didn't excite anyone. But Sela had spent her life reading the negative space of charts, and she knew what *unsurveyed; bad ground* usually meant. It meant reefs. It meant wrecks. It meant a graveyard wearing a featureless face.

"The table runs six tidal cycles," she said. "Spring series. Six tides out from the date encoded in the header—which I can't fully resolve yet, but the lunar notation gives me a window. If the table was current when it was sealed..." She did the arithmetic twice because she didn't trust the first answer. "Then the window it describes begins four days from now."

Silence. Okafor had stopped looking at the ceiling.

"Four days," Gideon repeated.

"Four days. And here's the part you're not going to like." Sela pulled the last sheet toward her, the one she'd saved, the one whose grid was different—denser, overprinted with a second layer of glyphs that didn't fit the tidal grammar at all. She'd been avoiding it the way you avoid the last page of a book you love. "This sheet isn't a prediction. Predictions repeat; the sea is regular. This one doesn't repeat. It's a schedule. Something was meant to happen at this anchorage, timed to a specific tide, and someone wrote down exactly when."

"Something like what?"

"I don't know yet. The overlay cipher is different—tighter, military-grade. But see this cluster?" She tapped a repeating group of five glyphs that appeared at intervals down the sheet. "In the merchant shorthand, this construction means *transfer of custody*. It shows up eleven times. Eleven transfers, all timed to slack water, all at night tides." She looked up. "Someone was moving something enormous, piece by piece, in the dark, on the tides. And this table was the timetable."

Gideon had gone very still. Sela had known her eight years, through storms and salvage claims and one memorable night when a crane cable had parted six feet from Gideon's head, and she had never seen her this still.

"The treasury," Gideon said. "Everyone says Meridia's treasury went down with the flagship. Never found. Two hundred years of fleets looking for it."

"I know what they say."

"If this is the timetable for moving it—"

"Then whoever sealed this chest knew where it went." Sela heard her own voice flatten, the way it did when fear arrived dressed as precision. "Or helped put it there. Or was running from the people who did."

* * *

Okafor spoke first, and what he said was: "Throw it back."

He said it gently, which was worse. Okafor gentle was Okafor frightened.

"Gideon. Listen to me. You found this in a flooded river after the worst storm in a decade. Chests don't swim upstream. Someone *put* it where you'd find it, or the flood moved it from somewhere it was hidden, and either way—either way—there are people who have spent two hundred years and more money than God looking for exactly this. You said Lark gave you a month. What do you think the Syndicate does when they learn a debt-ridden salvager pulled a Meridian naval cipher out of the river?" He shook his huge head slowly. "You don't owe forty thousand marks anymore. You owe them a reason to let you live."

"He's right," Sela said, and hated saying it, hated the way the beautiful thing on her table had curdled into a liability even as she touched it. "Gideon, I've been translating for two hours and I already understand why this was lead-lined and sunk in a river. It's not hidden. It's *buried*. There's a difference. Hidden things want finding. Buried things are hiding from something."

Gideon stood at the edge of the lamplight, and Sela watched her run the numbers—not marks, not debts, but odds. Weighing the grave against the gallows.

"My mother," Gideon said at last, "kept a chart on our wall when I was small. Hand-drawn. I used to think it was just decoration. After she disappeared I sold everything she owned except that, and I lost it in a card game when I was nineteen, and I've thought about it every year since." She turned. "It had that seal on it, Sela. Sun and wave. In the corner. I thought it was a souvenir."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the harbor water slapped against pilings, patient as arithmetic.

"So," Gideon said. "How long to crack the second layer? The transfer schedule. All of it."

Sela looked at the sheets—at the elegant dead language, the drowned admiral's clockwork, the eleven midnight transfers marching down the page toward whatever waited at Vhal-mer-Ath—and felt the last of the thrill drain out of the room, leaving only the cold, clear understanding she should have had from the moment she saw the seal.

Because she knew what she was holding. Not a treasure map. A *naval* document, in a *naval* cipher, describing a *naval* operation—and naval operations had authors, and authors had enemies, and enemies kept ledgers. Within a week, every salvage fleet on the coast would smell what she was smelling. Within a week, men like Mr. Lark would stop smiling.

"Three days," she heard herself say. "Maybe less, if I don't sleep."

"Take two," Gideon said, already reaching for the oilcloth. "We sail on the third."

*Sail,* Sela thought, as the door closed behind them and the chest's shadow lay long across her charts. *She's already decided. She walked in here with a target on her back and she's walking out with a fleet's worth.*

She sat alone with the lamp and the dead language and began, carefully, to copy the second sheet—because a cartographer's first law was never to let a single original leave her hands, and her second law, learned late and painfully, was that the things everyone wants are the things that get everyone killed.

By dawn she had broken the first line of the overlay cipher, and it read:

*Custody passes to the flagship at the sixth tide. Burn this after reading.*

Sela stared at the words for a long time, listening to the harbor breathe, and understood that somewhere out in the dark water, six tides away, other people were reading the same sentence she was—and that unlike her, they had gunboats.
