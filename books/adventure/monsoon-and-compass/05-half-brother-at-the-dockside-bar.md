## 5. Half-Brother at the Dockside Bar

The Salt Cellar smelled like every harbor bar in the world: old beer soaked into wood that had never once been dry, brine ground into the floorboards by ten thousand boots, and underneath it all the sweet-rot stink of men who'd stopped pretending they were going home rich.

Tomas Quintana knew the smell better than he knew his own daughter's hair.

He sat at his usual table—third from the back, back to the wall, sightline to both doors—and nursed a beer he'd been nursing for an hour because buying a second one meant admitting the first hadn't brought anyone to his table. That was the fixer's life in Port Calloway. You weren't paid for information. You were paid for being the kind of man people believed would sell it. The belief was the product. The actual selling was rare, and when it happened, you made sure it was expensive enough that nobody asked twice.

The rain came down outside in gray sheets, monsoon season arriving early and angry, and through the salt-fogged window the harbor lights smeared like wet paint. Somewhere out there, three salvage fleets were waking up. He didn't know that yet. What he knew was smaller: a dredge captain named Pell had come in two nights ago talking about something pulled from the river mud with a seal on it, and Pell talked too much when he drank, and Tomas had filed the detail away the way he filed everything—in the drawer marked *maybe worth something someday*.

Then the door opened, and the drawer flew open on its own.

Gideon Quintana stood in the doorway shaking rain off her shoulders like a dog, and the whole room did the thing rooms always did when she walked in—a half-second hitch, heads turning, the collective recalculation of men who remembered her name from the racing sheets and the debt notices pinned to the harbormaster's board. Forty thousand marks owed to the Vane Syndicate, printed right there under her name for anyone with eyes. She wore that number now the way other people wore scars. Everyone could see it. Nobody mentioned it.

Tomas's stomach performed its familiar maneuver: lift, then drop, then settle somewhere sour.

*She found me,* he thought. *Of course she found me. I'm the easiest find in the city.*

He considered leaving through the kitchen. He'd done it before—the last time she'd come looking, four years ago, after the Marisol job, after he'd sold her route to a rival crew for six hundred marks and a promise of more. He'd watched her walk into that ambush from the upper deck of a moored freighter, close enough to see her face when the boarding hooks came over the rail, and he'd told himself the same lie he always told himself: *she'll survive it, she always survives it, and survival builds character.*

She had survived it. She had not forgiven it. And she'd spent four years making sure their paths never crossed, which in a harbor the size of Port Calloway took discipline bordering on devotion.

Now here she was, crossing the room toward him, and the discipline had broken, which meant something had broken behind it.

"Gideon," he said, when she reached the table. Kept his voice level. Kept his hands where she could see them, flat on the wood, the way you did with people who had reason to expect a knife.

"Tomas." She didn't sit. She stood over him with rain still dripping off her jaw, and up close he could see what the racing sheets didn't print: the hollows under her eyes, the way her jacket hung looser than it should have on her frame. Debt did that. He'd seen it eat bigger people than her. "You look terrible."

"You look worse." It came out automatic, the old sibling reflex, and he hated how easily it surfaced, how the muscle memory of family lived in the body long after the family itself had bled out. "Sit down or don't. You're dripping."

She sat. That was something. Four years ago she wouldn't have given him the satisfaction.

The barmaid drifted over, and Gideon ordered nothing, which was its own message—I'm not staying longer than I have to, this isn't a visit—and Tomas waved the woman off before she could make it awkward.

"Let me guess," he said. "You need something."

"I need fleet intelligence."

"Everyone needs fleet intelligence."

"Three fleets. Corvus, the Harrow Group, and whatever Vane is running out of the deepwater basin. Who's mobilized, who's bought charts, who's got divers on retainer." She said it fast and flat, a shopping list, no decoration. "And I need it tonight."

Tomas turned his beer glass a slow quarter-turn on the wet ring it had left. Buying time. Not because he didn't know the answers—he already knew most of them, had known since yesterday when the Corvus took on dive gear at Pier Nine and the Harrow Group's agent started asking discreet questions about tide tables—but because the speed of her ask told him everything about how desperate she was, and desperation was leverage, and leverage was the only currency he'd ever been able to hold onto.

But there was another voice underneath the broker's voice, quieter, older, and it sounded like his mother saying *mira, Tomás, mira tu hermana* —look at your sister, look how she reads the water—and that voice had been getting louder lately, especially at night, especially since Marta's school sent home the second notice about tuition, especially since he'd started doing arithmetic at three in the morning about what a man's reputation actually weighed on a scale against a girl's future.

"What's the cargo?" he asked.

"That's not your business."

"It's exactly my business. You want me to tell you where three fleets are going to be, I need to know what they're all chasing, because if I point you at them and you get dead, my information dies with you and my reputation dies with it." He leaned forward. "Meridian chest. River salvage. Sun-and-wave seal. That's the story walking around the docks, anyway. Pell swears he saw the stamp himself before your winch hauled it up."

Something moved behind Gideon's eyes—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. She'd known the story was loose; she was measuring how loose.

"Pell talks too much," she said.

"Pell drinks too much. Same disease, different symptom." He let a beat pass. "So it's true."

"So it's true."

"And you're going after the flagship."

Her jaw tightened. She hadn't said it. He'd said it, and now it existed between them on the wet tabletop, and they both understood that he'd just demonstrated exactly why she shouldn't trust him: he could take a thread and pull the whole garment apart in three sentences. It was the family talent. Their mother had it. Gideon had it pointed outward, at storms and wrecks and water. Tomas had it pointed at people, which paid better and cost more.

"Here's my price," he said, before she could decide to walk. "A share."

That landed. He watched it land—a flicker at the corner of her mouth, not quite a sneer, not quite a laugh.

"A share. Of what? You don't even know what's down there."

"I know what a Meridian treasury is rumored to be worth, and so does everyone else, which is why three fleets are spinning up their winches. I want in. Ten percent."

"Ten percent." Now she did laugh, short and hard, with no humor anywhere in it. "Ten percent of a wreck nobody's ever found, guarded by fleets that will sink us on sight, while I owe the Syndicate forty thousand marks and they've got collectors who smile when they break fingers. Sure, Tomas. Ten percent. I'll draw up papers."

"You came to me."

"I came to you because you're the only person in this harbor who knows where the bodies are buried, and half of them you buried yourself." She leaned across the table, and for a moment the exhaustion fell away and he saw the thing underneath, the engine that had made her the best storm-chaser on the coast before the debts closed around her: pure, unlovely, magnificent stubbornness. "I don't have money. I don't have time. I have a boat held together with prayer and a crew of three, and I am asking you—asking—for what you know. Name a real price."

"A share *is* a real price. It's the only price I can afford." He heard his own voice crack slightly on *afford*, and cursed himself for it. She caught it too. Her eyes narrowed, the broker's instinct in her reading him the way he read other people.

"Afford," she repeated. "What do you mean, afford?"

"Nothing."

"Tomas."

"Don't use Mom's voice at me."

The words were out before he could stop them, and they hit the table like a dropped glass. Both of them went still. Around them the Salt Cellar roared on—dice games, arguments about hull prices, a drunk singing something mournful in Meridian creole that nobody under sixty could translate anymore—but at their table there was only the rain against the window and forty years of shared blood sitting between them like a third person who wouldn't leave.

"She's dead eight years," Gideon said finally, quietly. "You don't get to invoke her as a shield."

"No," he agreed. "I don't." He rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them, he'd made some kind of decision—he could feel it settling in his chest like ballast, heavy but stabilizing. "Marta's tuition is due. Eleven hundred marks, end of the month, or she's out of St. Brenna's and into the harbor schools, and you know what the harbor schools produce. Dock rats. Fixers. People like me."

Gideon said nothing. But her expression shifted, and he watched his daughter's future arrive in his sister's face as something other than leverage—as an actual weight, an actual child, an actual reason. That was the difference between Gideon and the men he usually dealt with. The men heard *eleven hundred marks*. Gideon heard *my niece*.

"How much do you actually know?" she asked.

"Corvus took on mixed-gas dive rigging yesterday, Pier Nine. That's deep-water work, past thirty fathoms. They're not chasing rumors; Reyen has coordinates." He ticked points off on his fingers, the broker's cadence taking over, and it felt strange and almost pleasant to spend the inventory on family instead of the highest bidder. "Harrow's slower—they're fitting out at the yard, two days behind, but they've got a gun deck and they'll use it. And Vane—" he paused, because this part mattered, this part was the part that might keep her alive, "—Vane hasn't mobilized anything. Which is worse. When the Syndicate moves quiet, it means they already think they know where you're going. Somebody's feeding them."

The silence stretched.

"You think it's someone on my boat?"

"I think somebody told Lark's office about a Meridian chest within twelve hours of it coming out of the river, and your crew is four people, and I'm the fifth, and I haven't told anyone." He met her eyes. "Do the math."

"I've done the math my whole life. It always comes out to you."

The words were cruel and true and he took them without flinching, because flinching would have been worse. This was the ledger between them, and they both knew it line by line: the Marisol job, the forged harbor passes he'd sold her name to get, the winter he'd borrowed against her boat's papers without asking. And further back, deeper down, the part neither of them said aloud: the night their mother's boat went out and only one of them was on it, and the question of whose fault that was had never been settled, only buried, and buried things in tidal country have a way of washing back up.

"I sold you out once," he said. "Once that counted, and a dozen times that didn't. You want a guarantee I won't again, I can't give you one. All I can give you is this: I'm not selling you to Vane, because Vane doesn't pay, Vane *owns*, and I've watched what ownership looks like from the cheap seats. I'd rather take my chances on a wreck with you than sell safe passage to them." He pushed his untouched beer aside. "Ten percent. Of whatever's in the strongbox, treasure or paper or sand. And I work. I don't sit ashore and collect. You need harbor doors opened, I open them. You need a berth nobody checks, I find it. You need to disappear for six tides, I make the paperwork say you never existed."

"Six tides," she repeated. "How did you—"

"Sela Vintra bought tide almanacs from the chandler on Tuesday. All seven years' editions. Nobody buys seven years of tide tables unless they're solving a cipher or planning a very strange vacation." He allowed himself the smallest smile. "I told you. Bodies. I know where they're buried."

For a long moment Gideon looked at him, and Tomas understood that whatever she decided in the next ten seconds would decide something larger—that this was the hinge, the moment where the family either stayed broken in the shape it had worn for four years or bent itself painfully back toward functional. He saw her weigh it. He saw her weigh *him*, the way you'd weigh a rope of uncertain age before trusting it with your whole weight over dark water.

"If you betray this," she said, "there won't be a next time. There won't be a conversation. I'll write you off the way you wrote off the Marisol run, and I'll mean it."

"Fair."

"And it's five percent. Paid after salvage, before debts—which puts you behind the Syndicate, behind Okafor's back wages, and behind Sela's equipment costs. You're fifth in line on a boat with four people."

"Fifth in line," he said, "on a boat that's actually going somewhere. I've had worse positions." He extended his hand across the table. "Deal?"

She looked at his hand the way she'd look at a weather front—measuring, untrusting, calculating what it would cost to go around versus through. Then she took it. His sister's grip was cold from the rain and strong from the winch, and it lasted exactly as long as a contract needed to last and not one second more.

"One condition of mine," she said, releasing him. "You meet the crew tonight. Okafor gets to look you in the eye. If he says no, deal's off."

"Okafor hates me."

"Okafor hates everyone. It's not disqualifying." She stood, pulling her wet jacket closed, and paused. Something crossed her face that wasn't anger and wasn't forgiveness but was adjacent to both—recognition, maybe, of two people standing in the same flood. "Tomas. The story about the chest. How far has it spread?"

"Far. And getting farther. By morning, every dock rat from here to Cape Verren will know a Quintana pulled Meridian gold out of the river." He hesitated, then gave her the rest, the part that had been sitting cold in his gut since sundown. "There was a man in here an hour ago asking after you. Not Syndicate—wrong clothes, wrong accent. Coastal, but not ours. He left when I wouldn't bite." He reached into his coat and slid a folded scrap of paper across the table. "He paid for his drink with this. Old coin. Meridian sun-and-wave on the face."

Gideon picked it up. In the bar's yellow light, the drowned nation's seal gleamed dully, wet-looking even though it had been dry for decades, and Tomas watched his sister's face do the thing faces do when the race they thought they'd entered turns out to have already started without them.

"Get your coat," she said. "We're leaving through the kitchen."
