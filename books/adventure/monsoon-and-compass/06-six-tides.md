## 6. Six Tides

The Patient Answer sat low in the water the way she always did, like a woman who'd stopped standing up straight because nobody was looking anyway. Forty years of trawling had given her a hull the color of old teeth and an engine that started on the third prayer, never the second. Gideon came down the dock with Tomas half a step behind her, both of them still smelling of the bar's fryer grease, and found Okafor already aboard, coiling line in the dark by feel.

He didn't turn around when their boots hit the deck.

"You brought him," Okafor said.

"I brought my brother."

"Those aren't the same thing, and you know it." He finished the coil, set it down square, and finally faced them. In the harbor lights his face was all planes and patience, the face of a man who had spent eleven years being the only adult on this boat. "Gideon. Tell me you didn't do what I think you did."

"Depends what you think I did."

"You told a room full of harbor people that you're holding something Meridian."

"I told one person, and he's family."

Tomas cleared his throat. "Debatable."

Okafor looked at the sky as if petitioning it directly. Then he looked at Gideon, and the patience in his face did something complicated—cracked down the middle, maybe, or folded over on itself. "Get below," he said. "Both of you. We need to have the conversation we should have had before the bar."

* * *

The galley of the Patient Answer was six feet by eight and smelled permanently of diesel and the ghost of ten thousand fried mackerel. Gideon spread the chest's contents across the chart table while Okafor bolted the door behind them—a habit he'd picked up from her, which she'd picked up from her mother, which was the closest thing to a family inheritance they'd ever passed down cleanly.

The tide-table lay open under the lamp: brass-bound, salt-stained, its pages dense with a shorthand no living nation used anymore. Beside it, the cipher wheel, and the strip of vellum where Sela's first translation sat in her small, furious handwriting.

"Six tides," Gideon said. "That's what the table gives us. The coordinates resolve on the sixth tide out from the flood that drowned the river channel—which was two nights ago. So we've got four tides left before every fleet on this coast can do the arithmetic Sela already did."

"And what's at the coordinates?" Okafor asked.

"The flagship."

He said nothing for a long moment. He crossed his arms, uncrossed them, put both hands flat on the table like a man steadying himself against weather. "The Meridian flagship. The wreck that has been the biggest maybe-treasure story on this coast since before either of us could read. The one that's killed more divers than the war did."

"That's the rumor version. Rumor says gold."

"What does the table say?"

Gideon hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but Okafor caught it—he always caught it—and she watched him file it away. "The manifest section is damaged. Sela's still working it. But the route is clean, the depth soundings are clean, and the tide windows are clean. Whoever encoded this wanted someone to be able to find it fast and quiet."

"Or wanted someone to find it at all," Okafor said quietly. "Fast and quiet cuts both ways, Gid."

"It's forty thousand marks, Okafor." She said it flat, without drama, because the number didn't need drama anymore; it had become furniture in her life, a thing she bumped into daily. "Lark gave me until the end of the month. You've seen what happens to captains who miss Vane deadlines. The Marisol. The Cutter brothers. They don't break your legs first—they buy your boat out from under you at auction, legally, smiling, and then you work their decks until your hands give out."

"So we run. We've run before. We point the Answer south, past the patrol lines, sell her in Corvo Bay, start over."

"We've done that twice. Each time costs us everything we rebuilt, and each time Lark finds us faster. This time he'll find us before we clear the headland, because I made noise tonight, and because—" she tapped the tide-table, "—because this exists. There's no invisible anymore, Okafor. That ended when I cracked this seal. The only question left is whether we arrive at the end of this broke and hunted, or whether we arrive owning the thing everyone else is killing each other to find."

"And if there's no gold?"

"Then I'm wrong, and we're ruined, and at least I'll be ruined moving toward something instead of away from everything."

Okafor stared at her. Behind him, Tomas sat on the bunk with his elbows on his knees, watching the exchange like a man watching tennis played over his head, and Gideon realized her brother hadn't spoken in three minutes, which for Tomas was a kind of prayer.

"There are three salvage fleets on this coast," Okafor said finally. "Corvus, the Harrow Group, and whatever Vane keeps under his own flag that doesn't have a name. Any one of them could take the Patient Answer apart with equipment alone. And you want to race them to a wreck in monsoon season."

"Not race them. Beat them. There's a difference. Racing means going fast. Beating them means going where they can't follow." She pulled Sela's chart fragment across the table. "The flagship went down on the edge of the Shoals of Names. Forty wrecks in six miles of water, most of them uncharted since the drowning, currents that reverse twice a day. Big salvage ships don't go in there. Their insurers won't let them. But the Answer draws nine feet loaded light, and I've been reading those waters since I was nineteen, chasing storm fronts along the shelf. If we go now, on the right tides, through the gaps—it's ours. Whatever it is."

Okafor was quiet again. Outside, the harbor slapped against the pilings, patient and indifferent.

"You've thought about this," he said. "More than tonight."

"I've thought about it since the river."

"Then say the rest of it. All of it. Because I can hear the shape of a lie of omission sitting on this table next to that brass wheel, and I'd rather hear it from you than find it later."

Gideon exhaled. "Sela thinks the encoding style isn't naval standard. It's personal. Someone built this cipher by hand, for a specific reader, and hid it somewhere it would only surface after a flood year. That's not a treasure map, Okafor. Treasure maps get sold. This got planted."

"Planted by who?"

"I don't know yet."

It wasn't the whole truth. The whole truth had a shape she couldn't say aloud yet—that the shorthand's flourishes had looked familiar to her before Sela ever translated a word, familiar the way handwriting looks familiar, the way a lullaby looks familiar when you find the score thirty years after the singer vanished. She kept that in her chest where she kept everything about Elena Quintana, in the sealed room where she kept the not-knowing, because the not-knowing was the last piece of her mother she owned.

Okafor studied her face for a long time. Whatever he saw there—the sealed room, or just the tiredness—he nodded once, slowly, like a door closing on a room he'd decided not to enter.

"All right," he said. "Say the plan. All of it. Then I'll tell you mine."

* * *

She laid it out the way her mother had taught her to lay out plans, back when the lessons were just games played with matchsticks on galley tables: assets first, then routes, then failures.

Assets. The Patient Answer, slow but shoal-water capable, with a winch that could lift two tons and a hull that had survived things hulls shouldn't survive. Sela, who could decode the rest of the table and navigate the Shoals by memory and mathematics. Tomas, who knew every fleet captain's debts, mistress, and drinking schedule, and could tell them which harbors were bought. Okafor, who could keep a forty-year-old engine alive with wire and stubbornness. And herself, for whatever a storm chaser was worth in monsoon season, which was more than anyone sane would guess.

Routes. Out on the midnight ebb, dark ship, running lights cold, through the dredger channel where the harbor watch's picket boat never patrolled after the second bell because the current there scared even the men paid to be brave. North along the coast road's blind side, then out across the shelf to the Shoals' western gap, timed to the fourth tide. Dive on the fifth. Gone by the sixth, before the fleets' big ships could even finish mobilizing, let alone find the gap.

Failures. She named them herself, honestly, because a plan you don't name the failures of is a wish wearing a plan's clothes. Failure one: the harbor watch stops them at the channel, bribed by the Syndicate, and everything ends in an office with Mr. Lark smiling in it. Failure two: another crew has already worked the table out—Sela was brilliant, but brilliance isn't exclusive—and they arrive at a stripped wreck. Failure three: the weather. Monsoon season meant the storm line was already stacking up two hundred miles offshore, and the Shoals in a blow were a meat grinder. Failure four—

"Failure four is me," Tomas said.

The galley went still.

He said it without flinching, which was new. The old Tomas would have made it a joke, deflected, grinned his way out of the room. This one sat on the bunk with his hands open on his knees and looked at her straight. "You're bringing me because I know things. You're bringing me knowing what I did. Two years ago I sold your route to a Harrow broker for three hundred marks and a promise, and you lost a season and nearly lost the boat. So failure four is me. I want it said in front of both of you so it can't be unsaid: I am the risk on this deck. Weigh me accordingly."

Gideon hadn't expected that. She'd braced for charm and gotten inventory instead, and it knocked something loose in her that she didn't have a name for.

"Why?" she asked. "Why come at all? I gave you a share, not a shackle."

"Because I'm tired," Tomas said simply. "Because I've spent nine years being the man who knows everybody and belongs nowhere, and I watched you tonight in that bar, and you looked like Mom." His voice caught on it, just slightly, and he pushed through. "And because if there's even a chance that chest is what I think it might be—if it's connected to her—I want to be on the boat when we find out. Even if you weigh me accordingly. Even if you chain me to the mast for the whole run."

Nobody spoke. The engine ticked as it cooled, ticking off seconds like a metronome for the silence.

Okafor broke it. "My turn," he said. "Here's my plan. My plan was: refuse. Refuse hard, tie the Answer to the cleat, take my tools off her, and let you two sink yourselves without me. That was the plan I built on the walk over here, and it was a good plan, and I believed in it right up until about ninety seconds ago."

"But?"

"But I'm fifty-three years old, Gideon. I've got a bad shoulder, a rented room above a chandlery, and a savings tin with enough in it to bury me respectably. Everything else I have is on this boat or in these two people, and I have spent eleven years watching you run from things, and I have learned something about running that nobody tells you: it doesn't cost less than fighting. It costs the same, paid slower, in installments, with interest." He stood, took his coat off the hook, and put it on, which in Okafor's language was a signature. "So here's my counteroffer. We go. On my conditions. One: nobody dives the wreck without a second diver topside. Nobody. Not you, not me, not some hero moment. Two: if the Syndicate shows before we're ready, we scuttle the evidence and run—we don't fight a gunboat, we are not idiots. Three: the moment this stops being a salvage run and starts being a war, we vote. All four of us. Majority rules, and I don't care if the majority is wrong."

"That's it? No condition about turning back?"

"Turning back stopped existing two chapters ago," Okafor said dryly. "I just want it on record that I noticed."

Gideon laughed—short, surprised, real—and felt something settle in her chest that hadn't settled since the river gave up its chest. It wasn't safety. Nothing was safe. It was the older, rarer thing: company. Four people on a rotten boat with one plan between them and nothing left to lose except each other, which was, she understood suddenly, the entire secret of how crews got made. Not loyalty. Not love. Just the arithmetic of having nothing else, added up honestly, out loud, in a galley that smelled of mackerel.

"Tides," she said, and pulled the almanac across the table. "Next ebb is 0147. That's our window. Wake Sela—she's sleeping in the forepeak, I told her to stay aboard tonight. Tomas, I need names: which fleet moves fastest when word leaks, and who among their captains owes Vane so much they'll sail in weather. Okafor—fuel, water, dive bottles, and the spare prop. We load silent, no dock lights, no receipts."

They moved. That was the miracle of it, the thing she'd remember later: how fast a crew forms when the plan is finally spoken. Tomas was up and reaching for his coat mid-sentence. Okafor was already unbattening the hold. The galley emptied around her, and Gideon stood alone at the chart table with the tide-table glowing under the lamp, and for a moment she let herself look at the shorthand flourishes again—the way the numerals leaned, the little wave-hook the encoder used instead of a plain zero.

She knew that hook. She'd seen it in the margins of her childhood homework, correcting her sums in a hand that was gone before she could ask why.

"Elena," she said softly, to the lamp, to the drowned nation, to whoever had planted a map in a river and waited twenty years for a flood. "What did you leave me?"

Above her, boots crossed the deck. The harbor breathed. And down the coast, unseen, in a map room full of a lifetime's work, a cartographer was about to smell smoke.
