## 7. Sela's Map Room Burns

Sela Voss had spent eleven years teaching herself to sleep lightly.

It was a habit born of necessity—of a childhood in the tenements of Corvane where the rent collectors came at odd hours and the neighbors' arguments ended in broken glass—but it had become something else over the years, something closer to devotion. She slept in the narrow cot behind her archive the way a lighthouse keeper sleeps beside his lamp: not for rest, but for duty. Every creak of the building's old bones had a name. The settling of the roof joists in cold weather. The groan of the water pipe when the tannery two streets over flushed its vats. The particular sigh of the front door when the wind found the gap beneath it that she'd never quite sealed because sealing it would mean admitting she might one day leave.

So when the smell of smoke threaded under her door at half past three in the morning, she was awake before her eyes were open.

For one merciful second she thought it was the tannery again. They burned their offcuts on Thursdays, and the wind sometimes carried the reek down Harrow Lane like an apology no one meant. But this smoke was wrong. It was sweet and resinous, thick with pitch and lamp oil, and underneath it—she sat up so fast her neck cracked—underneath it was paper.

Paper burning has a sound. Most people never learn it because most people are never close enough to hear it die. It is a whispering, a dry fluttering, like a thousand small wings being folded one after another. Sela had heard it exactly once before, when she was nineteen and a rival cartographer's shop went up on the waterfront, taking with it forty years of coastal surveys that could never be redrawn from memory. She had stood in the crowd that night with ash settling on her shoulders and thought: *that is what the end of knowledge sounds like.*

Now it was coming through her own floorboards.

She did not scream. Screaming was for people who believed someone would come. She pulled on her boots, grabbed the iron poker from beside the stove, and pressed her ear to the door.

Voices. Two of them, maybe three, low and urgent, from the direction of the map room—the great front chamber where her life's work lived in oak flat-files and cedar plan chests, where charts hung from ceiling hooks like the flayed skins of geography itself. A man said something she couldn't make out, and then another voice, sharper: *"Look for Meridian. Anything with the sun-and-wave. Burn the rest if you have to."*

The rest.

*Burn the rest.*

Her hand tightened on the poker until the cold iron bit into her palm. Eleven years. Eleven years since she'd scraped together the coin to lease this drafty former chandlery on Harrow Lane, since she'd carried her first chart case up these stairs on her back like a coffin. Eleven years of commissions and copying work and the slow, patient acquisition of other people's discarded maps—the surveyor's estate sale, the widow clearing out her husband's sea chest, the harbormaster who traded originals for drink. She owned things in that room that existed nowhere else on earth. Hand-annotated depth soundings of the Shoals of Names from before the last war redrew the channels. A pilot's logbook from a ship that had sailed into the Meridian exclusion zone in the year of the drowning and come back with its crew mad and its charts confiscated. Three generations of tide tables for a coastline that no longer officially existed.

And now someone was feeding it to the dark.

Sela was not brave. She had never once pretended to be. She was a woman who solved problems by going around them, by measuring the safe route and taking it twice. But there is a species of anger that does not consult courage first—it simply moves—and Sela discovered, standing barefoot-booted in her nightshirt with an iron poker and a heart slamming against her ribs, that the destruction of everything she loved qualified.

She opened the door onto smoke.

* * *

The map room glowed.

That was the horror of it, she thought later—that it was beautiful. Firelight moved across the hanging charts like sunset across water, gilding coastlines, setting whole archipelagos alight. A plan chest stood open, its drawers pulled out and stacked, and flames were climbing out of it in slow orange tongues, licking at the rolled vellum inside. The heat hit her face like an open oven door. Somewhere in the blaze, a chart she had spent four months restoring—the umber-and-gold portolan of the northern approaches, her masterpiece, the thing she'd shown to exactly no one because showing it felt like undressing—curled and blackened at its edges and then, all at once, wasn't anymore.

Three men. Two by the far wall, hauling drawers and rifling them, tossing contents into a spreading slick of oil fire on the floorboards. One near the window, holding a lantern, turning papers over with a booted foot as though sorting laundry. He was the one who spoke again, and now she could see him clearly enough to note the details her mind insisted on recording even as the rest of her wanted to run: gray coat, good quality but travel-worn; a face like a closed ledger; gloves. Gloves, indoors, in summer. Men who wore gloves while they worked were men who expected to leave fingerprints somewhere eventually, and planned ahead.

"Nothing marked," the gloved man said. "Check the backs. Meridian work was often disguised—overprinted, palimpsest. Look for the seal under other seals."

They were looking for Meridia. Of course they were. Word traveled fast along a harbor, faster than any ship, and Gideon Quintana had hauled a lead-lined chest out of the river three days ago with a drowned nation's seal stamped on its lid. Sela had decoded the tide-table cipher in Gideon's galley by lamplight, her fingers trembling not with fear but with the electric joy of recognition—dead shorthand, naval notation, coordinates six tides out—and she had thought, in the arrogance of the delighted scholar, *we have time.* She had assumed the race would start when the fleets mobilized, when the radio chatter began, when the world noticed.

She had forgotten that the world's cruelest habit is noticing early.

The poker suddenly felt very small. There were three of them and one of her, and the fire was between her and the flat-files along the north wall—the ones closest to the door, the ones holding the modern charts, the working copies, the things that could be redrawn. Everything irreplaceable hung or lay deeper in the room, past the fire, past the men.

Then she stopped thinking like a victim and started thinking like a cartographer.

The room had a second exit. Not a door—a window, high and narrow, the old loading hatch from the chandlery days, above the plan chests on the south wall. It opened onto the alley roof of the cooper's shop next door, a drop of eight feet onto tarred planking. She'd used it twice: once when she locked herself out, once when she decided she didn't want to explain to a debt collector why she kept a strongbox under her bed. It was rusted shut in winter but it was July, and she had oiled the hinges in spring because oiling hinges was the kind of thing Sela Voss did instead of sleeping properly.

But between her and the hatch lay the fire, and the men, and eleven years of her life going up in a light that got warmer and redder by the minute.

She memorized.

It was the only salvage available to her, and she took it the way a drowning woman takes air. Her eyes moved through the burning room in deliberate sweeps, the trained sweeps of a woman who had spent her professional life committing coastlines to memory, and she fixed each irreplaceable thing in place behind her eyes: the Shoals of Names soundings, third drawer, north flat-file, the annotation in faded brown ink reading *channel shifts after the quake year — do not trust the printed depths*. The mad crew's logbook, bottom plan chest, south wall, its final entry a scrawl about *lights under the water in rows, like streets*. The exclusion-zone survey with the hand-drawn inset of a reef shaped like a split tooth. The tide tables. The tide tables, six tides out, the numbers she had already decoded and written in her own shorthand in a notebook that was, thank every god listening, tucked under her pillow upstairs rather than lying on the worktable where she'd left it yesterday.

Her notebook was safe. The notebook held the cipher solution, the coordinates, the working notes. That was the mission, such as it was. But the archive—

The archive was not the mission. The archive was her.

A floorboard cracked like a rifle shot as the fire found the joists. One of the men swore and stepped back from a collapsing shelf. In the confusion of flame and falling debris, Sela made her choice.

She went left, along the wall, low, through smoke that tasted of copper and centuries. The heat pressed against her skin like hands. She reached the south wall, felt along the paneling for the hatch latch—*please, please*—and the rusted iron gave with a shriek that turned every head in the room.

"There!" the gloved man shouted. "The woman—stop her!"

She was already through. She went out over the sill headfirst, caught the gutter, dropped. Eight feet became four feet became the hard slap of tarred planking, a jolt up through her ankles, a scrape of splinters along one forearm she wouldn't notice bleeding until dawn. Behind her, the hatch framed a rectangle of furious orange, and a silhouette leaned into it—one of them, reaching—and then the cooper's roof gave her its slope and she ran, sliding, half-falling, down to the alley below.

She landed in garbage. She did not care. She lay among the fish heads and broken crates and looked up at her own windows filling with light, and for a long moment she could not move at all.

* * *

Harrow Lane filled with people the way wounds fill with blood—slowly, then all at once. The bucket line formed. Someone screamed for the harbor watch. A woman in a nightgown wept openly, though she couldn't possibly have known what was burning; she was weeping at the sight of fire itself, at the idea of it, at the way it ate whatever it touched without negotiation.

Sela watched from the mouth of the alley, ash sifting down around her like dirty snow, and performed the arithmetic of loss.

The building would survive. Stone walls, slate roof—the fire would gut the interior and the watch would drown it by morning, and the shell would stand there looking like a skull. The archive would not survive. She understood this with a clarity that felt almost clinical, the way she understood tidal mathematics: the fire had reached the plan chests, the chests held the vellum, vellum burns at a temperature lower than wood kindles. By sunrise there would be nothing in that room but charcoal and the smell.

Eleven years. Fourteen thousand hours, if you counted the nights, and she counted the nights. Gone in twenty minutes by three men in gloves who hadn't even wanted it—they'd wanted only the Meridian material, and had burned the rest merely to hide the shape of their search. That was the detail that finally made her cry, standing in the alley with soot on her face: not the loss itself, but its carelessness. Her life's work had been collateral. An erasure conducted for tidiness.

She cried for perhaps ninety seconds. She timed it without meaning to; grief, like everything else in her experience, had a duration, and knowing the duration made it bearable. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, leaving a black streak, and made herself do the thing she did best.

She took inventory of what remained.

Her notebook: safe, upstairs, under her pillow. She could not go back for it—not tonight, not with the watch crawling over the scene and whoever had hired the glove-men surely watching the street for exactly that return. She would need it retrieved another way, or abandoned, and the thought of abandoning it made her stomach clench, but the cipher solution lived in her memory too. She had written it down because cartographers wrote things down, but she had *learned* it the proper way, the old way, the way pilots learned coastlines before paper was cheap. Six tides out. The bearing. The reef pattern. The sequence of the encoded table. It was all still there, behind her eyes, in the vault that no one could burn.

And more than that. As she stood in the ash-fall, she realized the fire had done something to her memory besides destroy its sources. It had *fixed* them. Every chart she had glimpsed in those last sweeps of the burning room was now engraved in her recall with a vividness that calm study had never achieved. The Shoals of Names annotations. The logbook entries. The exclusion-zone inset. Fear, it turned out, was the finest fixative ever devised. The gloved man had ordered her life's work burned, and in doing so had forced her to carry all of it out with her, letter-perfect, in the one archive no torch could reach.

"They took nothing," she whispered to the alley, to the smoke, to herself. "They took nothing at all."

It wasn't true yet. But she intended, with the full force of her considerable and stubborn will, to make it true.

* * *

Dawn found her sitting on the seawall at the end of Harrow Lane, watching the smoke column thin against a bruised-pink sky. Her forearms were streaked with soot and one long scratch; her nightshirt smelled like a chimney; she had wrapped a stolen tarpaulin around her shoulders against the pre-dawn chill and must have looked, she supposed, like a beggar who'd lost an argument with a stove. The harbor woke around her indifferent to her ruin—gulls arguing over offal, the first trawlers grumbling out past the breakwater, deckhands coiling rope with the easy violence of routine.

She should feel destroyed. She catalogued the feeling carefully, the way she catalogued everything, and found that beneath the grief—real grief, hot and specific, grief for the portolan especially, for the four months of her life dissolved into smoke—there was something harder taking shape. Something with edges.

Whoever had sent those men knew about the chest. Knew enough to search for Meridian material specifically, which meant they knew the chest had been opened, which meant either they had eyes on Gideon's boat or they had heard the same harbor whispers Sela had heard and simply moved faster than anyone sane would have predicted. Either way, the race Sela had imagined beginning in some comfortable future tense had already begun, and its opening move had been made against *her*, the least dangerous member of the expedition, the one who posed no threat at all except that she could read what others couldn't.

Which meant they feared exactly one thing about her: what she knew. And what she knew was now the only copy in existence.

Sela looked out at the water, at the gray-green swell moving beyond the breakwater with its ancient patience, and thought about tides. Six tides out, the cipher said. Five remained. And somewhere out there, at coordinates only she and Gideon and Okafor and a dead nation's shorthand could name, the flagship of Meridia waited with whatever it was the glove-men were so afraid of finding.

She stood up. Her legs ached. Her throat ached worse, from smoke and from the ninety seconds she'd allowed herself. She pulled the tarpaulin tighter and started walking toward the docks, toward the *Patient Answer*, toward the woman who had dragged a chest out of a river and set all of this in motion without meaning to.

Gideon would need to know. Gideon would need to know that the enemy was not waiting politely at the starting line—that they burned archives in the night and wore gloves indoors and considered a lifetime of scholarship acceptable ash. And Gideon would need to know that Sela Voss, whose entire world fit in a single room and now fit in a single skull, was not going to weep about it twice.

Let them burn the maps, she thought, striding down the lane as the sun cleared the harbor roofs and lit the smoke gold one last time. She was the map now.

She just hoped she could reach the boat before anyone followed the same logic to its conclusion—and decided that the cartographer, like her archive, was evidence best destroyed.
