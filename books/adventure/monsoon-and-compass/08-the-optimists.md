## 8. The Optimists

The night before the chest came up out of the river, Gideon Quintana dreamed of her mother's hands.

They were younger in the dream than she remembered them being—unspotted, the knuckles not yet swollen from salt and rope—and they were folding a paper boat on the galley table of a boat that no longer existed. Elena Quintana's fingers moved with the certainty of someone who had done this ten thousand times, creasing the hull, raising the sail. She didn't look up. In dreams you never get them to look up.

*The water always wins,* her mother said, to the boat or to her, it was impossible to tell. *So learn what it wants.*

Gideon woke in the fo'c'sle of the *Patient Answer* with the storm front still two days out and forty thousand marks sitting on her chest like ballast. She lay in the dark listening to Okafor snore through the bulkhead and thought: I have spent my whole life inside other people's confidence. Theirs, mostly. Men who smiled.

She hadn't always owed money. That was the thing nobody believed when she told it—that there had been a time, brief as a calm eye, when the Quintanas were solvent, even comfortable, even *respected*, back when respect was something a salvage captain could earn with competence instead of collateral. Her mother had owned the *Kindly Tide*, a hundred-foot coastal dragger rigged for wreck work, and Elena ran her the way she did everything: precisely, quietly, without asking anyone's opinion twice. Gideon grew up in that wheelhouse doing homework by chart light while her mother threaded coastlines other captains called suicide. Elena never chased storms. She met them where they'd already been and took what they'd dropped, patient as tide itself.

*We don't gamble,* Elena told her once, when Gideon was eleven and begging to run ahead of a squall like the boys from Port Calloway did, engines screaming, cameras flashing. *Gambling is for people who think luck is a system. We read. The sea tells you everything if you stop talking long enough to hear it.*

Then one autumn Elena stopped talking altogether.

No note on the galley table. No cleared accounts. The *Kindly Tide* rode at anchor, fueled, provisioned, her papers in order, as though her captain intended to come back after lunch. Elena Quintana walked down the dock at Grayharbor on a Tuesday morning in October with a canvas bag over her shoulder, nodded to the harbormaster—someone remembered this later, the nod, how ordinary it was—and stepped onto a white-hulled courier boat flying no colors, and the sea closed behind her the way it closes behind everything.

Gideon was sixteen. Tomas was nineteen and already half-gone into the harbor's gray economy, and neither of them could make the arithmetic work: their mother, who planned tides three days out and kept duplicate charts of every route she'd ever sailed, who labeled the jam jars, who wrote *back by dark* on the slate even to go buy nails—this woman had simply evaporated. People said drowned. People said run off with a man. People said, in lower voices, *asked too many questions about Meridia*, and then changed the subject the way people do around a wound they can see but not name.

What she left was warnings. That was all. Gideon found them over the years the way you find things in a drowned house—one at a time, each one worse.

In the *Kindly Tide*'s logbook, on the last page, in her mother's small square hand: *If anyone offers to buy your debt, they are buying you. There is no such thing as a friendly lender.*

In the false bottom of her chart drawer, under a chart of shoals that didn't exist on any official map: *Never let them know what you've found before you know why they want it.*

And sewn into the lining of the old oilskin coat Gideon wore to this day, discovered only the winter the lining tore and she finally mended it herself because she couldn't afford a tailor—a strip of oilcloth, rolled tight as a cigarette, containing four words in faded ink:

*They smile first. Always.*

At sixteen, Gideon read those words and thought her mother had gone strange before the end. Paranoid. A woman alone too long on the water, seeing conspiracies in weather patterns. It took her eleven years and a great many smiling men to understand that Elena Quintana hadn't been paranoid. She had been *informed*.

* * *

The first smiling man was named Doss Merrow, and he ran chandlery credit out of a storefront in Grayharbor that smelled of tar and peppermint. Gideon was twenty-two, newly master of the *Patient Answer*—bought with the insurance from the *Kindly Tide*, which the underwriters had finally paid out on the theory that a missing ship was cheaper than an investigation—and she needed engine parts, and winter was coming, and Doss Merrow sat across his counter from her with his round pink face and his little laugh and said the words that would define the next decade of her life:

*Why, Captain, we're practically family here.*

He extended her sixty marks of credit against the season's salvage fees. Then ninety. Then, when the fees came in thin because the good wrecks were all claimed by fleets with sonar and lawyers, he extended the terms, warmly, generously, adding interest that compounded in a manner he explained twice, patiently, laughing at himself—"I'm a simple shopkeeper, Captain, the numbers confuse me more than you"—while the numbers did exactly what numbers do when nobody watches them. By the third year she owed him eight hundred marks on a debt that had begun at sixty, and when she went in to argue, he spread the ledger across the counter with both hands like a man presenting a gift, every line initialed, every initial hers.

*You signed, Captain.* He beamed at her. *I'd never hold a person to something they didn't sign. That's not how we do business.*

That was the lesson of Doss Merrow: the trap is legal. That's what makes it a trap and not a robbery. Robbery ends.

The second smiling man worked for someone bigger. His name was Corben Hale, and he represented the Vane Syndicate's maritime interests division, which meant he bought debts the way other men bought fish—by the lot, sight unseen, at discount. He appeared at the dock one morning in a coat too fine for Grayharbor and shook her hand with both of his, and said, *Miss Quintana, I understand you've had some difficult seasons. Mr. Vane believes in second chances. It's practically the company motto.*

He bought her Merrow paper for six hundred marks and forgave nothing. He restructured. New terms, new schedule, new signature—hers, again, because the alternative, explained so kindly, so reasonably, was default proceedings, lien seizure, the *Patient Answer* sold at auction to men who would strip her engines by Friday. He even threw in a small advance, cash, to help her through the winter. *A gesture of confidence,* he called it. Confidence. She would hear that word so many times over the following years that it lost its meaning entirely, became pure sound, the way a word repeated goes strange on the tongue. Confidence. The Syndicate had confidence in her. They demonstrated it the way a fisherman demonstrates confidence in a hook: by leaving line to run.

She learned later—it was Sela who told her, years afterward, in the flat voice Sela used for facts that should have been shouted—that the Vane Syndicate held paper on nearly every independent operator between Grayharbor and the Meridian Shelf. Not because independents were profitable. Because independents were *independent*, and a fleet that owes you is a fleet that works your grounds, salves your wrecks, looks away from your cargo, and votes, when votes are needed, the way creditors vote. Augustin Vane had built an empire not on gold but on obligation, and obligation, unlike gold, compounds.

By thirty-one, Gideon Quintana owed the Vane Syndicate forty thousand marks, and the number had stopped meaning anything. It was weather now. It was a pressure system parked permanently over her life, and she had learned to live under it the way coastal people live under anything—to sleep, to eat, to joke, while the barometer fell.

* * *

There was one more piece, and she never told anyone it existed, not Okafor, not Sela, not even Tomas—especially not Tomas. Three months after her mother vanished, a letter came to the dock office, postmarked nowhere legible, addressed in Elena's hand. No return address. One page.

Gideon had read it perhaps two hundred times since. She could recite it the way sailors recite depth soundings.

*Little Gull—if you're reading this, I couldn't explain without making you part of it, and part of it is not a place I will put my daughter. Know these things. First: I did not leave you. Second: do not look for me; looking is how they find you. Third: the country under the water was murdered, and some of the murderers are very respectable, and respectable men keep excellent records, and someday those records will matter more than any treasure ever did. Fourth: trust the ones who stay. Fifth, and most important: when the moment comes—and it will come, it comes for everyone in our family eventually—you will be offered a choice that looks like safety. Look closer. It is never safety. It is just a smaller cage with better lighting.*

*I am proud of you. I have always been proud of you. Be slow. Be stubborn. Read the water.*

*—M*

She had been sixteen, and furious, and grieving, and the letter had sounded like madness—the murdered country, the respectable murderers, the choice that comes for everyone in our family. She'd hidden it in the same oilskin pocket as the four-word warning and told herself her mother had cracked under something, pressure, grief, whatever it was that took a careful woman and turned her into a riddle written in disappearing ink.

Eleven years later, hauling a lead-lined chest bearing the sun-and-wave seal out of a flooded river, Gideon Quintana stood in the rain on her own deck and felt the letter rearrange itself in her memory, every strange phrase clicking into place like tumblers in a lock she'd been carrying her whole life without knowing it was a lock.

*The country under the water was murdered.*

Meridia.

*Respectable men keep excellent records.*

Vane.

*When the moment comes—you will be offered a choice that looks like safety.*

Lark's smile. Lark's extension. Lark's warm hand on her shoulder saying *we're reasonable people, Captain, the Syndicate admires resourcefulness.*

Her whole life, she understood suddenly, standing there with river water running off her chin—not shaped by her own choices, not even by her mother's disappearance, but by other people's *confidence*. Merrow's confidence. Hale's confidence. The Syndicate's confidence, vast and patient as a bank. Men who smiled while they ruined you, and ruined you so gently, so legally, so *reasonably*, that you thanked them for the extension. Her mother had seen it coming from the far side of a decade and had tried to arm her daughter with nothing but warnings, because warnings were all that could be carried out of the country under the water.

Behind her, Okafor was saying something about the chest, about throwing it back, about how some things the river keeps for a reason. And Sela was already kneeling beside the lead lining with her eyes going bright and terrible, and somewhere out past the breakwater, in a clean office above a clean harbor, a cheerful man named Lark was almost certainly smiling.

Gideon looked down at the seal stamped in the corroded metal—sun over waves, the mark of a nation that had been murdered by respectable men—and made the decision that would cost her everything she had left, which was not much, and buy back the only thing she'd ever actually wanted, which was not for sale.

*Be slow,* her mother had written. *Be stubborn. Read the water.*

The water, Gideon decided, was telling her to open the chest.
