## 9. Outrun the Harbor Watch

Okafor had spent eleven years learning every sound the *Patient Answer* made, and in all that time she had never once lied to him. Engines didn't lie. They grumbled when they were tired, they knocked when they were angry, and tonight, as he knelt in the engine room with a wrench in one hand and a flashlight clamped between his teeth, they told him plainly: *I will get you out of this harbor, old friend, but I will not forgive you for it.*

He spat the flashlight into his palm and listened again. The port-side cylinder had a tick in it that hadn't been there yesterday—a small metallic complaint, like a fingernail tapped against glass. Nothing fatal. Nothing he could fix at two in the morning with the parts he had. He'd wrapped the bearing housing in a rag soaked in oil to buy it quiet, and now he lay on his back beneath the block and made promises to a machine that deserved better than what they were asking of her.

"You'll have your overhaul," he murmured. "When this is done. New pistons. Fresh paint. A name painted proper on the stern instead of that faded ghost."

The engine answered with its tick.

Above him, through the deck plates, came the sounds of a crew preparing to become criminals. Gideon's boots crossed the wheelhouse in short, decisive strides—the walk of a woman who had stopped asking herself whether she was doing the right thing and started asking only whether she was doing it fast enough. Sela was somewhere amidships, moving in that careful, silent way she had, stowing charts in oilcloth because paper mattered more to her than blood and always had. And Tomas—Tomas was on deck coiling lines with hands that knew the work but kept pausing, half a coil at a time, as if his fingers needed reminding whose side they were on.

Okafor closed his eyes against the underside of the engine block and let himself feel it, just for a moment: the weight of what they were about to do. Not the danger. He'd been in danger before, hauling crab off the Shelf in whiteout squalls, riding swells that stood taller than the mast. Danger was weather. You respected it and you went through it.

This was different. This was choosing.

For eleven years Okafor had been invisible. That was the arrangement, unspoken but iron-clad: he kept the *Patient Answer* alive, Gideon kept them fed on salvage fees and storm-chasing contracts, and neither of them asked the harbor watch too many questions or looked too long at any manifest. Invisible people didn't owe forty thousand marks to men who smiled. Invisible people didn't matter to anyone except each other.

By sunrise, if the tide tables in Sela's head were right, they would be fugitives.

He wiped his hands on a rag that had been black longer than it had been cloth, climbed the ladder, and pushed open the engine room hatch into the cold pre-dawn air.

* * *

The harbor at four in the morning was a held breath.

Okafor stood at the rail and took inventory the way he always did before a run—not with charts and instruments but with his eyes and his memory. Fog lay over the water in low, greasy banks, thick enough to swallow a trawler at fifty meters. Good. The channel buoys would be lit, their bells tolling blind and patient, and if Gideon threaded by sound the way she could, they'd slip past the breakwater before the watch boats finished their shift change at five-fifteen. The watch changed at five-fifteen; he'd counted that rhythm for years the way other men counted rosaries. There was a gap, twenty minutes wide, when the night boat tied up at the customs pier and the day boat hadn't yet cast off. Twenty minutes when nobody official was looking at anything.

Twenty minutes was either everything or nothing, depending on who had been paid.

That was the part that sat wrong in his chest. Lark's visit had not been a private courtesy. Men like Mr. Lark did not make house calls; they made arrangements. And an arrangement with the Vane Syndicate, Okafor knew, ran downhill until it reached the pockets of every customs officer, harbormaster's clerk, and watch boat pilot in the district. Which meant the twenty-minute gap might be a gap—or it might be a mouth.

"Engine?" Gideon appeared beside him at the rail, a thermos in one hand, steam curling from its cap. She didn't look at him. She was watching the fog the way a card player watches another player's face.

"She'll run." He took the thermos when she offered it. The coffee inside was terrible, burnt twice over, the way only Gideon ever made it. "She'll complain the whole way, but she'll run. Port cylinder's got a tick. I want you under sixty percent on her until we're clear of the shoals."

"Sixty percent won't outrun anything."

"Sixty percent will outrun nothing *and still be floating*," Okafor said. "That's the trade. Take it."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more the memory of one. She'd been like this since the chest came aboard: wired tight, running hot, alive in a way that frightened him more than any debt collector. Gideon Quintana chasing a storm was a force of nature. Gideon Quintana chasing something she couldn't see was a woman walking backward off a cliff and calling it a dive.

"Tomas says the *Corvus* is still tied up at the fuel dock," she said. "Reyen hasn't moved."

"Reyen's smart. Smart captains wait for daylight and full tanks." He handed back the thermos. "We are not smart captains tonight."

"No," Gideon agreed quietly. "Tonight we're something else."

He wanted to ask her what. He didn't. Eleven years of standing next to a person taught you which questions were doors and which were walls, and *what are we becoming?* was a wall. Instead he said, "Sela's got the tide window at five-oh-two. Slack water lasts nineteen minutes. After that the ebb starts pushing us sideways across the channel mouth and we come out of the fog broadside to the lighthouse like a drunk presenting himself to a magistrate."

"Then we go at five-oh-two."

"We go at five-oh-one," Okafor said. "I don't trust slack water to be punctual anymore. Nothing else is."

Gideon looked at him then, fully, and for a second he saw past the captain to the girl he'd fished out of a flooded skiff a decade ago, seventeen years old and furious at the ocean for taking her mother and at the world for noticing neither of them. "You could stay," she said. It wasn't an offer. It was an absolution, laid out flat between them. "You've got no stake in this. No debt, no Meridian blood, no dead mother pointing you at wrecks. I could put you ashore at the fish pier and you'd be clean."

Okafor considered the offer seriously, because it deserved seriousness. He thought about his room above the chandlery, the small stove, the model ships he carved in winter, the whole quiet architecture of an invisible life. Then he thought about the sound Gideon's boots made crossing the wheelhouse when she thought no one was listening—quick, hard, alone.

"Somebody has to keep the engine honest," he said. "You'd sink us inside a week without me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I've got that doesn't embarrass us both." He turned back to the fog. "Go wake Sela. Tide won't wait and neither will I."

She squeezed his shoulder once—her hand small and strong, calloused across the palm where the winch handle lived—and went below.

Okafor stayed at the rail and watched the customs pier, where a single lantern burned above the watch boat's empty berth, and waited for the night to tell him what kind of night it intended to be.

* * *

They cast off at five-oh-one exactly, engines at idle, sliding away from their berth on the ebb's last breath like a thief leaving through the door he'd entered.

Okafor stood in the engine room with one hand flat against the block, feeling her heartbeat through his palm, and fed her fuel by degrees. Sixty percent, as promised. The tick kept time like a metronome counting down something. Above him the hull spoke in its own language—the slap of water, the groan of old welds—and beneath it all the fog pressed close against the plates, so thick that the world beyond the ship simply stopped existing. For a few minutes there was only the engine and the water and the four of them, sealed inside a steel shell, going somewhere together.

Then the first bell buoy tolled off the starboard bow, right where Sela said it would be, and the world resumed.

He climbed to the wheelhouse. Inside, it was dim and close, red-lit per Gideon's orders, every surface mapped by years of his own maintenance. Gideon stood at the helm with her whole body listening—head cocked toward the fog, hands light on the wheel, eyes flicking between the compass and the dark glass. Sela sat at the chart table with a stopwatch and a pencil, marking their progress in tiny numerals, lips moving as she reconciled dead reckoning against the bells. Tomas was at the bow rail, a shadow holding a shadow, calling depths by lead line because the echo sounder had died last spring and Okafor had never gotten around to fixing it, and because some part of Tomas apparently trusted rope and lead more than he trusted any of them.

"Bell nine," Sela said. "Bearing should open to port."

It opened to port.

They passed through the inner anchorage at five-twelve, threading between moored fishing boats whose riders slept below, and Okafor felt the first taste of it then—that thing he'd spend the rest of the night trying to name. Not fear. Not quite joy. It was the feeling of a door closing behind you that you can never walk back through, mixed with the strange lightness of having set down a weight you'd carried so long you'd forgotten it was there. Nobody was checking their papers. Nobody was logging their departure. For the first time in eleven years, the *Patient Answer* belonged to no ledger anywhere in the harbor.

"They haven't seen us," Sela whispered, as if the fog itself might inform.

"They haven't seen us *yet*," Tomas said from the bow, and his voice carried back along the deck flat and grim, and nobody argued with it, because arguing with it would have meant believing otherwise, and none of them quite did.

The breakwater came up out of the fog at five-nineteen—a long stone spine crowned with the green pulse of the outer light. Gideon brought them around it at a distance Okafor judged to be thirty meters, maybe less, close enough that he could hear the swell detonating against the rocks, a deep percussive boom rolling under the engine noise like distant artillery. Beyond the breakwater the sea opened, gray-black and restless, running a leftover chop from yesterday's weather.

And there, dead ahead in the channel mouth, riding her anchor light like a cat waiting at a mousehole: the watch boat.

Okafor's stomach dropped. "Day boat's early," he breathed. "Shift change isn't till five-fifteen—she shouldn't be—"

"She's not the day boat." Gideon's voice was very calm, which is how he knew she was afraid. "Look at her lines. That's the *Marten*. Night boat. She tied up forty minutes ago—I watched her do it."

"Then who's aboard?"

The question answered itself in the space of a breath. Somebody paid to be aboard. Somebody paid to sit in the channel mouth in the fog with a searchlight and a radio, on the one morning the *Patient Answer* happened to leave harbor without papers.

Lark's arrangement, running downhill, all the way to the waterline.

Gideon's hands didn't move on the wheel. Her eyes did—flicking once to Sela, once to the fog bank lying heavy off the northern arm of the breakwater, once to Okafor. He read all three looks in the order she gave them, because eleven years will teach you a language with no words in it. *Can we run? Can we hide? Can you give me more?*

"The northern fog's thicker," Sela said, following the same arithmetic. "But it puts us over the tail of the Grange shoal. At low water there's six feet under her keel line. Maybe five."

"How much does this ship draw?" Tomas asked, coming through the wheelhouse door with fog dripping off his oilskins.

"Four and a half," Okafor said. "On a good day. On today, with her sitting heavy, call it five even."

"So we have a foot," Tomas said. "A foot of water between our keel and a sandbar that eats trawlers for breakfast."

"And a searchlight behind us," Gideon said, "and a radio that's already talking to somebody, and six tides' worth of head start evaporating while we stand here doing sums." She turned the wheel two degrees. The *Patient Answer* leaned into it, obedient, faithful, terrified. "Northern passage. Okafor—sixty-five percent, no more. If she ticks louder than she knocks, ease off and tell me."

"Aye." He went down the ladder two rungs at a time.

* * *

What happened over the next eleven minutes, Okafor would later describe to himself as the longest conversation he'd ever had with a machine.

He gave the *Patient Answer* sixty-five percent and she took it, the tick sharpening to a buzz, the whole block shivering with effort, and he stood with his hand on the throttle like a man holding a dog back from a fight. Through the hull he tracked their progress by sound alone: the boom of the breakwater falling astern, the change in the water's voice as they left the harbor's shelter and took the open sea's chop on the bow, and then—closer, deeper, hungrier—the sound of the bottom rising to meet them.

The shoal announced itself as a texture. The chop shortened. The swell began to stand up and fold early, spending itself against sand a foot beneath their keel, and the *Patient Answer* started to tremble in a way that had nothing to do with her engine. Okafor had heard that tremble twice before in his life. Both times, the boats involved had spent the following week on the beach with their bellies opened.

"Ease her!" he shouted up the ladder, though Gideon already knew—she was reading the water the way he read the engine, and the wheel was moving under her hands in small constant corrections, feathering them along the shoal's spine like a needle along a vein. Above him, faintly, the searchlight of the *Marten* bloomed against the fog astern, a dirty yellow glow swinging in slow arcs, finding nothing, finding nothing, finding—

The light found them.

It hit the wheelhouse windows and turned the whole ship to stained glass, every drop of fog alight, and Okafor heard the radio crackle astern—a voice, distorted, calling something formal and official-sounding across the water. Calling them by name. *Patient Answer, Patient Answer, this is harbor patrol, heave to.*

"Heave to," Tomas repeated softly, from the top of the ladder. He'd come down without Okafor noticing. His face in the engine room's red glow looked ten years older than it had at the dockside bar. "They know the ship. They know her name. That means it's not routine."

"I know what it means."

"Gideon can't outrun them on sixty-five percent. Not with the shoal under us. If they launch a boat—"

"They won't launch a boat onto the Grange shoal in fog," Okafor said. "Nobody's that stupid."

"But they don't have to be stupid. They just have to write down our heading and pass it along." Tomas crouched beside him, and for a moment the two men looked at each other across the trembling engine, and Okafor saw that Tomas understood the shape of the trap better than any of them, because Tomas had lived inside traps his whole life. "There'll be someone waiting along the coast. Every inlet, every fuel dock. We're not escaping tonight, my friend. We're just choosing how far we get before the net closes."

Okafor looked at the engine. At the rag-wrapped bearing working itself warm. At the tachometer needle holding steady where he'd pinned it, faithful beyond all reason.

"Then we get far," he said.

He eased the throttle up to seventy percent, breaking his own rule, breaking his promise to the machine, and the *Patient Answer* answered him with a surge that pressed them both against the bulkhead and drove them north into the fog, off the shoal's spine, into deep water, while the yellow light swung and swung behind them and the radio voice called their name to a harbor that was already awake.

* * *

An hour later, with the *Marten* long gone astern and the fog thinning to rags, Gideon came down into the engine room and found Okafor sitting on his stool with a cup of terrible coffee, listening to the tick.

"Report," she said.

"She's holding. Barely. I'll want to pull that cylinder in port—if we ever see a port again." He looked up at her. Her face was salt-streaked and gray with fatigue, and there was a wildness in her eyes that hadn't been there at the rail four hours ago, or maybe it had, and he'd only just learned to see it. "We're through. Nobody followed past the shoal. Sela's plotted the first leg—we make the rendezvous point by tomorrow's dusk if the weather holds."

"The weather won't hold." Gideon almost smiled. "Front's building northwest. Big one. I felt it in my knee this morning."

"You felt it in your knee because you're fifty-three and refuse to admit it."

"I'm forty-one."

"You're fifty-three in storm years." He drank the coffee and grimaced. "Captain. Question."

"Ask."

"When we're out there—" He gestured vaguely eastward, toward the open sea, toward the coordinates six tides out that lived folded in Sela's oilcloth like a knife. "When we find whatever your mother left at the bottom of that water. What happens to us? To this?" He tapped the deck plate with his boot. The engine ticked underneath, keeping its stubborn time. "Eleven years, Gideon. I've been invisible a long time, and I find I've gotten used to it. Fugitive is a different profession entirely."

Gideon was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above them, Sela's pencil scratched at the chart table, and Tomas's boots paced the deck in slow, sleepless circuits, and the sea worked against the hull with the patience of something that had drowned prouder ships than theirs.

"I don't know," she said finally. It was, Okafor thought, the most honest thing anyone had said aboard the *Patient Answer* in years. "I know what I'm running from now. I don't know what I'm running to. But I know I'm done being invisible—done owing smiles to men like Lark, done pretending the harbor's the whole world." She put her hand on the engine block, flat, the way he did, as if she could feel the heart of the thing. "Whatever's at the end of those coordinates, Okafor—it's ours. First thing that's ever been just ours. I'm not giving that up."

He nodded slowly. It wasn't reassurance. It was better than reassurance—it was truth, and truth was the only cargo worth carrying on a night like this.

"Then keep her at sixty-five," he said, "and pray the tick stays a tick."

Gideon squeezed the engine block once, like a handshake, and climbed back into the gray morning.

Okafor stayed below with his coffee and his machine and the growing song of the sea, and it was only much later—when the fog had burned off entirely and the horizon showed him a smudge of smoke on the northern edge of things, too deliberate for weather, too steady for a fishing boat burning off its catch—that he allowed himself to understand what Tomas had told him in the engine room, in the red light, beside the trembling engine:

They weren't being chased.

They were being *herded*.

He climbed the ladder to tell the captain.
