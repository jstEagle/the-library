## 10. First Tide: The Fleet Wakes

The radio woke before the sun did.

Gideon had been half-asleep in the wheelhouse chair, boots up on the chart table, the Patient Answer's engine humming beneath her like a sleeping animal, when the static cracked and a voice came through—male, bored, clipped by distance and interference.

"—all vessels, all vessels, this is Harbormaster Corvane relay. Salvage advisory effective immediately. Meridian-era artifacts reported in coastal waters. Registered recovery fleets are advised to—"

She sat up so fast the chair shrieked against its bolts.

Okafor was already in the doorway. He'd heard it too. He stood there with two mugs of coffee, both forgotten now, steam curling up past his knuckles.

"Turn it up," he said.

Gideon spun the dial. The voice swam through static, broke apart, reassembled.

"—bounty posted by Vane Maritime Interests for verified Meridian salvage. Figures to be announced at noon broadcast. All unregistered vessels operating within advisory waters are subject to inspection and seizure. Corvane out."

Static. Then, beneath it, the low murmur of other channels waking up—the whole coast clearing its throat.

Gideon set her coffee down without drinking any of it.

"Noon," she said. "They're announcing figures at noon."

"Which means they already know what it's worth," Okafor said. "Which means somebody already told them."

* * *

By full light, the sea had changed character. Not the water itself—that was the same gray-green swell it always was this time of year—but the traffic on it. From the wheelhouse Gideon could count six masts on the southern horizon where yesterday there'd been one fishing boat and a lie. Trawlers flying survey pennants. A hydrographic launch running tight grid patterns, its sonar pinging like a dripping tap. And far off, too far to be sure but not too far to worry about, the black angular silhouette of something that wasn't a trawler at all.

Sela emerged from below decks with the tide-table cipher spread across a clipboard, pencil tucked behind her ear, and stopped dead when she saw Gideon's face.

"How bad?"

"Three fleets, minimum," Gideon said. "Vane's posted a bounty. They announce the number at noon."

Sela looked out at the horizon for a long moment. Her jaw worked. "I spent eleven years building an archive nobody cared about," she said quietly. "Eleven years of people telling me Meridia was a hobby for sentimentalists. Now every hull with a winch is out here." She tapped the cipher against her palm. "Six tides, Gideon. We have six tides of head start, and we just spent one of them asleep."

"We didn't spend it. We invested it." Gideon turned the wheel a few degrees, bringing them onto a heading that would look aimless to anyone watching—a course toward nothing, toward empty water, the kind of track a boat follows when it's lost or stupid. "Nobody knows we have the chest. As far as the world can tell, that advisory went out because some dredger pulled up a plate or a coin and got greedy."

"You believe that?"

Gideon didn't answer right away, which was answer enough.

* * *

Tomas came up around mid-morning, moving carefully, the way he did everything aboard—a man walking on a deck he didn't trust and a family he trusted less. He'd slept in the fo'c'sle, alone, by his own request, and Gideon had let him, mostly because arguing about it would have meant admitting she'd thought about it.

"Radio's chatty," he said, leaning against the doorframe.

"It is."

"I counted four different fleet channels coordinating. Kestrel Group's got two boats running sonar lines off the shallows. There's a big one—Corvus, out of Port Amiel—she's fast. Refitted ex-navy hull." He paused. "And there's chatter about a Syndicate vessel. Not a salvager. Something with guns."

Gideon kept her eyes on the horizon. "You still know people in these fleets."

"I know everybody in these fleets. That was my whole profession." Tomas rubbed the back of his neck. "You want me to work the channels? Find out who's closest, who's serious?"

"I want you to listen. Listening isn't transmitting. Nobody can trace listening."

He almost smiled. "You've learned paranoia since I last saw you."

"I've learned arithmetic." She finally looked at him. "Forty thousand marks, Tomas. That's what I owe. If the bounty they post at noon is more than forty thousand, then the cheapest thing the Syndicate can do is take the chest themselves and let me drown. If it's less than forty thousand, then the smartest thing I can do is sell it to the highest bidder and pay Lark off before he stops smiling. Either way—" she gestured at the water, the fleets, the whole bright morning gone wrong, "—the math changed while we were sleeping."

"And if it's exactly forty thousand?"

"Then somebody's being funny."

* * *

Noon came on a flat, glassy sea, the kind of calm that felt held, like breath.

They gathered in the wheelhouse—all four of them, though Tomas stayed near the door as if he might need to leave quickly. Sela had the radio log open. Okafor stood with his arms crossed, filling most of the space behind the chart table. Gideon tuned to the Corvane relay frequency and waited.

At 12:00 exactly, the voice returned, smoother now, rehearsed.

"All vessels, all vessels. By authority of Vane Maritime Interests and the Coastal Recovery Compact: bounty for verified Meridian flagship materials, including navigational instruments, sealed documents, or strongbox contents, is hereby set at three hundred thousand marks. Three hundred thousand marks. Verification by Syndicate assessors only. Unregistered possession of Meridian artifacts constitutes theft of cultural patrimony under Compact law. Report recoveries to your nearest Syndicate office. Corvane out."

Silence in the wheelhouse. Even the engine seemed to hold its breath.

"Three hundred thousand," Okafor said at last. "That's not a bounty. That's a war chest."

"That's a message," Sela said. Her voice had gone thin. "They don't post numbers like that unless they know exactly what's down there. Gideon—they know. They know what the flagship carries. Why else would you price paper at three hundred thousand marks?"

Gideon stared at the radio dial. Three hundred thousand. Seven and a half times her debt. Enough to buy the Patient Answer ten times over, buy a fleet, buy a life with no Lark in it, no smiling men on docks, no storm-chasing for salvage scraps. Enough money that she could feel it pulling at her like a current, even secondhand, even through a speaker.

Enough money that everyone else on this water could feel it too.

"They priced it high on purpose," she said slowly, working it aloud. "Not to attract buyers. To attract *finders*. They don't intend to pay anybody. They intend for every greedy fool on this coast to do their searching for them—and then the Syndicate collects from whoever comes up with something. 'Verification by Syndicate assessors only.' You bring them the chest, they take the chest, and if you complain, you're a thief under Compact law. It's beautiful. It's completely beautiful."

"So we don't sell to them," Tomas said. "We sell to Kestrel. Or Corvus. Open auction."

"With what protection?" Sela rounded on him. "The moment any fleet learns we're holding the prize, we're not sellers anymore, we're cargo. Three hundred thousand marks buys a lot of silence, and cheaper than a salvage operation."

"Then what's the plan?" Tomas's voice climbed. "We sail into the middle of three armed fleets with a lead box and good intentions? Gideon, I love ambition, I married ambition once, but—"

"You were married to ambition?"

"Now is not the time."

"It's never the time with you. That's the problem."

"Gideon." Okafor's voice, low, filled the wheelhouse the way his shoulders filled the doorway. Both of them shut up. He nodded at the windows—at the southern horizon, where the black silhouette had grown during the hour they'd been shouting at each other. It had a superstructure now, visible even at this distance. A mast with equipment on it that no salvager needed.

"That's been holding our exact heading for two hours," Okafor said. "Or our approximate heading. Question is whether that means anything."

Gideon took the binoculars from their bracket. The glass steadied the shape: long hull, low profile, a wake too clean for a working vessel. She couldn't make out a name at this range. She could make out the flag, though—or the absence of one, which was its own kind of signature. Ships that flew no colors belonged to someone whose colors you already knew.

"Course change," she said. "Slow, casual, nothing that looks like a reaction. Come left ten degrees. We'll see if he comes left too."

Okafor eased the wheel over. The Patient Answer leaned into the turn, wallowing a little—she was a trawler, not a dancer—and settled onto the new heading.

Gideon watched the glass.

One minute. Two. Five. The black ship held its line, patient as arithmetic.

"He's not following us," Sela said. "He's following the same water we are. There's a difference."

"There's no difference if he gets there first."

* * *

Paranoia, Gideon had learned, was less a feeling than a discipline. You practiced it the way you practiced knots: over and over, until it held without thinking. So she spent the afternoon practicing.

She checked the bilge twice, looking for anything that shouldn't be there—a transponder, a tracker, a device small enough to fit in a fist. Nothing. She checked the chest itself, lashed down under tarpaulin in the hold, and found the seal intact, the lead lining cold as riverbed stone. She checked the crew, which was harder, because checking the crew meant standing in the galley doorway watching Tomas clean fish with hands that used to be hers to trust, watching Sela recopy the cipher into a notebook she then kept inside her shirt, watching Okafor stand his watch with the particular stillness of a man who has decided something and hasn't said it yet.

It was Okafor she cornered first, at dusk, at the rail.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you decided this morning. When the advisory came through. You had a face."

Okafor was quiet for a while. The water slid past below them, black and indifferent. Somewhere south, a fleet was burning fuel and money to find what they already carried.

"When I signed on with you," he said finally, "it was storms and scrap. Hard work, honest risk. Now there's a gunboat-shaped shadow on our stern and a number on the radio that turns neighbors into hunters." He looked at her. "I'm not leaving, Gideon. Get that out of your head right now. But I need you to say out loud what we're doing, so I know what I'm staying for. Are we racing them, or are we hiding from them? Because those are different boats, different seas, and different ways of dying."

Gideon gripped the rail. Below, the wake unspooled behind them, straight and pale, a scar on the water pointing directly at everything they loved.

"We're racing," she said. "Hiding only works until someone checks the hold. And someone will. Six tides, Okafor. We go to the wreck, we take what's there, and we get ahead of the story before the story gets ahead of us."

"And after?"

"After, we decide what it's worth. To us. Not to them."

Okafor nodded slowly, and some of the stillness went out of him—not resolved, but settled, the way a man settles a debt he's agreed to pay. "Six tides," he said. "Then I'm staying for six tides."

From the wheelhouse behind them, Sela's voice, sharp with alarm:

"Gideon! Get up here. The Corvus just changed course."

Gideon ran. Through the glass, in the last copper light, the black ship was swinging smoothly onto a new heading—their new heading, the one they'd taken that morning to test him, the one that led nowhere except where they were going.

Behind her, she heard Tomas say, very quietly, to no one in particular:

"Somebody's talking to them."
