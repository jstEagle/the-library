## 11. Storm Line

The Corvus held their heading for eleven minutes.

Gideon knew because she counted. Counting was what she did instead of panicking—counted seconds between lightning and thunder, counted fathoms under the keel, counted the minutes a rival ship spent pointed at nothing before the nothing became proof. Eleven minutes was enough. The Corvus wasn't patrolling. It was following.

"Change course," Okafor said from beside her at the rail. Not a suggestion. A test of her, maybe, or of himself.

"And go where? They've got a faster hull, a longer range, and someone aboard feeding them our heading every time we breathe wrong." She kept her voice level, which took work, because Tomas's words were still sitting in her chest like swallowed ballast. *Somebody's talking to them.* She looked aft, where Tomas stood at the stern line with his hands in his pockets, watching the black ship the way a man watches a dog he once owned. "If I run, I confirm everything they already know."

"If you don't run, they catch us by morning."

"Then we'd better not be here by morning."

She went to the wheelhouse. Sela had spread the weather fax across the chart table, weighted at the corners with a coffee mug, a brass divider, and Gideon's lucky bolt—the one she'd pulled out of a hurricane's eye off Cape Verren six years ago and never explained to anyone. Sela tapped the paper with one finger.

"There." Her finger rested on a smear of ink and contour lines that looked, to a normal person, like a mistake. To Gideon it looked like a door. "The front's consolidated overnight. Cold drop over warm water—it's organizing into a line. Two hundred kilometers wide, running north-northeast, right across our track."

"I see it."

"Gideon." Sela straightened up, and behind her glasses her eyes did the thing they did when she thought Gideon was being mathematically stupid. "That is a squall line with embedded cells pushing sixty knots. That is not weather. That is a wall."

"That is a wall *they* won't sail through." Gideon leaned over the chart, tracing the line with her thumb. "Look at the Corvus's heading. Reyen's good—Ilsa Reyen doesn't make stupid runs—but she's got a fleet contract and a fleet captain's caution. Nobody chases salvage through a storm line. You lose masts, you lose cargo, you lose crew. Insurance men write hymns about how nobody does it."

"You're not insured."

"No," Gideon said, and felt something in her chest that was either terror or its close cousin, joy. "I'm broke. Which means I'm free."

Okafor had followed her in and stood filling the doorway, arms crossed, a man built like a bollard trying to decide whether to hold fast or snap the rope. "You want to sail *into* it."

"I want to sail through it. There's a difference. The line's moving northeast at eighteen knots; we cut the western edge where it's still forming, ride the gradient, and come out the back side while they're standing off waiting for it to pass. Twelve hours. Maybe fourteen."

"Or we come out the bottom of the sea."

"Also possible." She met his eyes. "But I've been chasing storms since I was nineteen years old, Okafor. Every salvage fee I ever earned, every mark I owe Lark and his smiling friends—I earned all of it running *toward* the thing everyone else ran from. This whole voyage, I've been sailing away from weather for the first time in my life, and look what it's bought us. A tail. A traitor. A race we're losing on speed." She tapped the chart again, hard. "So we stop racing them. We go where they can't follow. My whole life, this weather has been my curse. Tonight it's my weapon."

Sela was quiet a moment, doing calculations Gideon could almost hear whirring behind her eyes. "The western edge is forming over shallow water," she said finally. "If we hug the ten-fathom curve, the seas will be confused but not fully developed. Steep, short, ugly—but survivable if we take them bow-on and don't broach."

"That's my plan exactly."

"That is not your plan. Your plan is vibes and stubbornness. My plan has a ten-fathom curve." But she was already pulling fresh paper toward her, drawing the course in quick sure strokes, and Gideon loved her a little for it.

Okafor hadn't moved. "And him?" He said it without turning, without pointing, but there was no question who he meant. "We sail into a killing storm with a man aboard who might be selling our position by the hour?"

Gideon looked out the wheelhouse glass at Tomas. He'd feel the change in the ship soon; he'd grown up on these decks too, before he grew up away from them. "Then we keep him where I can see him," she said, "and we give him nothing worth selling. From tonight, only I know the final approach. Everyone else knows the storm run and nothing past it."

"And if he's innocent?"

"Then I'll apologize when we're rich." She turned for the door. "Get below, both of you. Strap everything that floats. We make the turn in twenty minutes."

* * *

They made the turn at dusk, and the sky went wrong in the way Gideon had always found beautiful and everyone else found damning.

The cloud line stood up off the horizon like a mountain range deciding to move. It didn't look like weather; it looked like geography—a country of green-black cumulus with lightning living inside it, flickering through the canyons like heat lightning over a city that hated visitors. The wind came ahead of it in long shoves, then gusts, then a steady roar, and the Patient Answer, bless her rotten old hull, leaned into it like a tired horse recognizing home.

"Tenth tide of my life I've regretted knowing you," Okafor said at her shoulder. He had his harness clipped to the rail and a second clip dangling, an offer and an order in one.

She clipped it. "Only the tenth?"

"The others were worse."

The first rain hit like gravel. Then the real rain came, a solid gray cataract that erased the world beyond thirty meters, and Gideon flew the ship by feel and instrument and twenty years of scar tissue. She'd done this a hundred times—no. She'd done this a hundred times chasing the *edge*, riding the fringe where the money was: downed yachts, sunken barges, insurance jobs that paid because no one else would go near the water. She had never once driven straight into the meat of it with nowhere to retreat, because retreat was always the plan. Chase the storm, skim the edge, get paid, get clear.

There was no clear tonight. There was only through.

The first big cell took them at 2200 hours. The Patient Answer climbed a wave the size of a chapel and hung at the top, propeller singing in air, and Gideon heard Sela—who had sworn she'd stay below—arrive at the wheelhouse doorway just in time to make a small sound like a stepped-on violin. Then the bow dropped and the world went white and they were inside it, buried in spray and roaring dark, and Gideon laughed.

She couldn't help it. It tore out of her, wild and involuntary, and Okafor looked at her like she'd gone mad, and maybe she had, but God, it was *good*. No debt collector could follow her here. No smiling man with a ledger. No black ship with a traitor aboard it. Here there was only water and wind and the oldest arithmetic there was: keep the bow to the sea, keep breathing, keep going.

"You're enjoying this," Okafor shouted over the engine noise.

"Terribly!"

"That's a sickness."

"It's the family business!" And then the next wave hit and there was no more talking, only work.

* * *

At some hour past midnight, in the relative lull between cells, Tomas appeared in the wheelhouse.

He was soaked, pale, gripping the doorframe, and he looked at her across the red-lit instruments with an expression she couldn't read—or could read too well, because it was the same expression he'd worn at fifteen, standing in their mother's kitchen, telling her Elena wasn't coming back this time either.

"You're insane," he said. "You know that. You're actually—"

"Watch the radar. Call out anything that isn't rain."

He watched the radar. He was good at it; he'd always been good at reading patterns, which was presumably why people paid him to sell them. For twenty minutes neither of them spoke except in numbers and headings, and the ship groaned and drove on, and somewhere in that wet red darkness Gideon realized she had missed this—not him, exactly, not the betrayal and the years, but this: working a problem shoulder to shoulder with the only other person alive who'd grown up waiting for Elena Quintana to come home.

"Why do you think she left the chest in the river?" Tomas said suddenly.

"What?"

"Elena. If she planted it—if Ilsa's story holds—she put it where *you* would find it. Flooded river, after a storm. She knew you dredge floods. She knew you can't leave a snag alone." His jaw worked. "She knew you'd rather die than let something stay sunk."

Gideon's hands tightened on the wheel. Ahead, the next cell was building, a cathedral of black lit from within. "Maybe she wanted me to have it."

"Or maybe she wanted you to *need* it. There's a difference, Gid. Mom never gave anyone anything without a hook in it." He paused. "I used to hate her for that. Took me thirty years to notice I do the same thing."

The cell hit them then, and there was no more conversation, only survival—and when it passed, Tomas was still there, still calling headings, still clipped to the rail beside her, and she decided not to think about what that meant yet.

* * *

Dawn came gray and battered, and the sea behind them was empty.

Sela confirmed it three times on the radar, then once more by eye from the bow, hair whipping, shirt plastered to her. Nothing. No Corvus, no fleet trawlers, no gunboat silhouette on the horizon. The storm line lay astern now, a broken rampart of exhausted cloud still muttering thunder, and beyond it the world was clean washed light and long swells.

"They'll come around the northern edge," Sela said, coming back into the wheelhouse, wringing out her sleeves. "By tomorrow. Maybe the day after—they'll have to wait for the fleet to regroup."

"Then we have today." Gideon killed the extra burners, letting the Patient Answer settle into a long economical stride, and felt the exhaustion land on her all at once, heavy as the chest in the hold. Around her the boat dripped and steamed and complained, alive, hers, unpaid-for. "One day. What's the tide table say?"

Sela unrolled the cipher sheets, damp-cornered but intact, and smoothed them on the chart table. Her finger moved down the columns, and Gideon watched her face change—watched the cartographer's calm crack and reassemble into something tighter.

"Gideon. The third tide column. I cross-checked it against the lunar tables last night, before—" she stopped, started again. "It doesn't match the flagship's position. The depths are wrong for open water. These readings only make sense over a shoal field, or..." She looked up. "Or a second location. Someone encoded two sets of coordinates into this table, and only one of them is the wreck everyone's looking for."

Gideon stood very still. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere below, Okafor was putting the kettle on, because the world ended and began again around kettles.

"Show me," she said.

And behind them, unnoticed at the wheelhouse door, Tomas had stopped drying his hair—and was listening.
