## 12. Man Overboard

The storm line had spat them out the way a dog spits out something it decided it didn't want after all.

Okafor stood at the wheel of the *Patient Answer* with both hands locked on the spokes and his shoulders aching from six hours of fighting weather that had opinions about where they should be. Behind him, the squall was still working itself out over the water—a black wall of rain trailing east, lightning stuttering inside it like a bad lamp. Ahead, the sea ran long and gray-green under a sky the color of old tin, and the trawler rolled through the swells with the tired, complaining rhythm of a woman who'd been asked to do too much for too long.

"Report," Gideon said, coming up the companionway with her hair plastered flat and her oilskins streaming.

"We're still floating," Okafor said. "That's the whole report."

She almost smiled. He could see her fighting it. Gideon Quintana fought most of her better instincts—smiling, sleeping, trusting—and usually lost to all three eventually, which was why he'd stayed with her through two wrecks, one bankruptcy, and now whatever this was. A man didn't follow a captain into a storm line on purpose unless he'd already decided how the story ended for him.

"Sela's below with the cipher," Gideon said. "Tomas is on the foredeck checking the lashings."

"Tomas shouldn't be on the foredeck."

"Tomas volunteered."

"Tomas wants us to think he's useful," Okafor said. "Those are different things."

Gideon looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped off her chin. "He hauled lines with us through the worst of it. Didn't let go once."

"He didn't let go because he's too stubborn to drown." Okafor turned back to the wheel. "I know the type. I *am* the type."

But she was already moving forward along the deck, one hand on the rail, and Okafor watched her go and felt the familiar knot in his chest that he refused, on principle, to name. Found family, Sela called it once, drunk on cheap rum after the Marisol salvage. Okafor had told her there was no such thing, that family was just the people who hadn't left yet, and Sela had said, *yes, exactly, that's what I said,* and he'd had no answer for that because there wasn't one.

The sea ahead was wrong. That was the thing he couldn't stop looking at. After a storm line, the swell should settle into a long, orderly procession—big, but honest. This wasn't honest. The swells came in from two directions at once, the dying storm's swell crossing the groundswell at an angle, and where they met they built up into pyramids of water that rose higher than they had any right to, hung there a half-second too long, and collapsed without warning.

"Captain!" he shouted forward. "Get off the foredeck!"

Gideon raised a hand to show she'd heard. Tomas, crouched by the anchor windlass thirty feet ahead of her, straightened up and turned toward the wheelhouse, and Okafor saw him open his mouth to say something—something smart, probably, something with that harbor-rat grin on it—

And then the sea stood up.

Okafor would spend the rest of his life trying to describe it and never quite manage it. It wasn't the biggest wave he'd ever seen. It was the *wrongest*. It came out of the crossing swells like something surfacing rather than something traveling, a gray-green cliff maybe five meters high, steep-faced, with foam shredding off its crest in the wind, and it was aimed at the *Patient Answer*'s port bow as if the ocean had taken personal offense.

"BRACE!" he screamed, and slammed the wheel hard to starboard, and knew even as he did it that it wouldn't matter.

The wave took the trawler on the bow quarter and threw her. Okafor felt the deck tilt past any angle a deck should reach, felt his boots leave the plates, felt the wheel spin free in his hands. Somewhere below, something heavy went over with a crash he felt through the hull. The world became white water and green water and the sky sideways, and through all of it—one detail, burned in forever—he saw Tomas.

Saw him come off the foredeck like a doll kicked off a table. Saw him hit the rail, fold over it, and vanish into the churn on the leeward side without so much as a scream, because the wave's roar ate everything, and then the *Patient Answer* slammed down onto her waterline, shuddering the length of her keel, and rolled upright, and Tomas was gone.

"MAN OVERBOARD!" Okafor heard himself bellow, already moving. "PORT SIDE! MAN OVERBOARD!"

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about man-overboard drills: they are lies. You practice in calm water, in daylight, with a fender wearing a hat playing the victim, and everyone stands around afterward nodding sagely about recovery angles and Williamson turns. Nobody practices at the tail end of a storm, in crossing seas, with a forty-year-old trawler that handles like a barge and a man in the water who is not wearing a harness because he was only going to be outside for two minutes checking lashings.

Two minutes. Okafor thought that with a fury that surprised him. Two minutes of carelessness, and the sea had been waiting its whole life for exactly that.

He got to the port rail and found him—or found the orange dot of him, Tomas's foul-weather jacket, bobbing in the trough maybe sixty meters astern and falling behind fast, one arm waving, the other pressed to his head. Alive. Alive and shrinking.

Gideon was already at the engine controls, shouting down the hatch: "Hard aport! Coming around!" And then to Okafor, over the wind: "Throw ring! Get a line on him!"

Okafor grabbed the life ring off its bracket, coiled the attached line, and threw it the way he'd thrown a thousand times—out and across, leading the target. The wind took it and dumped it ten meters short, and the line's coil caught on the rail stanchion and jerked to a stop, and the ring sat in the water doing nothing while Tomas fell further behind.

"Again!" Gideon shouted.

He hauled the line back hand over hand, fast, the rope burning his palms, and threw again, and this time the wind flattened his throw entirely, sent the ring skipping off a wave crest like a stone. Sixty meters became eighty. The *Patient Answer* was wallowing through her turn, her old engine groaning up to full revolutions, and every second the gap grew was a second the sea got to keep what it had taken.

And Okafor stood at the rail with a rope in his hands and understood, with total clarity, that the rope was not going to work. That the drill was a lie. That if he kept throwing rings at a man eighty meters away in breaking seas, he was performing rescue instead of doing it, and Tomas Quintana was going to die twenty meters at a time while everyone aboard felt very busy.

There was another way. There was always another way, and it was always the stupid one.

He shrugged out of his oilskin jacket. He pulled the knife from his belt and cut loose a length of the heaviest line on the winch drum—ten meters, no more, enough to reach and be reached—and tied it around his own waist in a bowline his father had taught him when he was seven years old, on a beach, in another country, in another life.

"Okafor." Gideon's voice. She'd seen. She was coming up the deck toward him with her face gone white under the windburn. "No. Absolutely not. We'll get another throw—"

"You won't." He stepped up onto the rail, one boot on the top bar, the sea heaving below him like something breathing. "He goes under twice more, we lose sight of him, and then he's a name on a list. I'm the strongest swimmer on this boat and you know it."

"I'm ordering you—"

"With respect, Captain." And here was the thing, the thing he'd never said in eleven years of crewing for her, and it came out of him now as easy as stepping off a rail: "You don't get to order me to watch family drown. That's not a thing this boat does."

He saw it land. Saw it hit her somewhere behind the eyes, deeper than argument. Then she nodded, once, sharp, and moved to the winch. "Line's yours. Sela! Come take this rope and don't let go of it no matter what you see! TOMAS!" She cupped her hands and roared aft, into the wind, again and again, his name like a weapon: "TOMAS! SWIM TO THE VOICE! TOMAS!"

Okafor jumped.

* * *

The water hit him like a fist. Cold—not killing cold, not these latitudes, but cold enough to squeeze the air out of his chest in one involuntary grunt, cold enough that his body wanted to spend its first ten seconds panicking about temperature instead of swimming. He surfaced, blew the spray out of his mouth, and got his bearings: the *Patient Answer* rolling huge above him, Sela on the stern with the rope wrapped twice around her fists and her mouth open in a shout he couldn't hear; and beyond her, a hundred meters of gray chaos, an orange jacket, an arm.

He swam.

Okafor had been a swimmer before he'd been anything else—had grown up in water, on water, under water, had a relationship with the sea that was older and simpler than fear. But nothing prepared you for swimming in confused seas, where every wave lifted you and dropped you and stole your horizon, where you breathed salt as often as air, where progress was measured in faith. He swam crawl with his head up, breathing on every third stroke whether he wanted to or not, and counted strokes because counting kept the animal part of his brain quiet. Twenty strokes. Forty. The rope paid out behind him, dragging, and he blessed Sela silently for being small and stubborn and anchored to a boat.

The orange jacket disappeared.

Went under between one stroke and the next, swallowed by the back of a swell, and Okafor put his face in the water and sprinted blind on faith alone, arms hauling, lungs burning, and came up the far side of the wave with his heart hammering and—

Nothing. Gray water. Gray water. Gray—

There. Ten meters, off his left shoulder, the jacket again—but lower in the water now, the arm not waving anymore, and Okafor felt the cold slide through him that had nothing to do with temperature. He put his head down and swam harder than he had ever swum in his life, and the last twenty meters passed in a red haze of effort, and then his hand closed on fabric.

Tomas was conscious. Barely. His eyes were unfocused, blood sheeting down one side of his face from a gash above the hairline, and when Okafor got an arm across his chest the man's whole body tried to climb him at once—the drowning panic, the animal need, hands scrabbling at Okafor's shoulders trying to push him under to stand on him.

"No." Okafor got his forearm under Tomas's chin and clamped it, turned the man's back to his chest, locked his legs around him. "No. Stop. Stop fighting me or we both die. I've got you. *I've got you.*"

Whether the words got through or the body finally ran out of panic, he'd never know. But Tomas went slack against him, breathing in great ragged sobs, and Okafor tipped his own head back and shouted toward the boat with everything left in his lungs:

"TAKE US IN! TWO ON THE ROPE! TAKE US IN!"

* * *

They nearly lost him at the boat. Of course they did. The sea doesn't give presents.

Hauling two grown men up a pitching hull on a rope is a different arithmetic than hauling one, and the first attempt ended with Tomas rising clear of the water and then dropping back with a smack that Okafor felt through their linked bodies. The second attempt, Gideon and Sela together on the line, Gideon with her boots braced against the coaming and her whole slight body bent backward like a drawn bow—they gained a meter, lost a meter, gained two. On the third try Okafor got one boot against the hull plating below the waterline and pushed, and the four of them made a machine of themselves for ten seconds, and then Tomas's weight came over the rail and crashed onto the deck, and a moment later Okafor followed, hauled up by hands under his arms, and lay on the wet steel plating of his own deck gasping like a landed fish.

Above him, the sky had gone soft. The storm was truly done now, way off to the east, grumbling its way out of the world, and the light through the thinning cloud was the pale gold of late afternoon, and it fell on the *Patient Answer*'s littered, salt-scoured deck like a benediction.

Sela was kneeling over Tomas, pressing a cloth to his scalp, saying his name in her flat, precise way until his eyes tracked her. Gideon stood over them both, dripping, shaking—actually shaking, Okafor saw, her hands trembling at her sides—and when Tomas focused on her face and managed a ruined half-smile and started to say something, she cut him off.

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. She knelt, took his bloody face in both hands, and pressed her forehead to his, and held it there, and said nothing else at all.

Okafor lay on his back and watched the clouds and let his arms throb.

Later—much later, after Sela had stitched the gash with sail thread and half a bottle of antiseptic, after the engine room flood had been pumped out and the broken lashing re-rigged, after the *Patient Answer* was running clean south-southeast through settling seas—Okafor found Tomas sitting alone on the aft hatch cover, wrapped in a blanket, watching the wake. Okafor lowered himself down beside him with the groan of a man whose entire body had filed a formal complaint.

They sat in silence for a while. The wake unspooled behind them, long and white and already closing over.

"I did the sums," Tomas said finally. His voice was scraped raw from salt. "Out there. In the water. First minute, I thought, this is it, this is how it ends, and I started doing the sums of my life. What I'd sold. Who I'd sold it to. What it added up to." He turned the blanket's edge over in his fingers. "It added up to nothing, Okafor. Forty years and it added up to nothing. And then this enormous idiot comes swimming through the waves for me, against orders, against sense, and I thought—" His voice went. He cleared his throat. "I thought, why? Why would he? I sold his captain out once. He knows it. Everyone knows it."

Okafor looked out at the water for a while before answering.

"My mother used to say a thing," he said. "Back home. She said: the tide doesn't ask the drowned man whose side he was on. It just brings him back or it doesn't." He shrugged. "You're on this boat. That's the whole of it. Whatever you were before you climbed aboard—that's the tide's business, not mine."

"That's not—" Tomas laughed, wet and cracked. "That's not how trust works."

"No," Okafor agreed. "Trust works like this: you almost die, somebody hauls you out, and then you spend the rest of the time earning it. One day at a time. Same as anybody." He pushed himself up off the hatch cover, joints protesting. "Starting tomorrow, you're on lines with me. Dawn watch. Every dawn. No arguments."

Tomas looked up at him, and something shifted in his face—the harbor-rat polish cracking away, the thing underneath raw and young. "Why dawn?"

"Because dawn's when the work is real," Okafor said. "Everything else is talk."

He was halfway to the wheelhouse when Sela's voice stopped him—coming down from the bow, where she'd been standing with the cipher pages weighted under a lantern, and there was something in her tone that turned his blood over. Not fear, exactly. Worse. Recognition.

"Gideon," she called. "You need to see this. Now."

Okafor turned. Ahead, low on the darkening horizon off the starboard bow, running lights had appeared—a ship, hull-down, moving on an intercept course. Moving fast. Too fast for a freighter, too steady for a fisherman, and painted on her bow in letters none of them could read yet at this distance but all of them somehow already knew.

Someone else had gotten the coordinates.

And she was faster than they were.
