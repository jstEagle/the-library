## 13. Second Tide: The Rival

The second tide came in gray and mean, a low swell rolling out of the southeast like something the sea had swallowed and decided it didn't want. Gideon stood at the wheel of the *Patient Answer* with her collar up against the spray, watching the horizon do that thing she'd learned to read before she could read words—the flat line going slightly wrong, slightly crowded, the way water looks when there's metal under it.

"Contact," Okafor said from the bow, not turning around. He didn't need to. His voice carried the way it always did, low and even, the voice of a man who had learned that panic was a luxury item he couldn't afford. "Two points off the port bow. Running lights dark."

Gideon's stomach did what it always did when running lights went dark on open water, which was to remember every story she'd ever heard about salvage crews that met other salvage crews at sea and only one of them came home.

"How far?"

"Four miles. Maybe five. She's big." Okafor finally turned, and his face was doing its best impression of calm, which was how Gideon knew he was worried. Okafor's real calm looked like nothing at all. His performed calm looked like effort. "Bigger than us. A lot bigger than us."

Sela emerged from the cabin with the tide-table cipher clutched to her chest like a hymnal, her glasses fogged from the temperature change. "Is it them? The Syndicate?"

"No," Gideon said. She'd been listening to the radio for two days, cataloguing voices, learning the Syndicate's choreography the way you learn a storm's rotation—by the shape of its noise. The Syndicate moved in threes. Escorts out wide, the money ship in the middle, everything talking in clipped code on frequencies they thought were private. This was one ship, alone, silent, coming straight at them out of the gray like it had been waiting behind the weather for exactly this moment. "This is somebody else."

"That's better," Sela said.

"It might be worse," Gideon said. "The Syndicate wants the chest. Everybody else wants the chest *and* doesn't have lawyers."

Tomas came up from below decks where he'd been pretending to sleep for six hours, which was a skill he'd apparently perfected sometime during the years he and Gideon hadn't spoken. He squinted at the shape resolving on the horizon, and Gideon watched him go through the same arithmetic she was doing: run or talk, run or talk, and both options ending somewhere expensive.

"She's flying no colors," Tomas said. "But I know that silhouette. That's a Corvus-class hull. Deep-water salvage rig, crane aft, reinforced bow for ice work." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice had changed, gone quieter, more careful. "There's only one ship on this coast that runs a hull like that."

"Name it," Gideon said.

"*Corvus*," Tomas said. "Captain's name is Reyen. Ilsa Reyen." He looked at Gideon then, really looked, with an expression she couldn't parse, and added: "My advice would be to turn thirty degrees south and never think about her again."

"Why?"

"Because Ilsa Reyen doesn't chase rumors. She chases certainties. And if she's found *us*, out here, on this heading—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "She knows where we're going. Which means somebody talked, or somebody's very good at math."

Gideon looked at the gray shape growing on the horizon and felt the strange, cold clarity that had been settling over her since the night she'd opened the chest. Fear was still there—it lived in her now like a tenant who paid no rent—but underneath it was something else, something that had been waking up slowly since Lark's first smile in her direction. Curiosity. The same fatal curiosity that had made her winch up a lead-lined box instead of dropping it back into the river where it belonged.

"Hold our course," she said.

Okafor turned fully around this time. "Captain—"

"We can't outrun a Corvus-class hull. We can't hide from a ship that already has our heading. If she wanted us dead, we'd already be dead—she's had gun range for ten minutes." Gideon heard her own voice come out steady and was faintly surprised by it. "So we hold course, and we see what she wants, and if it goes wrong, Sela has the cipher memorized twice over and the chest is lashed under the netting where nobody finds it without cutting the deck open."

"You've thought about this," Okafor said.

"I've thought about nothing else for two days."

* * *

The *Corvus* closed the distance with the unhurried confidence of a thing that had never once needed to hurry. Up close she was even more imposing—a hundred and forty feet of black steel, her crane folded flat along her spine like a sleeping arm, her hull scarred with the pale waterline ghosts of a dozen oceans. Her name was painted on the bow in flaking white letters, and beneath the name, almost worn away, someone had painted a smaller motto that Gideon had to squint through the spray to read: *WE ASK THE SEA POLITELY.*

"Somebody with a sense of humor," Sela murmured beside her.

"Somebody with a sense of irony," Tomas corrected. "It's different. More expensive."

A voice crackled across the radio, female, roughened by salt and cigarettes and decades of shouting over engines. "*Patient Answer*, this is the *Corvus*. You're a long way from anywhere in a boat held together with paint. Come about and match my heading. My captain would like a word."

"And if we decline?" Gideon keyed back.

A pause. Then, with something that might have been amusement: "Then we'll follow you until you run out of fuel, which at your tonnage and current burn rate will be approximately tomorrow afternoon. Captain's choice."

Gideon looked at Okafor. Okafor looked at the sky as if consulting it, got no answer, and shrugged with his whole philosophy of life in the gesture: *we were dead anyway, might as well be dead curious.*

"Matching your heading," Gideon said.

They ran together for twenty minutes, the little trawler wallowing in the wake of the big ship while the coast slid past off their starboard beam—drowned country, all of it, sandbars and shoals and the green-brown stain of the Meridian delta bleeding into the sea. Gideon had grown up on these waters and had never once thought of them as a nation's grave. Now she couldn't look at the coastline without seeing a headstone.

A gangway ladder dropped down the *Corvus*'s flank. Gideon left Sela aboard with orders to sink the cipher rather than surrender it, took Okafor because she trusted him more than she trusted anyone alive, and climbed.

Ilsa Reyen was waiting for her on the aft deck, and Gideon knew her instantly—not from photographs, though there had been one, once, in her mother's things, a woman with a shaved head and a harpoon scar down her cheek standing on some distant deck—but from the way she stood. Weight on the balls of her feet. Hands loose. Eyes doing the work of a whole crew.

Elena Quintana had stood the same way. Gideon had spent her whole life being told she stood like her mother, and had hated it, and here was the proof of why.

Ilsa Reyen was perhaps sixty, built like the anchor chain she'd probably been hauling since childhood, with the harpoon scar Gideon remembered and a face weathered down to its essential architecture. She looked at Gideon, and Gideon watched something happen in that face—something she had no name for. It wasn't surprise. It wasn't suspicion. It was the look of a person opening a door they'd sealed shut decades ago and finding the room inside exactly as they'd left it.

"Gideon," Ilsa said.

Not *who are you*. Not *Quintana's girl*. Just her name, first and only, spoken with the flat certainty of someone reading it off a manifest they'd memorized years ago.

"You know me," Gideon said.

"I've known you since before you existed." Ilsa's eyes went over her shoulder, cataloguing the trawler, the crew, the lashed cargo netting, missing nothing. "Your mother used to stand like that. Chin down, weight forward, like she was about to argue with the wind and expected to win." Something moved through the hard lines of her face and was gone. "God. You're the exact height, too. It's obscene."

"My mother's been dead eleven years," Gideon said carefully. "If you knew her, you're the first person who's ever admitted it."

"Oh, I knew her." Ilsa turned and walked, expecting to be followed, and Gideon followed because there was nothing else in the world to do. "Knew her better than most. Knew her debts, her temper, her tide-tables. Knew her the way you know a person you've shared a fo'c'sle with through two monsoon seasons and one shipwreck." She stopped at the rail and looked south, toward the drowned delta, toward whatever lay under the water at coordinates six tides out. "And I know what she left in that river, Gideon, because I'm the one who told her where to put it."

The deck seemed to tilt under Gideon's feet, though the sea was flat as poured lead.

"The chest," she said. "You knew about the chest."

"I helped pack it." Ilsa finally turned to face her fully, and up close her eyes were the pale washed-out gray of old chart paper, and they were looking at Gideon with that unbearable, ghost-seeing attention again. "Twenty-two years ago. Lead lining, sun-and-wave seal, cipher in the tide-tables. Elena did the encoding herself—said if anyone could crack it, it'd be someone who loved the water enough to learn its grammar." Her mouth twisted. "She always did talk like that. Like the sea was a language instead of a hazard."

"Twenty-two years." Gideon's voice sounded far away to her own ears. "She disappeared eleven years ago. Why wait?"

"Because the chest wasn't meant for you yet." Ilsa gripped the rail, and Gideon saw that her knuckles were white beneath the weathered skin. "It was meant for whoever came looking after Elena stopped coming home. That was the design. She planted it like a seed and waited to see what grew." Her gaze dragged across Gideon's face, feature by feature, hunting for something. "I told her she was gambling with her daughter's life. She told me her daughter was the only person alive stubborn enough to survive the gamble."

The wind came off the water cold and smelling of rust and kelp. Somewhere below them, the engines of two ships idled in counterpoint, patient as breathing.

"Six tides out," Ilsa said quietly. "That's where she's pointing you, isn't it? The flagship."

Gideon said nothing, which was answer enough.

Ilsa nodded slowly, as if a long calculation had finally resolved. "Then you should hear the rest of it. Because Elena left a message with me, Gideon. A message I was sworn to deliver only if she died—and only if you ever came asking about Meridia." She stepped closer, close enough that Gideon could see the fine map of scars on her hands, each one a story with a bad ending. "And I have to tell you, girl, looking at you standing there with her chin and her stubbornness and her complete disregard for self-preservation—"

She exhaled, long and slow, like a diver surfacing.

"—it's like watching a ghost walk back aboard."

Behind Gideon, Okafor shifted his weight, hand drifting toward the knife at his belt. Ilsa noticed and smiled without any humor at all.

"Easy, deckhand. I've been waiting eleven years to keep a promise. I don't intend to break it now that the heir's finally shown up." She jerked her head toward the deckhouse. "Come inside. Both of you. What I have to say doesn't belong to the wind."

Gideon followed her through the door, heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out—or maybe trying to get *in*—and the last thing she saw before the deckhouse swallowed them was the drowned coastline sliding past, patient and green and full of secrets, waiting six tides away for the daughter of Elena Quintana to come and dig up what the sea had been paid to keep.
