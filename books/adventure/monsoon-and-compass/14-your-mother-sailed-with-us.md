## 14. Your Mother Sailed With Us

Ilsa Reyen had buried a lot of things in her life—husbands, ships, the better part of her left hand—and she'd learned that the dead don't stay buried so much as they wait. They wait in the hold of a ship, in the bottom drawer of a chart table, in the sealed envelope you're not supposed to open until a condition is met. Elena Quintana had been waiting eleven years in the false bottom of Ilsa's sea chest, and now she was standing on the deck of the *Corvus* wearing her daughter's face, and Ilsa's hands would not stop shaking.

"Sit down," she said. "Both of you. Not you, Okafor—you can stand, you make me nervous sitting."

The galley of the *Corvus* was small and smelled of diesel and old coffee, and it was the only place aboard where Ilsa allowed herself to be an old woman instead of a captain. She poured three cups without asking, because asking was for people with time.

"You have her chin," she said to Gideon. "And her terrible habit of looking at weather like it owes her money."

Gideon didn't touch the cup. "You knew my mother."

"I sailed with her for six years." Ilsa sat down heavily across from her, and the chair complained the way old chairs do when they've carried more than furniture. "Before you were born. Before Meridia went under. She wasn't Elena Quintana then. She was Elena Vasquez, junior navigator aboard the *Meridian Star*, last flagship of a nation that no longer appears on charts."

Sela made a small sound—the sound a cartographer makes when someone says something impossible about geography.

"The *Meridian Star*," Sela said. "That ship was lost with all hands during the evacuation. It's in every salvage registry on the coast. There are dive schools named after it."

"There are lies named after it," Ilsa said. "Which is nearly the same thing and pays better." She wrapped her ruined hand around the cup, the scarred knuckles white. "You want to know how your mother died? You want to know why there's no grave? Then you'll hear it from the beginning, because the beginning is the only part of this story anyone ever gets right."

* * *

"I was nineteen," Ilsa said, "and stupid in the way nineteen-year-olds at sea are stupid, which is to say brave about the wrong things. The *Star* was a remnant vessel by then. Meridia had already started drowning—not the land, understand, the *nation*. The water came later, and slower, and it was almost a mercy compared to what came first.

"What came first was paper.

"You don't remember Meridia. Nobody does, really—they remember the word, they remember the seal, sun over waves, they remember that the treasury was never found and everyone likes a mystery. But Meridia was a real country once. Small. Wealthy in the way that makes neighbors hungry. And it had something rarer than wealth: it had legitimacy. Charters signed by kings and republics alike. Treaties. Sovereignty recognized by forty governments, written down, witnessed, sealed. You could drown a nation, but if the papers survive, the nation survives. Any court in the world would have heard Meridia's case. Any court.

"Some people understood that. One of them was a young man named Augustin Vane, who was then what he is now—a man who buys things that aren't for sale.

"Elena figured it out before any of us. She was the best navigator I ever served under, and I say that as a woman who has served under admirals. She read currents the way priests read scripture, but more than that, she read *people*, and one season she started reading the wrong ones. Officers taking meetings ashore and lying about where. Cargo manifests that didn't add up. A purser who suddenly had money. She came to me one night in the chart room—I'll tell it as she told me, because I've had eleven years to memorize her words and I don't trust my own anymore—and she said:

"'Ilsa. If a country can be stolen, where does the theft happen? Not in the harbor. Not in the treasury. In the archive. You steal a nation by stealing its paperwork and letting the water do the rest.'

"She'd found out that the fleet was being scuttled piecemeal. Not lost—*scuttled*. Opened seacocks in home ports, ships sent into storms they were built to survive, crews paid off or transferred or, in a few cases, drowned for knowing too much. And through it all, one ship kept moving cargo that appeared on no manifest: the *Meridian Star*, running dark, carrying the admiral's strongbox.

"The last admiral—Admiral Osei, though his name survives nowhere now—had made a decision. If Meridia was going to die, it would not die quietly. He loaded everything: the original charter, the treaties, the ledgers, the correspondence proving who had bought which minister and signed which order. Proof enough to resurrect a drowned nation or hang the men who drowned it. He meant to run it out beyond salvage depth and sink it somewhere only he would know.

"Vane wanted it sunk somewhere *he* would know.

"And Elena—" Ilsa stopped. Her voice had gone rough, and she took a drink of coffee she clearly didn't taste. "Elena was the one who convinced the admiral to change the plan. Don't ask me how. She never told me the whole of it. But the night before the *Star*'s final voyage, she came off the flagship carrying nothing but a canvas bag and a look on her face I've seen twice since—once on a woman whose house had burned with her children inside, and once in a mirror, the day I signed papers selling my own ship to pay a debt I didn't owe.

"She told me she was going back aboard. She told me the *Star* would go down where the admiral intended, and that no one—not Vane, not the fleets, not the historians—would find it. And she gave me a sealed envelope and said:

"'If I come back, burn this and never speak of it. If I don't—if my daughter ever comes to you with salt in her hair and her father's stubbornness in her jaw—give it to her. Only her. Not before. She has to find the chest herself, Ilsa. She has to choose it. If it's given, she'll sell it. If she chooses it, she'll keep it.'

"I asked her how she could possibly know she'd have a daughter. She smiled—that smile, the one you're doing right now without meaning to—and said, 'Because I've already decided to.'"

Ilsa looked up. Gideon had gone very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"She never came back," Ilsa said. "Eleven days later the *Meridian Star* was officially lost with all hands in the Corvus Deep, and the salvage registries wrote their fiction, and Augustin Vane bought up the wreck rights through nine shell companies so that no honest diver would ever get near it. And Elena Quintana—she'd taken her husband's name by then, did you know that? She'd gotten out, married, had you, tried to live a small life on a big coast—but she never stopped watching. Every few years she'd send me a letter with no return address. Weather reports, mostly. And warnings."

* * *

Gideon's voice, when it finally came, was flat in the way that deep water is flat. "Warnings about what?"

"About you." Ilsa said it gently, which for her was an effort like hauling anchor. "About what you'd become. 'My daughter chases storms,' she wrote, the last letter, eight years ago. 'She thinks she's running toward money. She's running toward the wreck. The chest will find her the way the sea finds every low place. When it does, tell her: the chest is bait. What sleeps under the flagship is worse than treasure.'

"I thought it was grief talking. A woman who'd lost a country and a crew, writing riddles to an old shipmate." Ilsa set down the cup. "Then two days ago my radio picks up harbor chatter—three salvage fleets mobilizing, Syndicate gunboats moving, all of it orbiting coordinates six tides out of Port Mercy. Coordinates that match nothing on any chart I own. Except I've spent thirty years owning charts, Gideon, and I know what those numbers are. They're tide-table readings. Meridian naval shorthand. Dead language, dead navy, dead nation—and somebody just dug up its arithmetic."

She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the scars on her hand.

"So I sailed to intercept you. Not for the chest. Vane can have the chest, and welcome—it's empty, isn't it? Or near enough. No." Her eyes were steady and terrible. "I came because a promise is a kind of anchor, and mine has been dragging eleven years. Your mother chose you for this, Gideon. Out of every possible life, she looked at the one you'd live—debt, storms, salvage, running—and she said *that one*. That's the one who'll finish it. Because she couldn't. Because the admiral couldn't. Because a whole nation of drowned people couldn't.

"She planted the chest. Or she arranged it before she vanished—left instructions, left the cipher, set it drifting down the years toward the flooded river she knew you'd dredge, because she knew you the way captains know weather. She knew you'd open it. She knew you'd lie to Lark. She knew you'd sail into the storm line rather than around it, God help me, because she did the same thing twice."

"That's insane," Tomas said quietly, from the corner where he'd been standing like a man at his own trial. "You're telling us a dead woman rigged a salvage operation eleven years long."

"I'm telling you," Ilsa said, "that Elena Quintana was the finest navigator of a drowned nation, and navigation is just the art of knowing where you'll arrive before you leave. Yes. Insane. Also true. Ask yourself why the Syndicate posted a bounty within hours of the chest surfacing. Ask yourself why Vane's collectors move like men who've been waiting for something specific. Ask yourself why a chest that heavy, that well-sealed, that lead-lined, was sitting shallow enough for a storm-chaser's winch to snag." She spread her scarred hand flat on the table. "It wasn't lost, girl. It was *delivered*."

The galley was silent except for the tick of the cooling engine and the sea doing its endless work against the hull. Gideon stared at the envelope Ilsa had placed on the table between them—her mother's handwriting on the front, faded to the color of driftwood, addressed in Meridian shorthand that Sela was already leaning toward with her whole body.

"Don't," Ilsa said, without looking at Sela. "It's hers. Hers alone."

Gideon picked it up. Turned it over. Eleven years of waiting weighed less than a letter should.

"When did she write this?" Gideon asked.

"The night before she went back to the *Star*." Ilsa stood, joints cracking like rigging in a blow. "The night before she drowned saving a country nobody remembers existed. Read it below decks, alone, and then come find me on the bridge, because there's one more thing you need to hear and I'll only say it once, and it's about what the admiral put in that strongbox besides paper."

Gideon got halfway to the door and stopped.

"My mother," she said. "Did she know she was going to die?"

Ilsa met her eyes across the little galley, and for a moment the old captain looked every year of her age and every mile of her service.

"She knew it was the price," she said. "She went anyway. Now you know where you get it from."

The envelope sat in Gideon's hands like a held breath, and above them both, through the deck plates, came the sound of the tide turning—six tides counting down, and somewhere out in the dark, the *Perpetual Dawn* already burning coal toward the coordinates only three living people could read.
