## 15. Third Tide: The Warning

Ilsa Reyen poured the tea like she was defusing something.

Gideon watched from the Corvus's cramped galley, the deck plates humming under her boots, the whole ship creaking with the particular complaint of a vessel that had been worked hard and apologized to never. Outside the porthole, the sea lay flat and pewter-colored, holding its breath between tides. Somewhere behind them, three salvage fleets were burning fuel to close a gap. Somewhere ahead, the flagship of a drowned nation waited on the bottom with whatever her mother had wanted her to find.

Or wanted her not to find. The message hadn't been clear yet, and Gideon's hands wouldn't stop moving—turning the tin cup, squaring it against the table's worn grain, turning it again.

"Drink," Ilsa said. "You look like a woman deciding whether to jump."

"I'm deciding whether to keep sailing."

"Same thing, most days." Ilsa sat across from her, and up close Gideon could see the age in her that the bridge distance had hidden—the sun-scoured creases, the white threading her braid, the scar splitting one eyebrow into two pale commas. She'd known Elena Quintana. She'd sailed with her. And for eleven years she had carried a message in her chest like ballast, waiting for a daughter who didn't know she existed as anything but a name on a headstone that wasn't there.

"You said you'd tell me when we were underway," Gideon said. "We're underway."

Ilsa looked at the porthole a long moment, as if the gray water might object to what came next.

"She made me swear two things. First, that I'd only give it to you if she was dead. Second—" Ilsa's mouth twisted, "—that I'd only give it if you were already committed. Already past the point where turning back was easy. She said, *if Gideon is the woman I think she is, she'll have found the chest, and she'll be too far out to turn around without breaking something in herself.*"

The cup stopped moving under Gideon's fingers.

"She knew about the chest."

"Elena knew about everything." Ilsa said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Sit down properly. This takes the time it takes."

* * *

The Corvus ran quiet through the slack water while Ilsa talked, and Gideon let the words come in the order they came, because interrupting felt like it might break whatever thread was holding the story together.

Elena Quintana had come aboard the Corvus twenty-three years ago at a port called Vell's Landing, paying passage with Meridian silver that shouldn't have existed. Not coin—ingots, stamped with the sun-and-wave, the kind of thing that got salvage fleets murdered over. Ilsa's father had been captain then, and he'd taken one look at the metal, one look at the woman carrying it, and asked no questions, because some questions cost more than the answers.

"She was running," Ilsa said. "Not from debt, not from the law. From an offer. Same offer, I'd wager, that's going to be put to you eventually, if it hasn't been already." Her eyes flicked up. "The Syndicate found her. Or she found them. Either way, Augustin Vane sat across a table from your mother and offered her a fleet of her own, a name that would open any harbor on the coast, and enough marks to drown in. All she had to do was bring him something she'd recovered."

"The charter," Gideon said quietly.

Ilsa's eyebrows rose a fraction. "So you know."

"We pulled documents off a wreck two weeks ago. Ledgers. Purchase orders for a nation." Gideon heard her own voice go flat and strange. "Vane's grandfather didn't just profit when Meridia drowned. He arranged the drowning. Sold the sovereignty out from under them before the water ever came, then bought the seabed rights for pennies. My mother found proof."

"Your mother found proof," Ilsa agreed, "and refused the offer, and walked off Vane's flagship with her life—which should tell you something about how frightened they were of what she knew—and spent the next decade making sure that proof would outlive her. She wasn't hiding from the Syndicate, girl. She was laying a trail. For you."

The galley seemed smaller. The hum of the engines seemed louder. Gideon became very aware of her own breathing.

"She planted the chest," Gideon said. "In the river. Lead-lined, sealed, weighted so only a dredge would ever bring it up. She knew I dredged floodwater. She knew I couldn't leave a snag alone."

"She told me once: *my daughter can't resist a knot. It'll be the death of her or the saving of her, and I mean to bet on both.*" Ilsa smiled, and it was a sad, weathered thing. "She built the cipher herself. Took her four years, learning the old naval shorthand from a Meridian clerk who'd washed ashore half-mad. She wanted the trail hard enough that only someone stubborn would follow it, and legible enough that someone stubborn could."

"And the message." Gideon's throat had gone dry. "You said there was a message."

Ilsa set down her own cup. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—flattened, formal, the cadence of recitation rather than conversation. Gideon realized, with a chill that started at the scalp, that Ilsa had rehearsed this. That she'd carried these words verbatim for eleven years, polishing them against the inside of her skull, waiting.

"*If you're hearing this, my love, then I failed to come back, and the Corvus kept its word, and you've gone and followed my knots all the way out here.*" Ilsa's eyes stayed fixed on Gideon's. "*Listen carefully, because I'll only say this once, even secondhand. The chest is bait. Not for you—for everyone. I filled it with enough real Meridian material to make every fleet on the coast bleed each other for the coordinates. But understand what I understood: what sleeps under the flagship is worse than treasure. Vane knows it. His grandfather's ledgers aren't the only copies. There is a strongbox in that engine room that has ended every life that's touched it, and it will end yours too unless you decide, right now, whose side the truth works for. Paper doesn't take sides, Gideon. People do. Decide before you dive, not after.*"

Silence. The engine hummed. Somewhere above them, Okafor's boots crossed the deck, heavy and unhurried, and the sound of it—that ordinary, faithful sound—nearly undid her.

"That's it?" Gideon managed. "That's the whole warning?"

"There was a postscript." Ilsa hesitated, and for the first time since Gideon had boarded, the old captain looked uncertain. "She said: *tell her the tide-table has a second key. If Sela hasn't found it yet, she will. And tell her—I chose the water for you. I'm sorry. I'd choose it again.*"

Gideon stood up so fast the chair went over backward.

* * *

She made it four steps onto the Corvus's aft deck before her legs informed her they had opinions, and she gripped the rail and stared at the water and breathed.

*I chose the water for you.*

Eleven years Gideon had built stories around her mother's disappearance. Ran afoul of the Syndicate. Drowned chasing salvage. Walked out on us because we weren't enough. Every story had one thing in common: Elena Quintana had left, and leaving was a verdict, and Gideon had grown up reading that verdict as *not enough*. Not worth staying for. Not worth a note, a forwarding address, a grave.

And now: *I chose the water for you. I'd choose it again.*

It didn't feel like absolution. It felt like being handed a live wire and told it was a family heirloom.

"Gideon." Tomas's voice, behind her. Careful. He'd learned, in the last week, to approach her the way you approach a dog that's been kicked by people wearing familiar faces. "Okafor says we've got maybe forty minutes of slack before the tide turns. Sela wants to run the numbers again before we commit to the final approach. And—" he paused, "—you look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse. I've seen a letter." She turned. Her brother stood silhouetted against the gray sky, and she searched his face for the boy who'd taught her to pick locks on their father's chart drawers, and found instead a man who'd sold her out once and was trying, clumsily, expensively, to buy his way back. "Elena planned this. All of it. The chest, the cipher, the trail. She planted it years ago and waited for me to trip over it."

Tomas absorbed that. He was good at absorbing—he'd always been the listener, the one who watched the room while Gideon burned it down. "Does that make it better or worse?"

"I don't know yet."

"It makes it both," he said. "That's usually how she operated. Remember the birthday presents? You'd get a gift that was also a lesson. A compass that only worked if you calibrated it yourself. A lockbox with no key." Something moved through his face, quick and painful. "She was teaching us the whole time. We just thought she was being difficult."

Gideon laughed—a short, broken sound that surprised them both. "A lockbox with no key. God. She buried the answer inside the puzzle and called it parenting."

"So what did the letter say?" Tomas asked. "The part that put that look on you."

Gideon weighed it. Telling him everything meant telling him about the strongbox, about *worse than treasure*, about the fact that her mother had predicted—had engineered—this exact moment: her daughter standing at the edge of commitment, being offered one last clean exit. And the part of her that had spent a lifetime not trusting Tomas whispered that information was the one currency he'd traded before.

But the other part—the part that had watched him haul himself back over the rail after the wave took him, coughing seawater and refusing to let go of Okafor's arm—whispered back that trust was a muscle, and hers had atrophied from disuse.

"She said the wreck is dangerous," Gideon said. "More dangerous than treasure. And she said to decide whose side the truth works for before I dive." She met his eyes. "I haven't decided yet. That's the honest answer."

Tomas nodded slowly. "Then don't decide alone. That's what a crew's for. That's what—" he almost said something else, swallowed it, "—that's what family's supposed to be for. Even the parts that come late and damaged."

* * *

They gathered in the Corvus's chart room because it was bigger than the Patient Answer's and because Ilsa, unasked, had offered it with a grunt that in her language constituted an embrace. Sela spread the tide-table transcription across the table, weighted at the corners with brass instruments, and Okafor leaned on the doorframe with his arms crossed, occupying space the way he always did—like a wall that had decided, provisionally, to be on your side.

"Say it again," Sela said. "All of it. Slowly."

Gideon gave them the message entire, watching their faces as it landed. Sela went still at the mention of a second key—Gideon saw her fingers start moving unconsciously, tracing cipher patterns on the tabletop, already working. Okafor's jaw tightened at *bait*, and didn't loosen. Tomas stood closest to the door, which Gideon noted, and chose not to read too much into.

"Bait," Okafor said flatly, when she finished. "Your mother rigged a trap, and we're the mice, and you're asking me how I feel about cheese."

"I'm asking whether we turn back."

"Turn back to what?" Okafor's voice wasn't angry; that was the terrible thing about it. It was patient. "Lark finds us by tomorrow either way. The Syndicate's posted the coordinates' value—we heard it on the band. Forty thousand marks for the finder of Meridian materials. That's your debt, Gideon. To the mark. They priced you personally." He unfolded his arms and stepped into the room. "There's no version of this where we sail home and the debt collector smiles and lets it go. You know that. I know that. The only question the message actually asks is whether we dive knowing it's a trap, or dive thinking it isn't."

"Dive knowing," Sela said quietly. Everyone looked at her. She was staring at the tide-table, her finger resting on a column of figures. "Because I found the second key. Just now. While you were talking." She looked up, and her eyes were bright with the awful excitement Gideon had learned to fear in her—the look she got when understanding arrived faster than wisdom. "Elena hid a second coordinate set beneath the flagship's position. It's not the wreck itself. It's a point two hundred meters off the reef, in deeper water. And Gideon—the depth markings. The second layer isn't written for divers."

The chart room went very quiet.

"It's written for a submersible," Sela said. "Or for someone who intends to send something down and never come back for it."

"Or," Tomas said slowly, "for someone who intends to retrieve something and needs to know exactly how deep to bury it afterward."

Gideon looked at the table, at her mother's careful dead hand encoded in numbers, and felt the two futures pull at her like opposing currents. Turn back: Lark, the debt, the smiling men, a lifetime of running with the bill forever coming due. Go forward: a trap her mother had built and warned her about, a strongbox that had *ended every life that touched it*, a truth that could buy her freedom or destroy the only people she had left.

Doubt sat in her chest like ballast. Momentum sat beside it, heavier.

"Captain?" Okafor said. "It's your call. But call it out loud. We vote with our feet after."

Gideon walked to the porthole. Below the gray water, six tides south, her mother's flagship lay split on a reef, keeping its secret the way the sea keeps everything—patiently, absolutely, until someone foolish enough comes down with a light.

*I chose the water for you. I'd choose it again.*

Her mother had stood in front of Augustin Vane and said no, and paid for it, and spent her last years building a ladder down into the dark so her daughter could climb down and finish the sentence. Turning back wouldn't honor that. Turning back would just be another way of drowning—slower, warmer, with better company.

"No," Gideon said. She turned around. "We don't turn back. We go forward—but we go forward *knowing*. Nobody dives blind. Nobody touches that strongbox until Sela cracks every layer in that cipher, and I mean every layer. If my mother left a third key, we find it first. And Okafor—" she met his steady gaze, "—you rig the lines. If anything goes wrong down there, you cut us loose and save the ship. That's an order."

"Resignation noted and ignored," Okafor said. "But fine."

"Sela, work the second coordinate set. Tomas—" she caught his eye, "—I want you topside when we make the approach. Watching the horizon. Everything you see, you say out loud. No exceptions."

Something flickered across his face—relief, or hurt, or both—but he nodded. "Everything out loud."

Gideon put her hand flat on the tide-table, on her mother's hidden arithmetic. "Then we're committed. Six tides out, one reef, one strongbox, and whatever sleeps under the flagship." She looked around the chart room—at the cartographer with burned archives and steady hands, the deckhand who'd stayed past every reason to leave, the brother still earning his seat. "My mother bet on us. Let's not make her a liar."

Above them, the Corvus's bell rang the turn of the tide, and the ship came alive beneath their feet, leaning south toward deep water and the dark that waited there.

And on the radio, crackling through the static, a voice too cheerful to be anything but Lark's began to read coordinates.
