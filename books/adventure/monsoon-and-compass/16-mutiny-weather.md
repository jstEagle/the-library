## 16. Mutiny Weather

Sela had always believed that a map was a confession. Every chart told you what its maker feared—shoals inked in careful hatching, uncharted water left blank like a held breath. So when she spread the Meridian tide-table across the chart table in the Patient Answer's cramped aft cabin and pinned its corners with a brass cup, a spool of thread, and Okafor's lucky knife, she did it with the reverence owed to a dying man's last words.

The lamp swung gently above her as the trawler rolled through the long swell of the fourth tide's aftermath. Outside, Gideon had them on a heading that made no sense to anyone but her—a dogleg northwest through empty water, away from the flagship coordinates, buying time against fleets Sela could hear crackling on the radio even through the bulkhead. *Corvus to all stations. Corvus to all stations.* The voices were getting closer. The water between them and the wreck was shrinking.

But Sela wasn't listening to the radio. She was listening to the paper.

* * *

She had started with the assumption that the tide-table was what it appeared to be: a working document, the kind a navigator aboard the Meridian flagship would have kept at his elbow. Tides for the home ports, moon phases, correction tables—all of it encoded in the shorthand of a dead navy, a compression system Sela had spent three years of her exile reconstructing from fragments salvaged off drowned libraries. It had taken her two nights to read the first layer fluently. Two nights, and then the coordinates had bloomed out of the cipher like a photograph developing in solution: latitude, longitude, a tidal window six tides out from the chest's recovery. The flagship. Everyone's prize.

It should have ended there. A code yields its message; the message is the point.

But Sela had been a cartographer too long to trust a document that was only one thing. Charts, in her experience, were like people—the surface told you where they'd been, but the scars told you what they'd survived. And this paper had scars.

She saw the first one under magnification on the second night, after everyone else had gone below. The tide-table was written in iron-gall ink, brown-black now, the hand confident and naval, each symbol formed with the drilled precision of a man who had written ten thousand of them. But certain entries—she counted eleven before she stopped counting—were in a different ink entirely. Darker. Fresher, though "fresher" meant decades rather than years. And the symbols themselves were subtly wrong. Not wrong the way an error is wrong. Wrong the way a forgery is wrong: each stroke correct in isolation, but the rhythm off, the spacing just slightly too deliberate, as if the writer were copying shapes he understood but didn't think in.

Someone had added entries to a dead nation's tide-table. Someone who knew the shorthand well enough to fake it, but not natively.

And those eleven entries, decoded on their own, gave a second set of coordinates.

They didn't match the flagship.

* * *

Sela sat back from the table and let the lamp swing, shadows tilting across the chart like slow birds. Her hands were steady—her hands were always steady; it was the one inheritance from her surveyor father that had never failed her—but something behind her sternum had begun to move, a cold slow current she recognized from bad moments in her old life, the moment when a coastline refused to match its chart and you understood that either the land had moved or someone had lied to you.

The second coordinates put their position roughly ninety miles south-southeast of the flagship site. Open water on every modern chart. Nothing there. Which meant one of three things.

One: the second set was a decoy, planted by whoever altered the table, designed to pull treasure hunters off toward nothing while the real prize sat quiet. In which case the alteration predated the chest's planting, and the person who altered it had known exactly what kind of fools would come dredging rivers for Meridian gold.

Two: the second set marked something real—something other than the treasury—and the flagship coordinates were the decoy. In which case everything they were running from, every fleet, every gunboat, every hour of terror, was aimed at the wrong patch of ocean.

Three: both sets were real, and the table was a message with two recipients—one meant to find the flagship, and one meant to find whatever waited ninety miles southeast.

Sela had learned, in the hard years after her archive burned the first time—not the fire that would come later, that was still ahead of her, though she didn't know it yet, and the memory she carried into this night was of an older, smaller loss, a university position lost to politics and a life reduced to what fit in two satchels—she had learned that when a document offers you three explanations, the truest one is usually the one nobody wants.

Nobody wanted the third one. Because the third one meant the chest hadn't been lost. It had been *sent*. And if it had been sent, then someone had chosen Gideon Quintana to find it—and had built a second door into the cipher, a door that led somewhere the salvage fleets would never think to look.

She copied the second coordinates onto a scrap of chart backing, folded it twice, and sewed it into the lining of her coat with three stitches of waxed thread. Then she sat for a long time doing nothing at all, because the next part required her to decide what kind of person she was going to be on this boat.

There were four people aboard the Patient Answer. Gideon, who owned the debt and the dream. Okafor, who owned the engine and the conscience. Tomas, who owned contacts in every harbor bar from here to the capital and a history of selling things that weren't strictly his to sell. And Sela, who owned the only fluent reading of a dead language in four hundred miles.

If she said nothing, the second coordinates stayed hers alone. Leverage. Insurance. A cartographer who had lost everything once does not easily hand over the only map in her possession.

If she said something, she had to say it to all of them—including Tomas.

* * *

She found him first, deliberately, before she went to Gideon. Not because she suspected him—though the thought was present, patient, unavoidable, the way a toothache is present—but because she wanted to watch his face when she mentioned the cipher, and she wanted to do it somewhere he couldn't arrange himself beforehand.

Tomas was on deck coiling line in the grey afternoon light, working with the economy of a man who had done dock work since he was nine years old. He was good at it. That was the thing about Tomas that made trusting him so maddening—he was genuinely good at nearly everything he touched, competent in the way of people who have never had the luxury of being bad at anything.

"Your sister's mother," Sela said, coming up beside him, "had excellent handwriting."

Tomas's hands didn't stop moving. But they slowed, half a beat, one coil laid slightly looser than the ones before it. "Her mother," he said carefully, "was Elena Quintana, same as mine. Half." He glanced at her, quick and shrewd. "Why?"

"The tide-table. Some of it's original—naval hand, proper ink, written by someone who thought in that shorthand the way you think in knots. But eleven entries are additions. Different ink. Copied hand." She watched him. "Whoever added them knew the cipher but didn't dream in it."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning the chest has two messages in it, and only one of them points at the flagship."

Tomas set down the coil. He turned to face her fully, and Sela gave him credit for this: he didn't perform surprise. He looked at her with the flat assessing gaze of a harbor fixer being shown information, weighing it the way he weighed everything—for value, for danger, for resale.

"A second set of coordinates," he said.

"You're not surprised."

"I'm a fixer, Sela. Nothing about a hidden compartment surprises me. Everything about hidden compartments is how I eat." He rubbed his jaw. "Does Gideon know?"

"Not yet. You're the first."

Something moved behind his eyes at that—something that might have been pleasure and might have been calculation, and the trouble with Tomas was that it was very often both. "Why me?"

"Because I wanted to see your face before I showed anyone else." She let that sit. "And because if there's a traitor on this boat, I'd rather learn it now, in daylight, than at night with a gunboat's searchlight on us."

The silence that followed lasted four or five swells of the hull. Tomas didn't flinch, didn't protest, didn't do any of the things an innocent man might have done and none of the things a guilty one definitely would. He simply looked back at her, and said, quietly:

"If I were selling you out, I'd have done it already. The Syndicate's known where we are since the third tide—I'd stake my share on it. Whatever happens next isn't going to turn on me talking." He picked the coil back up. "Tell my sister. All of it. She hates being managed worse than she hates being lied to."

It was, Sela thought later, the most honest thing anyone had said to her in years. It was also possibly a performance so good it deserved a medal. That was the weather inside the Patient Answer now, and she was beginning to understand why Gideon had named the feeling before Sela had: mutiny weather. Not a storm yet. Just the pressure falling, and everyone aboard watching everyone else's face for the first lightning.

* * *

Gideon took the news standing at the chart table, arms braced, reading the eleven altered entries upside down while Sela walked her through the philology—the ink, the hand, the rhythm of the forgery. Okafor stood by the door with his arms crossed, a wall of a man trying to be furniture and failing, because his whole body was listening.

"So the chest was planted," Gideon said. Not a question. Her voice had gone flat and precise, the voice Sela had heard her use only twice before, both times facing down men with clipboards and smiles. "Planted in that river. Where I dredge. Where I've dredged for six years."

"The flood moved it, maybe," Okafor offered. "Rivers move things."

"Maybe." Gideon's finger came down on the second coordinates, the ones Sela had finally transferred onto the master chart in pencil, faint as a watermark. "Or maybe my dead mother walked a forged tide-table into a lead-lined box and sank it upriver of my mooring, ninety miles from a second location, and trusted me to be curious enough to open it and smart enough—" her eyes flicked to Sela— "to have someone aboard who could read it."

"She couldn't have known I'd be aboard," Sela said.

"No. But she could have known that whoever opened it would need someone like you, and would go find one." Gideon straightened. For a moment she looked less like a debtor and more like something older, harder, a woman carved out of the same weather she chased. "Ninety miles southeast. What's there?"

"Nothing," Sela said. "On modern charts. Which is precisely what makes it interesting. The Meridian shelf dropped forty fathoms in the collapse—whole districts of the coast went under intact. If there's a structure down there, it won't be on any chart drawn after the drowning." She tapped the blank water. "This isn't nothing. This is uncharted. There's a difference, and the difference is usually history."

Okafor spoke from the doorway, low and reluctant, the way he delivered all his worst observations. "Then we got a problem. Because right now, every fleet on this coast is pointed at the flagship coordinates. That's loud. That's good—it means whatever's at the second spot stays quiet. But it also means somebody wanted the flagship found." He paused. "Think about it. Why plant a chest that leads to the most famous wreck in the sea? Everybody already knows where the flagship is—or thinks they do. The coordinates aren't a secret. They're bait. Somebody wanted every greedy fool on this coast racing to one spot, all bunched together, all looking down."

"And whoever reads the second layer," Sela finished quietly, "gets to go somewhere no one is looking."

The lamp swung. Three faces around a chart, and on the chart a small penciled cross in water no one had ever bothered to name.

"There's another possibility," Gideon said at last, and her voice had changed again, quieter, and Sela heard the fear in it and respected it, because Gideon's fears were never small. "That the second layer wasn't written by my mother at all. That someone altered the table *after* she sealed it. Someone who knew the cipher, who had access to the chest, who wanted a second set of coordinates read by exactly the kind of crew that would find them." She looked up. "Someone who wanted us to split our attention. Or wanted us at that second location for reasons of their own."

"You're saying the traitor wrote the map," Okafor said.

"I'm saying I don't know anymore which parts of this were left for me and which parts were left *in front of* me." Gideon pressed both palms flat on the table. "My mother. Lark. The Syndicate. Ilsa Reyen, who looks at me like a ghost. Now a ghostwriter in the margins of a dead navy's paperwork. Everybody's writing on this chart except us."

* * *

They argued for an hour. It was, Sela reflected, almost a luxury—an hour of argument, when the radio chatter said the Corvus was closing and the Perpetual Dawn existed somewhere over the horizon like a headache you hadn't felt yet. Okafor wanted to ignore the second coordinates entirely: follow the plan, reach the flagship, settle the debt, get out. Gideon wanted to send a boat party to the second site after the flagship, if the flagship yielded anything at all. Sela argued—against her own insurance, against the stitched scrap in her coat lining—that the second site should be surveyed *first*, quietly, before the flagship, because the flagship would soon be the most crowded piece of seabed on the coast and the second site would never be quieter than it was right now.

It was Tomas who broke the deadlock, arriving at the cabin door with rain in his collar and the particular set of his shoulders that meant he'd been listening to the radio longer than anyone knew.

"Fleets are converging on the flagship coordinates from three bearings," he said. "Corvus, the Harbormaster's Guild pair, and something big running dark—no transponder, which on this coast means Syndicate money. In about thirty hours that spot is going to look like a fish market." He came in, dripping, and put one finger on the penciled cross. "Meanwhile nobody—nobody—is pointed here. You want my professional opinion? The second coordinates are the only place on this ocean where we're worth more alive than followed. Everywhere else, we're just the courier."

"That's assuming the second site isn't a trap," Okafor rumbled.

"Everything's a trap," Tomas said. "The trick is choosing which one catches somebody else."

Gideon laughed—a short, sharp sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a woman recognizing her own blood speaking. She looked at the chart a long moment. Then at Sela.

"How sure are you? About the forgery. About the second layer being intentional."

Sela considered lying, or softening it, and discarded both. Cartographers deal in error bars; she gave Gideon hers. "The alterations are deliberate, skilled, and recent relative to the original. Whether your mother's hand or an enemy's, I can't tell you without more of the cipher to compare. But there's a third possibility I haven't said out loud." She took a breath. "That both layers are hers. That she built a table with two doors—one obvious, one hidden—so that whoever she trusted most would find the hidden one, and whoever she distrusted would exhaust themselves on the obvious one. A test and a treasure, in the same ink."

"And if we fail the test?" Okafor asked.

"Then we were never the intended reader," Sela said, "and we should know that before we're standing on someone else's grave."

* * *

Gideon made the call at dusk, alone at the wheel, and Sela watched her make it—the small stillness before commitment, the way she checked the sky not for weather but for permission. New course: south-southeast, ninety miles of uncharted water, toward a penciled cross and whatever had been written for them there.

The crew scattered to their stations without ceremony. That was the strange thing about mutiny weather, Sela thought, as she rolled the charts and lashed them down: it didn't feel like treachery. It felt like everyone becoming very polite. Okafor checking the engine bilge twice. Tomas volunteering for the midnight watch, unprompted, which he had never once done. Gideon humming to herself at the wheel, tuneless, a habit Sela had catalogued as a tell for fear.

And Sela herself, in the aft cabin, un-picking three stitches of waxed thread and smoothing the folded scrap flat under the lamp, adding one line to it in her own careful hand before sewing it back into the lining:

*Second layer verified deliberate. Author unknown. If found without me, assume I was wrong about someone.*

She blew out the lamp and lay in her bunk listening to the hull work against the swell, and to the radio through the bulkhead, where the voices of the fleets were calling to each other across the darkening water, all of them pointed at the wrong coordinates, all of them certain.

Somewhere ahead of them, in water no chart had ever named, something waited that had been arranged with a forger's patience and a native's love. Sela fell asleep composing the survey grid she would run when they arrived, and woke once in the night with a single thought sitting on her chest like ballast:

Eleven entries. She had counted eleven.

A twelfth had been nagging at the edge of her memory all evening, half-decoded, dismissed as noise.

By lamplight, refusing to sleep until she knew, she pulled the tide-table out again and found it: a twelfth altered entry, in the same false hand, tucked at the bottom of the final column where no honest navigator would ever look. It wasn't coordinates at all.

It was a date. Six tides out. The same tidal window Gideon had already fixed for the flagship dive.

And beneath the date, in the dead navy's shorthand, a single word that Sela translated twice because she did not want it to mean what it meant:

*Witness.*

Someone was expected to be watching, at that exact hour, from that exact bearing—and the second coordinates, she realized with a cold that started in her teeth and would not stop, were not a destination at all.

They were a vantage point.
