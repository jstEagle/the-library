## 17. Lark's Patience Ends

The message came at four in the morning, which told Lark everything he needed to know before he'd read a word of it.

The Vane Syndicate did not conduct its business at four in the morning. The Syndicate conducted its business over lunch, over brandy, over the slow and comfortable erosion of a man's options across a well-lit table. Four in the morning was for fires, for drownings, for the kind of arithmetic that ended in round numbers with no remainder. Mr. Aldous Lark had been collecting debts for eleven years, and in all that time he had received exactly three messages at four in the morning. He remembered each of them the way other men remembered funerals.

He sat up in his narrow bed above the chandlery on Cordwainer Row, lit the lamp without hurrying, and unrolled the flimsy paper the night clerk had slipped under his door. The Syndicate's cipher was elegant as always—little suns and little waves, an irony that would have been funny if it weren't so old—and by the time he'd worked through it, the lamp oil had burned a visible notch lower and his hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season.

*The Perpetual Dawn is fueled and provisioned. She sails on the morning tide. Her captain has sealed orders: recover any Meridian materials in the possession of one Gideon Quintana, salvage master, master of the trawler Patient Answer. Recover the finder herself. Terms are not to be discussed.*

Lark read it twice. Then a third time, because the third reading was where the sentence *recover the finder herself* stopped looking like bureaucratic phrasing and started looking like what it was.

He got dressed. He shaved. He tied his good tie—the grey one, the one that made him look like what he pretended to be, which was a reasonable man employed by reasonable men—and he went down into the wet dark of the harbor district to do the only thing left that he could still do about any of it.

He was going to ask permission to be kind.

* * *

The Syndicate kept its offices above the Old Custom House, in rooms that had once belonged to the port authority before the port authority learned who actually owned the harbor. Lark climbed the marble stairs at half past five, past the brass plaque that said MERIDIAN TRADE COMMISSION in letters worn nearly smooth, because the Syndicate had never bothered to replace it. There was a joke in that, somewhere. Nobody who worked there laughed at it.

Mr. Ferren was already awake. Ferren was always awake; Lark had privately concluded years ago that the man slept standing up like a horse, or perhaps didn't sleep at all but simply dimmed. He sat behind a desk of black walnut that had come off some drowned merchantman, doing nothing in particular with the air of a man who could do anything at all if it amused him.

"Mr. Lark." Ferren smiled. Ferren's smiles were famous along the waterfront, in the way that certain reefs were famous among pilots. "You're early."

"You woke me."

"We wake everyone eventually." Ferren set down his pen with a small click, precise as a lock turning. "You have questions."

"I have one question." Lark stood in front of the desk with his hat in his hands, which was a posture he'd practiced until it looked natural and which was, in fact, the most carefully calculated thing about him. "The Quintana woman. The orders say recover her. I'd like to know what that means, precisely, so that I can execute it properly."

"It means what it says."

"It says two different things," Lark said mildly. "It says recover her, and it says terms are not to be discussed. Those aren't the same instruction. One of them is a retrieval. The other is a disposal. I've done retrievals. I'm very good at retrievals. But I don't like finding out halfway through a job that I was carrying a shovel."

Ferren regarded him for a long moment. Outside the tall windows, the harbor was going from black to the color of dirty pewter, and the first gulls were arguing over something dead on the pilings below.

"You've grown attached," Ferren said. It wasn't an accusation. Accusations required the accuser to care.

"I've grown *familiar*," Lark corrected. "There's a difference. Attachment makes you stupid. Familiarity makes you efficient. I know how she thinks. I know she'll run rather than fight, and I know where running takes her, because I've watched her do it for eleven months. If you want the chest and you want the woman, I can deliver both with no mess, no noise, and no stories afterward. Stories are expensive. You taught me that."

"And if we wanted neither?"

Lark felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts when a swell comes under you from a direction you weren't watching. He kept his face pleasant. That was the whole trick of his life, when it came down to it: keeping his face pleasant while the water rose.

"Then I'd wonder why you sent me out to collect forty thousand marks from her over the better part of a year," he said. "Forty thousand marks is real money, Mr. Ferren. We don't spend a year cultivating an asset we intend to sink. That's not economy. That's waste. And the Syndicate—" he let the smile reach his eyes, just barely, "—the Syndicate does not tolerate waste. I learned that my first week."

Something moved behind Ferren's eyes. Lark couldn't name it and didn't try. Naming things like that was how collectors ended up in the river with their pockets full of chain.

"The Dawn sails this morning," Ferren said finally. "Captain Voss has her orders. You'll go aboard as Syndicate observer, with authority over the treatment of the prisoner, should there prove to be a prisoner. If your methods produce the documents intact and a Quintana who can still sign confessions, the Board will consider your judgment vindicated." He picked his pen back up. "If they don't—"

"I know what happens if they don't," Lark said. "I've written enough of those endings myself."

"Yes," Ferren agreed pleasantly. "That's rather why you're being given the chance. The Board likes its ironies served fresh."

* * *

The Perpetual Dawn lay at the naval berth east of the coal wharf, and she was the ugliest beautiful thing on the coast: a gunboat of maybe six hundred tons, grey as bad weather, with a bow raked forward like a chin lifted in contempt. Two quick-firing guns sat under tarps on her foredeck. Her crew moved with the particular silence of men who had been told not to talk about where they were going, which meant they knew, which meant they'd been told, which meant this had been decided weeks ago and everything since—the extensions, the offers, Lark's own patient monthly visits to a trawler that smelled of diesel and desperation—had been theater.

Lark stood on the quay and looked at her for a long moment, and understood, with the clarity that sometimes arrives too late to be useful, exactly how long he had been a prop in someone else's play.

"Mr. Lark." Captain Voss came down the boarding ladder to meet him—a spare woman with a face like a closed ledger and hands that hung at her sides the way tools hang on a wall. "You requested the privilege of handling the prisoner personally."

"I requested the privilege of doing it kindly," Lark said. "There's a difference, and the difference matters to me."

Voss considered him. "Kindness costs time."

"Time is cheaper than funerals."

"That depends entirely," Voss said, "on whose funeral." And she turned and led him up the ladder, and Lark followed, because following was the whole of his profession now, apparently, and he was growing tired of pretending otherwise.

* * *

They cleared the harbor on the morning tide under a sky the color of scoured tin. Lark stood at the stern rail with a cup of the Dawn's terrible coffee and watched Cordwainer Row shrink behind them—the chandlery, the dockside bars, the whole soft rotten ecosystem of debts and favors he'd spent a decade learning to swim in—and tried to feel like a man going to work.

What he felt instead was the specific dread of a man who has just realized that the people he works for have stopped needing him to be clever.

For eleven years, cleverness had been his armor. The Syndicate sent Lark because Lark could sit across from a drowning man and make the drowning feel like a negotiation. He'd built a whole self on that talent—the good tie, the easy laugh, the way he'd learned to say *we value our relationship with you* to people whose boats he was repossessing. It had seemed, once, long ago, like a kind of mercy. He'd told himself it was a kind of mercy. A hard kindness, a clean knife instead of a saw. Men paid Lark's debts and kept their fingers. Men who met the Syndicate's other collectors sometimes didn't keep their fingers, or their boats, or their habit of answering doors.

But armor only works while the enemy is aiming at you. Nobody aims at furniture. And somewhere between the fourth extension and the fifth, Gideon Quintana had stopped being a debtor to the Syndicate and become a line item, and line items didn't get the good tie. Line items got the Perpetual Dawn and a captain whose hands hung like tools.

*Recover the finder herself.*

He turned the phrase over again, the way you turn a stone over to see what's underneath, though you already know it'll be wet and crawling. The Board wanted her found. Not her debt collected—her *found*. Which meant the chest mattered more than they'd let on, and the woman mattered more than the chest, and none of it had ever been about forty thousand marks. Forty thousand marks was the leash. The leash had never been the point. The point was that a dog on a leash tells you where every other dog runs.

And Elena Quintana's daughter had gone and dug up something that made the Board stop pretending.

Lark drank his coffee and thought about the last time he'd seen her, three weeks ago, on the deck of the Patient Answer. She'd lied to him. He'd known it even then—he always knew, that was the job—but knowing hadn't spoiled it somehow. She'd stood there with salt in her hair and her crew pretending not to listen, and she'd smiled at him with her mother's exact smile and told him there was nothing Meridian in her possession, and he had said *of course, Miss Quintana, we understand completely*, and tipped his hat, and walked away down the pontoon feeling something he hadn't let himself name.

He named it now, alone at the rail, with the gunboat's engines thudding under his feet like a second heart.

Relief. He had felt relief. Because for one moment, one whole conversation, he had gotten to watch somebody refuse the Syndicate and walk away upright, and some starved corner of himself had stood up inside his chest and cheered.

That was the trouble with kindness, he thought. You practiced it long enough as a performance and one day the audience believed it, and the audience was you.

* * *

Voss found him there at noon. The coast was falling away astern; ahead, the sea had begun to organize itself into the long grey swells that meant weather out beyond the horizon, gathering its arguments.

"You should see this," she said, and handed him a signal flimsy.

It was from the harbor office at Saltern Point, relayed twice. A trawler answering the description of the Patient Answer had cleared the outer marks two nights prior, running dark, and had been logged passing the Corvus fishing grounds heading south-southeast. South-southeast. Toward the old Meridian shelf. Toward water no sane salvager entered except for one reason.

"She's making for the flagship," Lark said.

"She's making for something." Voss took the flimsy back and folded it with a precision that bordered on ceremony. "At her best speed, four days. We make it in two and a half. We will arrive first, or near enough." She paused, and then, in a tone that was almost conversational, added: "Your prisoner. When we take her. What condition do you intend her to be in?"

"Alive. Unhurt. Cooperative."

"Those are four conditions, not three, and the fourth is the expensive one."

"Cooperation is cheap," Lark said, "if you're the first person to offer her a fair deal in eleven months. Everyone else has offered her ruin. I'm offering her a signature. People are grateful for a signature, Captain, when the alternative is the river."

Voss looked at him for a moment longer than was comfortable, and Lark had the distinct sensation of being measured for something. Not a coffin. Coffins were simpler.

"Ferren says you're sentimental," she said at last.

"Ferren says that about anyone who hasn't killed anyone."

"No," Voss said. "Ferren says that about anyone who might still refuse." And she touched her cap—not quite a salute, not quite mockery, something in between, the gesture of a professional acknowledging another professional across a gap neither of them could close—and went forward to her bridge, leaving Lark alone with the swells and the weather building out beyond the edge of the world.

He stayed at the rail a long while. Somewhere south, in all that grey, a trawler with a borrowed name was running toward a drowned country with a chest full of somebody else's sins, captained by a woman who had inherited a refusal the way other people inherited houses. The Syndicate wanted her back on the leash, or wanted her gone; the orders were beautifully ambiguous, and ambiguity, in Lark's long experience, was what powerful men wrote when they wanted the option of denying whatever happened next.

He thought about the three messages he'd received at four in the morning. He thought about the men who'd sent them, and where those men were now, and how none of them had seen it coming either, right up until the water came over the gunwale.

Then he took out his pocket ledger—the real one, the one with the debts in his own hand, the one he'd carried for eleven years like a rosary—and found Gideon Quintana's page, forty thousand marks in neat columns, and looked at it for a long time without writing anything.

Ahead, the first rain of the front came walking across the water toward them, fine and silver and cold, and the Perpetual Dawn drove on into it, two and a half days from the wreck, two and a half days from the moment when courtesy would run out of sea room—and Lark stood at the rail in the rising wind and began, quietly, for the first time in his life, to think about which side of the gunwale he'd rather be standing on when it came.
