## 18. Fourth Tide: The Shoals of Names

The rain found them an hour after dawn, and Gideon stood at the wheel of the *Patient Answer* and let it.

She had spent her whole life reading weather the way other people read faces—looking for the lie in it, the turn, the moment when a clear sky stopped meaning what it said. This front had told her everything she needed to know since midnight. It came in off the northeast in long gray ranks, no drama to it, just a steady pressure of cloud like a hand laid flat on the sea, and under it the water had gone that particular bruised green she'd learned to trust before she'd learned to read. Not a killing storm. Not yet. A working one. A storm with a job to do, and the job was hiding them.

"Visibility's dropping," Okafor said from the bow, one hand on the forestay, his voice pitched to carry over the drumming rain. "Half a mile. Maybe less."

"That's the idea."

"That's the *hope*. Hope and idea aren't the same animal."

Gideon almost smiled. "No. They're not. But today they're swimming in the same direction."

Behind her, the wheel answered her hands with the small familiar complaints she knew better than her own heartbeat—the half-degree of slack to starboard, the shudder in the rudder stock when the swell ran quartering, the way the whole old trawler leaned into a turn like a tired horse leaning into a hill it knew. Eleven years she'd owned this boat. Eleven years of patching and coaxing and lying to marine surveyors. She had never once loved it the way she loved it now, running for her life through the first cold rain of a front she would have chased for money in another life.

Sela came up the companionway with the chart case wrapped in oilcloth and her sleeves rolled, and Gideon saw at once that the cartographer had not slept. Her eyes had the flat shine of a woman running on arithmetic and fury, and she carried the charts the way a soldier carries a weapon—close, checked, rechecked.

"Shoal water begins in four miles," Sela said, no greeting. She spread the chart on the chart table under the dodger and weighted the corners with a brass cup, a coil of line, and Okafor's abandoned coffee mug. "The graveyard. Everything from here to the wreck site is named after the ships that died on it."

"I know the name of it."

"You know the *nickname* of it. Look." Her finger traced the coast, and Gideon leaned in, rain running off her chin onto the plastic covering. "The Meridian pilots charted this water a hundred years before the hydrographic office ever did. Their soundings are on the second layer of the tide-table—depths, currents, the sandbars that move with every storm season. The official charts show eleven wrecks in this stretch." Her finger tapped. "The tide-table shows thirty-one. Twenty of them aren't on any chart the Perpetual Dawn will be carrying. Some of them are hulks sitting in twelve feet of water with their masts down and their hulls intact. A gunboat draws sixteen feet."

Gideon looked up from the chart and found Sela watching her with that expression she got when she'd solved something and was waiting for everyone else to catch up.

"You're telling me the drowned are on our side."

"I'm telling you the drowned don't care whose side they're on. They only care about draft." Sela rolled the chart with a snap. "The Meridians called this stretch the Shoals of Names, because every sandbar and every reef carried the name of the ship that made it. You don't navigate through it. You navigate *among* them. Like threading a crowd."

"Then thread us, cartographer. Call your marks."

* * *

For the next three hours, Gideon drove the *Patient Answer* the way a surgeon drives a knife.

Sela stood at the rail with a hand bearing compass and a stopwatch, calling depths and bearings in a flat, rapid voice, and Gideon turned them into wheel and throttle without letting a single one die in the space between hearing and doing. Okafor worked the bow, sounding with the old hand line when the depth finder stuttered in the rain, and Tomas sat in the wheelhouse doorway with the radio log in his lap, tracking the traffic they could hear and the traffic they couldn't.

"Sandbar, two cables, bearing two-six-five—the *Cormorant's Rest*, lost 1887. We pass north of her bones. Depth over the bar is nineteen feet, falling."

"North of the bones," Gideon said, and put the wheel over.

The trawler came about with a groan and slid along the bar's shoulder, and through the rain Gideon saw it—the long pale seam of broken water where the sea stood up over the drowned ship's spine and combed itself into white. Nineteen feet of water over a hundred and forty years of hull. The *Perpetual Dawn* would need twenty-two and a half, and would not have them.

"Current's setting us east," Okafor called. "Half a knot."

"Compensating." She eased the wheel a spoke. The boat answered.

It was, Gideon thought, the purest work she had ever done. No debt in it. No Lark, no ledger, no cheerful voice on a dock reminding her what she owed. There was only the water and the boat and the numbers, and the numbers were honest in a way nothing else in her life had been for years. The sea did not care about her debts. The sea cared only whether she read it correctly, and for these three hours, reading it correctly was the entire world.

"Reef, dead ahead—the *Saint Ferrol*, lost 1841. Her bow section stands six feet under. We thread the gap between her and the bar. Gap width is one hundred ten yards. Depth in the gap, twenty-one feet, but the tide's turned and it's shoaling."

"Shoaling how fast?"

"Fast. We go through now or we go around, and around costs us an hour."

"Through." Gideon throttled up.

The *Patient Answer* gathered herself and ran at the gap, and Gideon felt the old boat come alive under her in a way she had felt maybe five times in eleven years—that moment when a vessel stops being a machine and becomes a creature, when hull and helmsman and sea stop being three things and become one negotiation, continuous, instantaneous, conducted in the language of heel and helm and the set of the water. The rain came harder. The white water of the Saint Ferrol's bones rose on the port bow like a wall of lace. Sela counted the yards down in a voice that did not tremble, and Gideon steered by her counting and by the color of the water—green, green, gray-green, *pale*—and eased the wheel a half-spoke at the exact moment the pale water said *now*, and the trawler slid through the gap with ten feet of clear water under her keel and the drowned ship's reef close enough on either side that Gideon could have spat on it.

Okafor let out a breath he'd been holding for a minute and a half. "I have decided," he announced to the rain, "that I am too old for this."

"You're forty-four."

"I was forty-four an hour ago. I have aged."

"Mark it on the log."

* * *

Tomas was the one who heard it first.

He'd been quiet all morning—quieter than even his usual guilt made him—and he sat in the wheelhouse doorway with the radio headset half on, one ear cocked to the traffic, and at 11:40 by the wheelhouse clock he went very still, the way he went still when he heard something in a voice that other people hadn't learned to hear yet.

"Cap'n." His voice was careful. "You want to hear this."

She took the headset. Under the rain-static, a voice—male, bored, professionally courteous in the way that only men with guns behind them are courteous—was reading a notice. Not a mayday. Not a navigation warning. A *notice to mariners*, issued on Vane Syndicate letterhead, broadcast on the working channels, offering—Gideon listened twice to be sure—a salvage reward of one hundred and twenty thousand marks for information leading to the recovery of "certain Meridian materials," and a further reward of forty thousand for "the location and condition of the vessel *Patient Answer*, registry number 4471, currently wanted for questioning in connection with theft of Syndicate property."

"They named us," Sela said quietly.

"They named us and they priced us." Gideon handed the headset back. "Forty thousand. That's exactly what I owe them."

"They're not paying your debt," Okafor said. "They're buying you. There's a difference, and the difference is what happens after the money changes hands."

"Every trawler, every tug, every two-bit salvage skiff on this coast just became a paid informer." Tomas turned the radio log over in his hands, not looking at her. "Half of them know your hull on sight. You've been running this boat out of that harbor for eleven years."

"Then we stop being a boat they know." Gideon looked at the rain, at the gray wall of the front closing around them, and made the calculation she'd been carrying since midnight. "The front peaks tonight. Squalls to forty knots, visibility to zero, lightning enough to blind any radar worth the name. The gunboat can make twelve knots in this sea without breaking her people. We can make nine. On flat water she catches us by tomorrow noon."

"And in the front?" Sela asked.

"In the front, her radar is worth nothing, her lookouts are blind, and her captain has to choose between losing us and losing his hull on something that isn't on his charts." Gideon tapped the rolled tide-table where it sat in its case by the wheel. "But we have thirty-one wrecks and he has eleven. In the front, this graveyard stops being a graveyard and starts being a wall."

"You want to run *into* the storm," Okafor said. It wasn't a question. He said it the way a man says *you want to jump off the roof*.

"I want to run into the one place a gunboat can't follow."

"And if she follows anyway?"

Gideon looked at him. "Then she'll be following us into the Shoals of Names, in a forty-knot squall, with charts that are a hundred years out of date, and her name will end up on one of these sandbars, and they'll have to rename the shoal."

Nobody laughed. But nobody argued, either, and Gideon had been a captain long enough to know that this—this silence, this tired, frightened, calculating silence—was what a crew sounded like when it had decided.

* * *

The front hit them at 16:20.

It did not arrive so much as descend. One moment there was rain and a rising sea; the next, the world went gray and vertical, and the wind came down on them like something dropped from a height, forty knots and building, flattening the sea into a chaos of white and driving the rain sideways in ropes. Gideon lashed the wheelhouse hatch, double-clipped her harness to the jackline, and took the wheel with the tiller-extension in her left hand and the throttle in her right, and for the next hour she did not think about debt or Syndicates or dead mothers. She thought about water.

Sela stayed at the rail under the dodger, oilcloth over the chart case, calling marks through the speaking tube with her mouth an inch from the brass. The storm had turned her voice into something stripped and elemental, a sound with no person in it, only function: *bar, bearing, depth, now*. And Gideon answered, and the *Patient Answer* answered her, and the drowned ships of the Shoals of Names slid past them in the gray—one moment a pale seam of broken water off the port beam, the next a shadow under the bow that Okafor's hand line said was fourteen feet down and Sela's charts said was the *Admiral's Barge*, lost with all hands in the last year of the war—and Gideon threaded them, and threaded them, and threaded them.

Twice the depth finder went out in the lightning. Once the hand line parted. Once a swell lifted the trawler and set her down six feet to port of where Gideon wanted her, and Sela's voice came through the tube—*correct two degrees, the Saint Anselm's mast is on your starboard quarter, twelve feet down*—and Gideon corrected, and the shadow that was a century-dead mast slid by close enough to touch.

At 19:50, in the worst of it, with the wind at forty-six knots and the sea a moving mountain range, Sela's voice came through the tube one more time.

"Cap'n. Behind us. Two miles. Running lights, big ones, white over white over white, and a searchlight sweeping."

Gideon turned. Through the gray chaos, far back along the black lane of their wake, she saw it: the Perpetual Dawn, driving into the front after all, her lights blazing, her searchlight sweeping the water in long white strokes—and then the searchlight found the pale seam of the Cormorant's Rest bar, and swung away from it, and began, very carefully, to work along the edge of the graveyard like a man patting his way along a wall in the dark.

"She's following," Okafor said.

"She's following *outside*," Gideon said. "She's hugging the ten-fathom line. Her captain knows what's in here, and he's not coming in."

"Then we've lost her."

"We've *gained* the night." Gideon eased the throttle back a notch and let the old trawler settle into the storm's rhythm, running with it now instead of against it, the drowned ships sliding past to starboard like a congregation. "She'll stand off the shoals all night, sweeping, waiting for the front to pass. We'll be through the graveyard and into deep water by dawn, and by the time her radar can see again, we'll be six hours from the wreck site and she'll be eight."

Sela came aft through the rain, soaked to the skin, and stood in the wheelhouse doorway with water running off her chin, and for the first time since the archive burned, Gideon saw something in her face that was not grief. It was something older and harder and quieter.

"My mother used to say the sea keeps every name ever given to it," Sela said. "Every ship, every drowned man. She said the water remembers, even when the charts don't." She looked back at the darkness where the gunboat's lights swept and failed and swept again. "Tonight it's remembering on our side."

Gideon put her hand on the old wheel and felt the boat answer, tired and true.

"Then let's not waste the favor," she said, and turned the *Patient Answer* into the dark.

Behind them, the searchlight swept, and swept, and found nothing but the pale seams of the drowned, combing white in the rain.

And ahead—six hours out, at the bottom of a charted trench where the tide-table's second layer ended in a single line of Meridian shorthand that Sela had not yet decoded—the flagship waited, with whatever it was that slept under her, exactly as the dead woman had promised.

Gideon steered into the night and tried not to hear the promise in it.
