## 19. The Traitor's Ledger

Tomas Quintana had spent twenty years learning every way a man could lie on the water, and he had thought himself immune to the oldest one of all: the lie you tell yourself at three in the morning while everyone else sleeps.

He stood in the stern of the *Patient Answer* with a signal lamp cupped in his hands like something stolen from an altar, which was exactly what it was. The night was flat black, the kind of dark that came before weather, and somewhere behind them—two miles, maybe three—the running light of the *Perpetual Dawn* burned steady as a nail driven into the sky. He had counted the flashes. Three short. A pause. Two long. His code. His arrangement.

*Just position reports,* he had told himself for four days now. *Nothing that helps them catch us. Nothing that hurts her.*

The lamp's shutter clicked under his thumb and he sent the two long flashes, and the light out there answered with one short acknowledgment, and Tomas felt the lie collapse inside him all at once, the way a hull collapses when it finally takes water past its lines.

Because that was the thing about arithmetic. He was good at arithmetic. It was the only inheritance his father had left him besides the jaw and the temper, and he had used it his whole life: how much a favor cost, how much a silence earned, how many marks of debt a daughter accrued when a hospital in Port Callow kept her breathing through a winter her lungs didn't want to survive. Forty-one thousand marks. He knew the number to the coin. He woke up counting it.

And the Vane Syndicate held every page of it.

* * *

They caught him because Okafor never slept properly and Sela never slept at all.

Tomas heard the deck creak behind him and had just enough time to close the lamp's shutter before the light died, but it didn't matter. Okafor filled the companionway like a door deciding to be a wall, and behind him Sela stood wrapped in a blanket with her cartographer's eyes already doing the terrible thing they did—measuring, plotting, arriving at conclusions no one wanted.

"Three short," Sela said quietly. "Two long. Every night since the shoals." She wasn't accusing him. That was somehow worse. She was simply reading him aloud, the way she'd read a chart. "I watch the horizon from the bow. I've watched it my whole life. You can't help it, after enough years. Lights talk."

"Tomas." Okafor's voice was low and even, which meant he was angrier than shouting would have made him. "Put the lamp down."

Tomas put the lamp down. His hands were shaking and he hated them for it, hated the whole trembling architecture of himself. Somewhere below, Gideon was asleep in the chart room with the tide-table cipher open beside her, trusting him for the first time in eleven years, and the weight of that trust sat on his chest heavier than any debt collector ever had.

"Gideon," Sela said, and went to wake her.

* * *

They did not tie him up. That was the first surprise. They sat him at the chart table under the single swinging lamp, and Gideon stood across from him with her arms folded and her face gone very still—the stillness she wore instead of crying, he remembered, from when they were children and their father's temper came through the walls like weather.

"How long," she said.

"Since we cleared the harbor watch." His voice came out cracked. He started over. "Since the fourth tide. Before that, too—I signaled them once from the dockside bar, told them you'd bought charts. But the real arrangement started at sea."

"What have you given them?"

"Position. Heading. Speed. Nothing else." He said it fast, the way a drowning man grabs at driftwood. "I swear on our mother's grave, Gideon, nothing else. Not the cipher, not the route past the shoals, not the dive site. They asked. I stalled. I told them the chest was damaged in the flood, that half the table was illegible—"

"You told them what?" Sela's head came up sharply.

"I lied to them." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it, a sound like rope parting. "That's the joke, isn't it. I lied to the Syndicate to protect you, and lied to you to protect myself, and now I'm standing here having run out of people to lie to."

Gideon hadn't moved. "Why?"

The word landed like a gaff hook. Not *how could you*, not *you sold us out again*—though she had every right to both, God knew she'd said worse to him over the years, and earned the right too, after the harbor job where his information had gotten her boat impounded and her crew beaten. Just *why*. Quiet. Almost gentle. And that gentleness broke him open faster than rage ever could.

So he told them.

* * *

"Mara," he said. Her name came out of him like a splinter that had been working toward the surface for two years. "My daughter. You've never met her. Nobody has, lately—she's been in St. Verity's Hospital in Port Callow since the winter before last. Lungs. The wet-lung sickness, the kind dockworkers' kids get from the coal dust and the fog." He laid his hands flat on the chart table because if he didn't give them a job they'd start tearing at each other. "She's nine years old."

Sela made a small sound. Okafor didn't move at all, but something in his enormous stillness shifted, the way ballast shifts.

"The treatment works. That's the cruel part—it actually works. Sixty marks a week, every week, indefinitely, until she grows out of the worst of it or doesn't. I'm a harbor fixer. I make maybe thirty marks a week in a good season, and the last three seasons haven't been good." He looked at his sister. "You know what I tried first. I tried borrowing honest. The banks laughed. The credit houses laughed harder. And then a man in a gray coat found me at the Tideglass Bar—you remember the Tideglass—and he sat down uninvited and bought me a drink and said, 'Mr. Quintana, the Vane Syndicate believes in families. We're the only institution on this coast that does.'"

"He knew about Mara," Tomas said. "He knew her name, her age, her ward number at St. Verity's. He slid a ledger across the bar and my daughter's debts were already written in it. Forty-one thousand marks, itemized, dated back two years—which means they'd been paying her hospital bills before I ever knew they existed. Buying me. Seasoning the debt so that by the time they came to collect, I was already theirs." His laugh again, that awful dry sound. "You want to know why I did it? Because they never threatened her. Not once. They didn't have to. Every flash of that lamp, I could hear the man in the gray coat saying, *we believe in families.*"

The lamp swung overhead. Shadows wheeled across the chart table, across the drowned coastline of Meridia that Sela had inked there, across all of their faces.

"I did the arithmetic," Tomas said. "That's all I've ever done. One girl's life against—" he gestured at the cabin, at the sea beyond the hull, at everything— "against whatever this is. Against a chance. Against maybe. I chose the sure thing. I always choose the sure thing. That's the difference between you and me, Gideon. You bet on storms."

"No." Gideon's voice was strange. "That's not the difference between us."

She unfolded her arms. Came around the table slowly, and sat down across from him, close, the way she hadn't sat near him in eleven years.

"The difference is that someone gave you a bill and you paid it," she said. "Someone gave me one too. Forty thousand marks, Mr. Lark, the smile, the extension. And I ran. I've been running my whole life—from Dad, from the Syndicate, from every port where anyone knew my name. You stayed and carried it. That's not cowardice, Tomas. That's just a different cage." She exhaled. "It doesn't mean I'm not furious."

"Good," Tomas said, and meant it. "Stay furious. Fury I can work with. It's the gentleness that's going to finish me."

* * *

It was Okafor who raised the question that mattered, in the way he raised everything—flat, heavy, impossible to dodge.

"So what do we do with him."

The word *maroon* didn't get said. It didn't need to. Everyone at the table heard it anyway, sitting there in the lamplight like a fifth passenger. Out here, past the harbor watch, past the law, a crew handled its own justice, and the traditional sentence for signaling to hunters was simple: the next shore, alone, with whatever the tide brought you.

"We're two days from the flagship wreck," Sela said. "Mid-ocean. Marooning him isn't punishment, it's murder with extra steps."

"I'm not asking what's merciful," Okafor said. "I'm asking what's safe. Every night that lamp talks, the *Dawn* gets closer. Tomorrow night it might talk our exact position into a gunnery solution. I hauled that man's brother out of the water when a wave took him, and I'd do it again, and I will also put him off this ship if that's what keeps the rest of us breathing. Both things are true. I need to know which one wins."

"I win," Tomas said. "That's the answer. Whatever you decide, I don't get a vote, and I shouldn't." He looked around the table, at each of them, making himself hold it. "But before you decide, you should know what else I know. What they gave me along with the debt."

Gideon's eyes narrowed. "What do you mean?"

"The man in the gray coat wasn't just collecting. He was recruiting. That's how the Syndicate works—every debtor is a sensor, every sensor is a node. They showed me things. To keep me useful, to keep me convinced resistance was hopeless." He pulled a folded paper from inside his coat—his hands steadier now, strangely, now that everything was ruined and honest—and spread it on the chart table. "Shipping schedules. The *Perpetual Dawn*'s patrol rotations for the next month. And this."

Sela leaned over it and went rigid.

"That's a fleet disposition chart," she said. "Salvage fleets. Corvus Line, the Harrow Combine, the Meridian Salvage Trust—all three, with search grids marked." Her finger traced the penciled sectors. "These grids are centered on open water. There's nothing there. Unless—" she looked up, and Tomas watched the same realization move through her that had moved through him the night he first saw the paper, "—unless you know where to center them. Unless you have the coordinates."

"They don't have your cipher," Tomas said. "But they know what it points to. They've known for years, Gideon. That's why the debt collectors circle salvage captains like sharks. That's why Lark came back with the Meridian offer so fast. The Syndicate hasn't been hunting a chest. They've been waiting for someone dumb enough or desperate enough to find the wreck for them."

The cabin was silent except for the sea knocking against the hull, patient as a creditor.

"So use me," Tomas said. "That's my argument, such as it is. Don't maroon me and don't forgive me—use me. The *Dawn* expects my signals. Every night, three short, two long. If they stop, they know we caught him, and they stop trusting anything he sent, including the false positions I've been feeding them since the shoals." He tapped the fleet chart. "But if the signals continue—if I feed them a course that bends east, toward the Corvus Line's grid, where another fleet's guns will do your arguing for you—we buy days. Maybe a week. I am, as of tonight, the only double agent on this coast who's cheap enough to be believable, because I genuinely hate everyone involved."

Nobody laughed. But something in the room's weight changed—a fraction of degrees, a needle finding north.

* * *

They voted. Of course they voted; that was the thing about Gideon's crew, the thing Tomas had been watching from the outside for two weeks with a feeling he hadn't let himself name. Okafor proposed it, formal as a court martial: not marooning, not forgiveness—employment. The traitor becomes the instrument. His signals continue, under Sela's supervision, carrying lies. His freedom comes later, contingent, earned, and nobody pretends otherwise.

"Aye," Okafor said.

"Aye," Sela said. Then, quieter, not looking at him: "For Mara. Not for you. Yet."

All eyes went to Gideon.

She stood at the chart table a long moment, looking down at the fleet dispositions, at the penciled grids circling empty water, at her brother's hands lying flat among the drowned geography of a nation that had been sold out from under it by men who also believed in families—in owning them, anyway.

"When we make port," she said finally, "you introduce me to the man in the gray coat's ledger. Every page. Every name. If the Syndicate seasoned your daughter's debt, they've done it to a hundred others, and I want the whole rotten account." She looked up. "Aye. Use him."

Tomas nodded. It was more than he deserved and less than he owed, and both of those facts settled into him with the strange comfort of a burden correctly balanced.

"One condition," Okafor said, rising, filling the doorway again. "From tonight, the lamp talks where I can see it. And if the *Dawn* closes inside five miles because of anything you sent—" he didn't finish it. He didn't have to. Some sentences complete themselves.

"Fair," Tomas said.

* * *

Near dawn, the sky in the east went the color of old pewter, and the swell began to organize itself into long deliberate lines, the sea clearing its throat before speaking. Storm coming. Gideon stood at the wheel studying it with an expression Tomas recognized from childhood—the look she got watching weather, hungry and calm at once, a woman greeting the only honest thing on the ocean.

Tomas climbed to the stern rail with the signal lamp. Sela watched from the companionway, arms crossed, exactly as promised. Okafor loomed behind her.

Three short. Pause. Two long.

Out in the gray distance, the *Perpetual Dawn*'s light answered—one short, right on schedule, obedient, trusting.

*Still ours,* the light said. *Still blind.*

Tomas closed the shutter and stood a moment in the growing wind, and then he turned forward, toward the wheel, toward his sister, and called out over the rising voice of the sea:

"Gideon! This front—it's going to hit the search grids square. Every fleet out there is about to go blind in weather, except whoever uses it." He felt something unfamiliar move in his chest, something that might have been purpose, or might just have been the old family hunger wearing new clothes. "If you're thinking what I think you're thinking—now's the time to say it."

Gideon's grin, when she turned her head, was the storm-chaser's grin, the one that had gotten them all into this.

"Batten everything down," she said. "We're going to teach these fleets what weather's for."
