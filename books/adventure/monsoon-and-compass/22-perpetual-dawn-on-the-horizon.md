## 22. Perpetual Dawn on the Horizon

Gideon saw the gunboat before anyone else did, and for a long moment she said nothing at all.

It was a trick of the light at first—a smudge on the southeastern horizon where there should have been nothing but water and the bruised underbelly of a retreating front. She stood at the wheel of the *Patient Answer* with her coffee going cold in one hand and let the smudge resolve itself, the way she'd learned to resolve weather twenty years ago: don't blink, don't wish, just watch until the sea tells you what it's doing.

The sea was telling her something very simple.

"Okafor," she said, and her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Get up here."

He came up the companionway with his sleeves rolled and his hands still greasy from the bilge pump, took one look at her face, then one look at the horizon, and went very still. Okafor was not a man who wasted motion on panic. He'd been a dockhand, a diver, a debt runner, and a man who had once swum four miles through a burning harbor, and all of it lived in him as a kind of compressed calm that Gideon had come to rely on more than she relied on her own engines.

"That's navy gray," he said.

"That's Syndicate gray," she corrected. "Navy doesn't put chrome on its funnels."

The vessel was still ten miles out, maybe twelve, but even at this distance she could read it the way Sela read charts. Long hull, low freeboard forward, a single superfiring mount that could throw five-inch shells farther than Gideon cared to think about. The *Perpetual Dawn*. She'd heard the name twice now—once from Tomas, once from Ilsa Reyen—and both times it had arrived wrapped in the same tone people used for cancers and court judgments.

"How fast?" Okafor asked.

"Faster than us. Much faster than us." She set down the coffee. "But not faster than the shoals."

"They'll have pilots."

"They'll have *paid* pilots. There's a difference." She hoped she sounded more certain than she felt. "Wake Sela. And get Tomas on the radio—he'll want to hear this."

Something flickered across Okafor's face at Tomas's name, quick and unreadable, and was gone before she could decide what it meant. He went below without another word.

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse because the wheelhouse was where Gideon made her decisions, and everyone aboard had learned by now that decisions made anywhere else didn't count. Sela arrived clutching a grease pencil and a scrap of chart like talismans. Tomas leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who had already done the arithmetic and didn't like any of the sums.

"Twelve miles," Gideon said. "Closing speed puts them on top of us in about forty minutes if we hold course. If we run, they catch us inside an hour regardless—the Answer does nine knots on her best day and that was twenty years ago."

"So we surrender," Tomas said. Not cruelly. Just flatly, the way you'd state the tide.

"We negotiate."

"Gideon." He pushed off the doorframe. "That is a gunboat. Those are marines. Lark isn't coming to talk."

"Lark is always coming to talk. That's the whole point of Lark." She turned to the window again. The gray shape had grown a superstructure now, a white wake unspooling behind it like thread off a spool. "Vane doesn't spend shells on things he can buy. Shells are expensive. Lawyers are expensive. A smile costs nothing. He sent Lark because Lark is cheap, and because Lark works."

"And if you're wrong?"

She thought about the chest lashed in the hold beneath three feet of netting and two lies deep in false ballast. She thought about the photographs Sela had hidden in the lining of her chart case, though that was a card for later, a card so far in the future that playing it now would be madness. Mostly she thought about the fact that her hands were steady on the wheel and her stomach was a fist of wet rope, and how strange it was that those two facts could coexist in the same body.

"Then I'm wrong," she said, "and we're dead, and none of us will have to worry about the Vane Syndicate anymore. But I'm not wrong. Watch."

* * *

The hail came over channel sixteen at 14:40, in a voice Gideon knew better than she wanted to.

"*Unidentified trawler, unidentified trawler, this is the salvage vessel Perpetual Dawn requesting your immediate attention. Please respond.*"

Salvage vessel. As if a hundred and ninety feet of armed steel were out here looking for lost cargo nets. Gideon keyed the mic and let three seconds of static pass first—an old trick, making them wait, establishing that she was not a woman who scrambled.

"This is the trawler *Patient Answer*," she said. "Go ahead, Mr. Lark."

A pause. Then a laugh, warm as bathwater. "*Miss Quintana. You have no idea how good it is to hear your voice. We've been worried sick.*"

"I'm sure the Syndicate worries about all its debtors personally."

"*Only the ones with potential.*" Paper rustled somewhere on the other end; she pictured him in the gunboat's cramped radio room, immaculate despite everything, tie knotted, shoes polished, holding a script he probably wouldn't need. "*I'm going to be direct with you, because I respect you too much to insult you. My employers know what you're carrying. They know where you found it. And they've authorized me to make you a final offer—one offer, no extensions, no creative amendments.*"

"I'm listening."

"*Hand over the Meridian materials—all of them, the chest, the contents, anything you've copied or transcribed—and the Syndicate forgives your debt in full. Forty thousand marks, gone. Not deferred. Forgiven. Additionally, they will not pursue charges arising from your recent... nautical activities. You keep your ship. You keep your crew. You keep your life, Miss Quintana, which I should tell you is the part my employers were least inclined to include.*"

In the wheelhouse, nobody breathed. Sela had gone white around the mouth. Tomas stared at Gideon with an expression that said *take it*, and Okafor stared at her with an expression that said nothing at all, which from Okafor was its own kind of counsel.

Gideon looked at the horizon. The *Perpetual Dawn* was eight miles out now, close enough that she could see the flash of spray at its bow, the ugly elegance of it cutting toward them like a verdict.

"Mr. Lark," she said, "you've made a mistake."

"*Have I?*"

"You've assumed I want to keep my life badly enough to sell something that isn't mine to sell."

Another pause, longer this time. When Lark spoke again the warmth was still there but it had thinned, gone translucent, like ice over deep water. "*May I speak candidly? Off the record, as it were?*"

"You may."

"*I've read your file. All of it. Your mother's file too—I requested it specially, some months ago, and I won't pretend I don't know why you're out here or whose handwriting started you down this road.*" A breath. "*So I'll say this once, and then I'll go back to being the man my employers pay me to be. Whatever you think is in that chest, it is not worth what Augustin Vane will do to obtain it. He has sunk ships for less. He has sunk *nations* for less—you'll forgive me, but you of all people know that's not a figure of speech. Take the deal. Buy back your life. It's the only currency out here that appreciates.*"

For one vertiginous second, Gideon almost believed she might. That was Lark's gift, she realized—that sincerity he wore like a coat, the way he made menace sound like concern and extortion sound like friendship. Somewhere behind her ribs her heart was going like a drum in a parade, and every instinct she owned was screaming at her to say yes, yes, take the forgiveness, live.

Instead she smiled, because he couldn't see her, and somehow that helped.

"Here's my counteroffer," she said. "You tell your employers the chest is booby-trapped."

"*Pardon?*"

"Meridian naval ordnance. Pressure-sealed. Any attempt to open it outside the original vault conditions detonates the charge. I have the disarm codes. I am the only person alive who has the disarm codes, and I will die before I give them up, and unlike your marines I have nothing to lose, which makes me very hard to threaten and impossible to rush."

Silence on the channel. On the horizon, the gunboat held its course but did not accelerate, and Gideon allowed herself one internal breath.

Sela was staring at her. *There is no charge,* her eyes said. *You invented that.* Gideon gave the smallest nod. Yes. She had invented it. Invention was all they had left, and it turned out she was better at it than she'd known.

"*Miss Quintana,*" Lark said finally, and there was something new in his voice—not anger, not quite. Something almost like admiration, worn thin over disappointment. "*You understand I can't carry that to my principals without verification.*"

"Then verify. Send a diver. Oh—" she let the pause stretch, savoring it the way she'd never savor anything again if this went wrong, "—wait. You can't. Because the arming mechanism is tide-locked. It reads water pressure against lunar tables. Open it at the wrong point in the tidal cycle and the whole thing goes. You'd need someone who understands Meridian tide-tables, Mr. Lark, and I believe your last expert on Meridian tide-tables burned down with a map room in Harborside three nights ago. Terrible thing, that fire."

Beside her, Sela made a small sound—not quite a laugh, not quite a sob.

"*You're stalling,*" Lark said quietly.

"Of course I'm stalling. I'm a debtor. Stalling is the one skill my entire adult life has trained me for." She kept her voice light, breezy, a woman discussing the weather. "But here's the thing about stalling, Mr. Lark—it only looks like weakness from the front. From behind, it's called maneuvering. Six tides, that's all I need. Give me six tides and I'll bring the chest to you myself, disarmed, documented, with a full inventory your lawyers can drool over. You get to hand Vane a complete victory instead of a scorched hulk and a story about pressure charges he'll never be able to disprove. Which version do you want on your report?"

The channel hissed. Far away, the *Perpetual Dawn* began, very slowly, to turn—a lazy arc that brought its bow around parallel to their course rather than converging on it. Shadowing them. Herding them.

"*One tide,*" Lark said at last. "*You have until the next slack water. At slack water I come alongside, and either you hand over the materials or my boarding party does this the unpleasant way, and Miss Quintana—if it comes to that, I want you to know I will grieve. Sincerely. I've grown rather fond of you.*"

"One tide," Gideon agreed. "Slack water. You'll find me very reasonable."

"*Everyone says that right up until they aren't.*" A click, and the channel went dead.

* * *

For a while nobody spoke. The engine room ticked and groaned below them. The gunboat hung on their flank like a wolf pacing a fence line, close enough now that Gideon could see figures on its deck, small and patient.

"He bought it," Sela whispered. "He actually bought it."

"He bought the stall," Gideon said. "Not the lie. Look at his course—he's not leaving, he's caging. One tide, Sela. That's what I bought us, and I spent everything we had to buy it."

Tomas pushed off the doorframe and crossed to the chart table, and when he spoke his voice had changed—lower, faster, the fixer surfacing through the brother. "Slack water is in five hours and eleven minutes. This stretch of coast, the turn is gentle, maybe twenty minutes of true slack. A gunboat that size needs thirty minutes minimum to come alongside, deploy, and board in anything less than flat calm. So your window is real, but it's thin." He looked up. "What's the play, Gideon? Because I've watched you bluff men with guns before, and it worked right up until it very much didn't."

Gideon looked at the chart. The shoals of names lay ahead, a graveyard of wrecks that Sela had threaded them into an hour ago, where the water folded and lied and only a madwoman or a cartographer could follow. Beyond the shoals, the flagship. Beyond the flagship—

Beyond the flagship was a problem for the next tide.

"The play," she said slowly, "is that at slack water, the *Perpetual Dawn* cannot follow us where we're going. And between now and then, we're going to make them believe we're exactly where they think we are." She tapped the chart, on a position miles from the truth. "Okafor, I want the Answer riding heavy and slow, listing to port like we're taking water. Make it convincing. Sela, I need every wreck in this graveyard that draws more than fifteen feet—they'll want to send a boat ahead of the gunboat, and I want their boat spending the next five hours checking empty hulls. Tomas—"

He raised an eyebrow.

"Tomas, I need you on the radio, feeding their picket chatter back to me. Every word." She held his gaze and let him see that she knew exactly what she was asking, and what she was risking, and that she was asking anyway. "Can you do that?"

A muscle worked in his jaw. Whatever war was happening behind his eyes, it lasted three seconds, and when it ended he nodded once. "Yeah," he said. "I can do that."

"Good." Gideon turned back to the wheelhouse windows, to the gray shape pacing them on the bright water, to the five hours standing between her family and the guns. Her heart was still hammering, her stomach still rope, but underneath it all, for the first time since the smudge had appeared on the horizon, something else stirred—something cold and clear and familiar.

It was the feeling she got twenty minutes before a storm broke. Terror, yes. But also readiness.

"One tide," she murmured to the glass, to Lark, to the drowned nation sleeping somewhere ahead of her in the dark water. "Let's see what we can do with it."
