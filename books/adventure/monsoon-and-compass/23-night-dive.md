## 23. Night Dive

The sea went black an hour after sundown, and Okafor had spent forty years learning to distrust that particular black.

He stood at the rail of the *Patient Answer* with the anchor line humming faintly in the current below him, and he counted the reasons not to go down the way other men counted rosary beads. One: no diver had any business working a wreck site at night, not on a slack tide this short, not over a reef that could open a suit like a letter opener. Two: the gunboat was out there somewhere beyond the dark line of the horizon, and every lamp they burned was a signal flare to anyone with glass. Three—and this was the one that sat heaviest in his chest—he was fifty-eight years old, and the last time he'd been underwater at night was off the Ashen Shelf, when he was twenty-three and stupid and the sea had taken two men whose names he still said to himself on bad nights like a prayer he didn't believe in.

"You're doing the face," Gideon said behind him.

"I don't have a face."

"You have a whole liturgy of faces. That's the one where you're composing your own funeral."

Okafor turned. She was already half-suited, the dive harness cinched over her shoulders, her hair plastered flat under the hood, and she was grinning at him with that grin—the one that meant she'd already decided and was only waiting for the rest of the world to catch up. He'd known her since she was nineteen and feral, hauling salvage off a bottom-scraping barge with hands full of blisters and eyes full of weather, and in all those years he had never once won an argument with that grin. He'd stopped trying around year six.

"Slack water's forty minutes," she said. "Maybe fifty if the moon cooperates, which it won't, because there isn't one."

"There's a storm building northwest," Okafor said. "I can feel it in my knee, which has never once lied to me about weather and has never once been thanked for it."

"Then we go now and we're back before it matters." She pressed a dive torch into his hand, its casing warm from sitting near the engine. "You could stay topside. Run the winch. Nobody would think less—"

"I'm coming," he said, because they both knew the sentence was going to end there, and because Sela was below decks with a cracked rib and Tomas was under watch in the galley, which left exactly one person on this boat qualified to keep Gideon Quintana from drowning herself for a dead nation.

That was the job, as Okafor understood it. Not deckhand. Not engineer. Keeper. Somebody had to be the rope tied to her ankle, and he'd accepted the position years ago without ever being asked, the way you accept weather.

* * *

They went down the anchor line together, hand over hand, into water so black the torch beams seemed to bend.

Okafor had dived wrecks his whole working life. He knew the grammar of them—the way a hull spoke in rust and ripple, the way fish scattered like punctuation, the way silt hung in the beam like breath in cold air. But night diving was a different language entirely. The torch carved a cone maybe four meters wide and gave nothing back beyond it, so that the ocean became a room with walls you couldn't see, and every sound arrived from everywhere: the creak of his own harness, the rasp of his regulator, the distant groan of the *Patient Answer* swinging on her anchor overhead like a bell tolling through water.

Gideon swam ahead of him, slightly below, her torch sweeping the reef shelf in slow arcs. They were at eighteen meters when the reef rose to meet them—a spine of coral and old stone running east to west, furred with pale growth that flared ghost-white in the light and fell back into darkness the moment the beam passed. It looked, Okafor thought, like the backbone of something enormous that had lain down here to die and been slowly upholstered by the sea.

Then the beam caught metal, and both of them stopped swimming at the same instant.

It was just a fitting at first—a bronze eyelet crusted green, jutting from coral like a fossilized knuckle. Then another. Then the curve of them resolved, and the scale of it arrived all at once, the way a mountain arrives when cloud lifts: a keel. A keel longer than the *Patient Answer* stem to stern, lying along the reef crest like a fallen tree, its planking long since eaten away but its iron skeleton intact, ribs arching up out of the coral in rows, draped in soft white growth so that in the torchlight it looked less like a shipwreck than a cathedral built by something patient and blind.

The flagship of Meridia.

Okafor had heard the name his whole life the way coastal people hear all drowned-nation names—in songs, in bar arguments, in the careful silences of old sailors who'd been alive when Meridia still had a coastline. He'd never expected to touch it. Nobody touched history like this; history was supposed to stay in books and museums, behind glass, safe from hands.

His glove found the nearest rib. Through the crust of growth, the iron beneath was still solid, still cold, still holding the shape some shipwright had hammered into it two generations ago. A man had bent this. Men had bent ten thousand of these, and riveted them, and launched the whole impossible weight of it into water, believing it would carry them home.

"Okafor." Gideon's voice came thin through the comm line, stripped down to breath and consonants. "Look at the bow."

They swam along the spine, torches low, and the bow emerged from the dark like a forehead breaking the surface of sleep. It had been split—not broken, split, sheared almost cleanly along a seam that ran from the hawsehole down past where the waterline would have been, the two halves peeled apart like a fruit. Storm damage, maybe. Or something else. The interior gaped open, and their lights fell into it and were swallowed.

"The manifest said the strongbox was in the engine room," Gideon said. "Aft. Down through here."

"Through there." Okafor aimed his torch at the split. "Which is a hole in a dead ship, at night, at depth, with a tide that turns in—" he checked his watch bezel against his wrist light— "twenty-six minutes."

"Twenty-six minutes is plenty."

"Gideon."

She turned in the water and looked at him, and even through the mask he could see the expression, the one underneath the grin, the one she probably thought nobody ever saw: the look of a woman standing at the edge of an answer she'd been chasing since a lead-lined chest came up her winch line and rearranged her entire life. Fear was in there somewhere. So was grief. But mostly it was need, plain and enormous, and Okafor had learned long ago that you did not argue with that kind of need. You just made sure it came back up the line.

"Twenty minutes inside," he said, holding up fingers she could see. "At twenty I pull you out by the hair if I have to. We are not the first fools this reef has collected, and we will not be the last, but we will not be tonight's."

"Deal." And she turned and finned into the wound in the ship, and Okafor, muttering a word in his grandmother's language that would have gotten his mouth washed out, followed her into the dark.

* * *

Inside, the world became throat.

Their torches showed passages barely wide enough for shoulders, choked with collapsed decking and the soft grey drift of decades of sediment. Fish hung motionless in the beams—small silver things that scattered like thrown coins when Gideon's fins stirred the water. Every exhalation roared in Okafor's ears and bubbled away overhead into blackness, and the hull groaned around them at intervals, long settling sounds, the ship talking in its sleep, and each time it did Okafor's heart climbed another notch toward his teeth.

Claustrophobia was a thing he'd trained out of himself young, or thought he had. But this was different from a cargo hold or a flooded bilge. This was a grave. He could feel it the way you feel a church—some quality in the silence, in the held air, in the way sound behaved. Men had died in these passages. Men had run down this corridor with water climbing their legs, and the ship had gone down under them with the whole nation's treasury—or whatever it was—still lashed in its belly, and the sea had closed over the screaming, and everything since had been quiet.

Quiet, and patient, and full of furniture.

That was what got him, as they worked aft. The details. A lantern hanging from a bracket, its glass fogged white with mineral bloom. A boot, upright on the deck, preserved by cold and depth into something almost respectful. A doorframe with a brass number plate still screwed to it—*7*—and Okafor thought, absurdly, of the man who'd slept behind that door, who'd had a mother maybe, a girl in some port, a way of whistling, a grudge against the cook, and who had come down here in the dark and the cold while his country died around him.

"Okafor." Gideon's voice again, tighter now. "Engine room's ahead. Doorway's collapsed halfway. We go single file, and watch your tank on the frame."

The engine room opened around them like the chest cavity of the whole ship. Their lights found the great engine block first—a mountain of iron and copper rising through the gloom, its cylinders furred with growth, its walking beam toppled across the deck plates like a felled colossus. Shafts and gauges and hand-wheels, all of it wearing the same pale shroud, all of it beautiful in the way that ruined things are beautiful, which is to say beautifully and completely beyond saving.

And wedged beneath the fallen beam, jammed into the angle between engine mount and deck, half-buried in silt—

"There," Gideon breathed.

It was smaller than Okafor had imagined. He'd been picturing something the size of a cargo container, something worth three fleets killing each other over. What he saw instead was a strongbox scarcely bigger than a sea chest, iron-banded, its lid still sealed, its lockplate crusted but whole. It had been strapped down with purpose—someone had wanted this box to survive, had bolted its cradle to the deck itself, and the sea had done everything it could for sixty years and failed to move it.

Gideon hung in the water above it, torch steady, and for a long moment she didn't move at all.

Okafor watched her. He'd seen her take delivery of prize salvage, seen her hold deeds and ledgers and once, memorably, a bag of uncut stones worth more than the boat they sat on. She'd never gone still like this. This was the stillness of someone touching something sacred without meaning to—kneeling at a shrine they didn't believe in and finding, to their horror, that they believed in it completely.

Her gloved hand settled on the lid. Rested there. Just rested, the way you'd put a hand on a coffin, or a shoulder.

"Elena," she said, very quietly, and Okafor pretended not to hear, and the comm line carried it anyway, because water carries everything.

Her mother. Of course. Whatever chain had led from a drowned admiral's strongbox to a dead woman's daughter ran through this exact spot, and Gideon was kneeling on the far end of it, and Okafor understood suddenly that she wasn't reaching for treasure down here. She was reaching for a hand that had let go of hers twenty years ago in a harbor at dawn, and this was the closest she'd ever come to touching it again.

"We're taking it home," Gideon said—to the box, or the ship, or the woman, Okafor couldn't tell. "Whatever's in you, it's not staying down here."

"Twelve minutes," Okafor said gently.

She moved then, all business, and together they worked the box free of its cradle—prying, lifting, cursing through regulators, the fallen beam groaning above them in a way Okafor chose not to interpret. The box came loose all at once and nearly took Gideon's arm with it, and then it was between them, gripped by both their harness lines, heavy as sin and twice as hard to carry.

They backed out through the throat of the ship, past door seven, past the boot, past the lantern in its bracket, and Okafor made himself do the thing he'd been avoiding since they entered: he swept his torch back along the corridor one last time and gave the dead their due. All of them. The whistler and the cook-grudge and the girl in port and the admiral whose order had brought them here, whoever he'd been, whatever he'd known.

*Rest easy,* he thought, in his grandmother's language. *We'll carry the rest.*

* * *

Coming up was coming back to life.

The black water lightened by degrees—from ink to bruise to the deep green-grey of night surface—and then the hull of the *Patient Answer* materialized overhead, her running lights bleeding soft color down through the swell, and Okafor broke the surface into rain and wind and the smell of diesel and salt and living air, and he laughed, actually laughed, hanging on the ladder with a dead nation's strongbox banging against his hip, because that was what surfacing did to him, every time, and always had.

They hauled the box aboard. Gideon stripped her mask and stood dripping on the deck, staring down at it, chest heaving, and Okafor got the canvas cover over the thing before either of them could think too hard about what they were holding.

Below, Sela was waiting with a lamp and a pry bar and the expression of a woman who intended to open that box if she had to bite through the bands, cracked rib and all. From the galley, Tomas watched through the doorway with his hands wrapped around a mug he wasn't drinking, his face doing arithmetic he clearly hated.

And Okafor, pulling off his gloves, felt it—that prickle at the neck, the old sailor's sense, the one that had never once lied to him any more than his knee had. He stepped to the stern rail and looked northwest, where his knee had promised the storm.

Lightning flickered inside the cloud bank, silent at this distance, illuminating the horizon in slow pulses. And between pulses—there and gone, there and gone, right at the edge of seeing—a white running light, moving. Steady. Deliberate. Coming down the same bearing they'd sailed in on, as if it knew the way.

"Gideon," he said quietly.

She joined him at the rail. Together they watched the light grow, and neither of them said the name of the ship it belonged to, because saying it wouldn't make it slower.

"How long to weigh anchor?" she asked.

Okafor looked at the sky, at the sea, at the box under its canvas, and at the woman who had dragged all of them out past the edge of every map.

"Not fast enough," he said.
