## 24. The Strongbox

The engine room of the flagship was a cathedral of rust.

Gideon hung in the black water with her lamp beam cutting a cone through the silt, and the beam found ribs—iron ribs, arched overhead where the hull had folded inward like a ribcage around a heart that had stopped a century ago. The Meridian flagship. She'd seen drawings of it in Sela's salvaged charts, all proud lines and gun decks, the sun-and-wave banner snapping at the prow in some long-dead engraver's imagination. What remained was a wound in the reef, furred with coral, breathing slow clouds of sediment every time the swell pushed water through the broken stern.

Okafor hovered at her shoulder, his own lamp sweeping methodically left to right, the way he swept everything—deck, galley, horizon. Steady as ballast. He tapped her arm twice: *you lead, I follow.* They'd worked out the signals on the descent, in the boat, over twenty years of shared weather. Two taps meant trust. One tap meant trouble. Three meant *surface now, argue later.*

Her lamp caught it at last: wedged between two collapsed deck plates near what had been the engine housing, a box the size of a sea chest, iron-banded, its lid crusted white with a century of calcification. The admiral's strongbox. Sela's decoded manifest had been specific—*one sealed strongbox, admiral's custody, no inventory disclosed*—and Gideon had spent the whole dive telling herself that manifest might be wrong, that manifests were written by clerks and clerks lied, that maybe, just maybe, there was gold down here after all. Gold she could weigh against forty thousand marks. Gold she could pour into Lark's smiling hands and walk away clean.

The strongbox did not look like it held gold. It looked like it held a verdict.

She hooked the lift bag's shackle under the forward band and checked it twice. Okafor put his hands on the box's seaward edge, bracing, and looked at her across four feet of glowing silt. Even through the mask she could read his eyes: *last chance to leave it.*

She shook her head. He nodded once, unsurprised, and they filled the bag together, and the box rose out of the reef's grip with a groan of shifting plates and a bloom of sediment that swallowed their lamps whole. For three seconds Gideon hung blind in the dark inside a dead ship, listening to her own heartbeat hammer through the regulator, feeling the wreck press close around her like a hand.

Then the silt cleared, Okafor's light found hers, and they swam up out of the drowned nation into the ordinary black of the night sea, where the Patient Answer rode at anchor with her single riding light burning like a candle someone had forgotten to blow out.

* * *

They didn't open it on deck.

That was Sela's ruling, delivered in the flat voice she used when she'd already done the thinking for everyone: salt water and a century could have compromised the seals; the contents might be paper; paper and spray were enemies; therefore the box came below, into the chart room, and the hatch stayed dogged until it was done. Gideon suspected the real reason was simpler—that whatever was inside deserved walls around it—but she didn't argue. She was shivering anyway, the kind of deep cold that came from diving at night and stayed in the bones until dawn.

Tomas met them at the rail with towels and the ship's one bottle of decent brandy, pouring tots with hands that weren't quite steady. "You were down ninety minutes," he said. "I counted."

"You always count," Gideon said.

"Someone on this boat has to." He pressed a glass into her hand. His eyes went past her to the box, dripping on the deck between them, and something moved through his face too fast for her to name. She told herself it was curiosity. She would keep telling herself that for another day and a half, and by then it wouldn't matter anymore.

Below, in the chart room, Sela had cleared her table down to bare wood and laid out her tools like a surgeon: pry bar, mallet, chisels of three sizes, rags, and a tin of the preservative oil she used on her oldest maps. The hanging lamp swung gently with the swell, throwing their shadows around the small room like a crew twice their number.

"Everyone touch nothing until I say," Sela said, and set the chisel to the crust along the lid seam.

It took an hour. The calcification came away in flakes and sheets, white as bone meal, and beneath it the iron was pitted but sound—the work of people who had built this box to survive exactly what it had survived. There was a lock, a heavy brass thing gone green with age, but when Sela traced the escutcheon with her fingertip she made a small sound of surprise.

"It's not locked."

"What?" Okafor leaned in.

"No keyhole mechanism engaged. Look—" she tilted the lamp. "There's a bolt, but it's drawn. Someone closed this box and did not lock it. Someone wanted it to be opened."

Gideon felt the words go through her like cold water through cloth. *Planted,* Ilsa had said, or nearly said, in the way she said everything—by indirection, by story, by the memory of a woman who had sailed with the last admiral and died leaving warnings instead of instructions. *Your mother chose this path for you.* A chest in a flooded river. A cipher in a dead language. A box sealed but not locked, waiting a hundred years at the bottom of the world for a pair of hands that hadn't been born yet when it sank.

"Open it," Gideon said.

Sela lifted the lid.

* * *

No gold.

The thought arrived first, before sight, almost before breath—a flat little disappointment that lasted exactly as long as it took her lamp to find the topmost layer, and then it was gone, burned away by what she was actually looking at.

Paper. Reams of it, wrapped in oilcloth, layered with the care of people packing something they expected to matter forever. Sela lifted the first bundle out with both hands, reverent, and unwrapped it on the table, weighting the corners with her chisel handles.

The vellum underneath was thick as hide and written in a hand so formal it looked printed. Across its head, in ink that had gone brown but not faint, ran a device Gideon knew from the chest's lid, from Sela's charts, from a hundred salvage-yard rumors: the sun rising out of the wave. Beneath it, in letters each as tall as her thumb:

*CHARTER OF SOVEREIGNTY OF THE NATION OF MERIDIA*

Beneath that, clauses. Signatures. Wax seals the size of coins, still intact, still red, sunk in a groove in the vellum so deep the wax had never touched anything else in a hundred years.

Nobody spoke. The lamp swung. Somewhere above them the hull ticked and sighed against the anchor chain.

"That's—" Tomas started, and stopped.

"The founding document," Sela said quietly. Her fingers hovered over the vellum without touching it, the way she touched nothing she loved. "Not a copy. Not an enrolled duplicate. This is the instrument itself—the act that made Meridia a nation. You can't forge provenance like this. The seals, the hand, the substrate..." She shook her head slowly. "This is the country. This *was* the country. Everything else was built on the existence of this page."

Gideon reached out and turned the next bundle over. Ledgers. Dozens of them, bound in board covers gone soft with age, spines labeled in the same formal hand. She opened the nearest one at random and found columns—names, sums, dates—and the dates ran backward through the last years of Meridia, and the names were not Meridian names.

"Read me that entry," Okafor said. His voice had changed. It had gone very quiet, the way it went quiet before weather.

Gideon read it. Then she read it again, because the first time her eyes had refused to assemble it into sense.

"*Received of A. Vane, per arrangement, the sum of—*" The figure was obscene. It was more money than the Patient Answer would earn in forty lifetimes. "*—in consideration of conveyance of certain naval stores, munitions, and materials of war, delivery to be effected prior to the onset of the monsoon season, receipt acknowledged.*"

"Munitions," Sela repeated. She pulled another ledger across the table, then another, opening them with increasing speed, her earlier reverence burned off and replaced by something harder. "Look at the pattern. Six months before the drowning, Vane's house buys Meridia's coastal gun batteries' ammunition reserves—at a discount. Four months before, he purchases the grain contracts for the winter fleet. Three months before—" she stopped on a page and went pale. "Three months before, he insures the harbor warehouses. Through his own underwriters. And then—"

"And then the storm season came early," Gideon said.

The room was very still. Outside, the sea made its patient sounds against the hull, the same sounds it had been making over Meridia for a hundred years.

"They didn't drown," Tomas said. He'd backed up until he was against the bulkhead, brandy glass forgotten in his hand. "You're saying they were *sold*. He bought the guns' ammunition, bought the food, insured the wreckage, and let the monsoon do the rest. And when it was over there was no government left to contest the salvage claims, so he bought the seabed, the harbors, the—"

"The sovereignty papers went down with the flagship," Sela said. "Deliberately. The admiral must have known what was coming. He loaded the charter and the ledgers—*the evidence*, the whole accounting of what Vane had done—and sent them to the bottom rather than let Vane destroy them. Because destroyed documents end questions, but lost ones..." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "Lost ones can be found."

Gideon stood over the table with the lamp in her hand and let the whole shape of it rise out of the dark the way the flagship had risen out of the reef.

Augustin Vane. The name she'd heard spoken in dockside bars the way sailors spoke of reefs—with respect, with hatred, mostly with the careful neutrality of men who intended to go on living. The Syndicate hadn't started as a salvage concern. That was the story, the cheerful public story: a family firm that grew, that consolidated, that won. But the ledgers said the firm had been founded on a purchase, and the purchase had been founded on a murder committed slowly, season by season, contract by contract, on an entire nation. Forty thousand marks. Her debt, her terror, Lark's smile, the gunboat somewhere behind them in the dark—all of it, every coin of it, traced back down through the years to these pages, to a grandfather counting out the price of a country while the rain came in.

"He didn't build an empire," she said. "He inherited a theft. And everyone who's ever borrowed from him, everyone who's ever owed him—we've all been paying interest on a drowned nation."

Okafor set his empty glass down on the chart shelf with a click that sounded enormous. "Say it plain, Captain."

"If this surfaces—if these pages reach a court, a newspaper, a rival house, anyone—every charter the Syndicate holds is void. Every claim. Every harbor, every fleet, every debt they've ever collected." She heard her own voice go strange, steadier than she felt. "Vane doesn't own things. He owns *paper*. And here's the paper that unmakes him."

"So we sell it," Tomas said quickly. Too quickly. "One buyer. Quietly. Any of the great houses would pay—"

"Would they?" Sela asked, mild as acid. "Which house would buy the proof that buying countries is possible? Which house would want this loose in the world?" She began rewrapping the charter with the tenderness she'd show a wounded bird. "No. The moment this leaves our hands, it becomes a weapon in someone's arsenal, and weapons get used on people like us."

Gideon looked at the charter lying in its oilcloth, the sun-and-wave seal catching the lamplight, red as the day it was pressed. A whole nation fit inside a box small enough for two divers to carry. She thought of her mother, who had served the last admiral, who had vanished leaving warnings, who had planted a chest in a river like a woman setting a fuse and walking away. Elena Quintana had known what was in this box. Had known what it cost and what it could do. And she had not sold it. She had not even delivered it. She had hidden it where only someone who read tides the way she did would ever find it—which meant she had trusted the finding to blood, and the choosing to whoever the blood turned out to be.

"Captain," Okafor said softly. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look you get before you do the thing none of us can talk you out of."

Gideon laughed—short, unsteady, real. She looked down at the evidence of the crime that had shaped her whole life, her debts and her running and her mother's grave-less disappearance, all of it rooted in this iron box. And she felt, for the first time since Mr. Lark had smiled at her across a tavern table, something that wasn't fear.

"We're not selling it," she said. "And we're not spending it. We're going to finish what the admiral started."

Before anyone could answer, boots crossed the deck above them—fast, uneven, heading for the companionway—and Tomas's face, when Gideon glanced at him, had gone the color of the vellum on the table.

The hatch banged open. "Lights," Tomas said hoarsely. "Off the port bow. Running lights doused. It's been shadowing us since slack water."

Through the chart room's tiny rear port, far off across the black water, a smudge of darker dark was resolving itself into a shape—a long, low shape, moving without hurry, the way things move when they already know they've won.

The Perpetual Dawn had found them.
