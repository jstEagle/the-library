## 25. Betrayal at Anchor

Tomas Quintana had spent twenty years learning to read a harbor the way other men read faces. He could tell you which crane operator was drinking again, which customs clerk took bribes in coffee tins instead of cash, which ship at anchor was carrying something it shouldn't. It was a useful talent for a fixer. It had kept him fed through the lean years, kept his daughter in shoes and school fees, kept him one careful step ahead of the men who'd have taken everything from him if he ever stopped being useful.

It had not taught him how to read his sister.

He stood at the rail of the *Patient Answer* as the sun went down behind the Shoals of Names, watching the water turn the color of old bronze, and he tried to read Gideon now. She was at the helm, silhouetted against the last light, one hand on the wheel, the other holding a mug of tea she hadn't touched in an hour. Okafor was below with the engine, coaxing another day out of machinery that had been dying since before Tomas was born. Sela was in the chart room with her memorized maps and her photographs of documents that had already cost them a ship.

And somewhere out there, past the line where the sea went dark, was the *Perpetual Dawn*.

Tomas knew she was coming. He'd known since the fourth tide, when he'd keyed the first burst transmission into the little radio he'd hidden in the bilge locker—a radio the Syndicate had given him two years ago, along with a photograph of his daughter's school and a friendly reminder about what happened to fathers who forgot their arithmetic. He'd been sending position reports ever since. Short bursts. Encrypted. Guilt compressed into static.

He told himself every time that this would be the last one. That the gunboat would find them, take the chest, take Gideon, and leave the rest of them alive because Lark was a reasonable man and reasonable men didn't waste crew they didn't need. That was the arithmetic. He'd done it a hundred times in the dark of the forward hold, lying on a bunk that smelled of diesel and salt, running the numbers like a man counting coins he hated the weight of.

One chest against five lives. One dead woman's secrets against a living girl's future. Paper against blood.

The arithmetic always came out the same. That was the thing about cowardice—done properly, it looked just like prudence.

* * *

He almost didn't send the final report. That was the truth he would carry afterward, the splinter under the skin: he almost didn't.

They had anchored in the lee of a drowned reef an hour after dark, in water so shallow the trawler's keel seemed to whisper against the sand below. Gideon called it slack water anchorage—the only window when the currents through the wreck field went still enough to dive. Tomorrow, at the sixth tide, she and Okafor would go down to the flagship. Tonight, the sea held its breath, and so did they.

Tomas had volunteered for the middle watch. Nobody questioned it. He was the newest member of the crew, the one still earning back his place, and taking the ugly hours was what earning looked like. Gideon had clapped him on the shoulder as she went below—her hand heavy, warm, trusting—and said, "Wake me if anything moves."

*Anything moves.* He stood in the wheelhouse with the radio six feet away, hidden under a false panel he'd installed himself, and understood that the thing moving was him. Had always been him. Every mile they'd made toward the wreck, he'd been the wake trailing behind them, pointing home like a wound arrow.

He thought about his daughter. Mariel, eleven years old, gap-toothed, ferocious, drawing ships on the backs of her schoolwork because her father worked on the water and she wanted to follow him there someday. The Syndicate held forty thousand marks of her father's debt—not Gideon's debt; Tomas had his own, older and quieter—and Vane's collectors had explained, with Mr. Lark's particular smiling courtesy, exactly how debts transferred when a debtor died. How a girl with no family became a ward of whatever institution paid her keep. How certain institutions were cheaper than others.

He thought about Mariel, and then he thought about Gideon saying *wake me if anything moves*, and the two thoughts did not fit together in his chest. They ground against each other like hulls in a crowded harbor, and the sound they made was the sound of a man deciding.

He opened the panel. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Cowardice, done often enough, becomes a kind of craft.

He keyed the transmitter. Gave the position true and full: latitude, longitude, the reef's bearing, the anchorage depth, the slack-water window. Everything. If he was going to do it, he would do it completely—half-truths got people killed messier than whole ones. He added the detail that mattered most, the detail he'd been withholding for three days out of some animal loyalty he couldn't kill: *they intend to dive the flagship at sixth tide. Two divers. The rest will be aboard.*

Then he sat down on the deck plates and put his head in his hands and waited for the acknowledgment click that meant the *Perpetual Dawn* had heard him, and when it came—two short pulses, almost gentle—he felt nothing at all, which was its own kind of horror. Twenty years of small betrayals, and the body finally learns to stop billing you for them.

* * *

He must have slept, because the next thing he knew the sky was going gray at the edges and Gideon was climbing the wheelhouse ladder with two mugs of tea, and she was smiling at him.

That was the worst part. Not the gunboat—that came later, that came like a verdict long expected. The worst part was the smile, and the tea pressed warm into his hands, and the way she said, "You stayed awake. Good man," as though he were something a good man could be.

He drank the tea so he wouldn't have to answer. It was terrible tea, over-steeped, bitter enough to strip paint, and he thought: I deserve this tea. I will drink terrible tea for the rest of my life and call it penance.

Below, Okafor was checking dive gear by lamplight, laying out tanks and lines with the methodical tenderness of a man preparing gifts. Sela had the manifest pages spread across the chart table, her finger tracing the columns of a dead navy's handwriting, murmuring numbers to herself like prayers. The strongbox. The admiral's seal. Documents sealed in lead, weighted to survive the drowning of a nation. Gideon talked about it the way pilgrims talk about relics—carefully, half-afraid of what holiness might ask of them.

"They'll want to know what we found," she said over breakfast, meaning the Syndicate, meaning everyone. "When they come. And they'll come." She said it lightly, but her eyes went around the wheelhouse, touching each of them. "So we decide now what the answer is."

"The answer is we found nothing," Tomas said. His voice came out flat and strange. "We found an empty wreck and a lot of rust."

Gideon looked at him a beat too long. Not suspicion—she wasn't built for suspicion, or maybe she'd spent it all on other things—but something adjacent, something that made him set down his mug very slowly.

"Maybe," she said. "Depends what we find."

Okafor grunted from the corner, oil-black hands wrapped around his own mug. "Depends who asks, too. A man with a ledger asks different than a man with a gun."

"A man with a ledger has a man with a gun," Tomas said, and heard how it sounded, and wished he could climb out of his own voice and swim away.

Sela laughed—not much, just a breath of it—and started rolling up her charts, and the moment passed the way moments do aboard a small boat, absorbed into the routine of ropes and tanks and weather. But Tomas carried it with him all morning, down into the hold, into the bilge locker where he checked the hidden radio one more time and found the acknowledgment light still green, still patient, still waiting.

*They intend to dive at sixth tide,* his message had said. *Two divers. The rest aboard.*

He had handed them the schedule of his family's execution, and he'd written it in his own neat harbor-fixing hand.

* * *

The *Perpetual Dawn* came at midday, out of the heat shimmer, exactly where his coordinates said she would be.

He saw her first. He was on deck coiling line—useless work, work for hands that needed occupying—and he caught the glint off her superstructure while she was still a suggestion on the horizon, and for three full minutes he stood there with the coil of rope in his arms and said nothing, doing the arithmetic one final time, faster now, desperate, the numbers blurring.

If he warned them, the gunboat would know. They'd trace the warning to his radio, or assume it, which amounted to the same thing, and then Mariel's debt would become something worse than debt. If he didn't warn them, the crew might run—they had the shoals, Gideon's charts, a chance, a slim chance—and the gunboat would open fire on a fleeing trawler with four people aboard, and the Syndicate's marines would fish survivors out of the water or they wouldn't, and either way the chest would be taken and the people he—

He stopped. He couldn't finish the sentence even inside his own head. *The people he loved.* When had that happened? When had these people—his estranged half-sister who wouldn't look at him for nine years, the deckhand who barely spoke, the cartographer who flinched when he entered a room—when had they become the word he couldn't say?

Resistance means all their deaths. Surrender means most of them live. That was the whole equation. He'd solved harder ones for smaller stakes.

"Gunboat," he said. "Bearing north-northeast. Coming fast."

Gideon was up the ladder before he finished, spyglass already rising, and he watched her face go through recognition and calculation and settle into something cold and bright that he recognized from childhood, from the summer their mother left, from every storm Gideon had ever driven straight into because turning aside felt like dying.

"How long?" she asked.

"Twenty minutes. Maybe less. She's not hiding."

"No," Gideon said softly. "She wants us to see her coming." She turned, and the orders came out clean and quick—Okafor to the engine, Sela to secure the documents, Tomas to the lines—and then she paused, one foot on the ladder, and looked back at him.

"Tomas. Whatever happens—" She hesitated, and he watched her choose the next words with the care of a woman defusing something. "Whatever happens, nobody dies today for paper. Remember that. We're salvage crews, not soldiers. We bend."

*We bend.* He nodded. He smiled. He agreed with his sister with a liar's whole heart, because bending was exactly what he'd already done, snapped clean through, and the fracture was sitting in the bilge locker with a green light glowing on it.

* * *

The *Perpetual Dawn* filled the sky before she filled the sea. Three hundred tons of gray steel riding a bow wave like a wall falling forward, her guns—guns, actual guns, mounted on a ship sent to collect paperwork—trained casually seaward in the universal language of plausible deniability. She slowed a quarter mile out and a launch dropped from her side, white wake curving toward them, and standing in the bow, hatless, hands clasped behind his back like a tourist at a railing, was Mr. Lark.

Tomas watched him come and felt the strangest sensation: relief. Genuine, physical relief, loosening his shoulders, unclenching his jaw. Because Lark was reasonable. Lark was the smiling end of the machine, and machines with smiling ends only took what you gave them. This was the outcome he'd purchased with three days of betrayal in the dark. This was the price paid and the goods delivered. Everyone lives. The chest goes. The arithmetic closes.

He believed it right up until the launch bumped against the hull and Lark climbed aboard, and Gideon stepped forward to meet him, and Lark's eyes—warm, apologetic, human eyes—swept past her and found Tomas at the rail.

Lark smiled at him. Just for a second. A small smile, private, the smile of a man acknowledging a colleague's work across a crowded room.

And Tomas felt the bottom drop out of the arithmetic, because he understood, in one vertical plunge of certainty, that it had never mattered whether he sent the reports. The *Perpetual Dawn* had been coming since the day they left the harbor. His transmissions hadn't bought his daughter's safety. They'd bought nothing except his own knowledge of his own reflection, and Lark's smile was the receipt.

"Miss Quintana," Lark said warmly, removing his hat. "You've led us a remarkable dance. Six tides—I confess I lost money on you making four."

"You should bet on family," Gideon said. Her voice was level. Her hand, hanging at her side, was not quite still. "You're a long way from your office, Mr. Lark."

"I go where the accounts are." He gestured pleasantly at the trawler, the rigging, the scarred deck. "The chest, if you please. And then we can all go home and no one has to write any difficult letters."

"It's aboard," Gideon said.

Tomas's head came up. Across the deck, he saw Okafor go very still by the engine hatch, saw Sela's hands stop moving over the chart case. *It's aboard.* She was giving it to him. No bluff, no fight, no bending—just handing over the thing they'd bled for, and Tomas felt something crack sideways in his chest, because he'd sold them all to prevent exactly this surrender, and here it was anyway, arriving without his help, making his treason a wasted country.

But Gideon wasn't finished. "It's aboard," she repeated, "and it's yours. On one condition. You tell me what's in it."

Lark's smile flickered—the first honest expression Tomas had ever seen on him, a flash of genuine surprise, quickly banked. "You don't know?"

"I know what I hope it isn't." Gideon's chin came up. "I want to hear you say it."

For a long moment the only sound was water slapping the hull and the distant idle of the gunboat's engines, and Tomas stood at the rail with his guilt rising in him like floodwater, understanding suddenly that his sister had walked into this meeting knowing—knowing the way she knew storms—knowing that Lark would never say it aloud, that the Syndicate's whole empire depended on no one ever saying it aloud, and that she was buying time the way she bought everything: with nerve.

"Miss Quintana," Lark said gently, "the contents are not your concern. They never were."

"That's what I thought." She turned to Okafor. "Bring up the chest."

And Tomas moved to help him—because what else was there, because the play was over, because the only mercy left was speed—and as they hauled the lead-lined box up from the hold, its Meridian seal catching the noon sun, sun-and-wave, a nation's signature on a coffin lid, he caught Gideon's eye over the top of it.

She was looking at him strangely. Not angry. Worse. She was looking at him the way you look at weather coming, and she said, quietly, only for him:

"When this is done, brother, you and I are going to have a conversation about the bilge locker."

The chest went over the side into Lark's launch. The launch pulled away. And Tomas stood at the rail of the *Patient Answer*, watching his family's surrender recede toward the gunboat, waiting for the relief that did not come, because deep in the hold, under the false panel, the little radio's acknowledgment light had begun to blink—two short pulses, then two more—and the message underneath them, decoded in his head with the fluency of two years' practice, read:

*Search the trawler. All hands detained. The finder comes aboard.*

Behind him, Gideon began, very softly, to laugh.
