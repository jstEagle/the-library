## 26. Boarding Party

Okafor had counted the guns twice and gotten the same answer both times: four marines on the deck of the *Perpetual Dawn*, six more in the launch cutting toward them through the gray water, and one man in a good coat standing at the rail with his hands folded like a priest at a wedding.

"Twelve," he said. "Twelve against five."

"Four and a half," Sela said from the chart table, not looking up. "Tomas doesn't count."

"Tomas counts," Gideon said quietly.

Okafor looked at her. She was standing at the wheel of the *Patient Answer* with her hands loose on the spokes, watching the gunboat's launch come on, and there was something in her face he'd only seen once before—the night she'd taken them into the storm line, when the whole world went white and roaring and she'd smiled like it was a homecoming. It wasn't courage, exactly. Courage was what you found when you needed it. This was something she'd packed in advance.

"They're going to search us," Okafor said. "They're going to find it."

"No," Gideon said. "They're going to find *a* it. That's different."

The launch came alongside with a bump that shivered the old trawler's hull, and the first marine came over the rail before the lines were even fast—professional, unhurried, rifle slung across his chest like it was furniture. The others followed. They spread across the deck without being told, two to the bow, two aft, one at the companionway, and Okafor understood this was not their first boarded boat. Somewhere behind them all, Mr. Lark stepped down onto the *Patient Answer*'s wet planking, adjusted his cuffs, and smiled at Gideon like they were neighbors discussing a fence line.

"Captain Quintana." He said her name the way other men said *good morning*. "You've led us a long way. I hope the fuel bill isn't ruinous."

"You could cover it," Gideon said. "Consider it interest."

"I'll consider it." Lark's eyes moved over the deck—the coiled lines, the fish boxes, Sela at her charts, Okafor by the winch, Tomas half-hidden in the shadow of the wheelhouse with his hands very still. "Now. I'm going to ask you for the chest, and you're going to tell me where it is, and depending on how those two exchanges go, everyone here is going to have either a very good evening or a very bad one. I'd prefer the good one. I suspect you would too."

Okafor's heart was going like a drum in a parade, but his hands were steady, because that was the thing about him—Gideon had said it once, drunk, on the night they'd met, when he'd pulled her out of a flooded hold without dropping the lamp: *you don't shake, Okafor, even when you should.* He didn't shake now. He watched Lark watch Gideon, and he waited for the signal that hadn't been discussed because it couldn't be discussed, the signal that lived somewhere between the five of them like a fifth crew member nobody had named.

"The chest is below," Gideon said. "In my cabin. Under the bunk."

Lark's smile widened a fraction. "That was easy."

"It's heavy," Gideon said. "I got tired of carrying it around in my head."

And Okafor saw it—not in her face, which gave nothing, but in the rhythm of it. She was giving him the chest too fast, the way you throw a dog a bone so it doesn't sniff your pockets. Whether Lark saw it too, Okafor couldn't tell. The man's face was a locked drawer.

"Show me," Lark said.

* * *

The cabin was small enough that three people filled it: Lark in the doorway, a marine crowding behind him, Gideon kneeling at the bunk. Okafor stood in the passageway outside, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who has already lost and knows it—which was, as it happened, exactly the expression Gideon had asked him to wear an hour ago, in the galley, in a whisper, while Sela drew diagrams in spilled flour.

The chest sat under the bunk where she said it would be. Lead-lined, sun-and-wave seal, salt-scoured wood. It looked exactly like the chest they'd pulled from the river, because it *was* the chest they'd pulled from the river—or rather, its twin, the one Sela had built in eleven hours of furious carpentry while the rest of them slept in shifts, down to the hinge corrosion she'd faked with vinegar and a wire brush.

Gideon dragged it into the light. It hit the deck with the right weight—a sound like money, like consequence.

Lark crouched beside it. He ran his thumb along the seal. For a long moment he said nothing, and the marine behind him shifted, and somewhere above them boots crossed the deck, and Okafor counted his own heartbeats the way he counted everything, one, two, three, waiting.

Then Lark opened it.

Inside, nested in rotted cloth, lay documents. Dozens of them. Vellum stiff with age, wax seals the color of dried blood, a ledger bound in cracked leather with MERIDIAN ADMIRALTY stamped along the spine. Even Okafor, who had spent his life around machinery and men and very little paper, felt the air change. It looked real. It looked *magnificent*. Sela had aged the decoys with tea and smoke and a candle held too close, and she had copied the seals from memory, and she had wept a little doing it, though she'd denied it after.

Lark lifted the ledger. Turned pages. His fingers were careful, almost tender.

"Well," he said at last, softly. "Well, well."

He closed it. Stood. And looked at Gideon with an expression Okafor had never seen on him before—something almost like respect, threaded through with something else, something colder.

"You understand," Lark said, "that I have to verify. You understand that I can't simply take your word that this is everything."

"Take it or don't," Gideon said. "It's what I have."

"Mm." He handed the ledger to the marine. "Seal the cabin. Nothing leaves this boat until Mr. Vane's people have gone through every page." He turned to go, then paused, one hand on the doorframe, and glanced back. "Captain. A word of professional admiration. Most people, when cornered, hide the treasure. You put it under the bunk like a teenager hiding cigarettes. Either you're telling me the truth, or you are the finest liar I have ever been paid to catch."

He left.

Okafor let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since the storm line.

* * *

The trouble was the marines didn't leave.

They stayed aboard. Two on deck, one in the wheelhouse, one—worse—one detailed to stand in the passageway outside Gideon's sealed cabin, guarding the chest, while the launch went back to the *Perpetual Dawn* to fetch, presumably, an archivist. Night came down over the anchorage, and the gunboat's floodlights swept the trawler's rigging, and the five of them moved through the *Patient Answer* like prisoners in their own home.

They gathered in the galley, one at a time, casual, in ones and twos, because the marine at the companionway kept glancing in and they could not afford to look like a council of war. Okafor stirred a pot of nothing—salt pork and water, mostly water—and listened.

"He bought it," Tomas whispered. He was wringing his hands, and had been since sundown. "He took the bait. We're done."

"We're not done," Sela said. Her arm was still bandaged from the fire, and she held it against her ribs like a bird with a hurt wing, but her voice was flint. "He said *verify*. Verification means experts. Experts mean someone who's seen real Meridian vellum. My decoys are good. They are not *expert*-proof."

"How long do we have?"

She calculated with her eyes on the ceiling. "If they have an archivist aboard the gunboat—dawn. Maybe midnight if they're eager."

"And then?" Okafor asked, though he knew.

"And then they open the real strongbox," Gideon said from the doorway, "which is currently wrapped in oilcloth inside the bilge tank under the engine room, and they shoot us, and they take it, and that's the end of the story." She came in and shut the galley door behind her, which was either brave or pointless. "So we don't wait for dawn."

Okafor set down the ladle. "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have a plan-shaped object."

"That's usually how yours start."

She laid it out fast, low-voiced, in the shorthand of people who'd been improvising together for weeks: the bilge tank couldn't be opened without draining it, and draining it meant pumps, and pumps meant noise and time, and time was the one thing the marines would give them none of. But the *Patient Answer* was forty years old and rotten in the places only Okafor knew about, and one of those places was the seam aft of the engine room, the one he'd been meaning to weld since spring, the one that wept a little in heavy seas. If the seacocks were opened—if the sea were invited in, in the right place, at the right rate—

"You want to sink us," Okafor said.

"I want to *start* sinking us," Gideon said. "Slowly. In shallow water, at anchor, with a gunboat full of witnesses. Nobody lets a ship full of Meridian documents go down unexamined. They'll panic. They'll pull the chest off first—it's the obvious prize—and while they're hauling it up their ladder and arguing about it, we're in the water, and the tank stays shut, and the real papers stay dry in their oilcloth, and by the time anyone thinks to check the bilge we're gone."

"Gone where?"

"Away."

"That's not a place, Gideon."

"It's the only place we've got."

Silence in the galley. The pot muttered. Through the bulkhead they could hear the marine shifting his weight, bored, cold, alive.

"There's a flaw," Tomas said quietly. Everyone looked at him. He'd barely spoken in two days—not since the night Okafor had hauled him out of the water, not since whatever arithmetic he'd been doing with the Syndicate had finally come due. His face was gray. "When the flooding starts, they're going to think we scuttled her to destroy the evidence. Lark will know it's a bluff. He'll let her go down and dive for the chest later, and he'll let *us* drown, because dead captains can't testify."

Gideon looked at him for a long moment. "Yes," she said. "That's the flaw."

"So we need him to believe the ship is worth saving," Okafor said slowly, working it through. "We need him to believe *we* believe the papers are worth saving—that we'd rather fight than sink."

"We need chaos," Sela said. "Real chaos. Not performed. Performed chaos reads like performance. It has to start honest and get away from us."

Okafor wiped his hands on his apron. He thought about the seam aft of the engine room. He thought about the pump he'd rebuilt three times. He thought about the fact that he had never in his life abandoned a ship, that the *Patient Answer* had been his home longer than any house had, that Gideon was asking him to open her veins and trust a family of liars and a repentant traitor to close them again in time.

"When?" he said.

"Third watch," Gideon said. "When the gunboat's watch changes. When Lark's asleep, if he sleeps."

"And the signal?"

Gideon smiled, and it was the storm-line smile, and Okafor felt his stomach drop the way it always did.

"There won't be one," she said. "There won't be time for one. Just start pumping air into the tank and listen for me screaming."

* * *

Third watch came down gray and drizzling. The gunboat's lights swung. On the *Patient Answer*, the new sentry at the companionway stamped his feet against the cold, and Okafor, alone in the engine room with a wrench and a prayer, knelt at the seacocks and did the hardest thing he had ever done with his hands.

He opened them a quarter turn. Then a half.

The sea came in the way it always does—patiently, indifferently, finding the lowest places first. He heard it whispering under the floor plates. He started the bilge pump, loud, unmistakable, a machine shouting *we have a problem* into the night, and then he climbed the ladder and began to shout himself.

"Water! Water in the engine room! Seacock's let go—we're taking water!"

Chaos, as advertised, arrived honest.

The sentry came running. Boots thundered overhead. Gideon burst out of her cabin yelling about the chest—*the chest, get the chest up, get it off the boat*—and the marine guarding it hesitated between his orders and the rising water, and that hesitation was the whole plan in miniature. Lark appeared on deck in his coat, no hat, hair mussed, and for one unguarded second his face was not a locked drawer at all; it was a man doing arithmetic at speed, and Okafor, hauling the fake chest up the ladder with Gideon straining at the other handle, watched the numbers land.

If the ship sank, the chest might sink with it. If the chest was destroyed, Vane destroyed *him*.

"Get it aboard," Lark snapped. "Get it aboard *now*. All of you—Quintana, your people, in the launch, move—" Because a drowning crew was witnesses, and witnesses were leverage, and leverage was the only currency Lark had ever traded in.

They went over the side into the launch, all five of them, soaked and stumbling, the lead-lined decoy thumping down between their feet like a captured god. Behind them the *Patient Answer* sat lower in the water than she should have, listing gently, her running lights reflecting in the black swell, and Okafor looked back at her and felt something crack in his chest that he'd have to deal with later, much later, maybe never.

Aboard the *Perpetual Dawn*, under floodlights, Lark pried open the chest himself.

Okafor watched his face do it. Watched the confidence, the relief, the beginning of the smile—and then watched the smile stop, stall, die. Lark lifted out the topmost document. Held it to the light. Lifted another. His thumb moved across the vellum, back and forth, back and forth, and Okafor, who could read a man's hands better than most men read print, saw the exact moment the tea-stained forgery announced itself.

Lark looked up. Found Gideon across the wet deck. And laughed—one short, helpless bark of a laugh, entirely without humor.

"Oh, Captain," he said. "Oh, you *magnificent*—"

The shout came from astern then, from the *Patient Answer*, where the prize crew he'd left aboard had finally thought to check the bilge tank and found the seacocks closed and the tank dry and the oilcloth bundle gone from its hiding place—gone, because Sela had moved it an hour before the flooding ever started, sewn into the canvas of the mainsail itself, folded flat, invisible, riding above their heads the entire time.

But Lark didn't know that yet. All Lark knew was that the chest was ash, the ship was sinking, and the woman who'd done it to him was standing three meters away, dripping seawater, looking at him with an expression of perfect, terrible calm.

"Where is it?" he said. All the courtesy gone now, stripped off like paint in a storm. "Where are the documents, Gideon?"

Gideon looked past him, at the dark horizon, at the rain coming in off the sea, and Okafor saw her weigh something—saw her decide, in the space of a breath, how much truth a cornered man could be trusted with.

"Somewhere you'll never find it," she said. "Unless you let us go."

Behind Lark, a marine raised his rifle, and the floodlights swung, and the night split open.
