## 27. Sixth Tide: The Choice

They took her boots.

That was the detail Gideon kept returning to as she sat in the Perpetual Dawn's wardroom with her bare feet flat on a deck so clean it hummed. Not her hands—they'd left her those, cuffed in front of her like an afterthought, like courtesy. Not her coat, though it still held Sela's pencil stub and half a biscuit gone soft with spray. Just her boots. Someone had decided a prisoner shouldn't run, and running began with boots, and so they'd taken them, and now she sat with her toes curling against polished teak while somewhere below her the engines turned over at a patient idle, and somewhere behind her the Patient Answer rode at tow like a dog on a short leash, and somewhere ahead of her—she could feel it the way you feel weather coming in your teeth—the sixth tide was turning without her.

The wardroom smelled of coffee and gun oil. A long table ran its length under a lamp that swung gently with the swell, throwing shadows that leaned and righted themselves, leaned and righted, like men deciding whether to stand up or sit back down. On the far wall hung a chart of the coast, and someone had marked the flagship's position with a pin and a neat circle of red ink, and Gideon looked at that circle for a long time because looking anywhere else meant looking at herself reflected in the dark glass of the portholes—a woman in salt-stiffened clothes, hair dried crooked from the dive, thirty-one years old and worth exactly as much as what she knew.

Which was everything. That was the problem. That was the whole terrible arithmetic of it.

She had come up from the wreck with the strongbox strapped to her chest like a child, and Okafor had hauled her over the Patient Answer's rail, and for eleven minutes—she would count them later, she would count them every night for the rest of her life—she had been the richest woman on the water. Eleven minutes to hold Meridia's charter against her ribs and feel the paper breathe, feel the seal wax crackle faintly when the boat rolled, feel two hundred years of a drowned nation pressing up through oilcloth into her palms. Eleven minutes of Okafor staring at her and Sela already reaching for the manifest pages and Tomas standing apart with his face doing something complicated and ruined.

Then the horizon grew a gray shape, and the shape grew guns, and the eleventh minute ended.

They hadn't found the documents. That was the one card she still held, and she turned it over in her mind the way a gambler turns a chip he isn't sure is real. The boarding party had torn the trawler apart—mattresses slashed, bilge pumped through screens, Okafor's tool chest emptied onto the deck plates—and Lark had opened the lead-lined chest itself with the reverence of a man opening a reliquary, and inside he had found decoys. Blank paper aged with tea. Wax seals Sela had cast from soap molds. Enough to pass a searchlight inspection, not enough to pass a careful reading. Lark had read them carefully. He had stood there in the rain with the decoy charter in his gloved hands and looked at Gideon across ten feet of wet deck, and something had passed between them that wasn't anger and wasn't surprise and was maybe, horribly, respect.

*Where are they?* he'd asked, quietly, under the noise of his own marines.

*Deep,* she'd said. *Deeper than your divers.*

And now here she was, bootless in the wardroom, waiting for the man who collected debts to collect this one.

The door opened at the turn of the hour. Lark came in carrying two cups of coffee, which was such a perfectly Lark thing to do that Gideon almost laughed. He set one in front of her, unlocked her cuffs with a small brass key he kept on the same ring as his watch fob, and sat down across the table with his own cup cradled in both hands like a man warming himself at a fire only he could see.

"You're not going to drink that," he said. "You think it's drugged."

"I think it's drugged."

"It's not drugged. It's terrible. Ship's cook boils it twice." He drank his own to prove the point, and grimaced honestly, and Gideon thought: *he's nervous.* Mr. Lark, who had smiled at her across three years of installments and threats, whose cheerfulness was a weapon forged so fine she'd never once felt the edge until it was already in—Mr. Lark was nervous, and knowing it made her more afraid than any amount of calm would have.

"Where are my people?"

"Okafor and the cartographer are locked aft. Your brother is—" Lark paused, chose his word with visible care, "—being spoken with."

"Meaning they're squeezing him."

"Meaning they're *listening* to him. There's a difference. The first is ugly. The second is worse." He set down his cup. "Gideon. I'm going to talk to you plainly, which I've never done, and you're going to understand why I never did it, because plainness is a luxury of people who aren't paid to be pleasant."

"Talk."

"The tide turns in forty minutes. At slack water we make for Port Ceren, full steam, and I deliver you and whatever you know to the Syndicate's factor there. From Ceren you go inland. You will not see the coast again." He said it gently, the way you tell someone a terminal diagnosis. "Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless the documents surface." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, aligning the handle with the table's edge, a fussiness she'd never noticed in him before. "Here's what I know that you don't, because I read the old files when I took over your account—yes, you were a file, I'm sorry, everyone is a file. Augustin Vane has spent sixty years making sure no paper proving what Meridia was ever reached daylight. His grandfather didn't just buy the drowned land. He bought the *story*. Every archive, every registry, every widow's deed. When Vane says Meridia never legally existed, every court on the coast agrees, because the courts' charters descend from the same signature. If your strongbox says otherwise—if it's real, if it's complete—then it isn't treasure, Gideon. It's a bomb with a two-hundred-year fuse, and Vane knows exactly what harbor it's sitting in."

"And so he erases me."

"He doesn't negotiate," Lark said. "I want you to hear that from me and not from the factor at Ceren, who'll offer you things. Vane *erases*. People who cross him don't lose. They become people who never were. There are crews on this coast who can't remember why they stopped sailing with a certain captain, because the remembering was purchased out from under them. It's not violence, mostly. It's bookkeeping." He smiled then, briefly, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen since her mother's photograph. "I'm very good at bookkeeping. It's why they keep me."

Gideon wrapped her hands around the terrible coffee just to hold something warm. Through the porthole, the sea lay flat and pewter-colored, waiting for the tide to decide what it wanted to be. Six tides. She had counted them out loud in Sela's map room like a woman counting steps down into a cellar, and now the last one was turning, and she was on the wrong side of it, and the paper that mattered was either sunk beyond salvage where she'd sworn she'd put it—or it wasn't, because plans made in desperation have a way of being wrong in the details that matter most.

"Lark," she said. "Hypothetically. If the documents existed. If they could be produced. What's the play? Sell them to Vane and walk away rich?"

"No."

"That fast?"

"That fast." He leaned forward, and for the first time in three years of smiling at her across tables, he let his face go tired. "You're thinking of it as a transaction because I taught you to think of everything as a transaction—that's what debt does, it makes merchants of us all. But listen to me as a man who has been on the inside of this machine for twenty-two years. If Vane believes the documents exist, nothing you sell him buys safety. Gold changes hands; the fear doesn't. He would pay you, and feed you, and thank you, and within the year you'd drown in a flooded river with your boots on, and the file would close, and I would be assigned to collect from someone else. The only currency Vane honors is absence. The documents have to stop existing—in every way he can verify—or you do."

The lamp swung. The shadows leaned and righted themselves.

"But," Gideon said slowly, working it through, "if they stop existing in a way he *can't* verify—if they're sunk where no diver reaches, but not provably destroyed—then he spends the rest of his life wondering."

Lark looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she had never seen him do. He laughed—not his pleasant collecting laugh but a short, startled bark, quickly smothered, like a man coughing in church.

"Yes," he said. "Exactly that. Do you know how many nights I've lain awake wishing someone would figure that out?" He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger. "A threat that lives forever underwater is worth more than a fortune paid once. Vane's whole empire rests on the story that Meridia died natural and clean. As long as that charter might surface—as long as some dredge crew, some storm, some diver with a good lung capacity might bring it up on any given morning—he rules on sufferance. He knows it. It's why the Perpetual Dawn was built. It's why I'm on it. He sent us not to retrieve the documents, Gideon. Retrieval is impossible and he knows that too. He sent us to make sure *you believe* retrieval is impossible, and to take you apart until you tell him where they are, and then to sink that place so deep even you couldn't swear to it anymore."

"So I'm already dead either way."

"You're already dead either way *if you stay in his ledger*." Lark said it quietly, and the quiet was worse than shouting. "But ledgers can be closed. That's the choice in front of you, and I'd advise you to actually look at it instead of flinching past it. Option one: you tell me where the papers are, or convincingly pretend to, and spend the rest of your life inland, watched, alive the way a museum piece is alive. Option two: the papers go down for good—truly down, beyond doubt, beyond hope, beyond *value*—and you become, in Vane's arithmetic, worthless. Not harmless. Worthless. Nobody guards a ruin nobody can profit from." He spread his hands. "Freedom priced at the cost of the truth. That's the market rate. I didn't set it."

Gideon stared into the coffee. In the dark window her reflection stared back, and behind her reflection, faint as breath on glass, she could almost see another one—her mother at twenty-nine, standing in some other room with some other offer on the table, choosing. Elena Quintana had chosen the sea over the safe life, and the sea had taken her, and Gideon had spent fifteen years calling that a tragedy. Sitting in the warm light of the enemy's wardroom, she felt the word shift under her. Maybe it hadn't been a tragedy. Maybe it had been a price, paid in advance, for a daughter who would one day sit in exactly this chair and need to know the choice had been survivable.

"What would you do?" she asked. "If it were you."

Lark was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Outside, the light was changing; the pewter sea had begun to move, the slack water ending, the sixth tide gathering itself. Somewhere below, the engines changed their note.

"I joined the Syndicate at nineteen," he said, "because they were the only ones paying fair wages and I had a mother with a bad heart and a landlord with no patience. I told myself I'd get out. Everyone tells themselves that. Then the wages got better, and the work got easier, and the smiling became a skill instead of a lie, and one day you look up and you're fifty-one years old and you're the man other people's nightmares send letters to." He turned his cup again, aligning the handle, aligning it again. "What would I do. I would sink them. All the way down. And I would keep one copy of one page hidden somewhere no one would ever think to look—not to sell, not to bargain with, just so that on the worst night of my life I could take it out and know the truth was still *somewhere*, that Vane's empire was still balanced on a floor that could give way. Because that's the thing about the truth, Gideon. Sold, it's paper. Spent, it's paper. But *kept*—kept, it's a keel. Everything else floats on it or it doesn't float at all."

He stood, buttoning his jacket, becoming Mr. Lark again, the collector, the smile sliding back into place like a mask settling onto a face that had grown to fit it.

"The factor at Ceren will be aboard by morning. Whatever you decide, decide before then." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and spoke without turning around. "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth anything, I checked—I'd choose the keel."

The door shut. The lock didn't click; they weren't afraid of her anymore, and that frightened her more than locks.

Gideon sat alone in the swinging lamplight with her bare feet on the humming deck and did the arithmetic one last time. Freedom, wealth, safety—all of it priced against a dead nation's name, against Okafor's steady hands and Sela's burned archive and Tomas's ruined, loyal, cowardly heart, against a mother who had stood in a cabin like this one and said no and paid for it in a way Gideon was only now beginning to count. The tide was turning. She could feel it in the deck's new lean, the ship answering water that had made up its mind.

She picked up the coffee and drank it. It was terrible. She drank all of it.

Then she crossed to the porthole and looked south, toward the deep water past the shoals, toward the place where—if the plan had held, if the currents had obeyed her, if luck had finally chosen a side—a lead-lined chest was settling into the cold dark with a nation's soul folded inside it, waiting for no one.

And she began, quietly, to work out how to get a message to a woman named Ilsa Reyen before morning.
