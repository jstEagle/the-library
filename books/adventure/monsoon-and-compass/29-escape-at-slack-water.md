## 29. Escape at Slack Water

The tide turned at 3:47 in the morning, and for eleven minutes the sea forgot which way it was going.

Gideon had been counting toward that moment for two days. She'd counted it in her head while marines walked her past the gun mounts of the *Perpetual Dawn*, counted it while Lark poured her tea in a cabin too clean for a warship, counted it now as she stood at the rail under the eyes of a bored sentry and watched black water slide beneath the hull without direction, without pull, without opinion. Slack water. The sea holding its breath.

Eleven minutes. She'd calculated it six times, and the last three calculations had agreed with each other, which was more than she could say for anything else in her life lately.

"You're smiling," Lark said beside her. He had a way of appearing at her elbow like a bill come due. "I've learned to worry when you smile."

"I'm admiring your ship."

"It's not my ship. I'm admiring it too, if that helps. It's the finest thing I've ever been allowed to stand on." He said it without irony, which was somehow worse. "Miss Quintana. Gideon. May I call you Gideon? I feel we've moved past surnames, you and I."

"You can call me whatever gets me back to my boat."

"Your boat is a burned hulk with a hole in her engine room," Lark said gently. "Your cartographer is in my sickbay with shrapnel in her shoulder. Your half-brother is in a cell he asked for, because he's smarter than the rest of you. And the documents you risked all of this for are—" he spread his hands, apologetic, theatrical, "—wherever they are. Which is nowhere useful, I suspect. You sank them, didn't you? Before we boarded. That's why the chest was full of ballast and old chart paper."

Gideon said nothing. Out on the water, the flood current was dying by inches. She could feel it the way other people felt weather coming in their teeth.

"I'm not angry about it," Lark went on. "I want you to understand that. Frankly, I'm impressed. But Mr. Vane's patience has a shape, and you've reached the bottom of it. In the morning there will be a decision made about you and your people, and it won't be made by me, and I won't be able to soften it." He looked out at the water too, following her gaze as if he might see what she saw. "Is there something out there?"

"Tides," Gideon said. "I like to watch them turn."

* * *

They put her in a cabin with a bolted hatch and a porthole too narrow to fit a cat through, and that was fine, because Gideon hadn't needed the porthole. She'd needed the hours, and the hours were hers now, and she spent them doing what she did best: chasing a storm front, except this one was made of men and steel and it was anchored four miles off the Shoals of Names, fat and confident and pointed the wrong way.

Because here was the thing about the *Perpetual Dawn*, the thing its builders had never learned and its crew had never needed to learn: she was a harbor ship. A showpiece. Twin screws, armored flanks, guns enough to make any salvage fleet in the coastal waters wet themselves—and a draft that ran fourteen feet to the waterline, and a hull seam below the engine room where two plates met along a weld that Sela had spotted through the flooded lens of the Patient Answer's periscope glass and memorized the way she memorized everything, before the shrapnel took her shoulder and the fire took their ship.

*"There,"* Sela had said, weeks ago, when they'd first been run down alongside and Gideon had been dragged aboard this same deck. She'd been bleeding and gray-faced and her eyes had gone straight to the waterline, because Sela's eyes always went to maps and seams and the grammar of how things held together. *"That plate join. It's been patched—see the discoloration? Rushed work. A seam like that holds fine in harbor water. Under load, in a current, flexing—"*

She hadn't finished. She hadn't needed to.

And here was the second thing: slack water came twice a day, and between the tides the great ship's mooring anchors took her whole strain, and the current that normally scoured past that patched seam went still—and then, as the tide began to run again, it came back *hard*, funneling through the shoal channels the way it always funneled, and it hit the *Perpetual Dawn*'s downstream flank like a fist. Fourteen feet of draft meant fourteen feet of hull below the waterline, and somewhere down there was a weld that had never been tested against the Shoals of Names at spring tide.

Gideon lay in her bunk in the dark and did the arithmetic one more time. Tide table from memory—the real one, the Meridian cipher, which lived in her head now the way a hymn lives in a believer's. Slack at 3:47. Current resuming at 3:52, building to strength by 4:20. And Okafor, somewhere below decks in the engine spaces, where the Syndicate had put him to work because a man who could keep a dying trawler alive was worth more than a man in a cell—

Okafor, who had spent thirty years listening to hulls talk.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had been built out of nothing over three days of glances and borrowed moments, and it had exactly four moving parts, and every one of them was a person.

Part one was Tomas, in his cell, doing the cowardly arithmetic that had gotten him here—except that this time Gideon had done the arithmetic for him and slipped him the answer through the marine who brought his meals, a boy of nineteen who took bribes the way other boys took candy. *Tell them the documents are in the wreck's strongbox still. Tell them you heard Gideon say it. Tell them the dive team needs to go back down.* A lie to buy the gunboat's dive crew a morning of rigging gear instead of standing watches. A lie to fill their hands.

Part two was Sela, in sickbay, febrile and one-armed and utterly undiminished, who had spent her convalescence charming the assistant surgeon into letting her "organize the charts"—the *Perpetual Dawn*'s charts, the real ones, the ones with the shoal soundings and the hull diagrams—and had committed to memory everything she needed and left everything else exactly where a searching eye would find it undisturbed.

Part three was Okafor, whose job was the simplest and the hardest: get himself assigned to the night engineering watch, put his ear against that patched seam, and listen. If the weld sang—if it so much as whispered—he was to open the sea suction valves on the lower deck lines and walk away. Not jam them. Not sabotage them. Just leave them open, the way a tired man leaves a tap dripping, and let the sea do what the sea does when you invite it in at the exact moment the current turns and puts its shoulder against a bad weld.

And part four was Gideon herself, and her job was the oldest job in her family: read the water, pick the minute, and be ready to move when the ship under her feet started to die.

She'd told them all one thing before they'd scattered to their separate captivities, whispered across the rail of the launch that brought them aboard: *"Slack water. Third night. When the pumps start, everyone runs for the launches on the starboard side, away from the list. Don't be brave. Be fast."*

Okafor had nodded once, slow, the way he did. Sela had said, *"The seam is at frame ninety-one, port side, below the waterline,"* because Sela couldn't help being precise even while bleeding. And Tomas—who had betrayed them, who had sold their position with shaking hands because they held his daughter's debts, who had voted himself into a cell rather than look at them—Tomas had said, *"Why would you trust me again?"*

And Gideon had said, *"Because you did the math wrong the first time, and you know it now. People who've done the math wrong once check it twice forever after."*

* * *

At 3:31 in the morning, Gideon stood at her porthole and watched the water go glass-still, and her heart went still with it.

Slack water. The pause between breaths.

Somewhere below her, four decks down, a man with salt-cracked hands was standing in an engine room that smelled of hot metal and diesel, and he was pressing his palm flat against cold plating near frame ninety-one, and he was listening with the whole of his body the way only Okafor could listen. She couldn't see him. She couldn't signal him. There was nothing left to do but trust, and trust, Gideon had learned, was just faith with better bookkeeping.

At 3:44, the sentry outside her hatch yawned.

At 3:49, the water outside her porthole began to move—not drifting anymore but *drawing*, sliding aft in long silver ribbons as the ebb gathered itself, and Gideon felt her stomach tighten, because that was the fist closing. That was the current finding the ship's flank and leaning on it.

At 3:53, she heard it.

Not an explosion. Nothing so honest. A sound like a giant knuckle cracking, deep down in the bones of the ship, followed by a hiss that no one who has ever heard seawater entering a hull ever forgets. Then a bell. Then another bell. Then boots, many boots, hammering the deck above her head, and a voice on the ship's speakers crackling into life with the particular calm panic of a watch officer reading from a card he'd hoped never to read:

"All hands. All hands. Flooding, lower engine room, port side. Damage control parties to frame nine-zero."

Frame ninety-one, thought Gideon, and smiled in the dark, and said softly, to a dead woman and a drowned nation and a trawler at the bottom of the world: *Thank you, Sela.*

* * *

The ship began to list within ten minutes, and the list was the door.

Gideon had known it would be. A warship heels, her internal order dies, and in the dark and the tilt and the screaming alarms, nobody counts prisoners. She hammered on her hatch and screamed about water coming under her door until a terrified stoker wrenched it open to check, and she went past him into the corridor like smoke finding a draft.

The corridors were rivers of running men. Damage control parties shoved past her carrying patching gear; officers shouted questions no one answered; somewhere far below, the pumps had started their laboring roar, and Gideon knew—she'd built her whole plan on knowing—that they were losing. You don't pump out a hull that's opened along a seam at spring tide in the Shoals of Names. You pump to buy time, and time was the only cargo she'd ever cared about.

She found Sela in the sickbay corridor, one-armed, barefoot, having simply walked out during the chaos with a stolen jacket over her bandages, her eyes ferocious behind the pallor. Neither of them said anything. Sela fell in beside her and matched her stride, and that was the whole conversation.

They found Okafor at the starboard rail, soaked to the waist, grinning like a man at a funeral he'd enjoyed. "Sea suction valves," he said, breathing hard. "Left 'em wide open. Walked right out. Fella on watch thought I was going for coffee." His grin faded into something else—something older and warmer. "Ship talks loud when she's drowning. Same as anybody."

"The launches," Gideon said.

"Starboard side, high side, away from the list." Okafor jerked his chin. "Already got eyes on one. Davit's manned by a kid who's about to have a very bad night."

They went down the tilting deck together, three shadows among fifty panicked ones, and the *Perpetual Dawn* groaned around them like a leviathan with a spear in its belly, and Gideon felt—walking away from the Syndicate's finest gunboat as she settled by the head toward the graveyard of Meridia's fleet—a joy so fierce and clean it frightened her. Not firepower. Not gold. Not luck.

A tide table. A weld. An open valve. Knowledge, in the end, was the only weapon that had never once failed her, and she had finally aimed it herself.

They got the launch's falls cast off with Okafor's knife and a lie about orders, and the little boat dropped the last few feet to the water as the list reached ten degrees, and then they were pulling away into the dark, oars muffled, engines cold until they were clear, while behind them the great ship's searchlights swung wild across the water like the arms of something blind and enormous, grasping at fog.

They were a mile out, running silent on the ebb, when Sela spoke for the first time since the sickbay.

"Gideon." Her voice was thin and steady. "The charts I memorized. Vane's survey fleet soundings for the deep-water channels north of the Shoals." She paused. "There's a cove. Unmarked on any chart but his. Fuel cache, tender berth, a supply schooner on permanent station."

Gideon turned in the stern sheets. "You're telling me the Syndicate owns a hidden harbor."

"I'm telling you," Sela said, "that I know exactly where it is. And I know what's kept there besides fuel." Her eyes caught the faint starlight. "Records. Vane keeps his own records, Gideon. Even empires need accountants."

Okafor pulled his oar through the black water, unhurried, and looked at Gideon across the thwart.

"So," he said. "Do we run? Or do we go take a look?"

Behind them, the *Perpetual Dawn*'s distress rockets went up, red stars bursting over the Shoals of Names, lighting the graves of a drowned kingdom—and Gideon Quintana, fugitive, debtor, daughter of Elena, found she already knew the answer.
