## 32. Fire on the Water

Okafor had carried a lot of things in his life—crates of smuggled rum, drowned men, a debt that wasn't his—but he had never carried anything as heavy as hope at the wrong moment.

The plan was simple, which was how Okafor knew it would go wrong. Simple plans assumed the world cooperated. This one assumed that Gideon could walk out of Augustin Vane's cabin with a leather satchel full of a dead nation's sovereignty papers while Lark's marines were busy chasing smoke on the lower decks, and that Sela and Tomas would have the *Patient Answer* idling in the shadow of the Syndicate flagship's hull, engines low, lights dark, like a stray dog waiting under a banquet table.

"Simple," Gideon had said, checking the seal on her dive knife. "We're not fighting anyone. We're just leaving."

Okafor had been on enough boats to know that *just leaving* was the phrase people said right before they started swimming.

Now he crouched at the trawler's rail in the black hour before dawn, watching the *Perpetual Dawn* loom above them like a cliff that had learned to float. Her running lights burned steady and arrogant. Somewhere inside her, flooding water was chewing through the compartments Sela had picked out on the charts—the hull seam below the waterline, the one the Syndicate's own shipwrights had patched cheap because nobody ever audited a gunboat's plumbing. The slack tide window was closing. In forty minutes the current would turn and every advantage they'd bought with arithmetic would drain out of the harbor like bathwater.

Tomas lay flat beside him at the rail, breath fogging. "She's late."

"She's not late," Okafor said. "The tide isn't done yet."

"That's not what I meant."

Okafor knew what he meant. He'd watched Tomas do the cowardly arithmetic once already, and he'd voted—against his own better judgment—to keep the man aboard, to use him, to trust him exactly as far as a man could be thrown. But tonight Tomas had earned something back. It was Tomas who had walked into the Syndicate flagship's engine room with a wrench and a maintenance uniform and opened the sea cocks. It was Tomas who had come back out past two guards who thought he was one of them. Whatever else he was, he was here, in the dark, waiting for his half-sister the way family waits: badly, and anyway.

"There." Sela's voice, low, from the wheelhouse hatch. A shape detaching from the gunboat's stern ladder—a rope, a body coming down it hand over hand, then dropping the last few feet into black water.

Okafor threw the line. The shape caught it on the second pass. They hauled, and Gideon came up over the side streaming seawater, satchel strapped across her chest, eyes wild and bright as a storm front.

"Go," she gasped. "Go now."

Sela already had the wheel over. The old diesel coughed twice, caught, and the *Patient Answer* leaned away from the Syndicate's flank like a thief slipping out of a party.

For ninety seconds, it worked.

Okafor let himself believe it for those ninety seconds. He wrapped a blanket around Gideon's shoulders and she was laughing—actually laughing, wet hair plastered to her skull, holding the satchel like a newborn. "It's all there," she said. "Charter, ledgers, everything. Vane's whole empire is paper, Okafor. Paper I'm holding."

"Then hold it quieter," he said, but he was grinning too. He couldn't help it. Forty thousand marks, three salvage fleets, a gunboat, a syndicate—and she'd walked out through the front door while their own ship drank the harbor.

That was when the flare went up.

It rose off the *Perpetual Dawn*'s aft deck, red and stuttering, and turned the whole anchorage the color of an open wound. Okafor heard the alarm before he understood it—a bell, then voices, then the flat crackle of a loudhailer finding its voice across the water.

*Unidentified vessel, starboard quarter. All units, unidentified vessel.*

"They found the seam," Sela said. Her knuckles were white on the wheel. "They found it early."

"No." Gideon was staring back at the gunboat, and Okafor saw her face change as she did the math faster than any of them. "They didn't find it. Someone told them where to look."

Tomas went very still.

"You transmitted," Gideon said. Not shouting. That was worse. "After the vote. After we used you. You transmitted again."

"I sent one message." Tomas's voice cracked down the middle. "Before the dive. I told them you'd be at the wreck site—I thought if they took the chest there, they'd never board us, never—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I didn't know about the flooding. I didn't know you were going to sink their ship. Gideon, when that alarm went off, they knew it was us. There was no version after that where they let us go."

Okafor put himself between them, not because he feared violence but because he feared the other thing—the thing where a crew starts eating itself with two hundred meters of open water between them and the guns. "Later," he said. "Both of you. Later is a country we can visit if we live."

The searchlight hit them like a fist.

It swung off the gunboat's tower and pinned the *Patient Answer* in a circle of white so hard Okafor couldn't see the rail ends. And then the sound came—the sound he'd been dreading since the moment they'd slipped past the Harbor Watch, the sound he'd told himself they'd never hear because surely even the Syndicate wouldn't—

The autocannon opened up.

Not aimed at them, not yet. Walking rounds, short bursts, stitching the water fifty meters astern, close enough that the spray came over the transom warm and salt and smelling of cordite. A warning. A courtesy. Mr. Lark's handwriting all over it.

"He wants us alive," Gideon shouted over the engine. "He needs the chest!"

"The chest is empty!" Sela called back. "You have the papers—that's worse! That's worth more than the chest ever was!"

Gideon looked down at the satchel. Okafor watched her understand it in real time, the way you watch someone realize the floor is ice. She wasn't carrying leverage anymore. She was carrying a death sentence with a leather strap.

* * *

They ran for the shoals.

It was Gideon's call and it was the only call: thread the graveyard of wrecks where the *Perpetual Dawn*'s draft couldn't follow, same as they'd done coming in, except now they were doing it in the dark, under searchlight, with a gunboat's forward battery ranging on them and the tide turning against everyone at once.

Sela flew the old trawler like she'd been born at the wheel. Okafor worked the depth sounder and called the bottom to her—twelve meters, nine, seven and falling—and she answered each number with a turn that made the deck tilt and the cargo shift and Okafor's stomach file formal complaints. Behind them the searchlight kept finding them, losing them behind a hulk's silhouette, finding them again, patient as a lighthouse with a grudge.

"We won't make the channel," Sela said. "They'll cut us off at the narrows."

"Then we don't take the narrows." Gideon had the chart spread on the deck box, weighted with her boots, rain starting to spit across it. "There's a cut east of the breakwater. Six meters at high water. We've got maybe five and a half."

"Five and a half under a keel that draws four and a quarter," Okafor said. "That's a meter and a quarter of luck."

"I've run tighter."

"On purpose?"

She didn't answer, which was an answer.

The first shell changed the conversation.

It wasn't the autocannon this time. It was the forward gun—one round, high, deliberate, and it came down thirty meters off their port bow and stood the sea up in a white column that fell across the deck like a collapsed building. The *Patient Answer* rolled hard, hard enough that the port rail went under, hard enough that everything not tied down went across the deck in a single sliding avalanche—including the chart, including the satchel's spare coil of line, including Sela.

Okafor saw it happen the way you see things in disasters: in pieces, out of order. Sela's boots going out from under her. The wheel spinning free. Her hip hitting the binnacle. The sound she made—not a scream, a short punched-out grunt, the sound of a body being told something final. Then Gideon lunging for the wheel, catching it, hauling the bow off the wreck they were suddenly pointed at, and Okafor sliding on his knees across the tilting deck to where Sela lay crumpled against the companionway with her leg bent in a place legs don't bend.

"Sela. Hey. Hey, look at me."

Her face was gray. Her hands were pressed flat against the deck as if she could hold the ship steady by will alone. "The cut," she said. "Heading two-seven-one. Don't—don't lose the cut, I have it memorized, I have all of it—" Blood was pooling under her thigh, black in the searchlight's spill, spreading fast.

"Tomas!" Okafor roared. "Kit! Now!"

Tomas was already moving, tourniquet in his fist, and Okafor registered distantly that a man who'd sold them out twice was now kneeling in blood doing the best work of his life, and that this was what family actually looked like—not the trusting, but the showing up after.

The second shell hit closer.

This one didn't miss. It struck the water twenty meters off the starboard quarter and the shockwave came through the hull like a hammer through a coffin lid. Something below decks let go with a bang Okafor felt in his teeth. The engine note stumbled, caught, stumbled again.

"We're taking water," Gideon called from the wheel. "Slow. But we're taking it."

"And burning," Okafor said, because he could smell it now—diesel and hot metal and that sweet chemical reek of insulation cooking. A thread of smoke was curling up through the deck boards near the engine hatch, thin as incense.

The *Patient Answer*—his boat, their boat, the ugly faithful old trawler that had carried five people with nothing left to lose across six tides and back—was dying under them, and it was dying fast.

* * *

What happened next, Okafor would remember for the rest of his life in fragments, the way you remember a storm: not as a sequence but as a set of images that refused to arrange themselves into sense.

The fire finding the fuel line and going up the engine compartment like it had been waiting years for permission.

Gideon ordering everyone aft, then refusing to go herself until she'd pulled the chart tube and the photographs Sela had hidden in the map rolls—Sela screaming from the deck that the west roll, the *west* roll, the one with the plates sewn into the backing.

The satchel strap tearing.

Okafor saw it clearly, that one image, sharper than all the rest: Gideon at the rail, firelight on her face, the satchel swinging wide as the ship rolled, and the strap—the strap he'd checked, the strap she'd double-knot—parting at the buckle like it had been cut. The satchel went up, hung for a heartbeat against the burning sky, and dropped toward the black water between them and the breakwater.

And Gideon went after it.

No hesitation. No calculation. She stepped onto the rail and dove, fully clothed, into water that held the reflected fire like a second sky, and Okafor was moving before his mind caught up—life ring in one hand, line in the other, shouting to Tomas to take the wheel, *two-seven-one, she gave you the heading, hold two-seven-one*—and diving off the stern into the cold.

The water closed over him like a door.

Underneath, the world was chaos and orange light. Burning fuel had spread a skin of flame across the surface in patches, and Okafor swam under it, blind, lungs burning, following the bubbles of a diver who had gone deeper than sense. He found her at six meters, clawing at the satchel where it had snagged on something—a sunken piling, a piece of the breakwater's bones—and her eyes when they found his in the torch-beam chaos were the calmest he'd ever seen them.

*I have it,* her mouth said.

*Let it go,* his mouth said back.

She shook her head. And Okafor, who had spent a lifetime being the sensible one, the one who counted the marks and minded the fuel and said *we can't afford it*, understood in that frozen blue moment that he was looking at the reason he'd stayed on this madwoman's boat through all of it. Not the treasure. Not the debt. This. A person who would rather drown than let a stolen country stay stolen.

He got his arm around her chest and kicked.

They broke the surface into a world on fire. The *Patient Answer* was ablaze amidships, flames standing off her deck in the wind like banners, and the *Perpetual Dawn* was coming on slow through the smoke with her searchlight sweeping, and somewhere in the middle distance Tomas was holding their crippled, burning, sinking home on a heading through a reef cut with a meter of luck and no cartographer conscious enough to correct him.

Okafor got Gideon to the life ring. Got the line around her. Got his boots against a floating crate and pushed toward the stern where Tomas was leaning way out over the transom with his arm down like a crane.

"Got her—got her—pull!"

Hands. Deck. Wood under his cheek, blessed and smoking. Gideon coughing seawater, satchel still locked in her fist, fingers so frozen around the strap they had to pry them loose one at a time, and when they finally opened it there in the firelight, the charter was soaked but whole, the ink of a dead nation's name bleeding softly into its own history.

Sela was conscious by then, gray-faced, leg splinted with a boathook handle and tape, propped against the wheelhouse while the fire ate the boat around her. She was looking at the flames with an expression Okafor couldn't read until he did: not grief for the boat. Grief for the maps. The archive had burned once already; now the backup was going up in the same night, and all she had left of her life's work was what lived behind her eyes and whatever Sela Voss had managed to hide in places only Sela Voss would think of.

"The west roll," she whispered. "Tell me someone got the west roll."

Gideon held it up. Water-stained, scorched at one corner, intact.

Sela closed her eyes. "Then burn it all," she said. "Burn the boat. I don't care. I have what matters."

But Okafor cared. He stood in the waist of the *Patient Answer* as the fire climbed the mast and the pumps clanked uselessly and the gunboat's searchlight found them one more time, and he put his palm flat against the deck planks he'd caulked with his own hands eleven years ago, and he grieved the way sailors grieve: quickly, privately, and completely.

"Gideon," he said. "She's done. We jump or we burn."

"Not yet." She was staring past him, at the *Perpetual Dawn*, closing at a quarter mile, and Okafor watched her do the last arithmetic of the night. "Lark wants me alive. Lark wants these papers destroyed. Those are two different orders, and only one man aboard that ship has to choose between them."

"You're betting on Lark?"

"I'm betting on the man who tore up a ledger once before." She slung the satchel across her back, knotted the strap triple this time, and looked at each of them in turn—Okafor, splinted Sela, ash-streaked Tomas, the whole battered impossible family of them. "Get to the raft. Take the west roll. If I'm not behind you by slack water—"

"Don't say it," Okafor said.

"—then sail the cut and don't look back."

The *Patient Answer* groaned, settled, and began to go, and Okafor took Sela in his arms and stepped off the stern of the only home he'd had in a decade, into water lit orange as the inside of a furnace, as behind them the fire reached the fuel tanks and the night split open with a sound like the sea itself objecting.

The raft hit the water. The blast wave shoved it sideways. And through the smoke and falling embers, Okafor saw the gunboat's launch putting out toward them, and standing in its bow, coat immaculate, face unreadable, lantern raised like a man greeting guests instead of prisoners—

Mr. Lark.

He was looking not at the raft, not at the burning wreck, but at Gideon—at the satchel on her back—and Okafor saw him mouth one word across the water, a word with no sound in it at all:

*Choose.*
