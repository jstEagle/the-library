## 34. Sink the Truth

The water off the Verren Shelf was the color of old pewter, and it went down farther than anything had a right to go.

Gideon stood at the rail of the *Perpetual Dawn*—still the Syndicate's ship, still crewed by Syndicate marines who watched her with the flat patience of men waiting for orders that hadn't come yet—and looked at the depth sounder's readout like it was an old friend. Four hundred fathoms. Then four hundred and ten. The shelf edge fell away beneath them into a darkness no lamp had ever touched, a darkness where lead-lined chests could fall forever and never be found by anyone who didn't already know to look.

Lark stood beside her. He'd been standing beside her for most of an hour, hands folded behind his back, saying nothing, which from Lark was its own kind of language.

"You've been staring at that gauge like it owes you money," he said finally.

"Everything owes me money," Gideon said. "That's rather the theme of my life."

"And you're about to settle your accounts."

She didn't answer. Below her feet, in the gunboat's hold, under the eyes of two bored marines and one very bored bosun, sat the salvage chest with Meridia's sun-and-wave seal stamped into its lid—the chest she'd pulled out of a flooded river half a lifetime of tides ago, back when her biggest problem was forty thousand marks and a collector who smiled too easily. Back when running was still free.

Inside that chest, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in wax, dry as the day a dying admiral's clerk had packed it, lay the original charter of the sovereign nation of Meridia. And beneath it, the ledgers. Augustin Vane's grandfather's handwriting marching across decades of entries: payments to Meridian councilors, bribes logged as "harbor improvements," the purchase price of a country's drowning entered in neat columns like so much rope and canvas. Proof that the empire whose gunboat she stood on had not been built. It had been *bought*—with a nation as the currency.

Sela's photographs were already safe. Three exposures, hidden in the margin annotations of a chart of the Corvus Shoals, tucked into Sela's map case where no Syndicate searcher would ever think to look, because no one on earth searched Sela's charts—they were legendarily, weaponizingly boring. Gideon had held the negatives up to the grey morning light once, just once, before wrapping them again. Vane's signature. The seal of the last admiral, broken. The word *sovereignty* in a dead language, rendered in ink that had outlived the country it named.

That was enough. That would have to be enough.

"Mr. Lark," she said. "How deep is it here?"

He glanced at the sounder. "Four hundred twelve fathoms at this position."

"And what's the deepest salvage operation the Syndicate has ever run?"

Lark was quiet for a moment. She watched him decide whether to answer honestly, and then watched him decide he'd already passed the point where dishonesty cost him anything. "Three hundred and six," he said. "Off the Caden Trench. It took eleven months and we lost four divers. The board still talks about it the way other people talk about wars."

"So nothing comes back from here."

"Nothing comes back from here." He said it gently, the way he said everything, and she understood that he knew exactly what she was asking him, and exactly why, and that some part of him—the part that had torn up her debt ledger three days ago in a cabin that smelled of brass and fear—was glad to be asked.

* * *

They'd let her keep the chest. That was the strange arithmetic of the last two days.

After the escape at slack water—after the *Patient Answer*'s crew had flooded the gunboat's lower decks through a hull seam Sela had spotted from a hundred yards and Okafor had opened with a pry bar and a prayer—there had been chaos, recapture, and then, incredibly, negotiation. Gideon in the captain's cabin, soaked to the ribs, a marine's boot print fading on her shoulder, facing down a man named Hessler who commanded the *Perpetual Dawn* and who wanted very badly for this whole affair to end without another incident report.

The terms had come together the way bad weather does: gradually, then all at once. The crew would live. The trawler—what was left of her, burned to the waterline in the rescue gone wrong—would be written off as lost with all hands' cooperation. And the chest, the documents, the entire Meridian question would be surrendered intact to the Syndicate, because Hessler did not have the authority to destroy them and did not have the imagination to fake their destruction.

Only Lark had the imagination. Only Lark had the authority he'd quietly granted himself, the small rebellion he'd confessed to her in the dark of the hold: *I reported the documents destroyed and worthless. I tore up your ledger. I am now a man with one secret, Miss Quintana, and I would very much like to keep it.*

So the chest was hers to carry. And everyone aboard assumed she was carrying it to Vane.

Vane himself had summoned her—of course he had; a man like Augustin Vane did not send proxies for the interesting parts—and she had stood in his flagship's cabin among the mahogany and the maps of everything he owned, and he had offered her everything: the debt erased, a fleet, a name that opened harbors. All she had to do was hand over the charter. He'd made the offer sincerely, which was the worst part. He'd made it the way a man offers a chair.

And then he'd told her about her mother. Elena Quintana, standing in that same cabin twenty years ago, refusing the same offer, paying for it with her name, her livelihood, and eventually—he'd implied it with the delicacy of a man laying down a winning card—with her life. *Your mother chose poverty and disappearance over partnership,* Vane had said, and there had been something almost like respect in it, or whatever passes for respect in a man who buys countries. *I've always wondered if she regretted it.*

Gideon had told him she'd ask, if she ever found her mother's grave.

Now, standing at the rail with four hundred fathoms of nothing below her boots, she realized she'd known her answer since before Vane finished speaking. Maybe since the river. Maybe since the first time Mr. Lark smiled at her across a dockside table and she'd felt the specific dread of a woman realizing that charm was just menace wearing a good coat.

Her whole life had been shaped by other people's confidence. The Syndicate's confidence that debt made obedience. Vane's confidence that everything had a price, because everything always had, because no one had ever handed anything back. Her mother's confidence—the only confidence Gideon had ever inherited clean—that some things, once given away, could not be bought back, and therefore must never be given.

Elena had refused. Elena had paid. And the payment hadn't saved anything, hadn't changed anything, hadn't even bought her daughter a childhood with a mother in it.

But it had bought this. It had bought a chest in a river, timed like a letter posted twenty years late. It had bought a choice, offered to a daughter who'd never known she was inheriting one.

Gideon Quintana had spent her whole life running—from collectors, from creditors, from the memory of a woman who vanished. Running had a cost, and she'd paid it in installments for twenty years, and she had told herself it was free because the bills came due so slowly.

No more. You couldn't sink a thing and keep running. Sinking it meant stopping. Stopping meant standing still in open water with the Syndicate's whole weight bearing down on you and nothing left to trade.

It was the cheapest thing she'd ever done, and the most expensive, and both at once, and she found—to her genuine surprise—that she was smiling.

* * *

They did it at slack water, because of course they did. Gideon insisted, and Hessler, wanting the business concluded, indulged her. Slack tide at the Verren Shelf came at 16:40 by the almanac, and Sela's tables—which Gideon had memorized along with everything else, because Sela had made her, twice, with the patience of a woman teaching a particularly dense apprentice—put the drift at nearly nothing. A chest dropped at slack water went straight down. No current to carry it toward shallower ground. No chance, ever, of it fetching up somewhere a diver might stumble on it.

Okafor would have laughed at her for the ceremony of it. So she did it without ceremony, mostly.

The chest came up from the hold at 16:20, carried by two marines who handled it with the exaggerated care of men transporting something cursed. Gideon walked beside it. Inside, the charter lay wrapped in its oilcloth, and she had added to it—not much. A page torn from her logbook, written in pencil because pencil survives water better than ink:

*Meridia lives as long as someone remembers it was stolen. This is the remembering. — G.Q., who was told to sell, and didn't.*

And beneath that, the coordinates of Elena's unmarked grave on the cliff above Harrow Point, because a drowned nation deserved to know where its last loyal servant slept.

At 16:38 she stood at the stern rail with the chest in her arms. It weighed maybe sixty pounds. It had weighed more when it held a cipher and her future. Now it held a country, and it weighed almost nothing at all.

"Last chance," Lark murmured beside her. Not an offer. An observation. He was giving her the dignity of pretending she might still choose gold.

"I had my last chance in your office," she said. "You remember. You offered me an extension."

"I do remember." He looked out at the pewter water. "For what it's worth, I'm glad you're difficult."

"It's worth a great deal. It's worth everything I don't have."

She lifted the chest over the rail.

For one absurd moment she thought about keeping it. The thought arrived fully dressed, the way temptation does: she could sell it tomorrow, quietly, to any of a dozen governments or newspapers or enemies of the Syndicate, and buy back her life entire. Freedom, wealth, safety, Tomas's daughter's debts dissolved, a new keel laid by nightfall. All of it, right there, in her arms, in sixty pounds of lead and paper.

And then she thought about what Vane had said in his cabin, and what Ilsa Reyen had said on the deck of the *Corvus*, and what her mother had written in a code only a tide-table could read: *the truth is the only thing they can't survive.* If Gideon sold it, it became merchandise—one more thing with a price, one more entry in one more ledger, purchasable, suppressible, buryable by whoever paid best. Sold, the charter was just leverage, and leverage always ended up in the hands of whoever could afford it, which meant Vane, eventually, because Vane could always afford it.

But sunk—sunk beyond any salvage depth, beyond three hundred and six fathoms and every month of effort and every drowned diver—it became something else entirely. It became a story. A rumor with teeth. Because Sela's photographs existed, and Sela knew how to use them, and somewhere in the world there would always be a person who'd heard that the proof of Meridia's murder had gone into the sea *on purpose*, and who would lie awake wondering what else the Quintana woman knew, and what else she'd hidden, and whether the next chest was already in the next river.

Vane's empire would stand on a lie that could surface anytime. That was better than a knife at his throat. A knife you could parry. A doubt you could only drown in.

Gideon opened her hands.

The chest went over the rail cleanly, no splash to speak of, just a white seam in the pewter water that closed behind it like a mouth. It fell straight down at slack water, exactly as calculated, past the light, past the cold, past the reach of every winch and hook and greedy hand on the coast, down to the dark where drowned nations keep their papers.

Four hundred and twelve fathoms. Nothing comes back from there.

She watched the water until there was nothing to watch, which took about four seconds, and then she kept watching it anyway, because some things deserve more than four seconds.

"Well," said Lark.

"Well," said Gideon.

"He's going to spend the rest of his life wondering if you made copies."

"He is," Gideon agreed. "Isn't it wonderful?"

Lark laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly strangled, but real. She'd known the man three years and it was the first time she'd heard him laugh like something other than a transaction. "You understand what you've done. You haven't beaten him. There'll be no reckoning, no trial, no restoration. Meridia stays drowned. Vane keeps everything."

"No," Gideon said. "He keeps almost everything. And he'll never sleep well again." She turned from the rail. "I spent years thinking beating someone meant taking what they had. My mother taught me better without ever meaning to. Sometimes beating them means making sure they can never be sure. Doubt's the only debt I've ever collected on, Mr. Lark. Consider it paid in full."

* * *

Hessler released them at dusk, as arranged, into a shore boat with fuel for the crossing to Harrow Point and not much else. The marines watched them go with expressions Gideon couldn't read and didn't try to. Somewhere behind them, the *Perpetual Dawn* turned its bow toward the Syndicate's home port, carrying a captain who would file a report saying the documents were recovered and worthless—water-damaged, illegible, destroyed by time—and carrying Mr. Lark, who would sign that report, and who had torn a ledger page in half three days ago and called it, quietly, the first honest act of his career.

The shore boat was crowded and cold and smelled of diesel and salt, and it was, Gideon thought, the finest vessel she had ever stood aboard.

Okafor sat in the stern with his huge hands wrapped around his knees, watching the gunboat shrink. "You know he's going to tear the coast apart looking for those photographs."

"Yes."

"And Sela's going to publish them anyway."

"Yes."

"And then we're going to spend the rest of our lives looking over our shoulders."

Gideon settled beside him, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd stood through storms and boardings and one long terrible night diving a wreck by lamplight. "Probably."

Okafor considered this. He was a man who considered things properly; it was one of the thousand reasons she'd have followed him anywhere, and had. "Good," he said at last. "Be nice to have something worth looking over my shoulder about. Been years since I did."

Tomas said nothing at all. He sat apart, as he had since the betrayal and the confession and the vote that spared him, holding a letter from his daughter that he'd read eleven times. But when the first lights of Harrow Point came up out of the dark ahead, he moved down the boat and sat with the rest of them, close enough to touch, and no one remarked on it, and that was its own kind of absolution.

Sela was asleep against the gunwale with her map case strapped to her body like a child. In it, between the boring charts of the Corvus Shoals, three photographs waited for a newspaper office, a printing press, and a slow tide of truth that no one could sink, because it had never been in the water at all.

Gideon watched Harrow Point grow, and above it the cliff where an unmarked grave faced the open sea, and she thought: *I stopped running today.* Not when she sank the chest—before that. When she'd lied to Lark in chapter one of all this, or when she'd opened the chest against Okafor's advice, or maybe when she'd first chased a storm instead of fleeing one, all those years ago, because the storm was where the salvage was, and the salvage was where the living was.

Stopping, it turned out, cost less than running. She wished someone had told her. She wished her mother could have seen the splash.

The shore boat ran on into the dark, toward the harbor, toward the tavern where Sela was already planning her anonymous letter to the coastal papers, toward whatever came next for five people with no ship, no money, and nothing left to lose—which was, Gideon reflected, the exact inventory they'd started with, and look what they'd done with it.

Behind them, four hundred fathoms down, a nation slept with its papers in order.

Ahead, in the morning, the first edition would hit the stands.

And somewhere out on the water, in a cabin lined with maps of everything he owned, Augustin Vane was about to learn what his grandfather had bought, and what it had actually cost, and that the receipt had just surfaced—photographed, printed, and priced at exactly one impossible thing: the truth, given away free.
