## 38. A New Keel

The shipyard at Colder's Bend smelled of tar and cut pine and the particular sweetness of money that had finally stopped bleeding. Okafor stood at the edge of the empty slipway with his boots in the mud and his hands in his pockets, watching Gideon walk the length of the bare keel timber like a woman pacing out the dimensions of a new life.

It was a good piece of wood. He'd checked it himself three times—oak, dense as a fist, salvaged from a decommissioned pilot boat that had spent forty years arguing with the same coast they meant to argue with. The yard owner had wanted an absurd price for it. Okafor had paid it without flinching, because for the first time in longer than he could remember, the number on the paper hadn't made his chest tighten.

*Hers,* he thought. *Not owed to anyone.*

"You're staring," Sela said beside him. She had her arm in a sling still—the burn along her forearm wrapped in clean linen that she changed herself every morning with the grim efficiency of a woman who refused to be fussed over. Her other hand held a rolled chart, though there was nothing to chart here. Okafor suspected she just felt naked without one.

"I'm counting," he said.

"Counting what?"

"Everything we don't have."

Sela snorted. "That'll take you all morning."

It would have taken him all morning, once. But he'd done the arithmetic last night on the back of a tavern receipt, sitting by the window while Tomas slept off another evening of quiet drinking two rooms away, and the sum had come out different than he expected. Not rich. Nowhere near rich. But not drowning either. There was a difference between having nothing and owing everything, and Okafor had lived long enough on both sides of that line to know which side the sun rose on.

Gideon reached the bow end of the keel timber and turned. She looked thinner than she had before the Perpetual Dawn, before Vane's flagship, before all of it—but there was something settled in her now, something that had been missing since the day Okafor first signed aboard the Patient Answer and watched her scan the horizon like a debtor checking for collectors. She wasn't scanning anymore. Whatever she was looking for, she'd decided to build it instead.

"Okafor." Her voice carried across the slipway. "You going to stand there all day, or are you going to help me lay this thing?"

"It's already laid," he called back. "I laid it Tuesday while you were sleeping off the celebration."

"That was checking. Laying is ceremonial." She patted the oak. "There are rules."

"There are no rules. You invented those rules this morning."

"I invented them *last* night," Sela said mildly, unrolling her chart on a sawhorse as if to prove the point, "and I told her they were traditional. She believed me. That's what happens when you let a cartographer near your folklore."

Okafor laughed—a real laugh, from low down, the kind he hadn't managed since the Patient Answer went up in flames on the water with half its stern gone and all of its history. He crossed the mud to the keel and put his hand flat against the timber where Gideon's hand rested. The wood was cold and damp and smelled like the future.

"So," he said. "What do we call her?"

Gideon looked at him. Then at Sela. Then back down the length of the keel, following it toward the water line, toward the gray harbor beyond, where the tide was coming in over the mudflats the way it always did, indifferent and punctual and free.

"Not yet," she said. "She'll tell us when she's ready."

* * *

They worked through the mornings and drank through the evenings, and the boat began.

Okafor had built things before—patched hulls, replaced planking, kept the Patient Answer alive for six years on stubbornness and scavenged timber—but this was different. This was a body being grown from nothing, frame by frame, and every rib they steamed into place was a decision they got to make fresh. No inherited rot. No patched-over sins of previous owners. When Gideon said the hold would be deep enough for salvage gear, they built it deep. When Sela insisted the chart table go amidships where the roll was kindest, they framed the deck around it. When Tomas showed up with a crate of brass fittings he'd traded three favors to get—"don't ask whose favors"—they polished every one until it shone like something worth keeping.

Tomas was different now. Okafor had watched him carefully in the first weeks, waiting for the old slipperiness to resurface, the fixer's instinct to keep one door open, one lie banked like a fire against winter. It hadn't come. Maybe it would someday—Okafor wasn't naive enough to believe a man remade himself in a season—but Tomas hauled timber beside them with sweat running into his eyes and never once asked what his share was. His daughter had come to visit twice, a sharp-eyed girl of nine who inspected the half-built hull with the gravity of a harbormaster and pronounced it "acceptable, if the paint is blue." They had promised her blue.

On the third week, the yard owner's other tenants started drifting over to watch. Old builders mostly, men and women whose trades were dying as the big yards downcoast ate the small ones alive. One of them, a stooped rigger named Anselm who'd lost two fingers to a winch decades ago, took to standing at the fence every afternoon offering unsolicited opinions.

"Your garboard strakes are too proud," he said one day, apropos of nothing.

"They're fine," Okafor said.

"They'll drag in heavy weather."

"We *want* her to drag in heavy weather. Her captain chases storms for pleasure."

Anselm considered this. "Madwoman, is she?"

"Worse. She's happy."

The old man laughed until he wheezed, and the next morning he climbed the fence and picked up a drawknife, and after that there were four of them, then five, then a loose flotilla of retired shipwrights who materialized each dawn like gulls smelling fish, working for nothing but coffee, stories, and the rare pleasure of building something that wasn't owed to a bank or a syndicate or a grave.

Sela catalogued all of them. Of course she did. By the second week she had a ledger page titled *Colder's Bend Yard — Personnel & Skills*, annotated in her tiny precise hand, and when Okafor teased her about it she said, "Every crew needs a manifest. You taught me that. You wrote one for the Patient Answer the first day I came aboard—I found it in your kit. You listed me under 'assets: maps, nerves.'"

"Nerves?"

"You were assessing whether I'd panic in bad weather."

"And did I conclude—"

"You concluded 'unknown.' Very generous."

He remembered that. He remembered writing it, too—remembered the young cartographer who'd shown up at the dock with rain-soaked charts and a look in her eye like a woman fleeing something, wanting berth space on a trawler captained by a debt-ridden storm chaser because it was the only ship in port desperate enough to say yes. He'd taken her on because Gideon asked, and he'd written *unknown* because he didn't yet know that unknown was how every good thing in his life had arrived.

* * *

The money ran low in the fifth week, the way money does, and they gathered in the tavern on the corner—Gideon, Sela, Tomas, Okafor, and Anselm, who had appointed himself an honorary member of the crew on the grounds that nobody had voted against it—and counted what remained.

"Two hundred and forty marks," Sela said, pushing the coins into a sad little tower. "Plus whatever the photographs earn, if the newspaper ever pays, which newspapers famously do not."

"The story will run wide," Tomas said. "Vane's creditors are falling over each other to leak corroboration. We won't need to sell anything. The truth is doing our fundraising for us."

"The truth is slow," Gideon said. "Boats are fast."

They sat with that. Outside, the harbor lights smeared across wet cobbles, and somewhere down the coast a foghorn moaned like something lonely.

Okafor turned his mug slowly on the table. He had been turning something else over too, for days, and he supposed this was the moment to set it down.

"I have money," he said.

Everyone looked at him.

"Six years on the Patient Answer, and I never spent my shares. Couldn't. Didn't trust the world to leave me anything if I let go of it." He shrugged. "Old habit. There's eight hundred marks in a strongbox under my bunk—under what used to be my bunk, before she burned. Eight hundred and change."

"Okafor." Gideon's voice was careful. "That's your escape fund. That's the money that means you can always walk away."

"I know what it is." He met her eyes. "That's why I'm spending it. A man keeps an escape fund his whole life, all he ever does is practice leaving. I'm tired of practicing." He pushed back his chair. "Tomorrow I'll fetch it. Put it in the pile."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Tomas, quietly: "I've got ninety marks and a favor owed to me by a lumber factor in Vellport. I'll call it in. Oak for the transom."

"My sister left me a ring," Sela said, to her mug. "I never wear it. It's worth maybe three hundred. It should be worth more than that, honestly, she had terrible taste in everything except jewelry."

"Sela—"

"She'd have hated the Syndicate," Sela said, with finality. "She'd have loved the boat. It goes in the pile."

Gideon looked around the table—at the coins, at the people, at the strange arithmetic of it—and Okafor saw her throat work once before she got her voice steady.

"The Patient Answer cost me eleven thousand marks in debt," she said. "This one costs us everything we've got, and it's the cheapest thing I've ever bought."

* * *

They laid the frames through the tail of autumn, and the rains came, and they worked under canvas tarps with mud sucking at their boots, and the skeleton of the new ship rose against the gray sky like the ribs of some patient animal deciding to live again.

Okafor found that his hands remembered everything. How to read the grain of a plank before committing it. How much force a steamed rib could take before it protested. Where to drive a nail so it gripped forever. His father had been a shipwright, back before the debts and the syndicates and the long slow slide; Okafor had spent thirty years using those hands for hauling and patching and holding things together, and only now, knee-deep in shavings with the smell of fresh oak all around him, did he understand what they'd been waiting to do.

One evening Gideon found him alone in the yard, sanding the stem post by lamplight, and stood watching him for a while without speaking.

"You're good at this," she said finally.

"My father's trade. I ran from it young." He blew dust off the curve of the wood. "Ran from a lot of things young. Seems to be a family disease. Yours included."

"Mm." She leaned against the sawhorse. For a while the only sound was the rasp of the paper and the tick of the lamp cooling. Then: "I keep thinking about the chest. The original one. Sitting down there past any depth anyone can reach, holding the whole rotten foundation of the Syndicate, and nobody will ever find it but the sea."

"Good place for it. The sea keeps what it's given."

"Do you regret it? Any of it? We could be rich, Okafor. Set for ten lives."

He set down the sandpaper and looked at her. In the lamplight her face was all planes and shadows, her mother's face, though she didn't know that yet—or maybe she did, now, after the grave, after Ilsa, after everything.

"If we'd sold it," he said, "we'd have a bigger boat. And we'd be the same people, looking over our shoulders, waiting for someone bigger to come take it. I've been that person. I liked it better out here." He tapped the stem post. "Besides. Vane's empire is sitting on top of a lie that could surface any day. Sela hid the photographs well. Someday, some journalist or defector or grieving Meridian descendant is going to start pulling that thread, and when it unravels, I want to be nowhere near the loom. I want to be right here, building a boat that owes nobody anything."

Gideon smiled—small, real, the smile of a woman who had finally stopped running and discovered her legs still worked.

"When do we launch?" she asked.

"Spring. Maybe sooner, if the weather holds and Anselm stops complaining about the strakes."

"He'll complain about the strakes at the christening. He'll complain about them at her funeral, fifty years from now."

"Then she'll have lasted fifty years," Okafor said. "That's more than most of us get."

* * *

Winter closed in, and the work went indoors, into the loft above the yard where they bent wood and argued about rigging and drank Anselm's terrible cider. Tomas went upcoast twice to see his daughter and came back both times lighter, somehow, as if each visit burned off another layer of the old guilt. Sela began drawing the new charts—not of anywhere, but of everywhere, a master atlas of the coast they'd survived, with the Shoals of Names inked in careful detail and a small, precise annotation at the wreck site that read only: *sleeping.* Gideon chased weather on foot, walking the headlands whenever a front came through, watching the storms roll in off the water with an expression Okafor could only call homesick.

And on a bright, hard morning in late winter, with frost on the tarps and their breath hanging in clouds, they stepped the mast.

It took all of them—Okafor and Gideon and Tomas hauling, Sela sighting the vertical against her plumb line, Anselm shouting contradictory instructions from the fence with tremendous authority. The spar rose slow and trembling, caught its step, and stood. Above them, against the pale sky, it looked less like timber than like a spine, like something standing up after a long time lying down.

Nobody cheered. It wasn't that kind of moment. They just stood there in the cold, five people and a half-built ship, breathing steam, while the tide crept up the slipway toward the keel that waited for water the way all of them had once waited for it—as something that might drown them, and might, if they learned it well enough, carry them home.

"Blue paint," Okafor said at last. "We promised the girl blue."

"Blue," Gideon agreed. "And a name by launch day. She'll tell us. I'm sure of it now."

But it was Sela, that evening, sorting her charts by lamplight, who went suddenly still over a page—an old survey chart of the coast, water-stained, salvaged from the Patient Answer's cabin the week after the fire, the only document they'd pulled from the wreck. She stared at it a long time. Then she carried it to the tavern without a word and laid it on the table in front of Gideon, and pointed.

There, in the margin, in faded pencil—in handwriting Okafor recognized even before Gideon's face changed, because he'd seen it on notes and charts and one smuggled letter across six years of sailing with Elena Quintana's daughter—was a single word, written long ago by a dead woman's hand, in the blank space of open water where no ship was marked:

*Answer.*

Gideon touched the letters like they might dissolve. The tavern noise went on around them—glasses, laughter, the ordinary music of the living—but at their table the air had gone very still.

"Well," Okafor said quietly. "She told you."
