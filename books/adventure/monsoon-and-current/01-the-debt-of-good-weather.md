## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

The storm came in off the water like something owed.

Jude Vasquez felt it before she saw it — a pressure change in her back teeth, a taste of iron on the wind, the gulls going quiet all at once the way they did when the sky decided to stop pretending. She stood on the seawall at Grey Harrow with her camera braced against her shoulder and let the first gust hit her full in the chest, and for one clean second she thought: *this is the one. This is the footage that pays.*

Then she did the arithmetic, because she always did the arithmetic, and the second passed.

Three payments. That was what remained. Three monthly installments to the Marrow Fund — four hundred and ten each, plus the rolling interest that grew like kelp in warm water — and then they would take the truck. Not might. Would. She'd read the contract twice, once drunk and once sober, and both times it said the same thing in the same smiling language: *the Fund retains a security interest in all equipment, vehicles, and associated assets.* Associated assets. Her lenses. Her drone. Her weather station bolted to the roof rack, the anemometer she'd calibrated by hand in a motel parking lot outside Vellum Bay. Everything she owned that could be repossessed was everything she needed to earn a living, which was, she understood now, not an accident but a design. The Fund didn't lend money. It lent rope, measured exactly long enough to reach your own neck.

The front rolled over the headland and the light went green-brown, that bruised color sailors used to call *the widow's lamp*. Jude got moving. She'd learned years ago that fear and momentum were nearly indistinguishable in the body — same quick heart, same cold hands — so she had spent her whole life choosing momentum, because at least momentum went somewhere.

She ran the seawall to where the truck sat crooked on its worn shocks, threw her kit in the passenger seat, and pulled out onto the coast road heading north, toward the point where the storm surge would meet the ebb tide and do something ugly and beautiful and filmable. The wipers fought the first fat raindrops and lost. On the dash, taped to the plastic above the speedometer, a receipt fluttered: 410.00, MARROW FUND, THANK YOU FOR YOUR BUSINESS.

*Thank you for your business.* Brannic had said it too, the first time, standing in her driveway with his hands folded in front of him like a man at a funeral he'd personally arranged.

* * *

She caught the surge at Cutter's Point, twenty minutes ahead of the worst of it, and it was good. It was better than good. The sea drew itself up out of its bed like a thing waking angry, and the spray went forty feet against the black rocks, and the light through the storm's torn edge turned the whole wall of water the color of old brass. Jude stood hip-deep in the flooding parking area with her tripod sunk in sand and shot until her fingers stopped working, then kept shooting, because this was the only part of her life that still made sense: the storm did not care about her debt. The storm did not care about anything. It arrived, it spent itself, it left, and nobody sent a collector after it.

Her phone buzzed against her ribs. She ignored it. It buzzed again, and again, patient as dripping water, until finally she checked it with numb hands and found three texts from a number she knew by heart without wanting to.

**BRANNIC:** Beautiful weather we're having.
**BRANNIC:** Saw your truck go north. Hope you're getting something out of it.
**BRANNIC:** We should talk before month's end. Coffee's on me.

Jude looked at the messages for a long time while the rain hammered the screen and the sea tried to climb the cliff behind her. Then she typed back: *Busy working. Will pay on the 1st.*

The reply came in under a minute, which meant he'd been holding the phone.

**BRANNIC:** I know you will. That's why I like you, Jude. You're not like the others. See you soon.

She put the phone away and discovered her hands were shaking, and told herself it was the cold.

* * *

The coffee meeting happened anyway, four days later, because Brannic was the kind of man who treated refusal as a scheduling problem rather than an answer.

He chose the diner at the edge of Grey Harrow, the one with fogged windows and a pie case and nothing else worth describing, and he was already there when she arrived, sitting in the corner booth with two cups poured and his coat folded neatly beside him. He looked like what he pretended to be: a friendly middle-aged man with a soft jaw and reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, someone's uncle, someone's neighbor. He always ordered for her without asking — black coffee, no sugar, which was correct, which was the point. The correctness was the message. *I know you. I know how you take your coffee. I know what you owe down to the cent, and I know where you were last Tuesday.*

"Jude." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "You look tired."

"I've been chasing weather."

"And selling it?"

"Some of it."

"Mm." He stirred his own coffee though there was nothing in it to stir. "See, here's my concern. The network that bought your surge footage — Cutter's Point, the brass-colored wave, very dramatic, very you — paid you eighteen hundred. I know because we see the deposits. And your payment on the first is four hundred and ten, plus the interest accrual, which puts you at four sixty-two and change. So you're going to make it, this month. Barely." He sipped. "But barely isn't a plan, Jude. Barely is just slow drowning with extra steps."

"You called me in to tell me I'm paying on time?"

"I called you in because I like you." He set the cup down and aligned it with the table edge, a small precise gesture she'd seen him perform a hundred times, and understood that the alignment mattered to him the way prayer mattered to other men. "Because you're honest. Because when the others run — and they all run, eventually, everyone thinks they'll be the exception — I'd rather it not be you. So I'm going to give you some friendly advice, free of charge, which is more than the Fund ever does."

He leaned forward, and the overhead light caught his glasses, now down on his nose, and for a moment she couldn't see his eyes at all, just two blank discs.

"The Fund is restructuring. Regional portfolios are being consolidated. What that means, practically, is that accounts like yours — small, high-friction, collateral-heavy — are going to be moved from *renewal track* to *recovery track*. Do you know what recovery track means?"

"It means you take the truck."

"It means we take everything," he said pleasantly, "in whatever order is most efficient. Vehicle first, obviously. Then the gear — and I'll be honest, the resale on weather equipment is soft right now, so we'd likely bundle it. Then, if the balance remains outstanding after liquidation — and it would, Jude, it always does — we move to the personal instruments. Wage attachment. License suspension. There's a coastal work permit you need for the drone, I believe? Those get flagged." He spread his hands, apologetic, a man describing weather. "You wouldn't be able to work the coast at all. No coast, no footage. No footage, no income. You see the shape of it. It's not punishment. It's just arithmetic."

The word landed in her chest like a hook. *Arithmetic.* Everyone in her life lately spoke in arithmetic — interest rates, balances, days remaining — as if a life were a sum that could be collected.

"How long," she said. Her voice came out flat, which she was grateful for. "Before recovery track."

Brannic smiled again, and this time it didn't reach anywhere at all.

"Three payments," he said. "Give or take. But between us — and I am being your friend here — don't count on the give or take. The new regional director wants the portfolio cleaned by winter. Something about the snow closing the passes making collections difficult." He shrugged, as if the seasons were an unreasonable demand made upon him personally. "So. Three payments, if the weather's good. Less, if it isn't."

He slid a card across the table — cream stock, heavy, the Marrow Fund's logo rendered in a gray so pale it was almost an apology. A handwritten number on the back.

"That's a buyer I know. Legitimate. He pays cash for unusual finds — salvage, artifacts, documents, that kind of thing. If you come across anything out on the coast — and you're out there more than anyone, Jude, you find things — a call to that number could clear your whole balance in an afternoon." He stood, took his coat, settled it on his shoulders. "No pressure. Just keep your eyes open. Desperate people miss opportunities, and I'd hate for you to miss one."

At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at her with genuine warmth, which was the most frightening thing about him.

"Beautiful storm coming Thursday," he said. "They're saying the tide will drop lower than anyone's seen in eighty years. Low enough to show things." His smile widened by a single degree. "Keep your eyes open."

Then he was gone into the fog, and Jude sat alone in the corner booth with two cups of coffee going cold, doing the only math that had ever mattered to her: three payments, four months, one winter, zero ways out.

Outside, the sea was pulling back from the land like a held breath — and far down the coast, past the headland, past the rocks the charts marked with warnings instead of names, something the water had hidden for eighty years was beginning, slowly, to dry in the air.
