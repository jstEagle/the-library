## 2. What the Tide Gives Back

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

Jude Vasquez had been filming the storm surge from the bluff at Harrow Point when she noticed it — the sea pulling back farther than she'd ever seen it pull, farther than her grandmother had ever described, farther than the tide tables said the moon could ask. The water withdrew in long silver sheets, peeling off the rocks below the cliff like skin off a fruit, and what it left behind was not beach but revelation: a shelf of black basalt slick as glass, tide pools burning with anemones, and there — a quarter mile out where no one had ever walked — a mouth.

A cave mouth. Arched, barnacled, breathing cold air that smelled of stone and old salt and something else, something older than salt. The locals had a name for the stretch of coast beneath the point; everyone in Fetter's Cove did, though none of them would say it aloud to her, an outsider with a camera and debts. They only ever called it *the place you don't go at low water*, and they said it the way people say things they've been told not to say.

Jude stood on the bluff with her telephoto pressed to her eye and her heart doing something complicated against her ribs. She thought about the footage. She thought about the Marrow Fund, about Brannic's smile last Tuesday, about the number scrawled on the pink slip he'd tucked under her wiper — *three payments* — and how three payments was a way of saying *we are almost done being patient with you*. She thought about what a story like this might sell for. Unprecedented tide. Hidden cave. Coastal legend.

She thought about all of that for exactly as long as it took to climb down.

* * *

The descent took forty minutes and cost her a scraped palm and one boot full of seawater. By the time she reached the shelf, the light was going amber and long, and the cave mouth stood open ahead of her like a held secret. The floor of it was dry. That was the first impossible thing — dry stone, dust even, in a hollow the ocean owned twice a day. The second impossible thing was the smell: not rot, not fish, but tallow and old paper and something faintly sweet, like beeswax burned a hundred years ago and never quite gone from the air.

Her headlamp cut a cone through the gloom. The passage narrowed, then opened into a chamber tall enough to stand a truck in, its ceiling hung with flowstone the color of dried blood. And there, propped against the far wall where the lamplight couldn't quite reach —

She almost didn't look. Some instinct told her not to. But instinct was a luxury, and curiosity had always been her cheapest currency, so she looked.

A skeleton sat in a niche carved into the rock, arranged with a care that made her stomach turn. Not scattered, not slumped. Seated, upright, hands folded in its lap, facing the sea entrance as if it had been watching the water come and go for eighty years. The bones were stained amber by the minerals. A rusted chain lay coiled at its feet, links thick as her thumb, and around the skull's cranium — she saw this before she could stop herself seeing it — the ghost of a rope's compression, a groove worn into bone.

Nobody sits like that by accident. Somebody had placed him here. Somebody had wanted him watching the tide.

Jude stood very still in the dripping dark, her breath loud in her own ears, and felt the specific loneliness of being the first living thing to enter a room in decades. Then, because she was who she was — a woman who documented things for money and couldn't stop documenting them for free — she raised her camera.

The flash caught something on the wall beside the niche. Scratched into the stone, shallow and old, letters she'd have missed in any lesser light:

**TAM KEEPS THE COUNT**

Below it, smaller: *He was innocent. Ask Tam.*

* * *

"Ask Tam" was easy enough to do, because Tam was there.

She heard him before she saw him — a wet, rattling sound coming from deeper in the chamber, from behind a curtain of hanging rock. When she swung her light toward it, an old man was half-collapsed against the stone, one hand pressed flat to his chest, the other clutching a canvas satchel to his body like a mother holds a child. He was gaunt as driftwood, white-bearded, dressed in layers of wool gone gray with age, and his face was the color of candle wax.

"That'll be my heart," he said, when she came close enough. His voice was rough but steady, the voice of a man who had made peace with the sentence being read aloud. "Been waiting on it twenty years. Chose a poor day for it."

"What's happening?" Jude asked, stupidly, uselessly. "Do you need — I can get help, there's a clinic in the cove—"

"The cove's a mile," he said. "And the tide's already turning." He looked up at her with eyes gone pale blue as sea glass, and there was no fear in them at all, which frightened her more than anything else in the cave. "You're the one with the camera. The storm chaser. I've watched you on the bluff these two years past. Always running somewhere."

"I'm not running anywhere right now."

"No," he agreed, and something almost like humor moved across his face. "Right now you're standing in the one place on this coast nobody goes, holding a light on a dead man, talking to a dying one. Funny how the sea arranges things."

His breathing was getting worse. She could hear it changing — the rattle deepening, the spaces between breaths stretching. She knelt beside him, put her jacket under his head, and did the useless human things: held his hand, told him help was coming, knew it wasn't, watched him know it too.

"There's a book in the satchel," he said. "Take it out. Careful. It's older than me."

She unzipped the canvas. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax, was a ledger — a real ledger, leather-bound, brass-cornered, heavy as a brick. The cover bore no title, only a date stamped in faded gilt: a year eighty years gone. When she peeled back the oilcloth, the pages inside were dry. Dry, and legible, and written in a hand so fine and regular it might have been typeset. Columns of cargo. Names. Prices. And in the margins, notes in a different ink, a different hand — annotations, corrections, accusations.

"This is—" she started.

"That's the count," Tam said. "That's what I keep. My father kept it before me, and his mother before him — she was the smuggler's daughter, if you're wondering why a family of poor folk guards a rich man's book." He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended his lips were gray. "They hanged him for piracy. He wasn't a pirate. He was a courier, and the man who paid him stole the cargo and bought the jury, and the ledger proves every word of it. Names. Dates. Payments. It's all in there. Everything the courthouse never saw."

Jude turned a page with a care that surprised her. The columns were meticulous. In one margin, circled twice, a name leapt off the page at her — a name she'd seen before, recently, embossed in gold on a plaque outside the Marrow Fund's regional office. The founder's name. The man whose smiling portrait hung above the tellers, whose foundation now held the paper on her truck, her gear, her life.

She stared at it. She read it again. She felt the ground tilt slightly under her knees.

"You know the Fund," Tam said, watching her face. "I can see it in your eyes. Everyone on this coast knows the Fund. They've been collecting here four generations. Same family tree, same roots." He tapped the ledger's cover once, gently, with one crooked finger. "This is the root."

"Tam, I need to call someone, I need to—"

"You need to listen, because I haven't got long and the tide hasn't got longer." His voice dropped, went quiet and hard as flint. "There's a pass over the mountains. Corren Pass. First snow seals it — some years October, some years November, never later. On the other side is the provincial archive, and past that, the circuit court that still keeps a docket for overturned verdicts. That book has to be filed before the snow falls. Filed means entered into the record, witnessed, stamped — not sold, not photographed, not leaked. Filed."

"Why you? Why me?"

"Because I'm the keeper, and I'm done keeping." He closed his eyes for a moment, and Jude honestly believed he'd gone, and she leaned close, and his eyes opened again. "And because you're the one the sea gave me. I prayed for a courier. I asked for someone with fast legs and nothing to lose." He paused. "You've got debts. I know. Everyone knows. The Fund makes sure of it."

"They'll take everything I have if I leave," Jude said. Her voice cracked on it. "Three payments. If I walk away for even two weeks—"

"They'll take everything you have anyway," Tam said. "That's what they do. That's what they've always done. Read the book, girl. Every debt in it was manufactured the same way yours was. Same arithmetic, same smile, eighty years apart." His grip found her wrist — shockingly strong, a fisherman's grip, a keeper's grip. "I'm not asking you to be brave. I'm asking you to carry. One book, one pass, before the snow. What happens after is not your weight to lift tonight."

Outside, the sea had begun its return. She could hear it now — a low grinding at the cave mouth, the first thin sheets of water sliding back across the basalt shelf. An hour, maybe less, before the passage flooded and the cave became legend again for another lifetime.

Tam's breathing was failing in earnest now. Between labored pulls of air, he gave her instructions like a man dictating a will: the shepherd trails on the north side of the range, marked with cairns his family had built; a woman named Aldera Voss in the valley town who kept the archive's winter hours; the words to say and, more importantly, the words never to say on the telephone, because the Fund listened to everything on this coast, had always listened, would always listen.

"And Elias," he said suddenly, and coughed. "If a man named Elias comes asking — an auditor, young, precise, wears his guilt like a coat that doesn't fit yet — show him the margin on page forty. Just that. Let the numbers do the rest."

"Why would I trust anyone from the Fund?"

"Because I've watched him count," Tam said, "and a man who counts truly cannot serve liars forever. Arithmetic is honest. It's the only honest thing the Fund ever touched." He smiled, and it transformed his dying face into something boyish. "My grandmother wrote that. Page forty. You'll see."

The water was at the threshold now, licking the chamber floor in long translucent fingers. Jude wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth, sealed it, and slung the satchel across her chest. When she turned back, Tam had closed his eyes, and this time his chest barely moved, and she stayed anyway, kneeling in the rising water, holding the hand of a man she'd known for twenty minutes, until the sea came in and the room filled and there was nothing left to hold.

She swam out through the flooded passage in the dark, the satchel held high above the surface, and dragged herself onto the shelf as the cave mouth swallowed itself behind her. The storm had passed. The sky over the point was rinsed clean, and the first stars were out, and the tide was climbing the rocks with the patience of something that had all the time in the world.

Jude Vasquez sat on the black basalt with salt in her throat and a dead man's inheritance strapped to her chest, and she did not cry, and she did not pray, and she did not run.

She opened the ledger to page forty, by the light of her headlamp, and began to read.

What she found there made her check the date on the first snow forecast twice — and start walking before midnight.
