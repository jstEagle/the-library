## 3. Names in the Ledger

The keeper's hut smelled of kerosene, salt-rot, and forty years of a man living alone with a secret too heavy to bury. Jude had dragged Old Tam inside as best she could — he weighed less than driftwood, all bone and weather — and laid him on his cot, but there was nothing to be done for him. He had died an hour after sunset, his hand still curled around her wrist, his last words a repetition: *before the snow. Before the snow.*

Now she sat alone at his table with the ledger open before her and a storm lamp turned low, and she understood that she had not found treasure. She had been handed something, the way you're handed a live coal. You could drop it. You could let it burn through your hands. Or you could carry it.

The ledger itself was a miracle of stubbornness. Oilcloth covers gone stiff as shell, sewn spine intact, pages of rag paper that had drunk salt air for eighty years and refused to crumble. The ink had faded to the color of dried blood, but it held. Whoever wrote this had written it to survive. She thought about that — about a man who knew he was going to die, hanged by his neck in a courthouse square, who spent his final months keeping accounts in a book meant to outlive him.

She turned the first page slowly, afraid her breath would damage it.

*Inventory of the Kestrel's cargo, taken at anchor below Corren Head, being goods landed honest and goods landed otherwise, kept separate so no man can say I stole what was not mine to steal.*

Below that, columns. Dates. Names.

Jude had spent six years reading tides and barometric pressure the way other people read faces. She read numbers the way Tam must have read weather — instinctively, structurally, seeing the shape beneath the figures. And the shape of these figures was wrong. Every third entry, the "honest" cargo thinned while the "otherwise" column swelled, and beside each thinning sat a name she didn't recognize yet, followed by a small notation: *paid out, per arrangement.*

Someone was skimming the smuggler's own shipments. Someone inside.

* * *

The lamp guttered; she trimmed the wick and kept reading, and somewhere past midnight the hut's shadows stopped feeling like shadows and started feeling like witnesses.

The name appeared first as initials — *H.M.* — then, three pages in, spelled out whole: *Harlan Marrow.* Beside it, in the smuggler's careful hand: *received from H.M., per agreement, one-third share of seized cargo, being payment for silence regarding the buyer.*

Jude sat back. The name meant nothing to her yet, just ink. But the word *seized* snagged like a fishhook. Seized by whom? Cargo didn't seize itself.

She read on. The entries grew terser as the dates marched toward autumn — the smuggler, whoever he was, writing faster now, writing like a man who could hear hooves behind him. A shipment of silk and gunpowder landed at Corren Head. A buyer waiting inland. Then:

*Cargo taken on the road by men flying the customs flag, though the flags were bought paper. H.M. present at the taking. Goods conveyed to the warehouse at Grayharbor and sold within the week. My name given to the magistrate for the hanging. H.M.'s name nowhere spoken. The magistrate's daughter's debts were paid that same month — I have seen the receipt.*

There it was. Not rumor. Arithmetic. A man had been hanged so another man could sell his cargo, and the price of the rope had been entered in a column like any other expense: *one magistrate, purchased; one hanging, arranged; total cost to H.M., less than the value of one crate.*

Jude felt something shift under her ribs, some load-bearing wall of assumption giving way. She had spent years thinking of her debt as weather — a force of nature, impersonal, inevitable. Three payments from losing everything, and she'd never once asked who built the machine that was crushing her. Machines have builders. Debts have architects.

She turned pages until dawn grayed the window, mapping names. Harlan Marrow. The warehouse at Grayharbor. A shipping clerk named Voss who signed receipts. A magistrate called Aldous Renwick whose signature appeared on both the seizure order and, later, the death warrant — the same hand, the same ink, the same week.

And then, near the end, a page that stopped her cold. A list titled *Shares, ongoing,* and beneath it names that were not dead men's names. They were surnames passed down like the debt itself — because that's what they were. Inheritance. The skim had become an annuity; the annuity had become an institution. At the bottom of the column, in handwriting newer than the rest, someone had continued the ledger into the next century, entries in ballpoint pen:

*Marrow Fund, established. Collection rights assigned. Regional enforcement — B. Brannic.*

Brannic. Her Brannic. The smiling man who'd leaned against her truck two weeks ago and told her, with the warmth of a friend delivering terrible news, that the Fund appreciated her business and looked forward to many more years of it. The man whose chain of command ran upward through regional directors to a board, and above the board a trust, and above the trust — she'd never asked, had she? Never once asked where the money came from. People in debt don't ask where the money comes from. Asking is a luxury of the solvent.

But here it was, in ink older than her grandmother: the Fund's founding capital was a hanged man's cargo. Her interest payments were the interest payments on a murder, compounding across eighty years. Every collector's visit, every friendly ultimatum, every percentage point — all of it flowing backward through time to a rope in a courthouse square and a receipt for a magistrate's daughter's debts.

* * *

Sleep should have come. It didn't. Instead, around four in the morning, the flashback took her — not memory, she hadn't lived it, but something with memory's weight, assembled from the ledger's dry lines until they stood up and walked.

She saw the harbor as it must have been: lantern light on black water, the Kestrel riding low at anchor, its captain — call him what the ledger's margins called him, *the Kestrel* — counting crates by feel in the dark because lamplight carried to the customs house. She saw the meeting inland, the buyer with soft hands and a hard mouth, Harlan Marrow young then, ambitious, already keeping his own books. She saw the road ambush — how the customs flags were new cloth, how the "officers" wore their belts wrong, how the Kestrel noticed all of it and said nothing because saying anything would only hasten the noose. She saw the trial, eleven minutes by the ledger's count of the docket. She saw the square, the crowd, the carpenter testing the trapdoor twice because the magistrate wanted no mistakes — mistakes cost money.

And she saw, afterward, the thing the ledger recorded in its coldest entry: the Kestrel's daughter, a girl of nine, standing at the edge of the square while her father swung, holding a copper coin he'd slipped her that morning — *for bread, little bird, don't spend it all* — and Harlan Marrow watching from a first-floor window, checking his watch, because the hanging was scheduled between two appointments and he was a punctual man.

The girl's name was in the margin. *Edda.* The ledger tracked her — the Kestrel had written it knowing he wouldn't live to see: *If my Edda lives, let her know the coin was never for bread. It was for the ferryman. Tell her I paid my passage and owed no one. Let no one say her father died a thief. He died a creditor.*

A creditor. Jude read the word five times. The hanged man had understood it before anyone: that Marrow owed *him*. That the debt ran the other direction. Eighty years of compounding, and the original principal had never been paid — not in coin, not in truth, not in a cleared name.

Jude set down the lamp and pressed her palms flat against the oilcloth cover, and the coal in her hands burned steady now instead of searing. She knew what she was holding. She knew what it was worth — a journalist would pay for it, a rival fund would pay more, the Fund itself would pay most of all to see it ash. Enough to erase her debt three times over. Enough to buy back her truck, her gear, her momentum, her whole life of outrunning the collectors.

Enough to make her exactly what the Kestrel refused to be.

*He died a creditor.* She spoke it aloud to the empty hut, to the old man's cold cot, to the sea working at the rocks below. "Then I'm carrying his claim."

Outside, the tide had begun its long withdrawal again, and the cave mouth stood exposed in the pre-dawn gray like a held breath. Somewhere up the coast road, she knew without knowing how she knew, a car was already moving — the Fund did not send one man to a cave and then forget about it. Brannic had smiled when he said they'd be in touch. Men like Brannic always came back for the inventory.

She had maybe a day. Maybe less. The pass lay east, over the range, and the forecast — she'd checked it out of habit, the way a drowning woman checks the depth — gave the first snows ten days out. Ten days to cross on foot what her truck might have managed in six hours, if her truck still existed by nightfall.

Jue packed the ledger in oilskin from Tam's stores, wrapped it twice, and slung it inside her jacket against her spine, where a person carries the things they cannot afford to lose. She banked the lamp. She stepped out into the blue hour before sunrise, and the wind off the water hit her face like a hand.

Behind her, far up the coast road, headlights swung around the headland.

Not one pair. Two.
