## 5. Foothold Town

Foothold smelled of diesel and brine and the particular sweetness of fish left too long in the sun, a smell that got into your clothes and stayed there like a memory you couldn't wash out. Jude rolled the truck down the single street at walking speed, past the cannery with its broken windows boarded like eyes, past the church whose steeple leaned a degree or two east, as if the whole town had been listening to the wind so long it had begun to lean into it.

The pass road began at the far end of the street and climbed into cloud. She could see the first switchbacks, gray ribbons stitched up the green shoulder of the mountain, and above them the snowline, sharp as a ruled pencil mark. Three days, maybe four, before the weather closed it. Everyone in town said so the way people say things they've said every autumn of their lives — shrugging, certain, already bored by their own certainty.

She parked behind the chandlery, where the truck wouldn't be visible from the road, and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, doing what she always did: calculating. Fuel to the trailhead. Food for five days on foot if the weather turned. A room tonight, because sleeping in the cab with the ledger under her seat had begun to feel like sleeping inside a held breath. The numbers came out ugly. They always came out ugly now. Every mile she drove cost money she didn't have, and every hour she sat still cost more, because Brannic's collectors were paid to find her and she was paying herself to be found.

The ledger rode against her ribs in a waterproof bag she'd bought from a diver's shop two towns back, the kind of purchase that had felt extravagant at forty dollars and now felt like the only sensible thing she'd done in weeks. Under her jacket. Against her skin. She'd started thinking of it that way — not cargo, not leverage, but something closer to an organ. Something that would kill her if it were taken out wrong.

* * *

The newspaper office was above the hardware store, up stairs that groaned like a ship in weather, and the man who ran it was named Aldous Wren, which sounded like a name invented for the job. He was sixty-odd, ink-stained to the elbow, and he looked at her the way old journalists look at everyone: as though he were already writing her obituary and checking it for errors.

"The Corren Ledger," she said, when his door was shut and the kettle was on. "Eighty years old. Weatherproof oilskin. It names the men who bought the hanging of Tomas Reyes."

Wren did not reach for it. He reached instead for his pipe, tamped it, lit it, and watched her through the first smoke with the patience of a man who had been offered miracles before and learned they came with invoices.

"Show me one page," he said. "One. And tell me where you got it."

She told him about the cave, about Old Tam dying with his hand pressed flat on the oilskin like a man holding down a map in wind. She told him about the tide, the freak low water, the locals who wouldn't say the cave's name aloud. She watched his face while she talked, and she saw the moment the skepticism went out of it — not all at once, but the way fog goes off a headland, slowly, then suddenly.

He read the page she gave him twice. His hands were steady until the second reading, and then they weren't quite.

"I know these names," he said quietly. "Not from history books. From the courthouse steps. From charity galas. From the plaque on the library wing." He set the ledger down as if it were hot. "Girl, do you understand what you're carrying? This isn't a story. This is a landmine with a ribbon on it."

"That's why I'm here. I want to sell it. Exclusive. First publication rights, verification, whatever you need. I'm not asking for much." She almost laughed at herself. "I'm asking for less than I owe."

"And I'd print it." Wren stood at his window, looking down at the street, at the harbor, at the mountains going white at their edges. "I'd print it tomorrow and damn the consequences, and it would be the story of my life, and it would ruin this paper inside a month. You know who owns the building this office sits in? Through a holding company through another holding company?" He tapped ash into a saucer shaped like a fish. "Marrow. You know who owns the courthouse? Not the county. Marrow. The judge's chambers were renovated last year — Marrow money. The clerk's salary is subsidized. Half the jury pool works seasonal jobs on Marrow properties." He turned around. "You want to hand that ledger to a court, I'll drive you there myself and we can watch it disappear into an evidence locker that never gets opened again. You want to sell it to me, I'll pay you what I can, which is enough for bus fare and a funeral, and the Fund's lawyers will bury us both under injunctions before the ink dries."

"So what do I do with it?"

"What Tam asked, I expect." Wren's voice softened. "Carry it over the mountain. There are places money doesn't reach as fast as feet do. The provincial archive closes for winter in nine days. Get it into the public record on the other side of that pass, and it stops being property and becomes history. History is harder to buy."

"And until then?"

"Until then," Wren said, refilling her tea, "you're the most dangerous woman in this province, and you're carrying the proof in a dive bag against your chest, and the men looking for you are very good at their work." He smiled without warmth. "I'd leave town tonight."

* * *

She meant to. She'd gotten as far as buying food — hard bread, hard cheese, a block of salt, things that weighed little and kept forever — and she was loading it into the truck bed behind the chandlery when she heard the engines.

Two vehicles, coming slow up the main street, windows down despite the cold. She knew the sound of them the way you know a song you hate: the idle of those particular trucks, the aftermarket exhausts, the low growl that said *we are in no hurry because we always arrive*. Brannic rode in the lead one, and even at eighty yards, even in the failing light, she could pick him out — the pale coat, the easy smile, the way he waved at the fishmongers closing their stalls as though he were a man running for office.

Jude killed the lantern she'd been working by and crouched in the shadow between the truck and the chandlery wall. The ledger dug into her ribs as she bent, a small hard ache she was learning to live with, the way you learn to live with a stone in your boot.

Brannic's truck stopped in front of the newspaper office. Of course it did. Of course he knew. He'd probably known before she did that she'd come to Foothold; there were only so many roads, only so many towns, and men like Brannic didn't chase, they waited, the way tide waits.

She heard him go up the stairs. Heard Wren's door open. Heard nothing for a long time — no shouting, no violence, just the terrible quiet of two men talking in a room above a hardware store, one of whom owned everything and one of whom owned a printing press and a spine. When Brannic came back down, ten minutes later, he was smiling wider than before, and Wren wasn't smiling at all.

Then the collectors fanned out. Down the alley behind the chandlery. Along the harbor fence. Two of them walked the row of moored boats, shining flashlights into holds, unhurried, thorough, men doing inventory. Jude lay flat in the truck bed under a tarp and a coil of netting and listened to boots come close and stop and move on, and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that the truck was finished. Whatever happened next, that vehicle was a known quantity now. A marked thing. Running by road was over.

She slipped out the far side, over a fence slick with old paint, down through the boatyard where hulls stood on blocks like whale bones, and cut toward the fish market — the long shed at the harbor's edge where the night stalls were just setting up, lamps lit, ice shoveled across the tables in glittering drifts.

* * *

The fish market at dusk was the best kind of chaos: too many people, too many smells, too many voices arguing about price and freshness and the honesty of one another. Gulls screamed under the roof beams. Knives worked constantly, a sound like rain on tin. Steam rose off a soup stall in fat white plumes, and the whole place smelled of salt and blood and lemon and woodsmoke, and Jude moved through it with her hood up and her head down, letting the crowd carry her the way current carries kelp.

It nearly worked. It worked for six minutes.

Then a boy selling smoked herring looked at her twice — looked at her face, then at the shape under her jacket, then at her face again — and called out to his uncle, and his uncle called out to somebody else, and the information moved through the market faster than she could move through it, because information always does.

They came in from both ends of the shed. No guns drawn — Brannic was too smart for that, too fond of witnesses — just big men moving with terrible courtesy, saying *ma'am, ma'am, just talk to us*, closing like a net made of politeness. Jude went over a table of iced mackerel, scattering silver bodies across the cobbles, gulls diving after them in a shrieking white storm, and the crowd roared — half alarm, half delight, because a chase through a fish market is the best entertainment Foothold has had since the cannery burned.

She ran the length of the shed. Slipped on ice. Caught a hanging rail and swung around a corner into the gutting aisle, where the floor ran red-gray with rinse water and the knives never stopped. Behind her, the collectors came on, patient as erosion. Ahead, the far doors opened onto the quay, onto the truck, onto Brannic standing beside it with his hands in his pockets and his smile aimed at her like a lamp.

"There she is," he said warmly. "Jude. Come sit down. Let's talk about the truck."

She looked at him. She looked at the truck — her home for three years, her office, her archive of storms, every drive she'd ever chased still living in its scratched-up dashcam drives. And she understood that he wasn't offering her a conversation. He was showing her the price list.

"You burn it," she said, "and I've got nothing left to take."

"Oh, I don't think that's true." Brannic tilted his head, genuinely pleasant, genuinely frightening. "I think you've got exactly one thing left to take. And I think you love it too much to let it burn."

He was right, and she hated him for it, and she hated herself more for how well he knew it. So she did the thing he hadn't priced in. She turned and ran the other way — back into the market, back through the gutting aisle, out the loading doors onto the mudflats side, where the tide was out and the world was black stink and silver channels — and she heard, behind her, the whump of an accelerant catching, and saw, reflected in the wet sand, the orange bloom of her truck going up like a second sunset.

She didn't look back. Looking back is how they catch you. She ran the flats in the dark, ankles screaming, breath tearing, the ledger bouncing against her ribs like a second heartbeat, until the lights of Foothold were a smudge behind her and the mountain was a black wall ahead, and she stopped in the channel of a stream that came down off the range, cold enough to hurt, and drank, and shook, and admitted it to herself at last, standing alone in the dark with everything she owned burning eight hundred yards away:

No more running by road.

The pass opened in the morning. She'd be at the trailhead by dawn.

And somewhere behind her, she couldn't help noticing — somewhere in the dark between the town and the flats, someone else was watching the same fire, and making, she suspected, some arithmetic of his own.
