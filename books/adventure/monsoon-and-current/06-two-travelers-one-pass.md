## 6. Two Travelers, One Pass

**Elias**

The trailhead at Corren's foot was a scar of gravel and old fire, and Elias Vane had been standing on it for two hours when the woman finally came down out of the pines.

He had reconstructed her route the way he reconstructed everything — from fragments, from arithmetic. A fuel receipt in Foothold Town dated the morning after the fish market. A witness who'd seen a woman on foot moving north along the river road with a canvas satchel clutched like a child. The burned shell of her truck still smoldering in the market square when he'd arrived, rubber stink hanging over the fish stalls like a curtain. Brannic had assumed she'd run east, toward the coast, toward water, because people run toward what they know. But Jude Vasquez had spent her whole life running toward weather. She'd gone up.

So Elias had gone up too, alone, without telling Brannic, and stood at the mouth of the Corren Pass trail while the wind came off the glacier in long white breaths and the first snow of the season sat somewhere behind the peaks, waiting for its cue.

She saw him at forty meters and stopped so completely that the forest seemed to stop with her. One hand went into her jacket. He raised both his own, empty, palms out — an auditor's gesture, nothing to seize, nothing to declare.

"You're the one who counts everything," she said. Her voice was raw from cold and from shouting at collectors across a fish market two nights ago. "Elias Vane. I read your name off the recovery order."

"Then you know I'm supposed to take the ledger."

"Then you also know I don't hand things to men with orders."

The wind moved through the pines above them, and somewhere high on the mountain a cornice let go with a sound like a door closing in an empty house. Neither of them flinched. That, more than anything, told him they were the same kind of animal.

"I'm not here on orders," he said. "I'm here because my numbers stopped adding up."

* * *

**Jude**

She should have run. Every instinct she owned — the ones that had kept her ahead of Brannic's collectors through three counties and one burning truck — said *run now, run uphill, lose him in the scree*. But her legs were done lying to her. Two days on foot with no sleep and half a loaf of bread had spent whatever momentum she'd been living on since the cave, and besides, there was something in the man's face she recognized from mirrors: the look of someone whose whole architecture had quietly failed overnight.

He walked closer, slowly, hands still visible, and crouched ten feet away like a man approaching a wounded dog. Up close he looked worse than his file photo. Gray under the eyes. A pen mark of ink on his left thumb that he hadn't bothered to scrub.

"The Fund sent me to recover the contents of the cave," he said. "That was the assignment. I did the work. I always do the work." He laughed, short and airless. "Do you know what I found? The archive has a gap. Eleven days, eighty years ago. Cargo manifests that were entered twice everywhere else in the company's history — except those eleven days. And the dates of the gap match your ledger exactly."

Jude's hand came out of her jacket slowly.

"You audited a murder," she said.

"I audited an absence," he corrected, but his voice cracked on the word, and they both heard it. "There's a difference. There has to be a difference. I've built twenty years on there being a difference between a missing record and a buried body."

"There isn't," Jude said. "I've read it. Every page. The founder bought the rope and paid the judge and kept the cargo, and then he burned eleven days of paper so no one would ever do the arithmetic you just did." She pulled the ledger from the satchel, and the wind took the cover and flipped it open against her arm like the book itself wanted to be read. "You want to see? Look. Look at it. Then tell me about differences."

* * *

**Elias**

He read by the gray light of the trailhead, kneeling, the ledger flat on a slab of granite while snow began — not the blizzard yet, just the season's first scattered flakes, small and dry as ash. He read the way he did everything: completely, twice, lips moving slightly over the columns.

Cargo taken. Prices recorded in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. And on the final leaf, in different ink, different pressure, a name written like a confession: the founder's own initials, and beside them a sum labeled only *for the hanging*, itemized with the same tidy precision the Marrow Fund used today for repossessing trucks and boats and lives. Rope: four shillings. The judge's silence: forty pounds. The constable's discretion: twelve.

Eighty years, and the handwriting hadn't changed. Elias knew that handwriting. It was on his employment contract. It was on Brannic's collection orders. It was on the letter that had fired the last auditor who'd asked a question in 1974.

He closed the book and knelt there for a long moment with his palms flat on the stone, as if checking whether the mountain itself was still level.

"My arithmetic doesn't lie," he said finally. His voice came out strange to him, stripped of its office cadence. "It never has. That was the one thing I trusted about myself. And it says the company I have served for twenty years murdered a man to steal from him, and then built a lending house on top of the grave, and lent money against that grave ever since, and collected interest on it every quarter." He looked up at her. Snow was landing in his hair and he didn't seem to notice. "I signed the recovery order for this book. I drove here to take it from you."

"And instead?"

"Instead I'm asking." He swallowed. "I've never asked anyone for anything in my life. I've calculated. Asking is new. Forgive me if I do it badly."

* * *

**Jude**

What she felt was not trust. She wanted to be clear about that, sitting there on the cold granite with the most dangerous document on the coast open between herself and a Fund auditor. What she felt was recognition — the specific, unwelcome recognition of watching another person's life come apart in real time and knowing the exact sound it made, because hers had been coming apart for years in slower motion.

"They'll send Brannic," she said. "When you don't report in. They'll send him, and he doesn't kneel and read. He burns trucks."

"I know."

"He'll fly over the pass if he has to. Helicopters. Men with ropes."

"I know that too." Elias stood, joints cracking, and looked up at the pass — a notch of white between black walls, six thousand feet of bad weather between them and the valley where the provincial archive waited with its winter closure three days out. "Which is why you shouldn't go alone. You can't outrun a helicopter on foot. But a Fund auditor walking beside you, carrying Fund credentials, with a plausible story about having recovered the asset—" he tapped the ledger, "—that buys you the hours you need. Maybe the day you need."

"And what do you get?"

He was quiet for a moment. A gust came up the trail and flattened the grass along the switchbacks, and the snow thickened by one degree, the way weather does when it's deciding.

"A clean number," he said. "One entry in my life that balances. I put this in the archive, I watch the record corrected, and everything I signed my name to for twenty years means something other than what it meant yesterday. That's all. It's not noble. Don't make it noble. It's bookkeeping."

"It's never just bookkeeping," Jude said. "Not with you. You counted your way here against your own orders. That's not bookkeeping. That's defection with excellent documentation."

Something happened to his face then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go, thawing. "Terms?" he said.

"Terms. You carry nothing. The ledger stays on me. If we meet collectors, you talk first, and if you say one word that sounds like *recovery*, I go off-trail and you can explain the loss to Brannic yourself. And when we get down—" she hesitated, because the next part cost her, "—when we get down, we do it right. Archive, record, testimony. No selling it, no shortcuts, no deals. The old man in the cave made me promise. Over the mountains before the snow. We're already late."

"We're not late," Elias said, glancing at the sky with an auditor's eye for deadlines. "We're exactly on schedule for the weather to be early. Which is worse."

* * *

**Elias**

They started up at noon, and within the hour he understood that he had made a bargain with a force of nature wearing a borrowed coat.

Jude Vasquez climbed the way water moves — finding the line, committing to it, no wasted motion, no complaint. She'd been awake, by his estimate, for fifty hours, and she set a pace that would have broken a younger man, and when he called for a halt at the first shelter cairn she didn't gloat, just handed him her canteen without being asked, which was somehow worse than gloating.

"Why storm chasing?" he asked, because the silence at altitude felt like something you had to fill or it filled you instead. "Of all the ways to owe a Fund money."

"Because a storm is honest," she said. She was looking up the trail, at the pass, at the weather assembling itself behind the peaks in towers of gray. "It tells you exactly what it's going to do and then it does it. Nobody smiles at you first. I spent six years being smiled at by men holding contracts." She passed the canteen back. "Your turn. Why does a man spend twenty years counting for them?"

"Because counting is safe," Elias said. "Numbers don't lie and neither did I, not once, not ever, and I told myself that was integrity. Clean hands through dirty work." He screwed the cap back on the canteen and stared at it. "Then I found eleven missing days and discovered that clean hands are just hands that never reached for anything."

Above them, the pass had disappeared entirely behind a wall of white that hadn't been there an hour ago. The wind changed direction — came down off the summit now instead of up the valley, carrying a smell like iron and distance, the smell Jude had spent her life learning to read.

She stood up fast.

"That's not squall weather," she said. "That's the front. It's early. It's a day early, maybe two." She looked at him, and in her face he saw the thing he would carry down the other side of this mountain and for the rest of his life after: fear, yes, but underneath the fear a kind of fierce, terrible gladness, the look of a woman who had finally stopped running from storms and was, at last, running with one.

"Then we walk through the night," Elias said, and shouldered his pack. "Before it seals."

Behind them, far below at the trailhead, a sound rolled up the valley — engines, more than one, and the thin metallic slap of rotor blades testing the air.

Brannic had found the truck's ashes. Brannic had found the trailhead.

And the snow was coming down now in earnest, fat and silent, erasing their backtrail step by step, as if the mountain had already decided whose side it was on.
