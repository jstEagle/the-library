## 7. The Range Before the Snows

The mountain did not want us.

I understood that by the second hour of the climb, when the trail gave up pretending to be a trail and became a suggestion — a line of cairns half-swallowed by scree, a memory of a path that sheep had once known and men had mostly forgotten. The air up here had a taste to it, cold iron and old stone and something green and far below, the sea we'd left behind at dawn. Every breath cost more than the last. I counted my steps in twenties because it was the only arithmetic left that didn't frighten me.

Elias walked ahead of me with the ledger strapped inside his coat, against his chest, like a second heart. He'd insisted on carrying it after the bridge — no. Before the bridge. I was getting ahead of myself, the way I always did, my mind sprinting down the trail while my body labored somewhere behind.

We had started from the shepherd's track above Foothold Town while the sky was still the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges. The forecast said three days of clear weather. The forecast was a thing other people believed in. I had spent my whole professional life learning that weather made promises the way the Marrow Fund made them: warmly, precisely, and with no intention whatsoever of keeping them.

"Corren Pass closes when it closes," the innkeeper's wife had told us, wrapping our bread in waxed paper with hands like knotted rope. "Not when the calendar says. Not when you say. When it says." She'd looked at Elias's city boots and then at me, reading something in my face that I hadn't meant to show her. "You'll want to be over the top before Thursday."

It was Tuesday.

* * *

The rope bridge hung across a gorge that the maps called the Throat, which should have been warning enough. It was maybe forty meters of hemp and rot, strung between two granite shoulders, sagging into a curve that looked less like engineering and more like grief. Below it, thirty meters down, the meltwater ran white and loud, so loud we had to shout, and even shouting felt small against it.

"I'll go first," Elias said. "Lighter load distribution if—"

"You weigh what I weigh. Go on." I didn't mean to snap. Fear came out of me as sharpness; it always had. My father used to say I could cut glass with a bad morning.

He went, hand over hand, testing each plank with a delicacy that would have been funny under any other circumstances — this man who counted everything, weighing every board like a suspicious invoice. Halfway across he stopped, and the whole bridge swayed with his stopping, and I watched him look down into the white water for one long moment. Then he kept moving.

When it was my turn, the bridge behaved the way dying things behave: it held, and held, and then explained, gently, that holding had never really been part of its plan.

The first plank went under my left foot like wet bread. I heard it more than felt it — a soft fibrous sigh — and then the second plank tilted, and the third simply wasn't there anymore, and the rope in my hands went slack in a way that ropes are not supposed to do. The whole structure dropped a foot. Maybe two. The world became very simple very fast: the gorge, the water, the ledger in Elias's coat on the far side, the sound of my own pulse in my ears louder than the river.

"Elias—" I don't remember deciding to say his name. It just came out, raw.

He was already coming back. That's the part I keep returning to, the part I'll keep returning to when this is all paper and testimony. He didn't hesitate. He didn't calculate. The auditor who weighed every plank came back onto a failing bridge without weighing anything at all, and when the near anchor tore loose from the rock with a crack like a rifle shot, he threw himself flat and caught the trailing ropes with both hands, and the bridge swung out over the void with me still clinging to it, and for four or five seconds — I know because I counted, because counting is what I do when there is nothing else — we were both falling and neither of us fell.

His arms shook. His face was six inches from mine, bloodied where a frayed strand had caught his cheek, and he was saying, absurdly, calmly, "Don't pull. Don't pull. Let the rope take you. Trust the rope."

"The rope is trying to kill us."

"The rope," he said, "is the only thing on this mountain that isn't."

I climbed. Hand over hand along the sagging spine of the thing, while he anchored it against his body and took the strain until I thought his shoulders would come apart at the seams. And when I finally rolled onto solid stone on the far side, gasping like a landed fish, he crawled off the ruin of the bridge and lay beside me on the gravel, bleeding, laughing — actually laughing, a low helpless sound I'd never heard from him.

"What," I managed, "is funny."

"I've audited eleven years of risk assessments," he said. "Eleven years of documents about what people will do for money. And I have never once seen a line item for what a person does for nothing." He turned his head and looked at me, and the blood on his cheek had already gone dark in the cold. "You were going to let go of the ledger's location rather than drop it in the river. I saw you check the strap. Mid-fall, Jude. You checked the strap."

"I checked whether *you* were still holding it."

"That too," he said, and looked away first, which was new.

* * *

The shelter cave was a seam in the mountainside a thousand feet higher, marked on no map but known to every shepherd for two hundred years — a hollow deep enough to turn wind, floored with old straw and the black circles of a hundred fires. We got there as the light failed, blue hour draining into true dark, and built a small fire because small fires don't announce you and large ones do. Elias's cheek I cleaned with snow and taped with a strip of my shirt. He sat very still while I worked, the way men sit when they're not used to being tended and don't want to admit they're grateful.

The silence in that cave had weight. Outside, the wind moved through the pass in long slow breaths, and the temperature was falling in a way I could feel through my boots, through the stone, through my teeth.

"We should talk about tomorrow," he said at last.

"No."

"No?"

"Tomorrow can wait until tomorrow. Tonight the storm hasn't found us yet, and the fire's lit, and I am going to spend one hour not planning how to survive." I fed a stick to the flames. "Ask me anything else."

He thought about it. I could see him thinking, sorting possible questions the way he sorted columns, and then discarding the safe ones one by one, which frightened me more than any of the unsafe ones could have.

"Why do you chase storms?" he asked finally. "Genuinely. Not the answer for cameras."

I almost deflected. I had a whole polished answer about adrenaline and footage markets and the physics of supercells, a brochure version of myself I handed out to strangers. But the cave was dark, and the firelight made his face honest, and there was nowhere to walk to. That was the thing about a shelter cave above the tree line: it was the one place in the world with no exit, and therefore the one place where lying served no purpose.

"Because a storm is the only thing I've ever met that's bigger than my debts," I said. "Everything else in my life got smaller. The truck got older. The towns got poorer. The Fund got bigger. But a supercell doesn't care what I owe. It's ten miles tall and it doesn't care, and standing under it is the closest I come to feeling free." I laughed, short and bitter. "That's pathetic, isn't it? Renting freedom by the hour from the weather."

"It's not pathetic. It's accurate." He poked the fire. "I count things. You run from them. We're both doing arithmetic instead of living."

"And you?" I said. "Why does a man who can do anything audit debt for the Marrow Fund?"

The wind filled the pause for him. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the auditor's polish.

"Because I'm good at it, and being good at it felt like mattering. Because the numbers always balanced, and for a long time I mistook balance for rightness." He stared into the fire. "Do you know what I did, before the cave? Before you? I priced repossessions. Fishing boats, mostly. Homes. A woman's prosthetic leg once — it was listed as an asset, collateral value forty dollars. I signed the schedule. I didn't think about the leg. I thought about the forty dollars." His jaw tightened. "Eighty years ago, a man named Aldous Corren — the founder, the name on the pass — needed a smuggler to hang so he could keep stolen cargo and buy respectability. And the machinery that did it, the ledgers and schedules and valuations, the paperwork that turns a human life into a number you can spend — that machinery has a direct descendant, and I sat at its desk for eleven years and called it diligence."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, wind worried at the rocks.

"There's a page in the ledger," I said quietly. "Near the end. After the hanging."

"I read it. In the town, before the collectors came." He nodded slowly. "The daughter."

"Maren Corren Voss." I knew it by heart now; I'd read it so many times the ink lived behind my eyes. "Aldous Corren's own daughter. She married the hanged man's business partner's son — Tomas Voss. The ledger records a payment, the year after the hanging. Quiet. Untraceable. Money sent to a woman in the valley who never spent it, who passed it to her son, who passed it to his daughter, who passed it down — eight decades of a family that knew exactly what their grandfather's fortune was built on and couldn't figure out how to give it back." I looked at him. "The shepherds. The ones who'll be waiting on the other side of the pass. They're hers. The smuggler's blood runs in the people who are going to help us finish this."

Elias was silent a long time. "So the money comes home," he said finally. "Not to the sea. To the family."

"To the truth," I said. "The money's just the shape the truth takes."

And then the cave got very quiet in a different way, the way rooms go quiet when something unsaid stands up in the middle of them and everyone pretends not to see it. I watched the fire. He watched the fire. The unsaid thing stood between us, enormous, patient.

"Jude." His voice was barely above the wind. "On the bridge. When you said my name."

"I know what I said."

"Do you? Because I've been turning it over for six hours, and I can't make it fit any column. It doesn't balance. Nothing about this balances — the sacrifice, the trust, the way I came back onto that bridge without a single calculation." He turned to look at me fully, and the firelight moved in his eyes. "I have spent my entire life making sure of everything. And the only thing I have ever been sure of without proof is—"

"Don't," I said. My heart was slamming. "Don't say it in a cave, in the dark, the night before a pass that might kill us. If you say it now, it becomes a deathbed confession. It becomes something we said because we were scared."

"Isn't fear honest?"

"Fear is honest. So is morning." I reached across the fire and took his hand — his cold, rope-burned, ink-stained hand — and held it. "Say it tomorrow. On the other side. Say it in daylight, in the valley, when we're alive. Promise me that."

He laced his fingers through mine. "Tomorrow," he said. "In daylight."

We slept in shifts, back to back, sharing warmth the way climbers do, practical and intimate at once. I woke once in the black hours and heard the wind change — felt it change, a pressure shift in my ears, a new note in its voice, colder, wetter, the smell of it turning from stone to ice. Snow-smell. Early-snow smell.

I lay awake listening to it and did not wake him, because there was nothing either of us could do until dawn, and because he was dreaming, and in his dream his face was unguarded and young.

But I knew. The way I always know, three payments out, the way I knew the tide would open the cave. The mountain had decided.

Thursday had come early.
