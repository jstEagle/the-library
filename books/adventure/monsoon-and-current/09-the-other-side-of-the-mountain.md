## 9. The Other Side of the Mountain

The valley came to her in pieces.

First the sound — a low, woolly murmur beneath the wind, like the mountain breathing through its sleep. Then the smell: woodsmoke and wet stone and something green, impossibly green after three days of white. Then, at last, the light, going gold and horizontal across a floor of frozen pasture, and Jude understood that she had stopped falling. The slope had gentled without her noticing. Her boots were hitting earth instead of ice, and earth held her the way snow never quite did — grudgingly, but it held.

She had walked out of the blizzard the way a drowning woman surfaces: not by decision but by refusal to stop. Somewhere above her, hours ago now, Elias had stayed behind in the snow with Brannic's collectors, trading himself for her passage, and she had walked because stopping would have meant thinking about that, and thinking about that would have meant sitting down, and sitting down was death dressed up as rest. So she had put one boot in front of the other until the mountain ran out of mountain, and now here was grass, browned and brittle under frost, and here were sheep, huddled black against a fence line, regarding her with the flat indifference of creatures who had seen weather worse than her.

Her hands wouldn't close. She knew this distantly, the way you know a fact about a stranger. The ledger was inside her coat, against her chest, and she had spent the last hours checking on it at intervals like a heartbeat — still there, still dry, still there — because if she lost it now, after everything, after the bridge and the shelter cave and the man bleeding into the drifts so she could carry paper over a mountain, then there was no version of herself she could live inside afterward.

The hut appeared out of the dusk all at once, as if it had been waiting for exactly this light to show itself. Low-walled, turf-roofed, a curl of smoke standing straight up from its chimney in the dead air. A shepherd's hut, or what passed for one out here — the kind of place that existed on maps only as a smudge, a pen-stroke someone hadn't bothered to erase.

She hammered on the door with her forearm, because her fists were no longer hands.

It opened on a woman in her sixties with a face like a riverbed — deep-cut, dry-looking, all of it carved by things that had once been violent and were now simply terrain. Behind her, lamplight. Behind the lamplight, and this was the thing that made Jude's knees finally give, was a cot, and on the cot, propped on one elbow, gray-faced and bandaged and unmistakably alive, was Elias.

"You," Jude said. It was all she could produce. Her voice came out cracked down the middle, like ice underfoot.

The woman — Maren, she said, just Maren, no surname offered — got an arm under Jude's shoulder and hauled her over the threshold with a strength that had nothing to do with kindness and everything to do with practice. "Frostbite's honest work," she said, peeling off Jude's gloves. "It tells you exactly how far you went. Sit. Don't touch anything warm yet."

Jude sat. The room swam. There was a stove, and a pot of something barley-heavy breathing steam, and a shelf of jars catching the lamplight like small amber planets, and Elias, watching her from the cot with eyes that looked like they'd been open for a very long time.

"Brannic let me bleed for about twenty minutes," he said. His voice was sanded down to gravel. "Then his own men got nervous about leaving a Fund auditor as a corpse on provincial land. Paperwork, you understand. They radioed down to the valley." He paused. "Maren's people found me before the helicopter did."

"Maren's people," Jude repeated. Her tongue was thick. She was beginning to feel her fingers again, which meant feeling them hurt, which felt like a promotion.

"The Correns," Elias said. And when she only stared: "Tam's people. Tam was the last of them keeping the cave. The ledger names his grandmother's grandmother. The smuggler's daughter — the one recorded in the margin, the one who carried the accounts out the night her father was taken." He shifted on the cot and winced. "They've known what happened for eighty years, Jude. They've just never had proof anyone outside the family would believe."

Maren set a bowl in front of Jude and stood over her until she ate, which was its own kind of mercy, the unglamorous kind. The broth hit her stomach and radiated outward like a struck bell. Outside, the wind had dropped to almost nothing; the silence after three days of white roar was so complete she could hear the stove ticking, hear the sheep shifting in their fold, hear Elias breathing — shallow on the left side, where a collector's boot had found him, but steady.

"They're saying the pass is closed," Maren said. "Officially. Snowpack over the col, another system coming Thursday." She looked at Jude with those riverbed eyes. "But the archive in Vellmouth closes for winter on Friday. After that, nothing files until thaw. Nothing moves until thaw. If your paper isn't in the record by Friday noon, it's just a story again. Another eighty years of a story."

Jude looked down at the ledger, which she had laid on the table between them, water-stained at one corner, salt-rimed, its canvas cover scuffed nearly soft. Eighty years old and it had crossed the range in a blizzard in the pocket of a woman three payments from ruin.

"How far to Vellmouth?" she asked.

"By road? Four hours." Maren's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "But the Fund owns the road. They'll have the bridge checkpoint watched — they already called ahead, asking after strangers. You'd be stopped by dark." She tapped the table once, decisively. "There's another way. Old drover track along the river. My brother runs supplies up it when the pass shuts. It's ugly, it's slow, and it comes out behind the archive itself, through the mill yard." A beat. "We leave at four in the morning."

Elias started to sit up. Maren pushed him back down with one hand, the gesture of a woman who had been moving livestock and stubborn relatives her whole life.

"I'm coming," he said.

"You're coming," Jude said, "in a body bag, if you try to walk four hours on a bruised lung in a valley wind."

"Then I'll ride." He said it with the flat certainty of a man doing arithmetic, and Jude realized, with a lurch somewhere under her ribs, that he probably could ride — wedged into a supply cart among feed sacks, counting the bumps, auditing the route. That was Elias. He counted everything. Even his own survival, apparently, he filed under costs he was willing to pay.

She wanted to tell him what it had been like, walking away from him in the snow. She wanted to say *I looked back eleven times* or some true and terrible thing. But her hands were wrapped in Maren's salve and cloth, and the words wouldn't come out in any order worth saying, and maybe that was fine. Maybe some things kept better unsaid, packed away like the ledger, carried rather than opened, until the right room existed to open them in.

She slept four hours on the floor by the stove, the ledger zipped inside her coat even in sleep, one bound wrist resting on it like a hand on a sleeping animal.

* * *

They left in the blue-black hour before dawn, when the cold has teeth but hasn't yet decided to use them.

Maren's brother Doss drove the cart — a low-slung thing pulled by two shaggy ponies with ice in their fetlocks, loaded with barrels and feed and, tucked amid the sacks, a bandaged auditor who had insisted on bringing the Fund's own incident reports from the cave operation, because, as he put it, "the ledger proves the crime, but the paperwork proves they knew they were proving it." Jude rode beside Doss on the bench, wrapped in a horse blanket, her bandaged hands tucked into her armpits, watching the river come up alongside them — black water, fast under its lace of new ice, running the same direction they were, toward the sea, toward the coast, toward the cave where all of it had started with a tide that gave something back.

The drover track was everything Maren promised: ugly, slow, and empty. Twice they passed ruins — roofless stone, a collapsed kiln — the bones of a trade that had run these valleys long before banks and funds and smiling men named Brannic. Doss talked the whole way, unprompted, in the easy monologue of a man who drove alone too much: which families had sold out to the Fund, which had held, which fields had gone back to gorse. When Jude asked, carefully, why his family had held, he shrugged and said, "Grandmother said we were owed. Said someday somebody'd come over the mountain with the paper. Took eighty years. We figured the interest alone made it worth the wait."

Jude laughed — actually laughed, a short, cracked sound that startled the ponies — and then pressed her blanket-wrapped hands against her mouth, because laughing felt like something she hadn't earned yet.

Vellmouth assembled itself out of the river fog around ten o'clock: slate roofs, a church spire, the long shed-roof of the mill yard, and beyond it, squat and granite and lit with institutional yellow windows, the provincial archive. Smoke rose from its chimney. Lights burned on the second floor. Friday morning. Hours to spare, if the mill yard passage held.

Doss took the cart in through the yard gate with the confidence of a man delivering flour, nodded to a gatekeeper who clearly owed the Corren family more favors than he could count, and waved them toward a side door marked RECORDS — DELIVERIES.

Inside, the archive smelled the way all such places smell: dust, cold paper, floor wax, and time. A clerk named Ostrey — young, wire-rimmed, with the wary competence of someone who processes other people's emergencies daily — met them in the vestibule, took one look at Jude's frostbitten hands and Elias's bandages, and said, "You're the ones from the cave."

It wasn't a question. Word had traveled faster than they had. Maren's phone calls, presumably, and the helicopter pilots' radios, and the fish-market gossip that had followed Jude since Foothold Town. Everyone on both sides of the mountain seemed to know the story except, still, the record itself.

Ostrey led them to a reading room with a scarred oak table and a single window going gray-white with snow. He explained the filing requirements with bureaucratic tenderness: provenance affidavit, chain-of-custody declaration, certified copies, original sealed. The forms were carbon-triplicate, 1980s vintage, and required information in boxes that did not exist for situations like theirs.

Jude filled them out anyway. Her hands barely worked; she gripped the pen in a fist and wrote in letters that slanted downhill like a scree slope. *Acquired:* she wrote in the provenance box, and then, because the truth was stranger than any category, *recovered from a sea cave during an anomalous tide, from the custody of the deceased keeper, Tam Corren.* In the box marked *depositor* she wrote her own name — Jude Vasquez — and below it, in smaller letters, *with Elias Rehn, witness,* and felt the strange electric jolt of putting his name on government paper next to hers, the first document in eighty years to bind their two histories together instead of setting them against each other.

Elias dictated from the cot they'd dragged in — his contribution was the dates, cross-referenced against the Fund's internal memoranda, the purchase orders for the hanging-day "security services," the disbursement that matched, to the shilling, the cargo value recorded in the ledger's final entry. Watching him work, feverish and gray and utterly precise, Jude understood that this was his confession, delivered in the only language he trusted. Not words. Numbers. Numbers that could not be bought back.

At 11:47, Ostrey stamped the last page, sealed the original ledger in a buffered archival folder, entered it into the register, and slid the accession slip across the table.

Jude picked it up. It was a small gray slip of cardstock, an accession number, a date, a name. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Outside, the snow had begun again — fat, slow flakes coming down through the mill-yard light, the season's real snowfall arriving, as promised, to seal the pass behind them. It didn't matter anymore. What needed crossing had crossed. The truth was indoors now, catalogued, numbered, filed under a roof the Fund didn't own, in a building that would stand until spring and long past it.

Elias reached over and touched the accession slip where it lay in her bandaged hand, one finger, lightly, the way you test whether something is real.

"There's a hearing," he said quietly. "Provincial review board convenes when the roads open. They'll want depositions. They'll want—" he hesitated, choosing the word like a man stepping onto thin ice, "—they'll want us. Both of us. Under oath."

Jude looked at the snow coming down over Vellmouth, at the yellow archive windows behind them, at the slip of cardstock that was, as of 11:47 that morning, the law's own memory of a murdered man.

"And if they ask me what I'd have taken to make it go away," she said, "what I'd have sold it for?"

Ostrey, clearing his stamps away, answered before Elias could: "They always ask that." He smiled, small and tired. "And the answer's always the same. *Nothing. It was never for sale.*"

The snow thickened against the window. Somewhere south, past the closed pass, past the drowned cave, past the smiling offices of the Marrow Fund, a summons was already being drafted with their names on it — and for the first time in her life, Jude Vasquez found she was not running from the paper.

She was walking toward it.
