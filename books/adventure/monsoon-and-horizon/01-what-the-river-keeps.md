## 1. What the River Keeps

The Kestrel River had a way of apologizing after it hurt you. It would flood your camp, take your gear, maybe take a friend, and then, a few days later, when the water dropped and turned that apologetic shade of green-glass calm, it would leave something on the banks. A canoe paddle. A child's boot. Once, memorably, an entire piano, upright and somehow in tune, which Sefa had played badly for an hour before they'd both agreed never to speak of it again.

Jonah Amberdale had learned to walk the flood line the morning after every high water. Not for salvage exactly — he'd stopped calling it that after the licensing board made the word sound like a crime — but because the river's apologies were sometimes worth more than the work he was allowed to do.

Which was, officially, nothing. The blacklist didn't come with paperwork. It came with a phone that stopped ringing, a name that got quieter in booking offices, and a slow education in which outfitters would still sell rope to a man with his face. He guided crossings now — the kind that didn't appear on any manifest. Farmers' daughters who needed to reach a clinic over the pass. A widower moving his late wife's things to a valley where nobody asked questions. People the system had mislaid, same as him.

So when the river went down that third morning of thaw, Jonah pulled on his waders and walked the silt like a man reading mail.

The chest sat wedged in the roots of a drowned alder, half-buried, patient as a cat that knows it's already been fed. It was iron-banded cedar, the kind of construction you didn't see anymore unless someone was paying for it, and the water had scoured everything around it clean except the thing itself, as if the river had carried it a hundred miles just to set it down neatly.

"Hello," Jonah said, because talking to objects was one of the few habits the blacklist hadn't managed to price out of him.

The lock was bronze and it was not rusted. That was the first wrong thing. Forty years, if the chest had been in the water forty years, and the mechanism moved under his thumb like it had been oiled last week. The second wrong thing was the seal.

It sat centered on the lid, pressed into wax the color of dried blood: a mountain split by a falling star, ringed by nine stars. Jonah knew it. That was the third wrong thing, and by far the worst, because there was no reason on this earth for him to know it. He'd never seen it on a flag or a freight crate or a museum placard. He knew it the way you know a face from a dream — from the letters. His grandfather's letters, the ones wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboards of a house Jonah hadn't entered in six years.

He crouched there a long moment, water seeping cold through his waders, and let himself hope, briefly and dishonestly, that he was mistaken.

He wasn't. He pried the lid.

Inside, packed in wool gone gray with age but dry — *dry*, impossibly, insultingly dry — lay a document case of oiled leather, a brass instrument he didn't recognize, and a small tin that rattled. He opened the case first. The paper inside was heavy stock, cream-colored, written in a hand so formal it looked printed:

*By authority of the Provisional Government of Vhaloria, in its final lawful session, be it known that the treasury, archive, and patrimony of the nation are deeded unto those who fled her waters, and unto their heirs entire, recoverable by claim and proof of blood at the place beneath the Meridian ice...*

The rest was coordinates. And a name, signed with a flourish that had clearly been practiced in front of a mirror: **Edric Amberdale, Minister of the Interior.**

Jonah sat down in the mud.

His grandfather's name. His grandfather's title. Attached to a country that did not exist.

* * *

Everyone knew about Vhaloria the way everyone knew about the hole in a tooth — as an absence, something your tongue kept returning to. There had been a valley once, far upriver past the border markers, where maps now showed nothing but glacial meltwater and the words HYDROGRAPHIC SURVEY in small confident type. Forty years ago the nation had drowned — dammed, flooded, dissolved, depending on who told it — and its people had scattered, and then, with a thoroughness Jonah had always found vaguely sinister, it had been erased. No ruins preserved. No memorial. Textbooks gave it a sentence, if that. Ask an official about Vhaloria and you got the polite blank look of a person checking whether you were about to become their problem.

And Edric Amberdale — gruff, silent, Sunday-radio Edric, who had taught Jonah to read water and to whistle through cupped hands and almost nothing else — had signed himself Minister of the Interior of the place that wasn't.

Jonah rowed back to his cabin with the chest lashed in the bow like a passenger, and spent the evening not opening the tin.

This was a discipline. He made tea. He split kindling he didn't need. He reorganized a tackle box that had been organized since 2019. Outside, the river ran low and green and innocent, and Jonah understood, with the particular clarity of a man who has spent his life reading currents, that the river had not given him a gift. Rivers don't give gifts. Rivers deliver things to where they're going, and this chest had been going to him — or to anyone with his blood — for forty years, and every year it waited had made it heavier.

Finally, near midnight, he opened the tin.

Inside were three things. A key, small and iron, wrapped in waxed thread. A photograph: a group of perhaps thirty people on a dock, dressed in the plain dark clothes of refugees, all looking at the camera with expressions Jonah could only describe as *resigned defiance*. On the back, in pencil: *The last session, before the water.* And his grandfather was there — younger than Jonah had ever seen him, thirty maybe, standing slightly apart from the others, holding a ledger against his chest the way you hold a shield.

And a note, in Edric's hand but plainer this time, hurried, the practiced flourish abandoned:

*If you are reading this, the river has done its work and you have my blood, which means you also have my mistakes. Do not trust the fleet men. Do not trust the brokers. Trust the tide tables and no one who profits from forgetting. What is under the ice will either save our family or damn it, and I could not bear to choose which, so I have left the choosing to you.*

Jonah read it four times. Then he sat on his porch steps with his tea going cold and looked at the mountains to the north, where Glacier Meridian hung above the tree line like a held breath, and felt the wary curiosity of the afternoon harden into something with weight and edges.

Dread, basically. Dread with a schedule.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't avoid: chests did not float for forty years and surface by accident, sealed and dry, the week after the largest spring flood in a decade. Someone had timed that release. Someone had wanted it found *now* — or wanted it found by whoever was quick enough, and Jonah had a bad feeling that he was not the only person doing this arithmetic tonight. Somewhere downstream, probably, other men were walking other flood lines, and some of them had radios, funding, and no blacklist slowing them down.

And underneath the dread, unwelcome as a stone in a boot, was the other thing. The thing he resented himself for feeling.

Purpose. Actual purpose, arriving like the first warmth after a long winter, and aimed directly at the grandfather he'd spent six years refusing to forgive. Edric Amberdale had died with his secrets intact and his grandson estranged, and now he was reaching up out of forty years of silence to hand Jonah a mystery, a mission, and a moral problem the size of a glacier.

"You couldn't have just left me the whistling trick," Jonah told the mountains.

The mountains, being mountains, declined to answer.

He went inside, pried up the floorboard in the corner of the kitchen — the house was rented now, but the landlord asked no questions of a man who paid in cash — and unwrapped the oilcloth bundle he'd sworn six years ago never to touch. His grandfather's letters, forty of them, coded in the old guide's cipher Edric had taught him as a boy, back when teaching things was still how they loved each other.

He spread them across the table under lamplight, and began, with hands that were steadier than he expected, to decipher.

The first letter was dated two years before the flood. The coordinates in the margin matched the ones on the deed.

Under the ice. Beneath Glacier Meridian. His grandfather had put the truth of a drowned country somewhere only the tide tables could reach — and Jonah had until calving season to get there first.

Outside, the Kestrel ran quiet, keeping whatever else it kept.
