## 2. The Letters Under the Floor

The chest sat on Jonah's kitchen table for two days before he could bring himself to open it again.

Not because of the seal. He'd gotten past the seal — mostly. It was the smell. River water and old iron and something underneath both, something like a church that had been underwater a very long time. His cabin was small enough that the smell had opinions about where it wanted to live. It had chosen the curtains.

He'd tried everything. Boiled the chest's exterior twice. Scrubbed it with pine soap until his knuckles cracked. The smell remained, patient as a tenant who'd signed a lease.

"You're being ridiculous," he told himself on the third morning, standing in his underwear with tea going cold in one hand. "It's a box. Boxes don't haunt people."

But this one had been trying to.

He set the tea down, unlatched the lid again, and lifted out the contents properly this time: an oilcloth bundle, dry inside despite forty years and a river's best efforts, tied with cord that crumbled at the second knot. Inside the oilcloth lay a document — heavy paper, dense as cloth, stamped twice with that impossible crest. A mountain split by a wave, crowned with three stars. Vhaloria. The country that wasn't.

And beneath the stamp, in ink that had gone brown but not faded, a name.

*Elias Amberdale. Minister of Interior. By authority of the Provisional Government-in-Exile.*

Jonah read it four times. Each time, the words rearranged themselves into something worse.

His grandfather hadn't just been *from* Vhaloria. His grandfather had been *government*.

* * *

Jonah was nine years old the first time he understood there were letters under the floor.

He'd been hunting for a dropped marble — blue swirl, his favorite, lost during an argument about whether it counted as cheating to roll it between your knees. He pried up the loose board by the cold stove, the one Grandpa Elias always said was "structural" in a tone that meant *don't*, and found not a marble but a tin box, and in the tin box, letters. Dozens. Thin paper, folded small, addressed in a cramped hand to no one.

He'd taken one upstairs and read it at the kitchen table while Elias mended nets in the corner, and the letter was nonsense. Numbers where words should be. Words where numbers should be. Something about *the tide remembers what the shore denies*, which sounded like poetry until you realized it was instructions.

"What's this?" he'd asked, holding it up.

Elias had looked at him for a long moment — long enough that Jonah's ears went hot — and then said, mildly, "That's your inheritance. Put it back."

"It's gibberish."

"Most inheritances are." And then, because Elias Amberdale never let a lesson go unhooked: "You'll want the key someday, boy. Everyone wants the key eventually. The trick is deciding whether you want what it opens."

Nine-year-old Jonah had put the letter back, retrieved his marble from under the icebox instead, and thought no more about it — except that he thought about it constantly, the way you think about a locked room in your own house.

* * *

Now, thirty years later, Jonah spread the letters across the same table, in the same cabin, and wished desperately for the marble instead.

There were sixty-one letters. He knew because he counted them twice, the way you count things when counting is easier than feeling. They spanned nineteen years. The early ones were almost playful in their cipher — a substitution keyed to river stages, flood levels, the Kestrel's own moods. *High water* meant a category of person. *Freeze-up* meant danger. *Breakup* meant hope, or money, or both.

The later ones changed. The cipher deepened. And the handwriting — Jonah knew that handwriting better than his own; he'd learned his letters tracing it — grew smaller, tighter, like a man writing against a closing door.

He worked through the morning, using the decoder he'd finally built himself at fourteen out of spite: a card with the river-stage numbers punched through it, kept all these years in a boot box labeled, in his own teenage handwriting, *GIBBERISH (IMPORTANT)*.

He'd been a strange child.

By noon he had coordinates.

They weren't complicated once the key turned. Latitude and longitude, repeated across eleven letters with small variations, converging on a single point. He plotted them on the old survey chart tacked above his stove — the one with Glacier Meridian inked across its shoulder like a scar.

The point sat beneath the ice. Not near it. *Beneath.* Two kilometers inland from the calving face, under four hundred meters of glacier that had been grinding forward since before either of them was born.

An archive. It had to be. The deed in the chest spoke of records held in trust, of a treasury deeded to refugees, of documents "preserved at depth against erasure." Preserved at depth. In a country that had drowned itself.

Jonah sat back and laughed — one short, ugly bark that startled him more than the smell had.

"He hid a nation under a glacier," he said to the empty kitchen. "Of course he did. Why would he do anything sensible."

* * *

Flashback, though Jonah didn't have a word for it then. Just memory, arriving without permission:

He was twenty-two, home for his mother's funeral, and he'd asked the question he'd been saving for years. They stood on the porch in the blue hour, and Jonah said, "What did you do, Grandpa? During the sinking? What did you actually do?"

Elias didn't answer right away. He was looking at the river, which was doing what the river always did, which was keep things.

"I was the Minister of Interior," he said at last. "You know that."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Elias agreed. "It isn't." And then he'd gone inside, and Jonah had stood on the porch hating him with a hatred so clean it felt almost holy, and had not come back except twice, briefly, before the funeral that wasn't a funeral because Elias had left instructions that there be none — *no stone, no service, scatter nothing, I've already been scattered* — and Jonah had obeyed, because obedience was the only inheritance they'd ever exchanged comfortably.

He'd spent fifteen years telling himself the resentment was simple. The old man was a coward, or a bureaucrat, or worse — some gray functionary of a drowned thing who couldn't even give his grandson a straight sentence. Blacklisted guides know how to hold a grudge; it's load-bearing.

It was not simple. Standing at the table now, surrounded by sixty-one letters from a man who had spent two decades encoding coordinates into the language of a river — patiently, obsessively, in handwriting getting smaller and smaller — Jonah felt the grudge crack down the middle like breakup ice.

Because this was not the work of a man hiding.

This was the work of a man *leaving a trail*. For someone. Maybe, God help him, for Jonah specifically.

* * *

The eleventh letter was different from the others. Shorter. The cipher was the old simple one, the child's version, as if he'd wanted it readable by anyone stubborn enough to try.

Jonah decoded it standing up, heart going like a net drum.

*To whoever the river delivers this: the claim is real. The deed is real. What is buried under Meridian is real, and it is heavier than treasure. Bring proof of line or proof of purpose. Come before the sixth tide of the melt season. After that, the corridor closes, and I am sorry for what I made you carry.*

*I am sorry for most of it.*

— E.A.

Jonah read it three times. Then he did what any reasonable man would do with a message from a dead grandfather hidden under a floorboard and delivered by a flood: he put the kettle on, because the alternative was putting his head through a wall.

The kettle had just begun its low complaint when he heard the engine.

Not Sefa's engine — he'd have known hers asleep, drunk, or dead. This was a big motor, steady, expensive, coming up the Kestrel slow the way people come up rivers slow when they're looking at the banks instead of the channel.

Jonah killed his own lamp and went to the window.

Two men in a salvage skiff, new hull, government-gray, with a crane arm folded flat along the gunwale. The kind of boat that showed up after floods in exactly the way vultures show up after calving. One of them wore a headset and was talking to somebody invisible. The other was younger, wide through the shoulders, and scanning the shoreline with the particular patience of a man being paid by the find.

They moored at his landing without asking, which on the Kestrel was either courtesy or a threat, and with these two it was clearly a threat wearing courtesy's jacket.

Jonah opened the door before they knocked. Better footing that way.

"Afternoon," said the older one. He had a broker's smile — warm at the edges, arithmetic in the middle. "Beautiful stretch of water. We're running debris recovery downstream of the washout. Flood must have pushed a lot of material through here."

"River keeps what it keeps," Jonah said.

"Does it, though." The smile sharpened a degree. "We've had reports of a sealed container coming through this reach. Chest-sized. Marked. You see anything like that?"

Jonah let the silence sit for exactly as long as it took to become an answer and not a moment longer. "I see driftwood. I see a dead canoe every spring. I see whatever the river feels like showing me."

"And if it showed you a chest?"

"Then I'd show it the same respect I show everything else it spits up." He hooked a thumb toward the woodshed, casual as breathing. "Burn pile's back there. Most of it ends up kindling."

The younger one shifted, and Jonah watched him do it — watched the weight move to the balls of his feet, watched his eyes flick past Jonah's shoulder toward the cabin windows, doing the math on curtains and light. Behind those curtains, on the kitchen table, sat sixty-one decoded letters and a deed bearing a dead nation's seal, and Jonah's whole body was a held breath.

"Name's Corvane," the older one said, producing a card as if from nowhere. "Merrick Corvane. Meridian Recovery Group." The card was heavy stock, black, embossed with a wave-and-mountain mark that was *almost* the Vhalorian crest the way a counterfeit bill is almost currency. "If anything surfaces — anything at all — there's good money in it. Finders' fees. No questions."

"There's always questions," Jonah said, taking the card anyway, because refusing it would have been louder than taking it.

Corvane smiled his arithmetic smile. "Fair. Well. Keep an eye on your banks, Mr.—?"

"Amberdale."

Something crossed the broker's face then, quick as a fish under ice — there and gone, but Jonah saw it. Recognition, or its cousin. The name meant something to these men. Which meant the name meant something to whoever sent them.

"Amberdale," Corvane repeated, tasting it. "Well. Good luck with the season, Mr. Amberdale. Water's rising fast this year. Six tides, they say, before the narrows choke." He said it lightly, conversationally, the way a man mentions weather — and it landed in Jonah's chest like a hook finding flesh, because *six tides* was exactly what the eleventh letter had said, word for word, cipher for plain.

They cast off. The skiff slid downstream, quiet as money, and Jonah stood on his landing until the sound of the engine dissolved into the river's ordinary noise.

Then he went inside, latched the door, and looked at the table full of his grandfather's life's work, and at the black card in his hand, and understood with total clarity that the race had already started, and he was losing.

Six tides. Corvane knew. Whoever Corvane answered to knew. Which meant the only question left was the one Elias had asked him on this porch twenty years ago, the one Jonah had walked away from hating him:

Did he want what the key opened?

Jonah looked at the chart on the wall, at the point inked beneath the glacier, and felt something he hadn't felt since before the blacklist — not greed, not grief, but *purpose*, sharp and terrifying and alive, thrashing like a live catch in the bottom of a boat.

He needed a pilot. There was exactly one pilot worth the name on this river, and she hated him with impressive craftsmanship.

Jonah got out a pencil and started drafting the message he'd send her, crossing out his first three attempts, because some reconciliations take more than one cast.
