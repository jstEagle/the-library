## 3. Six Tides

The tide tables at the Kestrel ferry office were posted on a board so sun-bleached that the numbers had gone the color of old bone, and Jonah Amberdale had been staring at them for the better part of an hour without buying anything, which the woman behind the counter had begun to notice.

"You want a ticket or a divorce from those numbers?" she asked, refilling her coffee.

"Neither," Jonah said. "I want to know who maintains them."

"Harbormaster does. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"He copies them off the ice service bulletins and adds a margin of error." She tapped the board. "His margin of error is a prayer."

Jonah did his own arithmetic against the bulletins he'd read that morning at the library — the ones anyone could read, though almost nobody bothered, because the upper Kestrel was a dead route now, blacklisted for commercial traffic since the Meridian calving scare three years back. Six tides. That was what the math kept giving him no matter how he bent it. Six more navigable tides through the narrowing corridors before the chokepoints at Sorrow Narrows and the Weeping Gate closed for the season, and after that, nothing but a wall of compressed ice from here to the glacier's face until spring thaw.

Six tides to reach a drowned nation before a salvage fleet did.

He needed a pilot. He needed one specific pilot, in fact, which was a problem, because the last time he'd seen Sefa Okonkwo she had thrown a wrench at him. Not *at* him, exactly — she'd have hit him if it were *at* him — but near enough that the message had been received in full.

Her boatyard sat at the elbow where the Kestrel bent south, a sprawl of tarps and hulls in various states of dying, and Jonah stood at the gate for a long moment rehearsing openings. *Hello.* Too weak. *I owe you an apology.* True, but she'd hang up on it. *I need your help.* Honest, and also exactly the wrong thing to lead with, because Sefa Okonkwo could smell being needed the way sharks smelled blood, and she'd charge accordingly.

In the end he went with the truth, delivered sideways.

"I brought your wrench back," he said, holding it up as he walked into the yard.

Sefa was under a skiff, legs out, boots crossed. She didn't come out. "That's not my wrench."

"It's got your initials scratched in the handle."

"That's because I scratch my initials in everything I own, including things people steal from me." A pause, then her head emerged, dark hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of inner tube. She looked at him the way you look at weather you'd hoped had passed. "Amberdale."

"Sefa."

"You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy," Jonah said, and immediately regretted it, because there hadn't been another guy; there had just been two years of bad crossings and worse sleep, and she knew it, and pretending otherwise was beneath both of them.

She slid out from under the skiff and stood, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime around the previous equinox. "Let me guess. You're in trouble."

"When am I not."

"No, this is different. You've got the face." She pointed at him. "The face you get when you've found something you can't put down. Last time you had that face, we lost the *Maren* to the revenue cutters and I spent nine months paying it off. So before you say one more word: what is it?"

Jonah set the wrench on her workbench and told her. Not all of it — he kept the letters back, kept the grandfather back, kept the part where the country that never existed turned out to be the country his family had helped drown. But he told her about the chest, the seal, the claim deed, the coordinates under Glacier Meridian. And he watched her face do something complicated, because Sefa's grandmother had come down out of the Vhalorian resettlement camps too, though she'd never once said the word *Vhaloria* in Jonah's hearing, the way you don't say a name that was taken away from you.

"The salvage fleet's already fitting out at Corren Dock," he finished. "Three hulls, maybe four by departure. Sonar drones, dive support, the works. They'll be through the first narrows inside two days."

"And you want to beat them in what, exactly? The *Kestrel Queen*?" She named his boat with the affection of someone naming a sick animal. "She draws too much for the upper channels. You know that."

"I know that."

"You know I know that."

"I know you know that I know that," Jonah said, "which is why I'm standing in your yard instead of loading her."

Sefa laughed despite herself — a short, sharp sound, quickly folded away. She walked to the end of the yard where the water showed between the hulls, and Jonah followed, and for a moment they just looked at the river together, brown-green and patient, carrying whatever it carried.

"Six tides," she said.

"By my count."

"Your count's optimistic. Harbormaster's count is a prayer. My count says five and a half, and the half is the one that kills you." She turned. "Why me, Jonah? There are other pilots. Cheaper ones. Ones who don't hate you."

"Do they hate me?"

"Some of them. You got people blacklisted, remember? Half the upper-river guides can't take a paying client because of what you pulled."

"I know what I cost people." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "You most of all."

Something moved across her face — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but the place where forgiveness would go if it ever decided to arrive. "Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that I'm asking anyway. Because you're the best pilot on this river, and because you read water like other people read books, and because if I'm going to die in a narrows I'd rather it be with someone who'll tell me honestly it was my fault." He paused. "And because your grandmother's name might be in that archive. And I think you already know that, and I think it's why you haven't thrown me out yet."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. Then she picked up the wrench from the workbench, weighed it in her hand, and held it out to him handle-first.

"Keep it," she said. "You'll need it more than me where you're going. Conditions?"

"Name them."

"One: I navigate, you guide. On the water, my word is final. If I say beach, we beach. If I say turn back, we turn back, and you don't argue, because arguing with me in moving water is how boats die."

"Agreed."

"Two: whatever's in that archive, I get to see it. All of it. No editing for my protection, no protecting me from my own history. My grandmother earned the right to be remembered however she gets remembered."

"Agreed," Jonah said, and felt the weight of it settle somewhere under his ribs, because he wasn't sure he could keep that promise to himself, let alone to her.

"Three." She held up a third finger. "When this is over — if this is over — you tell me the whole story. The blacklist. What you actually did. Not the version you gave the inquiry board. The real one. And I decide whether we're partners or strangers afterward."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's the minimum." She stuck out her hand. "But it's the deal."

They shook on it, and her grip was dry and strong and brief, and neither of them smiled, because there was nothing in it yet to smile about — only a bargain between two people who trusted each other's competence completely and each other's character not at all, which, Jonah reflected, was how most working partnerships on the Kestrel had always been built.

* * *

They spent the afternoon fitting out the *Kestrel Queen*, and it went the way all their old work sessions used to go, which is to say Sefa insulted his knots and he insulted her engine maintenance and both insults were accurate and neither was taken personally, and somewhere in the middle of re-caulking the forward hatch Jonah realized he had missed this — not her, exactly, or not only her, but the particular rhythm of two people who knew the same boat so well they could argue in shorthand.

"Fuel," Sefa said, ticking items on a scrap of tarpaper. "Water. Cold rations — nothing that needs cooking past the second narrows, the stove's a fire risk in the ice fog. Ropes, pitons, the good headlamps, not the ones you bought at the ferry office like a tourist. Dive gear if we can beg it off anyone, which we can't, so plan on swimming cold."

"How cold?"

"Cold enough that you'll wish you hadn't asked." She wrote *wetsuits??* and underlined it twice. "And charts. Real ones, not the harbor office copies."

"I have charts."

"You have *my* charts, don't you. From the *Maren*. The ones that went missing."

"They went missing in a spirit of preservation."

"They went missing in a spirit of theft, Jonah." But she said it almost fondly, which he chose to interpret as progress.

It was full dark when they finished, and they were eating cold rice on the deck of the *Kestrel Queen* when Sefa stopped chewing.

"What," Jonah said.

She didn't answer. She was looking downriver, toward the bend, where the water caught the lights of something moving. Then the sound reached them — engines, several, running heavy and low, the way loaded hulls run — and running dark, which no legal traffic did on this stretch anymore.

"Corren Dock," Sefa said quietly. "That's them, isn't it. Your salvage fleet."

Jonah counted lights. Four. Maybe five. "They weren't supposed to depart until tomorrow night."

"They were never going to wait for your tide tables, Jonah. People like that make their own weather." She set down her bowl. "Question is whether we leave tonight on a half-loaded boat or tomorrow morning knowing they've got a tide and a half on us."

Jonah looked at the dark water, at the running lights sliding past the bend like a procession, and felt the six tides shrink in his chest to something smaller and harder.

"We leave tonight," he said.

Sefa was already moving. "Then stop eating and start coiling," she said. "And Jonah — pray to whatever you pray to that the harbormaster's margin of error runs generous."

He threw the lines while she took the wheel, and the *Kestrel Queen* slipped out into the current with her running lights still dark, chasing a fleet she had no business chasing, into the first of six tides that were already, quietly, becoming five.
