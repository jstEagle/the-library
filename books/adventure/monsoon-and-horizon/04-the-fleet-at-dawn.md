## 4. The Fleet at Dawn

Sefa Okonkwo had a rule about dawn: it was the hour when the river told the truth. Fog stripped away pretense, light came in honest and gray, and whatever you'd been lying to yourself about all night stood there on the bank, waiting to be acknowledged.

This morning, the river was telling her she was an idiot.

She crouched at the stern of her own boat — *her* boat, the *Marrow*, which Jonah had talked his way aboard of with nothing but a wax-stamped chest and that look he got, the one like a dog who knows he's been bad but has decided the ball is worth it — and watched three salvage vessels come up the Kestrel in formation, running lights still burning against the mist.

Three. She'd counted two last night and allowed herself to hope.

"Jonah," she said, not turning around, because if she turned around she'd have to look at him, and looking at him before coffee was how arguments started. "How many engines does the fleet have?"

"Combined? I stopped counting at forty."

"Good answer. Wrong question." She squinted through the fog. The lead vessel was the *Corvus Halcyon*, she was almost sure of it — the silhouette was wrong for anything else, that absurd forward crane like a heron's neck. "That's Havelock's flagship. That means the broker himself is aboard, or close enough to smell."

"You can smell a broker?"

"You can smell money deciding what you're worth. It's a distinct odor. Like copper and cologne."

Behind her, Jonah made a sound that might have been laughter or might have been a man swallowing his own dread whole. Sefa had known him eleven years, and in that time she had catalogued exactly four sounds he made instead of laughing: the swallow, the grunt, the cough-that-isn't, and once, memorably, a full sentence delivered entirely in falsetto when a bear got into their supply cache outside Tarn Hollow. She filed this morning's noise under swallow.

The plan had been simple, which should have been their first warning. Simple plans were what you made when you were tired, and they had both been tired for years. Run the first narrows at slack water, ahead of the fleet, use the *Marrow*'s shallow draft to slip the channel the big boats couldn't. By the time Havelock's people realized where they'd gone, Jonah and Sefa would be two tides ahead and smug about it.

Instead, the fleet had run the narrows in the dark, lights doused, hulls wrapped in bumpers, guided by something Sefa didn't have a name for yet but suspected was either very good local knowledge or very expensive bribery. They'd come through the chokepoint while Sefa slept in the *Marrow*'s cabin with one boot off, and now they were below her, between her and the open corridor, spread across the channel like a hand dealt face-up.

"They're holding position," Jonah said, climbing up beside her. He moved quietly; he always did, a habit from guiding, from years of learning that loud men fell into crevasses. "Why would they hold position?"

"Because they know we're behind them, and they're going to make us come to them." Sefa chewed the inside of her cheek. "Or because they're waiting for daylight to launch those drones."

"Drones."

"Salvage sonar drones. Little torpedo-shaped things with listening arrays. Havelock's outfit runs them in pairs. They sweep a channel, map every hull signature bigger than a canoe, and ping back coordinates." She'd seen them work once, two seasons ago, from a respectful distance, watching them comb a wreck site off the estuary like patient metal fish. "They'll find us by midmorning if we stay here."

"So we go around."

"Through the forest? With a boat?" She gave him the look. The look had a long history. It had ended arguments, begun others, and once, in a bar in Lowmarsh, caused a stranger to buy her a drink out of pure self-preservation. "There's no around, Jonah. There's the channel, and there's the rock. The fleet owns the channel until the next tide turns."

Jonah was quiet for a moment. Then he said, in the small voice he used when he'd already done the math and hated the result: "The sea cave."

Sefa turned around after all.

"The cave under Raven Point," he said. "Half a mile back. It floods at high tide, drains at low. Big enough for the *Marrow* if we go in flat and quiet. On their charts it's marked as a hazard — unstable overhang, no anchorage. Nobody goes in."

"Nobody goes in because half the ceiling is held up by optimism."

"Optimism has held up worse."

"That is the single most depressing true thing you've ever said." But she was already moving to the tiller, because the truth underneath her irritation was this: the cave was a good idea, and she hated that it was his, and she hated more that she'd spent three days back in his orbit feeling like a woman reading her own old letters. "Get the lines down. We go in silent. If we so much as sneeze against that rock, we're a story other guides tell."

They backed the *Marrow* out of the reed shallows and ran her downstream with the engine at idle, letting the current do the work, Sefa steering by feel and memory and the particular gray grammar of fog. Behind them the fleet sat fat and patient in the channel. Ahead, Raven Point leaned out over the water like a disapproving relative, and beneath its overhang, barely visible, the black seam of the cave mouth opened and closed with the swell.

She took them in on the back of a wave, engine cut, drifting, hands only, the hull whispering along stone. Inside, the sound changed — the river's voice dropped away and was replaced by drip and echo and the slow tidal breathing of the cavern. The ceiling arched up into darkness. Their wake lapped against walls that had never heard an engine.

"Kill the lamp," she breathed.

Darkness, total and soft. They tied off to a shelf of rock Jonah found by touch, and then there was nothing to do but wait, which was the part Sefa was worst at, and the part, she suspected, Jonah had brought her along specifically to do.

* * *

An hour past sunrise, the drones came.

She heard them before she saw anything — a thin electronic whine threading through the drip of the cave, out in the channel. Then, through the cave mouth, a pale green glow swept past like a searchlight underwater, and a pulse rolled through the stone itself. Not sound, exactly. Pressure. A question asked of the rock, and the rock answering: *empty, empty, empty.*

Except it wasn't empty. It was them, sitting in a wooden boat in a flooded sea cave, holding their breath like children playing a game whose forfeit was everything.

The sweep passed. Came back. Passed again, closer to the mouth. Each pulse made the water in the cave shiver, made droplets fall from the ceiling in little silver applause. Sefa found Jonah's sleeve in the dark and gripped it, not for comfort — she'd have bitten off her own arm first — but to keep herself anchored to something that wasn't the pulse counting her heartbeat back at her.

On the fourth pass, the drone slowed at the cave mouth.

It hung there. Green light washing faintly across the water inside, across the *Marrow*'s bow, across Jonah's face, which wore an expression Sefa had never seen on him before: not fear. Something older. Like a man listening to a voice he'd spent years pretending was gone.

Then the drone moved on, and the green went with it, and the channel belonged to the fleet again.

Sefa let her breath out slowly. "They didn't mark it."

"Charts say hazard. Their software believes charts." His voice was rough. "We're a ghost in a haunted house. Even ghosts get ignored eventually."

"Don't get poetic. Poetic Jonah makes decisions." She fished a cold biscuit out of the supply bag, broke it, handed him half without looking, because some habits survived even estrangement. They ate in the dark, listening to the fleet's engines idling out in the channel like sleeping animals.

It was Jonah who finally spoke, and Sefa knew, from the shape of the silence before it, that he'd been building up to this since the cave mouth.

"I need to tell you why the blacklist happened," he said. "The real version. Not the version where I was careless."

"I've heard the careless version. It never fit. You're many kinds of fool, but careless isn't one."

He was quiet a while. A drop of water hit the deck. Somewhere out in the channel, the *Corvus Halcyon* weighed anchor with a groan of chain that echoed in Sefa's teeth.

"Four years ago," Jonah said. "A client named Aldous Wren. Wealthy. Connected. Wanted a crossing into the Meridian headwaters in late season — the window everyone knows is suicide. I told him no. He tripled my fee. I told him no again. Then he told me he'd already hired two other guides, worse ones, and that if I wouldn't take him, they'd die trying, and their deaths would be on my conscience either way, so I might as well be paid."

"And you took him."

"I took him." In the dark his voice went flat, reciting. "Day three, he decided the weather window was closing and ordered the group to push the pass anyway. I refused. He reported me for refusing a lawful client instruction — said I'd abandoned the party, endangered lives. There was an inquiry. The other guides corroborated him, because he'd paid their fees too, and because the guild protects clients, not guides. My certification revoked. Blacklisted across every registered route in the range."

Sefa set down her biscuit. "You could have fought it."

"With what? His word against mine, times four witnesses on his payroll?" A breath. "Here's the part I've never told anyone. After the inquiry, Wren offered to fix it. One statement, one retraction, blacklist lifted. All I had to do was sign a confidentiality agreement about what actually happened on that mountain — including that he'd ordered the push that nearly killed two porters. Including that I carried one of them down on my back through whiteout."

"You signed the silence instead of taking the deal."

"I carried Tammas Rell's family to his funeral, and I signed nothing, and I stayed blacklisted, because the alternative was being paid in the currency of a man who thought lives were line items." His voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, just enough. "I chose the blacklist, Sefa. Every day since, I've wondered if I chose pride instead of a life. If staying ruined was its own kind of vanity."

The drip filled the silence. Out in the channel, the fleet began to move, one engine, then another, the sound of money going somewhere.

"Sefa," Jonah said. "Say something. Even if it's the look."

"It's not the look." Her throat was tight, which annoyed her. "You want absolution, and I'm the wrong person, because I left too. You know why I left?"

"Because I was impossible."

"Because you were impossible *and right*, which is worse." She found his shoulder in the dark and shoved it, gently, the way you shove someone you've decided to keep. "You protected a porter's memory over your own name. That's not vanity, Jonah. That's the least vain thing I've ever heard of. It was also stubborn, self-destructive, and completely predictable, and I should have stayed to argue you out of the worst parts instead of leaving you alone with them. So we're both carrying something. Fine. Put yours down for five minutes and eat your biscuit."

In the dark, he laughed — the real one, rare, unarchived. "Yes, ma'am."

"And Jonah? When we find this archive, and your grandfather's name is in it — whatever it says — you tell me the real version of that too. No recitations. I don't guide liars."

"No recitations," he agreed.

The tide turned an hour later. The cave drained around them by inches, the *Marrow* settling onto wet stone, and through the narrowing mouth Sefa watched the last hull of the fleet disappear up-channel toward the second narrows — ahead of them now, but not by much, and burning fuel they couldn't replace.

She stood at the cave mouth as the water fell, doing the math she was best at. Four tides left. Two narrows. One glacier. And somewhere ahead, a broker who thought he owned the ending of this story.

"He's running hard," she murmured. "Havelock doesn't burn fuel like that unless he's scared of losing something."

Jonah joined her at the mouth, the gray light finding his face. "Scared of losing the race?"

"No." Sefa stepped back into the cabin and started untying lines. "Scared of losing the argument. Get ready, Amberdale. Fifth tide comes early, and I intend to be embarrassing about how fast we take it."
