## 5. Country of Water

The waystation announced itself the way old things do: reluctantly, and only when you were already upon it.

Jonah saw it first as a shadow in the ice — a dark rectangle stitched into the glacier's flank, half-swallowed, like a door someone had tried to bury and given up halfway. Then Sefa saw it, and her whole posture changed.

"That's not natural," she said, cutting the outboard. The boat drifted in the green silence of the flooded channel. "That's a lintel."

"That's a door," Jonah said.

"That's a *door*," Sefa agreed, with the particular reverence she reserved for things she intended to complain about later. "In the middle of a glacier. Jonah. What kind of people put a door in a glacier?"

"People who were running," Jonah said.

The tide was with them, which meant they had perhaps three hours before the channel began to drain and the ice corridors above started grinding their teeth. He tied off at a shelf of blue ice that had heaved itself up against the entrance like a bouncer with opinions, and they climbed.

The door was metal, not wood — riveted, salt-scoured, and stamped with a crest that Jonah had now seen twice in his life and both times felt in his sternum first: the wave and the star, Vhaloria's seal, worn nearly smooth. Beside it, bolted at eye height, a small brass plaque. He wiped the frost from it with his sleeve.

*WAYSTATION SEVEN — KEPT FOR THE WANDERING. ALL WHO FLED ARE FAMILY. ALL WHO COME AFTER ARE GUESTS.*

Below that, in smaller letters, someone had added by hand, the paint still stubbornly bright after forty years: *Wipe your boots. Auntie Ivo will know.*

Sefa read it twice. "I like her already."

"She's probably dead, Sefa."

"Dead people can still know if you track mud. That's the whole point of aunts."

The lock had surrendered decades ago. The door opened on a groan that echoed down a corridor, and cold air breathed out at them — not dead air, but living, moving air, which meant the waystation was vented somehow, which meant it had been built to last. Jonah felt the hair rise on his arms. His grandfather's letters had mentioned waystations only once, in passing, the way you'd mention a staircase: *the stations along the Meridian route will hold if the ice holds.* As if they were infrastructure. As if an entire nation's escape route were a thing you could footnote.

They went in with headlamps, though they didn't need them for long.

* * *

The corridor opened into a hall the size of a chapel, and the chapel comparison was not idle, because the walls were covered in murals.

Not decoration. Jonah had seen decoration — the pretty alpine scenes wealthy clients paid him to hike past, the cheerful bears on café shuttters in the valley towns. This was something else. This was a people telling themselves a story one last time, in paint, on the walls of the last building they would ever build, and the story went like this:

On the left wall: a city on the water. White towers rising out of a bay so blue it looked invented, bridges like drawn bows, boats everywhere — a nation that had apparently considered boats less a technology than a personality. Vhaloria, before. Painted with such love that Jonah could almost hear it, the gull-cries and market noise, the bells.

The middle wall: the water rising. Not dramatically — that was the terrible part, the *patience* of it. The towers still stood, but the bridges were shorter. The boats were loaded with furniture, with cages of hens, with grandparents. And in the corner of the mural, small as a signature, a line of figures walking away across the ice with everything they owned on their backs, painted at child height, as if the painter had known children would be the ones to see it last.

The right wall: the waystation itself. Figures building it. Figures painting it. And above the door of the painted waystation, a painted banner, and on the banner, painted words in Vhalorian script that Jonah could not read — except that he could, suddenly, because his grandfather had used the same characters in his letters, the ones Jonah had spent two weeks deciphering by lamplight, and the phrase resolved in his head like a knot coming loose:

*We were never lost. We were moved.*

"Oh," Sefa said quietly. She had gone still in the way she only went still in dangerous water. "Jonah."

"I see it."

"No, I mean — look at the figures. On the right wall. The officials. Look at their faces."

He looked. Four figures stood at the center of the final panel, the ones who had organized the exodus, and the painter had given them faces — real ones, individual ones, the faces of specific human beings, because a refugee community does not paint its saviors anonymous. A tall woman with a stern mouth. A young man holding a tide chart. An old priest with his hands open.

And a broad-shouldered man with a beard going gray at the chin, standing slightly apart from the others, looking — there was no other word for it — *guilty*. Even in paint. Even in a mural meant to honor him. The painter had caught something in the set of his shoulders, the angle of his head, that said: *this man is carrying something heavier than the others.*

Beneath the four figures, in careful Vhalorian script and, underneath, in the trade tongue every mountain child learned:

*THE MINISTRY OF THE INTERIOR, WHO KEPT THE DOORS OPEN:*

*Minister Edran Amberdale.*

The name hit Jonah like the river had hit the chest — a thing surfacing that should have stayed down.

"That's your grandfather," Sefa said. Not a question. She'd seen the letters. She'd helped him burn the third one.

"Yeah."

"Your grandfather is *painted on a wall*."

"Yeah."

"As a *hero*."

Jonah didn't answer. Because the mural said hero, and the letters said something else, and the chest with the seal said something else again, and he was standing in a chapel built by the drowned with his own blood staring down at him from forty years of drying paint, and the three truths were not getting along.

* * *

They searched the back rooms because searching was something to do with their hands. The waystation had been left, not abandoned — that distinction mattered, Jonah was learning. Bunk frames stood in rows, stripped of bedding but not of order. A kitchen with pots stacked and a tin of tea leaves gone to dust. A children's room with a chalkboard still bearing half a lesson: *The tide goes out, the tide comes —* and nothing after, as if the class had simply stood up mid-sentence and walked into the rest of history.

In the last room, they found the ledger.

It sat on a desk under a stone paperweight shaped like the wave-and-star. It was a station log, kept in a neat, patient hand — arrivals, departures, rations. Auntie Ivo, presumably. Jonah read it the way you read a stranger's diary: guiltily, then helplessly, then with his throat closing.

*Day 12. The Okonkwo family came through, eleven of them. The pilot's daughter is six and has already learned to swear in three languages. I have written to her mother about it and received no reply I can print.*

Sefa made a sound. Jonah looked up. She was staring at the page.

"That's my family," she said. "That's — my grandmother was six when they came through the Meridian route. She used to tell us about a station with an auntie who fed them *actual bread*." Her voice did something complicated. "Jonah, this is my — this is my grandmother's bread."

"Your family came through here."

"Eleven of them." She touched the page, not quite touching the ink. "Eleven."

Jonah went back to reading, because if he didn't keep his eyes moving he was going to have to feel something, and he wasn't ready. The ledger turned mundane, then sparse, then — near the end — strange.

*Day 41. The Minister came through in the night with four men and a sealed wagon. He would not say what it carried. He asked me to record that the wagon passed, and nothing else, and he paid me in the old coin, which is now worth nothing, which he said was the point.*

*Day 42. The Minister came back alone. He sat in the mural hall for six hours. He asked me if I thought a man could do a wicked thing for a kind reason and still be a wicked man. I told him Aunties don't answer theological questions before breakfast. He laughed. It was the worst laugh I have ever heard.*

*Day 43. Gone. Left a letter for the archive, said it must reach the bottom of the world or not at all. I did not ask what was in it. Some things you carry better unopened.*

Jonah set the ledger down with great care, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Sefa."

"I read it," she said. She'd been reading over his shoulder. Of course she had.

"The wagon."

"I know."

"The sealed wagon, the night before he—"

"I *know*, Jonah."

They stood in the cold kitchen of a dead nation, and the quest quietly changed shape around them. An hour ago it had been a treasure: a deed, a treasury, a legal claim that would un-blacklist him and un-erase a country and maybe, if he was honest with himself in the dark where he kept his honest self, make his grandfather into the man Jonah had needed him to be. A secret hero. A misunderstood man. A door kept open.

But the mural's hero had hauled a sealed wagon through the night. The hero had sat for six hours asking whether he was wicked. The hero had sent something to the bottom of the world — and Jonah was racing a salvage fleet to dig it up.

"He didn't just help them escape," Jonah said. "He was the Minister of Interior. The scuttling — sinking Vhaloria, sealing the archive — that was *his ministry*. That was his *job*." His voice came out flat and strange. "The letters. The coordinates. He wasn't hiding the treasure from the world, Sefa. He was hiding it *for* the world. From everyone. Including me. Including *them*."

"Or he was hiding the proof of what happened at the crossing," Sefa said. "The thing you said the letters danced around. The massacre."

"The thing he *failed to prevent*," Jonah said, and heard his grandfather's own phrasing come out of his mouth, and had to put a hand on the doorframe. "God. I have his sentences in my head. I've had them my whole life and I didn't know they were *his*."

Sefa crossed the room and, in a gesture that cost her visibly, put her hand on his shoulder — one pat, brisk, immediately withdrawn, the Sefa equivalent of a full embrace.

"Okay," she said. "So it's not treasure."

"No."

"So what is it?"

Jonah looked back through the doorway at the mural hall. At the white towers, the loaded boats, the child-height figures on the ice. At the broad-shouldered man painted guilty forever.

"It's a confession," he said. "And I'm the only heir."

The tide clock in Sefa's pocket chimed the half-hour — two hours of channel left, maybe less, the ice already muttering somewhere above them. They packed the ledger, because some things you carry even unopened, and Jonah was the last one out. He paused at the brass plaque and, obeying forty years of standing orders, wiped his boots.

* * *

They were twenty minutes down the channel, running dark with the outboard muffled, when Sefa's hand closed hard on Jonah's arm.

"Lights," she breathed. "Behind us. Three of them. Running *fast*."

Jonah looked back. Far down the flooded corridor they'd just left, searchlights swung across the ice in long white blades, and beneath the drone of engines he could hear — faint, unmistakable, wrong — the sound of the salvage fleet's sonar pinging off the walls of the corridor like a heartbeat that wasn't theirs.

They'd found the waystation. Which meant they'd found the route. Which meant the fleet knew exactly where the sixth tide led — and they were no longer following the chart.

They were following *him*.
