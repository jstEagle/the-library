## 6. The Turncoat's Ledger

Sefa Okonkwo had been captured exactly twice in her life, and both times she'd made a point of being the most annoying prisoner in the history of captivity.

The first time had been a customs skiff out of Port Meridian, over a hold full of untaxed eel-smoke. She'd spent four hours explaining maritime law to a nineteen-year-old deputy until he'd let her go just to make her stop. The second time was now, and she was already working on her opening remarks.

They'd taken her at the second narrows, where the Kestrel pinched down to a throat of black water between walls of blue ice. She'd heard the fleet's engines an hour before she saw them — that deep, patient thrum of ships built for money rather than weather — and she'd tried to thread the gap anyway, because Jonah was waiting at the far rendezvous with a camp stove and a tin of tea and that particular hopeful expression he got when he thought she couldn't see it.

The net caught her propeller. The boarding hooks came after.

Now she sat in the mess hall of the salvage vessel *Iron Meridian* — a name so lazy it deserved its own fine — handcuffed to a pipe, surrounded by men who kept looking at her like she was a math problem they'd been told was easy.

"You're not going to get anything from me," she said pleasantly, to no one in particular. "I've been interrogated by professionals. One of them cried."

Nobody laughed. This fleet had no sense of humor, which she considered its most damning quality. Forty crew, drones stacked on the deck like sleeping gulls, sonar rigs worth more than every boat she'd ever owned combined — and not one person aboard who could take a joke.

The broker came down an hour after dark.

She'd seen him at a distance before, on the docks at Greywater: tall, silver at the temples, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who had never once been told no by anything smaller than a government. Up close he was worse. Up close he was *familiar*. He had the kind of face you'd trust with your luggage.

"Ms. Okonkwo." He pulled a chair around and sat facing her, backward, arms folded across the backrest. "Corvel Ashun. I'd offer tea, but my cook is terrible."

"Jonah's is worse," Sefa said. "You're ahead."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Gone fast. "Where is he?"

"Where's who? I know so many men named Jonah. It's a very common name among people who owe me money."

"Amberdale."

"Never heard of him." She leaned back against the pipe as far as the cuffs allowed. "Is he handsome? People always ask me about handsome ones."

Ashun studied her for a long moment, and then, to her genuine surprise, he smiled — a small, tired thing, like a lamp turned low to save fuel.

"You're good," he said. "I'll give you that. But I don't actually need you to talk. I need you to listen. There's a difference, and the difference is that listening doesn't require your cooperation."

"I'm listening," Sefa said. "I'm also unimpressed. They can coexist."

He reached into his coat and set something on the table between them. A ledger. Old, water-swollen at the edges, bound in leather gone the color of dried blood. Stamped into the cover, half worn away, was a crest: a horizon line beneath three falling drops.

Sefa went very still.

She knew that crest. Not from Jonah's grandfather's letters — those she'd never read, though Jonah had described them often enough, usually at two in the morning with too much rum in him, tracing the same wax seal over and over like a man checking a wound. She knew it from the waystation murals, from the refugee quarter in Port Meridian where old women still baked bread in the Vhalorian style and refused, to their dying days, to say the country's name aloud except once a year, on the night of the Drowning, when they said nothing else.

"You recognize it," Ashun said. It wasn't a question.

"It's a pretty pattern," Sefa said. "Very popular on dishware."

"My grandmother fled Vhaloria when she was nine years old," Ashun said, conversationally, as if discussing freight rates. "She came out on one of the last barges. She remembered the sound the capital made when the seawalls went — she said it sang. A long note, like a struck bell, that lasted almost a minute." He turned the ledger slowly on the table. "She died believing the country had simply drowned. An act of God. Ice and sea and bad luck."

"And you know better," Sefa said.

"I paid eleven years and a considerable fortune to know better." He tapped the ledger. "This came off a body in the flooded archive's outer gallery. Salvage teams have been picking at the edges of Glacier Meridian for a decade. Most of what comes up is silt and bones. Occasionally something like this." He opened it. The pages were dense with a tight, slanted hand — columns of names, dates, numbers. "A supply ledger. Rations issued to a work detail in the winter of the scuttling. Grain, rope, blasting powder." He looked up. "Blasting powder, Ms. Okonkwo. Issued *inside* a nation's own capital, by its own Ministry of Interior, six weeks before the seawalls failed."

The pipe bit cold against Sefa's wrist. She made herself keep her face bored.

"Your point being?"

"My point being that your Mr. Amberdale thinks he's racing me to a treasure." Ashun closed the ledger with a soft, final sound. "There is no treasure. There is a grave, and there is a record, and the record proves that Vhaloria was not drowned by the sea. It was sunk by its own government — deliberately, methodically, to bury what happened in the eastern districts that winter. And the man whose signature is all through this book, the Minister of Interior, the architect of the erasure—" He paused, and Sefa watched him choose his next words with the care of a man laying charges. "—was named Amberdale."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor.

Not *a* Amberdale. She understood that immediately, in the way you understand a thing you've been refusing to understand for days. Jonah had the letters. Jonah had the coordinates. Jonah had grown up on stories of a grandfather who was either a hero or a ghost depending on which uncle was telling them, and never once — not once in nine years of partnership, of sharing bunks and storms and the particular intimacy of people who trust each other with their lives if not their feelings — had Jonah ever said the old man's first name.

Because he didn't know it. Or because he did.

"So," Sefa said, and her voice came out level, which cost her. "You want the archive destroyed. Burn the evidence, keep the erasure permanent. Very tidy. Very profitable."

"Profitable?" Ashun looked genuinely puzzled, and that was the moment Sefa understood she'd misread him entirely, and that the truth was somehow worse. "Ms. Okonkwo, do you know what happens if that archive surfaces? Forty years of peace. Forty years of a hundred thousand descendants building lives on the belief that the sea took their country honestly. Do you know what it does to a community to learn its own founders fed it to the water?" He stood, walked to the porthole, looked out at the ice. "My grandmother lit a candle every year for a nation murdered by weather. If I open that vault, I tell a million people the candle was lit for a murderer's alibi. Some truths are kinder drowned."

"There it is," Sefa said softly. "The speech. Every man who burns a library gives that speech. You're not the first and you won't be the last, and it never once worked."

"And you'd publish instead." He turned back to her. "Let it all come out. The massacre, the scuttling, the Amberdale signature. You'd hand a hundred thousand grieving people proof that their own minister drowned them — and hand the Amberdale boy proof that his bloodline built the machine."

"He already knows," Sefa said. "Or he suspects. He's been carrying it since the waystation, walking around with it like a stone in his boot, and he hasn't turned back yet. That's the difference between you and him, Ashun. You think truth is a bomb. He knows it's a debt. Bombs you bury. Debts you pay."

For a moment — just a moment — she saw him waver. Saw the tiredness under the certainty, the shape of a man who had spent eleven years and a fortune buying himself the right to look away, and who needed her to be wrong the way other men needed God.

Then the ship's intercom crackled, and a voice said, "Mr. Ashun — drone contact, western channel. Small craft running dark."

And Sefa thought: *Jonah.*

* *

They locked her in a stores cabin, which was flattering, because it meant they thought she was worth locking up, and stupid, because they'd left her alone with the inventory.

It took her eleven minutes to get out of the cuffs — hairpin, hinge pin, the usual liturgy — and another four to accept that the door was genuinely locked and the vent was genuinely too small, and to settle in for the long game instead. Fine. She'd escaped from better rooms than this. She'd escaped from a room that was actively sinking at the time.

What mattered was the chart.

She'd seen it in the wheelhouse during her escort aboard: a tide table for Glacier Meridian's inner corridors, annotated in Ashun's own hand, marked with the fleet's planned approach windows. Six tides. The same arithmetic Jonah had done on the back of a fuel receipt, but professional-grade, corrected for the storm swell coming up from the south. And pinned to the corner, a note in different ink: *Vault access viable only on sixth tide. After calving begins, no approach. No exit.*

No exit. She turned that phrase over while she waited, listening to the fleet's engines change pitch above her, feeling through the hull the moment they altered course west — toward the small dark craft in the channel, toward Jonah, alone in a boat with no radio and a camp stove and hope.

The guard came at shift change. Big fellow, young, the kind of earnest that hadn't been yelled at enough yet. She heard his boots stop outside the door, heard the key turn, and she was sitting against the far wall looking like a woman who had spent four hours contemplating her sins.

"Captain says bring her up," the guard said. "Mr. Ashun wants a word about cooperation."

"Cooperation," Sefa repeated, standing, stretching her wrists. "My favorite word. Right after 'ventilation' and 'bribery.'"

The wheelhouse was empty — everyone topside watching the drone feed, everyone's eyes on the western channel where a blip moved through the ice shadows, quick and stubborn and impossibly alive. Sefa felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to examine until later.

The tide chart was still on the console. So was Ashun's pen.

She took the chart. She took the pen, on principle. And when the young guard turned to fetch her coat — a courtesy, God help him, she'd have liked him under better circumstances — she stepped backward through the wheelhouse's starboard door, dropped three feet into the tender lashed at the rail, cut the lashings with the pen knife she'd lifted from the galley during hour two of her captivity, and was gone into the ice dark before anyone thought to look down.

The fleet's searchlights swept the water behind her, found nothing but brash ice and fog. Ahead, the western channel breathed white and silent, and somewhere inside it, a small dark engine coughed twice and went quiet, waiting.

* *

Jonah heard her coming before he saw her — that particular rhythm of oars, uneven, impatient, furious — and came down the rocks with the lantern held high and his face doing several things at once.

"They took you," he said. "You were *taken.*"

"I was briefly detained," Sefa said, shipping the oars, "by the worst-run vessel on this coast. Their cook is terrible, their security is decorative, and their captain—" She climbed out onto the shingle, soaked to the knee, chart tucked inside her jacket like a heartbeat. "Their captain has a grandmother from Vhaloria and a ledger with your family name in it, Jonah. In *ink.*"

He went pale in the lantern light. "The archive—"

"Proves everything. The scuttling. The powder requisitions. Your grandfather's signature on all of it." She watched him absorb it, watched the ground move under him. "Ashun wants it buried forever. Says the truth would hurt more than the drowning did."

"And you believe him?"

"I believe he means it," Sefa said. "That's different." She pulled the chart free and held it out between them, the fleet's approach windows neat and precise, the sixth tide circled twice. "Here's what I learned on that floating mausoleum, Jonah. This stopped being a race somewhere around the second narrows. Ashun isn't chasing a treasure and neither are we. There's a grave under that glacier with a confession in it, and two families fighting over whether it gets read aloud." Her voice cracked, just slightly, and she hated it, and kept going anyway. "So we're going to read it. All of it. Even the parts with your name in them. *Especially* those."

Jonah looked at the chart for a long time. Then at her.

"Sefa," he said quietly, "the fifth tide's gone. Storm took it. We've got one window left."

"Then we'd better not waste it arguing," she said, and picked up her pack.
