## 7. Fifth Tide

The storm came up the valley like a debt collector.

Jonah felt it in his teeth before he saw it — that low pressure ache behind his molars that his grandfather had once called "the mountain's warning bell," back when Jonah had still been young enough to believe the old man's sayings were wisdom rather than superstition. By midmorning the sky over Glacier Meridian had gone the color of wet slate, and the wind was carrying ice pellets sideways, stinging like a swarm of furious bees.

"The fifth tide opens in forty minutes," Sefa said. She stood at the bow of the *Marmot's Regret* — her boat, her naming, and she'd refused to change it despite Jonah's decade-long campaign — with her hands jammed in her pockets and her chin tucked against the wind. "Maybe. If the barometer doesn't keep falling."

"And if it keeps falling?"

"Then we sit in this cove and watch our window slam shut." She glanced at him. "One tide left after this, Jonah. One. You understand what that means?"

He did. The sixth tide was the last navigable channel before the calving season turned the entire approach to the archive into a shooting gallery of house-sized ice. Miss the sixth, and they'd be back next year — if the fleet hadn't already stripped the vault by then, if the broker's divers hadn't burned every page that proved Vhaloria had ever existed.

"We make the fifth," Jonah said. "We have to. It puts us in position for the sixth."

"Everything puts us in position for the sixth. That's all we've done for four tides — be *in position*." Sefa's laugh had no humor in it. "We're the most positioned people on this river."

The *Marmot's Regret* was a river tug with delusions of seaworthiness, forty years old and held together by welds, prayer, and Sefa's refusal to acknowledge any of her flaws. Jonah had helped her rebuild the engine three winters ago, back when they'd still been partners, back when the silence between them had been comfortable rather than armored. Now they stood at opposite ends of the wheelhouse and communicated mostly in instrument readings.

"Barometer's falling," she said anyway, because she knew he was watching it.

"I saw."

"Then you know what I'm going to say."

"You're going to say we should wait."

"I'm going to say we *should* wait." She turned to face him fully, and the wind pushed her braids sideways like seaweed. "But I also know you, and I know that if I say wait, you'll look at me with those sad mule eyes and I'll end up going anyway, so let's skip the part where we pretend I have a choice."

Jonah almost smiled. Almost. "The narrows at Tarn Gap will be open for maybe two hours once the tide turns. After that, ice choke. After that, nothing until spring."

"Nothing until spring," she repeated. "Right. Wonderful. Let's go get murdered by weather."

* * *

They made Tarn Gap with twenty minutes to spare, and that was the last thing that went right.

The narrows were exactly what the tide charts promised — a corridor of black water between walls of blue ice, wide enough for one boat, narrow enough that Jonah could have touched both walls if he'd stood at the rail and stretched. The water ran fast and green with glacial flour, and the sound of it filled the gap like a held breath.

Then the storm arrived in earnest.

It didn't build. It simply *arrived*, the way storms did in high country — one moment wind, the next moment the sky falling sideways. Rain turned to sleet turned to something heavier and wetter that came down in ropes. The walls of the gap funneled the wind into a single screaming note, and the *Marmot's Regret* began to yaw, her bow swinging toward the ice like a compass needle finding north.

"Jonah!" Sefa's voice came through the wheelhouse door half-swallowed by wind. "Take the wheel!"

He took it. The wheel fought him like a living thing, bucking in his hands as the current grabbed the hull and shoved. Ahead, the gap bent left around a spur of ice, and beyond the bend he could see — when the sleet thinned enough to see anything at all — the white chaos of open water where the gap spat boats out into the lake.

"Chart!" Sefa shouted. She'd come up beside him, one hand on his shoulder to anchor herself, the other holding the stolen tide chart — the broker's chart, the only copy, the thing they'd bled for in chapter after miserable chapter of this journey. "I need to see the depth readings! The channel shifts at the bend!"

"It's in the wheelhouse!"

"It was in the wheelhouse! It's not there now!"

The wind took the words and scattered them, but Jonah understood. The chart — folded once, weighted with a brass clip, left on the chart table while they'd both been on deck — was gone. Or it was *going* somewhere, because a corner of white paper was flapping against the outside of the wheelhouse window, pinned there by wind and spray, tearing free by degrees.

Jonah's hand shot out and caught it. Or caught part of it — the corner, damp and shivering in his grip, the rest of the sheet flapping out over the water like a flag of surrender.

"Hold it!" Sefa screamed. "Whatever you do, hold it!"

The paper tore. The wind took the main body of the chart and flung it out over the rail, and Sefa — Sefa, who was forty-one years old and had once told Jonah, with great sincerity, that she intended to die warm and dry in bed surrounded by admirers — went over the rail after it.

* * *

Later, Jonah would replay those ninety seconds the way he replayed all his worst memories: in fragments, in slow motion, in the merciless detail of guilt.

He saw her hit the water. He saw the chart's white shape drifting away downstream, and he saw Sefa surface once, gasping, her arm outstretched toward it — not toward the boat, he realized with a horror that would stay with him forever. Toward the *chart*.

The water in Tarn Gap was meltwater. It was two degrees above freezing, and a human body in water that cold had perhaps ten minutes of useful function, and Sefa was swimming away from the boat.

Jonah threw the wheel hard over, brought the *Marmot's Regret* around in a screaming arc that put a fresh scar down the ice wall on the starboard side, and ran for the rail. The boat hook was there — it was always there, Sefa's one concession to safety, "because someone on this boat has to have survival instincts, Jonah, and it clearly isn't you" — and he went down on his knees on the wet deck and leaned out into the storm with it.

She came up a second time, ten meters downstream, and this time she had the chart in her fist, held above her head, held *up*, her other arm barely moving, her body already going rigid with cold. She wasn't swimming anymore. The current was moving her, and she was holding the paper above the water like an offering, like a woman holding up a candle in a flood.

"Take the chart!" she shouted, and her voice was already slurred at the edges. "Take the chart first!"

"Take my hand, you idiot!"

"Chart first! It's the only—"

He got the hook to her. She grabbed it with the hand that wasn't holding the chart, and Jonah hauled, and the current fought, and for one endless moment the whole world was the screaming wind and the green water and the weight of a woman who had decided, with her characteristic stubbornness, that a piece of paper was worth more than her life.

He got her over the rail on the third pull. She came across the deck like landed fish, streaming, shaking so hard her teeth chattered audibly over the storm, and even then — even *then* — she rolled onto her back and lifted the chart above her head, holding it out of the puddle she was making of his deck.

"Is it—" Her voice was a ruin. "Is it readable?"

Jonah took it from her. The paper was translucent with water, the ink feathered at the edges, but the depth soundings at the bend — the critical ones, the ones that would get them through the sixth tide — were intact. Legible. Saved.

"It's readable," he said.

"Good." She closed her eyes. "Then I didn't almost die for nothing. That would be embarrassing."

* * *

They ran the boat aground in a cove below a granite shelf and got Sefa below into the engine room, which was the warmest space on the vessel, and wrapped her in every dry blanket aboard. Jonah heated water on the little stove and made the terrible cocoa he always made, the kind with too much powder and not enough sugar, and she drank it in both hands, shivering, steam rising off her skin.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The storm hammered the hull. Somewhere above them, ice groaned and settled, the glacier talking in its sleep.

"Two degrees," Jonah finally said. "That water was two degrees above freezing. Ten minutes of useful function. You were in it for six."

"I counted."

"You *counted*?"

"I count everything. It's a curse." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Four minutes to spare. Very efficient."

Jonah set down his cocoa. His hands were still shaking, and he looked at them with a kind of distant anger, as if they belonged to someone else. "Sefa. It's a chart. It's a piece of paper. I could have read the depths off it before the wind took it, or we could have found another way, or—"

"You couldn't have. You know you couldn't have. It's the only copy of the broker's soundings, and the broker's soundings are the only reason we know the sixth tide channel exists at all." She said it flatly, factually, the way she read instrument gauges. "I did the math. The chart was worth more than me. It was an easy calculation."

"That is the single worst thing you've ever said to me."

"I've said worse. You were there for the incident with the customs official in Port Meridian."

"This is different." He heard his own voice crack and hated it. "Sefa, I have spent ten years watching people I care about make themselves smaller so the world fits them better. My grandfather made himself a monster to fit a war. My father made himself a ghost to fit a blacklist. I am not — I cannot stand on this boat and watch you decide you're *expendable*. Not for a chart. Not for Vhaloria. Not for anything."

The engine room ticked and hummed around them. Sefa looked at him for a long moment, and something in her expression shifted — not softened exactly, but settled, the way sediment settles in still water.

"Then here's what I'll offer," she said. "I won't do that again. Not the swimming-after-paper thing. In exchange, you're going to stop looking at me like I'm already a memory."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what you're doing. You've been doing it since the sea cave. You look at me like you're rehearsing my eulogy." She took another drink of the terrible cocoa. "I'm not dead, Jonah. I'm cold and damp and I smell like engine bilge, but I'm not dead, and I'd like to be spoken to accordingly."

A laugh escaped him — a wet, cracked, unexpected laugh that hurt his ribs. "You smell *awful*."

"Thank you. I work very hard at it."

* * *

The storm blew itself out by midnight, and the fifth tide closed without them.

They both knew it as soon as the barometer steadied. The window at Tarn Gap had come and gone while they'd been agrounded, and now there was nothing between them and the archive except the sixth tide — one channel, one window, one chance, with the broker's fleet somewhere behind them in the dark and the calving season beginning its countdown.

Sefa spread the damaged chart out on the chart table and weighted its corners with mugs. The ink had feathered, the paper was warped into gentle waves, but the soundings held.

"One window," she said. "Sixth tide. Probably forty hours from now, if the ice holds."

"If the ice holds," Jonah agreed. "If the fleet doesn't beat us there. If the weather—"

"Jonah." She looked up at him across the chart, and the lamplight caught the salt dried in her braids. "I need to say something, and you're going to want to argue, so let me get through all of it first."

He waited.

"I didn't go in the water for Vhaloria." Her voice was quiet, stripped of all its usual armor. "I need you to know that. I've been telling myself for two weeks that I'm here for the refugees, for the record, for justice, for all the noble reasons, and that's a lie. The refugees are a good reason. Justice is a good reason. But I went over that rail for a piece of paper because the paper was yours, and because you're—" She stopped. Started again. "Ten years ago I left this partnership because I couldn't watch you destroy yourself for strangers anymore. And now I've watched you spend two weeks destroying yourself for dead people, and I've discovered that I still can't watch it, and that apparently the only thing worse than watching you drown yourself is being on a different boat when you do it."

"Sefa—"

"I'm not finished." She held up a hand. "So here is my position, stated plainly, so you can't misunderstand it. I am not staying for justice. I am not staying for the archive. I am staying because when that glacier closes, I intend to be standing next to you, and I intend to walk back out of it with you, and if you do anything heroic and stupid between now and then, I will personally bring you back to life so I can kill you myself. Do you understand?"

Jonah found that he couldn't speak for a moment. The engine room was very warm and very small and the storm was gone and the woman in front of him was soaked to the soul and absolutely, terrifyingly certain.

"I understand," he said.

"Good." She rolled the chart carefully, wiped her eyes with a motion she clearly hoped he wouldn't notice, and nodded at the porthole, where the first stars were breaking through the clearing sky. "Then get some sleep. We've got forty hours, one tide, and a glacier that's about to have the worst day of its life."

Beyond the porthole, far up the valley, the ice of Glacier Meridian caught the starlight — vast, blue-white, and patient, holding its drowned nation and its drowned secrets in a grip that was about, at last, to begin letting go.
