## 8. The Drowned Archive

The sixth tide came in gray and silent, like a debt collector who'd finally stopped knocking.

Jonah felt it before he saw it — a change in the way the water moved through the corridor below their camp, a slow thickening, the ice groaning somewhere far off in its sleep. He'd spent three days learning this glacier's moods the way you learn a difficult relative's: the little sighs, the false alarms, the one deep sound that meant business.

"Sefa," he said quietly.

She was already awake. She was always awake before him now, which he suspected was less about vigilance and more about her refusal to let him die unsupervised. "I know," she said. "Water's up four inches since midnight. Tide's turning." She sat up in the shelter of the overturned skiff they'd hauled into the waystation's lee, hair flattened on one side, expression flat on both. "And before you say anything romantic about last chances — no. We're not talking about it again. We dive or we don't."

"We dive."

"Good. Because I've run out of arguments and I refuse to lose to you at arguing."

* * *

The entrance to the archive was exactly where his grandfather's letters had sworn it would be: a fissure in the glacier's underbelly, half-drowned even forty years ago, drowned completely now except for ninety minutes on each sixth tide, when the sea pulled back just enough to expose a black slot of air in the rock. The letters called it the Mouth. Jonah had always assumed that was melodrama. Standing above it, watching the water drain away with a sound like a held breath being released, he revised his opinion.

"It looks like it's exhaling," Sefa said, peering down. "That's either very poetic or very bad."

"Both," Jonah said. "It breathes with the tide. When it inhales, we don't want to be inside."

"Wonderful. A building with lungs and opinions." She checked her dive lamp, checked his, checked the spare twice because she was Sefa. "Ninety minutes. If we're not out by minute seventy-five, I'm coming in after you, and then we'll both be stupid together, which is apparently our brand."

They went down through the Mouth one after the other, into water so cold it felt less like temperature than like intent. Below the air pocket the passage narrowed, then opened without warning into the vault itself — and Jonah's lamp found walls.

Not stone. Shelves. Thousands of them, cut into the living ice and lined with lead-sealed canisters, rank upon rank, descending into dark below the reach of his light. The water here was still, almost warm by comparison, lit faintly from some mineral glow in the rock, so that the whole chamber hung around them like a cathedral that had decided, at the last moment, not to be a cathedral anymore but something humbler and stranger: a library that had lain down to sleep.

He'd imagined treasure. Gold bars stacked in salt-corroded racks, the treasury of a dead nation glinting in his beam. That was what the salvage fleet wanted; that was what the deed implied. But there was no gold. There were documents. Room after room of them, sealed against the water in lead and wax, and every single one of them more valuable than anything you could weigh.

"Oh," Sefa said softly, when she understood what she was looking at. "Oh, you absolute old man. You didn't hide the money. You hid the receipts."

The realization landed in Jonah's chest like a dropped anchor. His grandfather hadn't sunk Vhaloria to protect its wealth. He'd sunk it to protect its testimony.

* * *

The central chamber held the government record proper — the last sitting of the Vhalorian Assembly, transcribed in full, sealed by the Speaker's own hand. Jonah found the canister marked with the crest from the river chest, cracked the lead seal with numb fingers, and unrolled the vellum inside with the care of a man defusing something.

The transcript began conventionally enough. Quorum counts. Budget line items for refugee transport. A motion to thank the neighboring kingdoms for asylum offered. Then, near the end, in a different hand — cramped, urgent, unmistakably the handwriting from the letters under Jonah's floor — an addendum.

*Minister of Interior Amberdale, recording for the closed session:*

*We have confirmation. Kellin Ridge. Two hundred and six souls, unaccounted. The border garrisons did not lose them. They were never processed because they were never permitted to arrive. I have seen the manifests. I have seen the photographs taken by the survey flight before the order came down to burn them.*

*I move that the full record be preserved and placed beyond retrieval by any government, including ours. Especially ours.*

*The fleet that carries us out will be searched. Everything we carry will be confiscated or destroyed. Therefore the record must go down instead of out. I am told this is madness. I am also told, by men who have already begun rewriting the manifests, that it is the only madness left to us.*

*Let the glacier keep what we cannot. Let whoever comes after find it and judge us whole.*

Jonah read it three times. His hands were shaking too badly to read it well, but he read it three times anyway.

His grandfather had ordered the scuttling. Had chosen the site beneath Glacier Meridian personally, had spent the final weeks of the nation's life sinking its entire documentary memory into the ice — not to hide the massacre, as the broker believed, not to bury it forever, but to make it unburnable. Unreachable by the governments doing the burning. A vault no court could subpoena and no minister could shred. Evidence locked in cold storage until someone brave or foolish enough came to collect.

"He wasn't hiding it," Jonah said aloud, and his voice bounced strangely off the water, came back to him smaller. "He was filing it. Somewhere nobody could destroy it. Including himself."

Sefa hung beside him in the dim mineral light, saying nothing, which from Sefa was a eulogy.

There was more. In a second canister, the penitent's file: his grandfather's private correspondence from the years after, letters never sent, drafts of confessions abandoned mid-sentence, a list of the two hundred and six names copied out over and over in different inks as though he kept hoping a different hand might write them. The last document was dated eleven months before his death. It read, in full:

*I did not pull the trigger. I signed the paper that made the trigger possible, and I told myself the alternative was worse, and I have spent forty years failing to believe myself. If you are reading this, you are standing in the thing I built out of that failure. Take all of it. The guilty parts and the innocent ones. A country is both. So is a man.*

Jonah had resented his grandfather for twenty years — for the silence, the coded letters, the shame that hung over the family like weather. He had built his whole adult self out of that resentment, the way you build a levee out of whatever's lying around. And now, a hundred feet under a glacier, holding the man's actual words, the levee dissolved all at once, and what came through wasn't forgiveness and wasn't condemnation. It was something colder and cleaner: understanding. The man had been a perpetrator and a penitent, and the vault was both crimes and both apologies, filed together, inseparable, honest in the only way left available to him.

"You okay?" Sefa asked.

"No," Jonah said. "But I'm clear. There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed. "Okay is better, but clear gets things done."

* * *

They worked fast after that, copying what they could — photographing page after page with the waterproof case camera, Sefa reading names aloud while Jonah logged them, the two of them moving through the flooded stacks like librarians racing a closing time that was measured in tide, not hours. The names took longest. Two hundred and six of them, plus the manifests, plus the survey photographs, grainy aerial shots of columns of people on a mountain road who had never arrived anywhere.

At minute sixty, the Mouth began to whisper. Not exhale — inhale. A low drawstring sound from the entrance passage, the tide reversing, the glacier taking its first sip of them back.

"Time," Sefa said. "Now. Leave the rest."

"The rest—" Jonah started to argue, and then heard himself, and stopped. "The rest stays filed. That was the point."

"That was the point," she said, and pushed off toward the entrance ahead of him, because she'd decided somewhere in the last week that if anyone was going to drown in this family's history, it wasn't going to be him.

They surfaced in the Mouth's shrinking air pocket with four minutes to spare, gasping, laughing shakily at nothing, hauling themselves up the fissure as the water rose around their ankles, their knees, their ribs. The glacier inhaled the rest without ceremony. It had waited forty years. It could wait for the copies.

Above the ice, in the gray flat light of the tide's turn, Jonah sat on the skiff's hull and looked at the camera case in his lap. Everything. The guilty and the innocent. His grandfather's signature on the scuttling order. His grandfather's signature on the confession. Both real. Both his inheritance.

Then he looked up, and the horizon did the thing horizons do when you've been underground too long — it arrived all at once, enormous and indifferent.

And on that horizon, small and wrong and moving fast, came lights. Six of them, in formation, cutting across the ice plain toward the Mouth.

The fleet had found the breathing place too.

"They're early," Sefa said grimly, shouldering her pack. "Tide chart says they shouldn't be here for six hours."

"Tide charts assume people sleep," Jonah said, standing. "Brokers don't."

"Neither do we, apparently." She glanced at him, at the case, at the approaching lights, and made the calculation out loud, because she'd learned that Jonah thought better when she said the numbers first. "One copy of everything. One boat. Six boats coming. You know what I'm going to ask."

"I know what you're going to ask."

"So?"

Jonah looked at the lights, and at the glacier behind them, already sealing its mouth, already keeping what it had been given. He thought about a man who had put the truth somewhere no one could burn it, and about what it would mean to let the fleet burn it now, forty years later, one tide short of done.

"We transmit," he said. "All of it. Tonight. Before they touch us."

Sefa nodded once, unsurprised, already moving. "Then we'd better find high ground with a signal," she said, "because in about twenty minutes, Mr. Amberdale, everything your grandfather ever hid is going to be the most famous thing on this coast — and those gentlemen are going to want a word."
