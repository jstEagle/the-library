## 9. Calving

*Sefa*

The fleet came up the last corridor at dawn, and Sefa knew it before she saw it, because the ice told her. The floes downstream had begun to shiver in a rhythm that had nothing to do with tide — a slow, arrhythmic knocking, like knuckles on a door, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Jonah," she said, and didn't have to say more.

He surfaced from the chart, hair frozen into a shape that suggested he'd been sleeping in his hood again, which he had. "How long?"

"Hours. Maybe less." She looked up at Glacier Meridian's face — two hundred feet of blue-black ice veined white where forty years of meltwater had written its private correspondence down the front of it. "Glaciers calve when they're ready, not when we are."

"Then we go now." He was already stuffing the deed-wrapped originals into the dry bag, moving with the frantic economy of a man who had finally stopped arguing with himself.

They had spent the night in the archive antechamber — a pocket of air trapped beneath the glacier's overhang, lit by their headlamps and by nothing else, where Jonah had read until his hands shook. She had watched him read. She had watched a man meet his grandfather properly for the first time: not the saint of the letters, not the monster of the murals, but both, wearing the same face, signing the same name.

"You okay?" she asked, which was a stupid question, so she amended it. "Okay enough to swim?"

"No," he said cheerfully. "Let's go."

* * *

*Jonah*

The water was the temperature of a held grudge. Jonah swam the flooded gallery one last time, past the shelves where Vhaloria's government had filed itself into extinction — budgets, censuses, shipping manifests, and then, in the sealed case behind the false wall, the thing his grandfather had drowned a nation's treasury to hide: the full record of Kessarin Hollow. Names. Dates. Orders given, orders obeyed, orders ignored. Four hundred and eleven people who had trusted a minister who could not save them and so had chosen instead to bury them twice.

He had copied everything. Every page photographed, every photograph tripled onto drives distributed through their gear like seeds. Sefa carried one in her boot. He carried one against his sternum, taped there, because if he was going to die under a glacier he wanted the truth to die somewhere more useful than with him.

They were halfway back through the dive tunnel when the glacier spoke.

Not cracked — *spoke*. A sound like a cathedral bell the size of a mountain, transmitted through water straight into their chests. Jonah felt it in his teeth. Above them, he knew without looking, a seam the width of a road had opened along the ice face, and the sea beyond the corridor had gone white with the shockwave.

Sefa grabbed his arm through the dark. "That wasn't a warning shot," she shouted against his regulator. "That was the opening argument."

* * *

*Sefa*

They broke surface in the antechamber just as the fleet's lead cutter rounded the final bend of the corridor, running lights cutting the grey morning like scalpels. Three vessels. Drones overhead. And standing at the bow of the first, small and immaculate in a parka that cost more than Sefa's boat, the broker.

She had learned his name from the stolen chart's margin notes, in her own grandmother's handwriting, of all things: *Corvane Aldous-Vey.* Vhalorian-descended. Keeper of the erasure. A man whose family had survived the drowning and built a salvage empire on making sure no one ever asked what they'd survived.

He came ashore at their little gravel shelf with two armed crew and a portable speaker, which Sefa found almost charmingly theatrical, and addressed Jonah across ten meters of ice-glazed stone.

"Amberdale." His voice was smooth as ballast. "You've been busy."

Jonah didn't answer. He stood there in his dripping gear with the dry bag at his feet, and Sefa watched him do the math she'd already done: three guns, two of them, zero options.

"I'll be brief, since the glacier won't be," Corvane said. "The originals. In exchange, silence — real silence. Your blacklist lifted. Your grandfather's name quietly restored. A pension your family hasn't seen in forty years. Everyone walks away. The nation stays drowned. The dead stay unnamed. This is the only version of today in which you leave with anything."

The glacier chose that moment to disagree, loudly. A crack ran down the ice face like lightning deciding to take its time, and everyone on the gravel flinched except Jonah.

"You've read it," Corvane pressed. "You know what publication does. Half those refugees will learn their grandparents were victims. The other half will learn they were accomplices. You'll hand four hundred families a wound with no court to hear it. Is that mercy?"

"No," Jonah said. And here was the thing Sefa would remember for the rest of her life, the thing she'd tease him about at his funeral whenever that finally happened: he smiled. Not a brave smile. A genuinely, stupidly warm one, the smile of a man who has just realized the answer was simple all along. "It's not mercy. It's the truth. They're allowed to be different things."

"The originals, Amberdale."

"They were never mine to trade." He crouched, unzipped the dry bag, and took out the drives — all but the one taped to his chest, which Corvane didn't know about, which Sefa did, which was the entire plan. "But these? These I can give you."

He hurled them, one after another, into the black water off the shelf's edge. Corvane's men actually lunged for the third one, waist-deep into water that would kill them in four minutes, and Sefa had to admit it was the finest piece of theater she'd witnessed since a busker in Port Meridian faked his own drowning for tips.

"You're insane," Corvane said.

"Frequently. It runs in the family." Jonah tapped his own chest, once, over the drive. "Copies went out an hour ago. Narrowband burst transmission, relayed through six networks your money can't reach, because they don't use money. Refugee channels. Fishing cooperatives. A lighthouse keeper on the Verren coast who owes my grandfather's ghost a favor. By tonight, the massacre of Kessarin Hollow will be on more screens than your fleet has drones."

Corvane stared at him. Somewhere behind his eyes, Sefa could see the empire recalculating. "You understand what you've destroyed. Forty years of peace—"

"It wasn't peace," Jonah said. "It was anesthesia. I'm sorry. I know that's not what you want to hear from another orphan of the same country. But we weren't protecting the dead. We were protecting ourselves from them."

And then the glacier ended the negotiation.

* * *

*Sefa*

There is a moment before a calving when the ice goes quiet — truly quiet, quieter than it has any right to be, the way a room goes quiet before bad news. Every gull lifts at once. The water flattens. Even the wind stops to watch.

Then two hundred feet of Glacier Meridian leaned forward and let go.

Sefa heard it as a single syllable, vast and final, and then the world became falling ice and displaced sea. The wave came off the face thirty feet high and hit the corridor walls and stood *up*, folding taller, and she had approximately one and a half seconds to make the only correct decision of her life, which was: grab Jonah, grab the boat, point the bow at the wave rather than away from it, and hold on to each other like the last two people in Vhaloria, which, for the next ninety seconds, they effectively were.

The Kestrel-born hull climbed the wave the way she'd been built to, groaning, taking green water over the bow, Sefa's arms wrapped around the tiller and Jonah wrapped around Sefa, and above them the ice debris rained down — house-sized blocks detonating around them, close enough that spray stung like buckshot. One berg the size of a chapel came down directly where the antechamber had been. Where the vault had been. Where, had they lingered ten more minutes, they would have been.

The wave carried them out through the corridor mouth and set them down in open grey water like a hand setting down a cup, and Sefa discovered she was laughing. Actual laughter, hysterical and uncontrollable, while Jonah clutched the dry bag — empty except for rope and their thermos — and looked at her like she'd lost her mind, which, fair.

"We're alive," she informed him. "I checked."

"You're laughing at a tsunami."

"I'm laughing at *Corvane's face*. Did you see his face? Forty years of careful erasure and this scrawny blacklist guide throws his legacy into the ocean and the ocean says *no thanks, I'm busy*."

Behind them, the glacier kept calving — a slow, thunderous procession, the ice face collapsing inward on the vault, entombing the original documents under a thousand tons of fresh blue ice, exactly as Jonah's grandfather had intended, exactly as Corvane had paid a fleet to ensure, and entirely beside the point now. The truth was already gone from that place. It was in Sefa's boot, on Jonah's chest, in six relay stations scattered across the world's cold coasts, propagating.

Corvane's cutters limped out of the corridor an hour later, berg-dodging, drones shattered, dignity in pieces. As they passed, close enough to hail, Jonah raised his thermos to the broker's bow.

"To Vhaloria," he called across the water. "All of it. Every page."

Corvane did not reply. But one of his deckhands — young, Vhalorian-featured, wearing the old crescent-and-wave crest stitched inside his collar where nobody official could see it — looked over at them, and at the glacier that had just swallowed his employer's whole purpose, and quietly raised his own fist in the old salute of a country that officially never existed.

Sefa returned it. Of course she did.

* * *

*Jonah*

That night, anchored in the lee of a nameless island while the horizon glowed with the aurora the calving had somehow earned them, Jonah sat on deck with the drive from his chest in his palm and watched the confirmation pings come in. Narrowband, low bandwidth, text-only fragments — but they were coming. *Received. Received. Received.* A fishing cooperative in Verren. A diaspora hall in Torquay. A woman in a refugee settlement who signed her message simply: *My grandmother was from Kessarin Hollow. Tell me everything.*

He typed back: *Everything. That's the point.*

Sefa brought him tea that tasted faintly of bilge and victory, and sat beside him, and said nothing for a long while, which was its own kind of language between them now.

"They'll come for us," she said eventually. "Courts, fleets, lawyers, worse."

"Probably."

"And you're not going to run?"

Jonah looked at the northern sky, where the lights moved like the memory of a drowned country waving hello. "Where would I run *to*?" he said. "I spent forty years being from nowhere. Turns out I was from Vhaloria the whole time. You can't outrun your own address."

Below the horizon, under open water where the maps still showed nothing at all, the nation slept on under its new ceiling of fresh ice — its treasury, its guilt, its four hundred and eleven names, its stubborn refusal to stay erased. The truth was loose in the world now, traveling at the speed of grief and gossip, faster than any fleet.

Which meant, Jonah realized, watching the pings accumulate like stars coming out, that the hardest part was over.

And the strangest part — the part where they found out what a country does with its resurrection — was just beginning.
