## 10. Monsoon and Horizon

The rain came the way it always did in the Kestrel valley that time of year — sideways, then all at once, as if the sky had been holding its breath for a season and finally let go.

Jonah Amberdale stood under the eave of Sefa's boathouse and watched the river rise. Six weeks since the glacier closed behind him. Six weeks since he'd walked out of the ice carrying nothing but a waterproof satchel full of scans, and the truth had gone out into the world like water finding every crack in a foundation.

He'd expected chaos. What he hadn't expected was how quiet the chaos would sound from here — just his terminal pinging at all hours, messages from places he'd never been, written in languages he couldn't read, all of them carrying the same crest he'd once pulled from the Kestrel's muddy grip.

*The Vhalorian Archive is real. The deed is real. The massacre is real.*

Some messages thanked him. Some cursed him. One, from an old woman in a refugee settlement three continents away, simply said: *My mother sang songs about a country I thought I had dreamed. Tell me it was not a dream.* He'd sat with that one for an hour before answering.

"You're brooding," Sefa said from the doorway, two mugs in hand. She handed him one without ceremony. "I can hear it from inside. It sounds like a kettle deciding whether to scream."

"I'm not brooding. I'm reading."

"Reading with your jaw set like that? That's brooding." She leaned against the doorframe beside him, watching the brown water climb the pilings. "Which one is it today? The thank-yous or the death threats?"

"The thank-yous are harder."

"I know." She said it softly, and for a moment they just drank their tea while the monsoon hammered the tin roof like an impatient drummer.

Because it was true, and they both knew it. The threats he could shrug off — he'd been blacklisted for years; hate mail was practically a pension plan. But the thank-yous. The woman who'd dreamed her mother's country. The man who wrote to say his father had died still lying about where they came from, because the lie was safer than the shame, and now Jonah had taken even the lie away. Those letters landed in his chest like stones dropped down a well, and he never heard them hit bottom.

"They're not thanking you, you know," Sefa said. "Not really. They're thanking the archive. You were just the idiot willing to swim for it."

"That's the most comforting thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

"I'm here all season."

* * *

The memorial crossing had been her idea, though she'd deny it under oath.

It had come up the way most of Sefa's ideas did — mid-argument, over cards, after she'd lost three hands in a row and needed to change the subject. A group of Vhalorian descendants had written to Jonah asking if anyone could take them to the calving zone, to the open water where Glacier Meridian used to reach. Where the nation had lain hidden for forty years, and now lay hidden no more — drowned twice over, once by its own government and once by the sea.

"They want a guide," Sefa had said, fanning her terrible hand. "Someone who knows the channels. Someone they can trust not to sell them to a salvage fleet."

"And you suggested me?"

"I suggested us. Don't flatter yourself. You get seasick when someone else steers, and I don't know the story well enough to tell it. We're a package deal now. Like salt and rust."

Jonah had looked at her across the card table — this woman who'd nearly drowned holding a tide chart above the floodwater, who'd forgiven him things he hadn't fully confessed, who'd stayed on the sixth tide not for justice but for him — and felt something in his chest loosen that had been knotted since he was nineteen years old and first read his grandfather's coded letters.

"Package deal," he'd agreed. "But I get to do the talking at the hard parts."

"You always do the talking at the hard parts. It's your one skill besides drowning gracefully."

* * *

They left on the morning tide with eleven passengers and a boatload of flowers, candles, stones, and one truly alarming quantity of incense that Sefa had tried to confiscate at the dock on fire-safety grounds and been overruled by unanimous vote.

The passengers were everything the letters had promised. An engineer from the northern settlements who'd spent forty years pretending his grandmother's lullabies were nonsense. Two sisters who'd only learned last month that their family name meant *keepers of the eastern gate*. A teenager who'd found the archive transmissions on a school terminal and had to explain to his parents why he was crying. A retired ferryman who kept touching the rail and saying, quietly, like a prayer: *open water, open water, we made it to open water.*

Jonah piloted the first stretch himself, then handed the wheel to Sefa and went aft to sit with them, because this part — the telling — was his.

He told them about the chest in the river. About the seal he didn't recognize until he did. About the letters under the floorboards, the coordinates beneath the glacier, the six tides. He told them about the waystation murals, the name *Minister of Interior* painted beside his grandfather's face, and he watched the engineer's expression shift as he understood whose blood was speaking.

And then he told them the rest. The part he'd argued with himself over for six weeks, in letters he drafted and burned and drafted again.

"My grandfather helped sink Vhaloria," he said. "Not the invaders. Not the sea. Him, and the others in the last government. They scuttled the archive deliberately — flooded the vault, sealed the records — to bury proof of what happened at Tern Harbor. A massacre. Ordered by men who then called themselves protectors of the people. My grandfather signed the order. And then, near the end, he tried to leave a map so that someday someone would find it anyway. Both things are true. He was guilty, and he was penitent, and I am not going to sort him out for you into a hero or a monster, because he was neither, and neither am I, and neither — if we're honest — is anyone."

There was silence on the deck. The teenager raised his hand like it was a classroom, which broke the tension in a way nothing else could have, and Jonah answered questions for two hours. Some of them were angry. One man stood up and said, flatly, that the grandfather should have burned the evidence entirely rather than leave it where scavengers and brokers could turn grief into a commodity. Jonah didn't argue. He'd decided long ago, in the dark of the flooded vault, that the truth belonged outside any court's reach — including his own — and that meant letting people be angry with it, and with him, forever if they needed to.

Sefa listened from the wheel, saying nothing, which from her was a form of grace.

* * *

By late afternoon they reached the calving zone, and there it was: open water where the ice wall had stood. Blue and vast and utterly empty, except for the small white floes drifting loose, and beneath them — somewhere far below, past the light — the vault, collapsed now, crushed by the very glacier that had guarded it. The broker's fleet had gotten nothing. The glacier had gotten everything. The sea held the nation now, and the sea does not negotiate.

They anchored. The passengers came to the rail.

The old ferryman went first. He unwrapped a bundle of river stones he'd carried from his childhood village — stones from a bank that no longer existed above water — and dropped them one by one into the sea, saying names. His mother. His father. The neighbors. The teacher who taught him letters in a language the maps denied.

Then the sisters sang. It was the lullaby the engineer had spent forty years calling nonsense, and when the first line rose, thin and cracked over the water, the engineer took off his hat and held it to his chest, and by the third verse everyone was humming it, including Sefa, who claimed afterward that she'd merely been clearing her throat for four minutes straight.

The teenager lit the incense over Sefa's vigorous objections, and the wind carried the smoke out toward the horizon in a long silver ribbon, and nobody caught fire, and Sefa conceded the point with the worst possible grace.

Jonah stood at the stern, apart, as he seemed to do at every important moment of his life. He thought about his grandfather — the perpetrator, the penitent, the man who'd written coded letters to a grandson who resented him — and he thought about the question he'd carried out of the vault: *what do you do with an inheritance like this?*

He'd published everything. Guilty and innocent alike. He'd let justice fall where it fell, outside any court, beyond his control. And standing here now, watching strangers mourn a country he'd helped unbury, he realized the answer had arrived without him noticing, the way answers do.

You carry it honestly. All of it. The massacre and the deed, the shame and the songs, the grandfather who failed and the grandfather who tried. You don't curate it. You don't clean it. You walk out of the ice with nothing but the truth, and then you spend the rest of your life handing it to people gently enough that they can hold it.

That wasn't absolution. He understood that clearly, and was almost grateful for it. Absolution would have been too easy, and too much like the erasure his grandfather had chosen. This was harder and better: memory, inherited honestly, at last.

Sefa appeared beside him and pressed a mug of tea into his hands. "You're doing the face again."

"What face?"

"The one where you've solved something and haven't told me yet."

"I'll tell you on the way home."

"Jonah."

"It's nothing dramatic. Just — I think I'm done running from my own name. That's all."

She looked at him for a long moment, the way she had on the fifth tide, in the storm, when she'd chosen to stay. Then she said, dryly, "About time. Do you know how exhausting it's been being partners with a man at war with his own surname?" And she laughed, and he laughed, and the monsoon came down on both of them and neither minded.

* * *

On the crossing home, the river ran high with rain, and the passengers slept below decks, exhausted by grief and strangely lighter for it. Jonah took the midnight watch alone.

At the bend where the Kestrel narrowed — the same bend where, a lifetime ago, a flood had spat a sealed chest into the shallows and changed everything — he cut the engine and drifted.

The water was black glass. Somewhere upstream, the mountains held the glacier's bones. Somewhere downstream, the world was reading his grandfather's signature and arguing about what to do with it, and would argue for a hundred years, and that was right. Courts might convene or not. Descendants might claim the treasury or refuse it. None of it was his anymore. He'd set it down at the edge of the ice and walked away carrying nothing, and somehow he'd never been less empty.

A shape moved in the reeds at the bank. Low, deliberate, familiar.

Another chest. Wax seal glinting faintly in the starlight, riding the floodwater down from the high country, coming to find him — or whoever came next.

Jonah stared at it for a long moment. Then he reached for the gaff hook, and smiled, and said aloud, to no one, to the river, to the old man whose letters had started all of this:

"All right. Let's see what else you kept."
