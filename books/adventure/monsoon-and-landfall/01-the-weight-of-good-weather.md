## 1. The Weight of Good Weather

The storm was dying, and Dominic Vasquez chased it anyway.

It had been a beauty in its youth — a supercell with a mesocyclone you could hang your hat on, rotating like something alive and hungry over the eastern flats. But that had been three hours ago, near the state line, and storms out here didn't age gracefully. They went senile. They sprawled. They forgot what they were doing mid-thought and collapsed into rain and regret, the way old men fall asleep in diners with their coffee going cold.

He'd been on it since dawn, his battered F-250 eating miles of cracked asphalt and gravel spur roads, the radar app on his phone bleeding its colors across the windshield mount: green going yellow going orange going that deep arterial red he loved more than any color in nature. He loved it the way a man loves a woman who is bad for him, which is to say completely, and against all evidence, and with full knowledge of the cost.

The client rode shotgun. Mr. Abernathy — no first name offered, none needed; the man wore his formality like armor — was an insurance adjuster from the city, soft in the middle, sunburned at the neck where his collar didn't reach, and paying by the day for the privilege of nearly being killed. Dominic had promised him weather. Not a photograph of weather, not a description of weather, but weather itself: the pressure drop you felt in your back teeth, the green-black light that made everything look like a memory of itself, the wind that arrived before the rain did, pushing a wall of dust ahead of it like a herald.

What Dominic had actually delivered so far was a dying storm and a lot of driving.

"Is this it?" Abernathy asked, for the fourth time in an hour. He asked it the way a man asks about dinner when he's already eaten the bread. "Is this the tornado?"

"This is the tail end," Dominic said, and he said it with the particular warmth he could produce on demand, the charm that came out of him like sweat, effortless and slightly dishonest. "The show's mostly over, but you're getting the whole anatomy lesson. The rear-flank downdraft, the inflow tail, the—" He gestured grandly at the sky, at the bruised anvil spreading east like spilled ink. "Most tours, they show you pictures afterward. I'm showing you the corpse."

Abernathy looked at him. Dominic watched him do the arithmetic — not the arithmetic of meteorology but the arithmetic of money, of whether the day's fee had purchased enough spectacle to justify the invoice. Dominic knew that face. He'd seen it on every client, every creditor, every person who had ever held power over him and enjoyed holding it. It was the face of a man deciding whether you were worth what you cost.

Dominic hated that face. He smiled harder.

* * *

Here is what Dominic Vasquez owned, as of four o'clock that afternoon:

One truck. A 2009 Ford F-250, white once, now the color of old teeth, with a hail-pocked hood and a passenger seat that reclined only if you wedged a two-by-four under it. The odometer had rolled past two hundred thousand somewhere in Kansas and kept going out of spite. The transmission slipped in second gear unless you coaxed it, and coaxing it had become less a mechanical act than a relationship — he talked to it, apologized to it, sometimes swore at it in two languages depending on how the morning was going.

One compass. His father's, brass-cased, the glass scratched into a faint webwork by forty years of pockets and prairie wind. It didn't point north anymore, exactly; it pointed eleven degrees west of north, a deviation his father had never corrected because, the story went, he liked knowing exactly how much the world disagreed with him. Dominic carried it in the door pocket, beneath a tire gauge and a granola bar gone to powder, and he touched it sometimes at stoplights the way other men touched saints' medals.

And one debt.

The debt deserved its own paragraph. Maybe its own chapter. Maybe, if the Delgado brothers had their way, its own funeral.

Eleven thousand four hundred dollars. That was the number, though the number moved — it breathed, it grew, it had a metabolism all its own. Eleven thousand four hundred, plus the vigorish, plus the late fees, plus the mysterious "administrative costs" that appeared on the little pink carbon-copy receipts like fungi appearing after rain. Dominic had borrowed six thousand originally, eighteen months ago, to replace the truck's engine after the old one threw a rod outside Amarillo in a hailstorm he'd been chasing for a client very much like Abernathy. Six thousand had become eight. Eight had become eleven. The math was not math. The math was a language spoken only by the men who wrote it down, and they spoke it fluently, and cheerfully, and they never lost an argument conducted in it.

The Delgado brothers. Raúl and Tomás. Everyone in the county knew them, and everyone in the county liked them, which was the most frightening thing about them. They weren't loan sharks out of some movie, all brass knuckles and broken windows. They were optimists. They believed in people. They believed in Dominic Vasquez specifically, believed in his hustle, his charm, his gift for finding clients willing to pay good money to stand in front of God's worst temper. They believed in him so much they kept lending him money against that belief, and each loan came wrapped in such warmth, such genuine brotherly affection, that signing for it felt less like drowning and more like being hugged by the ocean.

Tomás did the numbers. Raúl did the smiling. Between them they owned half the paper in three counties — equipment liens, crop advances, medical debts, a beauty salon, two bail bonds, somebody's grandmother's Buick — and they collected it all with the serene confidence of men who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered.

They were due Friday.

* * *

The dying storm put down one last burst of energy as they crossed the flats — a brief, half-hearted gustnado, maybe sixty yards of rotating dust that stood up out of a harvested wheat field, wobbled like a drunk leaving a bar, and laid itself back down without hurting anything but a fence post and a section of plastic irrigation pipe.

"There!" Dominic shouted, with real feeling, because it was real feeling — that was the terrible thing about him, the thing he couldn't fix: even broke, even hunted by arithmetic, he still loved this. "You see that? That's a landspout. Textbook. Non-supercellular, forms off the convergence boundary—"

"I see dirt," Abernathy said.

"You see *dirt organized by physics.*" Dominic pulled the truck onto the shoulder so hard the gravel spat. He got out. He made Abernathy get out. He stood the man in front of the collapsing vortex with his hand on his shoulder like a father presenting a son, and he narrated — the rotation, the debris cloud, the way the light went strange and metallic — and for about ninety seconds, watching Abernathy's face change from skepticism to something adjacent to wonder, Dominic remembered why he'd chosen this life instead of a sensible one.

Then the gustnado died, and the light went flat, and Abernathy took out his phone and started typing, and Dominic understood that the invoice would reflect the gap between promise and delivery.

"Tell you what," Dominic said, sliding back behind the wheel, working the charm like a lever. "Next system comes through Tuesday. Big one. Dryline setup, cap's gonna break, all the ingredients. You book the follow-up day now, I'll give you the friend-of-the-house rate."

"The friend-of-the-house rate," Abernathy repeated, tasting it.

"Half off. Because you've suffered with me today. Because loyalty should be rewarded." He grinned. The grin worked on most people. It was working on Abernathy approximately sixty percent, which Dominic judged from long experience to be enough.

Abernathy booked the Tuesday. Of course he did. Men like Abernathy always booked the Tuesday — not because they believed in Tuesday's storm, but because hope, priced correctly, was cheaper than disappointment admitted to.

Dominic drove him back toward town with the sun falling red behind them and the dead storm's anvil glowing pink above the horizon like a wound in the sky. And in the long silence, while Abernathy thumbed at his phone, Dominic did the accounting he always did at the end of a day like this one, the silent ledger he kept in his head because writing it down would make it real:

Day's take: four hundred dollars, minus fuel, minus the lunch he'd bought Abernathy to soften him up, minus the tolls, minus the two-by-four he'd finally replaced in the passenger seat because even charm has capital expenses. Call it two hundred and eighty dollars clear.

Debt service owed Friday: nine hundred dollars. Interest only. Principal untouched, principal eternal, principal a monument to the man he used to be before the engine died, before the season went bust, before he'd walked into the Delgados' office above the feed store wearing his best shirt and his best smile and walked out lighter by six thousand dollars and heavier by a lifetime.

Gap: six hundred and twenty dollars, and five days to close it.

He ran the options the way he always ran them, fast and without sentiment. Chase another client? No systems coming until Tuesday, and Tuesday belonged to Abernathy now, at half rate. Sell the hail-dented aluminum canopy off the truck bed? Two hundred, maybe, and then he'd have no canopy, and the canopy was load-bearing in ways both literal and psychological. Call his sister in Phoenix? She'd stopped answering fourteen months ago, right around the second time he'd borrowed from her. Pawn the compass?

His hand found the door pocket. His fingers closed around the brass case without his quite deciding to, the way a hand finds a rosary. He held it there, on the wheel, through three miles of empty road, and he did not open the glove box, and he did not call the pawn shop, and he told himself this was resolve when he knew it was only fear — fear of becoming a man who'd sold the last thing his father ever handed him. There were worse things than being poor. He was living proof. But there was a floor, and the compass was the floor, and a man who sold his floor became a different animal entirely.

He put it back. He drove on.

* * *

Town rose out of the dusk the way towns do out there — not gradually but all at once, a water tower and a grain elevator and a scatter of sodium lights, materializing like a stage set when the play needs a backdrop. Dominic dropped Abernathy at the Motor Lodge with a handshake firm enough to leave an impression and a business card placed gently on top of the man's suitcase, and then he sat in the parking lot of the diner across the highway for a while with the engine ticking as it cooled, doing nothing, thinking everything.

The diner was called the Blue Flats, or it had been called the Blue Flats until the B fell off the sign two winters ago and nobody climbed the ladder, so now it said LUE FLATS in letters that glowed a sickly cyan after dark, and everyone in town just called it the Lue. The pie was honest. The coffee was criminal. The waitress, Dolores, had known him since he was nineteen and had long ago stopped asking how he was doing, because she'd learned that the answer was always the same and always a performance.

He went in. He ordered coffee he couldn't afford and pie he could afford less, because that was the disease of him — the spending of money he didn't have on small pleasures to keep from feeling the large absence. Dolores brought both without writing it down, and refilled the cup twice without being asked, and said, "Rough week?" in the tone of a woman reading tomorrow's weather in today's face.

"Good week," he said. "Great week. Storm season's turning."

"Mm." She topped off the cup again. "Your friends came by."

He went still. Not visibly — he was too good for visible — but inside, everything paused, the way a field goes still when it hears hawks.

"Which friends."

"Raúl and Tomás." She set the pot down on the warmer and wiped her hands on her apron, and her voice stayed level the way voices stay level when they're carrying something heavy carefully. "Tuesday crowd, usually. They came Wednesday. Sat in Tomás's usual booth, the one by the window. Ordered like kings. Left a tip like princes." She looked at him. "They asked after you, Dominic. Real friendly. Wanted to know when you'd be back through."

"What'd you tell them?"

"That I don't keep my customers' calendars." She shrugged, and the shrug contained multitudes. "Raúl laughed. Said of course, of course, everybody knows Dom's got a system, Dom's always good for it, Dom wouldn't miss a Friday." She picked up the pot again, poured him one last inch of coffee like a sacrament. "They left twenty minutes ago. Heading out toward the highway."

Toward the highway. Toward the route he'd come in on. Toward the Motor Lodge where Abernathy was checking in, where a stranger might mention a storm-chasing fellow, tall, charming, drives a white Ford with hail dents.

Dominic sat with his cooling pie and did the new math, the real math, the math underneath the math. Nine hundred by Friday. Two hundred eighty in the truck's floorboard, taped under the seat where he taped everything important, which was to say nothing important had ever been taped there. Four days. And now the Delgados weren't waiting passively for Friday — they were moving early, moving warm, moving through the town like weather themselves, asking after him in that cheerful way that was not a question at all but a countdown.

He thought about running. He always thought about running. He'd mapped it a dozen times in his head — south to El Paso, sell the truck, disappear into a city big enough to lose a name in. But running was a debt too, and its interest was worse than the Delgados': it compounded daily in the currency of becoming someone who runs. His father had run once, from a farm and a wife and a boy, and Dominic had spent his whole life measuring the crater that left. He wasn't built for craters. He was built for chasing things — storms, deadlines, the receding edge of solvency — and chasing meant staying in motion toward, not away.

Besides. Where would he run that the arithmetic couldn't follow?

He finished the pie. It tasted like nothing, which was appropriate. He paid — counted out bills from the seat-tape stash, leaving himself one hundred ninety and change, a number so precise it felt like a wound — and he tipped Dolores extra, because tipping well was the last luxury of the insolvent and also, possibly, insurance. Goodwill was the only credit line left to a man whose credit was gone.

Outside, the night had cleared completely, the way skies do after storms die, scrubbed and starry and indifferent. The air smelled of wet dust and cut wheat. Somewhere far off, coyotes were conducting business. The dead storm's remnants still hung on the eastern horizon as a faint gray bruise, and Dominic looked at it the way a gambler looks at a closed table — with love, and loss, and the unshakable conviction that somewhere out there, over the next ridge, the next system was already organizing itself, and it would be bigger, and it would be his, and it would fix everything, the way the next one always almost fixed everything.

He climbed into the truck. The transmission caught on the first try, which felt like an omen, though he couldn't have said of what.

He had nowhere to sleep but the cab tonight — the Motor Lodge was out of the question now, too visible, too associated with Abernathy, too near the highway the Delgados had driven toward. So he pointed the truck away from town, out along the county road toward the state land where the road turned to dirt and the dirt turned to suggestion, and he let the dark take him.

That was when he saw the headlights.

They came up behind him slow, patient, the high beams flicking once — twice — in a rhythm that wasn't accidental, wasn't a warning about wildlife, wasn't any of the innocent explanations he cycled through in the first three seconds. A third flick. Then the lights dropped back, hanging there in his mirror at a distance that was exactly polite and exactly menacing, the distance of a man following you home to make sure you get there, except neither of you said so.

Dominic's hands went easy on the wheel. His breathing went easy in his chest. Both were performances; underneath, his pulse was doing the work of a jackhammer in a silk glove.

He knew those headlights. Every debtor knows the headlights. He'd seen them in his mirror twice before in his life, and both times the vehicle behind them had belonged to cheerful men with ledgers, and both times the conversation that followed had ended with him poorer and them richer and everyone smiling about it.

But this time he didn't slow down. This time he didn't pull over.

This time he pressed the accelerator down and let the old Ford answer, and the needle climbed, and the county road unspooled ahead of him into the dark, and the headlights held their station in his mirror like a verdict in pursuit of a man who hadn't yet heard it read.

Friday was four days away. The night was young. And somewhere between here and the horizon, the road was about to decide what kind of man Dominic Vasquez actually was.
