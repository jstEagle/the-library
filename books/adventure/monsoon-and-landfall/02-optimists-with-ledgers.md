## 2. Optimists with Ledgers

The Delgado brothers kept their office above a feed store on Calle Ocho, in a room that smelled of alfalfa dust and lemon polish, and they received you the way a church receives a penitent — warmly, without judgment, and with an unshakable certainty that you had come to confess.

Dominic climbed the outside stairs at nine in the morning, because the letter under his door had said nine and he had learned that the only thing worse than owing the Delgados money was owing them punctuality as well. The stairs complained under his boots. Below him, a man hosed down the loading dock, and the water ran orange with red-dirt runoff toward a storm drain that gurgled like something being swallowed.

He paused at the top, hand on the rail, and let himself have one honest breath. Just one. The sky over the flats was doing its late-summer pantomime — towering cumulus stacked up along the eastern horizon like furniture in storage, beautiful and going nowhere. A dying storm. He'd spent three years learning to read those towers the way other men read faces, and right now every instinct in him said *dry week, no chase, no client footage, no income*. Which was, of course, why the Delgados had chosen this week.

*They know the weather too,* he thought, climbing the last step. *That's the whole business. Everybody in this valley reads the sky. Some of them just read it for rain.*

* * *

The office had two desks and a saint's candle burning on the windowsill, wax pooled into a white stalagmite the size of a fist. Raúl Delgado sat behind the nearer desk, reading glasses low on his nose, a calculator in front of him like an altar piece. He was fifty-something and soft-bodied, with a face like a friendly potato and hands that were surprisingly elegant, a pianist's hands, though Dominic had never seen a piano anywhere near him. Tomás occupied the far desk, younger by six years, leaner, with a mustache he maintained like a hedge and a habit of smiling exactly one second after everyone else stopped.

"Dominic." Raúl spread both arms, still seated, as if embracing the whole room. "Look at this man. Look at him. You been chasing weather again?"

"Chasing work," Dominic said.

"Same thing out here, eh?" Raúl laughed, delighted, and tapped the calculator twice as though it had told him a joke. "Sit, sit. You want coffee? Marisol makes coffee that could raise the dead."

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine, Dominic. That's why you're here." He said it without malice, almost tenderly, the way a doctor says *you've got a shadow on the film*. He turned the calculator around so Dominic could see the display, as if the numbers needed introducing. "Now. Let's look at where we are, together, like partners. Because that's what we are, no? Partners."

Dominic sat. The chair was vinyl and exhaled warm air when he took it, someone else's heat rising up to meet him.

"Original principal," Raúl read off a yellow legal pad, each figure placed down like a card, "four thousand two hundred. Interest at our agreed rate — very fair rate, I don't need to tell you what the banks wanted from a man with your collateral, which is to say nothing — brings us to five thousand one hundred forty. Minus the four hundred you brought in August." He made a small sad sound, a *tsk* with sympathy in it. "Which leaves four thousand seven hundred forty. And then there's the late fee structure we discussed, and the October payment that came in November—"

"It came," Dominic said. "It came in November."

"Came in November," Raúl agreed warmly, "which is not October. I'm not saying you're a bad man. I'm saying the calendar doesn't care about intentions. Neither do I, honestly. I love calendars. Best invention after the wheel." He smiled over the glasses. "So. Today, all-in, we're at five thousand eight hundred and change. Let's call it five-eight even, because I like round numbers and I like you."

Tomás laughed from the far desk, one second late, and went back to sharpening a pencil with a small knife, curls of graphite falling onto blotting paper.

Dominic looked at the number and did the arithmetic he couldn't stop doing, the arithmetic that lived behind his eyes now like tinnitus: the truck, paid down but not off; the camera gear, worth maybe half what he owed on it; the compass, worthless to anyone but him; the account balance, which he knew to the dollar because he checked it every morning the way some men check a wound. Two hundred eleven dollars. He owned a vehicle full of expensive instruments for measuring things he could not afford.

"I can bring you fifteen hundred by Friday," he said. "There's a rancher east of the highway, hay operation, wants a hail assessment before he insures the new crop. If the pattern breaks—"

"If the pattern breaks." Raúl repeated it gently, savoring it, and removed his glasses. Without them his eyes were smaller and older and much less friendly. "Dominic. My brother and I, we are optimists. People think because we smile, because we make coffee, because we don't send boys to break legs — people think that means soft. But optimism is a discipline. We believe everybody pays. That is our faith. It has never once failed us." He folded the glasses and set them on the pad, pinning the numbers. "You're a good bet. That's why we're still talking. A man who can read a storm three counties away is a man who understands patterns. So understand this pattern."

He slid a fresh sheet across the desk. On it, in careful ballpoint, was a new number: 6,200. And below it, a date seven days out.

"One week," Raúl said. "Full satisfaction, or we begin to discuss assets. The truck first, I think. It photographs well. Tomás already knows a buyer in Almagre who pays cash for chase rigs, cameras included, and pays *well*, because storm footage sells to the insurance men and the news stations, and my brother is a genius at knowing what a thing is worth to the person who wants it most." He beamed. "You see? Optimism everywhere you look."

"And if I find another way," Dominic said. "If I come to you with something bigger than the number."

Raúl's eyebrows rose, genuinely curious, and for a moment the friendliness in his face was real, which was worse. "Bigger than the number," he echoed. "Dominic, everything is bigger than the number. The number is just how we keep score." He tapped the date. "One week. Come with the money or come with a miracle. Either one, we'll take it. We're optimists."

Tomás looked up from his pencil then, and smiled — one second late, exactly on schedule — and said nothing at all, which from Tomás was a whole paragraph.

* * *

The door between the office and the back room stood open six inches, and through the gap Dominic could see a girl of maybe nineteen filing papers with fast, precise movements, like someone dealing cards she didn't want anyone to count. She had dark hair cut blunt at the jaw and her brothers' coloring and none of their softness. When he passed the doorway on his way out, she spoke without looking up.

"The coffee's better than the coffee they offer."

Dominic stopped. "I didn't ask for any."

"I know." She set down a folder and finally looked at him. Her eyes were level and unimpressed, the eyes of someone who had grown up watching this exact scene through that exact gap in the door, dozens of times, years of it. "Marisol. You met me at the fiesta two summers ago. You don't remember."

"I remember the fiesta," he said carefully. "Not much else."

"Nobody does." She came to the doorway, arms crossed, and glanced past him toward the desks, where Raúl had resumed his calculator and Tomás his knife. Her voice dropped to something meant for him alone. "One week is real. Don't mistake the smiles for slack. They gave the Ferreira man two extensions last year. Warm coffee, round numbers, *we're optimists*. Then in December they took his welding rig and his wife's car and sold the debt to a man in Almagre who is not an optimist at all." She said it flatly, a weather report. "And that was the first time. They never forgive twice. There isn't a second extension. There's just the part where they stop pretending to be your friends, and it happens overnight, and it's quieter than you think."

"Why are you telling me this?"

She considered the question honestly, which he hadn't expected. Outside, the hosed-down dock glittered and dried; somewhere below a dog started up at nothing.

"Because you looked at the number like a man reading a barometer," she said. "Falling fast. Most of them look at it like a price tag. You looked like you were trying to figure out what it was going to do next." She stepped back into the file room, conversation evidently over, then paused with her hand on the folder. "Friday's hay-ranch job won't cover it. You know that. Whatever you're actually planning — plan faster."

Dominic stood in the hallway a moment longer than made sense, listening to the small sounds of her working, the paper sliding home into place. Then he went down the complaining stairs into the white heat of the morning, and the number rode with him, 6,200, seven days, ticking like something mechanical sewn behind his ribs.

* * *

He drove out past the stockyards to the flats and parked the truck on a rise where he could see the whole eastern sky, and he did what he always did when the ground got dangerous: he watched the air.

The cumulus out there was dead architecture, gorgeous and hollow, towers built by morning heat and already collapsing inward, their tops shredding into cirrus like smoke off a snuffed candle. No cap break, no moisture at depth, nothing. A dry week. He'd known it before he walked up those stairs, and he'd offered the Delgados a hail assessment anyway, because desperation makes liars of careful men and he'd at least wanted to lie to someone besides himself.

Two hundred eleven dollars. Six thousand two hundred. Seven days.

He got out the compass without deciding to, the old brass one, Elias Crane's compass — no. Not yet. Not yet Elias Crane. He wouldn't meet the dying man until dusk, at the diner on the north road, though the day didn't know that yet and neither did he. For now it was just a compass, inherited from a stranger at a gas station two years ago in a trade for jumper cables, heavy in the palm, the needle settling north with a patience Dominic envied.

North was Kestrel Pass. He thought about it the way you think about a word in a language you used to speak. North was high country and early snow and a season closing like a door. South was Almagre, where Tomás's buyer paid cash for chase rigs. East was a hay rancher who might, if the sky performed a miracle, need a hail assessment. West was nothing but basin and heat shimmer and the long flat way out of town, which was also, he noted, the direction you'd run if you were the kind of man who ran.

The needle held steady through all of it, indifferent, pointing at a pass that was already thinking about winter.

"Plan faster," he said aloud, to nobody, to the girl in the file room, to the needle. His voice came out wrong in the empty cab, too loud, and he laughed at himself — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man who has just heard his own net worth spoken aloud in a friendly voice over a calculator.

Here was the thing he couldn't say in that office, the thing humiliation had carved into him on the vinyl chair with its borrowed warmth: it wasn't the money. Money was weather; it came, it went, you chased it or you didn't. It was the smiles. It was Raúl's genuine delight, Tomás's punctual laugh, the coffee he was welcome to, the *partnership* — the whole cheerful machinery of it, designed so precisely to make a man feel that his ruin was a reasonable arrangement between friends. They had built a business out of being kind to you while they took everything, and the worst part, the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, was that it worked. He'd sat in that chair and felt grateful. Grateful, for a week.

Never forgive twice. There wouldn't be a second time. There would just be the quiet morning when the smiles were gone and the buyer from Almagre was standing in his driveway with cash, admiring the cameras.

Dominic put the compass on the dash, where its needle trembled and steadied, and started the truck, and pointed it north on the county road for no reason he could defend except that north was the only direction that wasn't owned by somebody.

He was ten miles out, watching the dead towers burn off the horizon, when he saw the sign for the diner on the north road — RUTH'S, EAT, the neon long dead, the paint blistering — and remembered he hadn't eaten since yesterday, and pulled in, because a man with two hundred eleven dollars and seven days should at least die with coffee in him.

Inside, at the corner booth, an old man was coughing like the end of the world, and had a map folded under his coffee cup.
