## 3. A Trade at Dusk

The storm died twenty miles west of the diner, and Dominic drove through its corpse the way you drive through a wake—slow, respectful, headlights cutting the wet air into ribbons. What had been a wall cloud all afternoon had unraveled into rags of stratus, bleeding pink where the sun went down behind him. The flats steamed. Every arroyo he crossed ran with an inch of chocolate water, and the creosote came up sharp and clean through the vents, that smell like the desert exhaling after holding its breath all summer.

His phone sat in the cupholder, dark. He'd stopped answering it an hour ago. Three missed calls from the client, whose name he'd already forgotten, and one text from a number he knew too well: a single emoji. A smiley face. The Delgados never wrote words when a smile would cut deeper.

He pulled into the diner because it was there, the way oases are there—aluminum roof, a sign missing its L so it advertised itself as a DINER that had once been something else, maybe a motel, maybe a church. One truck in the lot, an old International with a camper shell and a bed full of core sample boxes. Dust on it in layers, like sediment. A man who hadn't been home in a long time, or had no home to be long at.

Dominic sat in his own truck for a minute with the engine ticking. He counted, the way he always counted. Four hundred and eleven dollars in the account. A truck worth maybe six thousand if he found a fool. A compass his father had given him, brass, the needle sticky with age but true. And against all of it, a number that grew like mold in the dark: nineteen thousand, four hundred, and change. The change was the insult. They always added the change.

"Right," he told the windshield, and went in.

* * *

The diner smelled of coffee that had been born before the monsoon and grease that had achieved immortality. A woman behind the counter with a name tag reading DOT gave him a look that assessed his solvency in under a second and decided he was worth a cup of coffee and nothing more. He took the stool at the end of the counter, ordered pie because pie was cheaper than dignity and came with the illusion of a meal.

The prospector was three stools down.

Dominic noticed him the way you notice weather—not all at once, but as a pressure. The man was sixty-something or seventy, hard to tell, because the desert ages people unevenly, taking the fat off the face and leaving the bone. A canvas jacket gone the color of the hills. Hands like root systems. He was drinking coffee in small, careful sips, and between sips he was coughing.

Not a smoker's cough. Dominic knew that one from his father, the wet rattle of it. This was deeper, structural, the sound of a house settling in a way houses aren't supposed to settle. Each cough ended in a pause that lasted a half-second too long, and in that pause the man's shoulders would rise and hold, like a man listening for something under the floor.

"You're staring," the man said, without turning.

"Sorry." Dominic looked at his pie. It was cherry, in theory.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." The man swiveled on his stool, and Dominic saw his eyes—pale, washed-out blue, the color of glacial melt, and sharp as a tack in all that ruin. "Hand me my hat. It's on the counter behind you. I keep forgetting where I put things. The forgetting is new."

Dominic handed him the hat, a felt thing crushed into the shape of a decade of hard use. The man set it on the bar in front of him and smoothed it with both palms, an absent ritual, a man petting a dog that wasn't there.

"Elias Crane," he said.

"Dominic Vasquez."

"Vasquez." Elias said it like he was tasting it. "Storm man. I saw you come in. You drive like a man being chased, but you park like a man being careful. That's an interesting combination."

"You were watching."

"I was watching the road. You're the only thing that's come down it in two hours." He coughed again, and this time Dominic saw the handkerchief come away and go back into the pocket fast, too fast, the way you hide a losing hand. But Dominic had seen the red on it. "Dot," Elias said. "Another coffee for my new best friend. He's buying."

"I am?"

"You're buying, because you're about to ask me questions, and questions cost." The pale eyes glittered. "Everything costs, Vasquez. That's the first law out here. The second law is that everything costs more than it looks."

Dominic should have laughed and paid for his pie and gone back to his truck and his four hundred and eleven dollars and his smiley-face text message. Instead he heard himself say, "You're sick."

"Observation. That'll be a dollar." But there was no bite in it, and something in the man seemed to settle, the way a load settles on a truck when you cinch the straps. "Cancer. They told me in a clinic in Yuma four months ago. Gave me a pamphlet and a number to call. I gave the pamphlet back. Kept the number in case I run out of other ideas." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the saucer, a man aligning himself with something. "I've been walking my claim ever since. Finishing the survey. A man should finish his work."

"Your claim," Dominic said. "Out here?"

"North. High country." Elias looked at him, and for a moment the tack-sharpness softened into something almost kind. "You're in trouble, son. Money trouble. It's all over you like the dust on that truck of mine. You've got the look of a man doing arithmetic in his head while he's talking to you. I've seen it in every mining camp from here to the border. It's the look of a man who's already sold something he didn't know he owned."

The pie sat in Dominic's throat like a stone. Outside, the last light was draining off the wet flats, and the first stars came out over the black line of the ranges to the north—his north, his debt, his week.

"Everyone's in trouble," Dominic said. "It's the economy."

"It's always the economy." Elias laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough, and the cough was longer this time, and when it was done the man's face had gone the color of the diner's coffee. He sat breathing in small sips. "Dot. Water."

She brought it without being asked. She'd been watching too, Dominic realized. Small towns watch. They know the difference between a man who is tired and a man who is dying, and Dot's eyes, when they flicked to Dominic, said: *you're the one he's been waiting for.*

* * *

They took a booth by the window because Elias said his legs were done with stools. The rain had started again, a fine, drifting rain that beaded the glass and turned the parking lot into a mirror holding one truck and one sign and one long empty road.

Elias unwrapped something from inside his jacket. It was a package in oilcloth, tied with waxed string, flat and rectangular, and he handled it the way you handle a sleeping animal. He set it on the table between them and kept his hand on it.

"Before I show you anything," he said, "I want to know what kind of man you are. Not the good-bad question. That one's useless—everyone's both, and the proportions shift with the weather. I mean the other question." He leaned forward. "When you give your word, does it stay given? Or does it renegotiate?"

Dominic thought about the Delgados. About the smiley face. About the week.

"It stays given," he said. "That's the problem. It's the only thing I've got that stays."

Elias studied him for a long time. The rain ticked on the glass. Somewhere in the kitchen a fan belt squealed and quit.

"June," Elias said at last.

"Your daughter?"

"My daughter." He said the name the way other men say the names of saints. "She lives in Kestrel Pass. Past the range, north side. She's a mechanic—good one, better than good, she could rebuild an engine in her sleep and has. She's twenty-eight." He stopped. Started again. "She's twenty-eight, and she hasn't spoken to me in twenty years, and that is my doing, all of it, and I'm not going to tell you the story because the story is mine to give her, not yours, and I'll not have you carrying my excuses along with my package. You carry the package. That's all."

"What's in it?"

"A map."

Dominic laughed before he could stop himself. "A map. Mister, I've got a map of every drainage in three counties in my head, and half of them are lies somebody sold to somebody. Maps are—"

"Verruga," Elias said.

The word went through the diner like cold water through a hose. Dominic felt his own face change and hated it, because he knew the man saw it. Verruga. The Lost Lode. Every prospector's ghost story, the one they told around fires to make the greenhorns dream: a silver vein so rich the old-timers said you could cut samples with a knife and the assay office laughed at them for bringing in ore *that* good, sure it was salted. Three men had found it in 1891 and two of them died getting out and the third went mad and the location died with him. A hundred and thirty years of people going into those mountains after it and coming out with nothing but frostbite and stories.

"You're lying," Dominic said quietly. "Or you're crazy. Or—"

"Or I found it." Elias's voice was flat as a level. "Eleven years ago. And I've spent eleven years making sure. Eleven years of walking that ridge with a hammer and a hand lens, of assaying samples in three different labs under three different names, of triangulating the old man's dying directions against what's actually in the rock. It's real, Vasquez. It's so real that if I say the word in the wrong bar, men will come out of the woodwork like termites, and some of them will have money, and all of them will have guns, and none of them will have any use for a dying prospector or his daughter." He pulled his hand off the oilcloth package. "Which is why it has to be her. Not a company. Not a syndicate. Her."

"Why you? Why me? Why not a lawyer, a courier, a—"

"Because lawyers cost money I don't have. Because a courier can be bought, and so can a lawyer, and so can a priest if the price is right." Elias coughed, wet and long, and came up gray. "Because you were the first man to stop. Do you understand that? I've sat in six diners in four weeks, watching the road. Truckers stop. Tourists stop. Cops stop. Not one of them looked at me and *saw* me. You looked. You're a man who looks at weather, and a man who looks at weather is a man who knows that what's coming is coming whether he watches or not, and watches anyway." He pushed the package an inch across the table. "I need a man who watches anyway."

Dominic didn't touch it. "What exactly are you asking?"

"I'm asking you to carry this to June Crane, Kestrel Pass, before the snow closes the road. That's it. That's the whole job. You don't open it. You don't read it. You don't sell it, trade it, lose it, or mention it to a living soul except to say you're delivering a dead man's effects to his daughter. You put it in her hands. Then you're done, and whatever's in it is hers, and whatever happens after is hers." His pale eyes held Dominic's with the fixed brightness of a fever. "In exchange—"

"I don't want your money."

"I don't have any money." Elias smiled, and it was a terrible smile, and a true one. "In exchange, I want your word. Your word that it stays given. That's the whole price. That's the only thing I've found in seventy-one years that can't be devalued, and I'm spending all of mine on this."

The rain thickened. On the highway, a pair of headlights went by without slowing, and the sound of them rose and faded like a wave on a far shore. Dominic thought about nineteen thousand four hundred and change. He thought about men who came out of the woodwork with money and guns. He thought about the compass on his seat, his father's voice saying *a needle doesn't argue, it just points*, and he thought about a woman in Kestrel Pass who hadn't spoken to her father in twenty years and was about to receive a package from a stranger instead of a phone call.

"This is a bad trade," Dominic said. "For me. You know that."

"It's the best trade I've ever offered anybody."

"That's not what I—" Dominic stopped. Ran a hand through his hair. "If anyone learns what this is, I'm dead. You understand that. I owe money to people who collect with crowbars and smiles, and if they think I'm carrying a map to the Verruga Lode, they won't ask me for it. They'll just take it, and then they'll take the rest."

"Then don't let them learn," Elias said simply. "A man who keeps a secret is only in danger once. A man who sells it is in danger forever."

The silence stretched. Dot refilled their coffees and retreated without a word, a woman who had seen deals made in this booth before and knew the etiquette of not existing.

Elias untied the string.

Under the oilcloth was a leather folio, soft with handling, and inside that—when Elias opened it just enough for Dominic to see the edge of it—a sheet of paper dense with fine, patient pencil work: contour lines, drainage marks, a ridge drawn in section like a cut of meat, and in one corner, small and precise, a cipher of numbers and letters that looked like nothing at all.

Dominic looked at it for three seconds and looked away, deliberately, like a man closing a door on a lit room.

"I'll take it to her," he said. "Before snowfall. My word."

"Say it whole."

Dominic exhaled. "I, Dominic Vasquez, give my word to Elias Crane that I will carry this to June Crane of Kestrel Pass, unopened and unsold, before the snow closes the passes, and that my word stays given." He heard how it sounded—half oath, half contract, a man who'd spent too much time around ledgers. But Elias's face did something Dominic hadn't seen it do yet: it unclenched. All the sharpness went out of it at once, and what was left underneath was just an old man, sick and tired and lighter by one weight.

"Thank you," Elias said. It came out cracked.

Elias retied the string, wrapped the oilcloth, and slid the package across. It was heavier than it looked, or maybe that was only what Dominic's hands knew about what was inside it. He put it inside his jacket, against his chest, where he could feel it when he breathed.

"Now," Elias said, "buy an old man his dinner. It's the last meal I'll let anyone else pay for, and I intend to complain about it thoroughly."

* * *

They ate chili and talked about nothing—the price of fuel, the dishonesty of tire manufacturers, whether the rain would hold. Elias told two stories about mules, both of which were funny, and one about a flash flood in '89 that was not funny at all, and Dominic found himself laughing in the wrong places and not caring. It had been a long time since he'd sat across from someone who wasn't calculating against him, or being calculated against. The dying man across the table was the most honest company he'd kept in a year.

It was full dark when they came out. The rain had quit, and the sky over the flats was scoured clean, stars in drifts, the Milky Way running north like a road of its own. Elias stood at the door of the International and looked at that northern sky for a long time.

"I can't make the drive myself," he said. "That's the truth of it. I could maybe make it to the foothills, and then I'd be a body on the side of the road for my daughter to identify, and she's had enough of my leavings." He put out his hand. His grip was still strong, a grip from before the sickness, a grip from the man he'd been. "She'll be angry. I want you to know that going in. She has every right, and she'll use it. Don't you argue with her. Don't you defend me. Just give her the package and let her be angry at the right person."

"And who's that?"

"Me," Elias said. "Always me." He let go of Dominic's hand and climbed into the truck with the care of a man handling his own last cargo. The engine caught on the third try. Through the open window, in the dome light, his face looked ten years older than it had at dusk.

"Vasquez. One more thing." He was looking north, at the black teeth of the range. "The passes close early this year. I've watched the sky my whole life and it's telling me the same thing it told the old men in '91: big snow, early. You've got maybe three weeks. Maybe less. And there's a survey crew working the north ridges—Halloran's outfit, out of the coast. If you see their orange flags, you go the other way, and you don't stop to ask why."

"Why? What are they surveying?"

Elias smiled that terrible, true smile.

"Same thing you're carrying," he said, and put the truck in gear, and drove north into the dark, taillights shrinking to two red stars, and gone.

Dominic stood a long time in the empty lot, the package warm against his chest, Dot's coffee cooling in his hand. Three weeks. A survey crew. A debt with a smiley face. He got in his truck and did not start it right away. Instead he sat in the dark and felt the folio's edges through the oilcloth, sharp as a promise, and understood that somewhere behind him, in the dark he'd driven out of, the arithmetic had already changed—and that everyone who counted on him was about to start counting differently.

He started the engine and pointed the needle north.
