## 5. The First Tail

The rain came in off the flats the way debt did — first a gray smudge on the horizon you told yourself was nothing, then a pressure behind your ribs that wouldn't let go.

Dominic Vasquez stood at the counter of the Fuel & Feed with a cup of coffee going cold in his hand and watched the storm build over the eastern mesa. It was a beautiful thing, honestly. A stacked anvil cloud with a bearded underside, hail shafts hanging off it like curtains in a house where somebody had died. Twenty years of chasing had taught him to read storms the way other men read faces, and this one was saying *I am not finished with you yet.*

Neither were the Delgados.

He'd done the arithmetic again at four in the morning, lying in the truck's cab with his boots against the steering wheel and the map folded into the lining of his jacket like a second heartbeat. Fourteen thousand, three hundred and some dollars. Interest compounding weekly. One week to pay or produce collateral, and the only collateral he owned worth anything was currently sewn into his coat and pointed toward a silver lode that could buy him out ten times over — or get him buried in a dry wash with his throat opened and his pockets turned out.

The coffee tasted like burnt pennies. He drank it anyway.

*You're being paranoid,* he told himself, watching the mirror above the register reflect the parking lot behind him. The lot held a rancher's flatbed, a woman's sedan with a cracked taillight, and his own truck, mud-crusted, one headlight fogged with condensation. Nothing else. Nobody leaning on a fender pretending to read a phone. Nobody idling at the pumps with sunglasses on under an overcast sky.

Then the gray pickup rolled past the window without slowing, and paranoia stopped being a word and became a temperature in his blood.

He knew the truck. He'd seen it parked outside the Delgado brothers' office on Cordero Street, back when he still walked in through the front door with his hat in his hand like a man visiting family. F-250, gunmetal gray, a hairline crack across the rear glass shaped like a lightning bolt. The kind of detail you noticed when you spent enough time in rooms where men discussed what you owed them in voices so pleasant they might have been discussing rainfall.

Two silhouettes inside. The passenger's arm hung out the window, fingers drumming the door frame in no particular hurry.

Dominic set the coffee down, left a five under the cup, and walked out to his truck at the pace of a man who had nowhere in particular to be. His heart was doing something else entirely. It was running ahead of him down the highway already, kicking up dust.

*Don't run,* he thought, turning the ignition. The engine caught with its usual asthmatic cough. *Running is a confession.*

He pulled out onto the two-lane heading east, toward the storm, because east was where he'd been going anyway and because the storm gave him cover the way a magician gives you a beautiful thing to look at while his hands do the real work. Behind him, half a mile back, the gray pickup eased out of town after him like a shadow detaching from a wall.

* * *

For twenty miles he played it straight. Sixty miles an hour, both hands loose on the wheel, radio murmuring farm reports through static. He watched the pickup in his mirrors the way you watch a storm cell on radar — noting its drift, its speed, whether it was organizing or falling apart.

It wasn't falling apart. It held his distance exactly, patient as interest accruing, close enough to see but never close enough to read a plate. That discipline frightened him more than aggression would have. Aggression meant panic. Patience meant orders.

At the junction where 191 bent north toward the foothills, he made his decision. There was a county road cutting southeast through the badlands — BLM land, cattle guards, washboard gravel, and eventually the network of dry washes that stitched the desert below the mesa like scar tissue. He knew that country. He'd chased hail down those two-tracks for a soil conservation survey three summers ago, back when his debts were merely embarrassing instead of mortal.

He took the turn without signaling.

In the mirror, the pickup slowed at the junction, hung there for a long five-count — deciding, confirming, calling someone, maybe — then swung onto the gravel after him and began to close.

*All right,* Dominic thought, and felt something in his chest that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite joy but lived in the neighborhood of both. *Let's find out who you are.*

The rain reached them before the badlands did. It came down the face of the mesa in visible gray sheets, and when it hit the gravel it didn't fall so much as arrive, everywhere at once, hammering the roof like a crowd demanding entry. His wipers churned uselessly. The world outside the glass went soft and swimming, the road a pale ribbon unspooling into nothing, and he drove by feel — the crown of the grade, the rattle of the cattle guard, the way the wheel wanted to wander left where the shoulder crumbled.

The headlights appeared in his mirror and stayed there. Closer now. Close enough that he could make out the shape of the push bar on the pickup's front end, the aftermarket kind with the welded points, the kind that turned a vehicle into a weapon if you believed in that sort of application.

His phone buzzed in the cup holder. Unknown number. He let it ring itself out. Then again. On the third call he answered without a word, just to hear.

"Vasquez." A voice he recognized — Ray Delgado, the middle brother, the one who did collections with the smile of a man recommending a restaurant. "Hey. You got a taillight out, friend. Thought you'd want to know."

"Appreciate the concern."

"We're concerned guys." Rain static chewed at the signal. "Thing is, we been looking for you. Got some paperwork. Elias Crane — you know that name? Old-timer found dead up by the diner two days back. Sad story. Sadder still, folks say he was seen talking to you. Real friendly-like. Trading things."

Dominic kept his voice level, his eyes on the ribbon of road. "I give people rides sometimes. I'm a generous guy."

"You're a leveraged guy," Ray said pleasantly. "That's different. Here's the generous part, though. You pull over, we talk about the map, maybe we talk about your balance sheet getting some forgiveness in it. Everybody drives home dry. Or—" a pause, filled with rain, "—you keep driving out into the middle of nowhere in a storm, and me and my cousin here start wondering why a man with fourteen grand outstanding is acting like a man with something to hide. And guys like us, we get curious, and curiosity costs."

"Ray," Dominic said. "Do me a favor. Look out your window. See that cell building over the mesa? That's a supercell. That thing's gonna drop hail the size of fists on this road inside twenty minutes, and you're tailing a storm chaser through it. Ask yourself who knows this country better."

Silence on the line. Then, softly, almost kindly: "We know it good enough."

The call died. Ahead, the road dropped through a cut in the rimrock and the badlands opened below — a maze of clay hills and arroyos, all of it turning the color of dried blood as the light failed, all of it about to become soup.

*Good enough* wasn't the same as better than him.

* * *

He took them into the washes at forty miles an hour, which on that ground was a prayer with a speedometer attached.

The two-track dove off the county road and immediately began to lie to him — switchbacking down through the clay, fording shallow draws where water already ran brown and fast, climbing ridges barely wider than his axles with air on both sides. The truck slewed and caught itself. Mud packed into the wheel wells in heavy wet ropes. Behind him the headlights held on, relentless, and once, when he crested a saddle, he saw the gray pickup sideways in a curve below, throwing a rooster tail of red water, its driver correcting hard, committed, angry now.

The hail arrived on schedule.

It started as a ticking on the roof, then a drumming, then a violence — white stones bouncing waist-high off the hood, shattering against the windshield in blooms of frost that the wipers smeared into blindness. Visibility dropped to thirty feet. The world became noise and white and the memory of where the road had been. Dominic killed his headlights — no sense advertising himself — and drove on instinct and the ghost of the terrain in his head: down this draw, left at the split juniper, along the bench above the big arroyo.

The headlights behind him vanished. Whether they'd stopped or turned or gone into a ditch he couldn't know, and he couldn't afford to care yet.

There was a place — he'd camped near it once, waiting out a hail core, eating cold beans while the sky threw rocks — where the main wash forked around a spine of rock, and the western branch ran narrow and deep between clay walls for a quarter mile before opening into a bowl. In dry weather it was a road. In weather like this it was about to become a river, which meant he had minutes, maybe less, and the math was simple: drive in, park deep in the narrows where the walls would hide the truck from above, and let the flash flood erase everything behind him.

Or misjudge it, and let the flash flood erase him too.

"Hold together, old girl," he said to the truck, and took the fork.

The narrows closed around him like a throat. Clay walls rose eight feet on either side, streaming with water, and the floor of the wash ran shin-deep and rising. He pushed upstream — figuratively; the flood would come down — until the bend hid him completely from the sky, then shoved the transmission into park and sat with his hands on the wheel, breathing, listening to the hail turn the world into applause for something terrible.

Water climbed. Ankle-deep on the tires. Mid-door. He watched it with the professional calm he didn't feel, remembering the gauge of that wash, the gradient above, the size of the country draining into it. It would crest and hold, crest and hold, and it would not reach the cab. Probably. Storm chasing was mostly *probably*, and every chaser he'd ever admired had eventually been wrong about one.

Through the windshield, the hail thinned to rain, and the rain fell straight and silver in the last gray light, and somewhere above and behind him, faint under the water-noise, he heard an engine revving and failing to find traction, and then nothing at all.

*They'll walk out,* he told himself. *Ray Delgado has walked out of worse. Men like that always do.* It wasn't comfort. It was bookkeeping — he didn't want their deaths on his ledger any more than he wanted his own.

Darkness came down. He ate half a bag of jerky and let the adrenaline drain out of him in slow, shaking increments, and when his hands finally steadied he did the thing he'd been avoiding since the diner. He unfolded the map.

Elias Crane's hand was shaky but sure — a draftsman's bones under a dying man's tremor. Ridge lines drawn in confident strokes, elevations noted in a cramped pencil script, and there, in the fold of the high country north of Kestrel Pass, a small star and two words: *Verruga. Ours?* The question mark undid him a little. Twenty years prospecting and the old man still hadn't let himself believe it.

Below the star, a cipher — numbers and letters that meant nothing yet, that Elias had promised would mean everything at the right landmark.

And beneath the paper, when he tilted it toward the dome light, the faint rectangular ghosts of stitching — something sewn into the backing, flat and many-paged. Letters, maybe. He hadn't looked. He wouldn't, not yet. Some promises had edges you didn't test until you had to.

Outside, the flood in the narrows crested, held, and began its slow retreat, leaving the clay gleaming like something newly born. The stars came out hard and cold between the breaking clouds.

Somewhere south of him, two men from Cordero Street were presumably walking out of the badlands, soaked and furious, carrying his name back to men who smiled while they did arithmetic. The hunt had begun in earnest now; there was no version of the story where it quietly ended. He'd lost them tonight, but losing wasn't the same as escaping, and everyone in this desert knew where the only road to Kestrel Pass began.

Dominic folded the map away, laid the seat back, and slept in the narrows with the water whispering down around him — and dreamed, for the first time in years, not of debt, but of a girl in a garage he'd never met, holding a compass she didn't know was coming.
