## 6. June Crane Hates Her Father

The rain came to Corazón Flats sideways that October, the way it always did when the monsoon decided to spend its last money in the foothills before climbing into the high country and dying as snow. June Crane heard it coming twenty minutes before it arrived — a sound like freight cars rolling down a grade that didn't exist — and she used those twenty minutes the way she used every hour of every day: working.

She was under a '68 pickup with a cracked head gasket, flat on the creeper, staring up at the rusted underbelly of someone else's bad decisions. This was the part of the job nobody romanticized, the part where you lay in the dark under a machine and listened to it tell you everything wrong with it in a language of drips and ticks and slow corrosive silence. June liked it down there. Down there, problems had causes. Down there, if something was broken, it was because a part had failed or a person had neglected it, and either way you could name the failure, order the part, torque the bolt, and be done. Nothing down there had walked out on her twenty years ago with a promise in its mouth like a stolen apple.

The first drops hit the tin roof of Crane & Daughter Auto — her father's joke of a sign, painted by his own hand back when he still thought jokes were things you made instead of things you were — and the whole building rang like a struck bell.

"Storm's here," called Denny from the office, where he did the books badly and drank coffee worse. He'd been with her six years, a lanky ex-line cook with hands too soft for pistons and a heart too stubborn to quit. "Radio says flash flood watch for the canyon road."

"Then nobody's driving anywhere," June said, sliding out on the creeper. Her coveralls were black to the elbow with old oil, and there was a smear of it across her jaw where she'd pushed her hair back without thinking. Forty-one years old, and she wore her father's build like an accusation: broad shoulders, big hands, the same heavy brow. People in town said she looked like Elias Crane, and they always said it carefully, the way you mention weather to someone whose house flooded.

"Anything else on the board?" Denny asked.

"The Herrera transmission. And the school bus needs its brakes bled before Monday, so don't let me forget."

"You never forget anything," Denny said. It wasn't a compliment exactly. It was more like a report on the local geography: June Crane does not forget. Mountains do not forget. Rain does not forget the sea.

She wiped her hands on a rag that had been gray since the Clinton administration and stood at the open bay door watching the storm come down the flats toward town. It moved like something alive, a gray wall dragging curtains of rain behind it, and lightning flickered inside it the way a thought flickers behind a closed eye. Twenty years ago, she would have gone out and stood in it. Twenty years ago, she'd been fifteen and her father had been the biggest man in any room, a prospector with forearms like bridge cable who could read clouds the way priests read scripture, and when the storms came he'd lift her onto the fence rail and name them — *that one's all hat, mija, no cattle; that one there, that one means business* — and she had believed, with the total faith of a child, that a man who could read storms could read anything.

Then he'd read the horizon one morning in early spring and said *I'll be back before the passes close*, and he hadn't been.

* * *

Here is what June Crane knew about her father, in the ledger she kept the way other people kept grudges — carefully, in permanent ink:

He left in April. He said three weeks. He sent nothing for eleven months. Then a postcard from a town so small it shared a zip code with a mine, saying *work is good, saving up, tell your mother I love her*. Her mother framed it. That was the kind of woman Rosa Crane had been — she framed the evidence of her own abandonment and hung it over the kitchen sink where she washed dishes for other people's families because Elias's prospecting money never materialized and somebody had to keep the lights on.

Her mother got sick two years later. Bad sick, the kind that starts quietly in the blood and then announces itself all at once, the way floods do. June wrote to the address on the postcard. The letter came back marked *addressee unknown*. She wrote again, care of the mining company. Nothing. She wrote to the county clerk, the assessor, a cousin in the territorial capital who knew people. What came back, eventually, was a rumor: Elias Crane had been seen in a saloon in Verruga Gulch, flush, loud, buying rounds, telling everyone within earshot about silver in the high country, silver enough to buy back every debt he ever owed. Silver enough to make his family rich.

That was the spring her mother died. June was seventeen. She buried Rosa Crane on a Tuesday in the rain and sold the house on a Friday because grief is expensive and nobody tells you that, and she took what was left — her mother's wedding ring, a toolbox, and the truck her father had bought new the year she was born and never bothered to sell — and she drove until the flats turned to foothills and the foothills turned to this town, which had a garage for sale and no questions worth answering.

She'd been here twenty-three years now. Long enough that the town had stopped thinking of her as the prospector's daughter and started thinking of her as the mechanic, which was the only inheritance she'd ever claimed. She fixed what her father broke. It wasn't a philosophy. It was just arithmetic.

And once a year, sometimes twice, some dusty wanderer came through asking after Elias Crane — old partners, old creditors, once a lawyer with a briefcase full of nothing — and each time June gave the same answer, delivered flat as a level: *Don't know him.* Each time the lie got easier. Each time it cost a little less. That was the thing about armor nobody warned you about: it fit better every year, until you couldn't remember what your own skin felt like against the air.

* * *

The storm hit Corazón Flats at four in the afternoon with the subtlety of a train derailment. Rain came down in ropes. The arroyo behind the garage, dry eleven months of the year, woke up brown and roaring, carrying fence posts and a plastic kiddie pool and somebody's lost dog, which swam out at the bend and shook itself off looking personally offended. Lightning walked the ridge tops. The power flickered twice and held.

June worked through it. There was always work, and work was the tide that kept the past offshore. By six the light had gone strange and green, the color the sky turns when it can't decide between rain and hail, and Denny had gone home to his wife, and June was alone in the garage with the radio murmuring flood warnings and the smell of ozone and hot metal, replacing a water pump by lamplight because the Herrera kid needed the truck tomorrow and promises, unlike fathers, were things June kept.

She heard the vehicle before she saw it — an engine laboring up the flooded street, low gear, careful throttle, someone who knew how to drive water. Headlights swung across the bay door and stopped. A knock. Then, when she didn't answer, a voice pitched to carry over the rain:

"Ma'am? Looking for a June Crane. Garage on Alder Street?"

June set down her wrench. She didn't move for a moment. Strangers asking for her by name, in a storm, at dusk — the last time that happened, the lawyer with the briefcase had wanted her signature on papers declaring Elias Crane legally dead, seven years after the last anyone saw him, and she had signed them standing right there in this doorway, feeling nothing, absolutely nothing, and had gone back inside and finished a brake job with hands that never shook once.

She crossed the concrete floor, flipped the deadbolt, and opened the door eight inches, chain on.

A man stood on her step getting rained on and apparently not caring. Late thirties maybe, lean in the way of people who live out of trucks, dark hair plastered down, a face that had been handsome before the road got its teeth into it. Mud to the knees. Eyes that did a quick professional inventory of her — the way hers did of engines — and then settled.

"June Crane?"

"Who's asking?"

"Dominic Vasquez." He said it like a name he was tired of carrying. "I need to talk to you about your father."

There it was. The sentence she'd been dodging for two decades, finally catching up with her in a downpour. June felt the old machinery engage — the cold, the flatness, the door already beginning to swing shut — and then the man raised his hand into the gap in the door, into the wedge of lamplight spilling out around her, and the light caught what he was holding.

A compass. Brass, palm-sized, the case worn to a dull gold along the edges where a thumb had worried it ten thousand times. The glass cracked in one corner. And on the back, scratched deep with a knife point, in handwriting she would have known blind, underwater, at the bottom of the sea:

*J.C. — find true north. Love, Papá.*

The world went narrow. The rain became a sound very far away. June was fifteen again, standing on a fence rail, and the compass was turning in her father's big fingers, and he was saying *a compass doesn't care how you feel, mija, it only knows where north is, and that's why you trust it more than people* — and then the world snapped back, twenty-three years wide, and the man on her step was watching her face with the expression of someone who has just watched a door close behind another person's eyes.

"Where," June said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, roughed up at the edges, "did you get that."

"He gave it to me." Dominic shifted his weight. Water ran off his collar. "Three days ago. At a diner outside Las Uvas. He was—" A hesitation, and she understood it before he finished, the way you understand a diagnosis from the shape of the pause. "He was dying. Sick, and hurt. He asked me to bring it to you. Along with a map."

"A map."

"To somewhere in the high country. He wanted you to have it before snowfall. Those were his words. Before snowfall."

June looked at the compass. The needle inside the cracked glass trembled slightly with each gust off the storm, swinging, settling, swinging — pointing faithfully at a north that had never pointed anybody home. Twenty years of not waiting sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Twenty years of *don't know him*, worn smooth as river rock, and now here was a stranger dripping on her step holding the one object on earth that could testify against her, engraved with a love she had spent half her life insisting had never existed.

Her father was dying. Had died? Was — the tense wouldn't settle, and neither would she.

"So he sends a stranger," she said. "Not a letter in twenty years. Not a phone call. Not one dollar when my mother was dying in a county ward. But the minute he needs a delivery boy, suddenly he remembers he has a daughter." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound, and she hated it. "What did it cost him? To hand you that? Did he cry? They always cry at the end. It's cheap. Grief is the cheapest thing there is when you're the one who caused it."

"Ma'am—"

"I'm not your ma'am." She unhooked the chain. Not to let him in — to make the point that the conversation was over. "You want a garage, I open at seven. You want charity, the church is two blocks east. You want to talk about Elias Crane, you're talking to a woman whose father is legally dead, and has been for years, and I signed the papers myself, and it was the easiest signature I ever put down."

"That's not true," Dominic said quietly. "You're shaking."

June looked down at her own hands. They were shaking. Oil-stained, scarred, steady under a running engine at two in the morning, steady under a collapsing jack, steady through her mother's funeral and the auction of her childhood — and shaking now, in front of a stranger, because of a piece of brass the size of a pocket watch.

She hated him a little for noticing. She hated her father entirely for causing it.

"Give me the map," she said.

"It's not mine to give. He made me swear. I swore." Dominic's jaw tightened, and behind the road-weariness she saw something she recognized from her own mirror: a man doing arithmetic with his conscience and hating the total. "I'm delivering it. That's the deal. But you should know — I'm not the only one who wants what's on it. There are men following me. Men who work for people who'd burn this town down for what your father found. So whatever you decide, decide fast, because the passes close soon and they know I'm headed here."

The wind slammed the rain against the building hard enough to rattle the windows. Somewhere up the street, the arroyo roared louder, eating its banks.

June stood in the doorway with the storm at her back and the stranger in front of her and the compass hanging in the space between them like a verdict. Every year of armor told her to close the door. Every year of armor said: *you owe that man nothing, he spent his debts on strangers' ears and saloon rounds while you learned to drive a hearse.*

But the compass needle, trembling in its cracked glass, kept finding north. Through everything. Through the storm, through the shaking, through twenty years of lies. It only knew where north was. That was why you trusted it more than people.

"Wait here," June said, and shut the door in his face.

She stood in the lamplight, breathing. Then she pulled the tarp off the shelf at the back of the garage — off the box she hadn't opened since the funeral, the box of Elias Crane's effects the lawyer had shipped her like a bill of lading — and dug beneath the folded shirts to the bottom, where a photograph lay face-down in a leather frame. Her mother, young, laughing, on a fence rail. And beside her, squinting into a sun that no longer existed, the biggest man in any room.

June put the photograph in her jacket, inside the pocket over her heart, where it couldn't see what she was about to do.

When she opened the door again, Dominic Vasquez was still there, soaked through, patient as weather.

"Get your truck off my street before it floods," June Crane said. "You're going to tell me everything. Starting with how my father died. And then—" she jerked her chin at the black wall of the storm, rolling toward the high country, toward the passes, toward whatever her father had found and whatever it had cost, "—then we're going to talk about whether I let you keep that map, or whether I take it and go find out for myself what was worth twenty years."

Lightning lit the ridge line white, and far up the flooded street, half-hidden in the rain, headlights blinked on — a gray pickup, parked dark since dusk, engine turning over.
