## 7. Monsoon Season

The sky had been lying to him all afternoon.

Dominic had learned to read weather the way other men read faces, and this one wore the expression of a poker player holding a full house — pleasant, blue-eyed, utterly false. The storm that had chased him out of the flats was dying behind him, its anvil collapsing into gray rags, and ahead lay nothing but heat shimmer and the long brown spine of the foothills. Good driving weather. Deceiving weather.

He'd stopped at a gas station outside Perdiz to fill the tank and buy coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration. The map rode in his jacket's inner pocket, against his ribs, where he could feel it with every heartbeat like a second pulse. He'd taken to checking for it the way a man checks for a wallet in a bad neighborhood — a quick pat, a reassurance, a lie he told himself about being careful rather than afraid.

The girl behind the register watched him pay with crumpled bills and said nothing, but her eyes did arithmetic. Everyone's eyes did arithmetic on him lately. He was starting to feel like a word problem.

Back on the road, the deception ripened. Clouds he'd dismissed as leftover debris began to stack themselves over the western range with an ambition that made his neck prickle. By the time he turned onto the gravel cut that climbed toward the pass road, they had gone from white to bruise to a green-black color he associated with hail and worse. The air pressure dropped so fast his ears popped twice. The wind died completely — that dead-calm stillness that wasn't peace but held breath.

Then the sky opened like a wound.

Not rain. Rain was polite. This was a vertical ocean, a cataract that hit the windshield with enough force to make the wipers surrender after two passes and simply smear. Dominic pulled onto the shoulder, engine idling, and watched the world dissolve. Visibility went from a mile to a hundred yards to nothing, just white roaring chaos and the sound of ten thousand drums played by someone furious.

He should have stayed put. He knew that. But the light was failing and the Delgados' week was ticking down and there was a motel forty miles up the cut, and forty miles of bad gravel in a monsoon cell seemed survivable if he drove stupid enough.

He drove stupid enough.

The arroyo crossed the road three miles on. He'd noted it coming in — a dry sandy channel thirty feet wide, benign as a scar. Coming back down the grade through the deluge, his headlights swept across it and found it no longer dry, no longer sandy, no longer benign. It was a moving wall. Brown water thick as batter, carrying whole mesquite trees, fence posts, half a stock tank, something that might have been a refrigerator, all of it traveling at the speed of a freight train with nowhere to be except downhill.

He braked too late on the slick clay. The truck's rear end stepped out, caught, slewed sideways, and slid — gently, almost apologetically — off the shoulder and down the embankment until the frame settled into the mud with a finality that sounded like a door closing.

Dominic sat with both hands on the wheel and listened to the rain hammer the roof and did some arithmetic of his own. Truck: stuck past his ability to unstick it. Phone: one bar, no signal in this cut, probably. Cash: enough for two more days of cheap food. Time: six days left on the Delgados' calendar. Options: shrinking faster than the visibility.

"Well," he said to the cab, because talking to himself had become a habit somewhere between the diner and now, "you wanted adventure."

He climbed out into the rain to assess the damage and got drenched to the skin in four seconds flat. The truck was nose-down in the wash's floodplain, rear axle buried to the differential, high-centered on a shelf of clay that the receding water was already undermining. He could see that much even in the dark. What he couldn't see was any way out of this cut until morning, and morning depended on whether the rain stopped, which it showed no sign of doing.

That was when the flashlight found him.

A beam swung out of the darkness upstream, steady and unhurried, and a voice came with it — a woman's voice, pitched to carry over the water without shouting, the voice of someone accustomed to making herself heard in bad conditions.

"You're either very brave or very stupid to be standing in a flash flood zone!"

"Statistically, stupid!" he called back.

"Good! Stupid I can work with. Brave people argue!" The beam steadied on him. "There's an overhang two hundred meters upstream. Rock shelter. You can't stay here — the water's still rising and your truck is in the debris path!"

He looked at his truck. His truck looked back at him, headlights drowning, already listing as the clay beneath it slumped. She was right, and he hated it, and he waded toward the light.

* * *

The rock shelter was a limestone overhang above the old channel, maybe twenty feet deep, floored with sand and the leavings of a hundred years of travelers — old cans, a rusted bed frame, initials carved into stone by hands that were dust now. A small camp stove hissed at the back, and beside it sat the owner of the voice: a woman in her fifties, silver-streaked hair plastered to her skull, wearing a rain shell older than Dominic and rubber boots that had been patched with what looked like bicycle inner tube. She was sorting pressed plant specimens into waterproof bags with the tenderness of a nurse handling infants.

Beside her, wrapped in a wool blanket and looking at Dominic with the flat, unblinking assessment of a cat deciding about a new houseguest, sat a boy of perhaps twelve.

"Odile Fontaine," the woman said, not looking up from her specimens. "Botanist. That's Teo. He doesn't talk much to strangers, so don't take it personally. Take off your shirt before you die of hypothermia and ruin my evening."

"I'm fine."

"You're shivering hard enough to buzz. Shirt. There's a towel in the green bag." Now she looked up, and her eyes were quick and dark and missed nothing. "You're the man who was asking questions at the Perdiz store about the pass road. Teo saw you."

Dominic glanced at the boy. Teo stared back, unrepentant, blanket pulled to his chin.

"Guilty," Dominic said. "I'm headed to Kestrel Pass."

"Mm." She handed him the towel herself, having apparently concluded he'd die first and apologize later. "Everyone who goes to Kestrel Pass is running from something or toward something. Which are you?"

"Toward," he said. "I'm delivering something. A promise."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction — the only concession she made to interest. "A promise. Those are rare cargo these days." She returned to her plants. "We're going the same direction. Up the high route. The low road will be gone by morning — you can hear it eating itself already."

She was right. Under the rain's roar, from downstream, came a deep grinding groan, geological, patient, the sound of the cut rearranging itself without consulting anyone.

"The high route?" Dominic rubbed the towel through his hair. "I heard the high route adds two days."

"It adds two days and subtracts your life, if you take the low road tomorrow. That wash has killed better drivers than you." She snapped a rubber band around a specimen bag. "You can wait here for the county to dig you out, which will take a week, or you can come with us at first light. We know the way. Teo knows it better than I do — he's got a memory like a topographic map."

Teo said nothing, but something in his posture shifted, a fractional straightening, the way a boy stands when he's been praised and pretends not to care.

"Why would you take me along?" Dominic asked. "You don't know me."

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "But you're stuck, we're here, and the mountain doesn't care how many of us it kills, so numbers help." She tied off another bag. "Also, you have a truck full of gear and a winch, and my vehicle is a Subaru with the moral constitution of a goat. It got us here. It will not get us further."

From under the overhang's lip, where the rain fell in a solid gray curtain, came a sound like the end of the world having a modest local event. The wash, freed of its banks, was doing its work.

Dominic looked at the woman, at the silent boy, at the rain, at the direction of Kestrel Pass, invisible now behind water and night. Six days. Two lost to a detour meant four left, and four was thin ice over deep water.

"First light," he said.

"First light," Odile agreed, and tossed him a foil blanket. "Sleep fast. Dawn comes early in monsoon season, and it always brings company."

* * *

Dawn brought not company but revelation: the low road was simply gone.

They stood at the edge of the cut where the highway had been and looked at a canyon. Not a damaged road, not a washed-out stretch — a canyon, fifty feet deep in places, raw earth walls bleeding water, the truck a distant orange smudge at the bottom of it, half-buried, abandoned to the geology. Somewhere in the night the flood had doubled itself, and the road between here and the pass had been deleted from the world.

Dominic felt the loss in his chest like a physical thing — the truck was the last asset he owned outright, the one thing the Delgados hadn't papered over. But there was nothing to be done, and standing there grieving made the loss heavier, so he filed it away next to everything else he owed and turned uphill.

Odile's Subaru sat where they'd left it, miraculously alive, and they loaded it in the gray light — her specimen cases, Teo's pack, a tent that had seen three continents, and Dominic's duffel with everything that mattered to him, including the map, which he transferred to his jacket pocket again with a pat that was becoming ritual.

Teo appeared at his elbow while he cinched the last strap. The boy pointed at the ruined road below, then at Dominic, then made a gesture — hand flat, sliding down, then curling into a fist.

"Yeah," Dominic said. "It took the truck."

Teo studied him for a moment, then reached into his own jacket and produced something: a smooth gray stone, river-worn, oval, warm-looking. He pressed it into Dominic's palm and closed the man's fingers over it with both small hands, then walked away without a word.

"What did he give you?" Odile asked, watching from the driver's seat.

"He didn't say."

"He won't. He gives stones to people he's decided to keep." She started the engine. "Congratulations. He's decided."

The high route was less a road than a rumor — two tracks of packed dirt switchbacking up through juniper and piñon, climbing along the flank of the range where the air went thin and cold and smelled of wet stone and pine resin. Odile drove it like a surgeon, patient and precise, narrating hazards in a low murmur: washout, ledge, scree fan. Teo rode shotgun and navigated with a folded paper map covered in his own annotations, tiny precise marks in colored pencil, a cartography of a landscape he clearly knew the way other kids knew video games.

They stopped at midday at a spring that Odile pronounced safe and drank from tin cups, and it was there, in the thin bright silence after the storm, that the questions started.

"So," Odile said, squinting at him over her cup. "Kestrel Pass. A promise. Whose?"

"A dying man's. I met him at a diner outside Perdiz. Elias Crane. He was coughing blood into a napkin and drawing a map on butcher paper, and he traded me the map for my word that I'd carry it to his daughter before the snow closed the passes."

"And the daughter?"

"June Crane. Mechanic, I'm told. Hates her father, from what the diner waitress said. Twenty years of not waiting for him."

Odile was quiet for a moment, turning her cup. "And what are you carrying, exactly? Besides paper."

Dominic weighed the lie and found it heavy, and the truth heavier, and chose the truth because lying to a woman who'd saved his life over eggs and tin cups felt like a debt he didn't need.

"A map to a lost silver lode. The Verruga. Worth more than anything I'll ever own."

"Ah." She nodded slowly. "And the people who want it?"

"How do you know people want it?"

"Because everything worth carrying across a mountain in monsoon season is wanted by somebody, and nobody hires a stranger to deliver treasure out of kindness." Her dark eyes were kind anyway. "Who's following you, Dominic?"

He told her. Most of it. The Delgados, the debt, the week, the collectors in the gray pickup he'd lost in a dry wash two counties back. He left out Halloran, mostly because he hadn't met Halloran yet, though the name would arrive soon enough like everything else in this story.

Odile listened without interrupting, which was rarer than honesty. When he finished she looked out at the range for a long time, at the clouds already rebuilding themselves over the western peaks, stacking up their afternoon ambitions.

"My husband died in a flood," she said finally. "Flash flood, in the Chiricahuas, nine years ago. He was a hydrologist. He understood water better than anyone I ever met, and it took him anyway." She turned the cup in her fingers. "Teo's parents died in one too. Three years ago, near Nogales. I found him afterward, in a church shelter, and he hadn't spoken since. Not a word. He talks with his hands and his maps and his stones." She glanced at the boy, who was crouched at the spring's edge, arranging pebbles in a line with total absorption. "So understand that when he gave you that stone, he wasn't being cute. He was doing the bravest thing he knows how to do. He was betting on another person."

Dominic looked at the stone in his palm. Gray, ordinary, warm from the boy's pocket. He closed his hand around it.

"I'm not a good bet," he said quietly.

"Nobody is. That's why bets matter." Odile stood, brushing off her pants, brisk again, the moment sealed away wherever she kept such things. "Come on. Storms build at noon in this country, and I want to make the saddle before the sky remembers what it's angry about."

* * *

They made the saddle by three o'clock, and the sky remembered by four.

This cell was different from yesterday's — colder, higher, carrying that green-black weight that meant hail, and it came down the eastern face of the range like something poured. They sheltered the Subaru in a hollow below an outcrop and themselves under the outcrop, and watched lightning walk the ridgelines with long white fingers, touching things, setting small fires the rain immediately drowned.

Teo sat close to Dominic. Not touching, but close, closer than he needed to be, and when the thunder cracked directly overhead — a sound like the sky splitting along a seam — the boy flinched hard, whole body, and his hand shot out and gripped Dominic's sleeve.

He didn't look over. He gripped and stared straight ahead at the rain, jaw tight, breathing in deliberate counts, and Dominic sat very still and let him hold on, and thought about a boy in a church shelter who hadn't spoken in three years, and about what it cost a person to reach out anyway.

When the worst had passed and the cell moved east, grinding its thunder over the far mesas, Teo let go. He smoothed Dominic's sleeve where he'd crushed it, an apology in gesture, and retreated behind his map.

"He hasn't done that in a year," Odile said quietly, packing the stove. "Held on to someone."

"I didn't do anything."

"No. That's usually how it works." She zipped the bag. "Camp's two miles on. There's a flat above the creek with decent drainage. We'll make it before dark if the road holds."

The road held. The flat was there, exactly as advertised, and they raised the tent and built a small fire under a lean-to of tarp, and ate beans and rice and canned peaches while the clouds over the peaks went pink and then purple and then honest black, studded suddenly with more stars than seemed legal.

It was Teo who broke the evening open.

He'd been drawing in his notebook by firelight — he drew constantly, Odile said; it was how he thought — and after a while he tore out a page and handed it across the fire to Dominic without ceremony.

On it, in colored pencil, was a truck. Orange, detailed, lovingly rendered down to the mud flaps, sitting at the bottom of a canyon with water around its wheels. And beside the truck, drawn with equal care, was a man standing on the canyon rim, and the man was waving. Not drowning. Waving. Alive, in the grammar of a twelve-year-old who communicated in pictures because words had been taken from him along with his parents.

Below the truck, in block letters copied from a road sign: COME BACK LATER.

Dominic stared at the page for a long time. Something in his chest had gone tight and hot, and he had to clear his throat before he could speak.

"That's pretty good," he managed. "The truck especially. You got the mud flaps right."

Teo shrugged, elaborately casual, ears going red in the firelight.

"We'll come back for it," Dominic said. "After the passes. If the road lets us."

Teo nodded once, satisfied, and took the page back, and folded it, and put it in his notebook, and that was apparently the end of the transaction.

Later, when the boy slept in the tent and the fire had burned down to a red heart, Odile sat across from Dominic with her knees drawn up and asked the question he'd been waiting for since the spring.

"What's in it for you? Truly. A man doesn't cross a range in monsoon season for a dead stranger's promise unless something's broken in him that needs the crossing."

Dominic fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"There's a ledger," he said. "With my name in it. And men who collect with smiles. Carrying this map is the only honest thing I've done in two years, and I think—" He stopped. Started again. "I think I needed to find out if I'm the kind of man who keeps promises or the kind who sells them. Because both kinds are in me, and I honestly don't know which one wins."

Odile considered this with the seriousness she gave everything, storms and specimens and strangers alike.

"Then the mountain will tell you," she said. "It's good at that. Better than ledgers." She stood and stretched. "Sleep, Dominic. Tomorrow we climb into the cloud forest, and after that the high country, and after that—" she paused at the tent flap, silhouetted against the nylon glow, "—after that, whatever's chasing you catches up. Things always do. Best to decide who you are before they arrive."

Inside the tent, in the dark, Dominic touched the stone in his pocket, and the map behind it, and listened to the creek running loud and clean below the camp, carrying the mountain down toward the sea one cupful at a time.

Two days lost. Four remaining. And somewhere behind them, on a road that no longer existed or on one that did, he could not shake the feeling that headlights were already climbing.
