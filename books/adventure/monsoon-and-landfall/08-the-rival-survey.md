## 8. The Rival Survey

The ridge had been waiting for him longer than he'd been driving toward it.

Dominic saw it first as a suggestion, a blue-gray rumor stacked against the horizon where the monsoon clouds broke apart like wet paper. The Verruga country. Elias Crane's map called it nothing so grand — just a pencil scrawl of elevations and a crosshatched square the old man had labeled *the throat* — but Dominic had spent three nights with the thing spread across his sleeping bag, tracing its lines by flashlight, and he'd come to know it the way you come to know a face in a photograph: intimately, and without ever having met it.

He topped the last rise before noon, truck straining in second gear, and pulled off onto a shelf of gravel where the road gave up pretending to be a road. Below him the valley opened like a cupped hand — sagebrush to the creek line, creek line to the talus slopes, and above all of it the ridge itself, running north-south like a spine, its granite shoulders dark with rain. The air smelled of wet stone and lightning that had already happened. Somewhere behind the peaks, thunder was still clearing its throat.

He got out. Stood with his hands on the warm hood, letting the engine tick itself cool, and did what he always did when the land finally showed itself: he read it. Wind out of the southwest, maybe fifteen knots, carrying the smell of more water. Cumulus building over the high country with that bruised, organized look he didn't like — not scattered fair-weather puffs but towers, stacking, feeding on each other. By four o'clock the whole sky over the ridge would be a argument between light and dark.

Good chasing weather. Bad everything-else weather.

It was then he saw the tents.

They were pitched in the lee of a moraine maybe two miles up-valley, close enough to count if you knew how to look — and Dominic knew how to look. Four of them, dome-style, the industrial orange of equipment nobody bought with their own money. A white pickup sat beside them, clean, new, its bed racked with survey tripods. And from one of the higher benches above the camp, a figure was moving along the contour with an instrument on a tripod, the way a man moves when he's being paid by the hour and knows exactly where the hours go.

Dominic stood very still.

He thought about the map folded into his jacket, against his ribs, where it had lived for six days now like a second heartbeat. He thought about the Delgados' arithmetic, the cheerful voices on the phone, the week they'd given him that was now down to four days. He thought about how a man with deep pockets finds a legend like the Verruga Lode, and how many ways there are for a legend to leak.

Then he made coffee.

Not because he wanted coffee. Because a man alone at a roadside pullout with a camp stove is a geologist, or a birdwatcher, or a storm chaser taking pictures — any of a dozen harmless things. But a man who turns around and drives away is running, and running men get followed. Dominic had learned that much from the gray pickup outside town. You didn't show your back to a hunt. You stayed, and you were boring, and you let them come to you.

So he set the little stove on a flat rock, filled the dented percolator from his jug, and waited, watching the steam rise sideways in the wind while the clouds over the ridge went on stacking themselves like something being built on purpose.

It took forty minutes. He'd have bet on thirty.

The white pickup came down the moraine track slow, kicking dust, and stopped fifty feet short of him with the studied casualness of a man who has rehearsed pulling up to strangers. Two men got out. The driver was big through the shoulders, sunburned to the color of old brick, wearing a canvas shirt with the sleeves rolled and a surveyor's vest whose pockets all sagged with purpose. The passenger was younger, leaner, with the careful hands of someone who handled instruments for a living and a face that hadn't decided yet whether it wanted to be friendly.

"Dominic Vasquez," the big one said.

Not a question. That was the first thing Dominic noticed, and he filed it away the way he filed away wind shifts — fast, without comment.

"Depends who's asking."

"Halloran." The big man crossed the gravel with his hand out, and his grip, when Dominic took it, was the grip of a man who arm-wrestled for money in his youth — firm past the point of warmth, held a half-second too long. "Miles Halloran. This is Petey Okafor, my instrument man. We're running a mineral survey up the ridge. Private contract."

"Whose contract?"

Halloran smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, the kind that had closed deals in boardrooms and probably a couple of bar fights. "Client confidentiality," he said. "You know how it is."

"I know how it is," Dominic said, and poured a third cup anyway, because his mother had raised him polite and paranoia was no excuse for bad manners. He handed it across. Halloran took it, and Petey wrapped both hands around his like the morning had a chill in it, which it did, up here, under those building clouds.

For a while nobody said anything important. They talked weather, because men in the high desert always talk weather first; it's the handshake the land requires. Halloran agreed the cells over the ridge looked mean. Petey said his grandmother in Tucson could smell rain coming two days out, and Dominic said his abuela had claimed the same thing about hail, and for a moment it was almost easy, almost human, three men standing around a dented percolator under a sky going to war with itself.

Then Halloran set down his cup, and the ease drained out of the moment like water through gravel.

"You've got something on you," he said. "Paper. Old paper. Hand-drawn."

Dominic didn't move. Didn't flinch. He'd spent years reading storms, and storms taught you the same lesson over and over: the ones that kill you are never the ones that announce themselves. "I've got maps," he said. "USGS quadrangles. Road atlases. A gas station map of three counties."

"I'm not talking about quadrangles." Halloran's voice stayed pleasant. That was the trick of him, Dominic understood suddenly — the pleasantness wasn't a mask over the threat, it *was* the threat, delivered softly, the way some storms arrive not as a wall but as a rising tide. "Elias Crane drew a map before he died. Sixty-one years old, prospector, bad lungs, been working the Verruga country on and off since before either of us could drive. Word travels. Word says the map traveled too."

"And word says?"

"Word says a storm chaser with a reputation and a truck payment is carrying it north." Halloran picked up his coffee again, drank, set it down. All very unhurried. "Word also says that storm chaser owes money. Quite a lot of it. To people whose collections practices are, let's say, more enthusiastic than most banks'."

The wind shifted. Dominic felt it on the back of his neck before he heard it in anything Halloran said — the southwest breeze dying, the air going still and heavy the way it does before the first drop falls. Above the ridge, the cloud towers had merged into a single mass, gray-green at the base, boiling upward. The light had gone the color of old brass.

"Here's my problem, Vasquez," Halloran went on, conversational, almost gentle. "My client paid for a survey. A thorough one. Every drainage, every bench, every contact zone on that ridge. Now, a survey takes weeks. But if somebody happened to be holding a document that could save everybody a great deal of time and expense — well. My client is a reasonable man. Reasonable men pay for shortcuts."

"How much?"

"That depends on the condition of the merchandise." Halloran's eyes came up from his coffee and settled on Dominic, and for the first time the pleasantness thinned enough to show the machinery underneath. "And on whether the merchandise comes with complications. A dead man's daughter, for instance. A promise sworn on a diner counter. Those are complications. Complications cost."

"You know about June Crane."

"I know about everyone involved in this transaction." Halloran turned his cup slowly on its saucer of gravel. "That's my job. I'm paid to know. I know your debt to the tune of the nearest thousand. I know the names of the men who hold it. I know you lost a gray pickup in a dry wash nine days ago, which was well done, by the way — I'd have taken the same line myself. And I know that in approximately"—he glanced at his watch, a heavy steel thing that caught the flat light—"four days, those men are going to stop being patient with you."

Petey shifted his weight. Said nothing. He was looking at the sky, and his face had the particular tension of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"So here's the offer, stated plainly, because I respect plain speech," Halloran said. "You give me the map. My client erases your ledger. Not pays it down — erases it. Paper gone, account closed, the Delgado brothers informed that you're under protection. You walk away owing nothing to anyone. You keep your truck, your compass, your reputation. And you never come within fifty miles of this ridge again."

"And if I don't?"

Halloran shrugged, and the shrug was somehow worse than the offer. "Then my client's other arrangements proceed. Surveys can be interrupted. Trucks break down in remote country all the time. Weather's coming in — you'd know better than anyone how quickly a man can get into trouble out here, and how long it takes anyone to come looking." He smiled again, that maintained smile. "I'm not threatening you, Vasquez. I'm describing the landscape."

"The landscape," Dominic repeated.

"It's what I do." Halloran finished his coffee and handed the cup back with a nod, courteous to the last. "Think it over tonight. Storm'll pin us all in place till morning anyway. I'll send Petey up at first light for your answer. No pressure." A pause, and then, almost as an afterthought, almost kindly: "Your compass is pointing wrong, by the way. Has been since the flats. There's iron in these hills. Don't trust it past the creek line."

He turned and walked back toward the pickup, and Petey followed, and the two of them receded down the gravel in the dead calm, two figures growing smaller against a valley that had gone the color of pewter.

Dominic stood by his stove, holding a cooling cup of coffee he no longer wanted, and watched the first fat drops hit the dust around his boots. One. Two. Then the sky opened like a wound.

* * *

The rain came down in ropes.

He got the truck's tailgate up and himself under the camper shell just as the world dissolved, and for the next hour he sat in the drumming dark listening to water hammer the fiberglass and thinking, which is a dangerous thing to do during a storm, because thinking is how men talk themselves into things.

The offer was real. He understood that now. Whatever else Miles Halloran was — and every instinct Dominic owned said he was the kind of man who described landscapes instead of making threats because descriptions couldn't be prosecuted — the offer itself was genuine. Erase the ledger. Close the account. Walk away free. Four days from now the Delgados would stop smiling, and everyone in three counties knew what happened after the smiling stopped. Marisol had told him that herself, quietly, across a kitchen table: *they never forgive twice.*

And all it would cost was a dead man's map. A piece of paper. Pencil lines on butcher paper, worth nothing, worth everything, depending entirely on what a man decided it was.

Dominic lay back on his sleeping bag and listened to the rain and tried to remember the exact sound of Elias Crane's voice. Wet gravel and cigarettes and something underneath it, something that had sounded, even through the ruin of the man's lungs, like trust. *Carry it to my daughter,* Elias had said. *Before snowfall. Swear it.* And Dominic, who had sworn oaths on poker tables and loan documents and once, memorably, to a woman in Yuma, had put his hand flat on that diner counter and sworn this one too, and had meant it, mostly, in the way you mean things at dusk when the light is kind.

But dusk was a hundred miles and six days ago. Dusk didn't have four days left on a clock. Dusk didn't know what the Delgados' collectors looked like when they stopped collecting and started something else.

He rolled over. Took the map from his jacket. Unfolded it carefully, there in the dark, by feel, because he wouldn't risk the flashlight — light leaked through camper shells, and there were men two miles up-valley who might be watching. His fingers knew the creases now. They found the crosshatched square, *the throat*, and traced south from it to the little penciled X, and west to the notation in Elias's cramped hand: *free-milling ore, float everywhere, follow the red dike.*

Free-milling. Dominic knew what that meant. Ore you could crush in a mortar and pan with gold — silver, here — straight out, no smelting, no chemistry. The kind of deposit that built towns and emptied them and started feuds that outlived everyone who started them. The kind of deposit a man like Elias Crane had spent sixty-one years and ruined lungs crawling toward, and had found, and had drawn, and had chosen — knowing what it was worth, knowing exactly what it was worth — to trade for a promise.

Chosen. That was the word that kept catching in Dominic's chest like a fishhook. Elias had known. Had held the richest thing he'd ever touch in his two shaking hands and given it away anyway, to a stranger, for a ride north and a name and an address. What did that make the map? Not merchandise. Halloran had used the word twice, *merchandise*, with the smoothness of a man quoting an invoice. But Elias hadn't treated it like merchandise. He'd treated it like a letter. Like the last true thing he had to say to a daughter who believed he'd abandoned her.

Outside, the rain kept falling, steady now, the leading edge of something bigger. The lightning had moved off east, flickering inside the clouds like veins of fire in smoked glass. In the morning, Petey Okafor would walk up the gravel with a thermos and a question, and Dominic would have to answer it, and every answer had a price tag.

He put the map away. Folded it along its worn creases, slid it back against his ribs, buttoned the jacket over it. Lay down. Closed his eyes.

Sleep came slow, and when it came it brought the flats with it — the wide hot country where he chased dying storms for clients who paid late, the smell of creosote after rain, the gray pickup in his mirrors. And in the dream, as in the waking world, someone was always asking him what he was carrying, and someone was always offering to take the weight.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the storm spent, the ridge rinsed clean and standing sharp against a sky the color of dishwater. Water stood in the low spots of the road, mirror-flat, holding the clouds upside down. Everything smelled scrubbed and cold.

Dominic was up before the light finished arriving. He'd slept badly, in the shallow drifting way of a man with a decision pending, and he'd woken with no decision at all — only a clearer sense of the shape of the hole where the decision should be. He boiled water, made coffee, ate half a can of beans cold because the stove fuel was low. He checked the sky. More weather behind the front; the monsoon wasn't done with this country, not by days.

At 6:40, Petey Okafor appeared on the road below the pullout, walking, carrying a thermos and a clipboard, moving with the careful tread of a man who has been sent to do a thing he'd rather not. Dominic watched him come the way he'd watch a cell build on the horizon — noting the approach, estimating the severity, keeping his options open.

"Mr. Vasquez." Petey stopped ten feet off, set the thermos down on the gravel like a peace offering, and stood with his hands in his pockets. "Mr. Halloran sent me for your answer."

"I figured."

"He also sent a number." Petey glanced at the clipboard, then seemed to think better of reading from it, and looked at Dominic directly instead. There was something in the younger man's face — a wariness, a reluctance, the look of a hired hand who'd signed on for instruments and contours and found himself couriering ultimatums. "It's generous. I'll say that. It's more than the survey's worth, probably. Mr. Halloran says his client doesn't haggle."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'm supposed to tell you we'll be proceeding with the survey as contracted, and wish you safe travels." Petey paused. "That's the official version."

"And the unofficial one?"

Petey looked at the sky. At the ridge. At the wet gravel between his boots. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, and he glanced once over his shoulder, the way men do when the landscape they're describing includes listeners. "The unofficial version is that Mr. Halloran doesn't leave loose ends. He told me that himself, my first week, when I asked why we were mapping a claim our client supposedly already had under option. He said, *there are no loose ends in this business, Petey, only unfinished ones.*" The young man shifted the clipboard under his arm. "I don't know what you're carrying. I don't want to know. But I've been in this trade four years, and I can count, and I know what happens to men who are counted as unfinished."

Dominic crouched by the stove, turning his cup slowly, saying nothing. The silence stretched. Somewhere upslope, water was dripping off the rocks in a rhythm too regular for rain — drip, drip, drip — like the land keeping time.

"Your client," Dominic said finally. "The one who doesn't hagggle. Who is he?"

"I don't know." Petey said it flatly, and it had the ring of truth rather than evasion — the flatness of a man repeating a fact he'd tested. "Halloran keeps that door locked. But I did the books last month when our accountant quit, and there was a wire transfer, and the origin bank was First Mercantile, and the account name was a shell — Meridian Holdings, some LLC out of Delaware. And I know one other thing." He hesitated, and Dominic watched the young man decide, visibly, whether to spend whatever it would cost to say the next part. "The wire memo field. It said *V-C-L.* Three letters. I've turned it over for a week and there's only one thing in this country those letters spell."

Verruga Consolidated Lode.

Dominic felt it go through him like cold water through a crack. Not surprise, exactly — he'd known since the diner that the legend had leaked, that Elias's secret had grown ears somewhere in the last months of the old man's life. But knowing a thing in the abstract and watching it arrive with a wire transfer and a survey crew and a client who doesn't haggle were different weathers entirely.

The Delgados. It had to be. Raúl or Tomás — and if Dominic had to bet, and he did have to bet, everything he had was already bet — it was Raúl, the elder brother, the one who smiled widest and counted longest. Buying the lode through a Delaware shell, hiring a legitimate survey crew as cover, sending Halloran to collect the map and erase the witness. Clean. Cheerful. Arithmetical. The Delgados didn't threaten men; they *resolved* them, the way you resolve an equation, and the solution was always the same.

Which meant the offer wasn't an offer. It was a purchase order. And the alternative to selling wasn't hardship — it was becoming an unfinished thing.

"Tell Halloran I need until tonight," Dominic said.

Petey nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising. He picked up his thermos, then stopped, and stood a moment in the wet gravel with his head tilted, listening to something — the drip off the rocks, or maybe just his own conscience finishing its sentence.

"For what it's worth," he said quietly, "when I walked up here, I hoped you'd already be gone."

Then he turned and walked back down the road, smaller and smaller against the washed gray morning, and Dominic stood alone at the pullout with his cooling coffee and his hidden map and four days on a clock that had just started ticking louder.

Above the ridge, behind the front, new clouds were already forming. He could see them stacking, white and orderly and patient, feeding on the moisture the storm had left behind. Building toward something. Everything out here was building toward something — the clouds, the debt, the survey crew on the moraine breaking camp with the brisk efficiency of men whose schedule had just accelerated.

Because they were breaking camp. He could see it from the pullout: the orange domes coming down, the tripods going into the rack, the white pickup loading like a ship taking on cargo. Halloran wasn't waiting for tonight. Halloran had gotten his answer in the shape of a request for time, and requests for time, in Halloran's business, were answers too.

They were moving. Upslope. Toward the throat.

And Dominic stood in the wet gravel with the map against his ribs and understood, with the clarity that only arrives when every door is closing at once, that the race had already started — that it had started nine days ago at a diner counter at dusk, and he had been losing it ever since, politely, one cup of coffee at a time.

He threw his gear in the truck. The engine caught on the second try, coughed, steadied.

Somewhere ahead of him, up a road the rain had half-erased, a daughter was waiting who didn't know she was waiting. Behind him, a ledger. Beside him, nowhere. Above him, weather.

Dominic dropped it into gear and drove at the ridge.
