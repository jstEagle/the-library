## 9. Marisol's Bargain

The rain had followed Marisol Delgado all the way up from the valley, thinning as the road climbed until it was less a storm than a memory of one — a fine silver mist that beaded on the windshield and made the pines along the shoulder look like they were wearing veils. She drove with one hand on the wheel and the other resting on the ledger book in the passenger seat, her palm flat against its cover the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you didn't entirely trust.

The ledger was her brother's religion. Tomás kept it in a locked case behind his desk, bound in green leather gone soft at the corners, every debt in the county written in his small, patient hand. He believed the numbers were neutral. He said so often, at dinner, at funerals, at the kitchen table where their father had taught them both arithmetic with beans and a knife: *A number doesn't hate anyone, Mari. It just is.* But Marisol had spent twenty-six years watching those numbers move through people's lives like water through soil, and she knew better. A number could drown a man as surely as a river. It just did it slower, and with better manners.

Dominic Vasquez owed them four thousand two hundred and sixty dollars. She knew it to the dollar because she'd written the last three entries herself, after Tomás had broken his wrist falling off a horse he had no business riding, and someone had to keep the books current while his hand healed in its cast. That was how it worked in their family. Tomás collected; Marisol recorded. Tomás smiled; Marisol remembered. Their father used to say they were two halves of the same handshake, and he'd meant it as a compliment.

The order had come down that morning with the coffee: *Go get Vasquez. Bring him back, or bring me something worth more than he owes.* Tomás hadn't specified what the something might be. He rarely did anymore. He'd learned to let people fill in the terrible parts themselves, because a man will always imagine a worse fate than the one you'd actually deliver, and fear does the collecting for you.

But Marisol had read the file. All of it, including the part Tomás thought she didn't know about — the survey crew working the eastern ridge under a man named Halloran, the wire transfers from an account that traced back, through two shell companies and a lawyer in El Paso who charged by the syllable, to her own eldest brother Raúl. Someone with deep pockets wanted the high country mapped. And ten days ago, a dying prospector had put a hand-drawn map into Dominic Vasquez's weathered hands at a diner booth with a cigarette burn on the Formica.

She'd done the arithmetic on the drive up. Four thousand against whatever the Verruga Lode was actually worth. It wasn't even arithmetic. It was a rounding error pretending to be a sum.

* * *

She found the truck before she found the man.

It was parked in the lee of a rock outcrop above a creek running loud and brown with snowmelt, its canvas tarp sagging under the weight of the mist, its left rear tire worn down to the cords. Even parked, even sleeping, it looked like a vehicle that had been argued with for years and had won most of the arguments. A compass hung from the rearview mirror, turning slowly, and beside it dangled a cheap plastic saint — San Judas, patron of lost causes — which struck Marisol as either a joke or a prayer, and possibly both.

The man himself was sitting on the tailgate with a camp stove hissing between his boots, heating something in a dented pot. He'd heard her engine, obviously; there was no road within earshot that a stranger would take by accident. But he didn't stand, didn't reach for anything, didn't do any of the things a guilty man does when collectors find him in the middle of nowhere. He just looked up with those storm-chaser's eyes — pale, patient, always reading the sky over your shoulder — and said:

"You're the little sister."

"Marisol." She stopped ten feet away, close enough to talk, far enough that the conversation couldn't turn physical without warning. Old habit. "Marisol Delgado."

"I know. You're the one who writes it all down." He stirred the pot. Beans, by the smell, with too much cumin. "I figured Tomás would send muscle."

"Tomás sends whoever gets the job done."

"And that's you now?"

"That's me today." She sat down on a rock across the fire ring from him, uninvited, and set the ledger on her knees. The gesture was deliberate. Let him see the book. Let him understand what she was holding. "You've been hard to find, Mr. Vasquez. You don't answer your phone, you pay cash at diners, and you've been driving through country where the roads have names but no signs."

"I've been driving through monsoon season. Everything's washed out or underwater. You want to complain about my route, take it up with the sky."

Something in her chest loosened, unexpectedly. Most men, when she came for them, went one of two ways: they groveled, or they postured. This one did neither. He talked to her like she was a person standing in weather, which — she realized with a small internal jolt — almost nobody had done since her father died. People saw the ledger first. The Delgado name second. Marisol herself somewhere far down the list, if at all.

"What's in the pot?" she asked, mostly to break her own train of thought.

"Beans."

"May I?"

He raised an eyebrow, spooned some into the lid, and handed it over without comment. It was good. Too much cumin, but good. They ate in silence for a minute while the mist drifted through the pines and the creek kept up its brown argument with the rocks below.

Then she set down the lid, opened the ledger to his page, and turned it toward him so he could read his own life in her brother's handwriting. Dates. Amounts. Interest compounding like mold in a damp wall. Four thousand two hundred sixty, and climbing.

"You know what happens at the end of the week," she said.

"I know what Tomás *says* happens."

"What Tomás says and what happens have never once been different things. That's the whole point of him." She closed the book. "He wants the map, Dominic. He doesn't care about you. You were useful when you were chasing storms for paying clients and making your payments. Now you're carrying something worth more than your whole miserable net worth through country we control, and he knows it, and Halloran works for Raúl, and the only question in this family right now is who gets to the lode first and how many people have to stop existing for it to happen cleanly."

Dominic set down the pot. For the first time since she'd arrived, he looked directly at her instead of past her, and she watched him decide something — watch the decision move through his face like weather moving across a flat.

"You just told me your own family intends to kill me."

"I told you what you already suspected. I'm not stupid enough to think I'm telling you anything new." She pulled the ledger back onto her lap. "Here's what I'm offering instead."

* * *

She'd rehearsed it on the drive. In the truck, alone, with the wipers keeping time, she'd said the words out loud twice, testing them, adjusting them, hating how much they sounded like Tomás no matter how she arranged them. An offer was an offer. There was only so much you could do to dress up a hook.

"The page," she said. "Your page. Four thousand two hundred sixty dollars, plus interest, plus the late fees Tomás invented because he was bored in March. One page in a green book. I hold the pen half the time anyway. I can make it disappear so clean that not even the paper remembers it was there."

Dominic stared at her. "In exchange for what? Nobody burns a page out of kindness. Your family doesn't have a bone in its body that points that direction."

"In exchange for the location of the Verruga Lode. Coordinates, bearings, the cipher key, however Elias Crane wrote it. Not the map itself — I don't want the paper, papers get people killed. Just the truth of it, told to me, privately, and then you go on to Kestrel Pass and keep your promise to the dead man, and I go home and my brother never learns you existed."

The wind moved through the pines above them, and somewhere upslope a branch cracked and fell, and neither of them flinched, which told her they were both people who had learned to sort sounds into dangerous and harmless a long time ago.

"You'd betray him," Dominic said. "Your own brother."

"I'd balance a book." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "That's all any of this has ever been to me, Dominic. Balancing. Tomás thinks the ledger is power. Raúl thinks it's a weapon. I'm the only one in the family who understands what it actually is — it's a list of everyone we've hurt, written in our own hand, and someday somebody's going to read it out loud in a courtroom, and I would very much like to have burned my pages before that day comes."

"That's not why you're here."

"No," she admitted. "Partly. Mostly—" She stopped. Looked at the fire, at the pot, at the plastic saint turning on the mirror. "Mostly because I read Crane's obituary. Did you know they ran one? Half a column in the *Kestrel Courier*. 'Survived by a daughter.' One line. Twenty years in the hills and the whole of his life fits in a sentence, and the daughter gets eleven words of it." She heard her own voice doing something it didn't usually do, and made herself stop. "You're taking that map to her. To the eleven words. And my brothers are going to take it away from you and feed you to a dry wash somewhere, and nobody will ever run an obituary for you either, and I keep thinking: I write the numbers. I should get to write one thing that isn't a number, just once."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. The stove hissed itself out. The mist thickened, beading on the ledger's green cover, and Marisol found herself wanting to wipe it off, wanting to protect the stupid book from the weather, and hating herself for that too.

"I'll tell you what I told Halloran's man," Dominic finally said. "And what I'll tell anyone else who asks between here and Kestrel Pass. I don't know where the lode is. I mean that literally — the map's in cipher, and the man who drew it died before he finished explaining it. I've got a piece of paper full of bearings I can't read, going to a woman who hates her father, through a pass that's going to close, with your family and Raúl's survey crew both hunting me." He shook his head slowly. "Even if I wanted to sell you the location, Marisol, I couldn't. I don't have it. I have a promise and a puzzle, and the promise is the only part that's mine."

She studied him the way she studied signatures — looking for the tremor, the hesitation, the place where a lie enters the ink. She'd caught Tomás lying twice in her life and Raúl maybe forty times, and she knew all the textures of it. Dominic Vasquez's voice held none of them. He was either telling the truth or he was the best liar in three counties, and if he was the best liar in three counties, then he deserved to win anyway.

"So that's no," she said.

"That's no. But it's a no with information attached, if you want it: the letters sewn into the backing. Odile found them — the botanist traveling with me. Elias wrote to June for twenty years and never sent any of it. Whatever your brothers think this map is, it's also an apology, and apologies are worth more than silver to exactly one person in this world, and she lives in Kestrel Pass."

* * *

Marisol sat with that for a while. The mist was turning to actual rain again, fine and cold, ticking against the ledger cover, and Dominic had moved the pot under the tailgate and was watching her with the unhurried attention of a man who read clouds for a living and had decided she was a slow-moving system, worth monitoring, no immediate threat.

She should report him. That was the job. That was what Tomás expected, what the family expected, what twenty-six years of being the second half of the handshake demanded. *Subject located, subject denies possession of actionable intelligence, recommends escalation.* Two sentences on a phone call and the machinery would start turning — Raúl's crew, Halloran's men, the whole quiet grinding apparatus that had ended better men than Dominic Vasquez in washes and ravines all across this state.

Instead she heard herself say: "If you're lying to me—"

"I'm not."

"—then my brothers will find you, and they won't offer you a deal first. Tomás offers deals exactly once per customer. You're getting yours right now, from me, and after this there's only the other thing."

"I understand the other thing," Dominic said quietly. "I've understood it since the diner. Elias told me what happened to the last man who crossed the Delgados. He described the wash they left him in."

The rain picked up. Marisol stood, brushed off her jeans, and looked down at the ledger in her hands — at the soft green leather, at the weight of it, at everything it had ever meant and everything she suddenly wished it didn't. Then, before she could talk herself out of it, she opened it to his page, tore the corner, and wrote in the margin in her small, patient hand — the same hand, the same pen, the same script that had recorded four thousand two hundred sixty dollars and every late fee:

*Collected in full. M.D.*

Not erased. Not yet. But noted, dated, witnessed by no one but the rain. If Tomás ever flipped to this page, he'd see it and rage and demand to know what collection had been made and where the money was. And she'd have until he asked to figure out what to tell him. Or she'd have until spring, when the passes opened and investigators came asking their own questions, and every page in this book became evidence.

It was a terrible plan. It was the kind of thing Raúl would call sentimental and Tomás would call suicidal, and they would both be right, and she tore the corner anyway.

"For what it's worth," Dominic said, watching her do it, "I think you already know how this ends for them. The fraud, the shell companies, Halloran. You've known for a while."

"I balance books," Marisol said. "Balancing means knowing which side the weight falls on." She tucked the ledger under her jacket, out of the rain, and turned back toward her truck. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked at him across the dying fire. "Kestrel Pass closes early this year. The jet stream's already dipping. You've got maybe nine days, and the low road's gone — mudslide took it out yesterday near Mile 40. You'll have to go high, through the saddle above Miller Creek, and there's a storm coming down out of the northwest that I wouldn't chase on a bet."

"You're giving me weather reports now."

"I'm giving you the only honest thing my family's given anyone in thirty years." She got in, started the engine, and rolled the window down against the drumming rain. "Don't die, Dominic. Dead men can't testify."

She drove back down out of the hills with the ledger warm against her ribs and the torn corner of his page whispering against the others every time she took a curve, and by the time she reached the valley floor she had composed and discarded six different reports for Tomás, and settled on the truest lie she knew how to tell:

*Lost him in the high country. Storm's coming in. We'll get him on the way down.*

Behind her, up in the mist where the pines wore their veils, a man with a compass and a dead man's puzzle broke camp and pointed his battered truck toward the saddle above Miller Creek — toward the storm she'd warned him about, the pass that would seal behind him, and the eleven words waiting at the end of it all.
