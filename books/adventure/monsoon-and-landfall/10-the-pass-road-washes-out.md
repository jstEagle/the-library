## 10. The Pass Road Washes Out

The rain had a sound, Teo had learned, that came before the bad sound. The ordinary rain was a whisper against the truck's roof, a thousand small fingers drumming. But underneath it, when the mountains were getting ready to shrug, there was a lower note — a hum in the water itself, like the earth clearing its throat. He heard it now, lying in the truck bed under the tarp with his ear pressed to the cold metal, and his whole body went tight as a wire.

Odile was asleep beside him, her breath slow, her hat over her face. Dominic was driving. Through the gap where the tarp didn't quite meet the cab's back window, Teo could see the back of the man's head, the set of his shoulders, the way his hands moved on the wheel — not gripping it like the other drivers Teo had known, but resting on it, the way you'd rest a hand on a horse that might spook.

The low road ran along the Río Bravo del Norte of these parts — the locals called it the Río Sucio, the Dirty River — a brown, muscular thing swollen with three days of monsoon. The road itself was cut into the canyon wall, half gravel, half ambition, and the river kept reaching up to taste it.

Teo was eleven years old and he had already learned that water was the most patient thing in the world. It could wait years for a hillside. It could wait a whole childhood for a bridge.

* * *

They had stopped at midday at a place called the Vado, a fuel stop and a café and a warning all in one building. The old woman who ran it had looked at their truck, then at the sky, then at Dominic, and said the thing that old women in these mountains always said, the thing that was less a prediction than a verdict.

"The pass road's open," she said. "For now."

Dominic had grinned at her, that easy grin that Teo had already learned was a kind of armor, and said, "For now is all I ever ask for."

But Teo had seen his eyes go to the clouds stacking themselves over the western ridgeline like gray plates on a shelf, and Teo had seen the grin not reach them.

They ate tamales wrapped in corn husks, and Odile bought a paper sack of dried chiles and a jar of something pickled, because Odile never passed a settlement without acquiring something that could be eaten in an emergency. Teo bought nothing. He sat with his back against the warm flank of the truck and watched the river.

It had come up maybe a meter since they'd crossed it two days ago, higher in the canyon. The color of it was wrong — not brown but a kind of living gray, the color of a thing with muscles. It carried whole trees. It carried a fence post with three rails still attached, sailing along upright like a ship that had lost its crew.

"Teo." Odile's voice, quiet, beside him. "Come eat."

"I'm eating."

"You're watching the river."

"Same thing."

She didn't argue. That was one of the things about Odile — she never argued with a true statement. She sat down next to him on the gravel, careful of her trousers, and opened the jar of pickled something, and they ate in silence while the water went by carrying the mountain, piece by piece, down toward the sea.

* * *

It happened in the middle of the afternoon, and it happened the way Teo had always known it would happen, the way it had happened before, the way it would happen again in every storm of his life: suddenly, and then slowly, and then all at once.

They were maybe forty minutes past the Vado when the hum under the rain changed key.

Teo sat up so fast he cracked his head on the cab's roof rack. "Stop," he said. His voice came out small. "Dominic. Stop the truck."

Dominic's eyes found him in the mirror. "What is it, pequeño?"

"The hill." Teo's hands were shaking and he hated that they were shaking. "Listen to the hill."

For a second Dominic just looked at him, and Teo braced for the laugh, the *kid, you're eleven, what do you know about hills* — but it didn't come. Dominic pulled the truck onto the widest shelf of gravel he could find, set the brake, and got out into the rain, and stood there with his head tilted, listening to a hillside, because an eleven-year-old had told him to.

Odile was awake now. She got out too. The three of them stood in the downpour on the shelf above the river, and the rain came down like the sky was trying to drown the mountains individually.

Then Teo heard it — they all heard it. Underneath the rain, a new sound. A grinding. Deep and wet and enormous, like something the size of a cathedral turning over in its sleep.

"Back up," Dominic said quietly. "Everybody back up. Get in the truck."

They reversed a hundred meters, two hundred, to a bend where the road climbed away from the river, and there they stopped and watched.

The hillside across the canyon let go.

It didn't fall. That was the thing Teo could never explain to anyone afterward. It didn't fall like rocks fall. It *flowed*, the whole face of the slope turning to liquid and pouring itself into the canyon, a gray-brown cataract of earth and trees and boulders the size of houses, and where it met the river the river stood up — actually stood up, a wall of water and mud taller than the truck, taller than two trucks — and came down the canyon at them.

"Drive," Odile said. Not screaming. Just: *drive*, the way you'd say *breathe*.

Dominic drove.

The wall came. Teo watched it in the side mirror, gaining, and the sound of it was the sound of his worst dreams, the sound he had heard once before through a tent wall in a camp in the foothills, seven years ago, when he was four years old and the world ended in water.

The road curved. The wall, heavy with mud, couldn't take the curve the way a truck could. It hit the canyon wall a hundred meters behind them and exploded sideways, and the blast wave of spray and gravel hammered the truck like a fist, and then they were through, around the bend, climbing, and behind them the low road simply ceased to exist.

Dominic pulled over at the first wide spot and sat with his hands on the wheel, breathing. Then he turned around in his seat and looked at Teo, and his face was strange — not frightened, not relieved. *Considering.*

"How did you know?" he said.

Teo shrugged, which was a lie, because he knew exactly how he knew. "The sound."

"The sound." Dominic nodded slowly. "Okay. The sound." He turned back around, put the truck in gear. "Remind me to always park next to you."

* * *

The low road was gone. That was the arithmetic of it, delivered that evening by a road crew foreman at a junction camp where a dozen stranded travelers huddled under tarps: the slide had taken two kilometers of the canyon road, and the engineers said ten days, maybe two weeks, before it was passable — and that was if the rain stopped, and the rain was not going to stop.

"The high road, then," Dominic said.

The foreman looked at him the way you'd look at a man who'd suggested swimming across a lake in January. "The high road's a suggestion, not a road. It goes over the shoulder of the Sierra del Viento. It's goat track most of the way, and it tops out above three thousand meters. In monsoon season." He paused. "With early snow possible."

"Snow," Odile said. "In September?"

"This year," the foreman said, "everything is possible. That's the problem."

They took the high road.

* * *

The high road was a suggestion, exactly as promised. It climbed out of the canyon country in long switchbacks, the truck's engine whining, the air going thin and cold and clean, the rain turning from warm to sharp. Below them the world filled with cloud, and they drove above the clouds, on a shelf of gravel with nothing on the left side but air, and Teo, who should have been terrified, found that he was not. Height didn't frighten him. He had made his peace with falling. It was water he didn't trust, and up here there was no water — only cloud, only stone, only the enormous silence of the world's roof.

They camped the first night in a hollow below the summit ridge, and the storm found them anyway, because storms always found them. The wind came off the peaks in long shoves, rocking the truck, and the rain went horizontal, and the tarp snapped and cracked like something alive trying to get in.

Teo lay awake in the truck bed and did what he always did on nights like this: he counted. Heartbeats between lightning and thunder. One-Mississippi. The storm was close. Two-Mississippi. Closer. He had gotten very good at this arithmetic. It was the only kind that had ever saved him.

Except that this time, when the lightning came and he flinched, a hand settled on his shoulder through the dark. Not Odile's. Odile slept like a stone and woke like one. This hand was bigger, rougher, and it didn't squeeze, it just *stayed* — warm and heavy, the way a hand is when the person attached to it has decided to be there for a while.

"Can't sleep?" Dominic's voice, low, from the cab window he'd opened between the seats.

"No," Teo said. Which was true, and also not the whole truth, and Dominic apparently knew that, because he didn't say anything else. He just left his hand there, and after a while, without deciding to, Teo started talking.

"I was four," he said. "When my parents died."

Dominic's hand didn't move. The rain hammered. Teo watched the lightning paint the underside of the tarp white, once, twice, counting without meaning to.

"There was a camp. My father was a botanist's assistant, before Odile. Before her it was just me and him and my mother, moving around, following the flowering seasons. My mother said we were nomads, and she said it like it was a good thing." His voice did something strange in the middle of the sentence and he pushed through it. "There was a flash flood. In the night. Nobody — the weather service said nobody could have predicted it, a storm cell that wasn't on any map, that just *made itself* over the valley. My father got my mother up the slope, but the water — " He stopped. Started again. "I was in the truck. My father put me in the truck on the high ground, and told me to stay, and I stayed, because I was four and I did what I was told, and the water took the camp. Took them. Took everything except me and the truck."

The silence after was so complete that Teo could hear Odile breathing, and he realized she was awake, had maybe been awake the whole time, and had said nothing, because that was Odile.

"The next morning I climbed down," Teo said. "Into the wash. And everything was — changed. The shape of the land was different. The place where our tent had been was a hole full of gray water. And I swore — I was four, but I swore it like a grown man — I would never be caught by water again. I would always know what the sky was doing. I would always hear the hill before it moved."

"And that's why you listen," Dominic said quietly.

"That's why I listen."

Another flash. Teo counted. Three-Mississippi. The storm was moving off, dragging its skirts west.

"Can I tell you something?" Dominic said. "And you can't laugh, because I'm a grown man and my dignity is already in bad shape."

"Okay."

"I chase storms because I'm scared of them."

Teo turned his head. Through the cab window he could just make out the shape of Dominic's face, lit faintly by the dash lights, and there was no grin on it. There was something older and tireder underneath where the grin usually lived.

"Everybody thinks storm chasers love storms," Dominic said. "Some of them do. I started because of a debt, and I kept going because — when you watch a thing that scares you, really watch it, learn its habits, learn its sounds — it stops being a monster. It becomes a *system*. Pressure gradients. Convergence. Something with rules. And something with rules can't sneak up on you." He was quiet a moment. "Your parents' flood wasn't on any map. That's not your fault, pequeño. That's not anybody's fault. Some storms just make themselves. But you — " and here his hand squeezed, once, gentle, "— you heard the hill today. Before any of us. Before me, and I do this for a living. You heard it because you've spent seven years learning the rules of the thing that took them. That's not fear, Teo. That's *skill*. The bravest thing I know."

Teo lay still. The rain was softening, the wind dropping, the storm moving away into the west the way storms did, indifferent, magnificent, gone.

"Odile found me two weeks after," he said. "She was surveying the same valley. She says she found me. I say I let her." A small, wet laugh escaped him, surprising them both. "She never talks about it. She just — stayed. That's how she loves people. She stays."

"I've noticed," Dominic said.

"She stayed for seven years. And now — " Teo hesitated, because the next part was the part he'd never said out loud, even to himself, even in the dark. "Now I think maybe I'm learning how to do what she does. Stay. For people. I followed you because of the map. Because of the silver, because I thought — adventure, I guess, an eleven-year-old's adventure. But that's not why I'm still here." He took a breath. "I'm still here because you stopped the truck when I said stop. You listened to an eleven-year-old about a hill. Nobody does that. Grown-ups never do that."

In the cab, Dominic was quiet for a long moment. Then, "Your dad would be proud of you. The hill-listening thing. That's a scientist's ear."

"You think so?"

"I *know* so. I've worked with scientists. They spend years learning to hear what the land is saying, and half of them never get as good at it as you just were today, at eleven, in the rain." He said it simply, without the grin, without the charm, and that was how Teo knew it was true.

The storm moved off. The clouds began, very slowly, to come apart, and behind them the stars came out the way they only did at altitude — hard, close, enormous, a whole cold city of them.

"Sleep, pequeño," Dominic said. "We've got a big climb tomorrow. Above three thousand meters the weather makes its own decisions, and I want you rested and listening."

Teo closed his eyes. The hand on his shoulder stayed a moment longer, then withdrew, and the cab window slid shut, and the truck rocked gently in the wind like a boat at anchor, and for the first time in seven years Teo fell asleep in a storm without counting.

* * *

They summited the shoulder of the Sierra del Viento two days later, in weather that had turned treacherous in the way the foreman had promised — the rain thinning into a cold needle-sleet, the temperature dropping with every switchback, the sky above the ridge gone the color of old tin.

It was Odile, riding shotgun now with the maps spread across her knees, who said it first. She had gone quiet for a long time, the way she did when she was reading a landscape the way other people read a page, and then she said, "Dominic. Look at the clouds."

He looked. Teo looked too, from the truck bed, and even he could see it — the clouds over the western peaks had changed character. They weren't monsoon clouds anymore, gray and bloated and tropical. These were different. Harder. Higher. White at the tops, and moving with a kind of cold purpose, the way ice moves when it's deciding to become a glacier.

"That's not rain," Odile said.

"No," Dominic said. His voice had gone quiet, and his knuckles, Teo noticed, had gone white on the wheel. "That's snow. Early snow. Weeks early." He glanced at the mirror, at Teo, and there was no grin on his face at all now. "The foreman said everything is possible this year. He wasn't kidding."

He downshifted, and the truck groaned up the next switchback, and Teo looked back the way they had come — down at the drowned canyon country, the lost low road, the whole green world disappearing under cloud — and then forward, at the white peaks waiting ahead, and he understood, in the way that children understand things sometimes more clearly than grown-ups, that the mountain had just closed one door behind them.

And that the only door left was the one they were driving toward.

He settled back against the cold steel of the truck bed, pulled the tarp up to his chin, and started listening to the sky.
