## 12. Halloran's Offer

The treeline had come apart behind him sometime after noon, the last stunted pines giving way to a slope of loose gray scree and wind-bent grass gone gold with frost. Dominic climbed with his head down and his collar up, the map riding flat against his chest in an oilcloth sleeve, and he told himself the ache in his shoulders was just altitude, just cold, just the ordinary tax of moving through high country in October. He was good at telling himself things. It was practically a profession.

Above him the ridge ran white-veined where the first snow of the season had found its cracks and settled there like frost on glass. The sky was doing something he didn't like — that bruised, expectant color it got before weather made up its mind. He'd spent half his life reading skies the way other men read faces, and this one had the set jaw of a storm deciding whether to be rude or ruinous. Either way, he wanted to be over the saddle before it finished thinking.

He stopped at a shelf of rock to check the compass, more for the comfort of the ritual than any real doubt about direction. Northeast by north, up the spine of the ridge, then down into the long basin June Crane was waiting in — or not waiting in; he'd learned enough about her by now to know she didn't wait for anybody, least of all her father's ghost carried by a stranger with bad credit.

That's when he heard the engine.

It came thin and wrong across the thin air, a sound like a mosquito with ambitions, and Dominic turned slowly, scanning the switchbacks below. A vehicle was crawling up the old mining track that clung to the mountainside like a scar — a black SUV, city-clean, absurdly out of place among the rusted ore carts and collapsed adits of a range that had been abandoned twice over. Nobody drove a machine like that up here for pleasure. Nobody drove a machine like that up here at all.

Dominic stood very still and did the arithmetic he was worst at: how long since he'd left Odile and Teo at the lower camp with instructions to wait two days; how fast a man could climb versus how fast a man could drive; how many ways this could end badly divided by how few ways it could end well.

The SUV ground to a halt two hundred yards below him, and a figure stepped out. Even at that distance Dominic knew the walk — unhurried, deliberate, a man who moved like the ground owed him deference. Halloran. The survey boss from the parley three days back, the one who'd poured coffee from a battered thermos and recited Dominic's own biography back to him like a man reading a menu: born in Yuma, chasing storms since he was nineteen, eleven thousand four hundred dollars to the Delgado brothers as of the fifteenth, accruing interest weekly.

Halloran started climbing. Not hurrying. That was the thing about the man — he never hurried, because hurrying was for people who weren't sure of the outcome.

Dominic considered running. He honestly considered it. But above treeline, on scree, with a storm building in the west and a man who clearly knew these mountains better than he let on, running meant dying tired. So instead he did what he always did when cornered: he put his hands in his pockets, leaned against the rock shelf like he owned it, and waited.

* * *

Halloran arrived breathing harder than he pretended to be, though he disguised it well, folding his hands behind his back and surveying the view as if they'd both arranged to meet here for the scenery. Up close, the mountain had already begun reclaiming him — dust on his polished boots, wind pulling at his expensive jacket, a flush in his cheeks that the altitude charged like a toll. There was something almost satisfying in seeing money lose an argument with gravity.

"Vasquez." He said the name like a verdict. "You're making good time."

"Didn't know I was racing."

"Everyone's racing. Most of them just don't know the course." Halloran squinted west, toward the wall of cloud assembling itself over the far peaks. "That's coming in tonight. You want to be off this ridge before dark, or you want to be a headline."

"I've been a headline," Dominic said. "Twice. Once for a tornado I actually caught, once for one that caught me. The second one paid better."

Something flickered across Halloran's face — not quite amusement, but the memory of it, worn smooth like river stone. He crouched, picked up a piece of scree, examined it, tossed it away. Buying time too, Dominic realized. Everyone in this story was buying time; the only question was who intended to pay for it.

"You're a hard man to find alone," Halloran said. "The botanist and the boy. Sentimental company for a man in your position."

"They found me. I'm easy to find. That's sort of my whole problem."

"Yes." Halloran straightened. "Your whole problem. Eleven thousand four hundred dollars, accruing. Let's talk about problems, Vasquez. Because I'm here to solve yours, and you're standing there pretending you don't have any."

The wind gusted then, hard enough that Dominic had to plant a boot against the rock shelf, and somewhere below them a slab of snow hissed loose and slid fifty feet down a couloir before stopping, a dry whisper like the mountain clearing its throat.

"Here's my offer." Halloran spoke over the wind without raising his voice much, which took a kind of discipline Dominic grudgingly respected. "You give me the map. You give me Elias Crane's lode. In exchange: your debt is paid. Today. Not reduced — erased. And on top of that, forty thousand dollars, cash, wired anywhere you like. You walk off this mountain free, and you never have to think about the Delgado brothers again except as a bad dream you woke up from."

Dominic kept his hands in his pockets so Halloran couldn't see them flex. Forty thousand dollars. Eleven thousand four hundred in debt. Twenty-eight and a half thousand dollars of clean profit for handing over a dead man's paper. It was more money than he'd held in his life at one time, including the year he'd been briefly, stupidly famous.

"That's generous," Dominic said carefully.

"It's business."

"It's generous *for business*. Which makes me wonder what the lode's worth to whoever's paying you."

Halloran smiled, and it was the smile of a man who'd been waiting for exactly that question and had prepared a boring answer for it. "The Verruga Lode is a legend. Legends are worth what someone will pay to chase them. My employer is paying. That's all you need to know."

"But here's what I keep stumbling over." Dominic pushed off the rock shelf and took a slow step sideways, angling so the wind was at his back and the drop was at Halloran's. Nothing aggressive. Just geometry. "Elias Crane drew that map with a shaking hand and blood in his lungs, and he gave it to me with one condition. One. Carry it to his daughter before the passes close. That's the whole contract. So when somebody offers me forty grand to break a promise to a dying man, I have to ask myself — and forgive me if this sounds personal — what kind of person does the asking?"

"A rich one."

"Yeah," Dominic said. "That's what worries me."

* * *

They stood there maybe ten feet apart, two men on a shelf of the world with the weather closing in, and Dominic watched Halloran decide how much truth to spend. It was like watching a card player count chips. The man had layers, and each layer cost something to peel.

"You're stalling," Halloran said finally.

"I'm admiring the view. There's a difference. Stalling has an exit strategy."

"Does yours?" Halloran glanced meaningfully at the western sky, then at the switchbacks, where the black SUV sat gleaming like a beetle on its back. "Because mine involves a warm truck and a hot meal. Yours involves that." He nodded at the storm front, which had begun to develop the towering, anvil-headed architecture of serious intent. "I've been watching that build since Yuma. It's going to dump two feet on this pass inside thirty-six hours. After that, nobody goes over. Not you, not me, not the ghost of Elias Crane."

"So this is a limited-time offer."

"Everything is." Halloran reached into his jacket — slowly, showing his hands, a courtesy that somehow felt more threatening than speed would have — and produced a folded paper. He held it out. "My employer's terms. Written. Signed. You'll notice the signature line is already executed. All you have to do is fill in your name and hand over the paper in your shirt."

Dominic didn't take it. "Who signs a deal before the other party agrees?"

"Confident people."

"Cocky ones, too. Hard to tell the difference till the cards turn over."

Halloran exhaled through his nose, a small show of frustration, the first crack in the polish. "Let me save us both the theater. You think you're protecting some sacred trust. You're not. Elias Crane sold you that map for a ride and a meal. He was dying, he was broke, and he was desperate to feel important one last time. You were convenient. That's all. Convenience isn't a covenant."

The words landed harder than Dominic expected, which told him they were aimed well. He thought of Elias in the diner booth, coughing into a napkin he folded smaller and smaller, hiding the red the way he hid everything else. He thought of the man's hands — big, cracked, careful hands — flattening the oilcloth on the formica like he was laying out a holy text.

"He said his daughter hates him," Dominic said quietly. "Said she had every right to. Said the only thing he could still give her was the truth about where he'd been. You want me to sell that for forty grand and a receipt."

"I want you to be realistic."

"I'm extremely realistic. It's my worst quality." Dominic looked west again. The storm had committed. The anvil top was spreading like ink in water, and the light on the ridge had gone the flat pewter color that preceded serious trouble. "Here's my counteroffer. I give you nothing today. Tomorrow, maybe. Ask me again on the far side of the saddle."

"There is no tomorrow on this ridge. You know that better than anyone — you read weather for a living."

"Then we'd better talk fast."

* * *

And then Halloran said the thing. Dominic would replay it later, lying awake in whatever shelter the night provided, turning it over like a stone with something alive under it.

Halloran checked his watch — an actual gesture, checking a watch above treeline with a storm inbound — and said, almost gently: "Emiliano wouldn't want you to freeze to death over principle. He's sentimental that way. Under all the arithmetic."

*Emiliano.*

Not *the Delgados*. Not *my employer*. Emiliano. First name. Familiar. The elder brother, the ledger-keeper, the one whose smile was wider and colder than Raúl's, the one who'd sat across from Dominic in that office smelling of leather and espresso and said *one week* like a priest announcing a sentence.

Dominic kept his face still. It was the hardest work he'd done in years, keeping his face still, because underneath it everything was rearranging itself — the survey crew on the ridge, the deep pockets, the coffee poured from a thermos while a man recited your debts like scripture. It wasn't a rival. It had never been a rival. It was the same debt wearing different clothes. The Delgados hadn't sent collectors after him. They'd sent *architects*. They'd built a second door out of his prison and offered it to him as freedom, and the price of walking through it was the map, the lode, and — he understood now, with the cold clarity of altitude — probably the witnesses. Odile. Teo. Anyone who'd seen the map and could describe it later.

"You work for Emiliano Delgado," Dominic said. Flat. No question mark. Give the man nothing to confirm, nothing to deny, nothing to use.

Halloran's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a recalculation, a flicker of reassessment, the look of a professional realizing an amateur has just done professional-grade math. "I work for a diversified portfolio of interests," he said. "Mr. Delgado is one of them."

"And the survey crew? The honest mapping job, the permits, the coffee?"

"Legitimate. Mostly. Cover stories usually are — that's what makes them cover." Halloran tucked the unsigned contract back into his jacket with the neatness of a man filing a document he fully expected to need again. "Look at it from his side, Vasquez. You owe him money. You're carrying an asset worth — conservatively — several million. You're heading into country where, in thirty-six hours, anything could happen to you. Avalanches. Weather. Bad luck. Men have died in these mountains doing nothing more reckless than walking." He spread his hands, reasonable, almost kind. "Emiliano would prefer you alive. He told me so specifically. But he also told me the map matters more than the messenger, and I've learned not to rephrase that man's sentences."

There it was. Laid out on the bare rock between them like an offering: *the map matters more than the messenger.* Said plainly, in daylight, above treeline, with a storm coming to bury the evidence. Halloran wasn't even bothering to disguise it anymore, because — Dominic understood with a lurch — Halloran believed the disguise no longer mattered. Believed Dominic had nowhere to go, no one to tell, and no tomorrow worth planning for.

"You know what the funny thing is?" Dominic said. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was the only kind of steady he'd ever been. "A week ago I'd have taken this deal. A week ago I'd have taken half this deal. A week ago I was a man being collected, and I'd have signed anything that made the arithmetic stop." He pulled the compass from his pocket and held it up between them, its brass case dull in the failing light, the needle swinging lazy and true toward northeast by north. "But Elias Crane made me swear on this. On the needle. And I've broken a lot of promises in my life — to banks, to clients, to a woman in Tucson whose name I still can't say without flinching. This is the first one I've ever been poor enough to keep."

Halloran studied him for a long moment. Whatever he saw, he filed it away; Dominic could feel himself being entered into some internal ledger, assessed, priced, written down.

"The offer stands until the snow starts," Halloran said at last. "After that, the mountain takes over, and I stop being able to protect you from it. Think about which force you'd rather negotiate with." He turned and started down toward the SUV, sure-footed on the scree in a way that confirmed everything — the man had walked these ridges before, in worse conditions than this, probably carrying worse purposes.

At the edge of the shelf he paused, half-turned, wind snapping his collar.

"For what it's worth," Halloran said, "the daughter's already on the mountain. She's been following you since the foothills. Emiliano knows. So does the boy and the botanist. You're not leading this expedition, Vasquez. You're herding it." And he dropped down the switchbacks, unhurried even now, leaving Dominic alone with the compass, the contract he hadn't signed, and the storm.

* * *

Dominic stood on the shelf for a long time after the engine sound faded, long enough that the cold worked through his jacket and began negotiating directly with his bones. The light was going strange — that thick, sourceless glow storms make, where shadows lose their edges and the world looks lit from inside the cloud.

He thought about what Halloran had said, and the more he turned it, the heavier it got. *She's been following you since the foothills.* June. Of course. He should have known — a woman who'd spent twenty years not waiting for her father wasn't going to wait for her father's map either. She'd come to take what was hers, or to burn it, or simply to stand where Elias couldn't reach her anymore and say aloud everything she'd rehearsed for two decades. And now Emiliano knew she was on the mountain. And Teo. And Odile. *You're herding it.* Every campfire, every shared meal, every mile of trust — catalogued, mapped, priced.

The betrayal wasn't the ambush to come. Ambushes he understood; ambushes were just weather with intentions. The betrayal was the shape of it — that the debt had never been a debt, that the week he'd been given had been a leash measured to fit, that every door out of the Delgados' office had opened onto another room in the Delgados' house. He thought of Marisol, the youngest, warning him once, quietly, that her brothers never forgave twice. He wondered, now, whether she'd known what the week was really for. Whether the warning had been mercy or management.

The first flakes came while he was still standing there — sparse, dry, spinning, the advance scouts of the main force. They landed on the oilcloth sleeve over his heart and did not melt. That was the detail that finally moved him: snow that doesn't melt means the ground has given up its heat, means winter has taken the mountain, means the passes are closing on schedule and every hour is now a debt of a different kind.

He clipped the compass to his chest strap, turned northeast by north, and started up the spine of the ridge with the storm at his back and the wind trying to take his hat, his hood, his balance, his name. Below and behind him, the black SUV was a shrinking speck on the switchbacks, and somewhere ahead — over the saddle, down in the basin — were a botanist, a boy, and a woman who hated her father, all of them herded together by a man in a black SUV who thought promises were inventory.

Dominic climbed. Not fast. Fast was for people who weren't sure of the outcome, and for the first time in longer than he could remember, he was sure of exactly one thing: the map mattered less than the messenger, whatever Emiliano Delgado's sentence said. The messenger was still breathing, and the messenger intended to stay that way all the way to Kestrel Pass — and if Halloran's crew wanted to argue about it, they could take it up with the storm, which had opinions of its own and no ledger at all.

Halfway up the spine, he stopped once, breath sawing, and looked back the way he'd come. The valley below had vanished into gray. Somewhere in that gray were the people who'd attached themselves to him uninvited and become, without ceremony or consent, the closest thing to family he'd had in twenty years. Somewhere in that gray, too, was danger he couldn't see and couldn't outrun and couldn't pay off.

He thought of Marisol's quiet warning — *they never forgive twice* — and understood at last that it hadn't been a threat. It had been a prophecy. And prophecies, he'd learned on a thousand flats chasing dying storms, always came with a window. You just had to move before it closed.

The snow thickened. The ridge narrowed. He put his head down and climbed into the white, toward the saddle, toward June, toward whatever waited on the far side of a promise he was finally, at long last, poor enough to keep.
