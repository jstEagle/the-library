## 13. First Snow on the Ridge

June Crane had been following a stranger for nine days, and she hated every mile of it.

She hated the way she'd learned to do it — reading tire ruts in the mud like her father had once read ore veins, noting which way the water ran through the washes, counting the hours between the truck's passage and the softening of its tracks. She hated that she was good at it. She hated that Elias Crane had taught her everything she knew about tracking, back when he still bothered to teach her anything at all, back when she'd sat cross-legged in the bed of his pickup with a rifle across her knees and he'd said, *you watch the edges of things, June-bug. The middle lies.*

The middle lied. The middle of her life was a garage in Foothill City with a hydraulic lift that groaned like an old man and a calendar she never marked because there was nothing coming. The edges were where things happened — the edge of town, the edge of the range, the edge of a road where a gray-haired man with a compass had stood in her doorway holding something that belonged to her father.

She'd slammed the door on him. She'd spent twenty years not waiting for Elias Crane, and she wasn't going to start waiting now, not even for his ghost, not even for whatever this Dominic Vasquez was carrying — the compass, maybe, the brass one with the dented lid that had lived in Elias's breast pocket since before June was born. She'd recognized it instantly. That was the worst part. Not the map, not the promise, not the dying-man's-plea the stranger had stammered out while she stood with a wrench in her hand and grease on her knuckles. The compass. Her father's compass, in another man's hand, pointed toward her like an accusation.

So she'd closed the garage, left a note for her neighbor to feed the cat, and driven into the mountains after a man she didn't know, chasing a dead man's errand she wanted no part of.

*You're just like him,* she told herself somewhere past the second ridge, and had to pull over and sit with that for a long time, engine idling, hands white on the wheel.

* * *

The snow came in the afternoon of the ninth day, the way early snow does — not falling so much as arriving, sifting down out of a sky the color of dishwater, fine as flour, indifferent as dust. It settled on the sage without melting. That was the thing about first snow above eight thousand feet: it didn't melt. It stayed. It was a message written in a language everyone in these mountains understood and pretended not to: *the passes are closing. Whatever you mean to do, do it soon.*

June saw the truck first — Dominic Vasquez's truck, mud to the wheel wells, one headlight cracked, parked in the lee of a granite shoulder where the old mining road bent around toward Kestrel Pass. Beside it, a second vehicle she hadn't expected: a sagging camper van painted the green of pond water, with a botany poster curling off its rear door and a boy of maybe twelve stacking firewood against the wind. And a woman — tall, silver-streaked hair braided over one shoulder, moving through the scrub with the unhurried authority of someone who belonged to no road at all — gathering something from under the rocks. Juniper, June realized. They were making camp.

And there, at the tailgate, hunched over a camp stove with a tin cup steaming in his hands, was the stranger himself.

He looked worse than he had in her doorway. Thinner. Rain-scoured. Like a man who'd been wrung out and hung on a line and taken down too soon. When he saw her truck grinding up the switchback, he stood so fast he knocked the cup over, and June watched him do the arithmetic of her arrival — the same truck, the same grease-stained jacket, the same face that had stared at him through a screen door nine days ago — and arrive at the only possible conclusion.

"You followed me." He said it like a man confirming a diagnosis.

"I came for my father's things."

"Your father's things are—" He stopped. Started again. "I made him a promise."

"You made a dying stranger a promise. I made a living girl one. I promised myself I would never again stand in a doorway waiting for Elias Crane to come home." She got out of the truck. The cold came up through her boots immediately, honest cold, the kind that didn't negotiate. "Then I broke it. So congratulations. We've both broken promises this month. His, mine, yours eventually — everyone breaks them. It's the family trade."

The woman with the braid had drifted closer, watching with the particular stillness of someone cataloguing weather. The boy had stopped stacking wood. June registered them both and dismissed them; she had no quarrel with bystanders.

"The compass," she said. "Give it back."

"It's not mine to give." Dominic's voice was steady but tired, the voice of a man who had rehearsed this argument against a campfire for a week and found the rehearsal useless. "It's yours. It was always yours. I'm carrying it *to* you. That's the whole — that's the entire point of—"

"Then hand it over and drive back down the mountain."

"I can't."

"Why not?"

"Because your father asked me to bring it to you in Kestrel Pass. Not on a roadside. Not halfway. In Kestrel Pass, before the snow closes the road, and I gave my word, and I know how that sounds, I know exactly how that sounds coming from a man like me—"

"What's that supposed to mean? A man like you?"

"A man who owes money to people who smile when they collect." He said it flatly, no self-pity in it, and that flatness was somehow worse than pleading. "A man whose word is worth what his collateral is worth. Your father took one look at me in that diner and decided I was worth a deathbed promise anyway. I've been trying to figure out why ever since."

June opened her mouth to say something cutting and found, to her fury, that nothing cutting arrived. Because the answer was obvious, and she'd known it since the doorway, and she'd been driving nine days to avoid saying it aloud: Elias Crane had always been a good judge of broken men. He'd simply never stuck around to see if they stayed broken.

"That's not an answer," she managed.

"No," Dominic agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

They argued as the light went. They argued while Odile — the woman with the braid, who introduced herself with a nod and no surname, as though names were a formality she'd outgrown — quietly set two more places at the fire. They argued while Teo, the boy, fed sticks to the flames with the solemn concentration of a child tending something fragile. They argued about the map, about the diner, about whether a coughing man in a vinyl booth had any right to conscript a stranger into his unfinished business.

"He had no right," June said. "Whatever he was dying of, it doesn't buy him a courier. It doesn't buy him *me*. Twenty years. Do you understand what twenty years is? I was fourteen. Fourteen-year-olds wait by windows. I did it for three years — birthdays, Christmases, the first day of hunting season, because he always came back for hunting season, that was the one thing, the one reliable—"

Her voice cracked on *reliable*, and she hated it, and kept going anyway.

"—and then he didn't. No note. No letter. Nothing but the truck payment getting made somehow, money appearing in an envelope like magic, like guilt, and then even that stopped. You know what I thought happened to him? I thought he found another daughter. Somewhere with better weather. I used to lie awake inventing her. She had his laugh and none of his habits and she lived near the ocean."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Above them the snow thickened, fat flakes now, catching in the juniper, hissing where they drifted too close to the flames.

"He talked about you," Dominic said finally. "In the diner. Eleven minutes, maybe twelve — he wasn't well enough for more. And most of it was fever and most of it was apology, but the parts that weren't — he said you could field-strip an engine blindfolded. He said you fixed the carburetor on his truck when you were fifteen and never let him forget it. He said—" Dominic paused, and June watched him decide whether to say it, and watched him lose the argument with himself and say it anyway. "He said you were the best thing he ever did and the one thing he did wrong, and he couldn't figure out how those could be the same sentence."

"That's easy," June said. "You leave. Then they're the same sentence."

"Is that what you think he did? Just — left?"

"I don't think anything. Thinking requires information, and he provided none. For twenty years." She wrapped her arms around herself. The cold had gotten inside her jacket somewhere along the last switchback and made itself at home. "I got one letter. One, when I was nineteen. Postmarked nowhere, no return address. It said *I'm working on something that will make it right.* That's all. That's the whole inheritance. *Working on something.*"

Dominic looked at the fire. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, careful, like a man walking on ice and listening for the crack.

"There's more than the map," he said. "There are letters. Sewn into the backing. I haven't read them — a friend of mine did, Odile, she—"

"I know," June said. "I read her statement. I tracked her van's axle grease from three counties away; I know everything about all of you, I've had nine days and nothing else to do." She turned. "What did they say. The letters."

Odile spoke for the first time, from the far side of the fire, her voice even as a level. "They said he left to pay a debt. Your mother's hospital debt. He went into the high country to work claims that might cover it, and the claim that finally did — the one the map leads to — took longer than a season. Longer than five. He wrote letters every year and never sent them because they were empty-handed letters, and he couldn't bear to come home empty-handed twice."

June stared at her.

"That's a story," she said. "That's a story strangers tell each other over fires. People write beautiful explanations for themselves all the time. Dead men especially. Dead men have excellent excuses; they never have to live with them."

"Yes," Odile said simply. "That's true. It might be a story. But it's a story written in his handwriting, addressed to you, dated every year on your birthday. Twenty of them. Men who abandon their daughters don't usually keep writing to them for twenty years. They just leave."

The words landed harder than June wanted them to. She looked at the fire because looking anywhere else was impossible. Twenty letters. Twenty birthdays. A man sitting alone in some freezing cabin writing *Dear June* year after year and sealing each letter into silence because shame is heavier than ore and easier to carry, or harder — she couldn't decide which, and the not-deciding was its own kind of grief.

"I hate him," she said, to the fire, to nobody.

"I know," Dominic said.

"I'm allowed to."

"You're allowed to."

"And I don't know how to stop."

Nobody answered that. There was no answer. Teo, who had been very still for a long time, got up without a word and put another log on the fire, and the sparks went up into the snowfall and were extinguished, one by one, like small efforts that don't survive the climb.

* * *

Exhaustion won the argument in the end, the way it always does, the way it had won every argument June had ever had with herself. Anger burns fuel like a rich mixture, and hers had been running hot for nine days and twenty years, and somewhere past midnight there was simply nothing left in the tank.

She woke once in the night — she'd refused the tent, taken her bedroll against the truck's leeward side, stubborn to the last — and heard the storm properly for the first time. Wind moving down the saddle above them with a sound like freight, snow driving horizontal, and beneath it all the small sounds of the camp: the stove ticking, canvas snapping, Teo's uneven breathing, Odile murmuring something in French to herself, half-prayer, half-inventory. And one other sound. Footsteps, careful ones, crunching and stopping, crunching and stopping.

She opened her eyes. Dominic was crouched a few feet away, wrapping something around her bedroll's foot end — a wool blanket, army surplus, the kind that smells like cedar and other people's fires. He'd tucked the compass inside it. Her father's compass, dented lid, worn brass, placed at the foot of her bed like a sentinel.

"Your feet were cold," he whispered, caught. "I could see them. From the fire."

"They're fine."

"Sure."

"Don't do things for me."

"Noted." He started to withdraw, then stopped, crouching there in the blowing snow, and for a moment neither of them moved, and the storm filled the silence the way water fills a hull. "For what it's worth," he said, barely audible over the wind, "he didn't talk about you like a man talking about something he lost. He talked about you like a man talking about something he was still trying to earn. There's a difference. I've owed money my whole adult life; I know the difference between regret and debt. Regret you carry. Debt you try to pay. He was trying to pay."

June lay still. The blanket smelled like cedar and woodsmoke and, faintly, underneath it, like the cab of an old pickup — diesel and pine air freshener and the particular ghost-smell of Elias Crane, which she would have known anywhere, which she had spent twenty years trying to forget and had failed completely.

"My mother used to say he had two speeds," she heard herself say. Her voice came out strange, scraped thin, younger than she was. "Gone and guilty. She said it funny. She forgave him faster than I ever will, and she's the one he bankrupted."

"Maybe forgiveness isn't a race," Dominic said. "Maybe it's more like weather. It comes when it comes. You can't chase it any faster than it moves."

"You're a storm chaser. You chase weather."

"Yeah," he said, and she could hear the tired smile in it even in the dark. "And I'll tell you what twenty years of that taught me. You can't make a storm happen. You can only put yourself where it's likely to break, and then stand there."

He went back to the fire. June lay awake a long time, watching snow accumulate on the truck's hood in a smooth white curve, thinking about her father standing in doorways of his own — cabin doors, assay office doors, post office doors — putting himself where forgiveness was likely to break, and standing there, year after year, until the standing killed him.

Somewhere in the small hours she slept, and dreamed of a window, and a boy's truck pulling away, and did not wake crying, which was new.

* * *

Morning came white and silent and terrible with beauty, the kind of morning that makes you furious at the world for being gorgeous when you feel like this. The storm had passed in the night, spent, and what remained was six inches of fresh snow over everything — the sage, the granite, the trucks, the world — and a sky rinsed blue, and the pass ahead of them glittering like a road paved with broken glass.

They broke camp without much talk. Odile moved through it efficiently, handing out coffee, checking Teo's ears and gloves with a mother's brisk tenderness disguised as logistics. The boy had adopted June overnight in the way children adopt grieving strangers — silently, by proximity, by leaving a hand-warmer packet on her tailgate without comment. She found it there when she packed up and put it in her pocket and said nothing, and that felt like the correct currency between them.

"We should talk about the road ahead," Dominic said, stamping feeling back into his boots. "Halloran's crew is somewhere behind us. So is Marisol Delgado. The pass closes within days — maybe today, if the next system comes through the way the barometer's falling. Once we're through the saddle, there's no coming back this season."

"I know. I've been following your weather reports for nine days. You talk in your sleep, by the way."

"I've been told."

"I'm coming with you," June said. It wasn't a question and she didn't make it one. "Not for you. Not for his map. I want his letters. All twenty of them. And whatever else he left — I want to be the one who finds it, not you, not Halloran, not anyone. If there's an inheritance, I'll read it myself. First. Before anybody."

Dominic considered her for a moment, and then nodded slowly, and there was something in his face that wasn't relief exactly, and wasn't surrender either. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a storm front organize itself on the horizon and understanding that it's bigger than him and he's going to be inside it regardless.

"Fair terms," he said. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When we find the cache — and we will, the cipher's nearly solved — you open the letter addressed to you. Not me. Not Odile. You. Whenever you're ready. Could be at the cache, could be at the pass, could be never. But it's yours, and nobody touches it before you do."

June looked at him for a long moment. Then out at the pass, white and blinding and waiting, the road her father had walked away on twenty years ago and never walked back down.

"All right," she said.

They rolled out an hour later, three vehicles strung along the old mining road like beads on a fraying thread — the mud-caked truck in front, the green van behind, June's rig bringing up the rear, because she trusted her own eyes and no one else's brakes. The snow squeaked under the tires. The sun climbed. Behind them, far down the valley, June caught in her mirror a smudge of movement where no movement should be — dark shapes on the white slope, four of them, maybe five, descending toward the road from the ridgeline like crows dropping off a wire.

She watched them for a full minute, doing the math on their bearing and speed, and then she leaned on the horn, three short blasts, the signal her father had taught her for *trouble, close the gap, stay together.*

Ahead, the truck braked. Ahead, the green van swerved to the shoulder.

The shapes on the slope began to move faster.
