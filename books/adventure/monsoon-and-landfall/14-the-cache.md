## 14. The Cache

The cipher had defeated them twice before dawn.

Dominic sat on a flat stone above the tree line with the map spread across his knees, its torn edge ragged where Halloran's man had ripped it in the ambush, and studied the last of Elias Crane's handiwork by the gray light coming up out of the east. The old man had drawn the ridge itself — every saddle, every scree chute, every twisted whitebark pine rendered in careful ink — but the final location was not marked. It was *written*, and it was written like a man who had spent twenty years learning not to trust paper that could be stolen.

*Where the mountain drinks,* Elias had penciled in the margin, small as a confession. *Count what she swallows.*

"Where the mountain drinks," Odile repeated now, crouched beside him, her breath steaming. She had her field notebook open, sketching the drainage below them from memory. "That's a spring line. Snowmelt comes off this face all summer. There'll be a dozen seeps."

"Count what she swallows." Teo was standing a little apart, the way he always stood when the sky looked heavy, his eyes moving along the ridgeline the way other boys watched traffic. "Swallows what?"

"Water," Dominic said.

"Then we count springs?" Teo frowned. "There's too many."

"There were too many yesterday," Odile said. "But look at the map again. He drew the seeps he knew about — see these little marks, here, here, here? Little circles with tails. Those are springs he mapped. So the cipher isn't asking us to find water. It's telling us which one is different."

Dominic turned the map ninety degrees, then back. The little tailed circles ran down the face of the ridge like beads on a string, seven of them, each labeled in Elias's cramped hand with an elevation. And at the bottom margin, almost lost in the foxing of the paper, a single line:

*Six drink. One keeps.*

He read it aloud.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Above them the ridge went up into cloud, and the wind came down it smelling of stone and cold iron, and somewhere far below, back down the valley they'd climbed out of, the world was still green and warm and belonged to people who had never heard of the Verruga Lode.

"One keeps," June said.

She had hardly spoken all morning. She'd hardly slept either — Dominic had heard her turning in the bivouac sack through most of the night, and once, very low, saying something to no one, or to someone who wasn't there anymore. Now she came and knelt and put her finger on the margin line, and her hand was steady even though her voice wasn't quite.

"He means one spring doesn't run dry. One goes underground and stays there. A sink." She looked up at him, and for a second the anger was gone from her face entirely and what was underneath was so young it hurt to look at. "My father used to talk about sinks. When I was little. I thought he was making it up. I thought —" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started over. "I thought everything he said was made up."

"It isn't made up," Teo said quietly. "We're standing on it."

* * *

They found the sixth spring an hour later, a thread of meltwater sliding out from under a shelf of granite, cold enough to ache. They counted it. They walked on. The seventh took them until midmorning, because it wasn't running at all — because that was the point.

It lay in a fold of the mountainside where two slabs of rock leaned together like a book half closed, and there was no water coming out of it, only going in. A dark slot under the stone, maybe a hand's width wide, and the snowmelt from the whole upper face funneled toward it in braided silver threads and vanished without a sound. No gurgle. No echo. The mountain drank, and kept.

June stood over it for a long time.

"He used to say water remembers where it's been," she said finally. "I thought that was just something drunk men said."

"Elias didn't drink," Odile said gently. "Not until the end. I've read his letters now, June. All of them. He was sober for nineteen years because he promised your mother, and then he broke it for one winter because he couldn't live with what he'd done, and then he got up again. That's in the letters too. He wrote everything down. He was terrified of being misunderstood and he did it anyway, to everyone, his whole life."

June didn't answer. But she crouched down at the mouth of the slot, and she reached into the cold dark with both hands, and she began, wordlessly, to pull loose stones out of the drain.

Dominic joined her. Then Odile. Then Teo, small and fierce, hauling rocks bigger than his own head and stacking them with the precision of a boy who had learned early that if you wanted a thing done you did it yourself. They worked in the wind for the better part of an hour, and the sun crossed behind cloud and came out again, and the water ran cold over their wrists the whole time, and near the bottom of the fill, under a slab that took all four of them to shift, Dominic's fingers found something that was not stone.

A tin box, soldered shut, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age.

And beside it, wedged deeper in the crack where the water could not reach, a canvas rucksack, rotted at the straps, heavy as a body.

* * *

They carried everything up out of the drainage to a shelf of granite sheltered from the wind, and there Dominic cut the oilcloth with his pocketknife while the others watched, and nobody breathed much.

Inside the tin: glass vials, packed in wool, eleven of them, twelve spaces. Each vial held a fist of gray-white rock veined with something that caught the light wrong — not glittering, exactly. Glowering. Like the light went into the stone and didn't want to come back out. Dominic lifted one to the sun and turned it, and the vein flashed a deep, liquid silver, and his hands, which had shaken in front of the Delgado brothers' ledger and hadn't shaken since, shook now.

"That's argentite," Odile said softly. She had taken a vial without asking, the way she took everything, and was holding it up against the sky. "Or I'm a botanist and I'm hallucinating. Look at the cleavage. Look at how it — Dominic. This is high-grade. This is *absurdly* high-grade. Eleven samples. He sampled the whole strike."

"The Verruga Lode," Teo whispered, like a prayer he'd learned from adults.

"The Verruga Lode," Dominic agreed. His voice came out strange. He set the vial down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that could go off. Eleven vials. Each one worth more than his truck, his compass, his debt, his life insurance policy, which was to say nothing at all. Each one worth more than everything the Delgado brothers could take from him, multiplied past the point where arithmetic stopped meaning anything.

He became aware that June was looking at him.

"Sell it," she said. Not a question. Not quite an accusation either. Something worse — an offer. "Right now. Today. You could walk away. You could pay them off, buy your truck a new engine, disappear somewhere warm. Nobody would ever find you." Her jaw worked. "I'd understand. Everybody leaves. At least you'd leave rich."

Dominic looked at the vials gleaming in their wool beds, and he thought about the Delgados' cheerful faces, and Halloran's reasonable voice above treeline, and the ledger page with his name on it, and he let himself feel the whole weight of the temptation, honestly, the way you test ice before you trust it.

Then he looked at June's face, and the temptation died without ceremony.

"No," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because it's not mine." He closed the tin. "Because a dying man gave it to me with instructions, and the instructions weren't *get rich*. And because—" He stopped, because the true reason was embarrassing, and said it anyway. "Because I've spent two years learning what it costs when people treat promises like weather. Like something that changes. I'm not doing it."

June stared at him. Wind pulled at her hair. Somewhere below, the hidden spring drank the mountain in silence.

"You're an idiot," she said at last, and her voice cracked clean through the middle of the word, and she turned away fast and stood at the edge of the shelf with her arms wrapped around herself, looking down at the valley, and no one said anything, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't break something.

* * *

The rucksack was heavier than ore.

Dominic opened it expecting tools, maybe — a prospector's kit, a hammer, a cold lantern. Instead the top layer was paper. Dozens of sheets, folded, bundled in twine, and beneath them a leather roll that clinked faintly, and beneath that, wrapped in a flour sack gone soft as cloth, a single envelope.

His name was on it. *June.* Written in the same cramped hand as the map margins, but slower, more deliberate, the letters of a man who had written and rewritten in his head for twenty years and finally committed.

No surname. Just *June*, the way you write a name when you have no right to anything longer.

Nobody moved.

The wind came up the valley and combed through the whitebark pines below them with a sound like surf, and the cloud shadows ran across the slope, and the tin of silver sat between them all shining its quiet impossible shine, and the envelope lay on top of everything, addressed to a woman who was standing three feet away and could not make herself reach for it.

"Well," Odile said at last. "Open it."

"No," June said.

"It's yours—"

"I know it's mine." Her voice was flat and shaking at once. "That's why I'm not opening it. Twenty years. Twenty years he could have sent this. One stamp. One letter. He had time to solder a tin. He had time to hide a rucksack under a spring. He had time for *all of this*—" she gestured at the whole mountainside, the map, the vials, the cipher, the monument of care that a dead man had built instead of picking up a pen — "and he never once wrote to me. And now he's dead, and now I'm supposed to stand on a frozen mountain and read his excuses, and everyone will watch my face, and whatever he says, it won't bring back one single day of—"

She stopped. Breathed. The anger ran out of her like water into a sink, and what was left was worse.

"What if it's bad?" she said, very quietly. "What if I read it and it's bad? Then it's the last thing he ever gave me and it's bad."

Teo stepped forward. He was fourteen and terrified of storms and he had followed a stranger across a mountain range because the stranger's truck felt safer than being alone, and now he picked up the envelope and held it out to June with both hands, the way you hand someone something breakable.

"Then don't read it alone," he said.

June looked at the boy. Looked at the letter. Looked, last of all, at Dominic, and there was a question in her face she couldn't say out loud, and Dominic answered it the only way he could.

"When you're ready," he said. "Not before. We've got a pass to cross and a storm coming, and that letter will keep. It's kept twenty years. It can keep another day."

June took the envelope from Teo. She didn't open it. She held it against her chest with both hands, over her heart, the way you hold something you're afraid of and refuse to put down anyway, and she nodded once, and turned to help pack the camp.

* * *

They buried the cache again — not well, because none of them were miners, but well enough, stones reset, water diverted, the slot left looking like every other silent seam in the mountain. Dominic kept three vials in his coat, wrapped in wool, proof against the day when proof would matter. Odile carried the letters. June carried the envelope. Teo carried the empty flour sack folded in his pocket for no reason anyone asked about, and Dominic suspected no reason was needed; some things you keep because carrying them is the point.

They were breaking camp when Odile went still, her head cocked, the way she went still around weather.

"Dominic."

He followed her gaze down the valley they had climbed out of. Far below, at the tree line, tiny with distance, movement. Figures. Four of them, maybe five, strung out along the slope they themselves had ascended that morning, moving with the unhurried purpose of men who knew where they were going because they were following tracks.

Snow was beginning to fall. Big, slow, deliberate flakes, the first real ones, drifting down through the windless air like the sky taking its time.

"They found the wash-out trail," Odile said. "They're a half day behind us. Maybe less."

Dominic put the map away, and touched the compass in his pocket, and looked north up the valley toward Kestrel Pass, where the clouds were already stacking themselves into something with intent.

"Then we don't sleep," he said.
