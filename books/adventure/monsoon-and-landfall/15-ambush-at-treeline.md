## 15. Ambush at Treeline

Teo woke to the sound of his own name being spoken wrong.

Not *Teo* the way Odile said it, with the soft o at the end like a door easing shut. Not the way Dominic said it, quick and casual, tossing it over his shoulder from the driver's seat. This was his name in a stranger's mouth, low and deliberate, and it came from somewhere below the tents, down among the black shapes of the krummholz where the spruce bent themselves into crouches against the wind.

He lay still in his sleeping bag and counted.

He had learned counting in the year after his parents' truck went off the road near Villa Hermosa, the year the storms began to have faces. He counted thunder between flashes to guess how far the sky was. He counted breaths to make panic pass through him like weather instead of staying like a tenant. He counted the seconds between Odile's snores and Dominic's, a rhythm he knew now the way other boys knew the rhythms of songs. Three breaths from Odile, one long exhale from Dominic, the wind, then again.

Three breaths. One exhale. Wind.

And underneath, a fourth sound that did not belong: the click of a boot heel on stone, patient as a clock.

Teo slid his hand out of the bag and found the cold of the ground through the tent floor, the kind of cold that meant the temperature had fallen all night, that meant the clouds above them were not rain clouds anymore but something with more intention in it. He had been afraid of storms since Villa Hermosa. He had run from them, hidden from them, learned their grammar so he could predict them the way you learn the moods of a dangerous animal. But this — this was not weather. Weather never whispered your name.

He crawled to the tent flap and looked out.

The camp sat on a shelf of ground just below treeline, the last place trees would consent to grow before the rock took over. They had made camp in the blue hour before dark, and now the dark had thinned into that gray that comes before dawn, when everything looks like a photograph of itself. The tents were two low humps. The truck was a darker shape beyond them, its hood furred with frost. Above, the ridge they had to cross stood against a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and the first snow of the season lay along its crest like a line of salt someone had spilled and not bothered to sweep.

Four men stood among the trees.

They were not trying to hide. That was the thing Teo understood first and understood worst — they were standing in the open, spaced out the way Odile spaced herself when she was surveying a slope, professionally, as if the camp were a plant community and they had come to take notes. The nearest one had a flashlight, off, held down against his thigh. The one farthest right held something longer and thinner, and Teo's stomach did a slow, cold roll when he understood it was a rifle, carried the way Dominic carried the tow strap: a tool, nothing dramatic, something you'd use and then put away.

He should wake them. The thought arrived whole and reasonable. Wake Odile. Wake Dominic. That was the procedure. That was what a person did.

But his body had its own older procedures, and they said: be small. Be stone. Be the rock the water goes around. He had spent two years after Villa Hermosa being the rock the water went around, and even now, even with Odile sleeping three feet away and Dominic's compass hanging in the tent beside him, some buried part of him believed that stillness was safety, that anything that moved got taken.

The man with the rifle turned his head. Even at that distance, in that gray light, Teo felt the turn like a hand on the back of his neck.

He moved.

He was out of the tent and into the cold so fast he left his boots half-laced, and the frost bit through his socks, and he didn't care, because behind him he heard the tent flap tear and a voice say, not loudly, almost politely: "Morning, folks. Sorry for the hour."

Then Odile's voice, awake and already hard: "Teo. *Aquí.*"

He ran to her. She was out of her tent in a wool shirt and no coat, her gray hair loose and wild, and she put one arm around him and pulled him behind her with a motion so smooth it was almost gentle, and then she stood between him and the men, and Teo saw her hands — her beautiful careful hands, the hands that pressed flowers and drew ferns and had taught him to sketch cloud types — curl into fists.

"You're on private claim ground," the man with the flashlight said. He was heavyset, with a red face and a voice like gravel poured slowly. "We're just here to talk to the man with the map."

"There is no man with a map," Odile said. "There is a botanist, her son, and a storm chaser who owes me a ride to the next valley. You are trespassing on a research transect. I will note it in my log, with your faces."

The man smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was patient. "Ma'am, everybody's got a log. Question is what gets written in it tomorrow."

* * *

It went wrong slowly and then all at once, the way weather does.

Dominic came out of his tent with his hands visible and his voice easy — Teo had heard that voice a hundred times, the one Dominic used on mechanics and loan men and anyone holding power over him, a voice like a hand held flat to show it was empty. "Gentlemen. Early season for visitors. Can I offer coffee? It's terrible coffee, but there's a lot of it."

"We'll pass," the red-faced man said. "Map, Vasquez. That's the whole conversation. You give it up, you walk down this mountain with your debt squared and your health intact. Everybody's told you what the alternative looks like. I'm told you don't listen good."

"I listen great," Dominic said. "I just don't hear anything in there worth the price."

Teo, pressed against Odile's side, watched the other three men shift. It was subtle, a redistribution of weight, the way a slope shifts before it slides. One drifted left, toward the truck. One drifted right, cutting the line down the slope. The one with the rifle didn't move at all, and that was its own message.

Odile's arm tightened around him. Her lips moved near his ear, barely breath: "The rocks. When I say."

"Odile," Dominic said, and something in his voice had changed — the flat charm gone out of it, leaving something quicker underneath. "Whatever happens in the next minute, you take Teo and you go uphill. Not down. Up. The scree field, the one I showed you. You can't be followed through that fast."

"I'm not leaving you on this mountain with—"

"Odile." And then, quieter, in a tone Teo had never heard him use, a tone with no performance in it at all: "Please. I've done the math on this my whole life. Let me do it once for something that matters."

The red-faced man sighed, theatrical, almost kind. "Last chance, Vasquez."

Dominic reached slowly into his jacket, and Teo's heart stopped, because he thought — they all thought — he was reaching for the map. But what came out was the compass, the brass one, Elias Crane's compass with the worn leather strap, and Dominic held it up in the gray light where everyone could see it.

"This?" Dominic said. "This is what you're after? Because this is just a compass. It points north. It doesn't point to anything you want."

The red-faced man's smile dropped away like a mask that had been held up by the smiling. "Take it," he said, and the world came apart.

Teo would spend a long time afterward trying to assemble those ninety seconds into an order that made sense, and he never quite could. There was the sound of Odile's voice — *¡Los roques, ahora!* — and her hand shoving him, actually shoving him, toward the boulder field, and his own legs answering before his mind did. There was Dominic's body suddenly in motion, the compass flung high and wide into the air like a coin, a bright spinning arc that every eye — even Teo's, even Odile's — followed for one helpless half-second. There was the red-faced man lunging, and the second man swinging something, and Dominic going down under it with a sound Teo would hear for years, a sound like a sack of grain hitting a floor, and then getting up, and going down again, and getting up.

There was Odile, who should have run.

She didn't run. Teo saw it happen the way you see a rockfall begin: the small motion first, the pebble, the one detail that tells you the whole slope is going. She saw the third man — the one who had drifted toward the tents — reach down and take the map tube from where it lay inside Dominic's open pack, and she saw the red-faced man turn toward the trees, the prize secured, the conversation over, and she did the arithmetic that Teo would later understand had nothing to do with the map and everything to do with the boy she'd pulled out of a flood two valleys ago.

She threw herself at the man with the tube.

They went down together into the frost and the stones. Teo was ten meters away, at the edge of the scree, screaming her name — he could hear himself screaming it, a thin faraway sound as if someone else were doing it — and the man rolled on top of her, and the tube came free, and then the red-faced man was there too, and there was a brief, terrible tug, and the map came out of the tube.

It tore.

Teo heard it tear. Over the wind, over Odile's voice saying something low and fierce in a language that wasn't Spanish, over his own screaming — he heard the paper part, that soft wet rip, like the sound of the world's skin splitting. Half of it stayed in the red-faced man's fist. Half of it was in Odile's hand, and she was on her knees now, and there was blood on her face, coming from somewhere near her temple, black in the gray light, running down along her jaw and dripping from her chin onto the paper she held, onto her father's mountains — onto Elias's mountains, the ones he had drawn with a shaking hand in a diner at dusk.

"Go," Odile said. She said it in Spanish and then in English and then she said it with her eyes, which found Teo's across the distance with a precision that cut him worse than any of it. "*Corre.* Up. *Ahora.*"

He ran. He hated himself for running. He ran.

The scree field took him the way it had taken them on the practice crossing, each step sliding back half a step, the whole mountainside muttering under his feet. Behind him the camp had become noise — shouting, the truck engine catching and revving, a single sound from the rifle that was not a shot but a warning crack, fired into the air or into the ground, he never learned which. He climbed until his lungs burned like he'd swallowed the frost, and then he climbed past that, because Odile had taught him that the body lies about its limits, and because the only thing worse than leaving her was stopping.

He didn't stop until he was above the fog line, where the world was suddenly, horribly beautiful: the sunrise breaking through the cloud deck below him, gilding the tops of the spruce, lighting the snow on the ridge so it burned pink and gold, the whole scene serene as a postcard, as if nothing down there in the gray had a face or a name.

He stood on a rock with his socks frozen to his boots and his breath tearing out of him in rags, and he did the only thing he knew how to do with terror.

He counted.

Dominic: last seen down, getting up. Odile: last seen kneeling, bleeding, holding half a map. The map: torn. The compass: thrown, lost, somewhere in the frost and the stones. The truck: running, in their hands. The pass: still ahead of them, still closed behind no one yet, still the only direction left in the world.

Half a map. He turned the phrase over. Half a map was not nothing. Half a map was half of Elias Crane's promise, and promises, Teo was beginning to understand, were the only currency that had ever been worth anything on this mountain.

A sound below him. Footsteps on the scree, uneven, laboring, and he flattened behind the rock with his heart slamming, ready to run again, ready to run forever —

"Teo." Odile's voice, wrecked and ragged and unmistakably hers. "Teo, it's me. It's only me."

She came up over the edge of the slope with her hand pressed to her temple and her coat torn and the half-map folded with impossible care into her breast pocket, and behind her, limping, gray-faced, one eye already swelling shut, came Dominic, with no map, no compass, no truck, and no reason left to keep climbing except the one he'd started with.

"They took the truck," Dominic said. He sat down heavily on the rock beside Teo, and for a moment the three of them just breathed, three ragged engines idling in the cold. Then Dominic looked at Odile, at the blood on her face, and his voice came out smaller than Teo had ever heard it. "You should have run."

"So should you," Odile said. "We appear to be a family of terrible listeners."

Teo laughed. It came out of him sideways, cracked and half a sob, but it was a laugh, and Odile pulled him against her good side, and above them the sun cleared the ridge and the snow on the pass caught fire, and below them, faint but unmistakable, engines were climbing the switchback road. Someone was coming up the mountain after them.

Half a map. No truck. A storm coming that even Teo, who feared all storms, could feel gathering in the west like a held breath.

"We keep walking," Dominic said, and stood up.
