## 17. Whiteout

The sky had been lying to us all morning.

It wore the pale, apologetic gray of a storm that means to be merciful, and I almost believed it. Almost. But I had spent fifteen years reading weather the way other men read faces, and there was a color in the west — a bruised violet pressed flat against the ridge like a thumb testing fruit — that said otherwise. That said: *I am not finished with you.*

"Wind's backing around," Odile said from the bed of the truck, where she rode propped against the spare tire with her arm bound tight across her chest. The wound from the ambush at treeline had gone from angry to quiet, which was worse; quiet wounds were the ones that killed you on the third day. "Northeast now. It'll funnel through the saddle."

"You should be lying down," I said.

"I *am* lying down. I am being extremely horizontal about this whole enterprise." Her smile was thin as paper but it held. Odile could make a joke out of anything except cruelty, and even then she'd try. "Drive, Dominic. We're losing the light we don't have."

Teo sat between June and Marisol in the cab, wedged into the middle like a keystone, and he hadn't spoken in an hour. He'd gone still in that particular way of his when the pressure dropped — a stillness I'd learned to read as barometer more than mood. His hands were folded in his lap, and his knuckles were white, and he was counting. I knew because he'd told me once, on the high road after the washout, that he counted to keep the thunder from finding him. *One for the doorframe. Two for the window. Three for Mama.* I didn't know what four was for. I hoped he never got there.

The road ended two miles below the saddle, the way roads do exactly when you need them most. After that it was Elias Crane's country: switchbacks cut by miners who'd been dead sixty years, scree fields the color of old bone, and a notch in the granite that Elias had described to me over coffee and blood in a roadside diner as if he were describing a woman he'd loved badly and long.

*"You'll come up through the teeth,"* he'd said, tapping the map with one yellowed fingernail. *"Two spires like a dog's canines. You pass between them and the land opens its hand. There's a cairn my father built, taller than a man, and past the cairn the trail drops toward Kestrel Creek. You follow the sound of water and you cannot get lost, boy. Water always knows the way down."*

Water. Right now the only water in the world was coming down on top of us.

It started as sleet, ticking against the windshield like someone tapping out a code nobody wanted translated. Then the temperature fell ten degrees in as many minutes — I watched it happen on the gauge, watched the needle drop like a man off a cliff — and the sleet turned to snow, and the snow stopped being weather and became a place. A country. A white nation with no borders and no mercy, descending on the mountain all at once, erasing the switchbacks, the scree, the sky itself.

The wind hit us sideways at the last switchback and the truck shuddered like a living thing.

"That's it," June said. "That's all she's got. Look at her temp."

She was right. The old truck had hauled us through monsoon flood and mudslide and ambush, faithful as a dog, and now she stood at eight thousand feet with her radiator steaming and her fuel needle flirting with empty, and there was nothing left to ask of her. I killed the engine and the silence that followed was enormous — the silence of a world where every other sound has been eaten.

"Everyone out," I said. "We walk from here."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about my strange little family, assembled by accident out of floodwater and bad luck: they argued with me about everything except the moments that mattered. Teo climbed down into the snow and immediately sank to his knees. June hauled him up by the collar like a kitten. Marisol Delgado — who three days ago had been sent up this mountain to finish what her brother started, and who had instead handed back the stolen half of a dead man's map and burned her own bridges behind her — pulled her hood down until only her eyes showed and said, in her family's cheerful ledger-voice, "Expenses so far: one truck, one map, one Marisol. All non-refundable."

"Keep walking," I said.

* * *

We walked.

I wish I could tell you I remember it clearly, but whiteout doesn't work that way. Whiteout is not darkness; darkness at least has edges. Whiteout is the world refusing to have edges at all. Up became a rumor. Down became a theory. The horizon dissolved and took distance with it, and after an hour I couldn't have told you whether the next step would land on stone or air. Snow came at us horizontally, then vertically, then in great slow spirals that seemed almost gentle, almost beautiful, the way a drowning man finds the water beautiful.

I led with the compass in my bare left hand and my right fist knotted in Teo's parka, because I had made a rule somewhere below the treeline and the rule was: *we do not lose the boy.* Behind him came June with one arm hooked through Odile's, bearing her along, and Marisol took the rear with the tire iron from the truck bed because she refused to carry a gun and refused to explain why, and I didn't ask. Everyone carries their own iron.

The compass said northeast. The compass had been saying northeast for two hours. And yet some animal part of my brain kept whispering that we were walking in circles, that the compass was broken, that the whole apparatus of direction was a joke the mountain was playing on the small stubborn mammals crawling across its shoulder.

"Tell me about the teeth," June shouted once, close to my ear, under the wind.

"What?"

"Elias. You talked to him. Tell me what he said about the pass." Her face was a raw red oval inside her hood, her breath tearing away in rags. She wasn't asking to navigate. I understood that. She was asking because if her father had described this place — had sat in a diner describing these very rocks to a stranger while his daughter twenty years away believed herself abandoned — then some part of him had been here, and some part of here had been him, and she wanted to stand inside that part before the cold took everything else.

"He said you come up through the teeth," I shouted back. "Two spires like a dog's canines. You pass between them and the land opens its hand."

"Opens its hand," she repeated. She said it the way you'd test a coin with your teeth. Then she nodded once, sharply, and kept walking, and I understood that she'd decided to believe it. Not the geography. The *hand*. The idea that the land her father loved might have been kind to him, might have been trying, in its stony way, to hold him.

The temperature kept falling. I could feel it in my feet first, then my fingers, then a deep ache settling into my jaw. Odile had stopped joking. That frightened me more than the wind did. She leaned on June and put one foot in front of the other with the grim economy of a woman spending her last coins, and twice she stumbled, and twice June caught her, and the second time Odile looked at me over June's shoulder and mouthed something I chose not to read.

And here is the arithmetic I did, because I'm a chaser and chasers do arithmetic the way other men pray. We had maybe three hours of daylight left, except there was no daylight, only a gray dimming that passed for it. The saddle was — according to the map, the half of it Marisol had returned, its torn edge softening already with damp — maybe four miles on. Four miles at our pace, in this, with Odile bleeding internally somewhere and Teo's lips going the wrong shade of blue. The math said stop. Dig in. Shelter rock, wait out the worst, move at first light.

The math said we'd probably live.

But Elias Crane had made me swear an oath over a cup of terrible diner coffee, with blood on his lips and his daughter's name in his mouth, and the oath hadn't said *before spring*. It hadn't said *when convenient*. It had said *before snowfall*, and the snowfall was here, arriving early like a creditor, and somewhere beyond this white blindness was a valley called Kestrel Pass where a woman had spent twenty years not waiting for him. Every hour we stopped was an hour the passes sealed tighter behind us. Every hour was a hour closer to the day Halloran's crew — scattered but not destroyed, humiliated but not finished — regrouped and came up the south approach, which was lower, gentler, and did not require a miracle.

Survival math said stop. The promise said walk.

I've spent my whole life trusting math. Storm cells, fuel gauges, interest rates, the precise weight of what I owed the Delgado brothers down to the penny and the day. Math had never once lied to me. It had also never once saved me. So I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than the chase, harder than the oath: I put the math in my pocket and I kept walking, and I told myself that if this was foolishness, it was at least foolishness with a compass.

* * *

The cairn saved us, or something did.

It appeared out of the white the way ghosts appear in stories — suddenly, impossibly, close enough to touch when a heartbeat before there had been nothing. A tower of stacked stone, taller than a man, furred with rime, leaning east like a listener. Elias's father's cairn. Sixty years dead and the old miner was still doing his job, still marking the way home for whoever came after.

June put both hands against it and stood with her head bowed and the snow streaming off her hood, and none of us said anything, because there are moments that belong to one person and the rest of us just hold the wind.

"Past the cairn," I said finally, my voice cracked and small in all that whiteness, "the trail drops toward Kestrel Creek. Follow the sound of water."

"There is no sound of water," Marisol said. "There is no sound of anything. There is no *anything*."

She was right. The whiteout had swallowed even sound; the wind screamed but it screamed everywhere at once, directionless, a wall of noise with no seams. We could have been standing at the edge of a thousand-foot drop or a flat meadow. The mountain had erased itself.

But Elias had said *follow the sound of water*, and Elias had crossed this saddle eleven times, and a man who crosses a pass eleven times leaves something behind besides footprints. He leaves a shape in his own memory, and he had poured that shape into me over one cup of coffee, and now it was all I had. So I closed my eyes — which changed nothing, which was the point — and I walked the pass in my head the way he'd walked it, and I listened with a sense that wasn't hearing.

"The creek cuts the trail on the left," I said. "He said the trail hugs the wall on the right after the cairn, and the drop-off comes fast. So we keep our right hands on rock. If the rock disappears, we've drifted left, toward the creek and the drop. Everybody keeps contact. Nobody moves without touching somebody else."

"A conga line," Odile rasped, and even half-dead she managed to make it sound like a scientific expedition. "Very well. I shall be the tail. Tails are dignified."

We went. Hand on stone, hand on shoulder, hand on stone. Teo in front of me so I could feel him trembling through his parka, and somewhere in the second mile he stopped trembling, and I want to tell you it was because he found courage, but I think it was simpler and better than that: I think he got tired of being afraid in the same direction everyone else was walking, and decided to spend his fear on forward instead. Once, when the wind gusted so hard we all bent like grass, he reached back without looking and gripped my wrist, hard, and held on until it passed, and neither of us ever mentioned it, and I will remember it until I die.

Twice the rock vanished under my right hand and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world, and both times I called a halt and we shuffled left, blind, feeling for stone, and both times the stone came back under my palm like a hand catching mine, and I thought: *this is what he meant. Opens its hand.* The land wasn't kind. But it was honest, if you listened, and honesty was more than I'd gotten from anyone with a ledger.

The light began to fail in earnest. Not sunset — there was no sun, there was no set, there was only a thickening of the gray toward something darker, and with the dark the cold came down like a verdict. My feet had passed through pain into a buzzing absence. Odile was being carried now, June and Marisol taking turns with her arm over their shoulders, and she had stopped pretending otherwise, which from Odile was a surrender flag.

"We can't—" June started.

"We can," I said. "One more rise. He said after the cairn the trail drops toward Kestrel Creek. One more rise, and then it's downhill, and downhill is easy, downhill is free."

"Dominic." Marisol's voice, stripped of all its family cheer, all its ledger-lightness. "If there is no rise—"

"There's a rise."

"How do you know?"

And I didn't. That was the truth of it. I didn't know anything anymore except northeast and a dead man's voice and the weight of four people breathing behind me. Every survival instinct I owned was screaming the same word — *shelter, shelter, shelter* — and I was walking past every boulder and overhang that offered itself because the promise outweighed the math, because a promise is the only currency that appreciates after the issuer is dead, because Elias Crane had bet his daughter's inheritance on a stranger in a diner and I would die before I let him lose that bet.

"One more rise," I said again, and it came out sounding like a prayer, because it was one.

* * *

We crested it at the last of the light.

There was no view — the whiteout held even here, even now — but there was a change in the wind, a sudden slackening, the way a fist unclenches, and beneath it, faint, intermittent, threaded through the roar like gold through quartz: water. Running water. Falling water.

Kestrel Creek.

Teo heard it first — of course he did, the boy whose ears had been tuned by terror into instruments — and he grabbed my sleeve and pointed down and to the left, and I nodded, and we turned downslope together, all five of us linked like paper dolls, and let the mountain hand us down.

The snow eased as we dropped, as if the storm had been only the mountain's gatekeeper and we had finally paid the toll. By the time we found the creek — black water running loud and certain under a bridge of ice — the flakes had thinned to a drifting lace, and I could see ten feet instead of two, and Odile laughed, actually laughed, a cracked and glorious sound, and said, "Told you. Water always knows the way down."

Below us, far below, a light blinked through the failing murk. One light. Yellow, steady, human. A ranch house, a way station, a cabin — something with walls and a stove and a door that locked.

And then, further off, down-valley, another light answered it. And a third.

June stood at the edge of the drop with the wind pulling her hood back and her hair whipping loose, and she looked at those lights the way a starving woman looks at bread, and then her face changed — tightened, sharpened — and she said, quietly, in a voice that carried anyway:

"That's too many lights for a valley this empty. Somebody's signaling somebody." She turned to me, and the snow caught in her lashes, and her eyes were the coldest and clearest things on the whole frozen mountain. "Dominic. We're not the only ones who made it over."
