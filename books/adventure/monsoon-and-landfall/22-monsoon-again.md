## 22. Monsoon Again

The storm built itself out of nothing, the way the good ones always did.

Dominic Vasquez watched it happen from the shoulder of a county road two hundred miles from anywhere, one boot up on the bumper of a truck that had been rebuilt twice and loved like family. To the southwest, over the flats where the land went on forever in shades of rust and bone, a cumulus tower was climbing with that terrible patient ambition he'd learned to read twenty years ago — the anvil spreading flat as a struck match, the green-black light underneath it, the first flicker of lightning stitching cloud to earth like someone sewing a wound shut.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "Maybe less."

"You said forty," Odile answered from the passenger seat, not looking up from her maps. She kept them folded in a battered leather case now, the same leather case she'd carried through three field seasons and one flash flood, and she smoothed the creases with her thumb the way other people touched rosaries. "You said forty minutes and I planned the day around forty minutes."

"I'm getting better at this."

"You are not getting better at this. You are getting *faster at being wrong*." But she was smiling when she said it, folding the map along its worn lines, and Dominic had learned over twelve months that Odile's insults were a dialect of affection, spoken fluently, meant kindly, never once accidental.

In the back seat, Teo had his head out the window like a dog, which at sixteen he would have denied under oath. The wind coming off the flats pulled his hair straight back and he was grinning into it, radio handset loose in one hand, calling out cloud bases and rain shafts with the easy authority of a boy who had decided storms were things you could name instead of things that could take everything from you. It hadn't come all at once. For months after Kestrel Pass he'd still gone quiet when the pressure dropped, still checked the exits in every room, still slept with his boots by the door. But somewhere between the spring melt and the first summer heat, something in him had turned toward the weather instead of away from it, and now here he was, spotting, calling, alive in the loud way teenagers are alive.

"Curtain's forming," Teo called. "West side's going rain-wrapped. Dom, there's a road cut about two miles up if we want to get ahead of it."

"Two miles up is a dirt track that turns to soup the second it rains."

"That's why we're leaving *now*, genius."

June laughed from the middle seat — actually laughed, head back, the sound bright enough to cut through engine noise — and Dominic caught it in the rearview mirror and held it there a moment longer than driving required. A year ago he hadn't known her name. Six years before that, a coughing man in a roadside diner had pressed a hand-drawn map into his hands and made him swear an oath over bad coffee, and Dominic had spent most of the year since trying to understand what exactly he'd been given. Not silver. He'd declined the lode claim in front of investigators and surveyors both, signed it over clean, and slept better than he had in a decade. What Elias Crane had left his daughter, and what his daughter had somehow, without asking, left him, was this: a truck with four people in it who chose to be there.

He put the truck in gear.

* * *

They chased the storm for ninety miles and lost it twice and found it again, and by late afternoon they were parked on a rise above a dry lakebed watching lightning walk the horizon in long silent strokes, the kind that came down inside the rain curtain where no camera could do them justice. The air smelled like creosote and wet dust and ozone. Rain hit the windshield in fat warm drops, then harder, drumming, until the world outside the glass went soft and gray and the wipers gave up pretending.

Odile brewed coffee on the camp stove in the truck bed under the tailgate awning, because of course she did; the woman had a portable kitchen that could survive artillery. June sat cross-legged on the tailgate with a grease-stained manual open on her knees, though she wasn't reading it. She was watching the storm with an expression Dominic had learned to recognize and still couldn't name — something between her father's squint and something entirely her own.

"He would have liked this one," she said, not turning around. Dominic didn't have to ask who. There was only one *he*, and there always would be.

"Yeah?"

"Elias chased weather too, you know. Not professionally. He just liked knowing what the sky was doing. Said a man who understood storms was never surprised by anything else." She turned a page she hadn't read. "I used to think that was just something prospectors said to sound wise."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was right, and I hate it, because it means he was right about other stuff too." She closed the manual. "Like the letters. Like waiting."

The letter was in a frame now, hanging in June's garage in Kestrel Pass, above the workbench, where she could see it every morning. *Judge me by the promise I kept, not the one I broke.* She'd told Dominic once, very quietly, very drunk on Odile's plum brandy, that she read it every day and it hurt differently each time, the way weather changes but never stops.

Teo climbed into the bed, dripping, and stole a cup of coffee, and Odile smacked his hand and let him keep it, and the four of them sat under the awning while the storm spent itself against the far edge of the lakebed, thunder rolling across the flats like furniture moving in a house overhead.

The mail had come that morning at the motel in town, which was how these things always started now. Three postcards, all from the same place, all unsigned except for a small neat *M* in the corner where a signature should be. One showed a courthouse. One showed a diner. One showed a stretch of highway with a caption that read GREETINGS FROM SOMEWHERE YOU AREN'T. On the back of the third, in handwriting Dominic knew better than his own by now: *Ledger's still balanced. Yours says paid in full. Don't make me audit you.*

Marisol Delgado sent postcards the way weather arrived — irregularly, from unpredictable directions, impossible to prepare for, always meaning more than they said. She'd testified in the spring. She'd sat in a federal courtroom in a gray dress with her hands folded and dismantled her brothers' operation sentence by sentence, cheerful as a teller counting bills, and when it was done she'd walked past Dominic in the hallway and said, "We're even now. Don't be sentimental about it." Then she'd vanished into a witness relocation program with a new name and, presumably, the same old arithmetic.

The postcards were the only proof she was alive, and maybe the only proof she was free. Odile pinned them to the corkboard above the kitchen table in the house they'd rented in Kestrel Pass — all seven so far, arranged in no order anyone could explain, a constellation of courthouses and diners and highways. Teo had started a theory that the locations traced a route, that Marisol was walking some private perimeter, checking the edges of her new life the way you check a fence line. June thought she was just lonely and wouldn't say so. Dominic thought about the youngest Delgado standing in the rain outside a diner a year ago, telling him *they never forgive twice*, warning him with the only currency she had, and he thought: she forgave herself once, and that was the hard part.

He kept the latest postcard in the glove box, under the registration. He didn't examine why.

* * *

The storm died at dusk the way summer storms do, collapsing inward, its last light going amber and strange, and they drove out of the wet onto roads steaming in the evening heat. June had taken over navigation from Odile, who had fallen asleep sitting upright with the dignity of a woman who claimed she was merely resting her eyes, and Teo was narrating the sunset to no one in particular, cataloging colors — *copper, salmon, that weird bruise purple* — with the seriousness of a scientist naming species.

"Left up here," June said. "Then we can take the ridge road home tomorrow. Slow way."

"The slow way adds six hours."

"I know how long it adds. I've driven it." She looked at him sidelong, and the last light caught her face, and Dominic understood that the slow way wasn't about hours at all. It was about the high country, and the pass, and the place where a cache had waited thirty years in a stone cairn for someone to do the arithmetic of love correctly. They drove it every few months now. Nobody ever suggested it. Everybody always agreed.

"Slow way," he said.

"Good." She settled back. "Also your driving is still terrible. You take corners like the truck owes you money."

"The truck *did* owe me money. We worked through it. It's a whole history you weren't part of."

"I'm part of it now." She said it lightly, flipping through the manual again, but Dominic heard the weight under it, the way you hear bedrock under topsoil. Part of it now. All of it, now. The debt, the chase, the whiteout on the saddle where he'd navigated by a dead man's remembered voice and a compass needle and pure refusal to stop — she'd demanded every detail on that first night in the miner's cabin, and she'd never once flinched from any of it, including the parts where he'd almost sold her father's map to save his own skin. *You didn't,* she'd said. *That's the whole story. Everything else is weather.*

The stars came out hard and close, the way they only did over dry country, and Teo fell asleep mid-sentence with the radio handset still in his hand, and Odile snored gently with terrible elegance, and Dominic drove north through the dark with his family scattered asleep around him like dropped tools.

A year ago tonight, give or take a week, he'd been alone in a truck exactly like this one — the same truck, actually, before June rebuilt the transmission and Odile reorganized the cab and Teo claimed the back seat permanently — running from a debt and toward a promise, calculating constantly, the way he used to, how little he owned. Truck. Compass. Map that wasn't his. Life that wasn't worth much on paper.

He owned different things now. None of them fit in ledgers.

* * *

They stopped past midnight at a gas station that was mostly a memory of itself — one pump working, a dog asleep under the ice machine, a clerk who looked at four storm-chasers piled out of a mud-caked truck at one in the morning and decided, wisely, not to have opinions. Dominic filled the tank. June went in for coffee and came out with coffee and, inexplicably, a paper plate of gas-station nachos, which she defended as a lifestyle choice.

That was when he saw the envelope.

It was wedged under the wiper blade, driver's side, weighted against the wind. No stamp. No address. Just his name — *Dominic* — in small, neat, familiar handwriting, the handwriting from seven postcards pinned to a corkboard three hundred miles away.

He stood very still. The dog under the ice machine opened one eye and closed it. Somewhere south, heat lightning flickered behind the mountains, silent, a rumor of the next storm already assembling itself over the horizon.

"What is it?" June asked, coming up beside him, nachos forgotten.

He worked the envelope free. Inside was a single sheet, folded once. No postcard this time. No jokes about audits. Just the small neat hand, filling the page:

*Dom —*

*I'm done running. Turns out you can't outrun a ledger, you can only decide what goes in it. So I'm deciding. There's a hearing in the fall — the last of it, the restitution, my testimony one more time — and after that I'd like to drive north and see whether the corkboard is really as ugly as Teo described it in his letter. Yes, I've been writing to the kid. Yes, he tells me everything. Yes, I know about the slow way. Everyone knows about the slow way.*

*I don't need anything. I want you to understand that first, because I know how you people are. I just thought somebody in that truck should know that the person who warned you about us turned out to be worth saving too. It took me a while to believe it. Postcards are easier than believing it.*

*Save me a seat on the tailgate. I hear the view is good during monsoon season.*

*— M*

Dominic read it twice. Then he handed it to June without a word, and she read it, and her face did something complicated and soft, and behind them Odile stirred awake in the front seat and asked what was happening, and Teo woke too, and the page went around the circle under the fluorescent hum of the gas station canopy while the dog watched with professional indifference.

"She's coming," Teo said finally, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, sixteen years old and not embarrassed about it. "She's actually — she's coming *here*."

"Fall," Odile said, taking the letter with careful hands, reading it a third time. "Monsoon will be ending. Early snow in the passes, though. She'll want to come before—" She stopped. Looked up. Smiled the smile she saved for plans she'd already made. "Before the roads close."

June laughed, wet-eyed, and slung an arm around Teo's shoulders, and Dominic took the letter back and folded it along its crease and put it in the glove box, on top of the registration, where the important papers lived.

South, behind the mountains, the heat lightning pulsed again — closer now, or maybe just clearer, the sky clearing the way it does before it decides to fill up again. Another system coming. There was always another system coming; that was the whole lesson, the one Elias Crane had known and Odile had mapped and Teo had learned young and hard and then learned again, gentler, from the back seat of a truck full of people who stayed. Storms didn't end. They just moved, and regrouped, and came back over the flats where the land went on forever, and you met them or you didn't, and who was sitting next to you when the rain hit was the only forecast that ever mattered.

Dominic got behind the wheel. Four seats occupied, one empty and waiting, the tank full, the north road open under the stars.

"Home?" June asked.

"Home," he said, and drove into the weather.
