## 1. The Navy Won't Discuss It

The wreck lay in forty feet of green water, and the navy had been pretending it wasn't there for eleven days.

Rafael Nystrom knew this because Rafael Nystrom had been counting. Counting was what you did when you were the kind of diver who worked the margins — not the salvage companies with their contracts and their insurance and their cranes, but the ones who came after, who read the tide tables like other people read horoscopes and knew which wrecks the official world had decided to forget. The *Corvina Star* had gone down nine years ago with a cargo manifest that said "agricultural machinery," which was a phrase that meant nothing and could mean anything, and the navy had surveyed it, cordoned it, and then — this was the important part — stopped coming.

Eleven days since the last patrol boat. Eleven days since anyone official had looked at the site. In Rafael's experience, eleven days was the exact length of time it took a bureaucracy to forget a thing it had already decided not to remember.

So at first light, with the river running flat and brown as old tea, Rafael went over the side.

* * *

Diving was the one thing Rafael had never gotten tired of. That was worth saying, because Rafael got tired of everything else — of towns, of faces, of the particular way landlubber conversations always circled back to where are you from and how long are you staying, questions that had no good answers. But forty feet down, where the light went amber and then to a kind of forgiving dusk, all of that fell away. Down there you were nobody from nowhere. You were just hands and lungs and a light beam, and the river didn't care about your name.

The *Corvina Star* sat on her starboard side in the silt, her hull furred with weed, her superstructure collapsed into itself like a dropped cake. Rafael swam the length of her twice before touching anything, which was discipline, and discipline was the only inheritance Rafael's father had ever managed to pass along: *look first, take second, and never be in a hurry underwater, because the river is patient and it can afford to wait for you to be stupid.*

There. Amidships, where the cargo hold had torn open like a pocket. Copper piping, a whole nest of it, green-skinned and beautiful. Brass fittings scattered through the silt like coins thrown at a wedding. And deeper in, half-buried, something rectangular and heavy that Rafael's light couldn't quite resolve — but that was for later, if later happened, and it usually didn't. You took what was easy and you left what wasn't. Greed was how divers became statistics.

Rafael worked for two hours. The lift bag went up three times, heavy with copper, and each time Rafael rode it to the surface, spilled the load into the skiff, and went back down without letting more than a minute pass on deck, because minutes on deck were where thinking happened, and thinking led to noticing things like *how quiet it is out here* and *when did I last have a conversation that lasted longer than a transaction.*

By midmorning the skiff sat low in the water, riding maybe four hundred pounds of metal that would fetch enough at Yorick's scrap yard to buy diesel, patches for the main boat's weeping seams, food that hadn't been dried or canned, and — if the scales ran honest, which they never did, but sometimes they ran only slightly dishonest — a week or two of not worrying. A week or two of not worrying was, as far as Rafael was concerned, the fundamental unit of human happiness. Everything else was decoration.

Rafael surfaced the last time, spat out the regulator, and was reaching up to haul off the mask when the sound reached across the water.

An engine. Big, disciplined, coming on fast.

* * *

The patrol boat came around the bend of the channel at a speed that suggested its pilot had been waiting eleven days for exactly this moment and intended to enjoy it.

It was gray, of course. Everything official was gray. It had a number stenciled on its bow that Rafael made a point of not reading, because reading numbers was the sort of thing that got remembered later, by people whose memories were professionally sharpened. Two sailors stood at the rail. One of them had a loudhailer, which he raised with the air of a man performing a role he'd rehearsed in the mirror.

"Vessel in the restricted zone. This is a closed site. You are to depart immediately."

Rafael stood up in the skiff, slow and unbothered, hands visible, the universal language of *I am not worth the paperwork.* "Didn't see any signs," Rafael called back, which was true, because Rafael had removed the last sign from its post six days ago, folded it up, and put it under the bunk, where it currently served as a very effective shim against a leak.

"This area is closed by order of the Regional Naval Authority."

"On account of what?"

A pause. The two sailors looked at each other, and Rafael watched them do it, watched the little silent conference pass between them, and understood with a familiar cold settling in the stomach that neither of them knew. They were following an order that had come down through four layers of command, and somewhere above them the order had been stripped of its reasons, the way a wreck gets stripped of its brass.

"That information isn't public," the loudhailer said finally.

"Right." Rafael nodded, agreeable, already coiling the dive line. "Well. I'm going, then."

"You're going *now*."

"I'm going now," Rafael agreed pleasantly, and fired up the skiff's engine, and made a show of being slow about it, because there was a certain dignity in slowness, and also because every second of theater was a second the sailors spent watching Rafael instead of the open cargo hold, where a tarp now covered the day's work under a convincing layer of fishing net and general disappointment.

The patrol boat shadowed them for two miles, close enough that its wake kept knocking the skiff's nose around, close enough that Rafael could see the younger sailor mouthing something to his companion and both of them almost smiling. Then the gray boat peeled away toward the channel markers and was gone, and the river closed behind it, brown and blank, keeping whatever it kept.

Rafael sat in the skiff for a while, engine idling, and let the anger arrive and then let it leave, the way you learn to when you work alone. Anger was expensive. Anger made you turn around and shout things at loudhailers, and shouting things at loudhailers got your name written down.

Because that was the thing about wrecks like the *Corvina Star*. Rafael had worked maybe sixty of them over the years, and they sorted cleanly into two kinds. There were tragedies — ships that had drowned honestly, through weather or wear or somebody's stupid mistake, and that lay open to the light like books face-down, everything readable. And then there were the others. Wrecks that sat in holes too deep for their reported draft. Wrecks whose hatches were dogged shut from the *inside*. Wrecks that the navy surveyed once, cordoned, and then forgot on a schedule so precise it might as well be printed in an almanac.

Nobody guards an empty room. That was the whole of it, really. The navy wasn't protecting the dead of the *Corvina Star*. It was protecting whatever the *Corvina Star* had been carrying, or proving, or covering for — and it had been doing so long enough that the guarding had become routine, and routine meant budget lines, and budget lines meant somebody, somewhere, had signed their name to forgetting.

Rafael turned the skiff upstream and didn't look back.

* * *

Yorick's scrap yard occupied a bend of the river two hours south, and Yorick himself occupied a chair outside it, a chair he had not visibly left in years, judging by the way the wood had grown around him like he was a tree that had decided on commerce late in life.

"Copper," Yorick said, when Rafael had unloaded the tarp and spread the day's take across his scale table. He picked up a length of pipe, turned it over, held it to the light with the gravity of a jeweler. "River copper."

"River copper," Rafael agreed.

"Salvaged lawful?"

"Salvaged lawful," Rafael said, which was technically true in the sense that Rafael had never been convicted of anything, a distinction Rafael guarded the way other people guarded passports.

Yorick weighed it all while making small disapproving sounds, which was his version of negotiation, and paid out a sum that was perhaps eighty percent fair, which in Rafael's experience was the ceiling of honesty anywhere on this river. Rafael took it in worn notes, bought diesel from the pump out back, and stood for a moment in the yard's rust-and-oil smell, doing arithmetic.

Patches. Caulk. Diesel. Food. It covered it, barely, with a thin margin left over — the kind of margin that looked like freedom and was actually just a different shape of trap. Enough to keep moving. Not enough to stop. Rafael had been living inside that margin for six years now, and had gotten so fluent in it that most days it didn't even feel like a choice anymore. It felt like a current. You didn't decide to drift; you just failed to anchor.

"You're heading upriver," Yorick said. It wasn't a question. Yorick tracked boats the way other men tracked weather.

"Thought I might."

"Monsoon's coming early this year." Yorick nodded at the sky, which was doing something pale and bruised-looking at its western edge, clouds stacking up with an ambition that Rafael didn't love. "Old-timers say the birds moved early. My knee says early. My knee's been right three seasons running."

"I'll keep ahead of it."

"Everybody keeps ahead of it," Yorick said, "until one year they don't." He settled back into his chair, conversation concluded, and Rafael walked back down to the water feeling the peculiar itch between the shoulder blades that Yorick had a talent for producing — the itch that said *someone noticed you today, someone will remember the direction you went.*

That was the rule, and Rafael recited it on the walk down like a prayer with the comfort taken out of it: *never sleep twice in the same town, never work the same stretch of river twice in a season, never let anyone learn your name well enough to miss you.*

It was a good rule. It had kept Rafael alive and free and unattached for six years, and if some mornings the boat felt less like a home than a shell you carried because you'd outgrown the last one — well. Shells floated. Homes sank. Rafael had seen plenty of both.

* * *

The main boat — the *Provision*, though Rafael mostly thought of her as *the boat*, because naming things was a commitment and commitments were against policy — sat at her mooring looking tired. She was thirty years old, wooden, sixty feet of stubbornness with a bilge pump that had developed opinions. Rafael spent the afternoon on the seams, driving in oakum, painting caulk over the worst of the weeping, and talking to her the way lonely people talk to boats, which is to say constantly and without dignity.

"Don't give me that look. You've been worse."

The boat creaked, unconvinced.

"We'll patch you proper next dry season. Real planking. Maybe even a nameplate, if you behave."

This was a lie they both understood. Next dry season was a country Rafael talked about visiting the way other people talked about retiring to the coast — someday, somewhere, when things were different. Things were never different. That was the arrangement.

While the caulk cured, Rafael sat in the cockpit with the chart spread out and a pencil, and did the thing that passed, these days, for planning. Upstream, past the fuel dock at Halfway Light, the river narrowed and the traffic thinned, and there was work to be found for a diver with a boat and no reputation to protect — a drowned ferry crossing that the ferry company had written off, a barge that had shed cargo in the spring floods, the usual leavings. Small jobs. Cash jobs. Jobs where nobody asked for papers because everybody working that stretch of river was equally paperless in spirit.

The pencil hovered over the chart. Above Halfway Light, the river bent north into country Rafael didn't know — hill country, older towns, names on the map that sounded like they'd been on the map since before the map was confident. Somewhere up there, according to a dockhand at Yorick's who talked too much, there was even a town with a harbor deep enough to winter in, if a person were the wintering type.

Rafael wasn't the wintering type. Rafael drew a line from the present position to the drowned ferry crossing, and then, after a moment, extended it — just lightly, just in pencil, easily erased — another inch further north, toward the unknown country, and told himself it was so the line would be ready if the ferry job fell through.

The pencil line sat there on the chart like a question Rafael declined to answer.

Evening came down the river the way it always did, gold going to copper going to that deep bruised violet, and the first real clouds of the season stacked themselves along the western hills, building thunderheads with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Rafael ate standing up, at the rail, watching the light go. Somewhere far off, thunder cleared its throat and thought better of it.

Six years of moving. Six years of one-week units of happiness, purchased with river copper, spent before they expired. And underneath it all, the thing Rafael never said out loud, not even to the boat: that the moving had stopped being escape a long time ago and become simply the shape of the life, the way a river's shape is just the memory of all the water that ever argued with it.

Rafael went below, checked the bilge out of habit, and slept.

In the morning, the *Provision* pointed her nose upriver, toward the drowned ferry crossing and the penciled inch beyond it, and the monsoon clouds followed at a respectful distance, like something that had already decided where Rafael was going to be, and was willing to wait.
