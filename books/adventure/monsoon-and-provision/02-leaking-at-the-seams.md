## 2. Leaking at the Seams

The bilge pump had a rhythm to it, and Rafael had learned to read that rhythm the way other people read weather. A healthy pump ran steady, indifferent, a mechanical heartbeat you could ignore for hours. This one had started stuttering somewhere past the second bend of the river, and by the time Rafael noticed — really noticed, not just filed it under *later* — the stutter had become a wheeze, and the wheeze meant water was coming in faster than the little machine could argue it back out.

Rafael knelt on the deck plates and pulled the inspection hatch over the forward bilge.

Water looked back up. Not an alarming amount, if you were the kind of person who alarmed easily, which Rafael had spent thirty-eight years training themselves not to be. But enough. Enough that the surface of it trembled when the engine turned over. Enough that it smelled like old wood and older rot, like a boat quietly confessing things its owner didn't want to hear.

"Okay," Rafael said aloud, because talking to the boat was cheaper than therapy and more honest than most conversations. "Okay. I hear you."

The boat's name was *Provision*, painted on the stern in letters that had faded from white to the color of weak tea. Rafael had bought her six years ago from a man in a river town whose name Rafael no longer remembered, for a price that had seemed fair and now seemed like the punchline to a joke everyone else got. She was forty feet of workhorse hull, half cabin, half open deck, built — according to the man who sold her — by someone's grandfather who "built boats to outlive their builders." The grandfather, wherever he was, was presumably disappointed.

Rafael shut off the engine and let her drift while they took stock. The current here was lazy, the banks wide and green and indifferent, dotted with fishing platforms on stilts and the occasional laundry line strung between trees like bunting for a holiday nobody was celebrating. Upstream, the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong — that deep yellow-green that meant the monsoon was thinking about arriving early, the way bad news always does.

The leak, when Rafael found it, wasn't one leak. It was a philosophy of leaks. The seam along the port side where the garboard met the keel had opened a crack the width of a fingernail, and around it, three planks showed the soft, dark give of wood that had been wet too long and dry too briefly, year after year, in the particular way of boats that are lived on but never loved properly. Rafael pressed a thumb into one plank and it dented, gently, like dough.

"You couldn't have done this downriver," Rafael told it. "You couldn't have leaked somewhere with a yard."

But there was a yard. That was the thing about rivers — they were strings of towns, and towns were strings of people who fixed things, and if you drifted long enough you'd find one. Rafael remembered passing a cluster of tin roofs and leaning cranes maybe four kilometers back, on the east bank: a shanty repair yard, the kind that smelled permanently of diesel and creosote and hung with the skeletons of small boats mid-surgery.

Rafael restarted the engine, kept the throttle low, and began the slow, humiliating process of motoring backward through their own wake toward help.

* * *

The yard was called — according to a hand-painted sign nailed to a post at a drunken angle — TULLOCH & SONS RIVERWORKS. There were no sons visible. There was, however, a woman in her sixties standing waist-deep in a half-sunk pirogue, wrestling with something beneath the waterline, and she looked up as Rafael brought the *Provision* alongside the nearest cradle with all the grace of a drunk docking a whale.

"You're taking on water," she said. Not a question. She said it the way a doctor says *you're bleeding*.

"Just a bit."

"Mm." She hauled herself out of the pirogue with an economy of motion that suggested she'd been doing it since before Rafael was born, wiped her hands on trousers that had given up being any color in particular, and walked the length of Rafael's boat without touching it, the way a horse trader reads an animal. Her eyes did the whole circuit twice. Then she crouched at the port garboard, pressed the same spot Rafael had pressed, and made a sound low in her throat that Rafael chose to interpret as professional rather than mournful.

"Tulloch?" Rafael offered.

"Sons moved to the city." She stood, knees cracking like green firewood. "Name's Amara Tulloch. You want the truth or the estimate first?"

"The truth."

"It's weeks early," she said, jerking her chin at the bruised sky upstream. "Monsoon. Should be a month out, six weeks if the river's feeling polite. It's coming now. My knee knew two days ago; my knee's never wrong." She looked at Rafael with the flat appraisal of someone who has watched a thousand drifters arrive and correctly predicted how many would still be there in a season. "And your boat is honest wood, which is worse than bad wood. Bad wood lies to you right up until it fails. Honest wood tells you everything and you haven't been listening."

Rafael felt that land somewhere below the ribs. "How bad?"

"Garboard seam's open, three planks soft above it, and your keel bolts—" she rapped the hull twice, listened, and grimaced, "—two of those are singing the wrong note. I can patch you sound for the season. I cannot make her new, and anyone who tells you different is selling you a boat."

"How much?"

Amara named a number. Rafael, who had learned to bargain before learning to swim, countered with a number roughly sixty percent of it, and they settled — after twenty minutes, two cups of terrible coffee, and Amara inspecting Rafael's dive gear with genuine interest ("Nystrom. You're the one who pulls scrap off the navy's leftovers? Small river.") — on a figure that would leave Rafael with almost nothing.

Which was fine. Which was the plan, such as it was. Dive jobs came upriver the way leaks did: regularly, and usually at the worst possible time. Rafael had a lead on one already — a drowned ferry crossing two days north, some cargo the insurance company wanted surveyed, salvage rights negotiable. Enough to refill the account. Enough to keep moving.

That was the arithmetic Rafael had run for years without ever writing it down: earn enough to fix just enough to travel just far enough to find the next job. Never a surplus. Surplus was heavy. Surplus was a reason to stay.

While Amara's crew — two nephews who materialized out of a shed like smoke — hauled the *Provision* onto the cradle, Rafael sat on a stack of creosoted pilings and watched the hull come up dripping into the light. It looked worse in daylight. Everything did. Along the keel, in the shade of the barnacles, the wood had gone the color of old paper, and in one place near the stern a previous owner's repair — a patch of newer timber, glassed over in a hurry — had lifted at the edges like a scab.

"There's a story in every layer of this boat," Amara said, appearing beside Rafael with a scraper and no ceremony. "This patch here, somebody did that in a panic. See the resin squeeze-out? Night job. Rain coming, probably. Somebody always fixing just enough to outrun the weather." She glanced sideways. "Sound like anybody you know?"

"I'm paying you to insult the boat, not the owner."

"I'm insulting the boat free of charge. The owner insults are included in the estimate." She set the scraper against the lifted patch and paused. "You going to be here when the monsoon hits proper?"

"No."

"Mm," she said again, in the tone of a woman filing information she'd already decided was true regardless of what came out of your mouth. "First storm of an early monsoon is a rude one. River rises fast, debris comes down, and every fool who waited too long to fix his boat comes floating past my yard wanting miracles." She worked the scraper under the patch's edge. "I'll have your seams caulked tight by tomorrow evening. After that, she'll hold — if you treat her kind."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the river will hold the conversation instead, and the river always wins." She peeled the patch away with a wet tearing sound and held it up: rotten through the center, black as burnt toast. "Look at that. Six years, maybe eight, somebody patched this and never came back to do it right. Just kept moving." She tossed it onto the scrap pile, where it landed with a soft, defeated slap. "Cost of never stopping, friend. It adds up. Wood keeps books better than banks do."

* * *

They finished at dusk the next day, and Amara was as good as her word — new caulking driven deep into the garboard seam, the soft planks sistered with fresh timber, the two keel bolts replaced with hardware she produced from a locked cabinet like a magician producing doves. The bill consumed nearly everything Rafael had, plus the last of the good coffee from the cabin stores, which Amara accepted with the gravity of a customs official.

Rafael stood on the deck that night, engine cold, watching lightning walk the horizon upstream in silent, patient strides. The air had weight now. The mosquitoes had opinions. Somewhere across the river a dog was barking at the thunder with more courage than sense.

Broke. Properly broke, for the first time in a couple of years. The account held enough for fuel and rice and not much else, and the ferry job upriver was the only number left in the ledger. Two days north. Drowned crossing, cargo survey, negotiable salvage. Rafael lay in the narrow bunk listening to the new caulking swell tight against the frames — a faint creaking, like the boat settling into a cleaner conscience — and tried to feel optimistic and mostly managed tired.

The radio crackled a little before midnight. Static, then a voice, clipped and bored, reading coordinates. Rafael sat up slowly.

It was the navy patrol channel, or close enough to it — the frequency drifters monitored the way animals monitor the wind. The voice gave a position upriver, near the old ferry crossing, and announced a temporary exclusion for "hydrographic operations." Duration unspecified. Rafael wrote the coordinates down anyway, because a lifetime habit of writing things down dies hard, and stared at them for a long moment.

Two days north. The same stretch of water.

Well. Exclusions were for people who cared about exclusions. And a navy that posted a notice at midnight was a navy that wanted the notice found by exactly nobody. Rafael had scraped a living for years off what official attention left behind — the wrecks too small to matter, the sites too remote to guard, the secrets too heavy to move. Where the navy drew a circle, there was generally something inside it worth the risk of stepping over the line.

Rafael killed the lamp and lay back down. Above, through the deck, the first fat drops of the early rain began ticking against the wood — one, then three, then a soft drumming, like fingers impatiently waiting for something to begin.

"One last job," Rafael told the dark. "One last job, and then we talk about actually fixing things."

The boat creaked. It might have been agreement. It might have been laughter.

At dawn, with the rain still holding its breath and the river running high and brown and fast, Rafael cast off from Tulloch & Sons and pointed the *Provision*'s bow north, into the rising weather, toward a wreck that the navy had decided, very suddenly, to care about.

Amara stood on the bank with her arms crossed, watching them go. She didn't wave. She just watched, the way you watch something you're fairly sure you'll see again — though not, her expression suggested, for the reasons anyone would hope.
