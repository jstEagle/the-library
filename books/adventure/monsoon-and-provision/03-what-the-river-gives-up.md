## 3. What the River Gives Up

The drowned ferry crossing sat two miles upriver from the shanty yard, and it had been there so long that the river had stopped treating it like a wreck and started treating it like a feature — a landmark, a hazard on the charts, a place where the current bent politely around a hull that had settled into the mud the way an old man settles into a chair he intends to die in.

Rafael had heard about it from the mechanic, of course. Everything upriver came through the mechanic eventually, along with gossip, engine parts of dubious provenance, and opinions nobody had asked for.

*Old *Vessany* packet boat,* he'd said, waving a wrench at the ceiling of his shed as if the wreck were hanging from it. *Sank before the war. Before your war, before my war, before anybody's war worth counting. They say she went down with all hands in the middle crossing, some bad night in the spring floods.* He'd spat into the oil bucket with the precision of long practice. *Nobody dives her. Bad luck. Also she's mostly silt now, which is worse than bad luck, because silt doesn't even have the decency to kill you quick.*

Rafael had nodded along, thanked him, paid for the patches, and thought: *silt means nobody's looked.*

That was the whole arithmetic of salvage diving, really. Every diver wanted a virgin wreck, and every diver knew that virgin wrecks were like honest men — rumored widely, encountered rarely. But a ferry crossing was promising in ways a deep-water wreck wasn't. Ferries carried people, and people carried things: money belts, jewelry, luggage, letters, the small portable wealth of ordinary lives. And a crossing was shallow. Forty feet, maybe forty-five at flood stage. You could work it without decompression stops, without mixed gas, without any of the expensive machinery that turned diving from a trade into a rich man's hobby.

So on the third morning after the repairs, with the sky the color of wet slate and the air already tasting faintly of iron — the mechanic had been right about the monsoon coming early; you could smell it gathering over the hills like a debt being called in — Rafael loaded the gear onto the patched and grumbling boat and puttered upriver to the crossing.

* * *

The first dive was reconnaissance, and reconnaissance meant mud.

The visibility down there was what the mechanic had promised, which was to say it wasn't. Rafael went down the anchor line hand over hand into water that went from green to brown to a brown so complete it felt less like seeing nothing than like being inside something — inside the river's own body, its slow cold digestion of everything it had ever taken. The light on the helmet cut a cone maybe three feet wide, and beyond the cone was a darkness that pressed back against the beam like something curious.

The hull rose out of the muck at thirty-eight feet, listing hard to port, and it was bigger than Rafael expected. Packet boats were working vessels, built fat and plain to carry cargo and livestock and families across a river that couldn't be trusted, and this one had been built by people who expected their work to outlast them. The planking was gone in places, rotted to lace, but the frames stood like ribs, and where the river hadn't gotten to the wood it had gotten to everything else, coating it in a gray fur of silt and weed that made the whole structure look soft, almost furred, like something asleep.

Rafael swam the length of the main deck twice, fanning silt off the obvious spots — the wheelhouse, the cargo hold, the passenger cabin aft — and found exactly what a diver finds on a wreck that's been worked or picked clean by decades of current: nothing worth the air. A boot sole. A lamp bracket fused to rust. The ghost outline of a trunk in the sediment, its contents long since dissolved into the general brown.

Disappointing, but not surprising. Rafael surfaced, ate half a tin of sardines standing up, argued briefly with the sky about whether it was going to rain yet (it was not going to rain yet, but it was clearly saving up), and went back down.

The second dive was when the current changed.

It did that on the crossings — Rafael would learn this the hard way later, but learned it now, halfway through the dive, when the gentle push against the left cheek became a shove against the whole body, and the cone of the light filled suddenly with drifting sediment like a snowstorm in reverse, flakes rising instead of falling. The river was turning over. It does that when the tributaries upcountry get their first rain and send a pulse of cold, heavy water down under the warm surface layer, and when it happens, everything the river has been quietly holding gets shaken loose.

Which is how, at forty-one feet, wedged in the gap between the ferry's collapsed cargo deck and the mud, Rafael's light caught metal.

Not rust-orange, not the gray-green of bronze, not the dull lead of a ballast pig. This was steel, and it was *clean* — or clean relative to everything around it, which in forty years of river meant one thing: it had been covered until very recently. The current-turnover had scoured the silt off it like a thumb wiped across a tabletop.

Rafael hung there in the brown water, breathing carefully, and looked at it.

A chest. Steel-banded, oak-cored by the look of the corners showing through the corrosion bloom, about the size of a sea chest, the kind sailors call a *small hope* because anything bigger is called a fortune. It was wedged nose-down in the wreckage at an angle that said it had fallen through the collapsing decks and been caught, pinned between a beam and the hull plating, held there by the wreck itself for four decades while the river forgot about it.

And stamped into the steel banding, repeated along every edge like a border pattern, was a seal.

Rafael knew seals. Salvage divers know seals the way pawnbrokers know hallmarks — not out of scholarship but out of commerce, because a stamp is the difference between scrap price and collector price. Most stamps meant nothing. Some meant shipping companies. One memorable time, a stamp had meant a diplomatic pouch, and that had been a week of phone calls and a very uncomfortable conversation with a man in a gray suit who had paid cash and asked no questions and made Rafael wish he'd kept the pouch.

This stamp meant more than any of those. This stamp meant a country.

Vessany. The double-headed river bird, wings spread over crossed oars, crowned with a wreath of reeds. Rafael had seen it twice before in a life spent around water: once on a mooring bollard in a port town that had since renamed itself, and once on a coin a beachcomber sold for the price of a meal. Both times it had been explained away the same way, with a shrug: *old Vessany, before the dissolution, before they broke it up and handed the pieces around.* A nation that had existed for centuries and then, in the space of a few bad years, had simply stopped — its borders redrawn, its ministries scattered, its records burned or boxed or lost, its name surviving mainly on old hardware and older grudges.

A dead nation's seal, on a chest, in a wreck everyone said was bad luck to dive.

Rafael should have noted the position, surfaced, thought about it. That was procedure. That was what a careful diver did, and Rafael liked to think of himself as a careful diver, the way a man likes to think of himself as a moderate drinker right up until the third glass.

Instead, Rafael reached for it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hauling a hundred and sixty pounds of silt-anchored steel off a wreck in near-zero visibility while a river current is actively trying to fold you into the wreckage: it goes wrong slowly, and then all at once.

The first problem was that the chest didn't want to come free. It was wedged, properly wedged, nose-down behind a deck beam with forty years of sediment packed above it like mortar. Rafael got fingers under the leading edge and pulled, and the chest shifted perhaps an inch, releasing a belch of trapped silt that erased the world entirely. For a full ten seconds Rafael hung in a darkness so total that the helmet light reflected straight back off the suspended sediment, a brown wall an inch from the faceplate, and breathed compressed air and listened to the regulator and the heartbeat arguing about tempo.

Then the chest came free all at once.

That was the second problem. Forty years of pinning pressure released in a single lurch, and the chest swung up and out and *around*, and its trailing corner caught the air line — the umbilical, the hose running back up to the surface supply, the single thread connecting Rafael to breathable atmosphere — and wrapped it. Not snagged. *Wrapped.* Once around the chest's lifting handle, cinched tight, dragging the line down into the wreckage with the weight of the chest itself.

There is a specific feeling that comes with watching your own air line go taut and downward, and it is not fear exactly. Fear is fast. This was slower and worse — a cold, clear, mathematical understanding arriving in the middle of the body: *the line is shortening, the chest is sinking back into the wedge, and I am attached to both.*

Rafael did the arithmetic fast. Cut the chest loose? No knife big enough for steel banding, and no time anyway. Drop the chest? That meant losing it back into the wedge, probably forever, and also the line was still wrapped around the handle regardless. Climb the line? The line led *down*, into the wreck, toward the chest.

What actually happened was uglier and simpler. Rafael hauled on the chest, hand over hand, using its own momentum against the wedge, walking it up and out of the gap inch by inch while the line drew tighter across the shoulder and the regulator began — very slightly, very politely, the way machines do everything — to breathe harder. Each breath cost a little more effort. The demand valve whined. Somewhere above, forty feet up through the brown, the surface float bobbed unknowing.

Three inches. Six. The chest's corner scraped free of the beam with a sound Rafael felt through the water more than heard, a groan of wood and steel, and then it was loose, and Rafael hugged it to the chest plate like a lover or a shield and kicked for open water, and the line unwrapped itself in one merciful spiral as the chest rotated free, and the tautness went out of the hose all at once like a held breath released.

Rafael surfaced beside the float, spat the regulator, and lay across the inner tube gasping in the gray morning light while the rain finally, anticlimactically, began — a thin, apologetic drizzle, as if the sky had only just remembered its appointment.

The chest sat in the bottom of the boat, streaming river water, wearing its seal like a uniform.

* * *

Getting it aboard had nearly killed Rafael. Opening it took considerably longer, because Rafael sat on the thwart for a good twenty minutes first, eating the other half of the sardine tin and having an argument with oneself that went roughly as follows:

*It could be nothing. It could be machine parts. Dead nations ship machine parts.*
*Dead nations don't seal machine parts with national crests on every band.*
*It could be documents, and documents are trouble. Documents are always trouble. Documents are how the man in the gray suit happened.*
*Yes. And?*

Rafael was many unflattering things — restless, broke, constitutionally incapable of staying anywhere longer than the boat needed patching — but not curious was not among them. Curiosity was the whole disease. It was why there was a warrant with Rafael's name on it somewhere downriver, though that story could wait, the way all the worst stories can.

The lock was old brass and surrendered to a flathead screwdriver with a sigh, the tumblers giving up their forty-year post without complaint. Inside, packed in wool that had somehow, impossibly, stayed dry — waxed canvas lining, someone had planned this — were papers. Dozens of them. Bundles tied with ribbon gone brittle as autumn grass. Envelopes sealed with the same reed-wreathed bird. And underneath everything, taking up the whole bottom of the chest like a foundation stone, a ledger.

Brass-bound. Heavy as a brick. Its cover stamped with the seal, and beneath the seal, embossed in letters that still held their sharpness after four decades underwater:

*REGISTRY OF HOLDINGS AND HEIRS — TO BE DELIVERED.*

Rafael turned it over twice, looking for the rest of the sentence. There wasn't one. Just that — *to be delivered* — which raised a question so large it made the chest feel suddenly much smaller, like a box that had been holding a door this whole time.

The bundles were letters. Rafael opened one, carefully, because paper that old disintegrates if you look at it wrong, and read a few lines in a spidery, formal hand:

*To Madame Ilsa Ferren, ferry keeper, Brasse-Lune crossing — if this reaches you, or reaches your children, or reaches anyone who remembers your name — know that what was taken will be returned. The registry lists what is yours. Hold fast. The river keeps what we give it, and gives it back when the time comes.*

Dated eleven days before the dissolution. Dated, Rafael realized with a slow chill, eleven days before this ferry sank.

The letters had never been delivered. The chest had never been opened. Someone had sealed a nation's promises into a steel box, hidden it in a ferry hull — hidden it *deliberately*, wedged it behind a beam, packed it in waxed canvas, planned for the river to keep it — and the river had kept it, and the people it was addressed to had spent forty years not knowing they were owed anything at all.

One of those names was Ferren. Ferry keeper. Brasse-Lune crossing.

Rafael set the letter down on the thwart, weighted it with the screwdriver against the drizzle, and stared at it for a long moment. Then the stare drifted, the way stares do, to the chest itself, and to the hull below, and to a question that had been forming since the moment the light first caught clean steel in the brown water.

Wrecks sink. That's what wrecks do — they're sunk by storms, by shoals, by fires, by the simple accumulated treachery of rivers. But Rafael had spent two dives inside this hull now, and the more the silt cleared, the less this looked like sinking. The seacocks were open. All of them. Every through-hull fitting in the engine space stood wide, their wheels spun fully back, which no crew does in an emergency unless the emergency is deliberate. And there, low on the starboard bow, was the scorch shadow — a fan of blackened wood beneath a gash in the planking that ran too straight, too clean, to be anything but placed charge.

The ferry hadn't sunk. The ferry had been *scuttled*. Sunk on purpose, with a chest of undelivered deeds sealed in her belly, by someone who wanted the records lost and the witnesses — the letter named a ferry keeper, and ferries carry passengers, and passengers talk — lost along with them.

Awe curdled. It curdled fast, the way milk curdles in a storm, all at once and irreversibly. Wonder at the find turned to dread in the space of one long breath, because Rafael had spent enough years around powerful men's secrets to know what they look like when they surface, and they look exactly like this: a steel box, a dead nation's crest, and a list of names that somebody, forty years ago, had considered worth a ship and everyone aboard.

Somebody had done this. Somebody had gotten away with it. And somebodies who get away with things for forty years tend to have descendants, and employees, and habits.

Rafael looked upriver, toward the wide gray bend where the water disappeared into rain, and downriver, toward the shanty yard and the fuel dock and the world where a boat with a patched hull and a stranger's name on the registration attracted exactly the wrong kind of attention.

The chest sat in the bilge, dripping, patient, smelling of river and old wool and other people's unfinished business.

Rafael had planned to sell it. That had been the plan, formed somewhere between the second dive and the screwdriver: haul it to a city, find a buyer, take the money, patch the boat again, move on. It was a good plan. It was the only plan Rafael had, really — the plan behind all the plans, the one that never changed.

But plans assume you know what you're selling. And Rafael sat in the drizzle with a dead nation's ledger in reach and a ferry keeper's name in hand, and understood, with the particular clarity that comes after nearly drowning, that this was not treasure.

This was evidence. And evidence has a way of making the water around it very crowded, very fast.

That night, anchored in the lee of the crossing with the drizzle thickening toward real rain, Rafael saw the lights — or rather, saw the absence of them. A fast boat came down the far channel running blacked-out, no nav lights, no lantern, just a shape moving against the last gray light and the low mutter of an engine that had been tuned by someone who cared about quiet. It slowed near the crossing. It lingered there, a dark shape on dark water, for the better part of an hour.

Then it turned and went back upriver, unhurried, the way men walk when they've checked a room and found it empty.

Rafael lay flat in the cockpit until long after it was gone, one hand resting on the brass-bound ledger, and began, quietly and without much hope, to calculate how far a patched hull could get before the monsoon closed the river.
