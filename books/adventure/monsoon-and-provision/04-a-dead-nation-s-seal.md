## 4. A Dead Nation's Seal

The chest sat on my deck like a guest who'd overstayed a funeral.

I'd hauled it up from the drowned ferry the evening before, and by morning it had dried into something almost respectable — black steel gone soft with rust at the corners, but the seal on its lid still crisp as the day some clerk pressed it: an eagle with its wings folded wrong, standing on a crown that wasn't quite a crown, and beneath it, in letters worn shallow but legible, *VESANY*.

I knew the name the way you know a word from a dream. Vessany had been a country once. Then it wasn't. Forty years ago, give or take, the maps got redrawn and the nation got folded into two of its neighbors like a shirt put away badly, and everyone agreed to stop mentioning where the seams were. My grandfather used to say Vessany didn't fall so much as get picked up and shaken until the pockets emptied.

Now I was sitting on one of the pockets.

I should have opened it on the riverbank, in daylight, with witnesses. That's what any sensible person would do. But sensible people don't dive scuttled ferries for scrap, and I'd learned long ago that when you find something the river has been keeping, you don't advertise. You open it alone, in the dark, and then you decide what kind of trouble you're in.

So I waited for full dark, anchored in the reeds off a nameless bend, ran my one working lamp down to a low amber glow, and set to work on the lock.

It fought me for an hour. Not because it was good — it was rusted half to lace — but because it was *stubborn*, which is a quality I respect even when it's inconvenient. I ended up talking to it. "Look," I said, "either you open or I drill you, and if I drill you I'll do it ugly." The lock, being dead, did not care. The drill went through ugly. Some negotiations are like that.

Inside, under a wool blanket that had somehow survived forty years underwater without rotting — waxed, treated, packed by someone who expected a long wait — there were three things.

First: documents. Dozens of them, sealed with the same folded-wing eagle, each envelope addressed in a hand so careful it looked printed. Names, towns, streets. Streets that no longer appeared on any map I owned.

Second: letters. A separate bundle, tied with cord, unsealed. I read the top one standing up, which was a mistake, because my knees decided independently that they were done holding me.

It was from a woman named Ilse Varga to her son, dated three weeks before the dissolution. *They are saying the banks close Friday. Your father says stay where you are and we will come to you, but your mother knows trains better than your father does. If we do not come, look for a man called Dorn. He owes us nothing and will help anyway, because that is the kind of man he is, and I want you to know such men exist.*

Underneath it, more letters. To sons, daughters, sisters, a cousin in a lighthouse. Letters about money hidden in floorboards. About names to trust. About a farm that would still be there, whoever flew the flag over it. None of them had ever been delivered. Every single one had been sealed into this box instead, by someone who believed — against all evidence, against everything that happened after — that someday they would reach their people.

Third: the ledger. Brass-bound, heavy as a sin, pages of vellum ruled in faded red. And this was the thing that made me sit down on my own deck planks like a man whose legs had been repossessed.

It wasn't a treasure list.

It was a *debt* list.

Names. Hundreds of names. And beside each name, holdings — houses, boats, orchards, fishing rights, a warehouse on a quay, a mill, a chapel roof — and beside the holdings, a column headed, in that same careful hand, *TO BE RESTORED*. Someone had gone through a collapsing country, street by street, family by family, and written down what had been taken from whom, with the calm certainty that the taking was temporary and the record permanent.

And the last entry — the last page, the last lines before the ledger simply stopped, mid-sentence almost, as though the writer had run out of country faster than ink —

The last entry wasn't a person. It was a place. A river town. It listed forty-one households. Fishermen, a ferry pilot's family, a tavern keeper, a schoolteacher, dockhands, a mechanic's father. *All of the above,* the entry said, *to be restored what was taken, when the water gives back what it holds.*

When the water gives back what it holds.

I read that line four times. The lamp guttered. Somewhere across the reeds a night bird made a sound like a rusty hinge complaining about wages. And I understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only when you're alone at midnight holding something you shouldn't have, that I hadn't found treasure.

I'd found a delivery.

Forty years late, but addressed. And I was, apparently, the courier.

* * *

Here is what I know about myself, and I know it the way you know a limp: I am very good at leaving. It's not a boast. It's a diagnosis. Give me ten minutes' warning and a working engine and I can be a rumor in three directions before anyone thinks to check the fourth. I've built a whole life out of not being anywhere long enough for anything to stick — debts, warrants, feelings, all of it slides off if you keep moving. My boat leaks because I never stay put long enough to fix her properly, and if that isn't a metaphor I'll eat my spare anchor.

So when the instinct came, it came on schedule.

It arrived around two in the morning, right on time, wearing its usual outfit: a tight feeling behind the sternum and a voice that sounded suspiciously like my own but smarter, saying *Rafael, old boy, whatever's in that box belongs to dead people and lawyers, neither of whom can hurt you if you're in another country. Sell it downriver. Cash. Disappear. You've done it before; you're practically professional.*

And honestly? For about an hour, I listened. I got the chest moved to the bilge space under the deck plates. I topped off the fuel. I checked the weather, which was turning — the air had gone thick and sweet the way it does before real rain, the monsoon gathering itself somewhere north like a debt collector with a map. I even laid out the route in my head: downriver, past the fuel docks, past the patrol lanes, out where the chart goes vague and so do I.

Then, around three, the fast boat came.

No lights. No engine noise until it was already abreast of me — and even then it was wrong, muffled, throttled down to a murmur, the sound of someone trying to be a shadow on the water. It swept past my anchorage maybe two hundred meters out, slow, deliberate, and I watched it through the reeds doing the thing searchers do: running a line, cutting hard, running another. Sweeping the bend. Sweeping the channel. Looking for something.

Looking for a chest.

I lay flat on my deck among the ropes and didn't move, breathing through my mouth, feeling every heartbeat individually like coins dropping one at a time. The boat made three passes. On the third, somebody aboard swept a hand-beam low across the water — not a proper searchlight, just enough to catch hull shapes in the shallows — and the beam crossed the reeds six meters from my bow and kept going, because my boat sits low and gray and honest-looking, like a workboat that has never done anything interesting in its life.

Which, until Tuesday, was true.

They gave up around four. The murmur faded downriver toward the fuel docks, and I stayed flat another hour anyway, because paranoia is free and sleep is expensive.

But here's the thing I figured out while I was lying there counting my own ribs: they were searching *here*. This bend. This stretch. Which meant they knew roughly where the ferry went down, and they knew recently — nobody mobilizes a night sweep over a wreck that's been sitting quiet for forty years. Somebody had been diving that crossing before me. Somebody had been looking, and failed, and now they were looking harder, and they had boats and radios and probably salaries, and I had a leaking hull, a drilled lock, and a brass book full of other people's stolen property.

Running was still an option. Running was always an option. It was practically my native language.

But I did the arithmetic, lying in the dark, and the arithmetic was ugly. My patched hull wouldn't survive open current in the weather that was coming — the mechanic had told me as much, with the cheerful fatalism of a man who charges by the patch. The monsoon was weeks early, he'd said, and I'd laughed, and now the sky was assembling itself overhead like a jury. Even in fair water, a fast boat with no lights doesn't need to be lucky twice. And I couldn't outrun them forever carrying forty pounds of brass that said, in effect, *this man knows where the bodies and the deeds are buried.*

Besides — and this was the part I didn't want to look at directly, so of course I looked at it directly — the last entry had named a town. A river town. Households. A ferry pilot's family. A tavern keeper. A schoolteacher.

I didn't know which town. The ledger used old names, Vessany names, names that had been sanded off the maps. But rivers keep their own records, and towns on rivers tend to stay where you left them, even if the sign changes.

Somewhere upriver or down — and I'd figure out which, because I was apparently a courier now whether I'd applied for the job or not — there were people whose grandmothers had written letters that were sitting in a steel box on my deck. Letters beginning *if we do not come.*

Nobody had come. For forty years, nobody had come. And now the only thing standing between those letters and another forty years of silence was me, and my boat, and my truly world-class talent for leaving.

The rain started just before dawn. Soft at first, testing, then committed. I stood at the rail with the chest's blanket around my shoulders — it smelled of wax and river and, faintly, impossibly, of someone's cedar-lined closet — and I watched the water go silver-gray and rough, and I made the decision the way I make all my decisions: badly, at the last moment, and for reasons I'd only understand later.

Not downriver. Not away.

Upriver. Toward the storm. Toward whatever town the ledger had been waiting on.

"Don't look at me like that," I told the boat, because the boat was taking on water again and giving me the damp, reproachful silence of a partner who warned me about exactly this. "We're not running. We're delivering. There's a difference."

The boat did not answer. Boats never do. But she took the first mile of upstream current without complaint, which from her counted as enthusiasm.

Behind me, far downriver, the fuel dock lights came on one by one in the rain. And moored among them, dark and patient as a held breath, sat a fast boat with no lights, waiting for daylight and orders.

I knew then that hours was all I'd bought myself, maybe less. Whatever came next would come polite, well-dressed, and thorough, and it would ask me questions in a voice trained to sound reasonable.

I put the kettle on. In my experience, you might as well meet trouble caffeinated.
