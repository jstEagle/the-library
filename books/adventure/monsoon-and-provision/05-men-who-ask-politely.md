## 5. Men Who Ask Politely

The fuel dock at Sable Reach was the kind of place that existed because the river demanded it and nobody loved it. A corrugated roof on rusted posts, a pump that took payment in cash or patience, a dog asleep in a wheelbarrow who had clearly decided long ago that guarding things was beneath him. Rafael had fueled there twice before, years apart, and both times the same man had run the till — a wide, slow-moving fellow named Anselm who greeted every customer with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own obituary.

Rafael tied off, checked the bilge — still dry, thanks to the patches and to two days of mercifully flat water — and went up the ramp to pay. The chest sat under the tarp in the forward hold, wrapped now in an old sailcloth that had once covered a dinghy, weighted down with chain and spare anchor line. Anyone looking would see salvage junk. That was the idea.

Anselm rang up forty liters without looking up from his crossword. "You're the third boat today," he said, filling in a square with a pencil worn to a stub. "Busy river."

"Everyone's running ahead of the weather."

"Mm." He filled in another square. "Weather's coming sideways this year, they say. Early." He glanced up for exactly as long as it took to make eye contact, then back down. "You want ice? I got ice. Nobody ever wants ice."

"I'll take ice."

That seemed to improve his whole day. He hauled a block out of the cooler, chipped it, bagged it, and charged Rafael for the bag too, which felt honest somehow. Rafael was carrying the ice back down the ramp when the black sedan pulled in.

It was a river town; nobody drove sedans to fuel docks. The car was dark gray, spotless despite the road dust, and it parked with the precision of a man who parallel-parked for a living. Two men got out. The first was tall and narrow, wearing a jacket too clean for the humidity, and he moved like a heron — all economy, no wasted steps. The second was shorter, broader, pleasant-faced in the way of bank managers and undertakers, and he carried a leather folder under one arm like it was a hymnal.

They didn't hurry. That was the first thing Rafael noticed, and the thing that mattered most. Men in a hurry were dangerous in the ordinary way — tempers, mistakes, witnesses. Men who weren't in a hurry had already decided how this was going to go.

* * *

The tall one stopped at the top of the ramp and looked at Rafael's boat the way an appraiser looks at a horse. The short one came down the ramp itself, unhurried, smiling.

"Afternoon," he said. "Mr. Nystrom, is it?"

"It's not."

"No?" The smile widened by a millimeter. "Forgive me. The harbor master at Vell said a diver named Nystrom came through Tuesday, paid cash, asked about dive sites north of the old ferry crossing. And the fuel ledger here has a purchase signed *R. Nystrom*, unless you've been forging other people's names at pumps, which would be its own interesting conversation." He opened the folder anyway, ceremonially, as though the name inside were a formality. "My name is Perrin. This is my colleague, Mr. Vasch. We're with Corvane Shipping. Regional assets office."

Corvane. The name landed in Rafael's stomach like a dropped anchor. Everyone on this stretch of river knew Corvane — the barges, the depot lights visible for miles, the way small docks leased their moorings from Corvane subsidiaries without quite understanding how that had happened. A company that big didn't send two men in a clean sedan over scrap metal.

"You're divers," Perrin continued, pleasantly. "Or you were. Contract work, mostly. Wreck assessment, recovery. That sort of thing. So we wanted to ask you about recovered artifacts."

"Artifacts."

"Items of historical interest. Ceramics, fittings, documents. Anything salvaged from wrecks along this reach, particularly anything older than, say, fifty years." He said it lightly, the way a man asks if you've seen a lost dog. "There's been some unauthorized salvage activity in the region. Nothing personal. We're simply trying to account for items that may have... surfaced."

Rafael kept wiping down the ice bag. Slow hands. Bored hands. "I haul propellers and copper pipe," Rafael said. "Last artifact I pulled up was a beer bottle from 1987. Drank the deposit money."

Vasch — the heron — had drifted down the ramp by then and was standing at the gunwale, looking into the boat without looking, the way you check a room for exits. His eyes went over the winch, the tanks, the patched seams. Then the tarp in the forward hold.

"That's a lot of chain for a boat this size," Vasch said. First words out of him, and they were about weight.

"River bottom's full of chain. Cheaper to keep it than untangle it from your own gear twice."

"Mm." He crouched, examined the patching on the port seam — good work, recent, done in a yard with proper clamps — and straightened up. "New planking soon," he observed. "She's tired."

"She's held longer than most employers have."

Perrin laughed, a small genuine-sounding laugh, and Rafael understood immediately that everything genuine about these men was going to be used as a tool. That was the whole trick of them. They weren't thugs. Thugs would have flipped the tarp by now, or made an offer with a gun in it. These two were doing something worse: building a record. Every question was being filed somewhere behind those pleasant faces, and the file was going to be complete before they left.

"So," Perrin said, settling against the rail post as though he might stay a while. "The ferry crossing. North reach. You dove it?"

"I heard about it."

"Heard what, exactly?"

"That there's a hull down there and a hard bottom. Bad visibility, bad footing. I dive for money, not for fun."

"But you were asking about it. In Vell. Asking where exactly, how deep, what the approach was like." Perrin turned a page in his folder with one finger, unhurried. "A man doesn't research a site he doesn't intend to dive."

"A man researches lots of sites. Diving them's a different budget."

Vasch had moved along the gunwale while Perrin talked. It was choreography — one man held your attention, the other closed distance. Rafael had watched fishermen work a school the same way. Now Vasch stood directly abeam of the forward hold, hands clasped behind his back, and looked at the tarp with the flat patience of a cat outside a mouse hole.

"What's under the sailcloth?" he asked.

"Spare canvas, a fouled anchor, and my lunch."

"May we?"

"No."

Rafael said it mildly, and that was important. Refusal was a right; panic was a confession. "Private vessel. You're welcome to look at anything floating on the outside of it."

Perrin smiled again, wider this time, and Rafael felt the smile like a hand on the back of the neck. Because here was the arithmetic, laid bare: these men could not legally board. But they could note the refusal. They could come back with a customs officer, a harbormaster, a magistrate's clerk — any of whom Corvane could produce the way other companies produced stationery. Or they could stop needing paperwork entirely, out past the channel markers, where boats developed problems and divers stopped surfacing.

"You're within your rights," Perrin agreed warmly. "Absolutely. Though I'll be honest with you, Mr. Nystrom — may I call you Mr. Nystrom? — honesty saves everyone time. Corvane isn't interested in your boat, your gear, or your career. We're interested in one item. Steel chest, roughly so—" he sketched a rectangle in the air with both hands, and the dimensions were exact, exact enough to hurt, "—bearing a particular seal. If it had come up in your nets, so to speak, returning it would be a simple matter. There'd even be compensation. Finders' consideration. Companies do hate ingratitude."

"How much consideration?"

"Oh, I couldn't quote figures without authority." Another page turned. "But I can tell you the range of outcomes. Item returned voluntarily: compensation, goodwill, a letter of reference, doors open. Item found later through less voluntary means: no compensation, considerable inconvenience, and a relationship with our regional office that tends to follow a man around. You've worked the river a while. You know how long the river's memory is, and how much longer ours."

Rafael looked at the ice melting in the bag. Looked at the dog in the wheelbarrow, who had lifted his head at last, sensing — dogs always sensed — that something in the air had gone wrong. Looked at Vasch's hands, folded and empty and somehow the most threatening thing on the dock.

And did the math.

Here was what Rafael knew that they didn't: they knew about the chest. Not suspected — knew. Knew the size, knew the seal, knew a diver had been asking about the crossing. Which meant someone had talked, or someone had watched, or Corvane had been waiting over that wreck for forty years like a spider that inherited the web. Either way, the chest wasn't hidden anymore. It was marked. Rafael was carrying something these men had already priced, and the only question left was whether the price got paid in money or in quieter currency.

And here was what they knew that Rafael didn't: everything else. Who else had been asked. What the next dock's ledger said. Whether there was a third man at the far end of the reach with binoculars and a radio. Whether the fuel pump's owner sold more than ice.

So Rafael lied. Carefully. The way you lie to a current — not against it, but across it, giving it just enough angle to push you somewhere it wasn't looking.

"The chest you're describing," Rafael said slowly, "came up three weeks ago. Off a barge wreck near Torlan, not the ferry crossing. I didn't know what it was. No seal I recognized — the plate was furred over, and I didn't scrub it, because a plate that old is worth more dirty to collectors and I sell to collectors." A pause, measured. "I sold it."

Perrin's eyebrows rose a polite fraction. "To whom?"

"A broker in Torlan. Runs a shop off the fish market, deals in marine brass and oddments. Cash, no paperwork, which suited us both." Rafael shrugged, a shrug calibrated to the exact degree of a man slightly embarrassed about cutting a corner. "I don't even remember his face. I was paid, I moved on. That's the trade."

"And the seal. You never cleaned it?"

"Never occurred to me it mattered."

Vasch shifted his weight, and Rafael watched him do it — watched the heron decide whether the fish was worth striking for. Because the story was good. It was good because it was mostly true: Rafael *did* sell things dirty, *did* deal in cash, *did* move on. The best lies are load-bearing walls built into a real house. But it had a flaw and Rafael knew exactly where it was: a broker in Torlan could be found, and a broker who'd bought a sealed steel chest would remember it forever, because brokers remember inventory the way priests remember sins.

Which meant the lie bought hours, not days. Maybe a day and a half, if the roads were wet. Perrin would drive to Torlan tonight. The broker would say he'd bought no such chest, or worse, would say nothing at all while someone else asked the questions, and then the arithmetic would change completely, and the next conversation wouldn't happen at a fuel dock with a dog in a wheelbarrow.

Rafael understood all of this while saying none of it. Understood something else too, colder: these men hadn't come to retrieve. Retrieval implied the thing still mattered, that it would go back into a drawer somewhere. These men had come to establish provenance of a different kind — to find out who'd touched it, who knew, how many copies of the knowledge existed. You don't erase a chest. You erase the people who can say it existed.

"Thank you, Mr. Nystrom." Perrin closed the folder. "Genuinely. You've been more helpful than most." He produced a card — heavy stock, embossed, absurd in this place — and held it out. "If you recall anything further. Details surface, sometimes days later. A name, a shopfront, a date. Men remember in their sleep what they forget awake."

Rafael took the card, because refusing a card was a statement and taking one was nothing. "And if I remember where the broker keeps his better stock?"

"Then you'd be a well-remembered man." Perrin smiled the widest smile yet, and it reached his eyes, which was somehow worse. "Enjoy the weather, Mr. Nystrom. It's coming early this year, they say."

He said it the way you'd say *enjoy the rest of your life*. Then both men walked up the ramp, unhurried, and the sedan pulled away with the same terrible precision, and the dog put his head back down, and Anselm filled in another crossword square and said, "Ice help any?"

* * *

Rafael stood on the deck for a long time after the car was gone, holding a card that cost more than the rope on the cleat.

The river ran brown and slow past the pilings. Somewhere upstream, rain was already falling on water that would arrive here by tomorrow, patient as everything else on this river. The forecast Anselm had mentioned — monsoon weeks early — wasn't gossip anymore. The light had gone the color of old pewter, and the wind had swung around, and the birds had gone quiet in the mangroves, which birds only did for two reasons: a hunter, or a storm. Sometimes both wore the same sky.

Rafael went below and looked at the chest.

Sailcloth off. Just for a minute. Just to look at the seal again, the crowned antler pressed into the steel, the thing that had seemed like a curiosity in the hold's lamplight and now seemed like a lit fuse. Forty years dissolved, and the nation it belonged to was dust, and the company that had helped make it dust had just sent two polite men to ask after it by its exact dimensions.

Rafael thought about running. Of course Rafael thought about running. Running was the oldest skill, older than diving, older than lying — point the bow downriver, burn the fuel, sleep in coves, be a rumor by Thursday. The boat could do it. Barely. The patched seams would hold in flat water and complain in chop, and the monsoon would test every one of them, and there was a nonzero chance the river simply took the boat, the chest, and the whole problem into its brown silence, which — Rafael admitted it — had a certain appeal. Let the river keep its secrets. It always had before.

But the sedan had driven toward Torlan, and Torlan was downriver.

They'd be waiting at the broker's by nightfall. And brokers talk, and ledgers talk, and by morning the description of the lie would be in a file next to the description of the boat, and every dock from here to the delta would have two polite men on it asking the same questions with the same folder. Running downriver meant running *through* them. Running upriver meant deeper into the reach where the chest had been buried in the first place, where whatever Corvane feared lived in whatever towns the drowned ferry used to serve.

Towns full of people whose grandparents had names Rafael had read by lamplight. Names in a brass ledger. Heirs.

Rafael stood in the cabin with the card in one hand and the ice melting through the bag onto the deck plates, and felt the fear finish its work. Fear was a tool like any other. Used badly, it made you freeze or bolt. Used well, it told you exactly what you had and exactly what it was worth to the people who wanted it — and Corvane had sent a sedan, a folder, and two professionals across half a region for a box of dead men's paper. Whatever was in that ledger, it was worth more than copper, more than brass, more than Rafael's boat and probably more than Rafael. Worth enough that they'd offered compensation with a smile, which meant they expected the smile to be enough. Which meant they thought everyone on this river could be bought, and cheaply, and had rarely been wrong.

The engine started on the second try. Rafael cast off, waved a coin at Anselm — "for the crossword pencil" — and pointed the bow upriver, into the pewter light, into the coming rain.

Hours. Maybe a day and a half. Enough to get clear of the reach, not enough to get clear of the problem. Somewhere ahead there had to be a dock deep enough to hide in, a storm thick enough to blur a wake, people with reasons of their own to dislike Corvane Shipping. Rafael didn't know their names yet.

But the ledger did.

Behind, far downriver, the first lightning stood up over the water like a mast, and the rain came walking across the reach toward the boat, and Rafael ran before it — not away this time, though it felt the same in the hands — toward the one place on the whole river the chest had ever been headed in the first place.
