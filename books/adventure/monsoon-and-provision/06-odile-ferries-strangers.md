## 6. Odile Ferries Strangers

Odile Marchand had a rule about weather, and the rule was this: when the gulls went quiet and sat down on the water like they'd given up on flying entirely, you got your boat under cover and you did not argue about it. The gulls had gone quiet an hour ago. The sky over the eastern hills had turned the color of an old bruise, greenish at the edges, and the air had that thick, sweet, pre-storm weight to it, like the whole river was holding its breath.

So she was running the *Petite Véronique* home early, ferry empty for once, no schoolkids, no market women with baskets of cassava balanced impossibly on their knees, nobody but her and the engine and the long brown water sliding past. Which was why she saw them.

A boat, up ahead, listing hard to port. Not anchored. Not really moving, either — just drifting with the current in the lazy, defeated way of a thing that has stopped trying. Smoke from a galley pipe, thin and stubborn. A bilge pump hose draped over the side, spitting water in fits and starts, like the boat itself was coughing.

Odile slowed and studied it through the spray-smeared windshield. River people came in two kinds: the ones who waved when you passed, and the ones who didn't want to be seen. This one was the second kind. The figure at the rail — she couldn't tell if it was a man or a woman, and honestly it didn't matter — hunched down when the *Véronique* approached, which was not the behavior of someone who wanted help. It was the behavior of someone who'd done the math on help and found the price too high.

But the boat was going down. That much was obvious even from fifty meters. The freeboard on the port side was gone; the waterline licked at the rub rail. And the storm behind the hills was not going to negotiate.

"Right," Odile said out loud, to nobody, as she often did. "Well. Can't have you drowning in my river."

She brought the ferry alongside slow, keeping her distance at first, and called across the gap. "Ahoy! You're taking water!"

The figure looked up. Sharp face, sunburned, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of tarpaulin. Eyes that did a quick, professional inventory of her — the ferry, the wheelhouse, herself — before answering anything. Odile knew that look. It was the look of someone who counted exits before counting friends.

"I'm aware," the stranger said. Voice like gravel in a tin cup. "Pump's losing ground."

"Name's Marchand. I run the Brasse-Lune ferry." She jerked a thumb at the bruised sky. "That," she said, "is coming here tonight, and it is not in a good mood. You can drift and bail all night in the open channel, or you can take a line and let me tow you in. Your call. But make it fast, because I'm not standing off a sinking boat in lightning weather out of politeness."

The stranger hesitated. Odile could practically hear the gears — the weighing of pride against survival, the suspicion that a towline was also a leash. She'd seen that hesitation before, in smugglers and debt-runners and one memorable tax inspector who'd rather have drowned than accept assistance from a woman he considered beneath his station. He had, in fact, nearly drowned. Nature is fair that way.

"Fine," the stranger said finally. "One line. Short tow. I can steer my own boat."

"You can steer a sinking boat," Odile agreed. "Delightful. Throw me your bow line."

It took three tries. The first throw landed short and sank; the second the wind took; the third the stranger finally put some shoulder into, and Odile hauled it aboard and made it off the forward bollard. The *Véronique* leaned into the pull, and the little boat — she read the name off the transom as it swung in line, *Wandering Ossian*, painted and repainted so many times the letters were more scar tissue than paint — groaned and straightened and began, grudgingly, to follow.

The stranger steered from the stern, both hands on the wheel, bailing whenever the pump fell behind, which was often. Every few minutes Odile glanced back. Every few minutes the stranger glanced at her. Neither of them said much. There was a whole conversation happening anyway, conducted in wake-angle and throttle, and the subject of the conversation was trust, and neither party was giving an inch.

* * *

Brasse-Lune harbor sits in the elbow of the river where the current slows and drops its silt, a crescent of gray water ringed by stilt houses and drying nets and the long low shed of the co-op. In fair weather it smells of fish and diesel and someone's frying plantains. Today it smelled of rain coming, which is a smell like iron and wet stone, and everyone in town was doing the same thing at once: battening, tying, hauling, cursing.

Odile brought the *Wandering Ossian* alongside the old fuel dock at the harbor mouth — not the main pier, she noticed, watching her passenger's face. The stranger had asked, quietly, for somewhere "not too busy," and Odile had obliged without being asked twice. People who ask for quiet docks are people with reasons.

"Fuel dock's fine," she said. "Nobody bothers it much since Corvane put their prices up. Tie off anywhere that holds."

The stranger tied off with the quick, economical knots of someone who'd done ten thousand of them, then stood at the rail a moment, looking at the town. At the shuttered co-op shed. At the church spire. At the children already being herded indoors by grandmothers with the reflexes of sheepdogs.

Then the stranger turned to her, and something crossed between them — not quite gratitude, because gratitude would have required admitting to needing any, and this person seemed constitutionally opposed to that. Something more like acknowledgment. Two professionals recognizing each other across a gap of water.

"What do I owe you?" the stranger said.

"For a tow?" Odile shrugged. "Buy me a drink sometime and tell me if the fishing's better upriver. We'll call it even."

The stranger's mouth twitched. Almost a smile. "Rafael," she said. "Rafael Nystrom."

"Nystrom." Odile tried it out. "You're not from anywhere around here."

"No."

"That wasn't a question, actually, but thank you for confirming it." She leaned on the rail, studying the boat while Rafael studied her right back. The hull was a patchwork — new planking over old, caulking squeezed in like mortar between bricks, a bilge pump that had clearly been bought used and loved by nobody. "How long have you been living on that?"

"Six years."

"And how long has it been leaking?"

"Six years."

Odile laughed, short and genuine. "You know, there's a mechanic up the street, Baptiste, who'd take one look at that hull and start weeping. Or praying. Hard to say with him."

"Maybe I'll keep my hull away from Baptiste."

"Mm." Odile tilted her head. "See, most people who say that are hiding something. But you don't strike me as a criminal. Criminals compliment my boat."

That earned her a real look — startled, wary, and underneath it, briefly, amused. "Your boat is fine."

"My boat is magnificent, thank you. My father built her, and my grandfather built his, and the family policy is aggressive flattery toward all vessels." She pushed off the rail. "Storm hits in an hour, maybe two. First real one of the season, early — everyone's saying so. If your pump dies in the night, hail the *Véronique*. Second house past the church, blue shutters, the one with the anchor rusted into the shape of modern art. I sleep light."

Rafael nodded, noncommittal, and Odile went back to her ferry, and that should have been the end of it. A tow, a name, a polite goodbye. Except.

Except that as she cast off, the wind shifted for a moment, and the tarp over the *Ossian*'s deck gear lifted and snapped flat again, and underneath it — wedged upright against the wheelhouse, lashed down with the same desperate competence as everything else on that boat — was a steel chest the size of a sea chest, dark with river silt, and stamped into its lid, plain as a brand on a cow:

The seal of Vessany.

Odile stopped rowing. Stopped breathing, almost.

Every child in Brasse-Lune grew up on the stories. How Vessany had been a real country once, with its own money and its own flag and its own stubborn little navy, until the big neighbors carved it up forty years ago and wrote it out of the atlases. How the soldiers came, and the clerks came after the soldiers, and the clerks burned the registries, and the men who bought the burned land cheap became rich, and the rich men's companies became empires, and one of those empires was named Corvane, and Corvane owned half the river now, including, as of last year, the ferry concession Odile's family had held for three generations.

And how, in the last days before the end, somebody — nobody knew who, everybody claimed a cousin who knew — loaded records onto boats. Deeds. Ledgers. Names. Proof of who had owned what, before. Smuggled downriver in the night. Some of those boats were caught. Some were burned. And some, the story always ended the same way, *some are still down there.*

Her grandmother, Élodie Marchand, had told those stories with her hands as much as her voice — chopping vegetables, slapping dough, pointing at the river. *Your great-great-aunt Sylvie ferried records the night the border closed,* she'd say. *They never found her boat. They never found her either.* And she'd go quiet in a way that meant the silence was part of the story.

And now here was a stranger, towed in off the river by accident, sitting on a steel box with the dead nation's seal stamped into it like a promise kept forty years late.

Odile looked at Rafael. Rafael, she noticed, was looking at her looking at the chest, and had gone very still, in the way of a person recalculating everything.

"That's a nice chest," Odile said carefully. "Old."

"It's salvage," Rafael said, equally carefully.

"Mm. Salvage." She let that sit. "You dive wrecks, do you?"

"When the work's there."

"Find many chests?"

"Enough."

"With seals on them?"

Rafael's jaw tightened slightly. "Mostly scrap," she said. "Copper, brass. This one was... heavier than expected."

Odile had grown up reading faces across a fish market; it was a survival skill in a town where everyone owed everyone money and half the prices were lies. And Rafael Nystrom's face was telling her, clearly, wordlessly: *I know what I have. I know it's dangerous. I am deciding, right now, whether you're a threat.*

Fair enough. Because Odile was having exactly the same thought in reverse.

"Well," she said lightly, "heavy salvage. Hope it was worth the dive." And she cast off the *Véronique*, and backed her ferry out into the rising wind, and watched the stranger and the chest and the listing boat shrink behind her.

* * *

The storm arrived at dusk, the way the early ones do — no long theatrical buildup, just a wall of rain walking across the water, and thunder that rattled the stilt houses like a landlord demanding entry. Odile sat in her kitchen with the shutters buttoned, tea going cold, listening to the river rise, and found she could not stop thinking about the fuel dock.

About the stranger who didn't wave. Who asked for a quiet berth. Whose boat was dying of six years of neglect, which meant six years of never stopping, which meant running from something. About the chest with the seal, and her grandmother's hands shaping the words *some are still down there*, and the specific, unreasonable conviction growing in her chest that this — this exact stranger, this exact week, this exact storm — was not an accident. Rivers didn't deal accidents. Rivers dealt consequences, on their own schedule, to whoever happened to be holding the rope.

By nine the wind had settled into its long steady roar and the rain had become a single continuous thing, and Odile gave up on tea and pulled on her oilskins, because she was her father's daughter and her father's rule had been: *when you can't sleep for worrying about a boat, go stand next to the boat.* The walk to the fuel dock was four minutes in fair weather and eleven in this, bent double against the rain, and she arrived soaked to the skin and mildly triumphant about it.

The *Wandering Ossian* was still afloat. Barely. The pump was running — she could hear it, a faint asthmatic whir under the storm — and there was a light in the wheelhouse, and Rafael was visible through the glass, sitting at the chart table, doing something meticulous with papers by lamplight.

Odile knocked on the wheelhouse door the way you knock on a stranger's door in a storm: firmly, apologetically, already apologizing for existing.

Rafael opened it halfway. Then, seeing who it was, all the way, which Odile chose to find encouraging.

"Thought you might've sunk," Odile said.

"Thought about it," Rafael said. "Decided against."

"Strong choice. Mind if I come in? I promise I'm only ninety percent rainwater."

There was a pause — the pause of a person deciding whether to let the world in — and then Rafael stepped back, and Odile climbed into the wheelhouse, and immediately understood a great deal. The space was clean. Ruthlessly, defensively clean, the way a home gets when its owner has spent six years ready to abandon it in ten minutes' notice. No photographs. No letters. A shelf of charts, rolled tight. A kettle, a dented pot, one mug. One mug. Six years on a boat and one mug, and Odile felt something twist in her chest that was part pity and part recognition, because she knew what a single mug meant. It meant a person who had decided, at some point, that hoping for company hurt more than planning for solitude.

"Coffee?" Rafael said. "It's terrible."

"Terrible coffee is the finest kind in a storm. Yes please."

Rafael made it without ceremony, and handed over the one mug, and drank from the pot directly, which answered several questions Odile hadn't asked yet.

"So," Odile said, settling onto the bench. "Rafael Nystrom. Salvage diver. Lives alone on a boat with one mug. Towed into my town on the eve of the monsoon by pure coincidence."

"Something like that."

"And carrying—" she gestured vaguely at the deck below, "—salvage."

Rafael's eyes came up sharp. "You checked."

"I *saw*. There's a difference. Checking implies I opened something, and I didn't, because whatever's under that tarp is yours until you decide otherwise." She wrapped both hands around the mug. "But I'll tell you what I told you at the dock, only slower, since we've got all night and nothing else to do. That seal on your salvage? I know it. Everyone here knows it. My grandmother's cousin — Sylvie — ran records down this river the week Vessany died, and never came back, and the story in my family is that some of what she carried is still in the water. Forty years, we've been telling children that story. And today a stranger drifts into Brasse-Lune towing one out of the river on a sinking boat." She shook her head slowly. "I don't believe in coincidence on this river. I believe in currents. Things sink, things surface, things wash up where they're needed. So. Which are you, Rafael Nystrom? Current, or consequence?"

Rafael was quiet for a long moment. Rain hammered the wheelhouse roof. Somewhere out in the dark, thunder rolled along the hills like furniture being moved upstairs.

"I don't know yet," Rafael said finally. And Odile noted — filed away, in the drawer where she kept true things — that it was an honest answer. Most people, offered that question, would have performed something. Confidence, innocence, menace. Rafael had just... declined to lie. Which was either very foolish or very brave, and Odile was increasingly sure it was the second.

"All right," she said. "That's the best answer I've had all year." She drained the terrible coffee. "Here's what happens now. Tomorrow the storm owns everything, so nobody will bother you. Day after, the town dries out and starts gossiping, because that's what towns are *for*, and a stranger on a quiet dock with a covered chest is gossip catnip. Corvane keeps a man at the fuel office who notices things for a living. So you've got maybe two days of being invisible, and then you're going to have to decide what you are." She stood, rinsed the mug, set it back in its solitary place. "When you decide — if you decide you need someone who knows this river, every channel, every wreck, every place Corvane's patrols get lazy — I'm second house past the church. Blue shutters. Knock loud; I sleep light."

Rafael walked her to the wheelhouse door, and there in the doorway, with the storm roaring and the harbor lights smeared sideways by rain, the two of them regarded each other like duelists who'd agreed to postpone.

"Why help me?" Rafael asked. "You don't know me. For all you know I'm trouble."

"Oh, I'm certain you're trouble," Odile said cheerfully. "The question is whose." She pulled her hood up. "My grandmother lost a country. My family lost a ferry concession to the company that helped burn it. I've been piloting other people's cargo past Corvane barges my whole working life and smiling about it, because smiling about it was the job. So when something with that seal surfaces in my river — *my* river, Nystrom, mine the way grief is yours — I'm not going to pretend I didn't see it. That's not charity. That's arithmetic."

Rafael looked at her for a moment longer. Whatever was behind those eyes — calculation, exhaustion, something older and tireder — it shifted, just slightly, like ice loosening on a channel.

"Two days," Rafael said. "Ask me again in two days."

"Done." Odile stepped out into the rain. "Oh — and fix your pump. Baptiste's shop opens at seven, he charges fair, and he cries at every hull equally, so don't take it personally."

She splashed back up the street through the downpour, grinning despite herself, and from the dark of the harbor she heard, faintly, absurdly, the sound of Rafael Nystrom laughing — one short bark of it, surprised out of a person who clearly hadn't planned on laughing, quickly smothered, but unmistakable.

Odile smiled all the way home.

Two days, then. Two days to dry out, two days for the town to start talking, two days for whatever slept under that tarp to start pulling on both of them like a tide. She fell asleep to the drum of rain on blue shutters, thinking about her grandmother's hands, and about Sylvie's boat that never came home, and about the strange, stubborn hope that rivers keep — that nothing truly important stays sunk forever, that sooner or later, everything surfaces.

And something had.
