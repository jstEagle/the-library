## 7. Brasse-Lune in the Rain

**Rafael**

The rain came in the night like a debt collector.

Not the polite drizzle Rafael had known on other coasts — this was a wall of water that arrived all at once, hammering the deck of the *Marta June* so hard it sounded like gravel being poured over a tin roof. By dawn the river had risen two feet and turned the color of strong tea. The patched hull held, barely, groaning at every seam like an old man climbing stairs.

Odile had towed them into Brasse-Lune the evening before, ahead of the storm, and now there was no leaving. The current ran white-brown and furious between the pilings; any boat that tried to nose out into it would be folded against the breakwater like wet paper. Rafael stood on the deck under a borrowed tarp, watching the town do what river towns do when the sky opens: nothing, gracefully.

Fishing boats were hauled up on rollers. Shutters went up. Children appeared in oilskins three sizes too big, splashing through puddles deep enough to drown a cat, and nobody told them to come inside. A dog trotted down the main street carrying somebody's lost sandal in its mouth like it was performing a civic duty.

"They'll be like this for weeks," Odile said, coming aboard with a thermos of something dark and sweet. "The monsoon doesn't knock. It moves in. You get used to it."

"I wasn't planning on staying."

"Nobody plans on staying. That's how everyone here got here." She poured two cups. "Drink. You look like a man who's been arguing with the weather."

Rafael drank. It was terrible coffee cut with chicory and too much sugar, and it was exactly right.

The trouble with being trapped was that Rafael had built a whole life out of never being trapped. There was always another fuel dock, another job, another stretch of river with no one in it who knew your name. Brasse-Lune was small enough that by the second day, people did know your name — or at least they knew *the stranger Odile towed in*, which amounted to the same thing in a town where information moved faster than the floodwater.

By the third morning, Rafael had been offered: a haircut (unsolicited), a opinion on the best brand of bilge pump (delivered loudly, from the dock), a plate of fried plantains (accepted), and a running argument about whether the 1971 ferry disaster was caused by the pilot's stubbornness or the shipping company's cheapness (declined, twice, then accidentally joined).

That last one should have been a warning. In a town that ran on gossip and grudge, everything old was still alive. Everything unsolved was still being solved, nightly, over drinks. And somewhere under the rain and the plantains, Rafael was carrying a steel chest stamped with a dead nation's seal, and half the town seemed to have opinions about dead nations.

*They don't know,* Rafael told themselves, watching the rain march across the harbor. *Nobody knows.*

But belonging, Rafael was beginning to understand, didn't require knowing. It just required showing up. And that was worse.

* * *

**Odile**

Odile Marchand had lived in Brasse-Lune her whole life except for two years she did not discuss, and she knew the town the way you know a face in the dark — by feel, by habit, by the sound it made when something was wrong.

Something was wrong with her new boarder. Not dangerous-wrong, or not only that. Rafael Nystrom flinched at kindness. When Madame Céleste pressed a second helping on them at the tavern, Rafael looked at the plate like it might contain a summons. When old Théodore asked where they'd learned to dive, Rafael answered with the flat precision of someone reading off a prepared statement, and Odile nearly laughed out loud, because she'd used that same voice herself, once, in a different life.

"You're going to have to relax eventually," she told them, poling the skiff across the flooded lane that had been, until Tuesday, a street. "The monsoon doesn't care about your schedule."

"I don't have a schedule."

"Everyone has a schedule. Yours is called *leaving*. I can see it. You've got one foot on the gunwale even when you're sitting down."

Rafael didn't answer, which was an answer.

Fine. Odile could work with fine. She'd grown up around people who didn't talk — her grandmother among them, a woman who could hold a silence the way other people held a grudge, which is to say lovingly and for decades. So instead of asking questions, Odile did what the town did: she introduced Rafael around, casually, the way you introduce weather. No announcements. Just names, faces, hands shaken under awnings while the rain came down like a judgment nobody remembered ordering.

First, the tavern.

Madame Céleste Aubert ran the *Ancre Mouillée* — the Dropped Anchor — a low room that smelled of woodsmoke, fried fish, and forty years of arguments that had never quite finished. She was sixty, built like a mooring bollard, and she took one look at Rafael and said, "You eat like a man who's been diving for his supper. Sit."

Rafael sat.

"Céleste's family has fished the east bank since before the bridge," Odile said, mostly for the pleasure of watching Rafael try to look interested without looking interested. "Her grandfather had oyster beds up past the bend."

"Had," Céleste said, with the particular bitterness of a woman who kept that word polished for use. "Before Corvane bought the water rights out from under everybody. My father fought it in court for nine years. We had paper. We had witnesses." She set down a bowl of fish stew in front of Rafael like she was dealing cards. "We had nothing they couldn't buy. Eat. You're too thin to argue with."

Rafael ate. And Odile watched something flicker across their face — quick, quickly buried — that she'd seen once before, on her own reflection, the day she'd found her grandmother's letters.

Then the schoolteacher.

Marielle Fournier taught all eleven children of Brasse-Lune in a building that flooded ankle-deep every monsoon, which she treated as an educational opportunity rather than an inconvenience. She met them on the covered walkway, chalk dust on her sleeves despite it being July, and shook Rafael's hand like she was taking attendance.

"The diver," she said. "Odile says you pulled things out of the old crossing wreck."

"Some things."

"My class has a theory about that wreck." Marielle's eyes had the bright, patient gleam of a woman who had spent twenty years extracting truth from children and could do the same to adults without breaking stride. "The official story says she hit the piling in fog. But my grandfather swore he heard explosions. Two of them. Before she ever touched anything."

"People say a lot of things."

"Mm." Marielle smiled. "People also said Vessany would last forever, and my grandmother's deed burned in an office fire along with four hundred others, so forgive me if I keep a file on things officials would prefer we forget." She tapped her temple. "It's all up here. Every name, every date. Someday someone will want it."

Rafael went very carefully still, the way a person does when a trapdoor opens under them and they decide, for the moment, to pretend it hasn't.

And finally, the dockhand.

Bruno Sarrail was thirty, enormous, cheerful, and missing two fingers on his left hand from a winch accident he described, whenever asked, as "a disagreement." He was loading storm shutters onto a cart when they passed, and he stopped, wiped his hand on his trousers, and offered it anyway.

"You're the one Odile dragged in," he said warmly. "Good. This town needed fresh blood. Everyone here has known each other so long we've run out of things to fight about."

"That's not true," Odile said. "We have plenty."

"We recycle them." Bruno grinned, then squinted at Rafael with sudden frankness. "Hey — you work salvage, yeah? My uncle had fishing grounds off the point. Corvane's tugs dragged them clean in '09, nets through everything, and when he complained they showed him a survey saying there was never anything there. Never anything! He fished it forty years!" He laughed, but it was the laugh of a man standing on a grudge, and Rafael stood very quietly inside the sound of it.

Walking back, Odile let the silence stretch until Rafael broke it, because she knew they would.

"They've all got stories," Rafael said finally.

"It's a town. Towns are made of stories."

"Corvane. All of them. Every single one."

"Small place. Big company." Odile shrugged. "You get used to hearing it."

"Yeah," Rafael said, in a voice Odile pretended not to notice. "I bet you do."

* * *

**Rafael**

On the fifth night of rain, Rafael sat in the tavern because there was nowhere else to sit, and discovered that this was apparently how towns caught you.

It happened slowly. Céleste saved them a table near the stove without being asked. Bruno dealt them into a card game whose rules changed whenever Bruno was losing, which was often, and loudly. Marielle brought a book about river hydrology and read aloud passages she found outrageous, and when Rafael corrected a figure — without thinking, just reflexively, because the number was wrong — the whole table turned and looked at them with such open delight that Rafael felt like a stray animal that had done a trick.

"Well," said Marielle. "The diver knows hydrology."

"I know rivers."

"Sit next week's storm out with us, then. I'm teaching the older ones currents. They could use someone who's actually been underwater instead of just in a textbook."

Rafael said yes. Rafael did not remember deciding to say yes. The word simply left, the way a coin leaves your pocket when someone bumps you, and afterward the table went back to arguing about whether the '71 ferry pilot was a hero or a fool, and Rafael sat there holding their beer and feeling, distinctly, like a man who had just signed something.

Because here was the thing Rafael understood, lying awake later on the *Marta June* with the rain drumming the tarp: these people were offering a place at the table without conditions, without questions, without knowing a single true thing about them. No warrant check. No references. Bruno had lost fingers and still shook hands first. Céleste fed strangers before she asked their names. The town's trust was casual, unearned, freely given — and it terrified Rafael more than the two men from Corvane ever had, because enforcers you could outrun, but a table with your seat already warmed? That you had to walk away from. On purpose. Looking back.

Enforcers threatened what you had. Belonging threatened what you might have to lose.

Rafael rolled over and stared at the ceiling boards, at the chest stowed beneath the bunk, at the ledger inside it full of names — Aubert, Fournier, Sarrail — names that matched the ones upstairs at the tavern, laughing over bad cards, unaware.

*They don't know,* Rafael thought again. But the thought had changed shape. It wasn't relief anymore. It was starting to sound like a question.

*How long can you sit at a table with people whose inheritance is under your bunk — and say nothing?*

In the morning, Odile appeared at the rail with two cups of that terrible chicory coffee and an expression that meant business.

"Get dressed," she said. "Bring the lamp oil. Tonight we're going to read your ledger properly — and Rafael?" She handed over the cup. "I counted the names last night. There are forty-one. I know where twenty-nine of them live."

She smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile.

"And three of them are sitting at Céleste's table."
