## 8. The Ledger's Names

The rain had settled into its long argument with the roof of Odile's boathouse, the kind of rain that didn't fall so much as move in. Rafael sat at a workbench under a lamp with a green glass shade, and the brass ledger lay open between them like something that had been waiting forty years for exactly this light.

Odile had made coffee the way her grandmother apparently had — boiled twice, sweetened past apology, strong enough to strip paint. She'd set two chipped mugs down without ceremony and said, "Drink it or don't, but don't tell me it's too sweet. I've heard that complaint my whole life and I've never once cared."

So they drank it, and it was too sweet, and neither of them mentioned it again.

The chest itself stood open on the floor, its documents wrapped in oilcloth that some careful dead hand had tied off in neat knots. But the ledger was the heart of it. Brass corners, a spine gone soft with river years, pages of dense, slanted handwriting in ink that had faded from black to the color of weak tea but hadn't surrendered a single word.

"Read me the first entry," Odile said. "Slowly. My reading's fine but my patience for old handwriting is not."

Rafael cleared their throat, which felt absurd, like clearing it before addressing a courtroom. "'Entry one. Vessany Provisional Registry of Holdings, compiled against dissolution. Name: Aurel Marchand. Trade: ferry pilot, licensed crossing at Brasse-Lune. Holdings: the ferry itself, the mooring rights east bank, and the house above the pilot's station.'"

Odile went very still.

"That's my grandmother," she said finally. "Aurel Marchand. The house above the pilot's station is — that's this house. That's *this* house." She laughed, a short startled sound. "I grew up in a listed holding and nobody told me."

"Keep going," Rafael said gently. "There's more."

"'Upon dissolution, all holdings revert to the Consolidated Trust for River Commerce, custodian: Edvard Corvane.'" Rafael looked up. "You know that name."

"Everyone on this river knows that name." Odile turned her mug a slow circle on the bench. "Corvane Shipping. They own the depot downriver, the fuel dock, half the barges. Old man Corvane built the whole thing in about six years after the collapse. Everyone always said he was lucky." Her voice flattened. "Nobody ever said *lucky how*."

They kept reading.

* * *

It took them three hours to get through the first twenty pages, and they'd have taken longer if Odile hadn't developed a system. She'd fetch people by name out of her memory of the town the way other people fetched tools — instantly, without looking. Rafael would read an entry aloud: a name, a trade, a holding, a seizure clause. And Odile would say, "That'll be Marta's family," or "That's the schoolhouse lot," or "Wait — say that name again," and lean in like a woman listening for a knock at a door she'd stopped expecting anyone to come to.

The entries had a rhythm to them, and the rhythm was the damning part. Not chaos, not war-scatter. Order. Every holding catalogued, every transfer pre-written, every signature line already carrying Corvane's name before the nation it belonged to had finished dying. Whoever compiled this hadn't been documenting a collapse. He'd been *catering* one.

"Listen to this one," Rafael said. "'Name: Tomas Ilves. Trade: netmaker. Holdings: workshop and drying sheds, north shore. Disposition: seized for unpaid harbor dues, assessed retroactively, rate doubled post-dissolution.' Doubled. After the country was gone. Who did he pay the first rate to?"

"A ghost," Odile said. "He paid a ghost, and then Corvane charged him double for the privilege." She drummed her fingers once on the bench. "Tomas Ilves. There are still Ilveses here. Little Ferenc Ilves runs the net shed now, third generation, rents it — you'll love this — *from Corvane*. His great-grandfather's workshop. He pays rent on his own family's floor."

Rafael felt something shift in their chest, low and slow, like ballast sliding. They'd opened the chest thinking in terms of value. Steel, brass, paper, provenance, what a collector would pay. Somewhere around entry fourteen — a widow named Sefa Brandt whose fishing rights had been "consolidated" into a Corvane subsidiary for a compensation payment of eleven marks, the currency worthless within the year — Rafael stopped thinking in terms of value entirely.

This wasn't treasure. Treasure you could sell. This was theft with paperwork, and the paperwork was so tidy, so patient, so *confident*, that no one had ever needed to hide it. It had just needed to be heavy enough to sink.

"They scuttled the ferry because of this book," Rafael said. "Not despite it. The resistance smuggled records into wrecks — your grandmother told you that. This was the copy they trusted to the river."

"And the river kept it." Odile reached over and touched the page — lightly, fingertips, the way you touch something you're afraid might flinch. "Forty years. It just waited."

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low and Odile refilled it without being asked, and somewhere past midnight the list they were making on butcher paper — Odile's idea, "we're not doing this in my head, my head floods" — had grown to fill the whole roll.

Names on the left. Living townspeople on the right. Between them, in Odile's blunt carpenter's pencil: what was taken.

Marta Osei, who ran the tavern where Rafael had eaten their first hot meal in weeks — descended from the Oseis, boatwrights, whose yard and timber rights had passed to the Trust "for safekeeping." Safekeeping that had lasted four decades and counting.

Henri Lacaille, the schoolteacher — the schoolhouse lot itself, deeded to a Vessany education cooperative, absorbed, then sold back to the town in 1979 so the town could keep teaching its own children. Odile wrote *sold their school to them* and underlined it twice.

Piotr the dockhand — no relation to any ledger name himself, but married to one, a Brandt, and when Rafael read Sefa Brandt's entry aloud Piotr's name went on the paper with a small asterisk and the note *by marriage, will want to know anyway.*

And then there was the warehouse.

"'Name: Deniz Kavala,'" Rafael read. "'Trade: provisioner. Holdings: bonded warehouse, Brasse-Lune waterfront, stone, two stories, iron doors. Contents at registry: grain, salt, rope, lamp oil, medical stores — provisioned for two hundred persons against six months of disruption.'"

Odile sat up straight. "The stone warehouse. The big one with the Corvane sign on it now. The one they use for—" she paused, working it out, "—for everything. Fuel drums, dry goods, the customs bond store. That's *the* warehouse. That's the building everybody in town has walked past their whole lives knowing somehow, without knowing why, that it used to be ours."

"It was provisioned," Rafael said. "Somebody stocked it for a disaster that was coming. Two hundred people, six months. They knew the country was going to be swallowed, and instead of running, someone filled a warehouse."

"And then a man named Corvane put a lock on it." Odile was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something steadier and colder than anger. "My grandmother used to say there were two kinds of men during the dissolution. Men who grabbed lamps, and men who grabbed oil. She never explained it. I thought it was just one of her sayings." She tapped the ledger. "Edvard Corvane grabbed the oil. All of it. Then he sold us our own light back, one tin at a time, at his price, for forty years."

Rafael didn't say anything. There wasn't anything clever to say, and Rafael had learned long ago that when a moment wasn't asking for cleverness, the kindest thing was silence and another pour of terrible coffee.

But privately — in the place where a person keeps the things they haven't decided to feel yet — Rafael noted that their hands had stopped wanting to think about the chest as cargo. Somewhere between the widow Brandt's eleven marks and the warehouse provisioned for two hundred, the word *salvage* had quietly fallen off this object, and the word *evidence* had taken its seat, and Rafael hadn't authorized the change. It had simply happened, the way weather happens.

* * *

Near two in the morning Odile stretched until her shoulders cracked and said, "One more. Pick one more and then we sleep, because if we finish the whole thing tonight we'll be useless tomorrow and I have a ferry to run."

Rafael turned pages at random and landed on an entry near the middle, and read it, and wished immediately they hadn't, and read it anyway.

"'Name: Yannick Solde. Trade: diver. Salvage and recovery, licensed. Holdings: dive gear, tender, and contract rights to wreck survey, lower channel. Note appended by registrar: Solde refused transfer terms. Refused twice. Third refusal logged. No further disposition recorded.'"

"No further disposition recorded," Odile repeated. "What does that mean?"

"In these books," Rafael said slowly, "it usually means the man disappeared, and writing 'disappeared' would have been an admission."

"A diver," Odile said. "He was a diver, and he wouldn't hand over his gear, and then the book just... stops talking about him." She looked across the bench at Rafael, and something passed between them that neither of them said out loud — that the ledger had been sitting in the dark next to a drowned ferry for forty years, and the last man it mentioned by trade was a diver who said no.

Rafael wrote *Yannick Solde — diver — refused — ?* on the butcher paper, and drew no line to anyone, because there was no one left to draw it to. Some names you carried without resolution. Rafael knew that from the inside.

Then Odile blew out the lamp, and in the sudden dark the rain sounded closer, and Rafael lay on the pallet she'd rigged in the boathouse loft listening to it and did not sleep for a long time.

Because here was the thing Rafael could not stop turning over, the thing that had teeth: the ledger was worth money. Real money, to collectors, to archives, maybe even to Corvane — God knew the lawyer's eventual offer would prove that. A person could take this book downriver tomorrow and be paid, and vanish, and never hear the name Marchand or Ilves or Brandt again. That was the old plan. That had always been the old plan. Move, sell, disappear. It was a good plan. It had kept Rafael alive for years.

But plans were made by the person you were before you knew things. And Rafael now knew things. Knew that Marta's tavern sat on stolen ground. Knew that little Ferenc Ilves paid rent on his own great-grandfather's floor. Knew that the warehouse with the iron doors had been filled by hand, by somebody's careful courage, against a disaster — and that the disaster had arrived wearing a suit and carrying a pen.

The chest wasn't treasure. It was a receipt. And receipts, unlike treasure, pointed at people.

Rafael fell asleep somewhere in that thought, and dreamed of green water and a man named Solde refusing, twice, and woke at dawn with the rain still going and Odile already up, standing at the workbench in the gray light, reading the butcher paper top to bottom with her arms crossed.

"We missed church," she said, which meant nothing in particular, "but we got the important part done." Then she turned, and her expression was complicated in a way Rafael couldn't yet read. "There's a barge coming through today. Corvane colors. And Marta wants to know why you spent all night in my boathouse, and I told her it was none of her business, and she said that in Brasse-Lune everything is everyone's business by breakfast, and she was right, because Henri the schoolteacher is already asking questions."

Rafael sat up. "What kind of questions?"

"The dangerous kind." Odile picked up the pencil and tapped the ledger's cover, once. "The kind that start with *what's in the box* and end somewhere none of us can steer back from." She held Rafael's eyes. "So before this town decides anything for us — I need to know what you're planning to do with it. Because I watched you all night, Nystrom, and you read those names like a man reading his own charges."

Outside, far downriver, a horn sounded — long, low, and getting closer.
