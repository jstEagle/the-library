## 9. Running Costs More

Rafael told themselves it was a supply run.

That was the story they rehearsed at the fuel dock, buying diesel with money that should have gone to a new bilge pump: *just downriver for parts, back in three days.* The chest went aboard under a tarp and a coil of anchor chain, because even liars need props, and Rafael had learned long ago that the best cover story is one that's ninety percent true. They were going downriver. There would be parts involved. The fact that the parts were attached to a buyer in Marrow's Bend who paid cash for unmarked salvage and never asked questions — that was just an efficiency of route.

Odile had watched them load. Of course she had. Brasse-Lune was a town where curtains twitched in shifts, where everyone knew everyone's business by breakfast, and Odile Marchand missed nothing that happened on her water. She'd stood on the ferry deck with a mug of coffee going cold in her hand and said, "You're leaving."

"Supply run," Rafael said.

"Mm," said Odile, and drank the cold coffee anyway, which Rafael would later understand was her way of swallowing a whole argument whole.

The truth was simpler and uglier. The enforcers had come once politely, and polite men always came back impolite. Every day Rafael stayed in Brasse-Lune was a day the town got painted onto whatever target Corvane was building. Rafael had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not being anywhere when consequences arrived, and here they were, sitting still, letting a town grow around them like barnacles. It had to be cut loose. Cut it loose now, sell the chest, take the money somewhere with no name, and let Brasse-Lune go back to being a town that argued about fish prices instead of dead nations.

That was the plan. The river had other paperwork in mind.

* * *

The first forty kilometers were beautiful, which should have been the warning.

The monsoon had settled into its working rhythm by then — rain that fell not in sheets but in decisions, long gray deliberations from a sky that couldn't make up its mind. The channel ran high and brown and fast, carrying whole trees like commuters late for work. Rafael ran the boat down the middle of it, hood up, hands steady, feeling for once like a person doing exactly what they were built to do. Alone. Competent. Unencumbered.

There was a specific pleasure in running a boat well, and Rafael had chased it across two decades and four hundred rivers. You read the water the way other people read faces. A V of current meant rock. A smooth green tongue meant the deep line through a rapid. The brown boil ahead meant something big moving under the surface, and you gave it room without thinking about why. Nobody to explain yourself to. Nobody's fear to manage. Just you and the water and the honest arithmetic of staying dry.

Around noon the arithmetic changed.

It started as a sound — a soft, wet knock from below the floorboards, rhythmic as a heartbeat with arrhythmia. Rafael killed the engine and drifted and listened. Knock. Knock-knock. Knock. Not the pump. Not the chain. Something structural, saying what rotting wood says when you ask too much of it: *I have been telling you about this for weeks, and you have been answering with caulk.*

Rafael lifted the floor panel and looked into the bilge and felt their stomach drop through the hull ahead of schedule. Water, yes — there was always water — but coming in fast, silver threads of it jetting from the seam where the yard had patched over the old patch over the original sin of the boat itself. The monsoon-swollen river was flexing the hull with every wave, and the patch was flexing back, and one of them was losing.

Rafael did what salvage divers do. They improvised. Bilge pump running full, a bar of epoxy putty jammed into the seam like a finger in a dike, the spare canvas lashed over the worst spot and tarred on the fly. For an hour it held. For another hour it mostly held. The boat limped downriver through the rain, riding lower by degrees, the way a tired man slides down a wall he keeps insisting he's leaning against.

And then the current took them past the old quarry mouth, and the wind came around out of the west, and the river stood up in short steep waves with no patience in them at all, and the seam opened like a book someone had been holding shut with one thumb.

Rafael heard it give. A long tearing groan, wood parting along the grain, and then the sound every boatman knows and dreads — not loud at all, really. Almost polite. The sound of water coming in like it owns the place.

They were eight kilometers from anything. The banks were mangrove and mud, the kind of shoreline that swallows boats whole and files them under missing. The bilge pump screamed and gained nothing. Rafael bailed with a bucket while steering with a knee, radio crackling static, and did the math they'd done a hundred times before in a hundred bad moments: swim or sink, walk or drown, and which lie to tell whichever authority fished them out.

Except this time there was a third option, and it came upriver against the current at twenty knots with its running lights on and a woman at the wheel who had apparently decided, sometime in the last week, that Rafael Nystrom was her problem now.

* * *

"You know," Odile said, throwing him a line with the ease of someone dealing cards, "most people wait until the boat actually sinks before they make me tow them twice."

Rafael, soaked to the sternum, hauling the chest — *the chest, God help them, they'd saved the chest* — onto her ferry deck, managed: "I'm efficient."

"You're a menace." She made the line fast and looked at the listing wreck behind her, riding on the end of the tow like a drunk on a friend's shoulder. "That hull is done. Done done. Not 'patch it in the morning' done. Firewood done."

"It has good lines."

"It has *good lines,*" Odile repeated, in the tone of a woman cataloguing evidence. "So does a coffin."

She turned the ferry back toward Brasse-Lune and Rafael sat on her deck in the rain, dripping, shamed, clutching a tarp-wrapped steel box that had cost them nearly everything twice now, and waited for the lecture. It didn't come. That was the strange thing about Odile — she could dismantle a man completely and somehow leave the important parts standing. She just drove the boat and let the silence do the talking, and the silence said things like *you almost died alone rather than ask for help* and *there are people back there who noticed you left at dawn* and *your coffee is getting cold again.*

By the time they tied up at her dock, dark had come down early the way it did in monsoon season, and half the town had gathered under the fish shed roof to watch the spectacle of the stranger's boat dying. The mechanic — Bruno, wide as a door, gentle as a nurse — clucked over the hull like a doctor over a patient whose family kept feeding it cigarettes. The schoolteacher offered Rafael a towel. Someone's grandmother pressed a bowl of fish stew into their hands without asking, the way food arrives in towns where hospitality is a reflex and not a decision.

Rafael ate the stew because refusing it would have required a kind of courage they didn't have.

* * *

Later, in the boathouse where Odile kept her gear and, apparently, strays, Rafael sat wrapped in a blanket that smelled of diesel and lavender — someone in this town believed those belonged together — and listened to the rain hammer the tin roof like applause from an audience that wanted something.

Odile hung their soaked clothes on a line. She did it without ceremony, the way she did everything, and then she sat down across from them on an upturned crate and looked at Rafael the way you look at a knot you intend to untie.

"I'm going to ask you something," she said, "and you're going to want to lie, and I want you to notice that you want to."

"That's a lot of instructions."

"I'm a pilot. Instructions are how I keep people alive." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Why do you run?"

"I don't run. I travel."

"Travelers come back." She nodded at the boat outside, the drowned thing under the tarp. "You were leaving at dawn with no plan to return. That's not travel. I've ferried travelers my whole life. They ask about hotels. You asked me last week which channels flood first in a storm surge. You weren't planning a trip, Rafael. You were planning an exit."

The rain filled the pause, generous, giving Rafael time to construct something. And Rafael was aware — distantly, academically — of the architecture going up inside them: the charming deflection, the plausible history, the version of themselves that was merely restless, merely independent, merely a person who liked rivers more than addresses. It was good work. It had fooled customs officers, ex-partners, one very persistent insurance investigator, and most versions of Rafael themselves.

But they were sitting in a boathouse in borrowed clothes, having been hauled out of the river for the second time in a month by the same woman, holding a bowl-stained spoon, and some part of them — the part that had surfaced at midnight off the scuttled ferry, looking at a dead nation's seal and feeling less like a thief than a courier — was simply too tired to build.

"There's a warrant," Rafael said.

The words came out smaller than intended. Rain covered the retreat.

"From Corvane?"

"Before Corvane. Before any of this." Rafael stared at the floorboards, at the water dripping off the clothesline, anywhere but Odile's face. "Eleven years ago. A salvage job. Private contract, big money, the kind you don't ask questions about because the questions are the product. We were raising cargo off a freighter that went down with its papers — deliberately, it turned out, though I didn't know that then either. There was a man on the crew. An inspector. He wasn't supposed to be there. He started asking questions *on site*, out loud, where the client's people could hear him."

Odile had gone very still. Stillness, Rafael was learning, was her listening.

"The job went wrong. Lines fouled, a crane swung, and this man — Tomas Reyes, his name was Tomas Reyes — got hurt. Badly. Crushed leg, spine damage. And afterward, when the investigators came, the story that was waiting for them was very tidy. My signature on the lift plan. My company, such as it was. My crane operator swearing I'd ignored the warnings." Rafael's voice flattened out, going somewhere far away. "What wasn't in the story was that the client had hired the crane company, scheduled the lift, and known full well there was an inspector aboard they needed distracted. What also wasn't in the story was that Tomas had found documents in that wreck that would have sunk the client — real ones, with signatures — and that his presence on my job site was not an accident."

"And the client," Odile said slowly, "was—"

"A shipping concern. Small then. Ambitious. Run by a man named Aldous Corvane." Rafael finally looked up. "He buried the whistleblower in my name, Odile. Not in the ground. In the *paperwork*. And when I tried to say so, I discovered how deep the paperwork goes — lawyers, port authorities, a maritime board with three members who all had contracts. The warrant came down for negligence causing grievous harm. Eleven years I've been paying a debt for a crime that was committed *against* both of us. Him with his body. Me with my name."

The boathouse was quiet except for the rain, and Rafael sat in the quiet feeling flayed and lighter simultaneously, the way you feel after vomiting — terrible, and yet.

"Why didn't you fight it?"

"With what? I had a boat and a dive kit. He had a fleet." Rafael laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I told myself running was strategy. Stay mobile, stay small, stay useful enough to hire and harmless enough to ignore. And maybe it started that way. But somewhere along the bottom of these years it stopped being strategy and became just... motion. You keep moving so you never have to arrive anywhere and be seen clearly. Because if anyone looks closely—" they gestured vaguely at their own chest, "—they'll find the thing I can't fix. The man in the hospital bed. The mother who wrote letters for two years and then stopped, and I never answered the last one, and now I don't know if she's alive and I'm too much of a coward to find out, because finding out means admitting I chose the river over going home and facing what I let happen."

Odile didn't say *it wasn't your fault.* Rafael braced for it and it didn't come, and the absence was somehow a kindness.

Instead she said: "My grandmother used to say that shame is a current. You can't swim against it forever. Eventually you either let it carry you somewhere or it drags you under." She stood, took the damp towel off the line, and dropped it in Rafael's lap. "Tomas Reyes. Is he alive?"

"I don't know. Last I heard — years ago — he was alive. In a chair. Suing everyone and losing."

"Then he can be found. Warrants can be challenged. Clients can be exposed — and Rafael." She crouched to their eye level, and her voice dropped into something fierce and low. "You are sitting on a steel box full of signed proof of exactly the kind of man Aldous Corvane is. You've been carrying your own exoneration up and down this river in a tarp and calling it cargo."

Rafael blinked at her. "That's — no. That's not what this is. This is Vessany inheritance, this is the town's—"

"It's both." She straightened up, pulling clothespins off the line, brisk again, practical again, as if she hadn't just detonated something in the middle of Rafael's life. "Same man. Same method. Bury the paper, blame the powerless, wait for everyone who remembers to die or scatter. Your warrant isn't separate from the ledger, it's the same theft wearing different clothes. Which means—" she counted on her fingers, mock-casual, "—the town gets its inheritance, you get your name back, and Corvane loses everything, all in one motion. Efficient. You'd like it."

And Rafael, who had spent eleven years believing the only exit was downriver, sat in a boathouse smelling of lavender-diesel and understood, with the slow vertigo of a diver realizing which way the surface is, that they had been running from the one direction rescue could come from.

"I don't know how to stop," they said quietly. It was the truest sentence they'd spoken in a decade.

"Well," said Odile, hanging the last shirt, "good news. Your boat's firewood. You literally can't."

Outside, the rain kept on, drumming the tin like a heartbeat that refused to quit. And down the lane, lamplit through the wet dark, someone was walking toward the boathouse — quick steps, a lantern swinging, and a voice calling Odile's name with an urgency that had nothing to do with weather.

It was the schoolteacher. And she was carrying papers.
