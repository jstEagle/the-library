## 11. Corvane Makes an Offer

The lawyer arrived on a Tuesday, which Rafael would later decide was the most insulting part of it. Not at dawn with guns. Not in the night, when a man's judgment goes soft. On a Tuesday, in daylight, in a pressed linen suit that had no business surviving the monsoon, stepping off a Corvane launch like he was arriving to appraise a property.

He was waiting at the top of Odile's dock when Rafael came back from checking the mooring lines. Just standing there under an enormous black umbrella held by a silent deckhand, dry as a ledger page, smiling the way men smile when they've already counted your debts for you.

"Rafael Nystrom," the man said. It wasn't a question. "My name is Anselm Vey. I represent Corvane Shipping." He extended a hand that Rafael did not take. The smile didn't waver. "I'd hoped we might speak privately."

"Whatever you've got to say," Rafael said, "you can say it where the rain can hear it too."

Vey glanced up at the sky as if consulting it, then back down, and something in his face shifted — not offense, just recalculation, the way a man shifts weight on a ladder. "Very well. Though I'll warn you, my notes are paper."

"Mine too."

* * *

They ended up in the tavern, because there was nowhere else, and because Vey seemed to understand that a public negotiation was its own kind of message. He bought a round for the room without asking anyone — a move so smooth and so arrogant that Marta the tavern keeper accepted the coins, bit one, and handed them back.

"Counterfeit," she said pleasantly. "Fits the company."

Vey laughed as if she'd been charming, chose a corner table with his back to the wall — a detail Rafael noted and filed — and spread nothing on the table at all. No folder. No contract. That was the first tell. Men who intend to honor agreements bring paper. Men who intend to dissolve them bring only themselves.

"I'll be brief," Vey said, "because I know you're a busy man, and because I suspect you don't enjoy my company any more than I enjoy this weather." He folded his hands. "Corvane is prepared to offer you two hundred thousand, delivered anywhere you name, in any currency you prefer. In addition, we will arrange the withdrawal of an outstanding warrant — Vessany Province, salvage theft, resulting injury, filed eight years ago. You know the one. It would simply cease to exist. No record, no file, no poster. A clean slate, Mr. Nystrom. Most men wait their whole lives for one."

The tavern noise went on around them — dice, argument, someone's terrible singing — but for Rafael the room went quiet and distant, the way it does underwater. Because Vey had done something no one had managed in eight years. He had said the warrant out loud. The thing Rafael never said, never wrote, never let surface even in dreams except as a shape, a pressure, a hand on the ankle dragging down.

*You know the one.*

Rafael stared at him. "How do you know about the warrant?"

Vey smiled again, gently, the way you smile at a child who has asked how the trick works. "Mr. Nystrom. We are Corvane. We have known where you were since before you knew where you were going. We knew what boat you'd buy, what engine you'd ruin, what dive jobs you'd take to pay for it. Knowing things is rather our entire business." He leaned forward slightly. "What we didn't know — until recently — was whether you were clever or merely lucky. I'm pleased to report the answer appears to be both, which is why I'm here instead of the men who usually handle situations of this nature."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll thank you for your time, wish you a safe monsoon, and leave." Vey spread his hands, all reasonableness. "We're not in the business of threats, Mr. Nystrom. Threats create martyrs, and martyrs create paperwork. We're in the business of *prices*. Everything has one. Even principles — especially principles, they're so expensive to maintain." He stood, buttoned his jacket. "You have three days. There's a fuel dock at Kilometre Forty-One. Leave word with the attendant. Or don't, and we'll all go on pretending this conversation was about the weather."

He left a card on the table anyway — heavy stock, embossed, absurd in a river town — and walked out into the rain, umbrella blooming over him like a black flower, and got back on his launch, and was gone.

Marta appeared with a rag and wiped the table where he'd sat, longer and harder than the table required.

"Two hundred thousand," she said. "That's real money."

"It is," Rafael agreed.

"He offered it sitting in *my* tavern." She wrung out the rag. "Man's got no shame at all. I almost respect it."

* * *

Odile found Rafael that evening on the ferry deck, sitting against the wheel housing with the card in one hand, turning it over and over like a coin that might have two different faces if you caught the light right.

She didn't ask. She just sat down, close enough that their shoulders touched, and looked out at the harbor lights smearing in the rain, and waited. She was good at waiting. It was, Rafael was learning, one of the many things she could do better than Rafael could.

"A lawyer came," Rafael said finally.

"Marta told me. Two hundred thousand and a magic eraser for your warrant." Odile took the card from Rafael's fingers and read it. "Anselm Vey. He smells like the inside of a bank vault, apparently. Three people have described him to me independently and all three used the word 'oily.'"

"They knew about the warrant, Odile. Eight years. I never told anyone here. Never told anyone *anywhere*." Rafael heard their own voice come out flat and careful, the voice used for reporting depth and bottom time. "They've been reading me like a chart since the day I came upriver. Maybe before."

Odile turned the card over, handed it back. "And what did you feel? When he said it out loud?"

Rafael opened their mouth to say *nothing*, which was the honest answer in the sense that a drowned man's last breath contains no air. What actually came out was: "Relieved. For about four seconds. Like someone finally said the thing that's been sitting on my chest for eight years, and now maybe I could put it down." A breath. "And then I understood what the relief was for. It wasn't kindness. It was bait. They dug up the worst thing I ever did and held it out to me like — like a rope. And you grab ropes when you're drowning. That's the whole design."

Odie nodded slowly. "My grandmother had a saying. *The crocodile offers to carry you across the river and calls it friendship.*"

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have eaten Vey alive."

"She'd have started with his shoes." Odile pulled her knees up. "So. Are you taking it?"

And here was the thing Rafael had been turning over all day, all through the rain, all through Marta's counterfeit-coin theater: the honest answer was *I don't know.* Two hundred thousand. A clean name. A life where walking into a port town didn't mean scanning the posts for your own face. Eight years of sleeping in shifts, of keeping moving, of never fixing anything because fixed things anchor you — all of it, gone, traded for a steel box full of dead people's paper.

"No," Rafael said. And then, because lying to Odile had stopped being possible somewhere around the second rescue: "But I thought about it longer than I want to admit. Long enough to be ashamed of."

"Don't be ashamed. Be accurate." Odile's voice was matter-of-fact, no judgment in it at all. "They built the offer to work, Rafael. That's what frightens me. Not that you might take it. That they priced it correctly. They know exactly what a clean slate costs a man who's been dirty for eight years, and they know it's less than two hundred thousand." She shook her head. "The question isn't whether you're strong enough to refuse. The question is why a shipping company thinks a box of forty-year-old paper is worth more than a clean slate plus a fortune."

Rafael had been circling that question all day without landing on it. Now Odile landed on it in one pass, the way she landed the ferry in whiteout channels nobody else would attempt.

Because that was the arithmetic, wasn't it. The ledger was worth more than the money. Worth more than the warrant, more than Rafael's whole future, more than whatever sum Corvane's accountants had signed off on without blinking. Which meant the names in it weren't historical curiosities. They were live claims. Land, rights, warehouses — things that, if they surfaced, would cost Corvane more than two hundred thousand. Cost them, probably, more than Rafael could imagine, which was itself informative, because Rafael could imagine quite a lot.

"They're not buying a box," Rafael said slowly. "They're buying a fire before it spreads."

"Yes. And notice what they're *not* doing." Odile held up fingers. "One: they're not sending enforcers to take it by force. Not yet. Force makes noise, and Brasse-Lune is already listening. Two: they're not threatening you. They're *thanking* you, in advance, for being reasonable. Three—" she paused, and her voice went quieter, and colder, "—three, they came to *you*. Only you. Nobody's approached me. Nobody's asked the town anything. As far as Corvane is concerned, the chest belongs to whoever holds it, and the people named inside it don't exist."

Rafael looked at her.

"That's the part that should keep you up tonight," Odile said. "Not the money. The money's just the price tag. Look at what's *not* for sale. Me. The schoolteacher. Marta. Half the dockhands. To Corvane, we're not heirs. We're not even obstacles. We're weather." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "They offered you enough to vanish on, and not one coin for the town, because they genuinely believe the town will never matter. That we'll grumble in the tavern and die owing them money, generation after generation, and call it normal."

* * *

Rafael didn't sleep. Not properly. Around the third hour of rain drumming the cabin roof, Rafael got up and lit the lamp and took out the brass ledger, because it seemed suddenly important to look at the thing everyone was pricing.

It lay open to the pages they'd matched to Brasse-Lune — the schoolteacher's grandfather with his seized orchard land, the dockhand's family with their fishing rights sold for a fraction and stamped *RESOLVED* in a clerk's impatient hand, the warehouse on the north quay that Corvane still operated, still profited from, forty years on. Signatures at the bottom of each entry. One signature recurring like a watermark: *A. Corvane, Liquidator.* Neat, confident, unhurried handwriting. The handwriting of a man signing away other people's lives on a schedule.

Rafael had seen that kind of handwriting before. Had, in a way that still woke them some nights, *been* that kind of handwriting — the person who signs the paper that ruins someone while telling himself it's procedure. The botched job, the injured man, the warrant. Rafael had spent eight years paying for one signature. These people had spent forty.

And now a lawyer in a linen suit had offered to make Rafael's signature disappear — wipe the debt clean — in exchange for helping bury theirs forever.

That was the shape of it. That was the whole ugly elegant shape. Rafael sat with the lamp low and understood, finally, what the offer really was. Not a bribe. A mirror. *Here,* Corvane was saying. *Here is what you are worth to us: everything, briefly, and then nothing at all. Take the money, take the clean name, become a rumor. You were always going to be a rumor. We're just offering to make it official.*

They didn't trust Rafael to matter. That was the deepest cut, deeper than the money, deeper than the warrant dragged into daylight. They had priced Rafael as a man who would sell — because men like Rafael always sell, that's what men like Rafael *were for* — and they hadn't even bothered to price the town, because towns like Brasse-Lune never mattered, that's what towns like Brasse-Lune were *for*.

Rafael closed the ledger.

Somewhere out in the dark, past the harbor mouth, a Corvane patrol boat ran its slow circuit, lights off, engine idling low. Listening. Waiting for Tuesday's answer.

Three days, Vey had said.

Rafael blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and made a decision that felt less like courage than like arithmetic: two hundred thousand buys one man's freedom. The ledger, read aloud, might buy everybody's. And Rafael Nystrom — drifter, runner, the man who'd spent eight years perfecting the art of not being anywhere — was suddenly, terribly, the only address Corvane had for that ledger.

Which meant, Rafael realized as the rain kept falling, that for the first time in eight years, running was no longer the safest option.

It was the expensive one.

And there were three days left to figure out what a man does when the crocodile offers to carry him across the river — and the whole town is watching from the bank.
