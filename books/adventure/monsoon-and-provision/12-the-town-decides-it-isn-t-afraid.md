## 12. The Town Decides It Isn't Afraid

The rain came down on Brasse-Lune like it had a personal grudge, and inside the Salt & Anchor tavern, forty people pretended it didn't.

Rafael stood in the doorway, half in, half out, which was more or less how Rafael had lived for eleven years. The tavern smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular fried-onion sweetness of Madame Okonkwo's fritters, which she made every storm night and claimed were a family recipe from a country she refused to name, on the grounds that it kept people arguing and arguing kept them buying drinks.

The schoolteacher, Amara Voss, stood at the front with a brass ledger under her arm like a hymnal.

Odile had argued against this for two days. Rafael had heard the argument through the thin wall of the ferry office, conducted in the low, rapid register the two of them used when they thought nobody was listening, which was to say loudly enough that the whole dock could follow along.

"You can't just *read* it," Odile had said. "The moment it's public, it's a target."

"It was always a target," Amara had answered. "It was a target for forty years while it sat at the bottom of the river. The only thing that's changed is that now *we* know what we're worth."

Now Amara tapped the ledger's cover and the tavern went quiet in the way taverns do — not all at once, but in a wave, from the front tables back, the way a storm front comes off the water.

"Forty years ago," Amara said, "the nation of Vessany dissolved. Some of you remember it. Some of your parents remember it. Most of you were told it didn't matter to us, because Vessany was upriver, and its paperwork drowned with it." She paused. "That paperwork didn't drown. It was sunk. On purpose. And it's sitting on that table."

Rafael's stomach did something complicated. In the doorway, unnoticed, was a good place to have a stomach.

* * *

Odile Marchand sat at the corner table with her back to the wall — a habit she'd picked up from her grandmother, who used to say you should always be able to see the door and the window, because trouble and weather both come in through openings. She watched Rafael in the doorway and felt the familiar itch of wanting to drag them inside and the wiser instinct of leaving them be. People like Rafael came to the threshold in their own time or not at all.

She'd told Amara the truth: she was afraid. Not of Corvane's men, particularly — she'd grown up on stories of men who burned deeds, and fear of them was like fear of rain, ambient and unhelpful. What she was afraid of was the *reading*. Her grandmother had smuggled records into wrecks so that someday they could be read aloud. If Amara read them and nothing happened — if the town nodded, finished their fritters, and went home — that would be a second death for the whole nation of Vessany, and Odile didn't think she could stand in the room for it.

Amara opened the ledger.

"Entry forty-one," she read. "'Marchand, Yolande. Ferry license, crossing rights, Brasse-Lune to Vessany City, plus two-thirds share of the ferry itself. Confiscated under Emergency Provision Twelve. Witnessed by H. Corvane, commercial attaché.'"

The tavern made a sound — not a gasp, exactly. More like the whole room inhaling through its teeth.

Odile's grandmother had been dead nine years, and Odile still heard her voice every time the ferry engine caught on the first try. *Two-thirds share of the ferry.* Her grandmother had told her the Corvane family had taken the crossing rights, but hearing it read from brass and paper, with a witness's signature, was different. It was the difference between knowing a thing and being able to prove it at a table with a magistrate sitting at the head of it.

"Keep going," said Madame Okonkwo, from behind her fritter tray, in the tone of a woman who intended to hear every word before the tray was empty.

"Entry fifty-eight," Amara read. "'Okonkwo, Beatrice. Provisioner's license, Vessany City market, with warehouse at Pier Four. Confiscated under Emergency Provision Twelve.'"

Madame Okonkwo set down her tray. She was a small, round, formidable woman who had run the tavern for thirty years and had once ended a bar fight by standing between the fighters and offering them fritters until they were too full and ashamed to continue. She looked at the ledger for a long moment.

"That warehouse is the Corvane depot," she said. "The big one, upriver. With the crane."

"It is," said Amara.

"My mother worked that market," Madame Okonkwo said, to the room in general, in the voice of a woman assembling a case. "Walked four miles each way. They told her the license lapsed. They told her the *nation lapsed*, like a nation is a subscription."

* * *

The dockhand, Tomas Reyes, had come in wet and planning to drink two beers and go home. He was now on his fourth beer and had no plans at all.

Tomas worked Corvane's dock six days a week. He loaded their barges, he signed their manifests, he wore their canvas jacket with their logo stitched on the breast like a small flag of surrender. He had never much thought about it. Work was work, and the Corvane pay was on time, which in a river town was practically a moral virtue.

But he was listening to Amara read — "'Reyes, Emilio. Dockhand's berth and mooring rights, Pier Four. Confiscated—'" — and doing arithmetic he'd never done before. His grandfather Emilio had worked the same pier. Had *owned* a berth at the same pier, apparently, in the way you own a thing until men in good coats arrive with a provision. Tomas looked down at the logo on his chest and felt, for the first time in eleven years of steady employment, like a scab.

"Entry seventy-three," Amara read. "'Voss, Daniel. Schoolhouse and teaching post, Brasse-Lune district. Confiscated—'"

Amara stopped. Looked up. The tavern looked back.

"That one's my grandfather," she said, and her voice, which had been steady as a metronome through forty entries, wobbled exactly once. She steadied it. "They took the school. Then they took the license to teach. Then the nation ended and everyone agreed there was nothing to be done, because you can't sue a country that no longer exists."

"You can't," agreed old Père Lucien from the corner, who had been a harbor master for thirty years and now mostly dispensed weather opinions and brandy. "But you can sue the men who took the country's things and kept them. Property doesn't drown, Amara. It just changes pockets. And pockets can be reached."

"How do you know that?" someone asked.

"Because I've been reaching into pockets my whole career," said Père Lucien, and the tavern laughed, and the laugh had a new sound in it that hadn't been there an hour ago.

* * *

Rafael stood in the doorway and listened to a dead nation come back to life one name at a time, and thought: *this is what the chest was for.*

Not the brass. Not the salvage value, which Odile had priced out loud one night — "maybe enough to fix your boat and vanish properly" — and not the documents, which were just paper now, brittle as autumn leaves. It was this. A room full of wet people realizing, all together, in the same hour, that the thing they'd each been told was *just how things went* had actually been a theft, witnessed, signed, and filed.

Rafael had spent years around people who'd lost things. Wrecks were full of them. But loss at sea was impersonal — the water took what it took. This was different. This was loss with a signature on it. Loss with a *witness*.

A woman near the door — Rafael didn't know her name; she sold rope and net-mending at the Saturday market — turned and caught Rafael standing there. She looked at Rafael the way people in Brasse-Lune looked at the boat that had come in listing, with curiosity and a kind of open arithmetic: *is this one ours now?*

Rafael looked away, at the rain.

* * *

Odile watched the room turn. She'd seen weather turn like this — a long gray afternoon where nothing happens and then the light goes strange and everyone on the dock stops talking at once, because they all feel the same drop in pressure.

"Entry ninety," Amara read. "'Corvane, Henri. Commercial attaché, Provisional Authority. Witnessed thirty-one confiscations under Emergency Provision Twelve. Compensation received: none recorded.'"

"None recorded," repeated Père Lucien, softly, the way a man says *checkmate*.

"His grandson runs the depot," said Tomas Reyes, before he could stop himself. Everyone looked at him. He shrugged, miserable and defiant at once. "I load his barges. Somebody had to say it."

"The founder of Corvane Shipping," Amara said, "signed away this town's ferries, warehouses, market licenses, school, and mooring rights, and called it an emergency provision. Then his family built a shipping empire on the pier they took from a dockhand." She closed the ledger. "That's the whole story. That's the nation of Vessany. It didn't collapse. It was *picked*."

The silence lasted four seconds. Odile counted.

Then Madame Okonkwo said, "I want my mother's warehouse," in a voice that carried clear through the rain on the roof, and the room broke open.

* * *

It was not a riot. Brasse-Lune was too small and too wet for riots. It was more like a flood — everybody talking at once, chairs scraping, Tomas Reyes standing up and sitting down and standing up again like a man testing ice, Père Lucien rapping a brandy glass with a spoon and being ignored, which was the traditional fate of brandy-glass-rappers.

"Order," Amara said, uselessly, and then, "if we do this, we do it *together* or not at all," which worked, because Amara was the schoolteacher and forty years of Brasse-Lune children had been trained by their parents to obey that specific voice even after growing up.

"Together how?" asked the rope-seller. "We write to the magistrate? The magistrate's office has a Corvane calendar on the wall. I've seen it."

"We write to the magistrate anyway," Amara said. "On the record. In writing. With copies. Corvane can lean on one person easily. It's much harder to lean on a town that's all saying the same thing in triplicate."

"And if they lean anyway?" Tomas asked. "They've got men. Boats. You've seen them at the fuel dock, asking polite questions." He glanced, without meaning to, toward the doorway, toward Rafael, and everyone else glanced the same way, because the whole town had heard about the polite men by now. Gossip in Brasse-Lune moved faster than the ferry.

Rafael, caught, stepped fully inside for the first time. Water dripped off their jacket onto Madame Okonkwo's clean floor, and she gave Rafael a look that promised a reckoning later.

"They came to my boat," Rafael said. It was the first time they'd said it in front of the town. "Two of them. Very polite. Asked about recovered artifacts. I told them I dive for scrap and scrap only." Rafael looked around the room at the upturned faces. "They didn't believe me. They're not the kind of men who come to retrieve things. They come to make things *unhappened*. I've seen what's left after they've visited a site. It's not wreckage. It's *absence*."

"So we give them nothing to find," said Odile, from her corner, and the room turned to her with relief, because Odile was the one who ferried people through weather nobody else would cross, and if Odile had a plan, the plan would hold.

"Where's the chest now?" Père Lucien asked shrewdly.

"Safe," said Rafael and Odile, at exactly the same moment, in exactly the same tone, and Père Lucien smiled into his brandy like a man who had just learned something useful.

* * *

The vote, when it came, was not a vote. Nobody wrote anything down. Madame Okonkwo simply asked, the way she asked whether anyone wanted the last fritter: "Are we afraid?"

And the tavern — the schoolteacher with the ledger, the dockhand with the enemy's logo on his chest, the rope-seller, the harbor master, the ferry pilot, the tavern keeper, and a dozen others whose names were in that brass book or whose parents' names were — answered, in a ragged chorus that firmed up as it went, the way a crowd learns it means what it's saying:

"No."

"Then we stand together," said Madame Okonkwo. "And somebody get Tomas out of that jacket. He can't plan a fight wearing their flag."

Tomas looked down at the logo. Then he took off the jacket, folded it with dockworker neatness, and set it on the fritter tray, where Madame Okonkwo regarded it with distaste and, after a moment, dropped a fritter on it, as if to bury it with honors.

* * *

The tavern emptied slowly, in knots of two and three, voices low and quick, plans already breeding in the rain. Amara wrapped the ledger in oilcloth. Odile stood, stretched, and caught Rafael's eye across the room.

Rafael had already drifted back to the doorway. It was where Rafael lived — the threshold, the half-in. Odile crossed the wet floor and stood beside them, both of them looking out at the rain hammering the harbor, the ferry riding its moorings, the dark line of the river beyond.

"You could have stayed in the corner," Odile said. "You talked instead."

"They were looking at me."

"Everybody was looking at everybody. That's what a town *is*." She paused. "You said *I've seen what's left*. Past tense. You've run into these men before."

Rafael said nothing, which was an answer.

"Rafael." Odile's voice was quiet under the rain. "Whatever it is you're carrying besides the chest — the town just voted to stand together. That word includes you. Whether you've earned it or not. That's the whole point of the word."

Rafael looked at the harbor, at the ferry, at the town that had, in one evening, decided to stop being afraid of a shipping empire — and Rafael, who had a warrant with their name on it and eleven years of exits memorized, felt the terrifying weight of being included in a *we*.

Across the water, far downriver, a single running light appeared through the rain. Then another. Two fast boats, running dark except for their navigation lights, moving upriver toward Brasse-Lune at a speed no honest cargo ever traveled.

Odile saw them a half-second after Rafael did.

"Those aren't fishermen," she said.

"No," Rafael said. "They're not."
