## 13. First Blood on the Water

The trouble with a town that runs on gossip, I learned that morning, is that gossip runs faster than boats.

I was at Odile's dock, re-tying the chest's tarp for the fourth time — it had developed opinions about the wind — when the schoolteacher's boy came pelting down the lane, barefoot, shouting before he was even in earshot. By the time his words arrived they'd been relayed through three grandmothers and a dog, but the core of it survived translation: two strangers in gray jackets had been asking at the fuel dock about me. Again. And this time they weren't asking politely.

"They've got a fast boat," the boy said, hands on knees, breathing like a bellows. "Black hull. Nobody's seen it before."

"Nobody ever sees the black hulls," said old Maret from her porch, without looking up from her mending. "That's the whole point of them."

I looked at Odile. She was coiling a rope with the deliberate calm of a woman who had already decided something and was simply waiting for the rest of us to catch up.

"How long to move the chest?" she asked.

"Move it where?"

"Somewhere they can't burn it."

* * *

Here is what I knew by noon, assembled from the town's excellent intelligence network: Corvane had stopped pretending. The lawyer with the soft voice and the soft briefcase had gone back downriver two days ago, and everyone agreed this meant one of two things — either they'd gotten what they wanted, or they'd decided to stop asking. The black-hulled boat had been seen twice at the mouth of the channel, sitting idle, the way a cat sits idle near a bird feeder.

And here is what I felt, which was different knowledge entirely: the old itch between my shoulder blades. Ten years of reading water and weather and men, and my body had its own ledger, and every entry said *go*. Pack the chest, run at night, be a rumor by dawn.

But the patched hull wouldn't survive open current in storm season, and I'd proven that once already, expensively. And there was Odile, who had hauled me out of the river twice and never once asked what I'd do for her. And there was the tavern keeper, who'd started leaving an extra bowl of stew near my usual table without being asked, as if belonging were a thing you could just set down next to a person and let them decide.

Running would cost more than staying. For the first time in my life, the arithmetic came out that way.

So we didn't run. We hid the chest instead — badly, temporarily, under the floorboards of Odile's ferry cabin, beneath a false layer of netting and fish boxes that fooled nobody who'd ever actually worked a deck, but which bought us the appearance of a ferry that was only a ferry.

It lasted until dusk.

* * *

They came at slack water, which any harbor child could have told them was amateur hour for cutting moorings — no current to carry a loose boat away, everything just drifts in place like furniture in a flooded room. But maybe they wanted us to see. Maybe that was the point.

I heard the skiff first. Not Corvane's black hull — a little open boat, someone's livelihood, tied at the far end of the dock. Then I heard the sound every river person knows in their spine: a knife going through wet rope. A short, fibrous tearing, almost gentle.

Then another. And another.

By the time I got outside, half the mooring lines on the north side of the dock were floating free, curling on the water like severed hair. Boats were knocking against each other, fenders squealing, somebody's grandfather out in his undershirt hauling on a line and swearing with impressive range. And at the end of the pier stood two men in gray jackets, dry despite the drizzle, watching. Not running. Watching. One of them held a jerry can.

The skiff went up like a struck match.

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that moment, because it wasn't heroism. It was cold, clarifying fear — the kind that strips away every plan you've been hiding behind. They hadn't come for the chest. They couldn't have known where it was; I barely knew. They'd come to say: *we can reach anything you love, anything you touch, anything tied to this town.* The skiff belonged to the mechanic's nephew. It wasn't even mine. That was the message. Nothing needed to be mine. Proximity was enough.

The fire lit the rain orange. People came running with buckets and hooks and the particular furious competence of people who have fought water their whole lives and are not impressed by a little flame. The skiff was lost — fiberglass burns fast and hot — but nobody else got hurt, because the town moved like one animal with forty hands.

And the two men in gray turned and walked back down the pier, unhurried, and their boat took them away into the dark, and the whole time not one person in Brasse-Lune stepped aside for them. Maret stood on her porch with a lantern held high, an old woman silhouetted against burning rain, and I swear the enforcers detoured around her light like it was a wall.

"Message received," Odile said quietly, standing beside me, soaked through. "Now we send one back."

* * *

Our answer, decided in the smoky aftermath while the mechanic's nephew stared at the spot where his skiff used to be, was simple: the chest could not stay anywhere findable. Not the ferry, not my boat, not any building in town, because buildings burn and boats get boarded and the black hull would be back.

But there was the quarry.

Everyone in Brasse-Lune knew the quarry — a flooded limestone pit half a mile upriver, thirty meters deep at the center, cold as a held breath even in summer, with ledges and tunnels where the old workings had collapsed into the water table. Kids dared each other to swim it. Divers avoided it, because the silt on the bottom was famous for swallowing visibility whole if you so much as waved a fin at it wrong.

Which made it, Odile pointed out with grim satisfaction, the perfect bank.

"We sink it deep, mark it quiet, and it stays there until we're ready," she said. "No patrol is going to spend days dragging a quarry on a rumor."

"And if they do?"

"Then the silt eats them alive and they come up blind and humiliated, and we'll have earned some entertainment at least."

We planned it like people planning a funeral — somberly, thoroughly, with everyone contributing and nobody quite saying aloud that we were burying the thing that had brought us all together. The mechanic donated lift bags. The retired harbor master drew the quarry's underwater layout from memory on the back of a supply manifest, which should not have surprised me and did anyway, because apparently everyone in this town had once done something remarkable and mentioned it only when useful. Even the schoolteacher showed up, ostensibly to supervise, actually because he'd heard "flooded quarry" and "night dive" in the same sentence and wanted witnesses in case we drowned.

The chest itself weighed more than grief. Steel, waterlogged contents, forty years of river silt in every seam — the four of us wrestled it onto the ferry's deck with a block and tackle and language I won't repeat. We rigged it with two lift bags and a descent line anchored to the quarry's north ledge, and we waited for full dark, because the last thing we needed was some kid on a dare swimming past a descending steel box and telling the whole river about it by breakfast.

* * *

Diving the quarry at night is a specific kind of madness. In the river, even in flood, there's current to tell you which way is forward, surface light to tell you which way is up. In the quarry there is nothing. You descend into water the color of strong tea and the temperature of a grudge, and your light makes a small pale room around you and beyond that room is the rest of your life.

Odile stayed topside handling the line signals — she'd grown up on this water but had a healthy respect for the quarry, which she described as "the river's meaner cousin." The mechanic worked the lift bags. I rode down with the chest, guiding it along the anchor line toward the ledge at eighteen meters where we'd wedged it under a slab of fallen limestone, tucked like a secret under a tongue.

Eighteen meters in black tea. My light found the ledge. The chest swung gently on its bags, obedient, almost peaceful. I remember thinking, absurdly, that after everything — the navy, the enforcers, the lawyer with his soft offers — the chest's hardest journey was this last calm ten meters down. I guided it under the overhang. The mechanic bled the lift bags. The box settled onto the limestone with a sound I felt more than heard, a deep stone-on-stone murmur, like the quarry accepting delivery.

And then my safety line snagged.

Not dramatically. That's the thing about drowning — the movies have lied to you. There's no thrashing, no dramatic gasp. There's a moment where you reach for the line and it doesn't give, and you check it calmly, professionally, the way you've checked a hundred fouled lines, and then you check again, and then the calm peels away all at once and underneath it is just you, alone, in the dark, in the cold, tethered to a wreck in a hole in the earth.

The line had run under the slab. When the chest settled, it had pinned the line against the limestone — my own descent line, my own rigging, my own careful plan, turned into a leash. I was fifteen meters from air with maybe six minutes of bottom time left, and the more I pulled, the tighter it cinched.

I did everything right. That's what I keep coming back to. I didn't panic-breathe, didn't shoot for the surface, didn't do any of the things that kill divers faster than the water does. I worked the problem. Knife out — the line was too far under the slab to cut where it was pinned. So I went down to it, flat against the limestone, cheek to cold stone, working my body into the gap like a hand into a glove, and the silt rose around me in a brown blizzard and took my light and my bearings and most of my hope.

In the whiteout, with the line finally in reach and my air gauge a rumor, I had a thought that wasn't a thought so much as a fact presenting itself: *this is what running costs.* Every year of it. Every harbor abandoned, every name given falsely, every job taken and fled. It had all been this — a slow tether, tightening, and I'd called it freedom because I kept moving before it could pull taut.

Well. It was taut now.

I sawed through the line on the third try, and the freed end whipped away into the dark like something released from a promise, and I kicked for the surface following the only direction I had left, which was the direction my bubbles went. I broke the surface somewhere in the middle of the quarry, nowhere near the line, coughing, half-blind, alive.

Odile found me with the spotlight in under a minute. She later claimed it was skill. The mechanic claimed it was luck. The schoolteacher, who had been quietly keeping count of our bottom times on the manifest, claimed it was mathematics. All three of them pulled me over the gunwale simultaneously, wrapped me in every dry thing on the boat, and began arguing about whose fault it was in the warmest possible terms, which I have since learned is how this town says *we're glad you're not dead.*

"You went back under the slab," Odile said, kneeling in front of me, gripping my shoulders hard enough to matter. "For the line. Why? The line's replaceable."

I didn't have an answer ready. The true one arrived late, the way truth does.

"It was tied off right," I said. "Everything was done right. It still nearly killed me." I was shivering too hard for eloquence. "I guess I wanted to know if it was my fault."

She studied me for a long moment, rain ticking on the cabin roof behind her.

"And was it?"

"No."

"Good." She said it like a verdict, like a door closing on a cell I'd lived in for years. "Because Corvane will tell you everything that goes wrong is your fault. That's how they keep people small. Don't help them."

* * *

We came back into the harbor near midnight, exhausted, smelling of quarry water and lamp oil, and the town was waiting. Not all of them — but enough. The tavern keeper had kept the kitchen open. Maret was on her porch with the lantern again, and as we passed she raised it, once, deliberately, the way you'd salute.

Word of the burned skiff had spread, of course, and with it word of what we'd done tonight, in outline if not detail. Nobody asked where the chest was. That was the part that undid me, sitting on the ferry's deck eating stew at one in the morning: nobody asked. Forty people knew we'd hidden something in the quarry, and the question never once crossed anyone's lips, because in Brasse-Lune a secret held for the town was the town's secret, full stop. I had spent ten years assuming every secret was a liability to be carried alone. These people carried it like a bucket line — passed hand to hand, lighter for everyone.

The mechanic sat down across from me, wrung out his cap, and said, "Nephew wants to know if Corvane pays for skiffs."

"I doubt it."

"Then we'll bill them another way." He said it mildly, the way you'd discuss weather, and went to refill his bowl.

I sat there in the lamplight, still shivering, still tasting quarry, and understood that something in me had finished changing somewhere between the slab and the surface. The old instinct was still there — it always would be, probably, a scar that itches before rain. But for the first time I could look at it clearly and see it for what it was: not wisdom. Just fear with a good vocabulary.

There was no version of leaving anymore. Not because the chest was buried and I couldn't take it — I could dig it up, I could run tomorrow, the option technically existed. But because I'd seen Maret hold her lantern high against armed men, and the mechanic talk about billing a shipping empire like an unpaid tab, and forty people keep a stranger's secret without being asked. Running from Corvane had always been possible. Running from *this* —

I put down the spoon.

Across the harbor, tied up at the fuel dock where it had no business being, riding low and silent with no lights showing, sat a black-hulled boat.

Two figures stood on its deck. They weren't looking at us.

They were looking at the quarry road.
