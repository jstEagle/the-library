## 14. Allies in Odd Places

Odile Marchand had spent her whole life learning which people you could count on in a storm, and it had taught her one thing above all others: they almost never looked like the people you'd have chosen.

She stood in the doorway of Baptiste's repair yard, watching the rain come down in sheets that turned the world beyond the awning into a gray smear, and she made a list in her head. Not a list of names — not yet. A list of qualities. She needed someone who could keep a boat running when the boat had every reason to quit. Someone who knew the harbor's moods better than the charts did. And someone, if it came to it, who was already inside Corvane's walls and might be persuaded to look the other way for an hour.

Three people. That was all the plan could afford. More than three and the secret would spread like bilgewater; fewer than three and it would die like a candle in the wind.

Baptiste was the first name on the list, mostly because he was standing right there, elbow-deep in an outboard motor, complaining to it in a language that wasn't quite French and wasn't quite anything else.

"You're going to want to hear this," Odile said, and he looked up with the expression of a man who had heard everything and believed half of it.

He wiped his hands on a rag that had been gray since before Odile was born and said, "If it's about your ferry's engine, I already know. It knows too. We've been having conversations about it."

"It's not about the engine." She glanced over her shoulder, an old habit, then stepped inside and let the awning drip behind her. "It's about a chest."

Baptiste's eyebrows went up slowly, like two caterpillars being asked to consider something serious. "The chest. The dead nation's chest. The one the diver pulled out of the scuttled ferry, the one half the town has been speculating about at Madame Serafin's counter for a week, the one I personally told three separate customers I knew nothing about while knowing slightly more than nothing?"

"That's the one."

"Ah." He set down the rag. "Then before you say whatever you came here to say, I should tell you: my grandmother was born in Vessany. Left when she was nine. Used to sing songs about it to me when I couldn't sleep — songs about rivers, which I thought was strange for a country I'd never seen, until I got older and understood that where she came from, the river *was* the country. Roads were rumors. Rivers were promises." He looked at her with sudden, unexpected gravity. "So. What's in the chest?"

Odile told him. Not everything — she kept Rafael's warrant out of it, kept the quarry hiding spot vague, kept the parts of the plan that didn't exist yet firmly nonexistent — but enough. The ledger. The names. The fact that some of those names belonged to people Baptiste repaired boats for, drank coffee with, had known his whole life. Land titles seized forty years ago by a man named Corvane, whose company now owned the depot upriver and half the dock leases in between.

Baptiste listened the way he listened to engines: quietly, thoroughly, and with his hands moving the entire time, as though stillness made him suspicious of whatever he was hearing.

"So the paperwork exists," he said finally. "Signed, stamped, sealed under water for four decades, and it says the land under our feet was never really his."

"It says worse than that," Odile said. "It says he knew it was never his when he took it."

Baptiste nodded slowly. Then he did something Odile hadn't expected: he laughed. A short, startled laugh, the kind a man laughs when the joke is on him and everyone he loves.

"My mother spent eleven years petitioning the regional office for the lease on our own yard," he said. "Eleven years. They kept losing the file. Then they found the file but the signature page was missing. Then they found the signature page but it had been signed by someone whose title didn't cover our parcel. Eleven years of that. My mother died thinking she'd done something wrong — filled out a form crooked, maybe, or used the wrong color ink." He shook his head. "Turns out the ink was fine. The whole inkwell was stolen."

"So you're in?"

"I'm in the way I'm always in," Baptiste said, "which is completely, and with opinions." He held up a finger. "Opinion one: whoever moves that chest moves it on a boat I've inspected. I don't care whose boat. If it sinks with the ledger aboard, I will consider it a personal insult, and I hold grudges the way my outboards hold compression — stubbornly, and past all reason." Another finger. "Opinion two: we do it the night of the worst storm, because Corvane's patrol boys are brave men in fair weather and furniture in foul. I've watched them tie up at the fuel dock in rain you wouldn't send a cat out in. They hunker. Hunkering men miss things."

"And opinion three?" Odile asked.

Baptiste leaned in, voice dropping to the register he used for confidential engine troubles. "Opinion three: you're going to need a third person, and I know exactly who, and you're going to hate it."

* * *

The retired harbor master lived in a house at the end of the point, in a chair he'd reupholstered himself using sailcloth from a boat that hadn't floated since Odile was twelve. His name was Anselme Roche, and he had run Brasse-Lune's harbor for thirty-one years before Corvane's depot made his job redundant, which was a polite way of saying that a company with its own cranes and its own security no longer needed anyone whose job was knowing things.

Anselme still knew things.

Odile brought him a bottle of the plum brandy he liked and sat down across from him without preamble, because preamble with Anselme was just a way of giving him time to decide he didn't want visitors.

"I need the channels," she said. "All of them. Especially the ones that aren't on the charts anymore."

Anselme poured two glasses without being asked, which was itself a message. "Which ones aren't on the charts anymore?"

"The ones you took off the charts yourself, back when you were harbor master, because they were too dangerous for strangers and too useful for friends."

He smiled the slow smile of a man who has been waiting years for someone to ask the right question. "Ah. You've been talking to Baptiste."

"I've been talking to everyone," Odile said. "It's a hazard of the profession."

"Then you know what I'm going to say, so let me save us both the small talk." He picked up his glass and set it down again without drinking, a habit of caution Odile had never quite understood and had long ago stopped trying to. "The old quarry channel is real. It runs deep enough for your ferry at any tide, and it bends twice in ways that will make a patrol boat's radar lie to it. I removed it from the charts in my fourth year, after a barge captain who didn't listen to me put thirty meters of steel on the rocks. The rocks are still there. The barge isn't. But the channel remembers, and so do I."

"And you'll pilot it? Or tell me how?"

"I'll do neither," Anselme said, "because I'm seventy-four years old and my night vision is a rumor maintained by pride." He paused, and then, with the timing of a man who'd spent three decades negotiating with shipping companies and enjoyed it more than he admitted: "But I'll sit in your wheelhouse and shout directions at you, and I'll be right every single time, and afterward I'll deny having been there at all. That's the arrangement. You get the knowledge. I get plausible deniability. Everyone gets what they're best at."

Odile felt something loosen in her chest. "You'd do that? For names in a ledger you've never read?"

"I've read the situation," Anselme said. "Thirty-one years of watching Corvane's predecessor buy this river piece by piece, always politely, always through lawyers, always leaving the previous owner with just enough money to move away and not enough to come back. I watched families leave. I watched their houses get sold to foremen. I watched the school lose children every year until they stopped teaching the upper grades." He refilled his glass, and this time he drank. "I am too old to be angry properly. Anger needs somewhere to go, and mine has nowhere left. But spite — spite keeps beautifully at my age. Like plum brandy. This plan is the most interesting thing to happen to my spite in a decade, and I intend to see it through."

He looked at her over the rim of the glass. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When it's over — when the magistrate has it, when the reading happens, whenever the town finds out — I want to be in the room. Not speaking. Just present. Thirty-one years I watched things happen to this harbor that I reported accurately and completely, and every report went into a drawer somewhere and stayed there. I want to watch one thing happen that doesn't stay in a drawer."

Odile promised him the room. It was the easiest promise she'd ever made, and somehow the heaviest.

* * *

The third person was the hard one, exactly as Baptiste had predicted, and exactly as Baptiste had promised, Odile hated it.

His name was Tomas Ferreol, and he was twenty-three years old, and he worked deck crew on a Corvane cargo barge, the *Meridian*, which ran supplies upriver twice a week and patrol gossip downriver slightly more often than that. He was broad-shouldered and quiet and had a scar through his left eyebrow that he'd gotten falling off a ladder at fourteen, though the version told at the tavern involved a fight with a customs officer, which Tomas had never confirmed or denied because confirming or denying either would require conversation, and Tomas avoided conversation the way other people avoided taxes.

He was also, Odile had learned from three separate sources she trusted, the nephew of Elise Ferreol, whose fishing rights appeared in the ledger under entry forty-one, seized along with two hundred meters of shoreline that now hosted Corvane's fuel tanks.

Baptiste had flagged him months ago, back when this was all speculation: *"That boy notices everything and reports nothing. Watch him."*

Odile had watched him. She'd watched him stack cargo in the rain with a precision that suggested he cared about the work even if he didn't care about the company. She'd watched him, twice, glance at the patrol skiffs' mooring times and then look away immediately, as though he'd noticed something worth noticing and decided, consciously, not to keep it. She'd watched him eat alone at Madame Serafin's counter, always at the late hour, always the same order, always facing the window.

And last Tuesday, during the storm that had knocked the power out for six hours, she'd watched him do something she couldn't explain: he'd walked to the harbor office in the rain, alone, and stood outside it for ten minutes, looking at the notice board. The notice board had nothing new on it. It had had nothing new on it for weeks. But he'd stood there anyway, in weather no sensible person stood in, reading notices he must have read a hundred times.

People do strange things when they're looking for something they can't name. Odile knew that better than most. She'd spent a year after her grandmother died walking the same stretch of shoreline every evening, telling herself she was checking the nets, knowing perfectly well she was looking for something the water had taken and wouldn't give back.

So she went to find him. Not at the tavern, where he never went. At the barge dock, at the end of his shift, in the rain, because Tomas Ferreol was a man you met in weather, apparently, or not at all.

She found him securing a line with knots so correct they were almost ostentatious, and she stood at the edge of the gangway until he noticed her, which took longer than it should have, because he was expecting trouble and she didn't look like trouble. She looked like a ferry pilot holding an umbrella she hadn't opened.

"You're Elise Ferreol's nephew," she said.

He looked at her the way he looked at most direct statements, which was briefly and then away. "Everyone's somebody's nephew."

"Entry forty-one," she said. "Two hundred meters of shoreline. Your family fished it for three generations before it became a fuel depot."

Something shifted in his face. Not much. A degree, maybe two. But Odile had spent years reading water and had learned to read faces the same way — not the big movements, which anyone could fake, but the small ones, the ones that happened below the threshold of intention. His eyes had gone very still. Stillness, in a young man who was usually in motion, meant something had landed.

"How do you know that?" he asked.

"Because I've read a ledger that says so, and because your aunt used to fish the north bank every morning until the tanks went in, and I remember her skiff sitting there when I was learning to run the ferry. Blue hull. Painted over twice. She sold the engine before she sold the boat, which tells you she expected to need the boat again."

Tomas absorbed this in silence. Then: "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I think you already know most of it," Odile said, "and because I think you've been standing outside the harbor office in storms reading a notice board that never changes, and I don't think you're reading the notices."

He didn't answer. He finished the knot he was tying — correctly, of course — and then he looked at her directly for the first time, which for Tomas constituted a significant escalation.

"What do you want from me?"

"One night," she said. "One night, probably the worst night of the season, when the patrols hunker down and nobody with a Corvane badge wants to be outside. I need to know what the night shift does in that weather. Where they go. How long the depot is effectively unwatched. Whether the crane runs in heavy rain or whether they lock it down."

"That's it?"

"That's the shape of it. There's more, but that's the part that matters."

Tomas considered this for a long moment, standing in the rain with his collar dripping, and Odile realized she was holding her breath, which was foolish, because she'd already decided she would understand if he said no. He was twenty-three. He had a job with a company that owned the dock his aunt's skiff used to tie up at. Saying yes cost him something real.

"Yes," he said. "But not for the ledger."

"No?"

"For my aunt." He looked downriver, toward the tanks, toward the shoreline that had been hers. "She's eighty-one now. Lives with my cousin up past the school. She hasn't been down to the water in nine years — her knees, she says, but I've seen her knees, and I've seen her look at the water from the kitchen window, and I don't think it's only her knees. I think it's that the water she looks at isn't hers anymore, and she can't stand to walk down and confirm it." He paused. "If this puts her shoreline back in her name — even on paper, even just long enough for her to see the paper — then I'll tell you everything the night shift does in bad weather. Everything. Including the parts that would get me fired."

Odile extended her hand, and Tomas, after a pause that lasted precisely as long as it needed to, shook it.

* * *

They met that evening in the back room of Baptiste's yard, among the spare propellers and the winter tarps, because it was the only place in Brasse-Lune where four people could talk without the tavern windows overhearing them. Anselme arrived with his brandy and his charts — the real charts, hand-annotated, the ones that had never been filed anywhere official. Baptiste arrived with coffee and a disassembled carburetor he claimed to be working on, which fooled no one, including presumably the carburetor. Tomas arrived last, wet from the shift change, carrying a notebook he'd bought that afternoon, which was the single most hopeful object Odile had seen in months: a man who intended to write things down was a man who intended to follow through.

Rafael was there too, sitting slightly apart, the way Rafael always sat slightly apart, listening more than speaking. When Odile laid out the plan — move the chest from the flooded quarry on the storm's worst night, run it up the hidden channel to the old icehouse landing, transfer it to the ferry, hold it ready for the depot gambit — Rafael raised exactly one objection.

"The quarry dive," Rafael said. "In storm runoff, visibility will be zero. I nearly drowned retrieving it the first time, in calm water. Doing it in flood conditions, solo, with a snagged-line risk—" Rafael shook their head. "I need a second diver on the surface line. Even a bad one. Even someone who just holds the rope and counts."

Baptiste raised his hand. "I count well. I also panic loudly, which in a counting context may actually be useful."

"You'd get in the water?"

"I'd get in up to my neck," Baptiste said, "holding a rope and a lantern and a thermos, in that order of priority. I have no diving certification whatsoever, but I have forty years of pulling drowning tourists out of swimming holes, and I know what a panicked diver sounds like from the surface, which is the most important skill in this operation and possibly in life."

Anselme cleared his throat. "And I'll want the ferry's route planned to the minute. Not approximately. To the minute. Storms have rhythms — the squall bands come through in pulses, eight to twelve minutes apart, and between pulses there are gaps where the rain thins enough to see fifty meters instead of five. A crossing timed to the gaps looks reckless from shore. A crossing timed badly looks like a wreck waiting to be investigated. We will be neither."

"We'll need to know the patrol schedule," Odile said. "That's Tomas."

Tomas opened his notebook to the first page and wrote, in careful block letters: *NIGHT SHIFT, WEATHER PROTOCOL.* Underneath: *Questions to answer by Friday.*

He read them aloud, because Tomas did everything aloud when he did it at all, and the questions were better than any of them had hoped. Did the patrol skiffs pull their radios in heavy rain, or run silent? Which door of the depot did the night manager use, and did he lock the others? Was the crane operator on-site overnight or called in? And — the question none of them had thought to ask — did the enforcers' fast boat have a heater, because if it did, they'd hunker aboard it rather than ashore, and if it didn't, they'd hunker ashore, and those were two entirely different problems.

"Heater," Baptiste said. "That's the kind of detail that wins or loses nights like this."

"That's why he's here," Odile said.

By the time the rain on the roof had drummed them all into a kind of shared drowsiness, the plan had edges. Rafael would dive the quarry on the appointed night, with Baptiste on the surface line and the thermos. Anselme would ride the ferry's wheelhouse and call the channel by memory against the chart in his lap, denying everything afterward. Odile would fly the boat. Tomas would deliver the patrol schedule by Friday, written in his block letters, delivered folded inside a copy of the shipping manifest where no one would think to look.

Nobody said the word *family*. It wasn't that kind of group, not yet, maybe not ever. But when the meeting broke up and they stood in the yard doorway watching the rain come down, Anselme said, "Same time Thursday," and Baptiste said, "I'll bring the good coffee this time, not the stuff I give customers I'm trying to upsell," and Tomas said, "Friday, then," and folded his notebook into his jacket, and Rafael — who had said almost nothing all evening — paused at the door and looked back at the four of them standing in the lamplight, and said quietly:

"I've been running alone for a long time. I'd forgotten what it looks like when people plan around each other instead of past each other."

Then Rafael left, and Odile stood in the doorway a moment longer, and thought about her grandmother, who had spent the last years of her life telling stories about a country that no longer existed to a child who hadn't known to remember them — and who had, without either of them knowing it, been assembling exactly this: a handful of strangers bound together by names in a ledger, planning something dangerous in a boatyard during a storm.

"She knew," Odile murmured to the rain. "Somehow, she knew it would take all of us."

* * *

Thursday's meeting was shorter, sharper, and colder, because the storm had turned serious overnight and the barometer had fallen so far that Baptiste's yard clock — a barometer disguised as a clock, a trick he'd learned from his father — had begun predicting the weather faster than the radio did.

Tomas delivered.

He unfolded the manifest copy at the table's center, and there, in block letters pressed hard enough to emboss the paper beneath, was everything. Patrol skiffs ran silent in heavy rain — radios pulled, engines cold, crews rotated below. The night manager used the east door and locked the rest at sundown. The crane operator was day-shift only; overnight, the depot's lifting capacity was zero. And the fast boat had no heater. The enforcers hunkered ashore, in the barracks above the fuel office, which meant that on the worst night of the storm, the entire Corvane presence on the water would consist of one cold, empty, anchored fast boat and however many bored men could be persuaded to peek out a barrack window at intervals.

"They check the quarry road once an hour," Tomas added. "On foot. One man. He cuts the walk short in rain — I've timed it, eleven minutes instead of twenty-five. And he carries no light, because the light draws attention to how little he can see."

Anselme studied the schedule the way he'd once studied tide tables, and pronounced it, in the flat authoritative voice of thirty-one years of authority: "We go between the second and third squall band. Window of nine minutes, maybe eleven if the front stalls. That's enough for the transfer at the icehouse landing if — and only if — the chest comes up from the quarry dry-side ready, slung and waiting, no rigging done on the water."

"Then we rig it beforehand," Rafael said. "Tonight. I'll go down and sling it to a lift point, test the lines, mark the ascent path with a weighted guide rope. All the work that can be done early gets done early. On the night, it's descend, clip, lift, breathe."

Baptiste volunteered his winch barge — the ugly one, the one he used for hauling sunken outboards, the one nobody in town would question seeing anchored near the quarry, because it was always anchored near the quarry, it was practically a landmark. "It has a boom rated for four hundred kilos," he said. "The chest weighs, what, a hundred and sixty dry? Two hundred wet? We're fine. We're gloriously fine. I checked the rating twice because I wanted to watch my own face when I confirmed it."

Odile laid out the ferry leg: icehouse landing to the holding berth behind Madame Serafin's boathouse, run through the hidden channel with Anselme calling marks. Total exposure on open water: under four minutes, if the squall gaps cooperated.

"And if they don't cooperate?" Tomas asked.

"Then we wait for the next gap," Anselme said. "Patience is free. Wrecks are expensive."

The room settled into the particular quiet of a plan that has stopped being hypothetical. Odile felt it settle over her like ballast — that specific heaviness, comforting and frightening at once, of knowing that soon the talking would be over and the doing would begin, and the doing would happen at night, in water, in weather, with everything they cared about suspended between competence and catastrophe.

She looked around the table. Baptiste, who had lost eleven years and a mother to a stolen file, testing a winch shackle with his thumb. Anselme, who had waited a decade for his reports to matter, annotating a chart he'd sworn never to show anyone. Tomas, who had shaken her hand for his aunt's sake, copying tide corrections into his notebook in handwriting neat enough to be a form of prayer. And Rafael, sitting slightly apart as always, but less apart than last week — close enough now that when Rafael spoke, the table leaned in.

"Well," Baptiste said, capping the coffee thermos with unnecessary ceremony. "I believe that's everything except the actual courage, which cannot be scheduled and will have to show up on its own."

"It usually does," Anselme said. "Courage is punctual that way. It knows what night it's needed."

They agreed on the signal for the appointed night: a single green lamp hung at the icehouse landing, visible only from the water, lit only when the quarry team had the chest slung and the squall gap confirmed. No lamp, no crossing. Simple as a tide table, and almost as reliable.

As they gathered their things, Tomas lingered. He rolled up the manifest, tucked it inside his jacket, and said, quietly, to Odile alone:

"My aunt asked me today why I've been coming home late. I didn't tell her anything. But she looked at me the way she used to look at the water, and she said, 'Whatever it is, be careful, and don't do it for money.' " He shrugged, the shrug of a young man embarrassed by his own honesty. "I told her I wasn't doing it for money. I don't think she believed me. I think she believed *in* me, which is different, and better."

Odile watched him walk out into the rain, toward the barge dock, toward the company that owned his hours and not, as of Thursday night, his conscience.

And she thought: *this is how it happens. Not with oaths. With notebooks, and thermoses, and aunts, and green lamps.*

* * *

The storm announced its worst night two days later, the way storms announce things: all at once, and then in installments. The barometer fell off the bottom of Baptiste's gauge. The radio issued warnings with numbers in them. And the enforcers, true to Tomas's intelligence, began hunkering — their skiffs tied double, their fast boat riding empty at anchor, their barracks windows glowing with the particular amber of men playing cards because going outside had stopped being an option.

At noon, Odile stood at the ferry dock and watched the river turn the color of gunmetal, and she felt the plan compress around her like pressure ahead of a front. Everything was rigged. The guide rope was set in the quarry. The winch barge was anchored. The charts were marked. The manifest with the patrol schedule was folded in her wheelhouse locker, next to the flare kit she prayed they wouldn't need.

All that remained was the lamp, and the window, and the crossing.

Rafael found her there, soaked through despite every garment designed to prevent it, and said the thing she'd been turning over herself all morning:

"Do you ever think about how much of this depends on people showing up? Not being brave — just showing up. Baptiste at his winch. Anselme in the wheelhouse. Tomas walking his eleven-minute rounds and choosing to walk them slowly. Me at the bottom of a quarry. You at a wheel. None of it works if one person decides the rain is too honest a reason to stay home."

"All the time," Odile said. "Every plan I've ever loved has been held together by attendance."

Rafael almost smiled. "Attendance. That's the word for it. Not loyalty, not courage. Attendance." They looked out at the water. "My whole life I've assumed showing up was the easy part. Turns out it's the whole job."

The afternoon went long and dark early, the way it does before a big blow, and by four o'clock the first squall band hit with a sound like the river being slapped by something enormous. Odile secured the ferry double, checked the icehouse landing lamp — unlit, waiting, its oil reservoir full, its wick trimmed — and went home to wait out the hours in the particular agony of a pilot who has done everything except the thing.

She slept badly, in the manner of all pilots before crossings, waking at intervals to check the wind by feel against the shutters, counting the squall bands as they passed overhead like freight trains. First band: ninety seconds of fury, then thinning. Second band: longer, meaner, the one Anselme had warned carried the front's true weight. Between them, gaps — brief, gray, precious, the river visible for fifty meters at a stretch, the far bank a rumor, the near bank a certainty.

At 2:40 in the morning, the knock came.

Not a loud knock. Baptiste didn't believe in loud knocks; he believed knocks were like engines, best delivered at the exact required force. Three soft raps, spaced evenly, and then silence long enough to confirm it wasn't the wind.

Odile opened her door onto a wall of rain and a silhouette holding a lantern with a green glass chimney.

"The gap's confirmed," Baptiste said, shouting over the weather at a volume calibrated to reach her without reaching the street. "Second band passed at 2:31. Anselme reads the next window opening at 2:52 and holding nine minutes, maybe eleven. Rafael's at the quarry. The chest is slung. The lamp is lit."

The lamp was lit. Somewhere downriver, at the old icehouse landing, a single green flame was burning inside glass, steady against the wind, saying the one word it existed to say: *ready.*

Odile dressed in the dark, because dressing in the dark was a pilot's skill and she'd been practicing it since she was sixteen. Oilskins. Boots. The wheelhouse locker key on a lanyard around her neck, where keys lived during operations, because pockets failed and necks didn't.

She walked to the dock through rain that had stopped being weather and become a medium, something you moved through the way fish move through water. The ferry lay at her berth, riding her lines, patient as a horse in a stable. Odile cast off the bow line first, then the stern, then stepped aboard and started the engine, and the engine — Baptiste's engine, rebuilt twice, cursed at weekly, loved unreasonably — caught on the second try, which in these conditions qualified as a miracle and would be described as such at Madame Serafin's counter for years.

Anselme was already in the wheelhouse. Of course he was. He'd arrived at midnight, he said later, because a man who has waited ten years for his spite to matter does not arrive at a reasonable hour. He sat in the corner seat with the annotated chart on his knee and a pencil behind his ear and the expression of a professor proctoring an exam he fully expected his student to pass.

"Channel marks," he said, tapping the chart. "First bend at the white stake — gone now, washed out in '09, but the bottom shelf is still there, you'll feel it in the wheel before you see it. Second bend hard to port at the drowned pilings. After that, straight shot to the icehouse. Total distance, eleven hundred meters. In the gaps, you'll make it in four minutes. In the rain, seven. Do not attempt it in the rain."

"I won't attempt it in the rain," Odile said, and meant it, and would spend the next twenty minutes discovering what it cost to keep meaning it.
