## 15. The Warrant Surfacing

The posters went up on a Tuesday, which in Brasse-Lune meant nothing and everything — Tuesdays were when the mail boat came, and the mail boat was when the town paid attention to paper.

I was mending nets with Tomas at the dock when the first one appeared, stapled to the pilings of the ferry landing at an angle that suggested whoever had done it was in a hurry or drunk or both. A face. My face, or close enough to it — grainy, blown up from some years-old photograph I didn't remember being taken, back when my hair still behaved and I hadn't yet learned what salt water did to it over time.

WANTED FOR QUESTIONING. SALVAGE THEFT. ASSAULT OF A VESSEL CREWMAN. REWARD OFFERED BY CORVANE SHIPPING. INFORMATION LEADING TO THE RECOVERY OF STOLEN ARTIFACTS.

Below all of it, in letters sized for people who read slowly: *Do you know this man?*

Tomas looked at the poster, then at me, then back at the poster, with the unhurried delight of a man who has just been handed the best gossip of his season.

"Well," he said. "You're famous."

"I'm not," I said. "That's not even — the hair's wrong."

"The hair's fine. The hair's very you." He folded the corner of his net over his hand like he was thinking about it. "Reward's how much?"

"Doesn't say. That's how you know it's insulting."

He laughed, and I laughed, because laughing was easier than standing there feeling the floor tilt under me. But underneath the joke I could feel the thing itself, cold and patient, the way the river is cold and patient in March. Corvane had stopped pretending. The lawyer — Vasseur, the one with the good shoes and the worse manners — had played his card, and the card was me.

* * *

By noon there were posters on every post in town. Literally every post. The lamp posts, the mooring posts, the post outside the bakery where Madame Oury hung her chalkboard specials, the post holding up the awning of the hardware store, which struck me as structurally irresponsible but nobody asked my opinion. Someone had even put one on the notice board between the lost-cat flyers and the announcement about the school's harvest recital, so that my face sat directly above a drawing of a cat named Biscuit who answered to no one.

Odile found me looking at that one.

"Good company," she said. "Biscuit's been missing since June. Whole town's kept an eye out. Nobody's found him."

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to tell you something about how this town treats things it's looking for." She tapped the poster with two fingers. "They look. They don't always find. And they don't turn on what they find just because someone paid for staples."

It was a kind thing to say. It was also, I noticed, said quickly, and she walked off before I could answer it, down the dock toward her ferry, moving like a woman with somewhere better to be. Which, knowing Odile, she did. Or she wanted me to think she did. With Odile those were usually the same thing.

* * *

The split came at supper, as splits do in river towns, where grievances are stored like preserves and opened on schedule.

I heard about it secondhand, mostly, because I'd made the mistake — or the choice, depending on who you asked — of eating at home that night. Canned beans, the good kind with the maplesyrup, eaten standing up at the little galley counter while rain ticked against the deck plates. But Brasse-Lune runs on gossip the way the tide runs on the moon, and by nine o'clock the accounts had reached me through three separate channels, each more vivid than the last.

What actually happened, as near as I could reconstruct it: at the tavern, old Marek — the retired harbor master, the one with the bad hip and the good memory — had stood up during the lull between card games and said, plainly, that a man with a warrant was a complication the town couldn't afford. That Corvane had already burned one skiff. That the next fire might not be aimed at a boat. That maybe it was time to ask our guest, politely, whether he might be more useful somewhere else.

And then Ameline Fournier — the schoolteacher, the one who'd read the ledger aloud in the first place, the one whose great-grandfather's fishing rights were sitting in that chest like a seed waiting forty years for rain — had stood up too, and said that if the town started handing people over every time a rich man stapled paper to a post, then the ledger wasn't worth reading, because we'd have proven the men who wrote it right.

And then the tavern had divided, roughly down the middle, the way taverns do, into the side that remembered the burned skiff and the side that remembered the seized warehouse, and both sides had been right, and both sides had raised their voices, and Madame Oury had reportedly ended the whole evening by announcing that the kitchen was closed to arguments and open to pie, and everyone had eaten pie, and nobody had resolved anything except that now everyone knew exactly where everyone else stood.

Which is its own kind of resolution, I suppose. Ugly, but honest.

* * *

I want to be honest here, in this account, because whatever else happens I'd like one honest record of myself to exist somewhere.

My deepest fear has never been drowning. Drowning is quick, and I've made my peace with the water; it's taken things from me but it's never lied. My deepest fear is this exact thing. A room full of people deciding, out loud, in front of each other, that I'm not worth the trouble. Not with violence. With arithmetic. With the quiet math of small towns everywhere: *he costs more than he brings.*

For fifteen years I've arranged my life so that no room would ever have enough information to do that math. I arrive after dark. I leave before introductions. I pay cash, I ask no favors, I remember birthdays of nobody. It's a lonely way to live but it's a safe one, and I told myself for a long time that loneliness was just the price of freedom, the way barnacles are the price of a hull.

Then Brasse-Lune happened to me. The storm happened. Odile happened. The ledger happened, and the names in it turned out to belong to people who saved me coffee without being asked and lent me tools without keeping count and invited me, twice, to things, actual invitations, written ones, with dates on them. And somewhere in there the math changed, because suddenly there were numbers on the other side of the equation, and I hadn't put them there. Other people had. That's the part that undid me. I could control what I cost. I couldn't control what I was worth to them.

And now the posters had given the room new information, and the math was running again, and I could hear it running, in the way people looked at me in the morning. Most of them the same as before. Some of them warmer, defiantly warmer, as if warmth were a weapon they could aim at Corvane. And a few — a handful, four or five that I counted, and I counted carefully — cooler. Not hostile. Just careful. The look of people who liked me fine but had boats of their own to think about.

I understood them. God help me, I understood them completely. If I were them, watching a stranger drag a dead nation's paperwork into my harbor with a shipping company's enforcers circling like sharks off the bar, I'd want him gone too. It's the sensible position. It's the position I've held my entire life about everyone.

It's a terrible position. I know that now. Knowing it doesn't make it hurt less to see it on other faces.

* * *

Day three of the posters, Vasseur himself came back.

Not to the tavern. Not to the dock. He came to the schoolhouse, which told me everything, because you don't go to a schoolhouse to threaten a fugitive. You go to a schoolhouse to threaten a community. He stood at the front of Ameline's classroom at the end of the day — the children sent home early, "boiler maintenance," a phrase I've come to understand means *an adult wants the building empty* — and he made his offer to the town through its teacher, politely, in writing, with copies.

The offer was this: Corvane would withdraw the warrant. All charges, all history, wiped clean. In exchange, the town would return the chest, decline further involvement, and encourage its guest to move along. There was a deadline. There was always a deadline. Men like Vasseur carry deadlines the way other men carry umbrellas.

Ameline read the letter aloud to the small crowd that gathered — word traveled fast, faster than the boiler could possibly have broken — and then she did something I'll remember longer than I'll remember my own name. She didn't argue with it. She held it up and said, "This is what fear looks like when it's typed on letterhead," and set it down on her desk next to the attendance book, and left it there, visible, all afternoon, like a specimen under glass.

People came to look at it. They filed past like it was a museum piece. Old Marek looked at it longest, and I watched him do it, standing across the street under the awning of the hardware store, pretending to price rope. When he finally crossed the road and went inside, I thought: there it is. The pivot. The moment a hard man decides which way the wind sits.

But I was wrong about which way he decided, or maybe I was wrong about how long it took him. Marek came out twenty minutes later and said nothing to anyone, and that night he was in the tavern again, and this time when the argument started he didn't stand up against me. He stood up beside the idea of me, which is different and worth more. "I've spent forty years watching Corvane take things from this river," he said, according to the fourth or fifth retelling I received. "A warrant is just the newest net they're casting. I don't help pull nets for men like that. Not for strangers. Not for neighbors either."

It wasn't a speech for me. It was a speech against them. But I've learned, late, that most courage works that way.

* * *

Still. Still.

Because here's the seam, the weak place in the hull of all this belonging, and I can't write around it: the town can vote to keep me, and the vote won't stop a match. The skiff that burned was a warning, and warnings escalate. Every day I stay, the price of me goes up, and the town pays it in paint and timber and nerves, and eventually somebody's child is going to be standing too close to something that burns. I've done the arithmetic myself, lying awake in the berth with the rain drumming, and my own math says leave. My own math has always said leave. Leave is the only skill I've ever mastered, and I am, I will admit it, genuinely excellent at it.

So on the fifth morning, before the fog lifted, I started packing.

Not dramatically. No duffel hauled up the dock, no farewell scene, none of it. I just began doing the thing I do, the quiet pre-departure ritual I could perform blindfolded: stowing loose gear, doubling the lines on the tender, checking the fuel gauge, wiping down the chart table, folding the charts I wouldn't need. It takes about an hour, start to finish, and I've done it so many times it has the rhythm of a prayer. Maybe it is one. The only liturgy I know.

I was halfway through — coiling the spare anchor line, the good line, the one I'd bought in Brasse-Lune, which felt like a small betrayal to coil — when Odile came down the dock.

She didn't say anything at first. She stood at the top of the ramp and watched, the way you'd watch a animal slip a fence, and I could feel her watching, and I kept coiling, because stopping felt like admitting something and finishing felt like lying, and I chose the lie because it bought me another minute.

"You're doing the thing," she said finally.

"What thing?"

"The departure choreography. Lines, charts, the wipe-down. You have a whole sequence. I've seen it once before, the morning after the quarry, and you got exactly as far as the fuel gauge before I caught you." She came down the ramp. "You alphabetize your canned goods when you're leaving. Did you know that? Beans, then corn, then the syrup ones. It's very tidy. It's the tidiest thing about you."

"That's not alphabetical, that's—"

"It's alphabetical, Rafael." She leaned against the cabin doorframe, arms crossed, rain dripping off the brim of her cap onto the boards. "So. Tell me where you're going. Specifically. Name the port."

I looked at the chart table. The charts were folded. One of them was the coast downriver, the route I'd have taken, the route I'd already half-flown in my head at three in the morning: past the bar, down the estuary, fuel at Kessel Point, gone.

"Rafael."

"There's a buyer," I said. "Downriver. For the brass alone, the ledger's worth — it doesn't matter. The point is the point isn't the money. The point is that every day I'm here, this town pays for me. And I've seen what Corvane does when it stops asking nicely. I've seen the skiff. I was on the dock when it went up. Somebody could have died, and next time somebody will, and it'll be because a stranger with a warrant wouldn't do the decent thing and leave."

"And whose decent idea was that?" she said. "Yours? Or theirs?"

"Mine."

"Did you ask theirs?"

"No."

"Then it's not decency, it's just leaving with better lighting." She stepped aboard, uninvited, which was its own message, and picked up the folded chart off the table and held it. "You want to know what I think? I think you've been running so long you've confused the exit door with a moral position. I think the warrant scares you less than staying does. And I think—" she stopped, and looked at me straight, and her voice dropped into something quieter and worse, "—I think there's a piece of this story you haven't told anybody. Not me. Not the tavern. Something older than the chest. Because I've watched you read that ledger, Rafael, and you don't read it like a man reading treasure. You read it like a man reading a bill he owes."

The rain came harder off the river and rattled the cabin roof, and neither of us moved, and the chart stayed in her hands, and my bags stayed half-packed, and the morning sat there between us like deep water.

She was right. Of course she was right. There was a story, and it was older than the chest, and it was the reason the warrant worked on me at all — because a warrant is only a hook if there's something on you to hang. And tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever I ran out of ways to coil rope instead of talking, she was going to make me say it out loud.

I reached for the chart. She didn't give it up.

"Not yet," she said. "Drink your coffee first. Then we'll talk about what you'd take back."
