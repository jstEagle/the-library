## 17. Storm's Worst Night

The rain didn't fall so much as arrive — sideways, in sheets, with the kind of insistence that made you feel personally addressed. By midnight the quarry road had turned to porridge, and the wind was doing its best to peel the roof off the mechanic's shed like a sardine tin.

We were supposed to move the chest tomorrow.

Odile stood in the doorway of her boathouse with a hurricane lamp in one hand and a look on her face that I'd learned to read as *I have already decided and am merely informing you.*

"Tonight," she said.

"It's a whiteout," I said. "You can't see your hand in front of—"

"I don't need my hands. I need the channel." She set the lamp down and started pulling oilskins off their pegs, tossing me the ones that smelled least like diesel. "Every patrol boat on this river is huddled under a roof right now, feeling clever about it. The quarry sweep won't start until the rain eases — Corvane's men are fair-weather bullies. This is the only window we get, and it's closing."

The mechanic — Bertille, who had hands like tree roots and opinions to match — had spent two days making her argument for us without saying a word: she'd re-caulked the ferry's seams herself, checked the bilge pumps twice, and left a thermos of coffee on the wheelhouse bench with a note that read, in blocky pencil, *FOR THE IDIOTS GOING OUT IN THIS. DON'T DIE. THE COFFEE IS GOOD.*

That's how I knew she was in. Nobody makes good coffee for a plan they're not invested in.

* * *

Loading the chest took four of us, a block-and-tackle, and more swearing than I care to record. The chest weighed as much as a stubborn calf and fought about the same. We wrapped it in tarpaulin, lashed it amidships where the ferry rode lowest and steadiest, and covered the whole arrangement with empty fish crates, so that to any passing eye we were just another supply run gone stupid in the weather.

The retired harbor master, old Anselme, appointed himself lookout and stationed himself at the bow with a sou'wester hat the size of a wagon wheel. He was seventy-three and moved like a man being charged by the minute.

"You know what I did in forty years of harbor work?" he announced, as we cast off. "I never once went out in weather like this on purpose."

"Why now?" Odile asked, easing us off the mooring.

"Because I'm retired," he said, "and this is the first time nobody can fire me for it." He seemed enormously pleased by this logic.

The deckhand with the conscience — Tomas, late of Corvane's own payroll — came along too, wearing his old company jacket, which he'd turned inside out. He said it was practical. I suspected it was also a statement.

And then there was me, which was the part that kept catching in my throat like a fishbone. A year ago — six months ago — three months ago — I would have done this alone. Would have insisted on it. Would have tied the chest into some leaky skiff and pointed myself downriver into the dark with a prayer and a pry bar, because trusting people had always felt like handing them the knife handle-first.

Instead I was standing on Odile Marchand's ferry, in the worst storm in a decade, surrounded by a mechanic's coffee, a retiree's hat, an inverted jacket, and a woman at the wheel who knew this river the way I knew the inside of my own mask.

I still wasn't entirely sure how it had happened. But the chest was aboard, the rain was biblical, and there was no version of tonight that involved going back.

* * *

The river in a monsoon is a different animal. You couldn't see the water; you could only see the rain hitting it, a gray churn from bank to bank, and every few minutes the lightning would go off like a photographer's flash and give you one frozen frame of the world — the black current, the bent trees, the bridge supports looming out of nowhere like the ribs of something drowned.

Odile flew blind anyway. Not blind, exactly — she flew by memory, by the sound the water made against the hull, by little corrections of the wheel that came half a second before I understood why they were needed.

"Sandbar off the old church point," she said once, swinging wide. "It grows every flood season. The church is gone but the sandbar keeps attending services."

"How far to the cut under the rail bridge?"

"Nine minutes. Maybe seven if the current's feeling generous." She glanced at me. "It isn't. Nothing about tonight is generous. That's why we planned it this way."

The plan, such as it was, had been drawn in charcoal on Bertille's workbench that afternoon: take the chest upriver through the flooded back channel, transfer it under the rail bridge to Anselme's flat-bottomed barge, which would be waiting on the far side, then run it the last stretch to the magistrate's landing before dawn. The ferry would double back and present itself, innocently damp, at its normal mooring. If Corvane's enforcers swept the quarry at first light, they'd find a hole where a chest used to be and no trail worth following, because the whole river would have spent the night rearranging itself.

Simple. On paper. On charcoal. In the rain, nothing was simple.

Halfway up the channel, the near-collision happened.

I heard it before I saw it — or rather, I heard the absence of it. Anselme, who had been narrating landmarks to himself in a low mutter ("buoy post... leaning fence... that's the third fence, we're past the bend"), suddenly stopped talking. After an hour of his commentary, silence from Anselme was louder than thunder.

"Anselme?"

"Light," he said. "Two lights. Coming down-channel. No running lamps arranged proper — just two white lights, high."

A boat coming downstream in this, with no navigation lights rigged — that wasn't a fisherman. Fishermen were either ashore or lit up like shrines. Two bare whites meant someone who didn't want to be identified, moving fast with the current, which put them on a collision line with us in about ninety seconds.

"Tomas, kill the wheelhouse lamp," Odile said, calm as a woman reading a grocery list. "Rafael, get low on the port side. Anselme —"

"I see them." He'd already crouched behind the bow crate stack, hat brim down, looking for all the world like a very large, very contented mushroom.

We drifted dark. Engine idling, barely enough way on to steer. The rain hammered the deck. And out of the gray ahead, the other boat resolved — a Corvane patrol skiff, aluminum hull, canvas canopy streaming water, two men aboard in company jackets. They were sweeping the channel with a handheld spotlight whose beam was all but useless in the rain, waving it around like a man searching a dark room for glasses he was wearing.

They passed us at maybe thirty meters. Close enough that I could hear their engine note change as the pilot eased off — close enough that for one long, dripping moment, I was certain the spotlight would swing our way and find four shapes and a tarped lump sitting dead in the water.

It swung the wrong way. Lit up a stand of drowned willows instead. One of the men shouted something at the willows — I caught the word *quarry* — and they throttled up and vanished downstream, hunting the hole we'd already emptied.

Anselme waited until the sound faded, then straightened up, adjusted his enormous hat, and delivered his assessment:

"They're looking in the quarry."

"Yes," Odile said.

"With flashlights."

"Yes."

"In *that.*"

"Yes, Anselme."

He nodded slowly, deeply satisfied. "Then we've already won. Everything after this is just paperwork." It was, I decided, the single most comforting thing anyone had said to me in years, and I intended to think about it later, when my heart rate dropped below that of a hummingbird.

* * *

The rail bridge appeared out of the rain all at once — a wall of black iron girders and falling water, the gap beneath it a narrow slot of churning shadow. Somewhere on the far side, Anselme's barge was supposed to be waiting, running no lights, holding position against the current with a sweep oar and pure orneriness.

"Transfer window is short," Odile said, bringing us in. "Current through here runs better than four knots. We get alongside, we pass the chest across, we're out. No talking, no fumbling. Rafael — you're on the chest. Tomas, you're on the line. I hold us against the piling. Anselme..."

"I wave at the barge," he said.

"You wave at the barge."

It should be noted that Anselme's idea of signaling was less a wave than a kind of ceremonial semaphore involving both arms and, apparently, emotional commitment. But in the white murk under the bridge, a shape flailed back at us from the darkness — the barge, exactly where it was supposed to be, with Bertille aboard, because of course she was. She'd refused to let anyone else handle her lines.

Getting alongside a flat barge in four knots of current, under a bridge, in a monsoon, is the nautical equivalent of parallel parking during an earthquake. Odile did it in one pass. She brought the ferry's gunwale kissing the barge's rail with a gentleness that made no sense whatsoever given the conditions, held it there with the engine breathing against the flow, and looked over at me.

"Your chest," she said.

I got my hands under the tarp and lifted with everything the last three months had taught me about heavy awkward things. Tomas fed the lashings across. Bertille received the far end with those tree-root hands and hauled, and for about four seconds the chest hung in the gap between the two boats — suspended over black water, in falling rain, under a bridge, while the current tried its best to peel the boats apart and take the chest with them.

Four seconds is a long time when you're watching something you've bled for hang in midair.

Then it landed on the barge with a thud I felt in my teeth, Bertille threw a turn of line around a cleat, and it was hers. Ours. On its way.

Tomas passed the return line back, Odile gave the engine a whisper of throttle, and the ferry slid out from under the bridge into open channel. Behind us, muffled by rain, I heard Anselme's counterpart on the barge — I never learned his name, some cousin of a cousin — call out a single word of thanks, and then the barge was a shape, then a rumor, then gone.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. The wheelhouse lamp came back on. The coffee in Bertille's thermos went around, scalding and perfect, and Anselme pronounced it "adequate for idiots," which from Anselme was a decoration for valor.

* * *

It was somewhere on the run back down-channel, with the storm still throwing everything it had and Odile steering by the sound of the banks, that the realization landed on me — quietly, the way the important ones do.

I hadn't touched the wheel. I hadn't planned the route. I hadn't loaded the chest alone, spotted the patrol alone, or made the transfer alone. For most of the night my job had been to lift when told to lift and stay low when told to stay low, and both times, the people telling me had been right.

For fifteen years my operating theory had been simple: everyone leaves, everyone leaks, everyone eventually sells you out or drowns you, so you move first and travel light. It had kept me alive. It had also kept me alone in a way I'd stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a chronic pain — it's just the weather of you.

But tonight there had been a chain of people, each holding one link, none of them able to do the whole thing, all of them doing their piece without being asked twice. Bertille and her coffee. Anselme and his hat. Tomas and his inside-out jacket. And Odile, flying blind through channels she carried in her head like sheet music, trusting me — a stranger with a warrant and a reputation for vanishing — to hold up my end of a chest that meant as much to her as it did to me.

Trust executed at speed. That was the phrase that came to me, unbidden, somewhere between the bridge and the mooring. Not trust discussed, not trust promised, not trust sworn on a dock with witnesses. Trust *used*, under load, in the dark, where it either holds or it doesn't.

It held.

We came back into Brasse-Lune harbor a little before four in the morning. The town was black except for the tavern, where a single lamp burned in the window — the schoolteacher, I'd learn later, keeping vigil on principle. Odile brought us alongside our own mooring with the same impossible gentleness, killed the engine, and sat for a moment in the sudden quiet, listening to the rain on the canopy.

"Well," she said.

"Well," I agreed.

"That's the hard part done."

"No," I said. "That's the easy part done. Now we walk into Corvane's own depot and hand it to a magistrate with their lawyers in the room."

She smiled — that crooked, unsentimental smile of hers. "Yes," she said. "Now the fun part."

From anyone else it would have been a joke. From Odile it was a forecast.

I climbed out onto the wet dock, legs shaky, hands raw, and looked back at the ferry sitting quiet in the rain — and at the four people still aboard, securing lines, wringing out hats, arguing amiably about whose fault the near-collision almost was (nobody's, obviously, but Anselme had opinions). Six months ago I would have been halfway downriver by now, alone, with a chest and a head start and a hollow where the relief should have been.

Tonight I stood on a dock in a town that wasn't mine, waiting for my people to finish tying off, and found that I didn't want to be anywhere else.

Which was when the tavern lamp flickered — and went out. All the harbor lights with it. The storm had taken the power grid down the whole reach of the river, and out of the dark downstream, faint but unmistakable, came the sound I'd been dreading all night: engines. More than one. Running upstream, toward the depot, toward the magistrate's landing — toward the place where, in a few hours, everything we'd just risked our lives to move was supposed to be read aloud.

Odile stepped off the ferry beside me, squinting into the black.

"They're early," she said. "Good. So are we."
