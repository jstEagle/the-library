## 18. The Depot at Low Water

The plan, as Odile explained it over the third pot of coffee that morning, was simple. The plan, as I understood it, was insane. These were not contradictory facts; they were the same fact wearing two hats.

"The dry-season audit," she said, tapping the map with a finger still smelling faintly of diesel. "Every year, Corvane has to open the depot to the regional magistrate's inspectors. Cargo manifests, tonnage counts, the whole boring paperwork circus. Which means officials on record, in Corvane's own house, with witnesses they can't make disappear as easily as a ferry."

"And we get the ledger to them how? Walk up and hand it over?"

"You," she said, "are going to be a contract diver."

I looked at her. She looked back with the serene confidence of a woman who had once talked a customs officer out of impounding her entire ferry by convincing him the paperwork error was *his*.

"Odile. They know my face."

"They know your face from posters that say you're a fugitive salvage thief. Nobody looks at a contract diver and thinks 'wanted man.' Everybody looks at a contract diver and thinks 'ugh, wet.' You'll be invisible. People don't see divers, Rafa. They see equipment that talks."

"That is genuinely the most insulting thing anyone's ever said about me, and I once worked for a captain who called me 'the ballast.'"

"Take it as a compliment. Invisible men survive." She slid a folded paper across the table — a work order, genuine-looking, bearing the letterhead of a diving contractor two towns downriver who owed Odile a favor involving a lost propeller and a discreet silence about why. "You're hired to inspect the depot's mooring pilings. Storm damage assessment. Very routine. Very boring. You will be so boring that boredom itself will protect you."

*

The depot at low water looked like what it was: a fortress pretending to be a warehouse. Corvane had built it where the river narrowed, all corrugated steel and floodlights and a crane arm that swung over the water like a heron with opinions. Two patrol boats sat at the dock, engines cold but crews visible. Beyond the chain-link, stacked containers bore the Corvane crest — a stylized wave inside a gear, which always struck me as honest advertising: machinery eating water.

I came in on the *Mireille*, Odile's ferry, flying the contractor's flag, wearing a wetsuit that fit like an apology and carrying tanks I'd checked three times because paranoia is just thoroughness with bad publicity.

"Remember," Odile murmured as we idled toward the dock, "you're Henri Delacroix. You're forty-one. You complain about your lower back."

"I am thirty-six and my back is fine."

"Not today it isn't. Today you are a man whose entire personality is his lumbar region. Trust me, no one interrogates a man about his spine. They flee from him."

The dockhand who waved us in barely glanced at my face. He glanced at my tanks, my gauge console, and the work order, in that order, and stamped something on a clipboard with the enthusiasm of a man stamping his own sentence. Odile handled the small talk — weather, fuel prices, the scandalous cost of rope this season — while I stood behind her radiating lumbar distress.

Then a voice cut across the dock, and my whole nervous system went from "routine" to "fire drill" in under a second.

"Delacroix?" A woman in a Corvane polo, clipboard of her own, squinting at me. "You're early. Vasseur said noon."

"I'm efficient," I said, in a voice I hoped sounded like a man named Henri.

"He'll hate that. He budgets for lateness." She made a note. "Pilings four through nine. Don't touch anything near container row C without an escort — that's bonded cargo."

Bonded cargo. In a river depot. Bonded cargo meant things someone didn't want counted, which meant things someone didn't want seen, which meant — I kept my face at a professional blankness while my brain did cartwheels — which meant the audit mattered even more than Odile thought.

"Storm damage," I said gravely. "Could be anywhere."

"Could be," she agreed, uninterested, and walked off. Odile caught my eye over the dockhand's shoulder and gave the smallest nod, the kind you'd miss if you hadn't spent a monsoon learning to read her.

*

Underwater, the world simplified. Down there there was only the green-brown murk, the tick of my own regulator, and pilings rising out of the silt like the legs of some enormous patient animal. I ran my hands along piling four, five, six, doing actual work — checking for storm scour, cracks, the soft rot that lets a dock fail at the worst moment — because the beautiful thing about a cover story built on real competence is that you can't be caught by someone asking you to demonstrate it.

Piling seven had a fresh scrape, bright wood showing through the grime. Something big had bumped it recently. Something with a hull.

And then, drifting downcurrent past me, a shape: a body. Not a person — a crate, wrapped in plastic sheeting, moving with the slow deliberation of something that had been dropped rather than lost. It snagged against piling eight and hung there, turning gently, like it was waiting to be introduced.

I should have left it. Every instinct honed by years of running said *leave it, Rafa, curiosity is how wrecks get bodies*. But I'd spent months learning that Corvane's secrets were exactly the size of crates, and my hands — traitorous, competent hands — were already moving.

The plastic had torn at one corner. Inside: paper. Bundles of it, wax-sealed, and through the tear I could read a fragment of a header. *Vessany Provision Authority.* More of the chest's siblings. Records the resistance had smuggled into the river forty years ago, fished out recently by men who wanted them destroyed — or hidden until after the audit.

Or, said a quieter voice, dropped here deliberately. Evidence sunk where only a diver would find it, in a depot about to fill with magistrates. Someone on the inside was leaving breadcrumbs.

I weighted the crate with a scrap of chain, marked the spot on my slate, and surfaced behind the moored patrol boats where nobody could see me breathe.

*

The trouble started at lunch, which I should have expected, because in my experience all trouble either starts at lunch or ends there.

I was sitting on a bollard eating the sandwich Odile had packed — ham, mustard, and a note reading *Henri complains about his back, remember* — when the lawyer found me.

Not *my* lawyer. Corvane's. M. Aubertin, silver-haired, soft-voiced, the kind of man who'd apologize sincerely while signing the order to have you erased. I'd met him twice before, both times across a table where he offered me money for the chest and I offered him lies for time. He stopped three meters away, tilted his head, and smiled the smile of a man confirming a suspicion he'd been enjoying.

"Henri Delacroix," he said, tasting it. "Funny thing about names. They're like boats — the good ones get reused."

"Sir?"

"You dive well, I'm told. Thorough. Quiet." He let *quiet* sit in the air between us like a coin on a table. "We had a diver once. Also quiet. Also thorough. He found something he shouldn't have, and now he's — well. Divers move around so much. Hard to keep track." He consulted a card he didn't need to consult. "If you happen to find anything unusual down there — papers, seals, old metal boxes — the correct procedure is to report it to me. Directly. There's a bonus for correct procedure."

"I inspect pilings," I said. "Wood doesn't have paperwork."

"No," Aubertin agreed pleasantly. "It usually doesn't." And he walked away, unhurried, leaving me with half a sandwich and a heart going like a bilge pump in a hurricane.

So. They suspected. Maybe not *me*, specifically — maybe just the shape of me, a stranger arriving precisely when strangers were inconvenient. The trap wasn't closing yet. But I could hear the hinges.

That evening, back aboard the *Mireille*, I told Odile about the crate and the lawyer. She listened with her arms crossed, and when I finished she was quiet long enough that I could hear the river working at the pilings below us.

"Aubertin spoke to you directly," she said at last. "He didn't report you to the enforcers."

"Why does that matter?"

"Because if he knew who you were, he wouldn't warn you off — he'd call them. He's testing whether you're a problem. Which means he's not sure. Which means—" she unfolded, pacing the narrow cabin, energized the way she got when a channel got tricky, "—we still have tonight. And tomorrow the magistrate arrives, and everything gets harder or easier depending on what we do before breakfast."

"What we do," I said slowly, watching her face, "or what *I* do. I can hear the 'Rafa' coming."

She grinned, entirely unrepentant. "The crate, Rafa. The crate with the Vessany seals, sitting at piling eight, in the depot, on the eve of the audit. If it's planted evidence — if someone inside wants it found — then it needs to be found *loudly*. By the right people. At the right moment."

"Or it's bait."

"Of course it might be bait." She said it cheerfully, the way she said *of course the current might rip us sideways* right before taking a channel at full throttle. "Everything worth doing might be bait. The question is whether we're fish or fishermen."

"I've been both," I said. "Fish is safer."

"Fish also ends up in a net." She put her hand flat on the chart of the depot, over the little penciled cross I'd drawn at piling eight. "Tomorrow morning, when the magistrate's barge comes in, every eye goes to the dock. Officials, enforcers, Aubertin, all of them performing their parts. And a contract diver named Henri Delacroix — complaining quietly about his back — surfaces beside the magistrate's gangway with a wax-sealed bundle in his hands and a look of innocent confusion."

"Innocent confusion."

"It's your best expression. You've had years of practice looking like you don't belong anywhere."

It was a terrible plan. It relied on timing, on luck, on a lawyer's uncertainty holding for twelve more hours, on a town full of descendants showing up tomorrow to watch an audit none of them could legally attend. It relied on me walking into the exact building where the men who wanted me erased kept their lights on all night.

I looked at the penciled cross. I thought about the ledger in its oilskin wrapping beneath my bunk, the brass corners worn smooth by dead hands that had trusted the river to keep their promise. I thought about Odile's grandmother, and the schoolteacher's chalk dust, and the tavern keeper who'd started calling me by a nickname I hadn't authorized, and the mechanic who'd fixed my boat twice and charged me once.

Somewhere in the last few months, without filing any paperwork, I had become a man who stayed. Staying, it turned out, came with obligations. Terrible, inconvenient, warm-hearted obligations.

"All right," I said. "But if this goes wrong, I want it noted that I complained about my back the whole time."

"Noted," Odile said, and reached for the coffee pot. "Now eat your sandwich. Fishermen need their strength."

Outside, the last light went off the water, and downriver the depot burned its floodlights against the dark like a stage waiting for its play — and somewhere between those lights and our little lamp, the trap and the plan lay perfectly superimposed, each indistinguishable from the other, both of them set to spring at dawn.
