## 19. Read Aloud

**Rafael**

The depot office smelled of varnish, cigarette smoke, and the particular staleness of rooms where men made decisions about other men's lives without ever getting wet. Rafael stood with rain running off their sleeves onto Corvane's polished floorboards and tried to look like a contract diver who had every right to be there, which was difficult, because the office door had just locked behind them.

"You're not on the roster." The man at the desk was young, nervous, wearing a Corvane lanyard like it might strangle him. "Contract divers sign in at the gate. You didn't sign in."

"I signed in at the gate," Rafael said. "Your gate man's got a cold. He waved me through." This was true, in the sense that Rafael had waved themselves through.

Outside the rain came down like the sky had a grudge. Through the office window Rafael could see the depot yard: cranes folded against the storm, cargo tarps snapping, the dry-season audit tents going up along the wharf in defiance of all meteorological reason. The magistrate's launch was due at the next slack tide. That was the whole plan. Get in, hold on, stall until the one honest official within two hundred river miles stepped onto Corvane property and made himself a witness.

What the plan had not accounted for was the second door, the one at the back, which opened now to admit a man Rafael knew by reputation and by the particular way he moved, like a man who had never once hurried because hurrying was for people with something to fear.

Voss Corvane's regional man. Durel.

"Ah," Durel said pleasantly. "The diver with the chest."

**Odile**

Odile was supposed to be at the bridge with the ferry, holding position, ready to run the ledger out the back way if everything went wrong. Instead she was standing in the depot yard under a cargo tarp with the schoolteacher on one side and the retired harbor master on the other, watching lightning walk across the river like something testing its footing.

"You said wait for the magistrate," Madame Irielle the schoolteacher said. "We have waited. The magistrate is late."

"He's not late. Rivers are late. Magistrates are just carried along."

"Odile." The old harbor master, Baptiste, spoke in the voice he'd used for forty years of telling captains things they didn't want to hear. "There are four of Corvane's men at the gate and they've turned away the audit clerks. If they seal that office before the magistrate lands, whatever's in it stops existing. That's how Corvane works. Not with bullets. With paperwork and locked doors."

Odile looked at the office windows, lit yellow against the rain, and thought of her grandmother's stories: the night the deeds burned, the neighbors who walked into the river and didn't walk out, the records smuggled into wrecks by people who believed someday meant something. Someday was currently forty minutes overdue.

"Right," Odile said. "Change of plan."

**Rafael**

"You understand my position," Durel said. He poured coffee from a Corvane-branded pot as though this were a social call. "There's a storm. There's an audit. And there's a diver in my office who, according to a warrant I have right here—" he tapped a folder without opening it, "—should be in a cell in Kessel Bay. And yet here you are. Almost as if you wanted to be found."

Rafael kept their breathing even. Behind them, Odile was supposed to be at the bridge. The ledger was in the waterproof satchel against their ribs, and every heartbeat pressed it there like a second pulse.

"I wanted a dry place," Rafael said. "It's raining."

"It is indeed." Durel smiled. "Here's what happens now. You give me the satchel, I tear up the warrant, and you walk out of here with money and a clean name. Or—" he spread his hands, "—my colleagues outside handle it their way, and the audit finds nothing, because audits never find anything that matters. You've seen how this goes. You've been running from it for six years."

"You know," Rafael said, "you're the third person to offer me a clean record this season. The first two were also lying."

"Was I lying about the warrant?"

That landed. Rafael looked at the folder. Six years. A man in a hospital bed who never woke. A debt Rafael had been paying to a company that had helped create it. The old arithmetic: run, run, run, and the running becomes the life.

"No," Rafael admitted. "You weren't lying about the warrant."

"Then—"

"But here's my problem." Rafael sat down, uninvited, in the chair across the desk, which was the most dangerous thing they'd done all season, because it meant they weren't leaving. "The ledger in this satchel has signatures in it. Real ones. Your founder's. A navy officer's. And a magistrate from forty years ago who took a bribe and wrote it down in his own hand because he thought the nation he was robbing would never speak again." Rafael smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. "You don't erase that by locking me in a room. You erase it by making sure nobody ever reads it. And it's a little late for that."

Durel's smile stayed exactly where it was, which was how Rafael knew the door was about to open and it wouldn't be Durel's colleagues being polite.

**Odile**

They came through the yard gate in a line of eight, and it was not eight people sneaking. Madame Irielle had insisted on the lanterns. "If we do this in the dark," she'd said, "we're conspirators. In the light, we're witnesses."

So they walked through the monsoon with lanterns held high, twenty-six townspeople of Brasse-Lune, soaked to the bone, past four Corvane enforcers who looked at the crowd and then at each other and discovered that their training had not covered this.

The gate man — the one with the cold, who had in fact waved Rafael through because Odile had paid him a month's wages and told him to be ill — let them pass.

At the office door, an enforcer put up a hand. "Private property."

"This is a leased river depot," Baptiste said, "on land the Corvane company does not own, and I have the harbor records to prove it, which is convenient, because I *am* the harbor records." He held up a ledger of his own, water-stained and forty years old. "Also, you're standing on my cousin's dock."

The enforcer looked at the crowd, at the lanterns, at Madame Irielle, who was a schoolteacher and therefore the most frightening person present, and stepped aside.

Odile went through the door first.

**Rafael**

The door opened and Rafael's heart did something complicated, because it was Odile, dripping, furious, alive, and behind her a crowd of lanterns like the river itself had come up the bank to collect a debt.

"You were supposed to be at the bridge," Rafael said.

"You were supposed to be a contract diver with a roster entry. We've both been busy."

Durel stood, and his voice went up half an octave, which Rafael would remember with pleasure for the rest of their life. "This is trespass. This is—"

"This," said Madame Irielle, setting her lantern on his desk and taking out her reading glasses, "is a public audit. The magistrate's launch is coming up the channel now — I can see its lights. And until it arrives, someone ought to make use of the time." She held out her hand to Rafael, palm up, the way she must have done for thirty years of homework. "The satchel, please. I'll be reading."

Rafael had imagined this moment a hundred ways and in none of them did they hand the chest's ledger to a retired schoolteacher with rain in her hair. They handed it over. It felt like setting down something carried too long.

Durel moved for the back door and found Baptiste in it, arms crossed, immovable as a piling.

"Sit down, Mr. Durel," the old man said. "You'll want to hear this. It's got your signature in it too."

**Odile**

The ledger opened to a page soft as cloth from forty years underwater, and Madame Irielle read in the clear carrying voice of a woman who had taught three generations to fear her dictation exercises.

"*Entry the fourteenth. Vessany Provision Office, final audit. The following holdings, seized under emergency decree, are recorded as transferred to the Corvane Shipping Concern: the Marchand ferry crossing and two vessels—*"

"That's us," Odile said, and her voice cracked on it, and she didn't care. "*Marchand.* My grandmother piloted that crossing. They put her ferry on a list and scuttled it with her neighbors' names inside."

Nobody spoke. The rain filled the silence. Then Madame Irielle went on.

"*—the Brasse-Lune warehouse and wharf, the Tessier fishing grounds, forty hectares of riverfront orchard, the quarry at Vail—*" She read the names of holdings the town had been renting back, all their lives, from the company that had stolen them. She read the names of the people they'd belonged to, and half the crowd was nodding at names they knew from their own family stories, and the other half was the names.

Then she reached the signatures.

"*Witnessed and executed: A. Corvane, founder.*" A sound went through the room like a low tide going out. "*Commander Hessler, Vessany Navy, who did on this date order the ferry *Provision* scuttled at the crossing with all records aboard, the crew and passengers having been removed to—" *she stopped, and her glasses caught the lantern light, and when she continued her voice was not steady, "—*removed to a location not recorded.*"

"The flowers we lay on that wreck every year," Odile said quietly, to Durel, to the room, to the dark window and the river beyond it. "We thought we were mourning an accident."

**Rafael**

The lights died at 9:40.

Not a flicker — a death, the whole depot plunging black, the storm outside going white and silent in alternating shocks. In the dark Rafael heard Durel's chair scrape, heard him say something low and urgent in a language of instructions, and Rafael moved without deciding to, the way they did in water.

They found Odile by the sound of her breathing and put the lantern in her hands. "Light. Now."

The match flared. The lantern caught. And in its circle stood three of Durel's enforcers, frozen mid-stride, and behind them the entire population of Brasse-Lune, standing shoulder to shoulder in the dark, holding their lanterns up.

Nobody had panicked. Nobody had run. They had simply turned, all of them, to face the men who moved in the dark, and lit it.

"Put them down," Durel said, and his voice had lost its pleasantness entirely. "This is company security matters. You're interfering with—"

"With what?" Baptiste asked, from the door. "Say it in front of the magistrate. He's tying off at your wharf right now, and he's brought clerks, and clerks love nothing better than a man who says incriminating things in front of witnesses."

The front door opened on wind and rain and the gray-green light of the magistrate's electric lantern, and a tired man in a wet coat stepped into the room, looked at the ledger open on the desk, at the crowd, at Durel, and said, in the voice of a man who had waited forty years for someone to hand him this on a plate:

"Somebody read me the signatures."

**Odile**

Later, Odile would remember it not as a battle but as a reading, which was somehow worse for the men from Corvane than any fight could have been. Because you can fight a fight. You cannot fight a roomful of people listening while a schoolteacher reads your grandfather's crimes out loud in his own handwriting.

The magistrate's clerks copied pages by lantern light. Durel's enforcers stood in the corner under Baptiste's cheerful guard, disarmed of nothing but their options. The storm hammered the roof and the town held the line in the dark, and when Durel finally said, "None of this is enforceable, these are forty-year-old documents from a dead country," Madame Irielle looked at him over her glasses and delivered the sentence Odile would carry for the rest of her life:

"A country isn't dead while its debts are still being collected, Mr. Durel. You've been collecting them all along. Tonight we're simply reading the invoice."

Outside, the rain began, very slowly, to slacken.

Rafael stood by the window with the empty satchel in their hands, watching the first gray hint of dawn come up the river, and Odile went to stand beside them.

"Well," she said. "You delivered it."

"I didn't do anything. A schoolteacher did everything."

"You stopped running long enough for a schoolteacher to exist in your life. That's not nothing." She bumped their shoulder with hers. "Come on. The magistrate wants statements. And Rafael — the warrant?"

Rafael looked at her. "What about it?"

"The magistrate just read the file Durel was holding. The one they used to own you." She was smiling, tired and fierce. "It's signed by a Corvane attorney. Fabricated evidence, page one."

Rafael stood very still in the gray light, six years falling off their shoulders like water off oilskin, and found that they didn't know what to do with hands that weren't holding something down.

"So," Odile said. "Now what are you going to run from?"

Rafael looked out at the river — at the town, at the lanterns still burning in windows, at the wreck of the *Provision* somewhere out under the calming water, holding its dead and its flowers.

"Give me a minute," Rafael said. "I've never had to think about it before."
