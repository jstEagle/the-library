## 21. Provision

The dry season came to Brasse-Lune the way good news comes to a poor household — slowly at first, then all at once, and then everyone wanted a piece of it.

For three weeks the river dropped. Not gracefully, not all in one go, but in stages, like a man taking off wet clothes one item at a time because he's too tired to hurry. First the floating dock sat up on its pilings like an old dog standing after a long nap. Then the sandbar below the church point surfaced, brown and glistening, and within two days there were children on it, digging holes and arguing about whose hole was deeper. Then the quarry road dried out enough that Odile could drive the truck down to where we'd sunk the chest — twice now, once to hide it and once to fetch it back — and she stood at the waterline looking at the mud like it owed her money.

"It's still down there," I said.

"I know it's still down there. I'm looking at how much easier you're going to have it this time."

She wasn't wrong. The water over the quarry shelf had gone from eight meters of green soup to four meters of something you could almost read a watch through. When I went down for the last of it — the brass fittings, the lockplate, the little stamped plate off the lid that the magistrate had asked to keep as evidence — I could see my own bubbles rising ahead of me like a trail of silver coins leading home.

But that's getting ahead of things. The chest itself was already up, already opened in front of forty witnesses in a depot office with no lights and a storm trying to tear the roof off, which is a story other people tell better than I do, because I spent most of that night crouched behind a filing cabinet listening to Odile read names in a voice that didn't waver once, not even when the enforcers started breaking their own furniture. What matters here is what came after. What matters is the morning I woke up in the loft above the tavern, heard roosters instead of rain, and lay there for a long time waiting for the old feeling to arrive.

The feeling being: *time to go.*

It always used to come like that. A quiet morning, a body rested, and somewhere under the ribs a small cold voice saying *you've stayed too long, they'll figure out what you are, move before moving gets expensive.* For eleven years I'd obeyed that voice the way other men obey a tide table. It had kept me alive. It had also kept me from ever owning so much as a second pair of boots, because boots imply a place to keep them.

I lay there and waited, and the voice didn't come. What came instead was the smell of someone frying plantains downstairs and the sound of the tavern keeper, Marta, hollering at her nephew about the propane, and I thought: *huh.* Then I got up, because a man can't lie around waiting for his own paranoia forever. It's rude to it.

* * *

The boat was worse than I'd let myself believe.

That's the thing about a leaking hull — you get so good at managing it that you stop seeing it. You learn your pump's rhythm, you learn which seam weeps and which one gushes, you develop a whole private relationship with the problem, and somewhere in there the problem becomes furniture. You stop calling it a crisis and start calling it Tuesday. My boat had been Tuesday for so long that when the mechanic, Ferro, finally backed me into hauling it out and took one long look along the keel, he made a noise I'd only previously heard him make at the sight of a carburetor full of river snails.

"Rafael." He said it the way a doctor says a name before delivering bad news. "Rafael, Rafael, Rafael." Three Rafaels is never good. "You've been caulking this boat with hope."

"It held."

"Hope holds nothing. Hope just makes the sinking slower so you have time to make peace with it." He ran a thumb along a seam near the transom and the caulk came away in a curl, gray and crumbly, older than our acquaintance. "This isn't even caulk anymore. This is archaeology."

He was right, and I knew he was right, and knowing it felt strange, because for years the answer to a failing boat had been simple: leave it on whatever bank it died on and walk away. Boats were consumables. So were towns, jobs, names on fuel ledgers signed with initials. Nothing was meant to last, least of all me.

But I stood there in Ferro's yard, in the flat gold light of the dry season, looking at a hull that had carried me through a scuttled wreck and a hidden chest and a storm night I still dreamed about, and I found I couldn't think of it as disposable. It had earned better. And — this was the part that took longer to admit — so had I.

"How much for a proper job?" I asked.

Ferro named a number. It was most of what the magistrate's office had paid me for the recovery work, minus what I owed Marta for the loft, minus what I'd quietly slipped the schoolteacher, Madame Ilsa, for the kids' field trip to the wreck site, because apparently once you start providing for people it's very hard to stop. It was, in other words, nearly everything I had.

I paid it. Standing right there, in the mud, without haggling, which Ferro later told Odile was the single most suspicious thing he'd ever seen me do.

* * *

The work took nine days, and I want to record honestly that I complained through most of them.

New planking came from a sawyer upriver, a taciturn man named Bello who cut garapa and wouldn't discuss price until he'd discussed the weather, the fishing, the magistrate's inquiry, and whether Corvane's frozen depot would be sold or simply left to rot like a whale carcass. By the time he got to price I'd agreed to dive a lost outboard off his cousin's pontoon for free, which is how business works in Brasse-Lune: currency is a rumor the town mostly ignores.

Ferro pulled the bad planks and I watched him do it, because I'd decided — and this felt genuinely new — that I was going to know my own boat. Every plank, every frame, every place where the previous owner had done something clever and every place he'd done something lazy. Eleven years of running and I'd never once learned a vessel properly. I knew them the way you know a bus: well enough to ride, never well enough to trust.

So I watched. I asked questions until Ferro threatened to charge me by the question, and then I kept asking, and eventually he gave up and started answering, because underneath the gruffness Ferro has the soul of a teacher who never got students worth the trouble. He showed me how to read grain direction before driving a screw. He showed me why a caulked seam fails at the ends first, and how to back-caulk so it doesn't. He let me drive the last dozen fasteners himself and inspected each one afterward with the air of a man finding his students' work less terrible than feared.

"You're doing this like you plan to keep it," he said on day six, not looking up.

"I do plan to keep it."

"Huh." He spat into the sawdust, which in Ferro's language is a round of applause. "The warrant's withdrawn, the chest is delivered, the magnate's lawyers packed up like a circus leaving town. Nobody's chasing you. Most men in your position would sell the boat and buy a ticket somewhere the name Nystrom means nothing."

"I tried that," I said. "Twice. Once in October, once the week after the inquiry opened. Got as far as the fuel dock both times."

"What stopped you?"

I thought about it honestly, because Ferro deserved honesty and because I hadn't fully articulated it to myself yet. "The list," I said finally. "Madame Ilsa's list. Of claims. Forty-one households filing on land titles and fishing rights and that warehouse, and every single one of them needs surveys done, markers set, documents ferried to the regional office, wrecks cleared off claimed ground so nobody builds on top of a drowned ferry and calls it theirs. That's diving work. Ferry work. Boat work." I looked at the hull, half-skinned, ribs bare as a fish skeleton in the sun. "And there's exactly one salvage diver in Brasse-Lune who already knows where half those wrecks are, because he put a chest in one of them personally."

Ferro grinned around the nail he was holding in his teeth. "Convenient."

"Almost like somebody planned it."

"Almost like you stopped swimming long enough for the current to hand you something."

* * *

There's a word in the ledger I kept coming back to, during those weeks. The old clerk who compiled it — some doomed Vessany functionary with beautiful handwriting and a sense of humor the dissolution clearly failed to kill — had headed one column *provision*. Not holdings, not assets. Provision. What each family was owed for their keeping: the seed stock, the salt stores, the winter oil, the boat shares. The stuff that isn't wealth so much as continuation. The difference between having money and having next year.

Odile caught me reading that column again one evening on the tavern porch, my feet up on the rail, the repaired boat moored below us looking — I'll say it, because nobody else will — handsome. Honest planking, fresh caulk running in clean beads, new paint the color of deep water. She leaned on the rail beside me with the particular ease of a person who has decided you're allowed to be within arm's reach.

"That column," she said. "You've been staring at it for a week."

"It's the interesting one. Everything else in the ledger is theft with signatures. That column's the part where somebody, forty years ago, sitting in a room while the country came apart around him, sat down and wrote out what people would need to survive what was coming." I tapped the page. "He couldn't stop the seizure. Couldn't stop the navy officer scuttling the ferry. But he could write down, in brass and ink, *these families will need provision,* and seal it into a steel box and sink it in a way that meant to be found. That's not bookkeeping. That's a promise with a delivery date."

"And you're the courier."

"I'm the courier," I admitted. "Forty years late, but the river doesn't charge late fees."

Odile laughed — she laughs easily now, in a way she didn't in October, and I take an unreasonable amount of credit for it — and then went quiet, looking at the harbor. The evening fleet was coming in, the boats riding high and light on the dropped river, lanterns starting to show along the quay. Somewhere a radio played something with too much accordion in it. Down at the far end, the Corvane depot sat dark and padlocked, its crane arm folded like a sleeping bird, waiting for the inquiry to decide whether it would be auctioned or torn down or — and this was Madame Ilsa's pet theory — turned into the school annex the town had been promised since before the war.

"You know what provision means on this river," Odile said. "Not in the ledger. Here."

"Supplies?"

"Supplies. But also — providing. Being the one who shows up with the thing everybody needs and didn't ask for yet." She nodded at my boat. "You stocked it for escape your whole life. Fuel, water, hardtack, a go-bag. Enough to run and nothing to give. Now look at it."

I looked. I confess I'd already been looking, all week, with a satisfaction I didn't entirely understand. Because the hold had changed character without my quite deciding it should. Where the go-bag used to sit there were now survey stakes and a bundle of painted boundary markers for the Fontaine claim. Under the bench seat: two coils of new lift line, a spare regulator Ferro had rebuilt for free ("consider it tuition"), and a crate of the good lamp oil Marta's sister sells, bound for the lighthouse keeper at Church Point whose own supply barge had stopped coming when Corvane froze the route. Lashed amidships, wrapped in tarpaulin against the dust: the parts for the schoolteacher's pump, ordered before the freeze and finally released, which Madame Ilsa had asked me to collect with the specific instruction to *not let the depot clerk shortchange us, Rafael, they respect you now, use it.*

They respect me now. I'm still not sure what to do with that sentence. For eleven years the goal had been to be forgettable — a face nobody could describe, a name given only in initials. Now dockhands greeted me by name, the sawyer saved me the straight-grained offcuts, and children on the sandbar waved at the diving boat the way they'd once waved at the patrol boats, except without the fear. It turns out being known is a skill like any other. I was bad at it at first. I'm tolerable at it now. On good days I'd call myself improving.

* * *

The shakedown run was the Fontaine claim, up past the church point, and it went wrong in the traditional small ways and right in the important ones.

Wrong ways: the wind came up quartering across the point and shoved us sideways onto the bar I'd been avoiding all season; the boundary marker paint turned out to be the wrong color, red instead of white, which according to Madame Ilsa meant *disputed* rather than *granted* and would cause "confusion"; and the Fontaines themselves, all six of them plus two cousins, met us at the bank with a dispute of their own about whether the old fig tree marked the line or merely shaded it.

Right ways: I dove the channel off their frontage, found the drowned punt they'd been losing nets to for a decade, rigged it, and floated it clear in forty minutes while the family argued cheerfully on the bank about fig trees. Old Fontaine — eighty if he was a day, hands like braided rope — watched me surface, spat thoughtfully into the water, and said:

"Nystrom. You know what my father said about the Vessany men? Said they were thieves, but honest thieves — they stole it all at once, in daylight, with paperwork, so you knew exactly what you'd lost." He looked out at his water, at the marker I'd planted, wrong color and all. "Corvane stole it slow, in the dark, so you never even noticed it going. Your paper box made us notice." He clapped me on the shoulder hard enough to mean it. "Come to supper Sunday. Bring the pilot."

Bring the pilot. As if Odile and I were a unit now, a package deal, a fact of the town's geography like the church point or the bar. And the strange thing — the thing I want written down somewhere, in case future-me goes soft in the head and hears the old cold voice again — is that I didn't argue with it. I said "we'll be there," and meant *we*, and slept that night without checking where the nearest open water was.

* * *

By the end of the dry season the boat was, for the first time in its life and mine, fully provisioned — and I mean provisioned in both senses, the ledger's and the river's.

Fuel enough to work a full week, not enough to flee a continent. Water tank full. Food locker stocked from Marta's kitchen against a standing arrangement she described as "you fix the tavern's well pump, I feed you, we're even, don't argue." Tools stowed where tools belong instead of scattered like evidence of a life lived in haste. The survey kit. The claim files, copied in Madame Ilsa's immaculate hand, waterproofed in oilcloth. A spare anchor, because Ferro lectured me for twenty minutes on the subject and I retain perhaps sixty percent of any lecture but that sixty percent was anchors.

And one more thing, which I added without telling anyone, in the space under the helm where the go-bag used to live: the brass plate off the chest's lid. The magistrate had photographed it and released it, saying the ledger was the evidence and the metal was just metal, and Odile had said I should mount it on the tavern wall, and Marta had said the same, and instead I'd wrapped it in cloth and put it in the boat. Not as treasure. As ballast, maybe. Or as a reminder of what the hull had carried and what the hull was for. Some mornings, before the engine started, I'd touch it the way Ferro touches wood to read its grain. Just to check I remembered.

The last week of the dry season, the river got so low the ferry scraped sand at the church point landing, and Odile came to the yard with a chart and a grim expression and announced that the channel markers needed resetting before the rains came back, because every year the flood moved them and every year somebody hit the bar in the first big water, and this year — with the claims filed and the town's whole future riding on getting goods and officials up and down the river — nobody could afford a sunk ferry in October.

"So we reset them," she said. "All fourteen. You dive, I drive, we take the long weekend."

"We," I said.

"We," she confirmed, and handed me the chart, and I looked at it and saw that she'd already penciled in the order of work, the lunch stop, and — in the margin, in her small sharp handwriting — a note beside the last marker, the one off the old wreck site: *flowers?*

Because that was the other thing the dry season had settled in us. October was coming. The anniversary was coming. The first rains were coming, and with them the date, and Odile had already decided — had probably decided the night the power went out in the depot office — that this year, when the monsoon arrived, we would take the ferry out to the crossing where the Vessany ferry had gone down with its passengers and its sealed chest, and we would lay flowers on the water for the dead, and then we would turn around and come home to a lit harbor.

Home. To a lit harbor. I'd read that note four times before I understood it was addressed to me too.

* * *

We finished the markers on a Sunday afternoon, the last one set off the wreck site in water gone glass-still and gold, and on the ride back the light did that thing it does at the end of the dry season — thickened, sweetened, leaning west like it was tired of standing — and Odile pointed the bow at Brasse-Lune and opened the throttle, and the boat, my boat, the honest boat, the provisioned boat, rose onto plane for the first time in its patched-and-planked life and just *ran*, clean and true, no pump whining, no seam weeping, no cold voice under the ribs telling me anything at all.

The harbor came up ahead of us, lanterns lighting one by one along the quay, and I realized I was grinning like a fool and decided not to care.

Then Odile eased off the throttle, nodded at the sky, and said, "Clouds over the ridge already. Early this year."

And I looked north, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise, and felt — for the first time in eleven years — not the urge to run from the rain, but the urge to be home before it started.
