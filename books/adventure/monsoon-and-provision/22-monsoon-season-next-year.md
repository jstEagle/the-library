## 22. Monsoon Season, Next Year

The first rain of the season came at four in the morning, and Rafael Nystrom was awake to hear it.

Not because of worry. That was the strange part. A year ago — two years ago, three — the sound of rain on a cabin roof had meant one thing: check the seams. Rain was the river's way of testing you, finding the soft wood, the bad caulking, the place where you'd cut corners because money was short and time was shorter. Rafael had spent eleven years listening to rain as an accusation.

But the *Provision* had been rebuilt in the dry months just past, plank by honest plank, and when the water came drumming down on the deck above the berth, Rafael lay still and listened to it the way other people listened to music. The boat didn't groan. It didn't shift or weep at its joints. It sat in the water like something that intended to stay.

*You could leave now,* said the old voice, the one that had lived in Rafael's head since before the warrant, since before the chest, maybe since before anything. *First rains. Nobody would think twice. Slip downriver ahead of the flood and be a rumor by Thursday.*

Rafael turned over, pulled the blanket higher, and went back to sleep.

That was the whole answer, really. Eleven years of running, and it ended not with a decision but with a man choosing sleep over departure, which is either the saddest or the most beautiful thing a person can do with their freedom, depending on the day.

---

By seven the rain had settled into the long, patient rhythm that Brasse-Lune knew how to live under, and Rafael stood on the dock watching the town do what it always did with weather: absorb it. Odile's ferry came sliding through the mist with her running lights on at midmorning, which meant the visibility on the crossing had gone to nothing, which meant she'd been flying blind again and enjoying it, because Odile Marchand treated fog and whiteout the way other pilots treated a clear day — as a mild disappointment.

She tied off, jumped down, and looked Rafael up and down with the expression she used for appraising hulls and men, which was the same expression.

"You're still here," she said.

"I'm still here."

"A year. I want it noted for the record that I called this in month two." She shook water off her cap. "The schoolteacher owes me a bottle."

"The schoolteacher bet against me?"

"Everyone bet against you. You were the safest bet in town to lose. Man who sleeps with his boots on doesn't stay anywhere." She glanced at his feet, bare and planted on the wet planks, and smiled. "Look at that. Barefoot on a dock in the rain. Somebody should write a ballad."

"Somebody should mind their own ferry."

"Same thing, most days." She nodded upriver, toward the bend where the drowned crossing waited under forty feet of green water. "I've been thinking about today. The anniversary. I thought we'd take the ferry out, if you're willing. Lay flowers on the wreck."

Rafael had been thinking about it too, though neither of them had said so out loud. A year ago almost to the week, Rafael had gone down into that scuttled ferry with a salvage light and come up holding a nation's worth of grief in a steel box. The dead had been down there the whole time — the ones who hadn't gotten off, the ones the navy officer had sealed into the hull with the records they died carrying. For a year the town had argued about acreage and boats and who exactly was owed the east bank meadow, cheerful and vicious about it in equal measure, and through all of it the wreck itself had sat quiet, visited by no one.

"It should have happened sooner," Rafael said.

"It couldn't. The inquiry had the site sealed until spring, and then the claims process, and then everyone remembered they were angry at their cousins." Odile shrugged. "Grief keeps. That's the one thing it's good at."

They loaded the flowers in silence — marigolds and river lilies from the tavern keeper's garden, bought with coins the tavern keeper refused and Odile forced into his apron pocket anyway — and cast off into the rain.

---

The crossing took twenty minutes in good weather and took fifty that morning, Odile working the wheel by memory and instrument while the world outside the windows dissolved into gray. Rafael sat in the passenger bench, which still felt like a novelty after a year. There had been a time when Rafael didn't ride in other people's boats. Other people's boats were things you stole rides on, or scuttled debts aboard, or abandoned at fuel docks in the dark. Sitting in one as a guest, hands empty, going somewhere on purpose with someone who knew the way — it had taken most of a year to stop waiting for the catch.

"Tell me what you remember about them," Rafael said. "The people on the ferry. Your grandmother's stories."

Odile kept her eyes on the channel markers, but her voice softened the way it did when she talked about the old country. "Grandmother said the last run out of Vessany carried teachers. That was the story she told, anyway — that when the dissolution came, the ones who wouldn't sign the new papers, the ones who kept the old registries alive, they put them on boats. Some made it downriver. This one didn't." She adjusted the throttle. "Forty-some souls. She said the river took them gently, which was a lie she needed, and the navy took their names, which was a lie everybody else needed, and the chest was the truth sitting at the bottom between the two lies for forty years."

"And we dug it up."

"We dug it up," Odile agreed. "And read it aloud in a depot office during a blackout while enforcers tried the doors. Grandmother would have paid money to see that. Actually—" she grinned, "—she did pay money to see things like that. She funded the resistance with a card game. Cheated, probably. She never denied it."

The wreck's buoy appeared out of the rain, a scarred orange float that the harbor master had repainted himself last spring and marked, against all regulation, with a small painted wreath. They came alongside it and let the ferry drift, engines idling low, and the rain fell straight down into the water with a sound like static.

Rafael went aft and opened the flower box.

There was no ceremony planned. That had been decided without deciding — no speeches, no priest, no magistrate. Just two people and a box of flowers over forty graves that had never had a marker until a buoy got one by accident. Rafael took a handful of marigolds and dropped them over the side, and watched them spread out on the dark water, bright as small fires, drifting slowly in the current above the hull.

"For the teachers," Rafael said quietly. It wasn't a prayer. Rafael didn't pray. But it was addressed to someone, which was nearly the same thing.

Odile dropped the lilies. "For the ones who carried the ledger down instead of burning it. Forty years underwater so it could come up at the right time." She paused. "And for my grandmother, who waited her whole life to hear those names read out and missed it by six years. Which I intend to hold against the universe indefinitely."

The current took the flowers in a slow spiral, around and around above the wreck, and then out toward the channel, and they watched until the rain blurred them away.

It was Rafael who spoke next, and it surprised both of them.

"I used to think the chest found me by accident," Rafael said. "Bad luck, wrong dive, right wreck. But standing here — I don't know. Maybe the river decides these things. Maybe somebody down there picked the one diver on the river too stubborn to sell and too tired to run." A breath. "Either way. I'm glad it was me. I didn't think I'd ever be glad about anything connected to that year."

Odile looked at him for a long moment, rain streaming off her cap brim.

"You know what my grandmother said about gladness?" she said. "She said it's not a feeling, it's a practice. You do it daily whether you mean it or not, and one morning you notice you've gotten good at it." She started the engine up to working speed. "Come on. Harbor's lighting up. And if we're late back, the tavern keeper will tell everyone we eloped, and I will *not* survive the gossip."

---

Brasse-Lune in the first rains was a different town than Brasse-Lune in any other weather, and Rafael had come to love the difference. The monsoon didn't shut the town down; it turned it inward and upward at once — every porch crowded, every awning occupied, the whole population arranged along the waterfront under corrugated roofs, watching the river rise and commenting on it like a theater audience. Smoke curled from chimneys. Someone had strung lanterns early, amber lights blooming one by one along the dock as the afternoon dimmed, and the harbor filled up with warm color reflected in black water.

The *Provision* sat at its mooring where Rafael had left it, riding high and dry, and beside it the ferry slid home into its slip, and the two boats knocked gently together like old friends greeting.

The tavern was full. Of course it was. First-rain night was second only to the inheritance distribution day in the local calendar of excuses, and the tavern keeper had laid in extra of everything and was already regretting it in the specific way of a man whose customers had out-drunk his forecast. Rafael and Odile pushed through the door dripping and were absorbed instantly — pulled into arguments about the east bank drainage, recruited as neutral parties in a dispute between two cousins over a boathouse, handed bowls of stew nobody remembered ordering for them.

The schoolteacher raised a glass from across the room. "A year!" she called. "A year since the reading!"

"To the reading," said half the tavern, and the other half said something about the drainage, and both toasts landed together in a warm collision.

Later, much later, when the crowd had thinned to the stubborn core and the rain had settled into its overnight rhythm, Odile found Rafael out on the tavern porch, watching the harbor. The lanterns burned steady. The boats rocked. Somewhere a dog was complaining about the weather on principle.

"One more year of this," Odile said, settling onto the rail beside him, "and they'll stop introducing you as 'the diver who found the chest' and start introducing you as 'our diver.' Fair warning. It's a demotion with benefits."

"What are the benefits?"

"They argue with you about drainage. They ask you to look at their boats. They save you a seat." She sipped her drink. "Terrible perks. Nobody ever warns you."

Rafael watched the water. The old voice was quiet tonight — quieter than it had been in years. Not gone; Rafael suspected it never would be entirely. But it had stopped sounding like an order and started sounding like a memory, the way an old wound stops aching and starts just marking the weather.

"There's a job tomorrow," Rafael said. "Ferry run up to the quarry landing, supplies for the claims survey crew. Then a salvage look at the Miller place's sunken barge, if the water lets us."

"That's not a question, that's a schedule."

"I know."

"You're telling me your plans."

"I know that too."

Odile smiled and looked out at the lit harbor, at the town that had taken in two strangers and made them fixtures, at the river that had given up a dead nation's secrets and then, having done so, gone back to being simply a river.

"First rains," she said. "And you didn't leave."

"No," Rafael said. "I arrived."

Down at the dock, the lanterns swung a little in the wind coming ahead of the next squall, and the *Provision* tugged once at its line — not straining to go, the way it used to, but settling deeper into the water, the way a boat does when it has finally decided where it lives.
