## 1. The Thief Who Wasn't Running

The salt flats gave nothing back. Not heat, not sound, not the three days of Lena Almeida's life she was spending on a boy who had stolen a sack of flour and half a wheel of cheese.

She crouched at the edge of a dry wash, reading the ground the way other people read letters. A scuff here, a heel-drag there, a place where someone had knelt to shake grit out of a boot too thin for this country. The tracks were small. Light. The stride of someone who had been running for longer than he'd been running from her.

Toma would have laughed at her if he'd been here. *Three days for a flour thief.* But Toma wasn't here, and that was the whole problem with her life: nobody was ever exactly where they were supposed to be, including her.

She found him at dusk on the second day, or rather she found where he'd stopped being careful. A hollow under an overhang of rust-red stone, sheltered from the wind that came off the flats at night like a knife with no handle. He was asleep, curled around his own ribs, the stolen sack serving as both pillow and blanket. The cheese was gone. All of it. Eaten in one sitting, probably, the way starving people eat—fast and guilty and without tasting anything.

Lena stood over him for a long moment before he woke.

He woke badly, the way people wake when sleep is the only safe place they have left. He came up swinging, all elbows and terror, and she took his wrist on the second swing and held it, not squeezing, just holding, the way you hold a door shut against a wind that isn't really trying to get in.

"I'm not going to hurt you," she said, which was true, and also the least convincing thing anyone had ever said in the Saltreach, because everyone said it, right up until they didn't.

He didn't believe her. She could see it in his face—the particular arithmetic of a child who has learned that adults are weather, unpredictable and occasionally fatal. His eyes were the color of dust after rain that never comes. His wrists stuck out of his sleeves like knots in a rope.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Pip," he said, and then, quickly, as if he could take it back: "I don't have to tell you."

"You don't." She let go of his wrist. "But I'm going to walk you back to Rivergate Junction anyway, so you might as well be somebody while we do it."

* * *

They should have made the junction in a day and a half. It took them three, because Lena stopped.

She stopped when Pip's gait went wrong on the morning of the first day—not limping, exactly, but moving like a machine losing parts, each step costing more than the last. She stopped and boiled water for salted broth she'd been saving for herself. She stopped again when his feet split open inside boots two sizes too large, boots he'd clearly been given by someone who hadn't looked at him, only at the fact of him. She cut the boots down with her knife and wrapped his heels in strips of her own spare shirt, and then she sat with him through the hottest hour of the day because moving in that hour would have killed him faster than hunger would.

Stopping costs more than moving. That was the first law of the flats, older than any ranger's oath. Every hour you sit is water drunk without distance gained, is night caught in the open, is a debt the desert collects with interest. Lena knew the law better than most. She'd written part of it herself, in the years after her father died, when stopping meant thinking and thinking meant remembering and remembering meant the thing she'd built her whole discipline to avoid.

And still she stopped. Three times a day, every day, she stopped, and every time she did, some small hostile voice in her own skull asked what she thought she was doing, wasting rations on cargo.

Pip noticed. Of course he noticed. Starving children notice everything about the people who feed them; it's how they decide whether to trust the hand or watch it.

"Why do you chase us?" he asked on the second evening, sitting with his wrapped feet toward the small fire she allowed herself, because the nights up here went cold enough to crack skin. "People who already lost?"

Lena fed another stick into the flames. The firelight turned the salt crust beyond their camp into a field of dull coins, worthless and endless.

"Merchants pay me to," she said.

"That's why you started." He was sharper than his size suggested. Starving whittles the body but hones the mind sometimes, like a river stone. "Why do you keep doing it? You could've let me go a hundred times. You caught me in hours. You've spent three days keeping me alive."

She didn't have an answer ready. That itself was an answer, though she wasn't honest enough with herself yet to hear it.

"Because if I let you go," she said finally, "you die out here. And I'd know it. And knowing it would be worse than the three days."

"That's not discipline," Pip said. "That's just being alone."

Lena looked at him across the fire—a boy of maybe eleven, all bones and stubbornness, asking questions that grown men paid her good money never to ask—and felt something shift behind her sternum, some plate of armor she'd forgotten she was wearing grinding against another one.

"Eat your broth," she said.

* * *

Rivergate Junction announced itself before she saw it: first the smell of woodsmoke and frying oil, then the sound—hammers, mules, argument, the particular music of a town that lived off the last dry road before the river country began. By the time they walked in, Pip had stopped flinching at her shadow. Progress, of a kind.

Her crew found her before she'd finished handing the boy over to the junction magistrate, a gray woman named Odile who wrote everything down twice and trusted nothing once.

"There she is," Brisa called from the porch of the Wayhouse, arms crossed, apron dusted with flour she'd been hoarding against lean weeks. "Three days. We had a pool going. I had two days, six hours. Toma had four days because Toma has no faith in anyone."

"You're back," Toma said, from under the wagon he was ostensibly repairing and actually sleeping beneath. Only his boots were visible. "The wagon missed you. So did I, but the wagon said it first."

Lena dropped onto the porch steps and let the town happen around her. Brisa pressed a cup of something hot and terrible into her hands—the cook's tea, brewed from leaves that had failed as food and been promoted to punishment—and sat beside her close enough that their shoulders touched, which was as close as either of them ever came to saying anything warm.

"The magistrate's clerk says you brought him in walking," Brisa said. "Fed him the whole way. Gave him your shirt."

"He was dying."

"He was flour-thief skinny when he ran off. He'd have made the junction or he wouldn't have. That's how it works out here, isn't it? Isn't that what you always say?" Brisa's voice was gentle even when her words weren't. It was her gift and her weapon both. "Stopping costs more than moving."

"I know what I always say."

"Then why'd you stop?" Toma asked, rolling out from under the wagon with grease on his chin and genuine curiosity in his eyes, because Toma had never learned to hide curiosity behind anything, which made him a bad liar and a good friend. "Three days, Lena. Water spent, rations spent, wages burning while we sit. You're the disciplined one. You're the one who taught me the first law of the flats."

Lena wrapped both hands around the terrible tea and stared into it like it might show her something.

"Because I caught him in hours," she said slowly, "and then I had him. And having him meant deciding what he was worth. And I couldn't—" She stopped. Started again. "Out there, moving, I don't have to decide anything. Distance does the deciding. Speed does the feeling. You put one boot in front of the other enough times and you never once have to look at what you're carrying."

Brisa and Toma exchanged a look over her head—the look, the one married couples and old crews share, the one that says *she's doing the thing again.*

"She means," Brisa said, kindly, mercilessly, "that she carries an emptiness around and calls it discipline so nobody asks her about it. Including herself."

"I hate when you say it like that," Lena said.

"I know," said Brisa. "I say it anyway."

Toma patted the wagon. "We love you, boss. Even the empty parts. Especially those, honestly—they make you predictable, and predictable pays the wages."

Lena laughed despite herself, a short rough sound, and drank the terrible tea, and for one evening the emptiness was a thing they could joke about instead of a thing she had to outrun. That was the trick of her crew, though she'd never admit it was a trick: they made the weight communal. Four people carrying one person's silence spread it thin enough to bear.

Pip was gone by morning—Odile had kin two towns over, a tannery family, work and a roof—and Lena told herself she felt nothing about it, and then caught herself checking the road south three times before noon, and understood that she was lying.

* * *

It was the fourth day, the day the money ran properly thin, that Sef Marrow came up the dry road in a hired carriage too fine for the dust it raised.

Lena saw him from the porch before she heard him, and her body reacted before her mind did—the old clench, the old readiness, ten years of rivalry folded into a single tightening across the shoulders. Sef Marrow. The man who'd raced her down the Rivergate the season her father drowned, who'd won that race and every race since by wanting things less cleanly than she did.

He stepped down from the carriage in a coat that cost more than her crew earned in a year, and he smiled at her, and the smile was polished and wrong, like lacquer over rot.

"Lena Almeida," he said. "Still chasing thieves across the salt?"

"Still buying your way out of them," she said.

His smile widened a fraction, and he reached into the carriage, and what he brought out made the whole porch go quiet: a ledger, sealed in wax, bound in oiled leather, heavy as a brick and dark as a closed door.

"I need a crew," Sef said. "Yours. One run, downriver, before the monsoon seals the passes. There's gold in it—real gold, not junction wages. And an apology, while I'm standing here. For that season. For everything."

Lena looked at the ledger. She looked at the route it implied—the Rivergate, the water her father's boat had gone down in, the job she'd sworn off with an oath she'd carved somewhere private and never spoken aloud since.

Behind her, Toma straightened up and said, quietly, "How much gold?"

And Brisa, watching Lena's face rather than Sef's, said nothing at all.
