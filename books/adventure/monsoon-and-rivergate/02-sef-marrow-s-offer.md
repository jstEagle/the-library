## 2. Sef Marrow's Offer

The dust came first. It always did with Sef Marrow—dust, then the sound, then the man himself, arriving like a debt you'd forgotten you owed.

Lena Almeida was mending a saddle strap outside the dry-season camp when she saw the riders cresting the eastern rise, three of them, kicking up a brown haze against the white heat of the flats. She knew the lead horse before she knew the rider: a rangy dun with a white blaze, all shoulders and bad temper, the kind of animal only one man in the territory was stubborn enough to ride.

She set down the awl and watched them come, and something old and cold turned over in her chest.

"Sef Marrow," she said aloud, just to hear how the name sat in her mouth after six years. It sat like a stone.

Toma looked up from the engine housing he'd been rebuilding, grease to his elbows. "That the one who shot at you in Corvane?"

"He missed."

"That's not what I asked."

Lena didn't answer. Brisa emerged from the cook tent wiping her hands on her apron, squinting into the glare. Even Pip stopped his endless practice of throwing knives at a fence post and drifted closer, the way boys do when they smell trouble or money, unable to tell the difference.

Sef reined up twenty feet out and sat there a moment, letting the silence work for him. He'd always been good at that—making other people wait, making waiting feel like their idea. He was thinner than she remembered, gray coming into his beard early, but the eyes hadn't changed. Pale as river ice, patient as a trap.

"Lena." He dismounted slowly, joints cracking, an apology built into the way he moved. "You look well."

"I look tired," she said. "There's a difference."

"There is." He smiled, and it almost reached his eyes. Almost. "I've come to say I'm sorry."

"For which year?"

He laughed—a real laugh, brief and surprised out of him—and for half a second Lena saw the boy he'd been, the one who'd raced her across the salt flats when they were both too young to know what the racing cost. Then the smile closed back up like a fist.

"All of them," he said. "But mostly the last one. And I've come to offer you work, so if you want to shoot me first on principle, I'd understand. Might even make negotiations easier."

Brisa snorted. Toma muttered something under his breath about men who apologize with gold in their saddlebags, because of course Sef's saddlebags were full of gold; you could see the shape of the coin purses through the leather, could see the way the dun shifted under the weight of them.

"Say your piece," Lena said. "Then leave."

* * *

They talked in the shade of the supply wagon, because Sef insisted on sitting, and sitting meant this would take longer than anyone wanted.

"One job," he said. "A single run. There's a sealed ledger needs escorting down the Rivergate before monsoon season closes the passes. Three weeks, maybe four if the rains come late. You know the water better than any ranger working it. You grew up on it."

"I grew up *off* it," Lena said. "That was the point."

"The pay is five hundred in advance. Five hundred on delivery. No questions about the cargo—it stays sealed, it stays dry, it goes where I tell you."

"No questions." She studied him. "Whose ledger?"

"A client's."

"Which client?"

"A client who prefers—" He stopped himself, exhaled through his nose. "You were always like this. A dog sniffing every corner of every room."

"A dog survives," Lena said. "A trusting dog gets sold."

He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the charm dropped away and something rawer showed underneath—the hunger, the need, whatever had driven him out here with three hired guns and a purse full of coin to beg a favor from a woman who hated him. "Lena. I'm asking straight. The Rivergate closes in weeks. After that, nothing moves until the floods recede, and by then it'll be too late for reasons I can't explain without breaking my word, and my word is the only thing I have left that's worth anything. I need you. Not any ranger. *You.*"

"Why?"

"Because you don't lose cargo. Because you don't lose people. Because—" he hesitated, and the hesitation told her more than anything else he said— "because some things on that river are yours by right, whether you claim them or not."

Her blood went cold and slow, like water moving under ice.

"My father's route," she said quietly. "You're asking me to run my father's route."

"I'm asking you to run a river."

"You know what you're doing." She stood, and the shade fell away from her, and the full heat of the afternoon pressed down like a hand. "My father died on the Rivergate. Ten years ago, in flood season, running cargo for men who never gave their names. And I swore—I swore on his grave—that I would never take that water again. That I would stay on land, on salt, on dry ground, where nothing drowns. You know that oath. You were there when I made it. You held the lantern."

Sef looked up at her, and for once he didn't smile. "I remember the lantern," he said. "I also remember you were seventeen and grieving, and grief makes oaths it can't keep. People change, Lena. Rivers change. Maybe it's time you found out which one you are."

* * *

She told him no. She told him no twice, firmly, with the door of her tent between them the second time, and he took his gold and his riders and went to camp on the far side of the rise to wait, because Sef Marrow understood better than most men that a no was often just a yes that needed time to die.

The crew gathered around the cook fire that evening, and Lena let them talk without her.

"Five hundred now, five hundred later," Toma said, turning his cup in his hands. "That's a season's wages. That's parts for the truck, a new pump, real medicine instead of the stuff Brisa waters down."

"I don't water it down," Brisa said. "I stretch it. There's a difference." But she was quiet a moment, and then: "It's a lot of money. I won't pretend it isn't. My sister's roof won't last another wet season. If we come back with a thousand pieces, I can put stone on it. Stone doesn't rot."

Pip sat cross-legged near the fire, trying to look older than fourteen. "I vote yes," he said. "Obviously. I've never been down a river. I've barely seen a river."

"You've seen the consequences of rivers," Lena said from the edge of the light. "Every refugee camp from here to Veyra's Landing is full of people a river moved."

"So we'll be careful."

"We'll be *wet*," Toma said. "Careful comes after."

They voted. Four hands went up—Toma, Brisa, Pip, and after a long pause, during which he stared into the fire like a man reading his own future, the quiet scout named Aldous who rarely spoke and never voted wrong. Four yeses.

Lena stood outside the circle, arms crossed, and felt the vote land on her like weather.

"It's not your decision," Brisa said gently. "We work for you. But you taught us we vote on jobs that might kill us. So we voted."

"And if I still say no?"

"Then we don't go," Toma said simply. "And we spend the winter eating beans and watching your face while you wonder."

Lena looked at them—at the engineer with his ruined hands, at the cook with her sister's leaking roof, at the boy who'd stolen bread from her camp eight months ago and never left—and felt the loneliness she carried shift its weight, the way ballast shifts in a hull. Stopping cost more than moving. She'd learned that on the salt flats years ago, chasing a starving thief for three days because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant drowning on dry land.

"I said no," she told them. "I'm saying it again. We're not taking the job."

They nodded. They banked the fire. They went to bed.

And Lena sat alone in the dark with the oath she'd made at seventeen, turning it over like a stone worn smooth by ten years of handling, wondering when exactly it had stopped being a promise and started being a wall she hid behind.

* * *

She packed before dawn.

Not everything—she wasn't foolish enough to make it obvious, even to herself. A spare shirt. The oilskin wrap her father's compass lived in. Rope, flares, the river charts she'd sworn she'd burned and had instead buried in the bottom of her footlocker beneath ten years of denial, still folded along their original creases, still legible, still waiting.

She told herself it was pragmatism. That a good commander prepared for every outcome, including the one where her crew mutinied out of poverty and took the job without her, and someone had to keep them alive. That five hundred pieces in advance would buy Brisa's stone roof and Toma's pump and a winter without beans, and refusing it was vanity, not virtue. That Sef Marrow was a liar but he was a liar who paid, and paid liars were more reliable than honest paupers.

She told herself all of it, line by line, the way you talk yourself across a rope bridge, and none of it was true, and all of it was true, and somewhere between the two lay the simple fact she couldn't say aloud even alone in the dark:

The Rivergate was where her father's secrets lived. And she had spent ten years running from them, and running had kept her alive, and alive was not the same as living, and she was so tired.

At first light she walked up the eastern rise to where Sef's camp smoked against the sunrise. He was awake—of course he was awake; men like Sef slept like cats, in snatches, one ear open—and he watched her come with an expression she couldn't read, hope and calculation braided too tight to separate.

"You said no," he said.

"I said no twice."

"And?"

Lena stood in the gray light with her pack on her shoulder and her father's compass against her ribs, and she heard herself say the words she'd rehearsed all night, the ones dressed up as terms, as conditions, as professional caution:

"My rules. The crew votes on everything past the first mile. The ledger stays sealed unless I decide otherwise. And if I say we turn back—if I say it once—we turn back, and you eat the advance, and you never send riders up my rise again."

Sef smiled his closed-fist smile and spat, ceremonially, to seal it the old way. "Done," he said. "One sealed box, one river, four weeks. What could possibly go wrong?"

"Don't," Lena said. "Don't ever say that on a job of mine again."

They struck camp within the hour. The dun carried the gold; the wagons carried the gear; Pip ran alongside shouting questions about rivers until Brisa threatened to feed him to one. And Lena rode at the rear, as she always did, watching the salt flats fall away behind her mile by mile, feeling the dread sit in her chest like a swallowed stone—cold, smooth, certain of its own weight.

Pragmatism, she told herself. Just pragmatism.

But on the third morning, when the ground began to soften and the air turned green and wet and the first distant thunder rolled down from the high country where the Rivergate waited, she caught herself touching the oilskin wrap over her father's compass the way you touch a wound to check if it's still bleeding.

It was.

And at the river town of Graywater Landing, where the boats were hauled out and the rain had already begun to whisper against the planks, a stranger was waiting at the dock—thin, rain-dark, watchful—who watched their convoy arrive with the particular attention of a man who has been waiting a long time for exactly these people, and who stepped forward before Sef could speak, and said:

"You have her eyes. Has anyone ever told you that? You have your father's eyes."
