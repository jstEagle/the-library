## 3. What the Ledger Weighs

Ivo had learned long ago that the best way to board a boat was to already be on it.

He came down to the Rivergate docks before dawn, when the fog sat thick as wool on the water and the night watchmen had gone home to their suppers and their grievances. The *Salt Lark* rode low at her mooring, a river-hauler with patched gunwales and a hull that had been mended more times than most men mended fences. He stood in the shadow of a fish-drying rack and watched her for an hour, the way he'd watched her for three days from the ridgeline above town, learning her rhythms. The engineer—Toma, broad-shouldered, oil-black hands—came up from the engine well twice, coughing at the cold. A woman with flour to her elbows—Brisa—argued cheerfully with a crate of yams that had clearly lost the argument. And near the bow, coiling rope with the economy of someone who hated wasting motion even on rope, moved Lena Almeida.

Ten years, and she still walked like him.

Ivo felt that old wound open in his chest, the one he'd stopped trying to close around year four or five. He pressed it flat, the way you press a splinter deeper so it stops catching, and stepped out of the shadows onto the gangplank.

"Passage downriver," he said.

Lena didn't look up right away. She finished her coil, set it down, and only then turned, and Ivo braced himself for what ten years does to a face he'd last seen at fourteen, screaming his name across burning water. What he got was worse than hatred: she looked at him the way she'd look at any stranger, mild and measuring, and found nothing worth keeping.

"We're not a ferry."

"I'm not asking for one." He held up his hands, empty, palms out—an old habit from border towns where empty palms were currency. "No coin. I pay in knowledge instead."

Brisa appeared at Lena's shoulder, wiping her hands on her apron, interested immediately. "What kind of knowledge?"

"The kind that keeps boats off sandbars between here and the falls." Ivo let his gaze travel the river, gray and swollen with early rain, running fast against its banks. "The channel's shifted since the spring melt. Your charts are two years old. There's a gravel bar grown across the narrows at Kiln Bend that will open your hull like a letter."

Toma had come up too now, silent as ballast, arms crossed. "And how would a man with no coin know our charts are old?"

"Because I drew them." It was almost true; the originals had been his father's, and Ivo had corrected them by hand every season for a decade, walking the banks, sounding the shallows, keeping the map alive the way you keep a grave swept. "Ask me anything about the water between here and Monsoon Lock. If I'm wrong, put me ashore and I'll walk."

Lena studied him. He endured it. Somewhere behind her eyes, he thought—not recognition, nothing so kind—but perhaps a shape, a familiarity, the way a song heard once in childhood can feel like memory without being one.

"You eat less than you talk," she said finally. "Aft cargo hold. You sleep in shifts with Pip."

"Pip?"

"The boy," Brisa said, grinning. "Don't ask. Welcome aboard, mystery man."

* * *

The ledger came aboard that evening, and Ivo understood then that everything he had protected for ten years was now sealed in oilcloth in the hand of the daughter of the man who wrote it.

Sef Marrow had delivered it at dusk with all his polished ceremony—the silver-sealed case, the assurances, the payment counted out in heavy coin while Lena watched him with an expression Ivo recognized because he'd worn it himself for years: the look of a person swallowing something that wanted to come back up. Sef had been charming. Sef was always charming. That was the whole architecture of the man, charm laid over rot like gilt over lead.

Ivo kept to the shadows of the deck while the crew gathered around the case. He told himself it was prudence. It was not. It was cowardice, plain as bread, and he hated himself for it with a comfortable, practiced fluency.

Lena broke the seal herself.

She peeled back the oilcloth with a knife, careful, unhurried—and Ivo watched her face go through the stages. First the merchant's assessment: weight, binding, condition. Then confusion, her thumb pausing on the cover's tooled leather. Then stillness—a terrible, total stillness, the kind that comes over people when the ground moves beneath them and they haven't yet admitted it.

"What is this?" Her voice came out level. Too level.

"Ledger," Toma said. "Debts, probably. Marrow said—"

"I know what Marrow said." She opened it.

Ivo moved before he decided to move. Later he would tell himself it was to see, to confirm, to protect—but in the moment there was no reason at all, only the pull of a thing ten years in the making. He crossed the deck and stood at her shoulder as lamplight fell across the first page.

The handwriting hit him like a fist. Elias Almeida had written the way he'd lived: fast, slanted left, pressing hard enough to emboss the next page. Ivo had spent years reading that hand—in letters, in margin notes, in the last unfinished entry of a journal he still kept wrapped in oilskin under his floorboards. Seeing it here, alive again under lamplight, made his throat close.

Lena's breath went out of her slowly.

"That's—" Brisa started.

"My father's hand," Lena said.

The word *father* dropped into the group like a stone into still water. Nobody touched it. Toma glanced at Ivo—Ivo felt the glance and gave nothing back—and Brisa quietly took the lamp and raised it higher, giving light without being asked, which was, Ivo would learn, simply who Brisa was.

Lena turned pages. Debts. Columns of them, dates and sums and names, written in a code that wasn't a code at all but shorthand, the private cipher of a man who'd kept honest books for dishonest houses. House Veyra—grain tariffs skimmed for eleven years, a drowned witness at Ferrow's Landing. House Calloway—bribed lock-keepers, falsified flood ledgers, three villages taxed into starvation while granaries stayed full. House Ambrose. House Pell. Every noble name on the river, each with its sins itemized in a dead man's neat, furious script.

"He was a factor," Lena said, mostly to herself. "He kept accounts. He never told me—" She stopped. Started again. "He never talked about work."

"No," Ivo said quietly. "He wouldn't have."

Her head came up sharply. For a moment they looked at each other across the open ledger, and Ivo saw the question form in her—the same question forming in Toma's narrowed eyes and Brisa's tilted head—and he had no answer ready that wasn't the whole truth, and the whole truth was a country he wasn't sure either of them could survive yet.

"How do you know what my father would or wouldn't have done?" she asked.

"I've read his writing before." It was true. It was also a door he could not yet open, and he watched her decide—visibly, deliberately—to leave it shut, because the ledger was still open under her hands and whatever was in it mattered more than a stranger's secrets. He both admired and resented her for that.

She turned another page. Another. The rain had begun somewhere upstream—you could hear it in the river's voice, a new urgency under the old one—and the lamp guttered and steadied. Then Lena stopped turning.

Her finger rested on the bottom third of the page, on a line written smaller than the rest, in the space where a factor records the disposition of an estate.

Ivo read it upside down. He had known it was there. He had read it a thousand times in memory. Knowing did nothing to soften it.

*To the heir: L. Almeida. All debts herein transfer by blood. Let her choose what I could not.*

Lena read it three times. Ivo watched her lips move slightly on the third pass, silent, and watched her knuckles whiten on the page's edge until the paper trembled. When she spoke, her voice was very quiet and very controlled, which Ivo knew—from knowing her father, from knowing herself without knowing her—was the most dangerous register she owned.

"Heir." A short laugh with nothing in it. "He listed me. In a book of debts. As if I were—" She closed the ledger. Opened it again, as if checking whether the words would behave differently the second time. They hadn't. "Every house on this river owes something. And I inherit the collecting."

"Not the collecting," Ivo said.

Everyone looked at him.

"The choosing," he said. "That's what the line says. *Let her choose.*"

"You don't know what it says. You don't know what he meant." But her voice cracked on *meant*, just barely, a hairline fracture through the level tone, and Ivo understood that the wound wasn't the inheritance at all. The wound was that the father she'd mourned as a stranger—a factor, an honest dull man who died on a river run—had been someone else entirely, someone with enemies worth poisoning rivers over, someone who had thought about her in his last months with enough care to write her name into danger rather than leave her out of it. Grief, reopened and re-inventoried. Ten years of mourning spent on the wrong man.

"He knew houses would kill for this," Lena said. "He knew, and he wrote my name in it anyway."

"Maybe," Brisa offered gently, "he trusted you with it."

"Maybe he sentenced me." Lena shut the ledger. Her hands were shaking now, fine and fast, and she pressed them flat against the cover as if holding something down. "Marrow knew. That's why he came to me. That's why the route, why the timing, why *me*. I'm not the escort. I'm the delivery. My name makes this paper worth killing for, and he's been paid by whoever's waiting downstream."

Toma said nothing, which was its own agreement. Brisa looked at the dark water and said nothing, which was hers.

Only Ivo spoke, and he spoke carefully, laying each word down like a plank over deep water. "Then the question isn't what the ledger weighs. It's who carries it. A name can be a chain or a key. He gave you the choice. Don't let Marrow make it for you."

Lena looked at him for a long time. Rain began to fall properly now, ticking on the deck boards, running off the awning in bright threads. Somewhere below, Pip was singing badly to himself, oblivious, and the sound of it was so ordinary that it hurt.

"Who are you?" she asked again, softer this time. Not suspicion now. Something rawer.

Ivo held her gaze and told her the truest lie he had. "Someone who owes your father."

She nodded slowly, accepting it the way you accept a bridge you can't inspect—because the alternative is staying on this bank forever. She tucked the ledger inside her coat, against her chest, where a heartbeat could keep it company.

"First watch is yours, then," she said. "Owed men stand watches."

She went below. Brisa followed with the lamp. Toma lingered a moment, studying Ivo with an engineer's eye for load-bearing structures, then followed too.

Ivo stood alone in the rain at the rail, watching black water run toward the falls, and let himself say it aloud at last, so quiet the river nearly took it:

"Hello, Lena."

Upstream, far back along the bank, a lantern blinked three times—paused—then blinked three times again. A signal. Someone on shore was talking to someone on this boat, and it wasn't Ivo.

He turned toward the stern, toward the faint glow of Sef Marrow's cabin lamp, and began, quietly, to count the ways a betrayal announces itself before it arrives.
