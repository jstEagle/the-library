## 4. Downriver

### Toma

The Rivergate narrowed on the second day the way a fist closes—slowly, then all at once.

Toma felt it in the engine before he saw it in the banks. The vibration changed pitch, a half-tone higher, the water fighting harder against the hull as the channel squeezed between walls of red stone that leaned closer with every bend. He knelt in the engine room with his ear against the housing like a doctor listening to a chest, and what he heard was effort. The river was working now. So were they.

He'd built engines for twenty years and boats for fifteen, and he could tell you this much for free: no machine likes a narrowing. Everything in the world wants to spread out, take up room, be lazy about it. Squeeze a thing and it either breaks or it gets honest. The *Salt Lark* was getting honest. Every bolt he'd ever doubted was announcing itself. The portside shaft bearing had developed a voice—a thin metallic whine, high and complaining—and Toma lay under the deck plates with a grease gun and a lamp, coaxing it quiet, while above him the rain began.

Not monsoon rain. Not yet. This was the overture, the soft gray drizzle that came down like something apologetic, beading on the hatch glass and running in long crooked lines. But Toma had run the Gate in flood years before, back when he was young enough to think rivers respected courage. He knew what that drizzle was promising. Somewhere upstream, past the last of the passes, the sky was already emptying itself into the high country, and all of it—all of it—was coming down this throat of stone toward them.

He climbed out of the engine room stiff-kneed and found Brisa in the galley doorway watching the water with her arms crossed.

"How's our patient?" she asked, meaning the boat, meaning everything.

"Complaining but cooperative." He wiped his hands on a rag that had been black so long it was more rag-color than cloth. "Bearing wants replacing in Veyra's Landing. If we make Veyra's Landing."

"If." She said it lightly, but she'd said it the way you say a word when you've been chewing on it all morning.

Toma looked downriver. The channel ahead bent hard around a spur of rock, and beyond the bend the walls closed until the sky was a strip of pewter no wider than a road. Rain stippled the surface, thousands of small rings, each one a tiny message from the weather above. *We're coming,* the rings said. *We're coming.*

He went back below. There was always one more thing to tighten.

### Brisa

Brisa fed people the way other people prayed.

It wasn't sentimentality, whatever Pip thought when he hovered at the galley door sniffing the air like a stray outside a bakery window. It was engineering. A crew was a machine made of meat, and meat ran on heat and salt and the specific alchemy of a meal served hot at the end of a cold watch. Feed them well and they'd forgive the boat its leaks, the river its temper, each other their histories. Starve them and watch how fast a family comes apart into passengers.

Because that was what they were becoming. She'd noticed it around the third bowl of stew.

Lena Almeida ate like a woman keeping an appointment—efficient, silent, done. But tonight she'd come back for seconds, and she'd stood at the counter while Brisa ladled it, and she'd said, unprompted, "The pepper's different."

"Bought it off a barge trader at the last lock. He swore it came over the mountains."

"It's good." Two words. From Lena, that was a speech.

And Ivo—the stranger who'd paid his passage in knowledge instead of coin, who sat at the edge of every gathering like a man attending his own trial—Ivo had washed his own bowl without being asked, and then, without being asked, had washed everyone else's too, stacking them in the rack with a care that made Brisa's chest hurt in some place she didn't have a name for. Men who washed dishes like that had usually lost kitchens. Had usually lost more than kitchens.

Even Sef Marrow, distant as a lighthouse seen from far offshore, had eaten what she gave him and left nothing. Small mercy. She'd caught him twice on the aft deck after dark, hunched over something in his hands—a shuttered lantern, a scrap of mirror, she couldn't tell—and both times he'd pocketed it quick as a boy hiding cigarettes and smiled at her with all the warmth of a locked door.

She didn't like it. She filed it away next to the pepper trader's warning—that there were riders on the towpath roads asking after a boat heading downriver, asking polite questions with hard eyes—and she kept cooking.

That night she made flatbread with the last of the good flour and honey she'd been saving for a bad day, because she was starting to suspect the bad days would need the crew more than the crew would need the honey. She set it out on the deck plates under the awning, and the rain drummed overhead, and five people and a thief sat in a circle eating bread with their hands, and nobody talked about the ledger, or the storm, or the men Sef might be talking to in the dark.

Pip laughed at something Toma said. Lena almost smiled. Ivo refilled the tea without looking up, as if he'd always been the one who did that.

Brisa watched them and understood, with the clarity of a woman standing hip-deep in a river she couldn't stop, that families don't form by decision. They form by repetition. By bowls passed hand to hand. By watches shared. By bread broken in the rain.

Nobody had voted for this. That was how you knew it was real.

### Pip

Pip learned three things that week, and only one of them was cards.

The first thing was that Toma cheated at cards, but badly, in a way that was almost generous—his tells were so loud they had to be deliberate, a kind of charity disguised as incompetence. When he had a good hand he tapped his knee. When he bluffed he looked at the ceiling. Pip took his coins for two nights straight before Brisa told him to quit embarrassing the old man.

"He's letting you win," she said, drying a pot. "It's how he says hello."

The second thing was that Ivo knew things. Not useful things, not at first—useless things, beautiful things. That the red stone of the canyon walls was older than any house on the river. That the marks chiseled above the third lock were toll-runes from before the noble families had names, cut by boatmen who'd charged passage in salt and stories. That if you held your palm flat on the deck planking during the deep-water stretches you could feel the river thinking. Pip tried it. The river did think. It thought in long slow shoves, patient as a debt collector.

The third thing—the real lesson—came on the fourth night, when the rain thickened from drizzle into something with intent, and the clouds tore open just enough to show a handful of stars, and Lena Almeida climbed onto the cargo cover and said, "Come here, thief. You're going to learn the sky."

Pip went. You didn't say no to Lena; you just found out later why you wouldn't have wanted to.

She pointed, and named. The Lantern. The Ferryman, lying along the southern horizon with his pole trailing. The Two Sisters, close together, one brighter—one faithful, one who'd wandered, which Pip privately thought was unfair of the stars to advertise. She taught him the one star that never moved, sitting due south above the river's mouth, and showed him how everything else wheeled around it like a family around a table.

"A boat with no compass can still find home," she said, "if someone aboard knows that star. Remember it. Whatever else happens."

"Why me?" Pip asked. "Why teach me?"

Lena was quiet a moment. Downriver the canyon walls glowed faint gray-green with the last light, and the rain ticked against the cargo canvas, and somewhere behind them Sef stood at the stern rail doing whatever Sef did at night, which was nothing Pip was allowed to know about.

"Because everyone should have one thing," she said finally, "that nobody can take off them. Not buyers, not houses, not floods. It stays yours even if they take the boat, the coin, the name." She glanced at him. "You've got quick hands. Quick hands get taken away—jail, chains, worse. A sky doesn't fit in anyone's pocket."

Pip lay back on the wet canvas and memorized it. The unmoving star. The Ferryman's pole. The Sisters, faithful and wandered.

He fell asleep up there, and woke once in the grey hours to find a blanket over him that hadn't been there before, and didn't ask whose, because asking would ruin it.

### Ivo

Ivo kept the night watch alone, which suited him, and then stopped suiting him, which surprised him.

It happened gradually, the way trust does—so slowly you can't name the hour it arrived. First it was Brisa leaving a covered mug on the chart box during his shift, still warm, no note. Then Toma showing him where the spare fuses lived, which on a boat is roughly equivalent to handing a man a key to your house. Then Pip, orbiting closer each evening with his questions, until one night the boy sat beside him on the rail and asked, point-blank:

"You knew Lena's da, didn't you?"

Ivo watched the black water slide past. "What makes you say that?"

"You watch her like a letter you can't read. And you flinch when the ledger box rattles."

Out of the mouths of thieves. Ivo considered lying, found he didn't have the energy, and settled for silence, which Pip accepted with the wisdom of a child who knows that silence is often just a yes wearing a coat.

But it was Lena herself who broke the pattern, on the fifth night, in the worst way possible.

He was at the wheel when she came up the steps, sleepless, and stood beside him without speaking for a long time. The rain had eased to a fine mist. The channel ran narrow and deep, the walls close enough to touch with a boat hook, and the *Salt Lark* moved through the gorge like a secret through a crowded room.

"Sef's been signaling," she said at last. Her voice was flat, careful, a floor tested before weight goes on it. "Shuttered lantern. Three flashes, pause, two. Same pattern, every night since the second lock. Upstream-facing." She turned and looked at him. "You knew. Don't lie to me—I've watched you watch him."

Ivo's hands tightened on the wheel spokes. He had known. He'd recognized the rhythm the first night—three and two, three and two—an old courier code from the river trade, position and speed, the kind of thing buyers paid for and partners died over. He'd said nothing because saying something meant explaining *how* he knew, and explaining how he knew meant opening the box he'd carried sealed for ten years.

"I suspected," he said.

"Suspected." Lena tasted the word and spat it out. "The ledger in that box has my father's handwriting in it. My name. And the man who hired me to carry it is selling our route to whoever's asking." She looked back downriver, into the dark where the gates of Veyra's Landing waited somewhere beyond the rain. "Tomorrow we pass the narrows. After that, the locks. After that—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Watch him, Ivo. And watch yourself. Because if you're holding anything else back, anything, I need it now. Tonight. Before the river decides for us."

Behind them, faint through the mist, came the soft click of a lantern shutter. Three flashes. Pause. Two.

Ivo looked at the woman who didn't know yet what he was to her, and at the man who had sold the road beneath all their feet, and at the black water rising inch by inch toward the top of the banks, and understood that the time for carrying things quietly was over.

"Below decks," he said. "There's something you need to see. Both of us. Now—before we reach the locks."

Lena's eyes went hard as river stone. "Then show me."
