## 5. The First Leak

Toma found the first hole the way you find anything wrong with a boat: by lying down and listening.

He'd gone into the bilge after the evening watch, chasing a sound that shouldn't have been there—a soft, rhythmic *tick* beneath the engine noise, like a fingernail tapping the inside of the hull. The Rivergate was running high with early rain, brown and fast, carrying silt that made everything sound muffled. But this tick was clean. Metallic. Patient.

He wedged himself between the fuel tank and the starboard stringer with a lantern hooked on his belt, and there it was. A hole. Bored, not broken—clean-edged, the diameter of his little finger, drilled through three centimeters of seasoned river oak as neatly as a cabinetmaker's work. No splintering outward, which meant it had been done from inside. No tool marks he recognized, which meant whoever did it had used something slow and quiet. A hand auger, maybe. A nail set in a wooden handle, turned ten thousand times while everyone slept.

And it wasn't alone.

He crawled the length of the bilge with his lantern held low, feeling along the seams where the planking met the frames, and found two more. One amidships, tucked behind the water tank where no one would look unless they were looking for exactly that. One aft, under the engine bed, in the darkest corner of the boat—the kind of place Toma himself would have chosen, if Toma were the kind of man who bored holes in boats.

Three holes. Below the waterline. All of them plugged.

That was the part that chilled him. Not the drilling. The plugs. Each hole was stopped with a tapered wooden dowel, waxed, seated flush, then painted over with hull tar so carefully matched to the boat's own coating that he'd run his thumb across two of them before he felt the seam. Whoever did this hadn't wanted the boat to sink. Not yet. They'd wanted the boat to sink *later*. Somewhere narrow. Somewhere deep. Somewhere with a lock gate chained shut and armed men waiting on the bank.

Toma lay in the bilge for a long time, lantern light pooling around him, river water whispering against the outside of the hull an inch above his head, and let himself understand what he was looking at. This wasn't sabotage. It was a schedule.

* * *

He came up onto the deck at midnight and stood in the rain a moment before saying anything, because once he said it, nothing would be the same.

The boat was the *Kestrel's Debt*—Lena had never told him what debt, and he'd never asked—and she was forty feet of stubborn old rivercraft that Toma had rebuilt twice and loved once, badly, in a year he didn't talk about. She leaked the way old women complain: constantly, harmlessly, from everywhere and nowhere. Her bilge pump ran on a timer. Every twenty minutes it coughed up a few liters of seepage and went back to sleep.

But tonight the pump was coughing more than seepage. He'd noticed it on his own watch, actually—that was why he'd gone below. The cycle had shortened. Twenty minutes, then fifteen, then eleven. A boat doesn't speed up its complaints for free.

Brisa was in the galley keeping water hot, because Brisa always kept water hot when the weather turned; she said rain without tea was just drowning with extra steps. Pip was asleep in the bow cubby, curled around the stolen ledger like it was a stuffed animal, which Toma found both adorable and alarming. Lena had the wheel. Ivo sat under the awning aft, awake, as he always seemed to be awake, mending a net nobody used.

Toma stood under the awning out of the rain and looked at all of them, one at a time, and hated what he was about to do.

"Lena," he said. "Kill the pump."

She blinked at him through the spray coming off the wheel. "Why?"

"Because I want to hear how fast we're taking water without it."

The silence that followed lasted four seconds, and in those four seconds Toma watched every face on the deck arrive at the same arithmetic from a different direction. Brisa set down the kettle. Pip's eyes opened in the dark of the cubby. Ivo's hands went still on the net.

Lena killed the pump.

They listened. Under the floorboards, in the well of the bilge, water was rising—not fast, not dramatically, but steadily, the way a lie rises in a room full of people who each thought they were the only honest one. Toma timed it with his thumb against his wrist. Two centimeters in six minutes. That was a hole the size of a finger, open, somewhere below. Maybe more than one. The dowels were weeping. Wax only holds so long against a river that wants in.

"There are three holes in this hull," Toma said quietly. "Drilled from the inside. Plugged so they'd fail downstream, past the narrows, where stopping means dying." He paused. "Someone on this boat did it."

* * *

Nobody said much for the next hour, which is its own kind of scream.

They ran the pump continuously and took turns in the bilge with lanterns and pitch and canvas patches, and the work itself was simple—Toma had patched worse in worse conditions—but the air above decks was another matter. You could feel suspicion moving through the crew like the rain moved through the rigging: finding every gap, filling every space, dripping off everything.

Pip was searched first, gently, by Brisa, who found nothing but a bent spoon, seven river stones of varying beauty, and half a biscuit the boy had been saving "for an emergency." Pip cried anyway, not because he was accused but because he understood, with the terrible clarity of children, that being searched meant the searching had to stop somewhere, and it wouldn't stop with him.

Brisa searched herself next, loudly, turning out her own galley drawers and her own pockets and daring anyone to find a hand auger among her spice tins. Nobody dared. Brisa's anger was the loud kind, the protective kind, the kind that put itself between the crew and the cold.

Lena didn't search anyone. Lena stood at the wheel with rain running off her jaw and stared downriver at the black water and the blacker sky, and Toma, who had known her four years, had never seen her look like that. Not angry. Not afraid. *Counting.* She was counting people, the way you count people when you've realized one of them isn't yours.

Which left Ivo.

Ivo, who had come aboard at the dock paying passage with knowledge instead of coin. Ivo, who knew things about the ledger. Ivo, who never slept, or slept so lightly it amounted to the same thing. Ivo, whose hands had been still on that net when Toma said *someone on this boat*, and whose face had done nothing at all.

It was Pip who said it, because children say the thing everyone else is thinking with their mouth full of fear. The boy came up from the bow cubby, wet-haired, hugging the ledger, and looked at Ivo across the deck and said:

"You were awake last night. I saw you. You were walking around."

"I walk at night," Ivo said. "I don't sleep well."

"You walked near the engine."

"I walked everywhere. It's a small boat." His voice was even. Too even, maybe. The voice of a man reciting facts he'd prepared.

Lena came off the wheel. She crossed the deck slowly, rain streaming off her, and stood in front of Ivo, and Toma heard her ask the question the way you ask a question when you already know the answer will cost you either way.

"Where were you at second watch?"

"Asleep. Badly." Ivo met her eyes. "You can check my bunk. It looks slept in, because I lay in it. It also looks untouched, because I got up. I don't know which of those convicts me more."

"It wasn't me," Pip whispered, to no one, uselessly, heartbreakingly.

"I know it wasn't you," Lena said, without looking away from Ivo. "You can't drill a hole straight through oak with a child's arm strength. It takes weight behind it. An adult. Someone with boat knowledge—" and here her voice changed, tightened, became something older and harder, "—someone who knows where the frames are. Where the stringers run. Where a hole won't be found until the river finds it for you."

Boat knowledge. And there were exactly two people aboard with boat knowledge deep enough to place those holes blind, behind the water tank, under the engine bed, flush-seamed and tar-matched. Toma was one of them.

Ivo was the other.

* * *

It went bad quickly after that, the way these things go bad—suddenly, and then all at once, and then irreversibly.

Brisa said the word nobody wanted said. "Search him." And Ivo, to his credit or his ruin, Toma couldn't yet tell which, said, "Fine. Search me. Search my kit. Search my boots. Take apart everything I own, which is one bag, one coat, and a book wrapped in oilcloth."

They did. Brisa and Toma together, because Toma insisted on doing it properly and Brisa insisted on doing it thoroughly, and they found: clothes worn soft at the elbows. A tin cup. A folding knife, dull. Rope. A flint. The oilcloth bundle, which turned out to be a book of river charts, hand-copied, decades old, annotated in a cramped left-slanting script with depths and channels and notes in the margins—"sandbar shifts after flood," "Veyra men patrol here," "do not trust the lock-keeper at Marrow Bend"—and one page folded back on itself, marked with a river-stone, showing a stretch of the drowned channel that wasn't on any chart Toma had ever seen.

No auger. No drill. No nails loose enough to bore oak. Nothing.

And still the accusation hung in the rain, because finding nothing proves less than finding something. Absence of evidence aboard a leaking boat is just another form of water.

"He could've thrown it overboard," Brisa said flatly.

"In moving water, at night, over the side of a boat where any splash carries?" Toma shook his head. "We'd have heard it. Sound carries on the river like a bell."

"Then someone brought tools aboard and took them off again," Brisa said. "Or someone did it before we ever cast off."

That landed on all of them at once, the way the worst possibilities do—silently, and heavily, and everywhere. Because the boat had spent two days moored at the dock before departure. Open. Accessible. Anybody could have boarded her. Anybody with an auger and an hour and a reason.

Except—

Toma thought about it now, lying on his side in the bilge with his shoulder against the frame, working a heated patch iron into the tar around the first hole while the river pressed its whole gray weight against the other side of the planking. He thought about it the way he thought about engines: sequentially, mechanically, cause before effect.

The holes had been painted over with hull tar. Matched tar. *Aged* tar—he'd checked, scraped a curl of it with his thumbnail, and the underside was oxidized, dulled, the same color as the rest of the hull's coating. Fresh tar dries glossy and stays glossy for weeks. This tar had been applied long enough ago to cure dull. Which meant the holes weren't bored during their two days at the dock.

They were bored before Toma had ever bought the boat.

Before Lena. Before the ledger. Before any of them.

Somebody had prepared this vessel years ago—or months, at least—for a journey that hadn't happened yet. Somebody had built a dead man's route into a living boat's hull, and waited.

* * *

He came up at dawn with tar on his forearms and the truth in his mouth, and the truth was worse than a saboteur.

"It wasn't done recently," he told them, in the gray light, rain still falling, the river high and brown and pushing. "The tar over those plugs is cured. Months old, maybe longer. Whoever drilled those holes did it before this voyage existed. Before we were a crew. Possibly before some of us had ever met."

Silence. The good kind, for once—the kind where everyone is thinking instead of accusing.

"So it's not one of us," Pip said slowly.

"No," Toma said. "It was never one of us. We were just the ones who bought the trap."

Lena's face did something complicated then—something Toma watched closely, because he'd learned over four years that Lena Almeida's face was a locked room and you only ever got shown the hallway. She looked downriver. She looked at the boat. She looked, briefly, at Ivo, and something passed across her expression that might have been recognition, or might have been dread, or might have been both wearing the same coat.

"The man who owned this boat before me," she said quietly, "was named Aldous Vey."

Brisa made a small sound, half gasp, half prayer.

"House Veyra," Toma said. "The name in the ledger margins. The name on the lock gates."

"The family that owns the locks ahead," Lena finished. "The family that chained them."

Ivo stood very still under the awning, holding his mended net, and when he finally spoke his voice was low and rough, like something dragged up from under water.

"Aldous Vey didn't build those holes for cargo runs," he said. "He built them for one specific journey. One specific boat. One specific passenger." He paused, and the rain filled the pause with static. "He built them for the man who was supposed to die on this river ten years ago. And when that man died anyway—somewhere else, in some other boat—the holes stayed. Waiting."

Lena turned to him fully then, and Toma saw her knuckles whiten on the rail, and saw her ask, in a voice gone thin and careful as wire:

"What man?"

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. Rain ran off his chin. When he answered, he answered quietly, and the answer went through the crew like a spike through a hull.

"Your father, Lena. The holes were bored for your father's boat. And somebody has been keeping them maintained ever since."

* * *

They sailed the rest of that day in a silence that had changed shape entirely—it wasn't suspicion anymore, it was grief wearing suspicion's clothes, and nobody knew quite how to take the clothes off.

Toma spent the daylight hours below, patching properly what he'd patched temporarily in the night. Canvas and pitch over each dowel, copper sheeting over the canvas, screws driven into fresh wood so the repair would hold against monsoon pressure. It was good work, honest work, the kind of work that lets a man think clearly with his hands while his head goes somewhere darker.

And his head went somewhere darker. Because if the holes had been maintained—if the tar had been renewed, if the plugs had been replaced—then someone had been aboard this boat within recent memory. Someone with access. Someone patient. Someone who wanted the *Kestrel's Debt* on the bottom of the Rivergate with a specific cargo and a specific crew.

He surfaced at dusk with the last patch set and climbed into the cockpit where the others were gathered around Brisa's stove, sharing tea that tasted mostly of worry, and found them talking in low voices about the lock gates ahead. Chained, according to the chart annotations. Guarded, according to Ivo's margins. House Veyra's territory, all of it, the whole narrowing throat of the river downstream.

"We could turn back," Brisa said, not meaning it.

"We can't," Lena said, meaning it completely. "The passes close in nine days. Behind us is desert and Sef Marrow and whatever game he's playing. Ahead of us is the drowned channel and whatever my father died trying to finish." She looked at her hands around the tin cup. "There's no version of this where going back is safer. There's only versions where we choose what we're sailing toward."

Pip, curled against Brisa's side, asked the question Toma was thinking: "Who maintained the holes?"

And Ivo—who had been quiet since dawn, who had been watching Lena with an expression Toma couldn't read and didn't want to—set down his cup and said:

"I did."

The word dropped into the cockpit like an anchor.

"Ten years," Ivo said, to Lena, to all of them, to the rain. "Every dry season, I found this boat. I checked the plugs. I renewed the tar. I kept the trap alive, because if I let it die, the people who wanted your father's route buried would know the plan had failed—and they'd build a new one, a worse one, one none of us would see coming." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I kept the holes so I'd know where they were. So that someday, when the right crew sailed this boat down this river, someone aboard would find them in time."

Toma stared at him. "You're telling us you sabotaged a boat for a decade to prevent a sabotage."

"I'm telling you," Ivo said, "that I've been waiting ten years for someone to notice what I couldn't fix alone."

Lena stood up. The tin cup fell from her hands and rolled, ringing, across the deck boards, and nobody moved to catch it. She stood in front of Ivo with the rain coming down harder now, the first real teeth of the monsoon arriving out of the south, and she said, very quietly:

"You knew my father."

"I rowed beside him," Ivo said, "for six years."

"And you let me buy his killer's boat."

"I let you buy the only boat on this river that already knows the way." 

Lightning opened the sky downstream, white and total, and in that single flash Toma saw the river ahead: narrower, faster, and lined, far off on the eastern bank, with the small hard lights of a lock station that should not have been manned this early in the season.

Manned anyway.

Lena turned back to the wheel. Her voice, when it came, was steady in the way that cost her something visible.

"Then you're going to tell me everything," she said, "before we reach those lights. Everything he planned. Everything he carried. Everything he died with." She put her hands on the wheel. "Starting with why the ledger has my name in it."

And the rain came down like the sky had been waiting ten years to say something too.
