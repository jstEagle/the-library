## 6. Monsoon Lock

Brisa had cooked through storms before. She'd fed a crew through the tail end of a cyclone off the Salt Coast, through three days of hail in the high passes, through a night when lightning struck the mast and Toma laughed about it for a month. She knew what weather sounded like when it was only weather.

This was not only weather.

The rain came down like the sky had been holding it back for years and had finally stopped pretending. It hit the deck in sheets, in fists, in a sound so total that Brisa could not hear her own pots rattling on their hooks, and Brisa's pots always rattled. The Rivergate, which had been wide enough all week to feel almost gentle, had swollen brown and furious, carrying whole trees past them at the speed of galloping horses. The boat groaned against its mooring lines like something alive and unhappy.

She stood in the galley doorway with a towel over her head that had stopped being useful an hour ago, watching Lena and Ivo argue on the foredeck. Their voices didn't carry. Nothing carried. But she could read the shape of it in their shoulders—Lena's set hard, Ivo's hands moving in small urgent circles—and she thought, not for the first time, that she had signed onto this job to cook for people, and somehow kept signing on because of moments exactly like this one. Moments where the world got loud and the people on her boat got quiet and true.

Toma came below dripping, hauling the pump handle like a man who'd made peace with his own arms falling off. "Water's up two fingers since noon," he said. "I've got the seams stuffed but the hull's talking to me."

"What's it saying?"

"That we should have turned around at the last lock." He wrung out his shirt over the bilge. "Also that your stew smells incredible and if I die tonight I want everyone to know it wasn't the food."

Brisa ladled him a bowl anyway, because that was what she did. You couldn't fix a hull with stew. But you could fix a crew, sometimes, or at least hold the cracks together long enough for someone else to patch them. She'd learned that on boats where nobody said they were family because saying it out loud would have jinxed it, and then one day the word just arrived, uninvited, and stayed.

Pip sat in the corner wrapped in every dry blanket the boat owned, shivering, reading the star charts Lena had given him even though there hadn't been a visible star in two days. He held the pages close like they were warm. "Do you think," he asked, mouth full of bread Brisa had forced on him, "that the river knows where it's going?"

"The river always knows," Brisa said. "It's us that doesn't."

* * *

They heard the lock before they saw it. Not the gates themselves—the bell. A single bronze note, hammered flat by rain, rolling down the gorge like a warning shot. Then another. Then a rhythm, deliberate and cold, and Brisa understood immediately that bells don't ring themselves.

Lena brought the boat in slow against the current, and what they found made her blood go cold, though Brisa only learned that later, from the way Lena's face changed. The lock gates ahead were chained shut. Not jammed, not broken—chained, deliberately, with iron links thick as a man's forearm, padlocked twice, and hanging from the near tower was a banner that had no business surviving this weather: the black-and-gold of House Veyra.

Riders lined the lock walls. Eight of them, maybe ten, in oiled cloaks, lances racked, faces hidden under hoods pulled tight against the rain. They looked, Brisa thought, less like soldiers than like men who had been paid to stand somewhere miserable and wait, which meant somebody had paid them well, and recently.

A voice came across the water, amplified by the gorge itself until it sounded like a verdict handed down from the sky.

"Boat on the river. By order of House Veyra, the lock is closed. Surrender what you carry and you will be allowed to pass when the waters fall. Refuse, and the river will take you, and no one will look for you until spring."

Brisa had heard threats before. This one was different. This one was patient. It didn't shout; it waited. It offered them a way out that was really a way into a cage, dressed up as mercy, and it counted on the fact that frightened people take bargains.

Lena did not answer right away. She stood at the bow, soaked through, staring at the chained gates, and Brisa watched her do the thing she'd seen her do once before, years ago, in a market square when a man tried to shortchange Pip's mother: stand very still, say nothing, let the other side commit first.

Then Sef Marrow spoke.

He'd been quiet for days—too quiet, distant at meals, sending signals upstream with a lantern code that Toma had noticed and nobody had wanted to ask about. Now, facing the riders, facing Lena, he finally said the thing out loud, and it landed on the deck like a body dropped from a height.

"I sold them the route," he said. "Weeks ago. Before I ever came to you, Lena. Veyra has known you were coming since before you said yes."

The rain seemed to get louder after that, as if the sky itself flinched.

Brisa felt it hit the whole crew at once—not the news, but the shape of it. Every shared watch, every card game, every night Sef had sat among them eating Brisa's food with his boots under her table—he had been a hired hand around their throats the entire time. He hadn't betrayed them mid-river. He'd never been on their side at all. The job itself had been the trap: bring the ledger down the Rivergate, deliver it straight into the hands of the family that wanted it buried, and collect twice—once from Lena's trust, once from Veyra's purse.

Pip stood up. The boy who'd stolen bread in three towns, who flinched at raised voices, whose whole survival strategy was to be small and quick and gone—he stood up, walked to the rail, and spat into the floodwater in the direction of the riders. Brisa would remember that longer than she'd remember the storm.

"You knew my father died on this route," Lena said. Her voice was low and level and worse than screaming. "You built the trap on his grave."

Sef's face did something complicated. There was a flicker there—Brisa saw it, a half-second of something that might have been shame, drowned fast under arithmetic. "I knew the ledger was worth more than grief," he said. "Grief doesn't keep a man warm."

"No," Lena said. "But it keeps a man honest. You wouldn't know."

* * *

The riders launched a skiff. Two men, shields up, poling hard against the current toward the boat's stern, where the mooring lines ran taut to the lock wall. If they reached the lines, they'd cut them loose from the wall and tow the boat in like a fish on a hook. Everyone on board understood it at the same moment, the way crews understand things—in the gut, all at once, without discussion.

There were exactly two choices, and both of them were terrible.

Stay moored, fight the skiff, lose eventually—eight lances against five people and a boy. Or cut loose now, upstream of the chained gates, into open floodwater, with a hull that was already leaking and a current that ate trees whole.

"Open water will kill us," Toma said flatly. "Maybe. Probably."

"Staying kills us certainly," Ivo said. "And gives them the ledger."

Brisa went below without being asked. Not to hide—to fetch everything that mattered that wasn't the ledger. The medicine kit. The dry clothes sealed in oilcloth. The tin of seed money Pip had been adding coins to since the salt flats, hoarded like a dragon's egg. And the big pot of stew, still hot, because if they were going to die on open water she refused to let it happen on an empty stomach, and if they weren't going to die, they'd want it more.

When she came back up, the argument on deck had ended. Not because anyone had won. Because Lena had picked up the axe.

"Veyra wants the ledger," she called across the water, loud enough that the riders on the wall would hear it through the rain. "Come and take it."

Then she swung.

The first mooring line parted like it had been waiting its whole life for permission. The boat lurched, swung, and the second line went—the stern swinging out, the current grabbing the hull like a hand closing around a wrist. The skiff with the two riders was suddenly bearing down on empty space where the boat used to be, and the lock wall, and the chained gates, and House Veyra's whole patient terrible bargain slid away downstream behind them.

"Hard to port!" Toma screamed, already moving. "Brisa—weight forward! Get everyone forward!"

She threw the pot down, grabbed Pip, and dragged him to the bow by his collar while the river took the stern and swung it like a door in a storm. The boat went sideways. Then it went backward. Then, for one long impossible breath, it went nowhere at all, balanced on the crest of the flood with the whole gorge roaring on either side, and Brisa—who had sworn off praying twenty years ago on a different boat in different water—prayed anyway, quickly, quietly, without ceremony, the way you grab a rail when the deck tilts.

Then the current decided. It always decides.

The nose came around, the river caught them properly, and they were running—open throttle, engine howling, Toma feeding it steam with one hand and bailing with the other, the leak gaining below decks while the rain erased the horizon entirely. Behind them, faint through the roar, a horn blew from the lock wall. Furious. Impotent. The sound of powerful men discovering that some things cannot be bought in advance.

Brisa found Lena at the wheel, both hands locked on it, hair plastered to her skull, and pressed the tin of Pip's coins into her jacket pocket. "In case," Brisa said. "You keep it. I'm busy."

Lena looked at her—really looked, the way you look at someone when the noise stops mattering—and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the last hour hadn't already said.

Ahead, the river bent left into a channel none of the charts showed, a dark seam in the rock where the floodwater poured through like it had an appointment. Ivo appeared at Lena's shoulder, pointing, shouting over the storm: "That's the way. That's the drowned channel. That's where your father's boat went down—and it's the only water Veyra won't guard, because it isn't on any map they own."

Brisa saw Lena's jaw tighten. Saw her look at the safe, known river, and the chained locks, and the riders, and then at the black seam in the rock that had already killed one Almeida.

She turned the wheel.

"Hold on to something," Brisa told Pip, wrapping one arm around the boy and one around the mast, "and whatever happens, don't let go of me."

The boat leaned into the turn, the drowned channel opened its mouth, and the monsoon swallowed them whole.
