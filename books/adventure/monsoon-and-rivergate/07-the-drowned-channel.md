## 7. The Drowned Channel

The maps ended at the bend called Widow's Tooth, and that was where Ivo took the wheel.

He had not asked. That was the first thing Lena would hold against him, later, when there was time for holding things against people. For now she was forward, bailing, and Brisa was at the tiller with her knuckles white around the wood, and Toma had both hands inside the engine housing like a surgeon elbow-deep in a chest. The boat groaned. The river had swallowed its banks whole days ago, and what they were riding now was not a river but a brown animal the size of a country, dragging trees and dead cattle and one entire boathouse past them at a speed no sane person would call water.

"Left," Ivo said.

Brisa looked at him like he'd lost his mind. "There's no left. There's a cliff."

"There's a channel under it." He kept his voice level, because a voice that shook at the wheel of a boat in floodwater would shake everyone else too. "It's on no map you own. It runs behind the Tooth, through the old quarry cut, and comes out two miles down where the gorge narrows. It saves us half a day, and half a day is the difference between running this flood and drowning in it."

"And how," Brisa said slowly, "does a stranger paying his passage with talk instead of coin know about a channel that isn't on any map?"

Ivo didn't answer. Not yet. There wasn't time to answer, and there was also too much to answer, and those two facts had been sitting on his chest for eleven days now, since the dock at Rivergate Head, since he'd stepped aboard with nothing but a satchel and a story about being a surveyor. Eleven days of card games and shared watches and Pip falling asleep against his shoulder during the night shift. Eleven days of becoming, without permission or plan, someone these people might grieve.

"Because I've run it before," he said. "Once."

"When?"

"Ten years ago." He turned the wheel. "With a man named Almeida."

* * *

The channel opened like a wound in the cliff face, thirty feet wide, black water pouring through it, the rock walls so close that Toma could have touched them from the gunwale if he'd been fool enough to reach. Rain came sideways. Somewhere above them, thunder rolled along the gorge like a barrel going downstairs.

Lena climbed back from the bow, soaked through, one hand still on the bail bucket. She stood beside him at the wheel and said nothing for a long moment. Then:

"You said Almeida."

"I did."

"My father ran this channel ten years ago." Her voice was flat as slate. "His boat went down in it. They never found the wreck. They never found him. So either you're lying, or you know something about my father's death that you've sat on for eleven days while my crew patched holes in this hull and bled for your secret."

"Lena—"

"You don't get to say my name yet."

The channel carried them forward whether she spoke or not. Ivo held the wheel steady through the worst of the current, the boat corkscrewing once around a submerged quarry crane, and he used every second of it, because what he had to say could not be said fast and could not be said slow and there was no speed at which it would land gently.

So he said it plain.

"I was your father's partner. For three years before the Rivergate run. We hauled cargo, we ran surveys, we mapped the drowned channels together — that's how I know this one, I charted it with him, soundings in his hand still sit in my satchel if you want proof. And I was aboard his boat the night it went down. I am the only reason anyone knows where it sank, because I am the only one who came out of the water alive."

Lena's face went through something Ivo had no word for. He watched it happen and kept steering, because steering was the only useful thing he could do, and because looking away felt like cowardice and looking closer felt like cruelty.

"You were there," she said. "You survived. And in ten years you never came to me. You never wrote. You let me grow up thinking the river just *took* him — no witness, no grave, no answer — and then you walked onto my boat with a ledger in your bag and paid your passage with *conversation*—"

"The ledger was his." Ivo heard his own voice crack on it, which surprised him; he'd thought he was past cracking. "Everything in it is his handwriting. The debts, the houses, the names. He built it over six years, buying information, calling in favors, trading salvage rights for secrets. Every noble family on this river owes money or blood or silence to somebody, and your father wrote down who, and how much, and to whom. House Veyra owes a fortune to creditors they buried. The Calloways bought their docks with money that belonged to a hospital fund. Page after page. And on the last page—" He stopped. The rain filled the pause. "On the last page there's an inheritance clause. His estate, such as it was: the boat, the charts, the ledger itself, and the claim on every debt listed. Left to his daughter. Lena Almeida. He dated it nine days before the channel took him."

The boat shot through the narrowest throat of the cut, rock scraping close enough to shower sparks off the hull fitting, and neither of them flinched, because by then the words mattered more than the rocks.

"He was going to give it all to me," Lena said, barely audible under the roar. "And instead I found out from a stranger on a leaking boat, mid-flood, because Sef Marrow sold me a job and didn't tell me the cargo was my own father's ghost."

"Yes."

"Why are you telling me now? Why not at the dock? Why not day one?"

"Because at the dock you'd have thrown me off." Ivo said it without apology, because it was simply true. "And because the ledger couldn't stay with me anymore. Sef was closing in. Veyra's riders were already asking after it upstream. It needed to be on the river, moving, in hands that didn't know what they were carrying — because hands that knew would be hunted, and I have spent ten years being hunted, and I am tired, and I am out of clever places to hide it." He exhaled. "That's the ugly truth. I used you. I put your boat between the ledger and everyone who wants it burned. I told myself it was protection. Some nights I even believed it."

Lena turned and looked at him fully then, rain streaming off her jaw, and Ivo understood that whatever she decided about him in the next ten seconds would decide the rest of his life.

"You let me think he died alone," she said.

"No." The word came out of him like something torn. "No. He didn't die alone. He died *loud*. He died giving orders. When the hull hit the quarry crane, he got me into the rigging line first — pushed me up the rope with both hands while the water came in. I was nineteen and I weighed nothing and he put me above himself like it was arithmetic. I watched him go under standing up, both hands on the wheel, trying to hold the bow into the current long enough for me to reach the bank. Ten years, Lena. Ten years I have known exactly how he died and I have never once managed to say it out loud to the one person who needed to hear it, because every time I tried, I heard his voice instead telling me to keep the ledger safe, and keeping it safe meant staying silent, and staying silent meant—" He stopped. "It meant this. It meant you learning it from me on a boat in a storm, hating me, and being right to."

* * *

They came out of the channel into open floodwater an hour before dawn, and the rain slackened to a hard gray drizzle, and the crew collapsed in shifts on the deck planking. Pip slept curled against the mast. Toma fell asleep sitting up with a wrench still in his hand. Brisa put on coffee because Brisa always put on coffee, it was how she said *we're still here*.

Lena and Ivo sat on the stern bench under the tarp, three feet apart, not speaking.

The anger went out of her slowly, the way heat leaves stone after sundown. Ivo watched it happen and did not rush it. He'd learned that much in ten years: grief cannot be hurried, only accompanied.

"He taught you the channels," she said finally.

"All of them. He said a map was just a promise other people made, and promises break, so learn the water itself." Ivo smiled, small and crooked. "He used to make me recite soundings with my eyes shut. Depth, current, hazard, exit. Channel by channel. I can still do the whole river that way. I did it tonight, mostly. He's the reason we're through the Tooth."

Lena laughed — one short, broken sound that was half sob and had no intention of being either. "He used to do that with me. On the salt flats, before the river. Made me memorize wells the same way. Depth, flow, poison, shade." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "I thought I'd forgotten all of it. I spent ten years making sure I forgot all of it. Running."

"I know," Ivo said. "I read your file."

She looked at him sharply. "What file?"

"Sef keeps files on everyone he hires. I bought a look at yours the week before the dock." He winced at his own honesty, but there was no point in half-truths anymore; the night had burned those all away. "Ranger service, commendations, the incident at Dry Fork where you pulled four people out of a flash flood and then requested transfer to the emptiest posting on the books. I knew before I met you that you were a woman who saves strangers and can't be saved by anyone. I hired on anyway. I told myself you'd earned the truth more than anyone alive. I told myself a lot of things."

"Did any of them help?"

"No." He considered. "One. I told myself that if I ever got the chance, I'd tell you how he died, and it would cost me everything, and it would be worth it." He looked out at the brown water sliding past, the drowned trees riding the current like a funeral procession. "It cost everything. It was worth it."

For a long time Lena said nothing. Then she reached across the three feet between them and took the satchel — his satchel, the one with the soundings in it — and opened it, and spread her father's charts across her knees, and read them by the dim lantern light with her fingers tracing the depth numbers the way children trace letters.

"He numbered them wrong on purpose," she said suddenly. "Look — here, at the quarry cut, he wrote fourteen feet where it's obviously twelve. A margin for error, so a lazy pilot wouldn't trust the number and would take it slow." She laughed again, quieter. "That's him. That's exactly him. Generous and paranoid in the same stroke."

"That's him," Ivo agreed.

They sat until the lantern burned low, passing pages between them, and somewhere in there the anger finished dying and something else took its place — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever fully, but a shared weight, set down between two people who had each been carrying their half alone for ten years. Ivo felt it settle. He had imagined this moment a hundred times and always imagined it as absolution. It wasn't. It was just company. Company, it turned out, was worth more.

Near dawn, Lena folded the last chart and handed it back.

"The ledger," she said. "When this flood is done — when we're clear of Veyra's riders and Sef's buyers and whatever else the river sends — I'm deciding what happens to it. Not you. Not Sef. Me. It has my name on the last page, and I'm done letting other people read my name for me."

"It's yours," Ivo said. "It was always yours. I was just the courier who wouldn't quit."

She almost smiled. Then the smile stopped, because Toma came down from the bow at a run, shouting, and what he shouted emptied the warmth out of the morning in a single breath:

Smoke on the water behind them. Two boats, running dark, no lights, closing fast through the flood haze — and painted on the lead hull, fresh and wet and unmistakable, the green serpent of House Veyra.
