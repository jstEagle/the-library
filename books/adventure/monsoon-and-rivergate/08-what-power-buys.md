## 8. What Power Buys

The river spat us onto the sandbar like something it had no further use for.

We came aground in the grey hour before dawn, the *Kestrel's Debt* grinding her belly across wet sand with a sound I felt in my teeth. Toma was already over the side before we stopped moving, up to his knees in floodwater, hands pressed to the hull as if he could hold her together by will alone. Brisa hauled Pip out by the collar. Ivo stood in the shallows holding the ledger above his head, wrapped in oilcloth, and for a long moment none of us spoke. We just listened to the boat settle, groan, and go quiet.

"She'll hold," Toma said at last, and his voice cracked on the second word. "She'll hold if the water doesn't rise another hand."

The water would rise another hand. The monsoon wasn't finished with us; it had only paused to breathe.

I waded ashore and looked at where the river had put us—a spit of pale sand maybe forty paces long, crowned with dead reeds and a single leaning palm that had given up standing straight years ago. On one side, the flooded channel we'd just survived, brown and furious. On the other, a drowned forest stretching to the horizon, treetops poking through the water like drowned men reaching.

"Nowhere to run," Brisa said behind me. She said it lightly, the way she said everything, but her knuckles were white around the handle of her cleaver.

"We're not running," I said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Sef Marrow came at midmorning, when the rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist and the light turned everything the color of old pewter.

He came in a longboat flying House Veyra's colors—grey and gold, a heron spearing a fish—and he came with twelve soldiers in waxed canvas, crossbows resting easy in their arms, and he came alone to the waterline, which was its own kind of message. He wanted me to see that he didn't consider us dangerous anymore. He wanted me to see how far I'd fallen.

He'd dressed for it. That was the thing I noticed first, before anything else—before the soldiers, before the smile. Sef wore a riverlord's coat now, deep green wool with silver frogs, dry as bone, and boots that had never touched mud. Ten years ago he'd worn patched canvas like the rest of us and complained about the price of tar. Ten years ago he'd been the fastest knife on the upper Rivergate and half in love with being broke and free.

Now he stopped three paces from me on the wet sand and looked at my crew—at Toma smeared with pitch, at Brisa with her cleaver, at Pip, thin as a heron's leg—and I watched him do arithmetic. I watched him decide what we were worth.

"Lena." He said my name like an apology he'd rehearsed. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other boat."

"I did." His mouth twitched. "It's at the bottom of the channel."

"Then we're even."

He sighed, and for a moment—just a moment—the mask slipped and I saw the man who'd once shared a fire with me on the salt flats, back when we were both nobody, both hungry, both certain the river owed us something. Then the mask went back up, smoother than before, and I understood that it wasn't a mask anymore. It was just his face.

"You've made this difficult," he said. "I want you to know I take no pleasure in that."

"You sold our route to Veyra before we ever cast off."

"I sold them a route. Whether you were on it was your own bad luck." He spread his hands. "Lena. Look around you. You're on a sandbar with a dying hull, a leaking season, and a ledger that every house from here to the delta wants burned. There is exactly one way off this bar alive, and I'm standing on it."

Pip moved then. Just a step, just sideways, until the boy's shoulder touched mine. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. Sef's eyes flicked to him and something cold passed over his face—the look of a man doing sums and disliking the total.

* * *

Here is what I had not expected: not the soldiers, not the trap, not even the betrayal. I had expected all of those, had carried them downriver like ballast. What I hadn't expected was the offer.

"Your father," Sef said, "kept meticulous accounts."

The name landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. I let it sit there. I didn't give him the satisfaction of watching it sink.

"He kept accounts of everything," Sef went on, softly now, the way a man speaks when he's found the soft place in you and means to press it. "Debts. Favors. Crimes. Every house on this river owes him something, Lena, whether they admit it or not. Veyra owes him a massacre they paid him to forget. The Tollhouses owe him their charter. Half the nobility of the Rivergate built their fortunes on paper he signed and kept copies of." He gestured, and one of his soldiers brought forward a lacquered case, opened it, and showed me gold enough to buy a fleet—or a funeral. "And the ledger names you. Almeida, heir and executor. Do you understand what that means? Not a bounty. An inheritance. Legitimacy. A name that opens doors instead of closing them. You could walk into any court on this river and be received."

"I've been received before," I said. "By ropes."

"Not like this." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice, and there it was again—that flicker of the old Sef, the one who'd been my friend before he was my rival, before whatever happened to him happened. "You've spent ten years running from that name. I'm offering you the chance to stop running. To stand still and have the whole river stand still with you. Power, Lena. Real power. The kind that doesn't leak."

I looked at him. Really looked.

And I saw it—the thing I'd been circling since the dock, since the sealed ledger, since the first coded signal he'd sent upstream. Sef Marrow had gotten everything he'd ever wanted. The coat, the soldiers, the house colors, the gold, the fear. And it had eaten him hollow the way floodwater eats a riverbank: slowly, invisibly, until one day the ground gives and there's nothing underneath but river. There was no one behind his eyes. There was a transaction.

"You're afraid of them," I said quietly. "Veyra. All of them. That's why you sent signals upstream. That's why you chained yourself to their boat. You thought their power would keep you safe, and now you can't get off the leash."

Something moved behind his face. Hurt, maybe. Or recognition.

"Power keeps you alive," he said.

"No," I said. "People keep you alive. Power just keeps you company while you die."

* * *

Behind me, I heard the crew come up the sand. I hadn't called them. They'd simply come—Toma dripping pitch, Brisa with her sleeves rolled, Ivo carrying the ledger against his chest like a sleeping child, and Pip, who should have been hiding behind all of them and instead planted himself at my left shoulder, chin up, hands empty, thirteen years old and trembling and absolutely immovable.

Sef looked at the boy. "You'd throw away your inheritance for a thief and a cook?"

"I'd throw away your version of it," I said. "Yes."

"The ledger names you heir. That's law, on this river. Veyra's law, even. You could burn every one of them to the ground with a signature."

"I know what it names me." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "My father wrote that page the year he died. I've had ten years to wonder what he'd have wanted me to do with it, and I finally know. He didn't write debts to collect them. He wrote them because somebody on this river had to remember what was owed—to the towns, to the drowned, to everyone your masters decided didn't count. He wrote it *for* them. Not for me. Not for you. And I am not going to sell my father's grave to the people who dug it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere out in the drowned forest, a bird cried once and went quiet.

Sef exhaled through his nose. When he spoke again, the warmth was gone entirely, and what remained was worse: pity, worn like a badge. "Then you're a fool, and you'll die a fool, and the river will keep your bones with your father's." He turned and raised one hand toward his soldiers. "Take the ledger. Take the girl if she resists. The rest—" he glanced at my crew, and I saw him make his final calculation, cheap lives against cheap gold— "the rest can swim."

Twelve crossbows came up.

And Pip—thirteen years old, ninety pounds soaking wet, which he very much was—stepped forward and put himself between me and the nearest soldier, arms spread wide, and shouted in a voice that cracked clean down the middle:

"SHE'S THE RANGER! SHE CAUGHT ME IN THREE DAYS ON THE FLATS AND SHE CRIED WHEN SHE THOUGHT NOBODY WAS LOOKING AND IF YOU WANT HER YOU GO THROUGH ME FIRST!"

Nobody went through anybody. Because that was the moment the river chose to speak.

* * *

It came out of the drowned forest without warning—a wall of brown water where there had been knee-deep flood, rolling the leaning palm flat, swallowing the reeds, lifting the longboats like toys. The soldiers' line dissolved into screaming men clawing for gunwales. Sef's lacquered case of gold went spinning into the current, and I swear I heard him cry out—not for himself, but for it, which told me everything I'd needed to know.

"BOAT!" Toma roared, and we ran.

We got the *Kestrel's Debt* off the bar as the surge took her, all hands hauling, Brisa at the sweep, the hull groaning like a living thing. Behind us, Sef stood waist-deep in rising water, coat ruined, soldiers scattered, watching his leverage dissolve downstream—and watching me go with it.

"This isn't finished, Almeida!" he screamed across the water. "There's a gorge ahead and Veyra owns every gate on it!"

I looked back at him once—hollowed-out man, drowning in the power he'd traded everything for—and I found, to my surprise, that I didn't hate him. I pitied him. And pity, I was learning, was a much more dangerous thing to carry.

Ahead, past the drowned trees, the river narrowed into black stone walls, and the sound coming out of that gorge was not a sound water should make.

"Hold on to something," Brisa said, and put her shoulder into the sweep.
