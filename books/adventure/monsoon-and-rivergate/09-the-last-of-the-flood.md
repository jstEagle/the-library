## 9. The Last of the Flood

The gorge announced itself before they could see it—a long, grinding roar that rose up through the hull and into their boots, into their teeth, into the small bones of their ears. Rain came sideways now, hard as thrown gravel, and the river beneath them had gone the color of wet iron, swollen with a week of monsoon and carrying whole trees in its grip like a child carrying sticks.

Toma stood in the engine well with his sleeves rolled past his elbows, both hands buried to the wrist in the guts of the boiler, and he was laughing.

"You're laughing," Brisa called from the wheel, both fists locked on the spokes as the boat yawed through a standing wave. "Toma. *Toma.* We are about to enter a flooded gorge in a leaking boat, and you're *laughing.*"

"I rigged the hull!" he shouted back, delighted, mad, rain streaming off his chin. "Do you know what I did? I ran copper from the boiler through the patched seams—here, here, and here—" he slapped the plating with a wet palm, "—and when the water hits the hot metal it flashes. Steam. It vents out through the holes and it *pushes back.* The river can't pour in if the boat is already pouring out."

"That's not how boats work," Pip said. He was lashed to the rail with a rope harness Brisa had tied herself, double-knotted, and his knuckles were white around the line.

"It's how *my* boats work," Toma said, and threw the valve.

The engine screamed. Steam hissed from three seams along the starboard hull in long white plumes, and for a moment, in the gray light, the *Rivergate's Daughter* looked like a creature breathing fire through its wounds. The boat surged forward, riding the thrust of its own bleeding, and Brisa hauled the wheel hard to port and took them into the gorge.

* * *

The walls rose on either side—black stone slicked to a shine, close enough that Lena could have touched them with a boat hook. The flood had swallowed the old channel markers whole; there was nothing now but water moving at the speed of a galloping horse and Brisa's memory of a route she'd run once, ten years ago, in high summer, at a third of this volume.

"Sandbar on the right where the channel dog-legs!" Brisa shouted. "Trust me or pray, but do it fast!"

"I'm doing both!" Pip yelled.

Ivo and Lena crouched amidships over the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth, their bodies curved around it like a shell around a heart. Spray came over the bow in solid sheets. Lena's hands were numb; she could feel the ledger's corners through the wrapping, sharp and patient, her father's debts and her father's handwriting and her own name in ink ten years old.

"He ran this gorge," she said. Not a question. Ivo heard her anyway.

"He ran it slower," Ivo said. "In daylight. In June." His hand found hers over the oilcloth, brief and hard. "He'd have loved this. He'd have hated it. Both at once—that was your father."

Behind them, above them, the sound of the pursuit came down the gorge like weather: engines, two of them, Veyra boats running the flood with soldiers at the rails and lanterns burning behind oiled glass. Sef Marrow's voice carried over the water, hoarse from shouting, still trying to buy what he could no longer take.

"LENA! You're drowning with it! Give it up and live! That's all this is—one ledger for six lives! Do the arithmetic, girl! You were always good at arithmetic!"

Lena did not turn around. "Toma," she called. "How much steam do we have?"

Toma looked at his gauge, then at the plumes bleeding from his beautiful, terrible patchwork, and grinned like a man watching his own funeral pyre. "Enough to be a legend," he said. "Not enough to be a survivor. Pick one!"

"Legend," Brisa said, and drove the boat at the dog-leg.

* * *

The world became water.

The channel turned and the flood did not; the river hit the bend and reared up, and for four seconds—Lena counted them, four seconds of her life spent entirely inside white noise—the *Daughter* was more airborne than afloat. Toma's steam vents shrieked. The hull slammed down on the far side of the sandbar with a blow that threw everyone to the deck, and the ledger went sliding, and Lena went after it on her stomach, one hand out, Ivo's fist closed in her collar, and together they caught it an arm's length from the scuppers where the river waited with its open mouth.

Then the second Veyra boat, running too hot and too close, hit the sandbar at full speed.

The crack of it was lost under the roar, but Lena saw it—the other boat's bow crumple, the deck buck, men spilling into the current like coins from a torn purse. The first boat sheered away, its pilot choosing his own life over his passengers, and suddenly the gorge held only one pursuit: Sef's longboat, lean and black, threading the flood with the skill that had made his name before greed unmade it.

Sef stood at its stern. Even at this distance, even through rain, Lena could see him clearly: a man who had spent ten years hollowing himself out to hold one ambition, and who now looked, in the white water, like a lantern with the candle gone out.

* * *

The falls were not on any map. They were not supposed to exist—a shelf of drowned rock where the gorge floor dropped away, invisible until you were on top of it, and the river went from moving to *falling* in the space of a single heartbeat.

Brisa saw it first. She had spent her whole life reading water, and she read this the way she read faces: instantly, and with her whole body. "DROP!" she screamed, and threw the wheel, and the *Daughter* swung her stern around in a turn no sane pilot would have attempted, presenting her flank to the current, buying three seconds of sideways slide while the edge of the world came up beneath them.

They went over.

It was not like the dog-leg. It was not like anything. The boat dropped, struck, buried her bow in the foam, and came up rolling, and Toma's vents fired in a last defiant cough of steam that righted them by inches. The pool below the falls was a churning bowl of wreckage and rain, and into it, seconds later, came Sef's longboat—sideways.

Lena saw it happen the way you see a fall in a dream, too slow and too complete. The longboat hit the foam broadside. The flood took it under. It rose once, broken, men clinging to it like ants to a leaf, and then the whirlpool below the falls caught it and began, patiently, to pull it down.

"LENA!" Brisa's voice, raw. "We can't—the current—"

"I know," Lena said.

She was already moving.

* * *

They came around in a wide arc, Toma feeding the boiler everything it had left, Brisa fighting the wheel against the pull of the pool. Pip was at the bow rail with a coil of rope in his hands, the boy who had once asked her why she chased people who'd already lost, and his face was set like a man three times his age.

"Throw it true," Lena told him.

"I always do," Pip said.

The rope went out across the foam, a pale arc against black water. It fell short. Pip hauled it back, hands tearing, and threw again, and this time Sef Marrow—Sef, who had sold their route, who had betrayed them mid-river, who had stood on a soldier's deck and offered to trade her crew for a ledger—caught it with both hands.

The whirlpool pulled. The rope sang. Lena braced against the mast with the line wrapped twice around her forearms, and the burn of it went all the way to the bone, and she did not let go.

He came up over the side like something dredged from the river's own throat—soaked, half-drowned, coughing black water, his fine coat gone, his gold gone, everything he had traded for gone. He lay on the deck of the boat he had sold and looked up at her, and for the first time in all the years she had known him, Sef Marrow had nothing to say.

Lena crouched over him, rope burns bleeding down her arms, rain hammering her back.

"You did the arithmetic," she said. "One ledger for six lives. Here's mine." She pulled the knife from her belt and cut the rope free of his wrist—not to cast him off, but so that he would know the next thing she said was not a transaction. "I'm not you, Sef. I was never going to be you. That's the whole answer. That's all of it."

She stood, and turned her back on him, and took her place at the ledger.

Behind her, Sef Marrow wept into the rain where no one would see, and no one did, except perhaps the river, which had seen everything already and kept it all.

* * *

They came out of the gorge at dusk, if the gray light could be called dusk, into water gone suddenly, shockingly gentle. The flood spread wide here, a brown sea between drowned trees, and the rain softened from knives to needles to a long steady whisper. Toma's vents sighed and went quiet. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above the cloud, the sun was setting, and none of them needed to see it to feel it happen.

Brisa steered with one hand now. Pip had fallen asleep against the rail, rope-burned and grinning in his sleep. Ivo sat with the ledger between his knees, no longer guarding it, just resting a hand on it, the way you'd rest a hand on an old dog.

Lena stood at the bow and watched the water.

Ahead—far ahead, a day, maybe two, if the flood let them—the river towns waited. Rivergate. The low country. All the places her father's ink had touched, all the names in all the columns, waiting ten years for someone to read them aloud.

She looked back once, at her crew: the engineer asleep in his engine, the cook at the wheel, the thief, the partner, the rival pulled from the flood. Not one of them was paid to be here anymore. The money had drowned with the gorge.

They were here anyway.

"Brisa," Lena said. "Take us downriver."

The *Daughter* moved on through the soft rain, toward the towns, toward the names, toward whatever a woman becomes when she finally stops running—and on the deck behind her, Sef Marrow opened his eyes, and looked at the ledger in Ivo's lap, and said, very quietly, "You don't even know what's on the last page."

Lena turned around.
