## 10. Where the River Ends

The storm broke at dawn the way a fever breaks—suddenly, all at once, leaving everything washed and trembling.

Lena stood on the deck of the *Saltmark* with her hands on the rail and watched the clouds tear open over the eastern hills, letting down long shafts of gold light that turned the flooded river into hammered bronze. The water was still high, still brown and fast, but it had lost its fury. It moved now like something tired, something that had said what it came to say.

Behind her, the boat creaked. The *Saltmark* would never be the same—Toma had patched her hull three times in as many days, and she carried the scars of it, seams of tarred canvas and hammered tin like a body that had survived something it shouldn't have. But she floated. That was more than Sef's boat could claim.

Sef himself sat wrapped in a blanket near the stern, shivering, saying nothing. He hadn't spoken since Lena hauled him out of the falls. His soldiers were gone—scattered downstream or drowned, she didn't know and didn't ask. What remained of House Veyra's power on this stretch of river was a broken longboat snagged in the willows and a man who had sold everything he had for a prize that had slipped through his fingers.

Lena looked at him once, briefly, and felt nothing she could name. Not hatred. Hatred required believing he'd been worth it.

* * *

They made the first river town by midmorning.

Riversmeet was half-drowned—the lower streets under two feet of brown water, boats tied to second-story balconies, people hauling furniture to upper floors and standing in doorways watching the flood carry away another season's work. When the *Saltmark* came gliding down the main channel, half the town turned out onto the levee to look at them. A leaking boat crewed by a desert ranger, an engineer with burn-scarred hands, a cook built like a fortress, a boy thief, and a stranger nobody knew—that was not a sight Riversmeet saw every day.

Lena tied off at the levee steps and stood there a long moment with the ledger in its oilcloth wrapping under her arm. She had carried it a thousand miles. She had bled for it, nearly drowned for it, watched men die for it. Every mile of the Rivergate it had grown heavier, until now it weighed more than the boat itself.

Brisa came up beside her, arms crossed. "Well," she said. "You going to tell us what you're doing, or are we guessing?"

Lena looked out at the crowd on the levee. Fishermen. Dockhands. A woman holding a child above the waterline. Old men who remembered her father's face, maybe, though none of them would know hers.

"My father wrote this ledger," she said. "Every debt owed on this river. Every secret the great houses paid to keep buried. Names, amounts, dates. Crimes." She unwrapped the oilcloth slowly, letting the morning light fall on the pages. "House Veyra wanted it. Sef Marrow wanted to sell it. I could have sold it too. There are buyers in the delta who'd pay enough to buy every one of us a house."

Pip, perched on the rail, swung his legs. "So why don't you?"

"Because it wasn't written for buyers." She turned to face the crowd. "It was written for you."

She climbed the levee steps, and Ivo followed without being asked, carrying the ledger for her when her arms shook. And there, in the drowned light of a breaking storm, Lena Almeida began to read.

* * *

She read for three days.

Not all of it—some pages were coded, some water-blurred—but enough. She read the names of the houses and the names beneath them: the harbor fees Veyra had doubled during the famine year while grain rotted in their warehouses; the magistrate in Two Forks bought and paid for twice over; the ferry licenses revoked so one family's barges would have no competition; the eleven miners at Cinder Hollow whose deaths were recorded as accident and paid out at four silver each.

She read them aloud from the levee steps, and then, when word spread, from the market square, and then from the porch of the meetinghouse in the next town upriver, because people came from miles to hear it. They brought chairs. They brought food. An old woman pressed a cup of chicory coffee into Lena's hands and told her she'd known her father, had danced with him once at a harvest fair before the war took his left hand's strength, and Lena had to stop reading for a full minute because her throat closed.

The towns decided what to do with their own names. That was the part no buyer would ever have understood, the part Sef never could: the ledger wasn't a weapon. It was a mirror. In Riversmeet, they tore the magistrate's commission in half in front of the whole square. In Two Forks, they simply posted the pages on the ferry office door and let shame do the work shame does. In Cinder Hollow, the miners' widows read the four-silver figure aloud and then burned the page themselves, and held each other, and wept, and something in the town changed shape around that fire—not healed, nothing healed that fast, but *turned*, the way a river turns toward the sea.

Some towns did nothing. One burned the whole packet unread. Lena let them. Her father had written it for them, not for her judgment.

On the fourth day, only one page remained.

Hers.

*Almeida, L.—heir and beneficiary. All claims, debts, and properties of the Almeida line descend to the named party upon presentation of this ledger.*

Her inheritance. Her legitimacy. Everything Sef had offered her on the sandbar, everything her name entitled her to—a house in the delta, standing in the world, a surname that opened doors instead of closing them. Ten years she had run from that name, and here it was, offering to take her back.

Ivo stood beside her at the rail of the *Saltmark*, moored again at Riversmeet. "You don't have to burn it," he said quietly. "Your father wrote it. It's yours."

"That's exactly why I do." She struck the match against the rail. "He spent his life paying other people's debts. He'd have hated this page."

The paper caught, curled, went to ash on the wind over the brown water. Lena watched the last fleck of it vanish and felt—nothing dramatic. No thunderclap. Just a quiet, like a pack coming off shoulders after a very long walk.

Stillness, she thought. So this is what it costs. Nothing. It costs nothing at all. Running had cost her everything, every year, and she'd called the running discipline because calling it fear would have meant stopping.

* * *

They stayed in Riversmeet through the week the water fell.

Toma rebuilt the hull properly this time, with good river timber the townsfolk gave them free—"salvage rights," the harbormaster said, winking, for services rendered. Brisa cooked for half the levee district by the third night, having annexed a kitchen and three apprentices. Pip taught the dock kids every card trick he knew and lost most of his stolen coin back to them within two days, and grinned about it.

And on the seventh evening, with the river back inside its banks and the stars coming out clean over the water, they sat on deck around Brisa's stove, and Lena asked the question she'd been avoiding since the falls.

"So," she said. "Job's done. Ledger's delivered. Nobody's getting paid." She looked around the circle—Toma, Brisa, Pip curled against Brisa's side, Ivo with his scarred hands wrapped around a tin mug. "I can't offer you anything. I've got a boat that barely floats and a reputation that just got a lot more complicated."

Toma snorted. "You're offering us the same thing you've offered since the salt flats. Bad pay, worse weather, and the company of lunatics."

"Best offer I've had in years," Brisa said.

Pip raised his hand like he was in school. "I'm staying. You haven't finished teaching me the stars. And somebody has to keep Ivo honest—I've seen him cheat at cards."

"I have never once cheated at cards," Ivo said gravely. "I merely win."

Lena laughed—actually laughed, the sound strange and rusty in her own chest—and looked out at the river moving dark and easy toward the sea. Somewhere down there was the delta, and the buyers, and the great houses licking their wounds, and a hundred other ledgers waiting to be written by someone braver than she'd been ten years ago. Somewhere out there was the next job, the next chase, the next thing to run toward or from.

But not tonight. Tonight the boat rocked gently at her lines, and the stove ticked as it cooled, and the people around it were not crew, not employees, not passengers.

They were hers. And she was theirs. Chosen, both directions, no ledger required.

"Then it's settled," Lena said, and reached for the cards. "Same watch rotations as the river. Brisa cooks. Toma fixes things before they break, for once. Pip—"

"Aye?"

"—you're on dishes."

The boy groaned, the others laughed, and down the darkening Rivergate the water ran on, carrying the last of the monsoon toward the sea, past towns where her father's name was finally at rest, and hers—she found—was finally at home.
