## 1. The Man with Two Names

The bus let him off at the bottom of the Kellwater switchback road because the driver refused to go higher in that fog, and Mirabel Ishikawa was glad of it. He wanted the last mile on foot. He wanted to arrive the way water arrived—slowly, without a splash, finding the lowest place and settling into it.

He had been Martin Cole for eleven months now. Before that he had been Daniel Voss for six weeks in a river town whose name he had already let go, and before that, for one bad winter, he had been nobody at all, which was the hardest name to wear and the warmest. Names were coats. You put them on until they smelled like you, and then you traded them before anyone noticed the fit.

The road climbed through pine and wet slate. Somewhere above, hidden in the cloud, a locomotive whistle sounded—two notes, long-short, the old junction signal—and something in his chest answered it before his mind could stop it, the way a dog lifts its head at a bell. He kept walking. He did not look up. Looking up was how men got remembered.

His father's compass rode in his breast pocket, wrapped in a square of oilcloth, and he did not take it out. He had not taken it out in a year and a half, not since the hearing, not since the board read the findings in their flat administrative voices while he sat with his hands flat on the table like a man showing he held no weapons. The compass had been in his pocket that day too. His father had bought it secondhand in a port city with the first money he ever earned, and it had a hairline crack across the crystal that caught the light at a certain angle and threw a small star across the dial, and his father had said, the only time he ever said anything sentimental, *a man who knows where north is can't be properly lost.* Mirabel had thought about that sentence a great deal since. He had concluded that his father had never been properly ruined, which was a different condition, and one for which the compass offered no guidance.

The debt was the ruin's remainder. Not money—money would have been simple, money could be worked off in a season of hard labor. It was the other kind, the kind that accrues when a bridge fails and three men go into the water and only the paperwork survives. The inquiry had found irregularities. The inquiry had found that the irregularities traced back to a junior engineer who had signed off on a load calculation he had not personally verified, because his mentor had told him it was verified, and because you did not check a man like Aldous Vane. You did not check the sun. And then the bridge had spoken its opinion on the matter, in the language of twisted steel, and Vane had retired with honors while the junior engineer's name became a cautionary footnote in the trade journals.

He was not bitter about it anymore. He had spent a long time being bitter and had found it heavy to carry uphill. What remained was simpler: he had been a man who built things, and now he was a man who measured dead things, and the distance between those two professions was exactly the width of his shame.

Kellwater appeared out of the fog all at once, the way mountain towns did—no approach, just arrival. A junction town, built where two rail lines met a river and decided, out of mutual exhaustion, to become a place. Timber buildings on stilts against the spring melt. A water tower with a rusted seam. Rails running through the main street like exposed veins, polished bright on top by the few wheels still using them. Beyond the town, upriver, the gorge walls closed in gray-green, and above them, invisible but present the way a held breath is present, the high country where the maps went blank and the government had drawn its little hatched lines meaning *closed, pending, do not enter.*

He had chosen Kellwater the way he chose everything now: for its smallness, its forgettability, its distance from anywhere anyone might look for a disgraced engineer. The notice had been posted in a district paper three valleys over, barely a notice at all: *SURVEYOR WANTED. Decommissioned lines, upper Kellwater district. Piece rates. Apply land office.* Nobody applied for piece-rate work on dead rail. Nobody, therefore, would wonder why a man had.

The land office was a stone building with a green door that had been painted over so many times the panels had lost their geometry. Inside, a clerk with ink-stained cuffs looked up from a ledger with the enthusiasm of a man interrupted from a nap he hadn't known he was taking.

"Martin Cole," Mirabel said. The name came out of him smoothly now, worn smooth, a river stone of a name. "About the survey contract."

The clerk found the posting, read it as if seeing it for the first time, and pushed a paper across the counter. The terms were insulting. Per kilometer of line walked and measured, per structure assessed, per tunnel inspected—*inspected from the portal only,* a line in the contract specified, as if even the land office had given up on its own dead infrastructure. Payment monthly, in scrip redeemable at the company store, which was also the land office, which was also, as far as Mirabel could tell, the town's entire economy.

"You'll want to start with the low line," the clerk said. "The Kellwater–Vane Cut." He said the name without any inflection at all, and Mirabel felt the name go through him like a splinter he'd swallowed years ago and had learned to digest. *Vane Cut.* They had named a mountain pass after him. Of course they had. The man had moved a mountain, or claimed to; the tunnel through the shoulder of it had been the wonder of its decade, and Mirabel had stood at the breakthrough as a young man with his hard hat in his hands and felt his whole life arrange itself around that single moment of daylight coming through rock.

"Fine," Mirabel said.

"You'll sign as the name on your papers?"

"I'll sign as Martin Cole."

The clerk didn't care. That was the beauty of the low places of the world; nobody cared enough to look. The clerk cared about the ledger being balanced and the scrip being counted and the fog lifting by noon so the supply mule could come down. Mirabel signed, took the district map—a reprint so old the decommissioned lines were still printed in living red, as if the map hadn't been told—and left.

* * *

The inn was called the Riverstone, and the woman behind its counter looked at him the way innkeepers look at everyone: a fast inventory of hands, boots, eyes, and the weight of the bag. Her name, stenciled on the register, was S. Oduya. Her name, spoken by a drunk in the corner who was being ignored expertly, was Sera. Her eyes, which missed nothing and recorded everything with what Mirabel would later understand was not curiosity but bookkeeping, stayed on him one beat longer than politeness required.

"One room," he said. "Long term, if the rate's fair. I'm surveying the old lines."

"Alone?"

"Alone."

"Piece rates?" She almost smiled. "The land office pays in promises and store scrip. You'll eat better if you settle accounts with me directly. Soup and bread, one scrip note a day, and I don't ask what you find out there."

"That's generous."

"It's arithmetic." She slid the register toward him. "Name."

"Martin Cole."

He wrote it. His false signature had its own character now, slightly cramped, slightly hurried, the hand of a man always about to be done writing. He had practiced it in the early months until it stopped being a disguise and became a habit, and there was a particular grief in that he did not examine. The true signature—the one on the bridge drawings, the one in the inquiry record, the one that had been read aloud in a hearing room with three men's names attached to the water—was a different hand entirely, younger and more confident, and he kept it the way you keep a scar: not hidden, exactly, just not displayed.

The room was under the eaves, with a window that looked out on the gorge and, higher, the cloud line. He set his bag down, unwrapped the compass out of pure habit, and held it in his palm without opening the oilcloth all the way. He could feel the needle's restlessness through the cloth, a small live tremor, like a bird that hadn't been told the season had changed.

He put it away. He washed his face in the basin. In the clouded mirror over the basin he checked the stranger's face he wore: the beard he'd grown to change the line of his jaw, the gray he'd let into it early, the stoop he practiced, shoulders curled inward as if permanently apologizing to doorframes. Mirabel Ishikawa had stood straight. Surveyors stood straight; it was the whole profession, standing straight and looking at what was actually there. Martin Cole stood like a man reading the ground for other people's reasons.

Downstairs, the common room filled with the evening trade: mule drivers, a timber crew, two clerks from the land office drinking on credit. Mirabel ate his soup at the end of the bar and listened, because listening was the one skill his old life had never taught him and his new one demanded constantly.

"—closed the upper line for good, they say. Not that it was open."

"—my cousin drove freight on the Vane Cut for eleven years. Eleven years, and they seal it with a signature and a padlock."

"—the land office buys everything the valley won't sell, and then the valley wonders why it's poor."

The complaints had the worn smoothness of things said for years with no expectation of result. He catalogued them and filed them away and felt nothing, because feeling things about other people's grievances was a luxury of men with clean names.

Sera Oduya collected his bowl. "First line you'll walk?" she asked. Not idly. He understood that nothing she said was idle.

"The low line. The old cut."

"Nobody walks the cut anymore." She said it lightly, but her eyes did the thing again, the bookkeeping thing. "The tunnel's been sealed since the rockfall. Six years, seven. Men from the district came up and put a steel door on it and told everyone it was unsafe, which is the land office's favorite word. Unsafe, pending, closed. They have a word for everything they don't want to explain."

"Rockfalls happen," Mirabel said.

"Rockfalls happen," she agreed, and carried the bowl away, and he had the distinct sensation of having been weighed, entered into a ledger, and shelved—not dismissed, but not yet believed either. He would learn, in time, that this was how Kellwater treated all strangers: with patience, because the mountains had taught the valley that everything strange eventually either left or became local, and both outcomes could be waited out.

* * *

He started up the low line the next morning in fog that turned the world to gray paper. The rails came up out of the weeds and ran beside him, rusted brown at the joints, bright only where some animal had crossed them a thousand times. He walked the center of the track bed because it was level and because walking a rail line felt, even now, like a kind of prayer he no longer believed in but remembered the posture of.

The work was simple and he was good at it, which was its own small humiliation. Chain and level, the old instruments, nothing that could be traced or registered. He measured the decay of culverts and the heave of frost under the ballast, and he wrote his figures in Martin Cole's cramped hand, and the figures were honest because figures were the one place he refused to lie anymore. A man could steal a name. He would not steal a number.

By the third day the line had climbed into the shoulder of the mountain, and the trees closed in, and the fog lifted just enough to show him the portal of the Vane Cut ahead: a dark arch in the rockface, and across it, bolted into the living stone, a steel door with a land office padlock gone orange with rust.

He stood before it for a long time.

Seven years ago he had stood on the far side of that mountain and watched daylight come through it for the first time, and Aldous Vane had put his hand on Mirabel's shoulder—his hand, heavy as a pier cap—and said, *remember this day, boy. It's the only kind of miracle we get to make.* And Mirabel had remembered it. He had remembered it through the inquiry, through the water, through every false name since, the way you remember a promise someone else broke.

The padlock was rusted. The door was steel. The contract in his pocket said *inspected from the portal only.*

He took out his notebook and wrote the portal report: door intact, masonry sound, drainage clear. All true. He signed it Martin Cole. Then he stood there a moment longer in the fog with his father's compass a small hard weight against his chest, and he noticed—because he was an engineer, because noticing was the disease he'd never been cured of—that the drainage ditch below the portal was running clear when it should have been choked, and that the gravel of the old access track had been disturbed recently, recently enough that the rain of two days ago had softened but not erased it.

Something had been through here. Or someone.

Mirabel Ishikawa, who was now and intended to remain Martin Cole, a man whose whole art was not seeing things, stood in front of a sealed door at the end of a dead line and made the first mistake of his new life.

He decided to come back.
