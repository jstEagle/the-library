## 2. Closed Lines

The tunnel at Kilometer Forty-One was supposed to be a hole in the mountain and nothing more.

That was how the contract described it—*Bore 7, decommissioned 11 years prior, portal sealed with masonry bulkhead, interior condition unknown.* A line item. A number on a schedule that Mirabel Ishikawa was being paid eleven rupees a day to walk down and confirm still existed. The district land office wanted its closed assets catalogued before the monsoon season made the upper gorge roads impassable, and it wanted the work done by someone cheap, quiet, and uninterested in asking questions.

Mirabel had spent three years becoming exactly that man.

He came down to the portal at first light, when the mist still stood in the pines like a congregation waiting for something to begin. The old rail bed ran along the river's north bank, ties rotted to black lace, rails pulled up years ago except where landslides had buried sections too deep for the salvage crews to bother. He walked it with his instrument case over one shoulder and his father's compass in his coat pocket, not because he needed it—the bearing to the portal was written in his field book—but because he had never once left it behind. Some inheritances you don't get to decline.

The bulkhead appeared through the trees as a gray rectangle, mortared stone laid across the bore's mouth with a steel access door set into its center. Someone had painted a number on it once: **7**. The paint had faded to the color of old bone. Weeds grew from the mortar joints. A drainage ditch below the door had silted nearly level.

Everything about it said *dead*. Eleven years dead. Sealed, forgotten, filed under *closed* in some clerk's ledger and never thought of again.

Mirabel set down his case, took out his notebook, and began the ritual of professional boredom. Portal condition: fair. Masonry: intact, minor spalling at northeast corner. Access door: present, padlocked—

He stopped.

The padlock hung open.

Not broken. Not cut. Open, its shackle clicked shut around nothing, dangling from the hasp like a tongue lolling out of a sleeping dog's mouth. Rust had bloomed across the lock's body in the familiar orange lace of long neglect, but the shackle itself—where a key or a hand had recently turned it—was bright. Scrubbed clean by contact. Days ago, maybe less.

Mirabel stood very still and let the forest make its small noises around him. Water in the ditch. Wind working through the pines. Somewhere upslope, the dry knock of a woodpecker going about its business, indifferent to him.

An open padlock could mean many things. It could mean a maintenance crew had come through and failed to relock it—a crew that would have logged the visit, and whose visit would appear in no record he'd been given. It could mean kids from Kellwater, though Kellwater was two days' walk downstream and the children of that town, in his limited observation, had better sense than to play at the mouth of a sealed bore in landslide country. It could mean an animal, though animals did not turn shackles.

Or it could mean what the rust pattern said it meant: that someone had opened this door recently, carefully, with the key, and had either forgotten to close it or had not cared whether anyone noticed.

His training said: note it, report it, move on. His contract said the same thing in blunter language. *Surveyor shall document condition of closed assets and report all anomalies to the district land office within three days.* He had signed it. The signature on it was not his name, but it was his hand, and he had learned the hard way that a false name does not lighten the weight of a true promise.

He wrote in his notebook: *Access door unsecured. Lock appears weathered; shackle recently manipulated.* And then he sat down on his case and looked at what he'd written for a long time, and understood that he was already lying—not on paper, but in intention. Because the sentence he had not written was the sentence that mattered: *I am going inside.*

* * *

The door swung on hinges that screamed, then gave, then admitted him into air that tasted of cold stone and standing water and time.

He lit his lamp and held it up. The bore ran straight into the mountain, double-track gauge, the old rails still in place here—too deep in the tunnel for the salvage crews to have wanted them, or perhaps simply forgotten when the money for demolition ran out. Water dripped somewhere ahead with the patience of geology. The lamp's light reached perhaps thirty meters and died against a darkness that swallowed it without comment.

He walked. His boots rang on the ties, each step a small struck bell that the mountain kept and returned to him a half-second late. This was the part of the work he had always loved and had trained himself, these three years, to stop loving: the moment a structure took you in and told you what it was. Every tunnel had a voice. You learned it through your boots before you ever saw anything. Bore 7 spoke in a low, even tone—sound masonry, sound drainage, no groaning of stressed rock, no whisper of moving water where water shouldn't be. Whoever had built it had built it well. Whoever had sealed it had sealed it properly.

And someone had been inside since.

He found the first tool mark forty meters in, at knee height on the eastern wall, where the lamplight caught a scrape of bare stone among the soot and mineral stain of a decade. Fresh. The rock beneath showed pale, unweathered, almost wet-looking in its newness. Not a pick mark—he knew pick marks the way other men knew handwriting—and not blast. A chisel. A wide cold chisel driven with care, prying at something, and beside it, half-hidden under a shelf of calcite, the evidence of what had been pried: a rectangular scar in the masonry facing, the size of a large book, where a section of lining stone had been levered out and—

And put back. That was the detail that turned his spine to wire. The stone had been replaced. Mortar had been smeared into the joint, hastily, with fingers or a trowel, and the smear was newer than everything around it. Newer than the soot. Newer than the decade.

Someone had opened the wall, taken something out—or looked at something—and sealed it again, badly, in a hurry or in confidence that no one would ever come looking.

Mirabel crouched and put his palm flat against the patched joint. Cold. Damp. The mortar hadn't cured hard; it still gave slightly under pressure, like old bread. Weeks old. Perhaps days.

He should leave. Every instinct he had refined over three years of careful invisibility said *leave*: log the anomaly, walk back down the rail bed, send his report to the land office, collect his pay, and be gone from this district before anyone connected the stray surveyor to the opened tunnel. Curiosity was a debt with ruinous interest. He had learned that too, in the years when he had still been somebody, in the life that had ended with a hearing and a verdict and a train he still sometimes saw falling in his sleep.

But his hands were already moving. That was the truth of it, the humiliating mechanical truth: while the sensible part of him argued for retreat, his hands had taken out the chisel from his kit—the small one, the one he used for sampling ballast—and fitted its edge to the mortar joint, and begun to work.

The patch gave way in pieces. Behind it, the lining stone sat loose in its socket, exactly as it had been left, and came free with a grinding whisper into his arms.

The cavity behind it was shallow—half a meter deep, cut into the space between the lining and the living rock, the kind of dead void every tunnel has and no tunnel uses. It was empty except for dust, a few flakes of rusted iron that might once have been a box's hinges, and, pressed flat into the dust at the back, the clean rectangular ghost of whatever had lain there.

Something had been stored here. Recently. Recently enough that its outline remained sharp in the undisturbed dust, sharp enough to read dimensions off it: roughly thirty centimeters by twenty-two. The size of a field journal. The size of a ledger. The size of a book.

Mirabel knelt there a long time with the loose stone in his lap and the lamp guttering between his feet, and felt something in his chest that he had not felt since the hearing—since before the hearing, if he was honest, since the last morning he had stood on a right-of-way with a problem in front of him that was *his* to solve. It was not quite excitement. Excitement was too warm a word. It was closer to hunger, the sudden animal recognition of food after years of eating without tasting.

Someone had hidden a document in a sealed tunnel in a gorge that the land office itself had closed. Someone with a key. Someone who had then, apparently, retrieved it—because the cavity was empty now, and the dust told him the removal was the most recent act of all. The tool marks outside were older than the patch; the patch was older than the empty socket. A sequence. Open, hide, return, retrieve. And the retrieval had happened within days, because the dust had not yet drifted back into the print of the thing that had lain there.

Which meant whoever it was might come back. Or might, having retrieved what they wanted, consider the matter closed. Either way, they had not expected a surveyor. Nobody expected a surveyor; that was the whole point of surveyors, they were furniture with notebooks.

He photographed nothing—he owned no camera. Instead he drew. He cleared a space on the rail and worked for an hour by lamplight with the patience his father had beaten into him: the cavity, dimensioned; the patch, described; the tool marks, sketched with their angles noted; the dust impression, outlined and measured twice. He wrote down the date. He wrote down the bearing. He did not write down any conclusion, because conclusions were things that got men called before tribunals, and he had sworn off conclusions the way other men swore off drink.

Then he rebuilt the wall.

This was the part that surprised him most, afterward. He could have left the patch breached—could have reported it, could have let the land office's own people come up and find it and ask their own questions. But his hands, those traitor hands, mixed mortar from his repair kit and pressed the stone home and smoothed the joint until it matched the surrounding masonry better than the original patch had. Better than whoever had been here before him. There was no reason for that beyond pride—pride, the sin that had ended his first life—and yet he did it, and doing it felt like putting on a coat he'd outgrown and finding it still fit.

He cleaned his tools. He swept his boot prints from the ballast with a pine bough, which was absurd, which was the behavior of a guilty man, and he knew it, and he did it anyway. He locked the padlock shut with a twist of pliers that left no mark a layman would read, and stood back, and looked at the portal until it was once again only a hole in the mountain, a number in faded paint, a line item in a schedule.

*Interior condition: as expected,* he wrote in the official log that evening, by candlelight in his room above Sera Oduya's inn, the window cracked to the smell of rain coming. *No anomalies.*

The lie sat on the page looking exactly like the truth. That was the skill that had gotten him his disgrace, in the end—work clean enough that no one thought to look twice. He had promised himself he would never use it again. He had told himself that the man he was now, the nobody, the eleven-rupees-a-day shadow, did not lie because he did not do anything worth lying about.

He copied the true record—the drawings, the measurements, the sequence—into the back pages of his own field book, in his private shorthand, the code he'd used as a young engineer for notes he didn't want foremen reading. Old habits. He told himself it was caution. He told himself he would decide later what to do with it, that information was not action, that knowing a thing and doing a thing lived on opposite sides of a border he had no intention of crossing.

Outside, the first fat drops of the evening rain began ticking against the glass, and far upstream, invisible in the dark, the river went on talking to itself about the mountain.

He slept badly. In the gray hour before dawn he woke with the dust impression printed on the inside of his eyelids—that clean rectangle, thirty by twenty-two, the shape of a book that had been hidden by someone with a key and taken away by someone in a hurry. He lay listening to the rain and understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own instruments, that the careful architecture of his invisibility had developed its first crack. Small. Hairline. The kind of fracture that holds for years, or lets go all at once.

Three days, his contract said. Report anomalies within three days.

He had two days left to decide whether a lie was an anomaly.

* * *

He spent the next day surveying the culverts at Kilometer Thirty-Eight, honest work, dull work, and told himself the whole way through it that the tunnel was finished with him. Then, near dusk, walking back down the rail bed with the light dying gold behind the western ridges, he passed the place where the old line met the river's bend, and saw, pressed into the mud of the abandoned right-of-way, a boot print that was not his.

It was fresh—rain-sharpened edges, this morning's rain still pooled in the heel. Large. Military or quasi-military pattern, the kind of boot issued to government escorts and district police. It pointed upriver.

Toward Kilometer Forty-One.

Mirabel stood in the failing light with his compass heavy in his pocket and watched the print fill slowly with seep water, and made the only decision available to a man who had just lied in an official log: he stepped off the rail bed, climbed the embankment, and erased the print with the flat of his boot before the mud could hold it for anyone else to find.

Then he walked back to Kellwater in the dark, faster than he needed to, telling himself the whole two days that the quickening of his pulse was only the exercise.
