## 3. Gear Without Tracks

The river narrowed on the second day, and the valley with it, until the walls of the gorge pressed so close that the water seemed to run through a seam in the world. Mirabel Ishikawa walked the old maintenance path above the current, the one cut into rock by crews who had believed this line would carry freight for a century and had been wrong by thirty years. The path was still sound. That was the thing about dead infrastructure—it outlasted its purpose the way bones outlast the animal.

He had told himself he would go as far as the second culvert, log the washout damage, and turn back. He had written it in his field book that morning in his careful surveyor's hand: *Km 61.2 to Km 68.0, assess drainage, return by dusk.* A tidy sentence. A boundary drawn around himself, the way he drew boundaries around everything now. Two names, two sets of limits, and never the twain.

The culvert was fine. The culvert was boring. And somewhere past it, where the gorge bent north and the light came down at a different angle, he saw the glint of canvas through the pines and forgot, entirely, that he had ever intended to turn back.

* * *

It was not a camp you stumbled on. It was a camp you were shown into.

That was his first thought, standing at the treeline with his hand resting on the strap of his instrument case like a man touching a talisman. The site sat on a gravel bench above the river, flat, dry, defensible—chosen by someone who understood fieldwork. A wall tent, guy-lines taut. A cook stove, black iron, squatting under an awning of oiled cloth. Crates stacked in ranks. And laid out on a folding table in the open air, exposed to the weather, arranged with a precision that made the back of his neck prickle: instruments.

A theodolite on its tripod, caps still on the lenses. A level, boxed but unfastened, lid open like a mouth about to speak. Chain tapes coiled in their figures-eight. A barometer. Two plumb bobs. A plane table with a sheet of drawing paper clipped to it—and on the paper, half a contour sketch, stopped mid-line, the pencil still lying in the gutter of the board where someone had set it down.

Mirabel had spent twenty years around men who handled instruments. He knew what care looked like and what haste looked like and what panic looked like. This was none of those. This was order. This was a camp paused, not abandoned—as if the whole crew had stepped behind the tent to argue about a bearing and simply never come back.

He should have turned around then. He knew that too. There is a species of knowledge that arrives in the body before the mind signs off on it, and his body was telling him, plainly, that nothing good had happened on this gravel bench. But his feet stayed planted, and after a moment he heard himself say, aloud, in the flat voice he used for readings: "Well."

He crossed the open ground the way he'd been taught to cross suspect ground years ago, in another life, on another frontier—no hurry, no cover-seeking, just a man with a contract walking toward equipment that might belong to his own employer's subcontractors. Plausible deniability was a habit like any other. He wore his borrowed name the way he wore his coat: loosely enough to shed quickly.

The stove was cold. Not recently cold—cold the way stone is cold, cold all the way through, cold for days. He touched the flue pipe and got nothing but mountain damp. Under the awning, a kettle sat on the plate. He lifted it. Half full. The water inside was clean, no scum, no insects—which meant it hadn't stood long enough to grow anything, or it had been boiled and left, or—

No. He was doing what frightened men do, which is build elaborate structures out of insufficient data. He set the kettle down and made himself walk the camp in a grid, the way he'd grid a survey, methodical, corner to corner, recording before interpreting.

Tent first. Bedrolls inside, four of them, rolled and tied—not struck in haste, not dragged out and rummaged. A card table with a lantern, the glass smoked from use. On the table, a ledger. His heart did something complicated when he saw it, because ledgers were his native country; he had kept them, falsified none himself but audited plenty, and he knew the grammar of official paperwork the way priests know liturgy. This one was a government issue field ledger, the district stamp on the flyleaf, and it was locked shut with a brass clasp. No key anywhere he could see. He didn't force it. Forcing things was how a man became a story other people told.

Cook crates: rice, oil, tea in tins, a sack of flour sewn shut again with a proper stitch. Tools hung on a line between two trees, each tool in its place, shovel, axe, pick, the handles worn smooth by hands that had used them for months. A rifle rack—empty. Three brackets, three rifles, three empty brackets. He stood in front of those brackets longer than he stood in front of anything else in the camp.

Men who leave a camp take their rifles. Men who are taken from a camp leave their rifles—if whoever took them already had guns of their own, which they would, which anyone would. Empty rifle racks were either the least alarming thing here or the most, and he genuinely could not decide which, and that inability was itself a kind of alarm.

* * *

Then the tracks.

The bench was gravel over hardpan, poor tracking ground, but along the river margin the mud held prints the way wax holds a seal. He worked the margin slowly, down on his heels, hands on knees, reading the ground the way his father had taught him to read it before he'd ever taught him surveying—the compass came later; the ground came first.

Boot prints arrived. Government-issue hobnails, the pattern he knew from every district office corridor of his former life, four distinct sizes, coming upriver from the direction of Kellwater. Dozens of passages back and forth between the landing where a boat must have tied off and the camp itself. Supply runs. Work parties. All of it arriving.

None of it leaving.

He walked the whole margin twice. He went upstream past the camp to where the gorge pinched and the mud gave way to bare rock, looking for the exit prints, the departing column, the trail of men walking out under their own power or otherwise. Nothing. Downstream, same. The only tracks out were his own, and the deer trails, and the faded sign of some earlier traveler weeks gone.

Four men—or five, or six, the bedrolls said four but bedrolls lie—had come to this bench with instruments and rifles and a government ledger, and had not left it on foot. No bodies. No blood. No drag marks, no scuffle-churned ground, no scattered kit. Just absence, arranged neatly, like a room made up for guests who never arrive.

Violence would have been easier. He found himself thinking that with a clarity that disturbed him. If there had been blood on the stones, bodies in the river, a burned tent, he could have catalogued it, reported it, grieved it, filed it away in the part of himself where he kept everything he refused to look at directly. Violence has a shape. It ends. What was here instead was a question wearing the shape of an answer—a camp that insisted nothing had happened while screaming that something had.

He sat down on a supply crate and ate half a biscuit without tasting it, because his body needed fuel and he had learned long ago to feed it whether or not his mind was present. The river talked to itself below the bench, patient, indifferent. Somewhere upslope a woodpecker worked a dead pine with idiot persistence. The ordinary sounds made the silence of the camp louder.

* * *

By late afternoon he had done something he could not undo: he had opened the plane table's clip and looked at the half-finished sketch.

It was a contour study of the gorge upstream—of exactly the stretch he had planned to survey tomorrow, though he had told no one that, though the plan existed nowhere but in his own head and in the penciled route notes in his field book. The lines ran confident and clean up to a point, and at that point they stopped, mid-stroke, the way the pencil lay beside them. The last contour closed around a feature the draftsman had labeled in a small, disciplined government hand:

*SRC-0.*

Source, zero. The forbidden bearing. Every man in these mountains knew the rule without anyone restating it: the river's source was closed country, closed by an order older than the railroads, closed in the particular way things get closed when closing them serves someone. Survey teams mapped around it the way water flows around a stone. You did not put SRC-0 on a sheet of paper. You especially did not begin a contour study of it and then stop halfway through the first line.

Unless you had been ordered to stop. Or unless stopping had been done to you.

Mirabel stood over the plane table for a long time, and the engineer in him—the one he kept buried under the borrowed name and the cheap contracts and the deliberate smallness of his life—that engineer looked at the half-drawn contour and felt not fear but appetite. That was the shameful truth of it. Some part of him, the part that had once loved a fresh sheet of drawing paper more than sleep, looked at an unfinished line and wanted, with a hunger he hadn't permitted himself in years, to finish it.

He capped his own reaction like capping a lens. Careful. Careful was the whole architecture of his survival. Careful men did not sit at dead men's tables reading dead men's contours.

But were they dead? That was the question the camp refused to answer. No bodies. If they were dead, the bodies had been removed with a thoroughness that required organization, vehicles, time, and indifference to witnesses—which meant indifference to whatever witnesses lived up this gorge, which meant there were witnesses, which meant there were people upriver whose existence the district office did not officially acknowledge. If they were not dead, then four armed government surveyors had walked away from their rifles and their instruments and their half-boiled kettle without leaving a single print going out, which was a trick no army on earth could teach.

Either answer was worse than the question.

* * *

He made his camp that night two hundred meters downstream, off the bench, in a hollow under a leaning spruce, and he did not light a fire, and he lay awake listening to the river and rehearsing the report he would file.

Because he would file a report. That was the plan, the sane plan, the plan of a man named Halloran—his borrowed name, flat and forgettable, a name that fit in a ledger row without crowding. *Contractor H. reports decommissioned line Km 61–68 passable; notes evidence of recent third-party activity upstream, possibly another agency's crew; recommends inquiry through proper channels.* Proper channels. The phrase was a kind of prayer. He had survived seven years on proper channels, on being nobody worth a channel's attention.

But even as he composed it, lying in the dark with his father's compass in its case against his chest the way other men slept with a hand on a weapon, he could hear the flaw in the report, the crack down the middle of it. A contractor who found a government camp would note it. Fine. A contractor who found a government camp stripped of men and rifles would do more than note it—he would come down off this mountain fast, and he would not stop to eat a biscuit on a crate, and he would certainly not spend an hour studying a forbidden contour sketch. Everything he had done today was the behavior of a man who had already decided to stay. His report was a letter he was writing to a version of himself that no longer existed.

The moon came up the gorge late, the way it does in deep country, and when it finally cleared the eastern wall it laid a white road down the river's surface, running upstream, running toward SRC-0, bright as a surveyed line. He watched it for a long time.

His father's compass, he thought. The old man had carried it through two wars and one bankruptcy and had given it to him with a sentence Mirabel had never forgotten: *A needle doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you where you are. The rest is your decision.* He had taken it as a lesson in navigation. Lately he suspected it had been a warning.

Where am I? he asked it silently, and the answer was humiliatingly precise: two days' walk from the nearest telegraph key, one day beyond the last point where his employer's authority reached, lying in the dark next to the belongings of four missing men, holding a half-finished map of a place he was forbidden to map.

In the morning, he told himself, I'll decide. In the morning the report will seem obvious and I'll walk down and file it and be nobody again.

He fell asleep believing that. It was the last comfortable belief he would hold for a very long time.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold, condensation beading the tent canvas of the dead camp, and Mirabel went back up the bench before breakfast, which was itself a confession—he was not a man who skipped meals, and he knew why he was skipping this one. He wanted to see the camp in first light, before his own presence smudged it, to read it once more like a fresh page.

Nothing had changed. Of course nothing had changed. But in the low angled light he noticed what yesterday's brightness had hidden: a faint path leading from the rear of the tent up the slope behind the bench, toward the cliff wall, barely a thread through the ferns, worn by repeated passage. Not an escape route. A working route. Someone had gone up that slope many times, deliberately, over weeks.

And at the top of the slope, forty minutes of hard climbing by his watch, where the gorge wall split around a buttress of gray stone, he found the tunnel mouth.

Sealed. Timbered, plugged with a masonry bulkhead, official closure plate bolted across it bearing a date nine years old and a district stamp he recognized from his former life. The kind of door that stays shut.

Except the mortar around the plate was bright. Fresh-cut. Someone had unbolted that plate recently—weeks ago, not years—and bolted it back. And from the gap beneath the bulkhead's lowest course, where the masons had always left a weep hole for seepage, the smell came out at him: cold stone, old timber, and underneath both, faint but unmistakable, lamp oil.

Someone was using the sealed tunnel. Someone had been going up that slope from the survey camp, again and again, carrying lamps into a bore that the government had officially declared dead nine years ago.

Mirabel stood in the tunnel's cold breath with his hand flat against the new-cut mortar, and the dread that had been blooming in him since yesterday finished its work and became something harder and more dangerous. Not dread anymore. Purpose. The ugly, thrilling, irreversible kind.

He had come up this river to log washouts. He would go down this river carrying something else entirely—he just didn't yet know what it was, or whose it was, or what it would cost him.

Behind him, far below, the river kept talking. Ahead of him, through the weep hole, the dark of the sealed tunnel breathed in and out like something sleeping.

He took out his field book, crossed out the morning's tidy plan, and wrote a single new line in his surveyor's hand, the neatest lie he had ever told himself:

*Km 69.4. One more day.*
