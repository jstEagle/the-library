## 4. The Ledger of Small Debts

Sera Oduya kept a ledger, but not the one the tax man saw.

The official book sat under the bar's glass top, columns ruled in red ink: rooms let, meals sold, spirits poured. It balanced to the copper because it had to. The other ledger lived behind her eyes—small debts, small credits, the currency a mountain town actually ran on. Old Piet owed her for the winter he couldn't pay and had repaid in firewood cut before dawn so she'd never see him do it. The Widow Anselm owed her nothing anymore, not since Sera had quietly stopped charging her for coffee after her son went into the gorge and didn't come back out. And the land office in Kellwater owed her a debt so large and so old that she had stopped thinking of it as collectible and started thinking of it as a stone she carried in her boot: always there, always reminding her how she walked.

Her father had owned this inn before her. Her father had also owned the parcel behind it, two acres of good bottomland along the river, until the district surveyor came through one autumn with a chain and a compass and a document with a seal on it, and by spring the parcel belonged to the Crown's reserve and her father belonged to a bottle. He died still insisting the boundary stone had been moved. Everyone in town had nodded politely, the way you nod at grief, and no one had done anything, because what was there to do against paper? Paper didn't bleed. Paper just took.

So when the tall stranger with the careful hands came down off the mountain two days early, gray around the mouth and walking like a man whose skeleton had been rearranged, Sera Oduya opened the second ledger and made an entry.

*The surveyor. Something up there showed him his own face.*

He came in at dusk, shaking rain off his coat in the doorway like he always did, precise even in misery, and asked for his room key in that flat voice he used when he wanted to be furniture. She handed it over. He climbed the stairs. Through the floorboards she heard him cross the room, stop, and then not move again for a long time—a silence with weight to it, the silence of a man standing very still because motion might mean deciding something.

She sent the soup up anyway. That was the entry she made: one bowl of barley soup, no charge, debit column blank. In her experience a man who wouldn't eat was a man getting ready to run, and running men were bad for business and worse for towns. Besides, she wanted him downstairs. A shaken man alone upstairs keeps his secrets. A shaken man across a table from a woman who feeds him starts handing them over piece by piece, like coins he doesn't know he's spending.

He came down within the hour. Of course he did. Nobody eats soup alone in a room when there's a stove below and rain on the roof.

"You look," she said, setting a second bowl down unasked, "like something followed you home."

"Nothing follows me." He said it too fast, the way people say things they've rehearsed. "I finished the section early."

"The section." She sat across from him without asking, which was her privilege as owner of the table. "The closed line past the tunnel?"

His spoon paused above the bowl. Small thing. She logged it. "You know the lines?"

"I know everything about these lines. Everybody here does. My grandfather hauled timber on them when they were alive." She tore bread, gave him half whether he wanted it or not. "We remember them better than the government does. The government remembers them only when it wants to sell something or bury something."

"They're just old tracks."

"Nothing is just anything." She watched him eat, finally, mechanically, hunger winning over caution the way it always does. "You went up past the tunnel. Two days upstream is the narrows, and past the narrows—" she shrugged, letting the geography finish the sentence for her, because everyone in Kellwater knew what was past the narrows, and nobody said it aloud, which was its own kind of knowledge. "Nobody goes up there. Not since the survey crew."

He looked up. First real look he'd given her since he'd arrived three weeks ago, all his careful invisibility dropped for a second like a mask slipping sideways. "What survey crew?"

And there it was. Sera Oduya had tended bar for twenty years; she could read a face the way her father read weather. This man had known. He had walked into something upriver and come back down carrying it, and now he was asking her what it was, either because he genuinely didn't know or because he wanted to hear how much *she* knew, and she honestly couldn't tell which frightened him more.

"Government team," she said. "Two years back. Came through town, bought supplies, asked questions about the source valley. Polite men. One of them, the young one, played cards with Piet's boys and lost cheerfully." She refilled his bowl from the pot. "They went up the river and never came down. Search party found their camp neat as a chapel. Instruments oiled and stowed. Stove cold. Boots gone."

"Gone?"

"No bodies, no tracks leading out. Tracks leading in, plenty. Tracks going out—" she drew a line in the spilled salt with one finger, then erased it with her palm. "Nothing. The district office called it misadventure. Flash flood, maybe, though there'd been no rain. Said the river took them." She smiled without any warmth in it. "The river takes what we love and gives back nothing. Very convenient, our river."

He was quiet a long moment. "And nobody questioned it?"

"Questioned it to whom?" She laughed, short and hard. "To the land office? The same office that moved my father's boundary stone? The same office where complaints go to die in a drawer?" She leaned forward. "Listen to me, surveyor-or-whatever-your-name-is. I've watched you for three weeks. You eat little, talk less, tip exact, and you flinch every time a coach comes up the road. You're hiding from something, and I don't care what—that's your business, and in this town we believe a man's business is his own. But if whatever shook you up on that mountain has the land office's smell on it, then it's got my smell on it too, and my father's, and everybody's in this room."

She gestured, and he noticed—for the first time, she could see him noticing—that the common room wasn't empty. It was never empty. Piet at the corner table mending a net that hadn't needed mending in years. The Anselm widow knitting by the window with her chair angled toward the door. Two teamsters who were not talking to each other but were both listening to everything. A town of people who had learned, over generations, that survival meant watching without appearing to watch.

"We see everything here," she said softly. "Coaches. Strangers. Which way the officials' horses are shod. Who buys rope. Who asks about the upper valley. You think you've been invisible for three weeks?" She almost pitied him. "You've been the most interesting thing in Kellwater since the bridge washed out. We simply agreed, all of us, without saying a word, to leave you be. That's how it works. That's the whole trick of it."

Something moved through his face—she watched it go, that flicker of a man realizing the walls have ears and the ears have names and the names have debts. Fear, maybe. Or relief. With people like him it was hard to say.

"Why?" he said. "Why leave me be?"

"Because you paid your bill on time and fixed the gate hinge without being asked." She stood, collected the bowls. "Small debts, small credits. That's all anybody's honor ever amounts to, if you keep the books honestly."

At the stairs he paused, and she felt him wanting to ask something—the question pressing against his teeth—and then he swallowed it and climbed, and she heard his door close soft as snowfall.

* * *

Upstairs, Mirabel Ishikawa sat on the narrow bed with his father's compass open in his palm and did not sleep.

*So they watch,* he thought. *All of them. Kindly, quietly, completely.* Three weeks he had spent perfecting his invisibility, and the whole time he had been the best-lit object in the valley, seen and catalogued and—strangest of all—protected. She had lied to no one for him yet. But she had told him the shape of the country: that here, watching was loyalty, and silence was a shared possession, and the land office was a wolf the town had learned to feed slowly while keeping count of every meal.

He thought about the camp. The instruments laid out neat as a chapel. No tracks out.

He thought about the torn pages he hadn't mentioned to anyone, folded now inside his coat lining, and the bearing the dead chief's compass had held, pointing upriver toward water no grant should touch.

Below, through the floorboards, he could hear the inn settling into night—and beneath that, faintly, Sera Oduya moving through her closing ritual, and beneath *that*, at the edge of hearing, the sound of the front door opening once, briefly, near midnight. Boots on the boards. Low voices. His body went rigid before his mind caught up.

Then it settled. Because the voices were Piet's and the widow's, and the words, when the wind turned them toward the window, were not about him at all. They were about the land office. About new notices nailed to the granary door that afternoon—something about a *re-survey*, something about the *upper waters*, something about men coming from the district seat before month's end.

Mirabel lay back in the dark and understood, with the cold clarity of a man reading a gauge needle sliding into the red, that the story he had stumbled into was not behind him on the mountain. It was already here. It had been here all along, nailed to doors, carried in ledgers, waiting.

In the morning, he would need to decide whether to walk into the district archive—or run.

He reached for the compass. The needle swung, wavered, and settled: steady as ever, pointing upriver, toward the source.
