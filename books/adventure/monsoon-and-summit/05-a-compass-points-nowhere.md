## 5. A Compass Points Nowhere

He went back to the tunnel on the fourth day, and he told himself it was for professional reasons.

That was the lie he'd been rehearsing since Kellwater. A surveyor returning to a site to verify measurements was as ordinary as rain. He had his contract papers folded in his breast pocket like a talisman, the district land office's stamp blue and official, and if anyone stopped him on the old haul road he would say: checking sight lines, confirming the bore's alignment against my earlier notes. Nobody stopped him. The road was empty in both directions, which was its own kind of answer.

The morning was gray without conviction, the kind of sky that couldn't decide whether to commit to weather. Mirabel climbed the switchback above the river with his instrument case on his shoulder and his father's compass in his coat, and at the tunnel mouth he stood a long time in the shadow of the portal masonry, listening.

Water dripped somewhere inside. Wind moved through the bore with a low organ note, the sound of a throat too large for any body. Nothing else.

He had spent three days not coming back here. Three days of legitimate work on the lower line, chaining distances through bracken, leveling across culverts, filling his notebook with numbers so clean and so meaningless they could have been written by anyone. That was the discipline of being nobody: you filled your hours with tasks so small they left no room for the large ones. But every night at Sera Oduya's inn, over her thin soup and her thinner questions, he had felt the tunnel pulling at him like a missing tooth worries the tongue. The tool marks. Fresh steel bright against forty years of rust. Someone had opened that bore recently, and someone had gone in, and the district land office — his employer, his cover, his whole architecture of invisibility — had no record of it.

He should have reported it. Reporting it was the safe move; reporting it made him a diligent contractor instead of a man who knew things. And yet some instinct older than caution told him that the moment he mentioned that tunnel to the land office, he would stop being a stray surveyor and become a loose end. Men who erased survey teams did not file reports about their own handiwork.

So he had said nothing, and the silence had grown teeth, and now here he was, back at the mouth of the bore, doing the one thing more dangerous than telling: looking.

He lit his lamp and went in.

* * *

The tunnel ran four hundred meters into the mountain before the old collapse sealed it — or had sealed it, once. The reopened section began at the two-hundred-meter mark, where the rockfall had been cut back just enough to admit a man walking bent. Beyond that pinch the air changed. Colder. Stiller. It tasted of stone that hadn't been breathed in decades, and under the stone smell, faint but unmistakable, lamp oil and tobacco — cheap tobacco, the kind sold in twist-paper at the Kellwater general store.

Mirabel marked the pinch point in his notebook out of pure habit, then hated himself for the neatness of it. Chainage 2+07.3. As if this were a job.

The chamber beyond the pinch opened into the old construction adit, a widened pocket where the original crews had staged their drills and muck carts. His lamp found the walls first: drill scars in fans, chalk survey marks faded to ghosts, a date scratched by some dead navvy — 1889 — half-swallowed by lime weep. Then the floor.

A cache. Small, deliberate, tucked into a drainage channel behind a slab of fallen lining. A canvas satchel, army pattern, its buckles done up. On top of it, placed rather than dropped, a brass compass case.

He knelt. Every nerve in his body was shouting the same instruction it had shouted at the abandoned camp two days upstream: touch nothing, walk away, be nobody. But his hands were already moving, because his hands had never once obeyed that instruction in thirty-one years, and because — he understood this only now, kneeling in the dark with his own pulse in his ears — the neatness was the message. Things laid out carefully were things meant to be found. Or meant to be found by someone specific. The question was which.

The compass case opened onto a standard surveyor's prismatic, glass intact, needle live. Not his father's — his father's rode in his own coat, its card yellowed, its bearing ring worn smooth by a dead man's thumb. This one was newer, government issue, the kind issued to field parties by the crate. Its bezel was set to a single bearing, locked there, as if whoever had last used it wanted the direction preserved.

He read it twice to be sure.

North-northwest, twenty-two degrees west of true. Upstream. Into the gorge. Toward the source valley — the valley every map in the district office shaded in hatched lines and labeled, in the flat bureaucratic voice that makes erasure look like typography: NO SURVEY PERMITTED.

He sat back on his heels and let the lamp gutter and thought about nothing at all for a full minute, which took effort.

Then he opened the satchel.

* * *

Inside: a waxed-cloth packet, dry despite the drip of forty years, tied with tarred twine. Inside that, a book. Government-issue field journal, cloth boards, the stamp of the Department of Lands and Internal Survey on the flyleaf, and beneath the stamp, in pencil, a crew designation and a date from six years past.

The same crew whose camp he had found. The same date range. The same neat instruments laid out on the same cold stove, waiting for men who had walked in and never walked out.

His hands were steady until they weren't. He noticed the tremor only when the pages began to flutter against his fingers, and he pressed the journal flat against his knee and made himself breathe the way he'd taught himself to breathe in the bad years: four counts in, seven held, eight out. A trick borrowed from a bridge foreman who'd survived a collapse in the lowlands, who'd said fear was just a load path with nowhere to go and you had to give it one.

He gave it the reading.

The journal was meticulous. Daily entries, chainages, water levels, strata sketches — competent work, better than competent, the hand growing more confident page by page until he could see the junior engineer finding his footing. Entries about weather. About the cook burning the rice. About a mule named Corporal who ate a theodolite tripod leg and was forgiven. A human document. Six men, isolated, doing careful work in a gorge the maps pretended was blank paper.

And then, eleven days before the final entry, the work changed character.

They had left the permitted corridor. The language shifted from chainage and level to words like *boundary* and *grant* and *stone*. Sketches appeared that were not engineering sketches: a carved marker half-swallowed by hillside root, its inscription rendered in careful facsimile. Rubbings. A drawing of a valley floor with contour lines drawn in a different ink than the rest of the page — added later, he guessed, over an original survey that showed something else entirely.

Mirabel turned the pages slowly, and the story assembled itself the way a structure assembles under load: quietly, then all at once.

The crew had surveyed upriver past the limit of their commission. Something had made them do it — the journal didn't say what, not directly, but the entries grew shorter and denser, the handwriting tighter, as though the men were writing fast to get it down before it could be taken from them. There was a passage about ruins. Pre-flood ruins, older than any settlement the district records acknowledged, terraced walls drowned under centuries of slide debris, and a boundary stone whose inscription did not match the grant on file — dated wrong, or cut wrong, or moved, the entry wasn't specific, just insistent, underlined twice:

*Stone does not agree with grant. Stone is older than grant. Stone cannot be forged. Grant can.*

Underlined twice. By a man who understood what he was writing and where he was writing it.

Mirabel read that line three times. Around him the tunnel held its breath. The drip kept its slow metronome. Somewhere far off, wind moved through the upper bore and made its organ note, and he thought: this is what it sounds like inside the thing that will kill me, if I let it.

Because he already knew how these stories ended. He had lived the opening chapters of one himself. A man finds something the powerful have paid to bury; the man reports it or doesn't; either way, the man becomes a problem, and problems in this district were solved with river water and unmarked graves. Six surveyors had walked into this gorge carrying exactly the knowledge now open on his knee, and six surveyors had ceased, in every record the government kept, to exist. Their camp was still waiting for them. Their instruments were still laid out neatly, because neatness was cheaper than explanation.

He should close the book. He should put it back in the satchel, return the satchel to the drainage channel, walk down the mountain, finish his contract, collect his pay, and remain Mirabel-nothing of nowhere, alive.

Every instinct he owned voted for that. Unanimously.

He turned to the last written page.

* * *

The final entry was dated six years ago, in autumn, in a hand he recognized from the earlier pages — the crew chief's, the confident one, the one that had underlined the stone. It was short. Shorter than any entry in the book. It read, in full:

*Escort recalled us today. Lieutenant says orders from the district. We are to descend tomorrow. I am not satisfied that we will all arrive.*

Below that, nothing. No farewell, no summary, no location fix. Just the next leaf — torn.

Not ripped in panic. Torn cleanly, at the gutter, with a blade or a thumbnail run sharp along the hinge, the way an engineer tears a page when he wants the tear to look deliberate rather than desperate. Mirabel tilted the stub toward the lamp. The remaining edge was feathered white, fibers standing up like grass after a scythe. One page. Possibly two. Whatever the chief had written last — the coordinates, the evidence, the thing worth killing six men over — had been removed by someone who intended it to survive somewhere else.

Or by the chief himself, intending to come back for it, and never coming back.

Mirabel held the journal up to the light and looked at the stub again, longer this time, the way he'd learned to look at fracture surfaces. A tear tells you about the forces that made it. This one told him: patience. Foreknowledge. A man tearing out his own last words in a tunnel, hiding them, walking out to meet an escort he did not trust.

*I am not satisfied that we will all arrive.*

The sentence went through him like a spike through a rail. Because the chief had known. Had written it down, calmly, in the flat prose of a working engineer, and then had torn away the proof and hidden it — here? Nearby? Somewhere along the river? — and had gone down the mountain anyway, because there was nowhere else to go, and because maybe part of him believed that six licensed men of the government survey could not simply be erased.

They could be. They had been.

Mirabel closed the journal and sat in the dark with his hands flat on the boards, and for a while he let himself feel the full weight of the arithmetic. Six men. One stone. One grant signed by someone with authority enough to make the forgery stick. And now one stray surveyor under a false name, holding the dead crew's own book, crouched in the tunnel where somebody had very recently cut through forty years of collapse — recently enough that the tobacco smell was still fresh, recently enough that whoever reopened this bore might come back.

That thought arrived late and hit hard. The tool marks were fresh. The cache had been found once already — or placed once already. Either way, he was not the first visitor. He snuffed the lamp on reflex and knelt blind in the black, listening past his own heartbeat, and heard only water and wind, and after ten minutes that felt like an hour, he relit it and got moving.

* * *

He took the journal. That decision made itself somewhere below the level of argument, the way his hands had opened the satchel. He reasoned at himself the whole climb down — you're carrying a dead man's evidence, you're carrying your own death warrant, put it back — and his body ignored every word of it, wrapping the book in the waxed cloth, binding the packet tight, sliding it flat against his spine inside his coat where a notebook usually rode. Old habit. In the bad years he'd carried his real name the same way: flat against the skin, where no search would casually go.

At the tunnel mouth he paused once more and took out his father's compass, because there was a question he had been avoiding since the chamber, and the mountain deserved better than avoidance.

The dead crew's prismatic was locked on a bearing: north-northwest, twenty-two degrees west. Upstream. Into the forbidden country. He set his father's compass beside it on the portal stone and let both needles settle.

They agreed. Exactly. To the degree.

Two instruments, six years apart, pointing at the same fold in the mountains — the crew's locked bearing and his father's free-swinging needle, which pointed nowhere in particular and therefore, at this moment, at precisely the place the torn pages had gone to die. The source valley. The hatched blank on the maps. The place where the stone was older than the grant.

His father had bought that compass secondhand in a port city and carried it through three careers and a bankruptcy, and had given it to Mirabel with one sentence of instruction: *It doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you where you are. The rest is yours.* Mirabel had spent five years using it to stay lost. Now it lay on cold stone agreeing with a dead man's last setting, and the needle did not tremble, and neither, he noticed, did he.

That frightened him more than the tunnel had.

He put both compasses away. He ate half a ration of bread standing up, tasting nothing, and started down the switchbacks as the gray sky finally committed to rain — a thin, cold, preliminary rain, the monsoon's advance rider, testing the ground. Below him the river ran high and brown and loud, pouring out of the gorge, out of the blank space on the map, carrying snowmelt and silt and whatever else the mountains wanted to give back.

He walked the haul road toward Kellwater with the journal against his spine and the rain starting to find the seams of his coat, and he conducted the argument one last time, fairly, both sides, the way he'd been trained to weigh loads.

Flee. Finish the contract. Be nobody. The valley would flood or not flood; the stone would stand or be drowned; none of it would be his grave.

Stay. Follow the bearing. Find the torn pages. Become, permanently and publicly, the man who knew.

Every instinct voted flee. But instincts, he thought, descending through the wet bracken with a dead crew's book warming against his back, were just habits with seniority. And he had spent five years learning exactly how a man disappears — which meant he was the one man in this district who also knew, measurement by measurement, chainage by chainage, what it costs.

In Kellwater that evening, Sera Oduya set the soup in front of him and studied his face with those quick dark eyes that missed nothing in her house, and said, "You've been upriver."

"Lower line," he said. "Culvert work."

"Mm." She refilled his bowl without being asked, which she only did when she'd decided something. "There were men through town yesterday. Land office men. Asking after contractors." She set the ladle down, gently, the way you set down something you might need as a weapon. "I said I'd let them know if any passed through."

He looked at her. She looked at him. Neither of them said the next sentence, and the soup steamed between them like a border.

Upstairs, alone, he unwrapped the journal and read the chief's last line again by candlelight: *I am not satisfied that we will all arrive.*

Neither, Mirabel thought, am I.

But the bearing was set, and the needle agreed, and somewhere upstream, in the hatched blank where no survey was permitted, a torn page waited in the dark for whoever had the nerve to come and read it.
