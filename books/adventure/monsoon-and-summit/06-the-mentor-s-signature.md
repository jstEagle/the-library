## 6. The Mentor's Signature

The district archive occupied the back half of the land office building in Kellwater, which meant it occupied the back half of a building that had been built to be forgotten. The front half still had a clerk's window and a bell you could ring for service; the back half had a door with a padlock older than Mirabel and a smell like paper left out in weather for thirty years. He'd walked past that door every day since arriving. He'd made himself not look at it.

Now he looked at it.

The padlock was open. Not broken—open, hanging loose on its hasp as if someone had been interrupted mid-thought. The land office men had come through town two days ago asking about a stray surveyor, and Sera Oduya had lied to them without being asked, and they had gone upriver after him, or so the town believed. But someone had been here first. Someone had wanted into this room before they arrived, or after, or both.

Mirabel stood in the alley behind the building for a long moment, doing what he always did: counting exits. The alley ran to the mill road on one side and dead-ended at a drainage cut on the other. One window at the rear of the archive, barred. The front door onto the street. Two ways out, one of them bad. It was enough. It had always been enough. That was the whole architecture of his life now—enough, barely, arranged so nothing ever required more than he could carry alone.

He went in.

* * *

Inside, the archive was a single long room lit by one high window, and it was organized by no system he could determine, which told him something about how much anyone cared what happened in here. Deed boxes stacked to the ceiling. Survey plats rolled and tied with string gone soft as cloth. Grant registers bound in cracked leather, their spines labeled in three different hands across three different decades, none of them consistent. Dust lay over everything in an even gray skin, except along one aisle where the dust had been disturbed—smeared, swept by a coat sleeve, marked by the round heel-print of a boot that had stood too long in one place.

Someone had been looking for something specific.

Mirabel should have left then. He knew that the way he knew a bridge was rotten—by feel, by the particular wrongness in the air of a place that had already decided to fail. The land office men were upriver hunting him. Whoever had searched this room might come back. And yet his feet carried him down the disturbed aisle the way water carries itself downhill, because there was a question in this room now, and he had learned, over these last weeks, that questions did not release him. They held on. They waited. His father's compass sat heavy in his breast pocket, pointing its useless faithful north, and Mirabel thought: *you are a man who follows bearings. Admit it.*

He started with the grant registers, because grants were where land became paper and paper became power. The most recent volume covered the last six years. He pulled it down and set it on the reading table under the window and opened it, and the smell rose up around him—mold and iron-gall ink and the faint sweetness of decaying glue—and for a moment he was twenty-three years old again, standing in Aldous Vane's office while the old man explained why a surveyor's signature was the most dangerous object in the world.

"A signature is a confession," Vane had said, tapping the page with one blunt finger. "Everything else can be argued. A drawing can be redrawn, a measurement re-measured, a word reinterpreted. But when you put your name to a document, you are saying: *I saw this. I stake my honor that it is true.* Never sign anything you haven't verified with your own instruments and your own eyes. Never sign for another man's work. And never—" and here the old man had smiled, that dry, crooked smile that had made generations of junior engineers feel chosen, "—never let your name travel anywhere your conscience wouldn't want to live."

Mirabel had written it in his field notebook. He had lived by it. Right up until the Kesswick Junction collapse, when the inquiry needed a man to blame and found one who had signed a stress report he'd been ordered to trust, and his name had traveled somewhere his conscience couldn't follow, and he had buried that name in the mountains and become nobody.

He turned the pages of the register slowly. Grants, grants, easements, rights-of-way, each entry a small transaction in the great dull machinery of ownership. He was halfway through the fourth year when his eye caught the phrase *source valley*.

He stopped.

The entry was dated fourteen months ago. *Land grant, source valley drainage scheme, parcels 1 through 47, comprising the whole of the upper river basin above the gorge.* Forty-seven parcels. The whole basin. Every acre from the gorge wall to the river's forbidden headwaters—the valley Nadya Kesh's people had farmed for longer than any map recorded, the valley the drowned survey team had walked into and never walked out of. The grant authorized, in language of magnificent bureaucratic blandness, "impoundment works and controlled inundation pursuant to the downstream flood mitigation program."

Controlled inundation. They were going to drown it. Deliberately, legally, on paper—and call the drowning a safety measure.

Mirabel's hands had gone cold. He noticed it distantly, the way you notice rain starting. He turned the page to the certification block, because that was procedure, that was training, that was the part of him that still worked even when the rest of him had stopped.

There were three signatures required. The district commissioner's—he recognized the name, a man named Harrow, fussy and ambitious. A witness for the land office. And the certifying engineer, the man whose professional judgment attested that the surveys underlying the grant were sound, that the boundaries were true, that the flood line calculations would hold.

Aldous Vane.

The signature was unmistakable. Mirabel had seen it thousands of times—on his own apprenticeship papers, on the drawings Vane corrected in red ink at midnight, on the letter that had finally reached him in the mountains, two years late, saying only: *Keep your head down. This will pass.* The old man wrote his name the way he did everything, with a slight forward lean, the *V* sharp as a surveyor's mark, the final *e* trailing off like a line running off the edge of a plat. Even reproduced here in faded iron-gall ink, it leaned forward. It moved. It looked alive.

It looked exactly like the signature of the man who had taught Mirabel that a signature was a confession.

* * *

He didn't remember sitting down. He was on the reading stool, the register open in front of him, the high window throwing a pale trapezoid of light across the page, and the room was very quiet, and somewhere outside a cart went by and the sound seemed to belong to another country.

He read the entry again. Then again. Looking, he realized, for a flaw—for some technicality, some clerical error, some way the document could be other than what it was. That was what Vane would have done. Find the weakness. Test the structure. Nothing stands unless it can bear load.

But the document bore load. The parcel descriptions matched the coordinates in the dead team's journal—the bearing that pointed upriver, toward the source, toward the shrine in the gorge. The flood line elevations were given to the tenth of a meter, precise, confident, the work of a first-rate engineer. And the date of the underlying boundary survey—the survey on which all forty-seven parcels rested—was listed as having been completed eleven months before the government team had entered the valley.

Eleven months before. But the survey team had gone in fourteen months ago and never come out. Which meant either the dates were wrong—or the survey the grant relied on had never happened at all. Or it had happened, and been erased, and replaced.

The torn pages. The journal's missing final pages. Reyes, if he lived—if any of them lived—might know what those pages said. But Mirabel found he no longer needed them to understand the shape of it. A boundary survey that predated the surveyors sent to conduct it. A flood scheme certified by the finest engineer in the district. A valley full of people whose claim to their own land appeared nowhere in the record, because their claim was older than records, and records were the only thing the law could see.

And underneath all of it, leaning forward, moving, alive: *Aldous Vane.*

Mirabel closed the register. His hands were steady. That frightened him more than shaking would have. Somewhere in the last ten minutes he had passed beyond the reach of trembling, into a colder country.

* * *

He stayed in the archive until the light through the high window went from white to amber. He told himself he was being thorough. He copied the grant number, the parcel list, the dates, the certification block, everything, into his notebook in his smallest hand. He checked the adjacent entries and found two more grants, downstream parcels, part of the same "mitigation program"—the program that would require the reservoir the source valley's drowning would create. The scheme was vast. Ten thousand people lived below the gorge, Vane would say later, or someone would say; Mirabel could already hear the argument being built, brick by reasonable brick, because he had been raised inside that mind and knew its architecture better than any living man. *The valley floods anyway, boy. Every monsoon. We are not drowning it—we are choosing when. Order instead of chaos. Arithmetic instead of prayer.*

He could hear it because Vane had taught him to think that way. That was the betrayal inside the betrayal: not that his mentor had signed, but that Mirabel could construct the defense himself, could argue the old man's case better than the old man could, and feel, even now, the terrible gravity of its reasonableness pulling at him like current.

He shut the notebook.

The boot print in the aisle. The open padlock. Someone else had come looking for this same document—recently, urgently. The land office men? Tying off loose ends before going upriver? Or someone else entirely—someone who also knew what was in this room?

He looked at the register's neighbors on the shelf. One box was out of sequence, pushed back at a wrong angle, its string retied in a knot Mirabel himself didn't use but recognized: a surveyor's knot, quick-release, the kind every field man learned. Whoever had searched this room had known documents and known haste.

He was still crouched there, examining the knot like a fool, like a man reading tea leaves, when he heard the front door of the land office open and close, and footsteps cross the clerk's room, and stop.

Not entering. Waiting. Or listening.

Mirabel moved without deciding to move, the way he'd once thrown himself clear of a buckling trestle—not thought, just the body's old arithmetic. There was a gap behind the deed boxes at the room's far end, a shadowed slot between shelving and wall, wide enough for a thin man and his fear. He folded himself into it as the archive door swung inward.

Two men entered. He saw them in slices through the gap: boots, coat hems, lamplight. One carried a lantern though the window light hadn't failed yet—a man who liked certainty. They went directly to the grant registers. Of course they did. They pulled the same volume, the recent one, and turned pages, and Mirabel watched the lantern light tremble slightly and understood that these were not calm men. These were men checking whether a thing was still where they'd left it.

"Still here," one said quietly. "Harrow wants it left in place. No new filings, no amendments. If anyone pulls this register again, we're to know who."

"And the surveyor?"

"Gone to ground, or dead in the gorge. Either way." A pause. "Vane says leave him. Says he'll surface or he won't."

Mirabel closed his eyes. *Vane says.* As if the old man's voice still commanded rooms, still bent men to it, still leaned forward like his signature. And beneath the cold, beneath the betrayal settling into him like groundwater into a foundation, Mirabel felt the other thing rise, the thing he'd have paid money to be rid of: relief. *Leave him,* the voice had said. The mentor, even now, even guilty—even perhaps especially guilty—had spent a word on his student's life.

That was the war, and it began right there in the dark behind the deed boxes, with dust in his throat and his heart going like a pile driver: the truth he owed the dead versus the loyalty he owed the living. The torn pages versus the trailing *e* of a familiar hand. *Never sign for another man's work,* the old man had said, and had signed, and Mirabel could not make the two sentences fit inside one world.

The men finished. The lantern swung away. The door closed, the front door opened and shut, and the archive settled back into its silence.

Mirabel stayed in the dark a long time. When he finally came out, his legs were stiff and the light through the window was nearly gone. He returned the register to its shelf, aligned it precisely, and erased his presence from the room as best he could—an old habit, the habit of a man with two names.

Then, at the door, he stopped. Because there was one thing he couldn't erase, and he was only now, in the failing light, letting himself see it plainly: the grant's certification block had a space for the date of countersignature by the district commissioner, and that space was blank. Harrow had not yet signed. The grant was drafted, certified, sealed into the record—but not executed. Not final. There was still a step between paper and drowning, and that step had not been taken.

Which meant the scheme could still be stopped—not by running, not by hiding, but by putting the forgery in front of eyes that mattered, before Harrow's pen touched paper. Before the monsoon. Before the water.

Eleven months of falsified survey. A valley of forty-seven parcels. A mentor's name on the confession.

Mirabel slipped out through the alley into the blue dusk, past the drainage cut, up toward the inn where Sera Oduya would have soup waiting and questions she was too wise to ask. Halfway there he understood what he was actually deciding, and the understanding landed in him like a stone dropped down a well—no splash, just depth.

He was going to need the rest of the journal. The torn pages. The proof.

And the only man who might know where they were cached was dying at the bottom of a flooded tunnel, eleven days upriver, in the direction his compass had been pointing all along.
