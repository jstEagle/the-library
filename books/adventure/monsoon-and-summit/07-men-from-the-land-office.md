## 7. Men from the Land Office

Sera Oduya had kept the Kettle & Compass for eleven years, and in that time she had learned to read men the way she read weather off the mountains. The clouds came up the gorge with a certain gray weight before rain; the land office men came into her common room with a certain set of their shoulders before trouble.

They arrived on the Thursday coach, two of them, out of step with each other because one was tall and moved like a man who had never carried anything heavier than a ledger, and the other was short and broad and walked like a boot heel looking for something to crush. The tall one wore a good coat brushed clean of road dust. The short one wore his coat like a threat he hadn't gotten around to making yet.

They took the corner table by the window without asking for it—the table Sera reserved, in her own head, for people she didn't want sitting anywhere else—and they ordered coffee and then, when it came, did not drink it.

"Proprietor," the tall one said. His voice was smooth as a filing clerk's. "We're from the district land office. Conducting inquiries."

"Congratulations," Sera said. "Inquiries about what?"

"A surveyor." He said it the way you'd say *stray dog*. "A contract man, working the decommissioned lines. We believe he may have come through Kellwater."

Sera poured herself a cup at the bar and carried it back, standing where she could see both their faces and the door behind them. She had a rule about land office men: never turn your back, never answer straight, never let them finish a sentence if you can help it. Eleven years of quiet resentments had taught the town that rule too, though nobody wrote it down. The office took taxes and gave back boundary disputes. It closed the mill road and called it an improvement. It had reclassified half the eastern slopes as crown reserve three winters ago, and the families who'd grazed those slopes since before anyone remembered were told, politely, in writing, that they had always been trespassing.

So no, Sera thought. Nobody in this town would be falling over themselves to help these two.

"We'd like to ask some questions," the tall one continued. "About travelers. Recent ones."

"You can ask," Sera said. "Whether I answer is another matter."

The short one laughed—not warmly, but like a man demonstrating he had a sense of humor filed somewhere in his personnel record. Then he stopped laughing abruptly, which was more honest.

They asked anyway. They asked about the coach schedules. They asked who had taken rooms in the past month. They asked whether any man had come through asking after the old tunnel line above the falls, the one sealed since the collapse year.

And then the tall one opened a leather folder and turned it toward her, and Sera's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain air coming under the door.

It was a sketch. Charcoal on cheap paper, done by someone with real skill—done fast, probably from a photograph or a description given by someone who had seen the man close. A face maybe forty years old, hollow-cheeked, dark hair going gray at the temples, a jaw set like a man chewing on something he couldn't swallow. And she knew it. Of course she knew it. She had fed that face soup for two weeks. She had watched it over her counter every evening while the man sat in the corner by the stove, eating slowly, listening to everything, saying almost nothing.

The sketch was unmistakably him. The man who called himself Merrick. The surveyor with the careful hands and the compass he touched like a relic.

"That's quite a likeness," Sera said, and made her voice flat as slate. "Whoever he is."

"You recognize it, then."

"I recognize that it's well drawn. That's not the same thing."

The tall one studied her. He had the patience of paperwork, that one. The short one had the patience of a trapline. Between them they covered most of the ways a man could be hunted.

"The district office contracted a survey team this spring," the tall one said. "Government work. That team has gone missing. Their instruments were found abandoned upstream. One of our junior contractors—a freelance man working the closed lines independently—was in the area. We want to talk to him. As a witness."

*As a witness.* Sera turned the phrase over. In her experience, men from offices said *witness* the way butchers said *stock*. You didn't come two days' road into the mountains with a charcoal sketch and a silent partner to interview witnesses. You sent a letter. This was not a letter.

"Well," she said. "I keep a rooming house, not a registry. Men pass through. They pay, they sleep, they go. If one of them was your surveyor, he didn't leave a forwarding address."

"He's been here," the short one said. Not asking. "Two weeks, give or take. Eats alone. Drinks tea, not ale. Keeps to the corner table." He looked at her with eyes like a ferret doing arithmetic. "That's what we were told in Lowmark. By the stationmaster there. Man matching this sketch bought a ticket to Kellwater on the fourteenth."

Sera sipped her coffee. Her heart was hammering and she made herself pour the next sentence out slow, like syrup, so they wouldn't hear it.

"Then he left again," she said. "Men do. That's rather the point of a junction town."

"When?"

"I don't keep a diary of my guests' departures. There's a ledger. Room numbers and dates. If you have legal authority to inspect it, show me the paper. Otherwise it stays shut."

The tall one produced paper. Of course he did. A warrant of inquiry, stamped and countersigned, authorizing inspection of commercial registers within the district. It was even legitimate—she checked the seal twice, hating herself for hoping it wasn't. The law, when it chose to arrive in Kellwater, always arrived properly dressed.

She went and got the ledger. She laid it open on the bar and stood beside it, and when the tall one ran his finger down the columns she watched him find the entry: room four, fourteen days, paid in advance, name *Merrick*, occupation written in her own hand as *surveyor*, departure column blank.

Blank. She had left it blank. She noticed that now, staring at her own book, and felt a small cold shock at herself—because she had left it blank on purpose, weeks ago, without admitting to herself why. The man had settled his bill the night before he went upriver. He'd said he might be gone some time. She had entered the payment and skipped the departure line, and told herself it was sloppiness.

It had not been sloppiness.

"So he hasn't checked out," the tall one said softly.

"He settled his account. After that his business is his own. Men go upriver from here all the time—fishermen, hunters, fools. The gorge doesn't require my signature."

The short one leaned in. Up close he smelled of tobacco and metal polish. "Here's how this goes, madam," he said pleasantly. "You tell us where he went, or you tell us you don't know. Those are the only two answers that cost you nothing. Everything else starts costing."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's arithmetic."

"Then here's my arithmetic," Sera said. "I don't know where he went. That's the truth, and it happens to also be the answer that costs me nothing, which I'm sure will disappoint you."

The tall one closed the ledger gently, like a man closing a coffin lid. "We'll need to speak with your staff. And a few of the townsfolk. Routine."

"Kellwater's full of routine," Sera said. "Enjoy it."

* * *

They spent two days in town, and Sera watched them work, and the watching frightened her more than the questions had.

Because they were good. That was the thing she hadn't expected. She had imagined land office men as clerks in bad boots, men who got lost between the coach stop and the tavern. These two split the town like surgeons. The tall one took the respectable people—the factor, the schoolmistress, the pastor—and interviewed them in soft, courteous tones, leaving each one feeling flattered and slightly dirtied. The short one took the rivermen and the track-walkers and the boy who swept the platform, and he didn't bother with courtesy at all; he just asked questions until people ran out of lies, and a few of them had run out fast.

By Friday evening, half the town knew a sketch existed, and the other half knew it by Saturday morning. Sera heard it in fragments at her own tables: the drawing was pinned up behind the bar of the freight depot; the drawing was shown at the church door; the drawing showed a man who had eaten soup at the Kettle & Compass every night for two weeks, and the innkeeper had told the officials nothing, and wasn't that interesting, and wasn't that dangerous.

That last part was what kept her awake. Not the danger to herself—she had priced that already, honestly, the way she priced flour. The danger was the arithmetic of a small town. Kellwater held perhaps three hundred souls, and among them were always four or five who would trade a stranger's name for favor, or for spite, or simply for the pleasure of being the one who knew. She had seen it before, in the strike year, when the company men came asking who had organized the walkout. Two names had been sold inside a day. One of the men named had been crippled by the company police and left the valley forever.

So she did what she could, quietly. She told Tam, her pot-boy, that if anyone asked, the surveyor had been a passing geologist and dull as ditchwater. She mentioned to the Widow Ferris, whose memory was long and whose grudges were longer, that the land office had been the ones who took the eastern slopes. She left the rest to the town's native hostility toward men with stamps in their pockets.

But lying for him—that she had not planned. She understood that now, turning it over in the dark above her kitchen. She had lied the way you catch yourself speaking a language you didn't know you'd learned. *He left again. I don't know where he went.* Clean sentences, delivered without a tremor, and every word of them false or near enough. She had built the lie out of materials she'd had lying around: the blank ledger line, the town's resentments, her own flat voice.

And underneath the lie, colder than the fear, was the discovery.

Someone else had decided his life mattered.

Not Mirabel—Ishikawa, whatever his true name was; she still only half-believed *Merrick* was false, because a man who lied about his name usually lied badly, and this one lied beautifully, thoroughly, down to his boots. No: someone else—herself, apparently—had looked at a charcoal sketch held by two men with a warrant and a silent partner, and had chosen, instantly, without deliberation, without consulting a single one of her principles, to stand between.

She had spent eleven years building a life with no dependencies. No husband, no children, no partners, no debts except the mortgage she paid like clockwork. Independence was the whole architecture. And in one afternoon she had discovered a load-bearing wall she never remembered building—one that ran straight through her common room and under the mountains to wherever that man was walking, carrying her lie in his pocket along with his father's compass.

It terrified her. Not the officials. Them she understood; they were just weather with badges. What terrified her was herself—how fast the decision had been made, how little she had been consulted. She was fifty-one years old. She had buried a husband and outlasted a company and survived on the principle that you owed strangers politeness and nothing else. And she had thrown a lie across a warrant like a woman throwing her coat over a puddle for a man she'd known two weeks.

Soup, she told herself. It started with soup. He'd come in shaking the first night, soaked through, and she'd put a bowl in front of him because he looked like a man who hadn't eaten in a day, and he'd tried to pay and she'd waved it off, and something had started then that she hadn't noticed until it was load-bearing.

* * *

Saturday night the two of them came back to the Kettle & Compass.

They came late, after the room had emptied, and this time the tall one did not order coffee. He sat down at her clean bar and folded his hands and said, "Madam Oduya. We've completed our inquiries in town."

"Wonderful. Will there be a report? I do love a report."

"There will be consequences," the short one said, "if certain facts come out later that weren't stated now."

"Facts," Sera said. "Name one."

The tall one slid a second sheet of paper across the bar. Another sketch—but this one was different. Rougher. Done from memory, or from a description, and it was not the surveyor. It was a woman. Middle-aged, strong jaw, hair pinned back, an apron. Drawn standing behind a bar.

It was her.

"Now that one's badly drawn," Sera said. "My nose is not that generous."

"An innkeeper in a junction town," the tall one said, "who obstructs a district inquiry into the disappearance of a government survey team. That's a serious matter, madam. Interference. Concealment. Depending on what's found upstream, it could be read as complicity."

"In what?"

"They haven't decided yet," the short one said. "That's rather the point. It depends what they find. Or what they need to find."

There it was, laid out plain as a rail spike on a tie: the machinery, visible for once. Guilt as a fluid, poured to fit whatever container the office required. Sera had grown up watching that machinery from a distance, the way you watch a river from high ground. Now it was at her door, and the ground was not so high.

"What is it you actually want?" she asked.

"For you to remember," the tall one said. "Details surface. Under pressure, memory improves. Where he went. Who he spoke to. Whether he said anything about the tunnel above the falls, or the old camp, or—" he paused, delicately, "—the source valley."

"The source valley is closed," Sera said. "Has been since before I kept this house. Nobody goes there. Your own office posts the notice."

"Notices are for ordinary people," the short one said. "Your surveyor isn't ordinary. Neither, it turns out, are you."

Sera wiped the bar in front of them, slowly, a gesture that was half hospitality and half dare. "Here's what I remember," she said finally. "A man stayed fourteen nights. He was quiet, polite, paid cash, tipped Tam, fixed my sticking shutter without being asked—he's handy, whoever he is—and he left upriver on a Tuesday. I don't know where he went. The gorge has forty side-cuts and he could be in any of them, or over the pass, or dead in a crevasse, and none of it would surprise me. That's my memory. Write it down exactly like that, and sign it yourselves, because I won't."

The tall one actually smiled. It was the worst thing she'd seen all week. "You'd have made a fine witness for the office, madam, if the office had hired you instead of hunting you."

"And you'd have made a fine human being, if someone had ever hired you for that."

The short one stood. His chair scraped. For one long moment Sera thought the pleasant part was over, and she found, examining herself in that moment, that she was less afraid of that than she should have been—which was its own kind of discovery, and not a comfortable one.

But they left. They had a schedule; she could see that. Whatever was happening upstream had a clock on it, and clocks make men polite.

Before they went out the door, the tall one left the warrant copy on the bar, deliberately, she was sure. A message: we know where you keep your papers. We know where you sleep. We know what you are worth, and what you owe, and on whom.

Sera burned it in the kitchen stove that night, after the lamps were out, and watched the seal curl and blacken, and felt a satisfaction so fierce and so foolish that she laughed alone in the dark kitchen, briefly, like a woman whistling at a funeral.

* * *

On Sunday morning, Tam came down early and found her already at the stove, and told her, hesitantly, that the officials had gone to the depot and taken the first coach down-valley. Gone. Just like that, the way weather lifts.

Gone, Sera thought, is not the same as finished. Men like that don't leave; they rotate. They'd be back in a month with more paper and better sketches, or they'd send worse men with fewer manners. The town would remember the sketch. The town would remember that she had lied. Somewhere in Kellwater right now, four or five people were holding that fact like a stone in a sling, deciding whether and when to throw it.

And upstream—she did the geography in her head, the way she did everything, in costs and distances—if the man had gone up the gorge toward the source valley, he was walking deeper into exactly the country those two had been asking about. The closed country. The country where a government survey team had vanished so cleanly that even the rumor of it had been scrubbed, and where the only man who might know why was now walking toward it with her lie folded in his pack.

She stood at her window that evening and looked up the gorge, where the river went white around the first bend and disappeared into shadow, and she caught herself doing something she had not done in eleven years of keeping this house.

She was waiting for someone.

Not passively—she distrusted waiting as a virtue; her husband had died of passive waiting, in a sense, waiting on a doctor who never came. No: actively. Calculating. She caught herself estimating coach times and river levels and how many days a fit man would need to reach the falls, and whether the supply boat went up as far as the narrows this time of year, and what a person would need to carry, and whether she knew anyone with a boat.

She caught herself planning a route upstream.

The realization arrived with its own weather. She set down her cup. She said aloud, to the empty room, to the mountains, to no one: "Well. Look at that."

Eleven years of independence, and here was the ledger's hidden page, the debt she'd never entered: someone else's life, entered against her own, in her own hand, in ink she couldn't recall dipping.

The terrifying discovery was not that the land office men had come. Men with warrants come and go; they are a season, like the rains. The terrifying discovery was that when they showed her that charcoal face and asked her where he had gone, some part of her—some part older than her principles, older than her grief, older than her careful architecture—had answered before she did.

*Not him. Not through me.*

And now, upstairs, pulling her father's old river map from the bottom of the trunk where it had slept for thirty years, tracing the gorge with one finger past the falls, past the narrows, up toward the forbidden bend where the notices began—now she understood what that voice had committed her to.

If he was up there, and if those men went up after him with their fluid guilt and their containers—

Then somebody was going to have to warn him.

And the coach only went so far.
