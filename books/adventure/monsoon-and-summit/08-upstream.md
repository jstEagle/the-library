## 8. Upstream

The contract papers burned quickly. That was the first surprise—how little it took to unmake a man's official existence. Three pages of district land office letterhead, a surveyor's stamp, a signature that belonged to no one, and the cheap paper went up in the stove of Sera Oduya's back kitchen like it had been waiting all along to be smoke.

Mirabel fed them in one sheet at a time, watching each curl and blacken, and felt nothing he could name. Not grief. Not relief. Something closer to the sensation of stepping off a platform onto a moving train—the moment when your weight is still on solid ground but your momentum has already committed you.

"You'll want to burn the pencil too," Sera said from the doorway. "They can lift writing off anything these days."

He looked at her. She was holding a cloth sack that smelled of bread and dried fish, and she was not looking at him directly, which was her way of granting privacy while refusing to leave him alone with his decision.

"The pencil stays," he said. "I may need to write something down before this is over."

"Then write it small." She set the sack on the table beside him. "Bread for two days. Fish for three if you're careful. There's a flask of the good tea under the loaf—don't tell me I gave it to you, because I didn't."

"Sera."

"Don't." She turned away, busying herself with a pot that didn't need tending. "You're going upriver after whatever took those men, and I'm an innkeeper who lent a room to a stranger who paid cash. That's the whole story. If anyone asks, I never learned your face well enough to describe it."

"You described my face to no one," Mirabel said, "except the men who came asking, where you told them I had a scar I don't have and left on the south coach."

"A very memorable scar. Terrible luck, meeting a man like that." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were dry and level and older than her voice. "The gorge road ends at the old kilometer post. After that there's only the river path, and the river path doesn't forgive mistakes. Take the left fork at the split boulder even though the right looks better. The right goes into the wash and the wash goes nowhere."

"How do you know the river paths?"

"I grew up here before it was interesting to anyone with a stamp and a seal." She wiped her hands on her apron, once, twice, as if cleaning off more than flour. "Everyone upstream knows things the land office would prefer forgotten. That's rather the point of us."

He wanted to say something true—to thank her properly, or warn her that helping him might cost her the inn, the town, more—but every sentence he assembled sounded like a debt being recorded, and he had spent six years learning that debts were how men like Aldous Vane held power over other men. So instead he said: "If I'm not back in three weeks—"

"Then I'll know you found what you were looking for," Sera said, "and I'll decide then whether to be sorry." She picked up the pot and carried it out of the room, and that was the end of the conversation, because Sera Oduya did not believe in endings that invited argument.

* * *

He left Kellwater before dawn, walking the dead line north with his pack on his shoulders and the survey team's compass in his breast pocket, where it lay against his heartbeat like a second, more honest organ.

The compass had troubled him since the tunnel. It was a government-issue instrument, brass-cased, the kind issued by the dozen to crews across the mountain districts—and its needle did not point north. Or rather, it pointed north the way a drunk points at his house: generally, with a persistent lean toward something else. He had tested it against his father's compass on the flat rock above the sealed bore, rotating both instruments through their arcs, and his father's needle swung true and steady while the government compass dragged, hesitated, and settled again and again on the same bearing: upriver. Northeast by east, into the gorge, toward the source valley that appeared on no map he'd been permitted to see and appeared on every map the valley people kept in their heads.

Local iron, he'd told himself at first. A seam of magnetite in the gorge walls. Surveyors' compasses lied all the time; that was why engineers learned to distrust any single instrument.

But he had checked the geology maps. There was no magnetite in the gorge.

Which meant someone had weighted the needle—or the bearing itself was the message, left by a crew that knew their instruments would be searched and their papers confiscated, and so hid their finding in the one place no clerk would think to look: inside the tool they were required to carry.

*Whoever you were,* Mirabel thought, walking the rotted ties in the gray light, *you were engineers. You thought like engineers.* And engineers, when they suspected they were about to be erased, did not write memos. They left load paths. They left redundancy. They left the truth distributed through the structure so that no single failure could destroy it.

A torn journal. A weighted compass. A camp arranged too neatly, boot prints arriving and none departing. He turned the facts over as he walked, and each time he arrived at the same unbearable arithmetic: five men had come up this line carrying instruments, and somewhere ahead of him the instruments remained, and the men did not.

By midmorning he reached the split boulder. The right-hand path was wider, drier, graded—almost certainly cut by the same crews that had built the dead rail line, a service track for maintenance gangs. The left was a goat trail hugging the water, slick with spray, half-swallowed by fern.

He took the left, because Sera had told him to, and because everything in him that still trusted official infrastructure had died somewhere between a falsified grant and a sketch of his own face in a stranger's hand.

* * *

The gorge narrowed through the afternoon until the walls pressed close enough that he could have shouted across and been heard at a conversational volume. The river ran fast and green below him, channeled between granite ribs, and the sound of it filled the passage so completely that his own footsteps vanished inside it. He was aware, walking, of a strange acoustic solitude—the roar erasing him, making him a ghost even to himself.

He stopped at dusk on a shelf of rock above a bend and ate half the bread and drank the tea, which was excellent, and let himself feel, for exactly four minutes by his watch, the thing he had not allowed himself to feel in Kellwater.

It was terror. He named it honestly, because his father had taught him that an engineer who misdiagnosed a load would build a bridge that failed, and a man who misdiagnosed his own fear would make decisions that failed the same way. He was afraid. Properly afraid, down at the foundation level. He had burned his only legal identity. He was following a compass bearing planted by missing men into a gorge that the government had closed to survey, carrying evidence that had already gotten five people disappeared, toward a valley whose flooding had been certified by the one man whose approval had once meant more to him than his own name.

And beneath the terror, shamefully, undeniably: liberation.

Six years of borrowed names. Six years of small contracts in small districts, of introducing himself with a stranger's consonants, of flinching whenever a document needed signing, of practicing invisibility the way other men practiced golf. He had told himself it was safety. Walking the river path with the dark coming down and the water roaring below, he understood at last that it had also been a kind of death—one administered slowly, by his own hand, in installments. The man with two names had gradually become a man with no name at all, because a name you never use is a name you lose.

Tomorrow he would cross the closed boundary. Tomorrow he would be, officially, a trespasser in a district that erased trespassers. But tonight, on this shelf of rock, eating stolen kindness in the form of bread, he was—for the first time since the disgrace—moving toward something instead of merely away from it.

He spread his coat on the stone and slept badly, the way men sleep when they have chosen motion over hiding and both options are terrible.

* * *

On the second day the path ended.

Not gradually, the way trails usually die—thinning, wandering, giving up. It ended the way a sentence ends when the writer is dragged away from the desk: mid-stride, at a place where the rock had been cut back and steps had been chiseled and then, abruptly, no more steps. Beyond the last chiseled foothold the river path became a scramble—wet slabs angled at thirty degrees, handholds worn smooth by water, the green torrent close enough to reach out and touch.

Mirabel stood at the break and looked back the way he had come.

That was the mistake. From this angle, compressed by the gorge's geometry, he could see the path behind him for nearly a kilometer—a thin brown thread stitched along the wall—and he could see, with an engineer's involuntary precision, that the thread was not passable in reverse at any speed. Two of the slabs he had crossed that morning had been traversable only because he'd gone slowly, testing each hold. Going back down them in rain, or in haste, or pursued—

There is no path back down.

The thought arrived without drama, the way the worst thoughts do. He had known it abstractly when he burned the papers. Now he knew it concretely, in load-bearing terms: he had committed himself past the point of reversal, and the structure would carry him forward or it would drop him, and either way the decision was no longer available for revision.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a kind of detached professional interest, the way he'd once watched a needle deflect on a strain gauge, and then he made himself do the thing his father had drilled into him across a hundred childhood summers on half-built bridges: assess, don't agonize. The slab ahead. The hold above it. The next move. Nothing else exists except the next move.

He climbed.

The gorge deepened through the day. Twice the path crossed the river on fallen trunks furred with moss, and twice he crossed them on all fours, feeling the wood flex under his weight with a springiness that his hindbrain translated, unhelpfully, into figures—span, deflection, factor of safety, all of them inadequate. Once he passed a cairn of stacked stones with a strip of red cloth tied to the topmost rock, faded almost to pink, and he stood before it longer than he should have. Someone had marked this route. Someone had wanted people to find the way up. The land office had closed the gorge; the valley people had signed it, quietly, in cloth and stone.

Toward evening the walls opened slightly and he came upon the second camp.

He knew it was theirs before he saw the instruments. The same terrible neatness. A tent frame stripped of canvas but standing. A fire ring swept clean. And laid out on a flat rock, aligned with a care that hurt to look at: a theodolite under an oilcloth, a chain box, a second compass identical to the one against his heart.

He knelt and lifted the oilcloth. The theodolite was in perfect order. Beneath it, folded into quarters, was a page of graph paper covered in a surveyor's hand—elevations, chainages, a running profile of the river—and at the bottom of the page, in the same hand but pressed hard, written fast:

*They are coming back for us. If you read this, the bearing is real. Follow it. Tell the valley what the stone says.*

The stone. Reyes's journal had mentioned a boundary stone—no. No, the journal's final pages had been torn out. He knew about the stone from nothing. And yet the words landed in him like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was there, and somewhere in the archive of his mind a door he'd been leaning against for days swung open: the grant in the district records, Vane's signature, the flood scheme for the source valley—and grants depended on boundaries, and boundaries depended on stones, and a stone that said the wrong thing would undo everything.

*Tell the valley what the stone says.*

He sat back on his heels. The river roared. Somewhere above him, past the head of the gorge, the source valley waited with its forbidden water and its erased people, and somewhere behind him, moving faster because they traveled the serviced roads he had refused, the men from the land office were climbing into the mountains after a stray surveyor with a memorable scar that did not exist.

Mirabel folded the page into his breast pocket beside the compass, repacked the theodolite exactly as he'd found it—he could not have said why, except that the neatness deserved to be answered with neatness—and stood.

The last light was leaving the gorge. Ahead, the path climbed out of the granite toward a saddle he could just distinguish against the sky, and beyond the saddle, faint as breath on glass, a smell he had not encountered in six years of hiding: woodsmoke. Not one fire. Many.

Someone lived up there. Someone had always lived up there, on maps that showed nothing.

He tightened the pack straps, put his thumb against the compass case, and began to climb out of the gorge toward the smoke—while far below him, at the mouth of the trail, small and certain in the failing light, a lantern came around the bend and started up the river path after him.
