## 9. The Drowned Tunnel

The river turned against him at dawn.

Mirabel had been walking the gorge for two days, following the dead team's bearing, and for two days the water had been his ally—loud enough to cover his passage, cold enough to keep him alert, steady enough to trust. Engineers trusted water the way other men trusted God, and Mirabel had spent enough of his life reading rivers to know this one's moods. It had been running high but honest, brown with snowmelt, its voice a constant argument he could ignore.

Then the argument changed.

He heard it before he saw it—a new note in the river's voice, deeper, like a door swinging somewhere underground. He stopped on the ledge, pack straps cutting his shoulders, and looked upstream to where the gorge narrowed to a throat of black granite.

The bore was there. He'd known it would be—the survey notes from the tunnel had mentioned a second adit, an exploratory bore driven into the gorge wall decades ago and abandoned when the rock proved rotten. The journal's compass bearing ran straight through that stretch of gorge. If the dead team had gone anywhere, they'd gone past the bore.

But the river had found something too.

Water was pouring out of the adit mouth in a solid green rope, thick as his waist, arcing out over the gorge and joining the main current twenty feet below. The bore wasn't abandoned anymore. The spring flood had backed up somewhere inside the mountain, found the old workings, and was using the tunnel as a second throat.

Mirabel stood on the ledge and made the calculation he'd been avoiding since Kellwater.

The bearing pointed through. The gorge wall above the bore was sheer for two hundred feet—no climbing it with his gear. Below, the river ran white through a slot no wider than a cart track. The only way forward was through the tunnel. The only way through the tunnel was now a question of timing, because the river inside it would rise and fall with the snowmelt cycle, and he'd been in the mountains long enough to read that cycle the way other men read clocks.

It would drop at midday. It always dropped at midday, for an hour, maybe two, before the afternoon melt sent it climbing again. He'd have one window.

He ate cold rice and waited, watching the water rope out of the adit and marking its height against a quartz vein in the rock. At eleven it began to fall. By noon the rope had thinned to a rope's shadow—a broad, rushing sheet that no longer arced but hugged the tunnel floor, shin-deep where it spilled over the lip.

Mirabel tied his survey line to a boulder, checked the coil, and stepped into the mountain.

* * *

The dark inside was total. He ran his hand along the bore's left wall—old drill scars under his palm, then newer ones, and his fingers stopped.

Fresh tool marks. The same ones he'd found in the sealed tunnel outside Kellwater, the same bite of a recent pick, but here they were everywhere, a whole corridor of them, and among them he found what he'd found nowhere else: boot scuffs at knee height, drag marks, the smear of someone bracing a heavy load against the wall.

Someone had come through here recently. Someone with equipment. Someone moving fast, or moving hurt.

The water was shin-deep and viciously cold, pulling at his boots, and the bore sloped gently downward, which meant the water was accelerating, which meant somewhere ahead it was either getting shallower or the floor was dropping away. He kept his hand on the line, kept counting his paces, kept the compass open in his mind's eye. Two hundred paces. Three hundred. The dark pressed on his eyes until he saw patterns in it, ghosts of drill-light, and he told himself the tightness in his chest was the cold water and nothing else.

At four hundred paces the bore turned, and the water deepened to his knees, and he heard the sound he'd been dreading: not the river's argument anymore but its roar, close and huge, somewhere ahead in the black.

And under the roar—barely—a human voice.

He stopped so fast the current staggered him. He stood in the flooded bore with the mountain's pulse in his ears and listened until his legs ached, and there it was again. A voice. Thin, cracked, coming from above and to the right.

"Hello?" His own voice sounded absurd, flattened by the roar.

The voice stopped. Then, after a long moment, it came again—louder, desperate, a word he couldn't make out, and then a name, or what might have been a name, and then just a raw sustained cry that needed no translation.

Mirabel unslung his pack, found the carbide lamp, and lit it. The flame steadied. He held it high.

Twenty feet up the bore wall, where a side drift branched off at an angle, a face looked down at him out of the lamplight. Young. Hollowed. Filmed with a beard that hadn't been chosen. Two eyes so wide with disbelief that for a moment neither of them moved.

"Don't—don't go, don't go, please—" The voice broke apart on the rocks of its own urgency. "I can't—I've been calling for—how long has it been, do you know how long—"

"I'm coming up," Mirabel said. "Stay where you are."

"I'm not going anywhere," the man said, and laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound Mirabel had heard in months. "Believe me. I'm not going anywhere."

* * *

The side drift climbed at a shallow angle, dry above the flood line, and Mirabel hauled himself up it with the lamp held in his teeth, and at the top of it he found the man.

He was wedged into a niche where the drift narrowed, one leg stretched out in front of him at an angle legs didn't go. He'd rigged a tarp from drift timber to shed the drip line, and around him, arranged with a neatness that Mirabel recognized and dreaded, were the tools of a government survey team: a theodolite, a ranging pole, a canvas instrument case stenciled with the crown-and-river mark of the district land office.

"You're one of them," Mirabel said. "The survey crew."

The man's eyes went from Mirabel's face to the lamp to the darkness behind him, checking, always checking, the way a man checks a door. "Tomas Reyes," he said. "Junior engineer. Third district survey, source valley assignment." A breath. "You're not land office."

"No."

"Then who?"

"Nobody," Mirabel said. "A contract surveyor. I found your camp upstream. Two days back down the gorge."

Something moved through the young man's face—hope and horror fighting for the same ground. "You found the camp." Not a question. "Was it—was everything still there? The instruments?"

"Everything. Laid out neat. Stove cold. No tracks out."

Reyes closed his eyes. "Neat," he said. "Yes. He was like that. Chief Amado was like that." He opened his eyes. "The leg went on the fourth day. I was crossing a scree slope inside the main gallery and the whole face of it let go. Chief Amado splinted it. He carried me two days, and then the flood came up the bore and we couldn't go down anymore, and he—" Reyes's voice snagged. "He went to find a way out. That was nine days ago. Or ten. Or twelve. I stopped counting because it was making me worse."

Mirabel crouched and looked at the leg. The splint was good work—lashed drift timber, proper padding, the kind of splint a man builds when he intends the patient to survive transport. The flesh around it was swollen and hot-looking even in the lamplight, and there was a smell underneath the wet rock smell that Mirabel didn't like at all.

"When did you last eat?"

"There's a ration tin. Chief Amado left me most of it." Reyes's mouth twisted. "I've been making it last. That's the problem with being an engineer. You can't stop calculating. I know exactly how many days I have left and I hate that I know."

Mirabel got his own food out—rice, dried fish, the last of the hard cheese—and put it in the younger man's hands without ceremony. Reyes ate the way drowning men breathe, and Mirabel looked away to give him the privacy of it, and in looking away he saw, wedged in the crack of the drift wall, a leather journal cover. Empty. The pages gone.

"You're the one who hid the journal," Mirabel said. "In the sealed tunnel. Outside Kellwater."

Reyes stopped chewing. For a long moment he just looked at Mirabel, and Mirabel watched him do the arithmetic—stranger, upstream, knows about the camp, knows about the journal—and arrive at the only answer there was.

"You read it."

"Some of it. The last pages were torn out."

"Chief Amado tore them out." Reyes set down the tin. His hands were shaking, but his voice, for the first time, had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man reading from a document. "He said if we were separated, the pages had to be somewhere the land office would never look, because the land office already knew exactly where we'd been. He hid them and he hid the location in his head and he—" Reyes stopped. Started again. "Mister. Whoever you are. I need to tell you something and I need you to not interrupt me, because I've been rehearsing it for twelve days and if I stop I don't know if I can start again."

"Go on."

Reyes leaned forward as far as the splinted leg allowed, and the lamplight moved on his face, and when he spoke again it was barely above the sound of the water below.

"They didn't lose us," he said. "Nobody lost us. We were taken. All of us but me. The escort—the men assigned to protect the survey—they were land office security, and on the night after we found the boundary stone, they woke us up and they took the chief and the others up the gorge in the dark, and I only escaped because I fell down a scree slope and they decided I was dead." His breath shuddered. "They decided I was dead and they left me, and I crawled into this bore because it was the only dark place, and I've been here ever since. They weren't bandits, mister. They had documents. They had a wagon. They knew our names."

The water roared below them. Mirabel found that his hands were very still in his lap, stiller than he'd ever felt them, the stillness of a man standing on ground that has just shifted.

"The boundary stone," Mirabel said. "What boundary stone?"

Reyes looked at him for a long moment. "You haven't read the whole journal."

"The last pages are gone."

"Then you don't know." Something kindled in the younger man's face—not hope exactly, but the ember under hope, the thing that survives being buried. "Then you came all this way on half a story. On faith, or stupidity, or—" He laughed again, softer this time. "The stone's backdated. The grant. The whole grant is backdated, mister, the boundary stone was cut with a tool that wasn't invented until after the date carved on it, and Chief Amado proved it, he proved it with measurements, with weathering rates, with everything, and he wrote it all down, and that's why they took him. That's why they'll take anyone." His voice dropped further. "And the flood scheme—the one the grant makes legal—it drowns this whole gorge and the valley above it. Every farm. Every shrine. Everyone."

Mirabel sat in the dark of the drowned tunnel with a stranger's broken leg between them and the river's roar underneath, and he thought of a signature he'd found in the district archive, a signature he knew better than his own, and he said nothing at all.

"You came upstream," Reyes said quietly. "Alone. On half a story." He studied Mirabel's face with the frank, exhausted clarity of a man who has nothing left to lose by looking. "Why?"

Mirabel took a long breath. "Because a page was missing," he said. "And I couldn't stand it."

Reyes nodded slowly, as if that were the most reasonable thing he'd ever heard, and maybe it was. "Chief Amado was the same way," he said. "He used to say a survey isn't finished until the numbers close. You can't leave a line open. You can't leave a page torn out." He gripped Mirabel's wrist, sudden and hard, his fingers cold as the water. "The numbers haven't closed, mister. The chief is up the gorge somewhere, or he's dead, and the pages are hidden, and the monsoon is coming, and if it comes before anyone sees what's in those pages, then everything he did was for nothing and the valley drowns on schedule." His grip tightened. "I memorized the cache coordinates. All of them. He made me memorize them the night before they took him—said a junior engineer should be able to carry the survey in his head if the instruments were lost. I can give you the bearing. I can give you the pace count. I can give you the elevation."

Mirabel looked at the splinted leg, at the swollen flesh, at the twelve days of hunger in the young man's face, and he understood what was being asked, and what it would cost, and that there was no version of the next eleven days that didn't include both of them.

"First," he said, "we get you out of this tunnel."

* * *

They came down out of the bore at dusk, when the flood line dropped to its lowest, Mirabel walking backward down the flooded drift with Reyes's good arm over his shoulders and the man's splinted leg held clear of the water, both of them deafened by the roar, both of them half-blind in the dark, moving at the speed of the slower man's pain. It took four hours to cover what had taken Mirabel twenty minutes alone. Twice the water rose around their knees and they had to stop and cling to the drill-scarred wall and wait for it to fall. Once Reyes fainted, and Mirabel held him against the rock and counted his own heartbeats until the younger man's eyes opened again.

They came out of the adit mouth into the last grey light, and Mirabel made camp in a hollow above the gorge with the river running loud below, and built a fire small enough to hide, and fed Reyes, and re-splinted the leg with proper bandaging from his own kit, and watched the fever move in the younger man's face like weather.

"The land office men," Reyes said, from the edge of sleep. "If they find me—"

"They won't."

"They left me because they thought I was dead." His eyes opened, fever-bright. "They'll correct the mistake if they find out they didn't."

"I know," Mirabel said.

"You can't carry me and move fast. You have to know that. The cache is six days up the gorge at my pace, which means nine at yours, and the monsoon—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "The chief made me memorize the storm dates too. Front makes landfall in eleven days. Maybe twelve."

Mirabel fed the small fire and did the arithmetic he'd been doing all day, the arithmetic that kept coming out wrong no matter how he arranged it. One man. One broken man. Six days of gorge. Eleven days of sky.

"Then we split the difference," he said. "We build a litter tomorrow. I carry you, and we move at the pace the leg allows, and we get there with two days to spare."

"And if we don't?"

Mirabel looked up the gorge, where the dark had closed over the river's source like a held breath.

"Then we don't," he said. "But the numbers close, Reyes. One way or another, they close."

The younger man watched him for a moment, and then, for the first time since the lamplight had found him, he slept—really slept, deep and unguarded, the sleep of a man who has finally handed his weight to someone else.

Mirabel sat up alone with the fire and the river and the compass in his hand, its needle steady on the bearing that ran up the gorge toward the torn pages and the forged stone and the valley that would drown unless the numbers closed in time.

Somewhere behind him, down the dark gorge, he thought he heard—though it might have been the water, it might easily have been the water—a sound like boots on rock.

He banked the fire and did not sleep.
