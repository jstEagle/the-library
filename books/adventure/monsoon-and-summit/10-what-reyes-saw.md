## 10. What Reyes Saw

The fever broke sometime in the night, and Tomas Reyes woke to the sound of water.

Not the roar of it — he'd had enough of that, three days of it hammering through the flooded bore while his leg screamed and the dark pressed in like a hand over his mouth. This was quieter. A drip, steady as a metronome, somewhere off to his left. And beneath it, breathing. Someone was sitting near him in the dark, and that someone was alive, and for a long stupid moment Reyes couldn't remember whether that was good news or bad.

Then he saw the shape of the man against the gray seam of the tunnel mouth. Broad shoulders hunched forward, elbows on knees, a compass turning slowly in his fingers like something being prayed with. Not one of the escort men. The escort men wore their cruelty like uniform buttons, polished and visible. This one sat like a man carrying weight he hadn't asked for.

"You're the stray surveyor," Reyes said. His voice came out ruined, scraped thin. "The one they're hunting."

The man didn't flinch at the word *hunting*. That told Reyes something too. "Mirabel," he said. Just that. No surname offered, no hand extended. "Ishikawa" was a name Reyes would learn later, much later, when names stopped being dangerous things to say out loud.

"Reyes. Tomas Reyes. Junior engineer, government survey team, third district." He recited it the way you recite anything when you've been alone too long — to prove to yourself the words still fit you. Then, because the dark made honest men of them both: "The rest of them are dead. Or worse than dead. I don't actually know which."

* * *

He told it in order, because disorder was all he'd had for eleven days and he was sick of it.

They'd gone upriver in the last week of the dry season, six of them plus an escort of four land office men who were supposed to be protection and had been, from the first morning, something else entirely. Crew chief was a woman named Adaeze Okonkwo — twenty-two years in the service, surveyor's hands, the kind of chief who checked your stadia readings twice not because she doubted you but because she believed the mountain deserved accuracy. Reyes had been her junior for fourteen months. She'd taught him how to read ground. How the river told you where it had been if you knew how to listen to gravel.

The commission was routine on its face. Boundary verification for a land grant — the Meridian Grant, the papers called it, though nobody in the crew used the name without a certain flatness. They were to confirm the boundary markers along the upper watershed and certify the grant's effective date. Certification meant signatures. Signatures meant the grant became real, became history, became the kind of document that outlives everyone who signed it.

Okonkwo had read the commission twice on the first night and then read it a third time slowly, and Reyes had watched her face do something complicated.

"The effective date's wrong," she'd said finally, not to anyone, to the fire. "This valley was surveyed before. I surveyed it. Eight years ago, provisional line, never ratified." She tapped the paper. "This says the boundary was established six years ago. But these marker descriptions—" she'd looked up, and her eyes had that lit quality they got when the numbers agreed with each other, which was her version of joy— "these descriptions match my provisional line exactly. Word for word. Coordinates I took eight years ago, dated six years ago."

Reyes hadn't understood yet. He'd been twenty-six and eager and mostly interested in whether the escort's cook made more rations than theirs. He understood later, lying in the drowned dark with his leg broken in two places, exactly what she'd seen: someone had taken her old work and backdated it. Built a forgery out of real survey data the way you build a counterfeit out of genuine plates.

* * *

The ruins came next, and the ruins were what killed them.

Four days up from the last marker, above the gorge where the river narrowed to something you could shout across, Okonkwo found walls. Cut stone, fitted without mortar, older than any architecture the district archives admitted to. Terraces climbing the valley flank in long green steps, grown over but unmistakably engineered — drainage channels, foundation lines, a plaza half-swallowed by hillside. Pre-flood settlement. Generations of it. A people who had built their lives around this river's moods and left their signature in stone.

And among the ruins, half-toppled, a boundary stone.

That was the thing Reyes kept coming back to, the detail he'd turn over in the dark until his hands shook. The stone was old. Lichen had gotten into the lettering; moss had claimed its shaded face; whatever marks had been cut into it originally had weathered past reading. But on its land-office side, fresh chisel work. New characters cut over old ones, shallow and recent, re-cutting the boundary to match the grant's claimed line. Someone had stood at that stone with a chisel within the last few years and moved a border that had stood longer than the district itself.

Okonkwo photographed everything. She measured everything. She made Reyes take independent readings so the numbers would exist twice, in two notebooks, in two handwritings. "Paper burns," she said. "Numbers survive if you put them in enough heads." She made him memorize coordinates. He'd complained about it at the time — rote drill, every evening, the whole team reciting bearings around the fire like schoolchildren — and now those drills were the only reason anything survived at all.

It was the escort commander, a soft-voiced man named Herrick, who finally asked to see her field journal. Politely. Over tea. On the ninth day.

She refused. Cited survey regulations, chain of custody, the requirement that raw field notes go to the archive sealed. Herrick smiled and said he understood completely, and the next morning there were six additional men in camp wearing plain clothes and carrying government rifles, and Okonkwo was under arrest for tampering with official boundary markers — the very markers, Reyes understood with a nausea that hasn't left him, that her own evidence proved had been tampered with by someone else.

"They didn't even search us properly," Reyes said, and heard his own voice crack on it. "That's what I can't — they didn't need to. Herrick knew what we'd found because he'd been sent to make sure we found it and then to make sure it went nowhere. We weren't a survey team. We were a confirmation crew. They needed certified eyes to walk the line so the grant could be defended later, and when Okonkwo read the ground instead of the paperwork—" He stopped. The drip filled the silence, patient as ever.

"They took her downstream. Took Osei and Lindqvist too, the ones who'd backed her readings. Me and Marchetti they left, because Marchetti had already agreed to sign whatever they put in front of him, and me—" Here was the part that lived in his chest like a splinter, the part he'd been circling for eleven days. "Me because I ran. Herrick gave the order to bring me along and I went into the gorge instead. Into the flood channel. Leg went in a rockfall. They assumed the river finished the job."

His voice did something ugly. "It should have. If I'd stayed, maybe — I don't know what staying does, they'd have just taken three of us instead of two. But I ran, and Marchetti signed, and somewhere downstream there's a certification with my chief's survey data in it and my silence wrapped around it like a bandage."

Mirabel said nothing for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was low and even, the voice of a man reading instruments. "The journal. Her field notes. Where are they?"

"Gone. Confiscated with everything else." Reyes turned his head against the pack roll Mirabel had wedged under it. "But the pages — the important ones, the stone, the ruins, the backdating analysis — she tore them out herself. Night before the arrest. She could feel it coming, the way you feel weather. She tore them out and she cached them."

"Where?"

"She wouldn't tell me. Said it out loud to no one, ever. Said loose mouths drown valleys." Reyes laughed, and it hurt, and he laughed anyway, a short broken sound. "But she drilled us. Every night, coordinates, until we could recite them asleep. She said a cache is only lost if one head holds it. So she split it — gave each of us one bearing set, told us to hold it private, told us the sets only mean something together. Marchetti has one. God knows what Marchetti's doing with it, probably trading it for a desk job. And I have mine."

He closed his eyes. Reciting them was easy; his body had kept the drill going through fever and pain and drowning fear, some stubborn clerk in his skull refusing to close the ledger. He gave them to Mirabel slowly, twice, watching the man's lips move through the second pass.

"Above the gorge," Reyes said. "Shrine on the ridge line, old one, pre-dating the district by centuries. The bearing sets triangulate to it. She put the pages where her own ancestors used to put things worth keeping." His mouth twisted. "She's valley-born, you know. Okonkwo. Nobody in the land office ever bothered to learn that. It's why they picked her to certify — thought blood would make her loyal to the paper. Instead it made her loyal to the stone."

* * *

The drip kept its time. Somewhere above them, daylight was moving across the gorge mouth in its slow arc, and Reyes found himself thinking about Okonkwo's hands, the way she'd held her notebook open flat in wind like it was a living thing that might startle.

"I keep doing the arithmetic," he said quietly. "Eleven days. What Herrick's done with her in eleven days. Whether a confession got written for her. Whether she—" He stopped again. He was stopping a lot. "You should know what you're carrying me into. Those pages aren't just evidence. They're the only copy of her findings in the world, and the men who erased her team will burn the whole gorge down before they let them reach daylight. You take me up that bearing, you're not rescuing a witness. You're carrying a fuse."

Mirabel stood. In the gray light from the bore's mouth his face was hard to read — a man whose profession was reading ground, giving nothing away about the ground he himself stood on.

"Then we move fast," was all he said.

But Reyes watched him check the compass afterward, watched his thumb find the bearing Reyes had given and hold there, and thought: *he already knows*. Whatever brought this stranger upriver into a drowned tunnel after a dead man's trail, he'd already chosen. Reyes had run from Herrick's men and broken on the rocks for it. This one was walking toward them with his eyes open.

Outside, far downstream, faint as a memory, Reyes heard it — or thought he heard it, fever still living somewhere in his ears. Engines. More than one. Moving up the valley road toward the gorge.

The monsoon was eleven days out. The land office was closer than that.

"Herrick travels fast when he smells paper," Reyes whispered. "We should go tonight."
