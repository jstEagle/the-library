## 11. The Monsoon Forecast

Eleven days. That was the number I kept arriving at, and the number kept arriving at me.

I did the arithmetic the first night on the litter, by candle stub, in the back of a shrine Reyes insisted was safe because it was dedicated to a god of small mercies. I did it again the second night under an overhang, with rain starting to spit against the stone. I did it a third time on the fourth morning, lying flat on my stomach in the mud beside a sleeping Reyes, because I wanted the number to change and it wouldn't.

Eleven days until the monsoon front made landfall, if the pressure signs I'd learned to read in thirty years of building rail through monsoon country meant anything. And we needed nineteen to reach the valley at the pace a broken-legged man could travel on a litter dragged by one exhausted engineer.

The gap was eight days. Eight days of arithmetic that would not be argued with.

"You're doing the sums again," Reyes said. He didn't open his eyes. His voice had recovered some of its shape since the tunnel, but it was still the voice of a man talking around pain, each word set down carefully, like a stone on a cairn.

"I'm doing the sums."

"Don't. They don't get better with repetition."

"Everything gets better with repetition. That's what repetition is for."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then the leg shifted under the canvas I'd lashed to two cut poles, and the almost became a hiss through his teeth, and I went back to checking the splint, and the morning went on, and the number stayed eleven.

* * *

The litter was my own design, which is to say it was a compromise, which is to say it was a failure I could live with. Two green alder poles, lashed with rawhide I'd soaked and stretched, a canvas bed scavenged from the survey camp's abandoned stores, a drag harness that ran across my chest and shoulders. I'd built trestles that carried two hundred tons of locomotive over gorges. This thing carried one hundred and forty pounds of junior engineer and every hour it cost me something I couldn't name precisely but could feel depleting, the way a battery depletes, evenly, invisibly, until the light goes out.

Reyes, to his credit, understood what he cost. He'd stopped apologizing on the second day, which was a mercy, because the apologies had been worse than the weight. Instead he'd started navigating. He'd been the team's junior surveyor; he knew the gorge country from the upstream side, from maps drawn before the maps were withdrawn. He called the route from the litter like a man calling a train through fog.

"Past the shrine there's a goat track on the north bank. It looks like nothing. It's everything. The south bank floods first."

"Your chief found that?"

"The chief found everything. That was the problem." He said it without bitterness, which was its own kind of bitterness.

I hauled. The harness bit into my shoulders. The river ran beside us, high and brown and getting higher, and every hour it made a sound a little more like a crowd, a little less like water.

* * *

On the fifth day I climbed above the gorge to read the sky.

This was a thing Vane had taught me, back when teaching me was the center of his life and mine — no, that's sentiment. Back when I was useful to him and believed I was loved, which in the arithmetic of Aldous Vane amounted to the same thing. He'd stand on the highest cut of a new line with the wind flattening his coat and read the clouds the way other men read ledgers. *The monsoon isn't weather, Mirabel. It's a schedule. The mountains owe the sea a debt every summer, and the sea always collects. Your job is to know the collection date.*

I stood on the ridge with the wind flattening my coat and I read it.

The cloud deck had come down a thousand feet in two days. It was no longer sitting on the far ranges like a lid; it was pouring over them, spilling eastward in long grey tongues, and beneath it the air had gone soft and heavy in the way that isn't heat but weight — the weight of water waiting for its excuse. The pressure, such as I could feel it in my ears and my joints and the ache of an old break in my left wrist, was falling the way a lift falls when the cable is cut.

Eleven days. Maybe ten. The front would make landfall on the coast, take three days to climb to the high country, and then the river we were walking beside would become a different animal entirely.

I stood there longer than I needed to, because there was a second calculation to do and I kept hoping the first would distract me from it. It didn't.

Tracks. I'd found them the day before, where the goat track crossed a seep of wet clay: boot prints, government issue, two men, moving fast, and moving *upriver*. The same boots I'd measured outside the abandoned camp a lifetime ago — I'd taken the habit of measuring boot prints the way other men check their watch, a leftover from the years when knowing who had walked a line before you was the difference between a survey and a grave. Size eleven and a half. Stride long. They'd passed this point perhaps thirty hours ahead of us.

Two men on fast legs gain on a man dragging a litter at four or five miles a day. The mathematics of pursuit are simpler and crueler than the mathematics of weather. They didn't need eleven days. They needed four.

Unless they didn't know we were ahead of them. Unless the rockslide at the shrine had buried more than Reyes's freedom. Unless they'd turned back. I ran those *unlesses* like a gambler runs a losing hand, and the hand didn't improve with looking.

I climbed down to the litter and told him the truth, because he'd earned the truth in a flooded tunnel and I wasn't going to cheapen it now.

"Two of them. Behind us. A day and a half and closing."

Reyes looked up at the spilling clouds, then at the brown water, then at me. He'd gone very still, the way he'd gone still in the tunnel when I first called down to him — the stillness of a man deciding how much fear a situation can hold before it stops being useful.

"Then we don't go by the track," he said.

"It's the only track."

"It's the only track *for a litter*." He was already working at the lashing on his splint, and I caught his wrist — gently, but I caught it.

"Say the rest of it."

"The river narrows at the Narrows — obviously — a kilometer of gorge where the banks go vertical. The old line crossed it on a trestle. The trestle's gone; I saw the piers from the survey. But the *abutments* are still there, both sides, and there's a winch house on the north abutment. Construction era. They'd have had a cableway for hauling stone." His eyes had the light I remembered from the tunnel, the light of a man whose mind worked even when his body had resigned. "If the drum's still in the winch house, and if there's any cable left on it, we cross the river in the air. And a cableway doesn't leave boot prints."

"And if the drum's not there?"

"Then we're standing on an abutment when the land office men arrive, and we settle it with the truth about who can climb better with a broken leg." He looked down at the leg in question. "I'd give it to them."

* * *

The winch house was still there, and the drum was still in it, and the cable — this is the part I still don't believe, and I was the one who pulled it — the cable was still on it.

Forty meters of galvanized wire, coiled and rusted but whole, and a drum that turned when I put my shoulder to the crank with a scream of oxide that must have carried down the whole gorge. The builders of the old line had built things to last. That was the religion we'd all belonged to, once: build it to last, build it honest, build it so that a man forty years downstream could stand on your work and trust his life to it. I thought about Vane's signature on that land grant, sitting in the same archive as the drawings of this trestle, and my hands shook on the crank, and I told myself it was the effort.

Getting Reyes across took the rest of the day and most of what I had. I rigged a bosun's chair from the canvas and the rawhide, ran the cable across the gorge — I threw a light line first, weighted with a wrench from the winch house, four throws before it caught on the far side, and I don't know what I'd have done on the fifth — and then I hauled him across hand over hand on the fall, watching him swing out over the brown water with his broken leg sticking out of the chair like a flag of surrender.

Halfway across, he looked up at me and shouted over the river: "You know what the chief would say?"

"Tell me."

"He'd say the numbers don't care about your feelings. But he'd say it *smiling*." And then he was across, and I was alone on the north abutment with a cable to recover, and I stood there one extra second looking at the water, because the numbers didn't care about my feelings, and the numbers said eight days short, and somewhere behind me two men in government boots were finding the goat track and the seep of clay and the boot prints of a man dragging a litter, and reading them like a ledger.

I recovered the cable, cut it, and dropped the drum into the gorge. Let them find the winch house empty. Let them stand on the abutment and do their own arithmetic.

* * *

That night, in a hollow above the river with Reyes asleep and the rain beginning in earnest — not the monsoon yet, only its advance scouts, testing the ground — I laid it all out on the flat of my mind like instruments on a survey table.

Eleven days to landfall. Nineteen days of travel at litter pace. Eight days short. Two men behind us, four days out and closing, unless the gorge slowed them, unless the rain slowed them, unless they turned back — three *unlesses*, and I'd already spent that hand.

Against it: the valley elders, if they'd take us in. Nadya Kesh, if the name Reyes remembered meant anything. A people who had been moving boundary stones for generations, which meant they knew how to make a stranger disappear into a landscape — and knew, better than any document, what was about to be done to them.

And against that: what did I carry that was worth eight days of their time? A junior engineer with a broken leg and a memorized coordinate. A compass bearing. My word, and my word was worth exactly what my name was worth, and my name was a false one in a borrowed coat.

I lay awake doing sums until the sums stopped being numbers and became a single figure, a countdown, the kind they paint on blast schedules: *ELEVEN DAYS TO DETONATION*. I'd painted them myself, on three lines, and stood under them with a plunger switch in a leather case, and waited for the signal, and the signal had always come.

This time I was on the bridge when the charge went off. That was the difference. This time the blast was the schedule, and I was inside it, and there was no one upstream with a plunger to save me.

The rain thickened. Down in the dark the river climbed a little louder.

Eight days short, I thought. Then I stopped thinking it, because Reyes had taught me something in five days on a litter that Vane never had: the numbers don't care about your feelings, but numbers are not the whole of the survey. There's always ground truth. There's always the country itself, unread, unaccounted, full of goat tracks that look like nothing and are everything.

Eight days short on paper. We'd see what the paper was worth when it met the gorge.

I slept, badly, with the compass under my hand, its needle resting on the bearing that pointed upstream, into the rain, toward the valley, toward the source — and toward whatever was coming down the river behind us, faster than the water, faster than the storm, patient as arithmetic.

In the morning, the first thing we found was a cairn on the north bank, fresh-built, no moss on its stones.

Someone knew we were coming.
