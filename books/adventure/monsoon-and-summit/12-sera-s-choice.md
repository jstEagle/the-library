## 12. Sera's Choice

The rain came to Kellwater three days before the monsoon did — a thin, apologetic drizzle that made the gutters sing and the dust lie down. Sera Oduya stood in the doorway of the inn with a broom she wasn't using, watching the water bead on the rail of the old platform across the square, and thought about the man with two names.

He'd been gone eight days now. She'd counted them the way you count anything you're afraid for: not admitting you're counting.

* * *

They came at dusk, when the light was bad and the streets were emptying. Two men from the land office, the same pair who'd shown her the sketch — the lean one, Voss, with his voice like a filing cabinet, and the heavy one, Brandt, who never spoke if he could stand there instead. They'd been back twice since then, asking their soft questions, buying their dinners, tipping too well. The town had learned their shapes the way a dog learns the shape of the man who comes to take strays.

This time they didn't ask questions in the dining room.

"Madam Oduya." Voss stood at her bar, hat in hand, courteous as a summons. "Some routine matters have arisen regarding your guest registration. We'll need you to come down to the office and clarify a few entries."

"At this hour?"

"The office keeps late hours during survey season."

She looked past him, through the window, and saw the third man. He was new — younger, standing by the district cart with its canvas cover tied down, and he was looking at her inn the way an assessor looks at stock. Not at her. At what she owned. At what could be sold or burned without troubling anyone's sleep.

"I'll close up," she said. "Give me ten minutes."

"No need," said Voss pleasantly. "Your girl can mind it. Can't she, Marta?"

Marta was sixteen and had worked the kitchen since her mother died, and she was standing in the pantry door with a ladle in her hand like a weapon she hadn't decided about. Sera caught her eye and gave the smallest shake of the head. *Don't.*

"All right," Sera said. "Let me get my shawl."

It was in the taking of the shawl that she understood it — not suspected, not feared, but *knew*, the way you know weather in your knees. The shawl hook was by the back door, and the back door was barred from outside. She saw the new bar, fresh-cut wood, bright as a scar, fitted into brackets that hadn't been there last week.

They had barred her own door against her return.

Her hands did not shake. That surprised her most. She took down the shawl, folded it over her arm like a woman going to evening service, and walked out the front with the two officials, and behind her she heard Marta begin, loudly, to scrub a pot that didn't need scrubbing.

* * *

The land office at night was worse than by day. By day it was bureaucracy; by night it was just a room with lamps and men and a table, and the table had been cleared of everything except a single folder.

"Sit, please," said Voss.

She sat. Brandt stood by the door. The third man — the assessor — had not come inside.

"You registered a guest," Voss began, "in the spring. Male, traveling alone, contract surveyor. You wrote the name 'Corben Hale' in your book."

"That's the name he gave."

"It isn't his name."

"Then I miswrote it," Sera said. "I write what people tell me. I'm an innkeeper, not a magistrate."

Voss opened the folder. Inside were papers, and she recognized her own handwriting on the top sheet — the registration page, torn neatly from her book. Torn, not copied. They had taken it.

"You fed him," Voss said. "You fed him information. The soup trade — we know about the soup trade, madam. A very charming arrangement. Fragments of story for broth. What fragments did you give him? What did he tell you?"

"He told me nothing," Sera said. "I told him where the road washed out. I told him which farmers would sell him eggs. If that's information, then half this town is guilty of it."

"Half this town," Voss repeated softly, "is exactly what concerns us."

He turned the folder around. There was a document inside, official seal, dense text, and she read enough of it to understand the shape: an order of administrative detention, extended custody pending inquiry, renewable indefinitely. Her name was already written in it. Already signed. Dated tomorrow.

"You've signed it," she said.

"We've prepared it," Voss corrected. "Signature follows cooperation. It always does." He folded his hands. "Here is what happens now, madam. You will tell us everything the surveyor said, everything he asked, everything you observed. Then you will sign a statement confirming that he acted alone, that no one in Kellwater assisted him, and that you never saw the field journal, the compass, or any documents. And then you will take a rest. A long rest. There are sanatoriums in the low country. Very quiet. Your inn will be managed in your absence — the district has an administrator who handles such things."

"The town won't allow—"

"The town," said Brandt, from the door, in the first words she'd heard him say all week, "will be told you sold the inn and moved away. People believe what's convenient."

Sera looked at the detention order. At her name in someone else's handwriting. And something happened in her chest that she would spend the rest of her life trying to describe — not fear, though fear was there, but a kind of cold clarity, like the moment on a river crossing when the plank tips and you understand that hesitation is the only thing that will drown you.

She had spent forty years being careful. Careful with accounts, careful with opinions, careful with the resentments of the land office, careful the way small people in small towns must be careful. She had told herself it was wisdom. Sitting in that lamplit room, reading her own disappearance already drafted in triplicate, she understood it had only ever been fear wearing wisdom's coat.

"All right," she said. "I'll cooperate. But I'm tired, and frightened, and I don't think well hungry. Could I have some tea first? There's a kettle in the clerk's room. I saw it this afternoon."

Voss hesitated. Courtesy was his armor and she had found the seam in it — a man like that could refuse a prisoner water, but not a lady tea.

"Brandt," he said.

Brandt went. His boots went down the hall, and the outer door opened and shut, and for perhaps ninety seconds Sera Oduya was alone in a room with a lamp, a folder, and a window.

The window was painted over on the lower panes — they always were, in government buildings, so the public couldn't watch itself being processed. But the upper pane, above the paint line, was old glass, wavy, and the latch was iron, and iron rusts in mountain towns where the rain comes sideways nine months of the year.

She poured the inkwell into the folder. It seemed important, somehow, that the paperwork drown even if she didn't escape. Then she climbed onto the table, reached up, and worked the latch with fingers that still did not shake.

The drop was eight feet onto a coal shed roof. She was fifty-three years old and had not jumped off anything since she was nine. She jumped anyway, landed hard enough to bite her tongue, rolled, slid, dropped again into the alley behind the office, and ran — not toward the inn, because they'd expect that, but toward the tannery, where the smell kept everyone away and the fence had a gap in it that every child in Kellwater knew and every adult pretended not to.

* * *

The gap in the tannery fence delivered her into the yard of Widow Anselm, who was sitting on her step in the dark shelling beans, and who looked up at the breathless, bleeding-lipped innkeeper and said, without surprise:

"Took you long enough."

"You knew?"

"Whole town knows, dear. Has since they put the bar on your door. Old Pem cut that bar — he does their carpentry — and Pem talks to his wife, and his wife talks to everyone." The widow went on shelling. "Question was whether you'd run or whether you'd sit in that room being sensible until they carried you out. Sensible's been tried in this valley before. Didn't end well."

"They'll search for me."

"They'll search the roads south and west, because that's where people flee to. Nobody flees upriver. Nobody goes into the gorge before the monsoon; it's suicide." The widow's hands paused. "Which is why, I suppose, that's where you're going."

Sera said nothing.

"The boy Marta packed you a bag. It's under my bean barrel. Bread, cheese, the good oil, a coil of rope Pem donated, and a lantern with a shuttered flame. There's a poncho that belonged to my husband, God keep him, and a packet of dried fish that tastes like punishment but keeps forever." The widow finally looked up, and her eyes in the dark were steady as stones in a wall. "My grandmother came from the source valley. Before the grant. Before all of it. Whatever that engineer is carrying upstream — whatever the dead surveyors found — it belongs to those people more than to any of us. See it gets there."

"How do you know where he went?"

"Because I feed everyone, dear, and everyone talks. He bought river rations. He asked about the gorge trail. Men don't buy river rations to stay home." She tipped her head toward the dark mass of the mountain. "There's a path behind the chapel that joins the gorge track above the flood line. Take it tonight, walk till dawn, hide by day. And Sera—" the widow caught her wrist, grip surprisingly strong, "—the officials have a third man. The quiet one with the cart. He didn't go into the office with the others. He went upriver yesterday morning, alone, ahead of them. Whatever's waiting in that gorge, it may already be between you and your engineer."

Sera stood with the bag in her hand, and the rain came harder, drumming on the widow's roof, the true monsoon arriving at last out of the black south.

"Why?" she asked suddenly. "Why is the whole town helping me? I pour soup. I keep books. I'm nobody."

Widow Anselm smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile.

"Nobody?" she said. "Child, you're the only person in Kellwater who ever wrote down what the land office owes us and made them pay it. You think we don't remember? Every family here remembers who kept honest accounts when honesty was expensive." She released her wrist. "Go. And don't die in my yard; it's bad luck for the beans."

* * *

Behind the chapel the path was a rumor of a path — a goat track widened by smugglers' feet generations ago, switchbacking up through wet bracken while the rain fell in earnest now, the real rain, the monsoon's vanguard, turning the world to a single sound. Sera climbed with the shuttered lantern unlit, feeling her way by memory and nerve, her lungs burning, her fifty-three-year-old knees informing her at length of their objections.

She let them object. She kept climbing.

Twice she stopped and looked back. Kellwater lay below in the rain, a scatter of yellow windows, smoke rising crooked from chimneys, the land office dark except for one furious lamp. Somewhere in that town were people who had lied for her, packed for her, cut fences for her, and would deny her name to any official who asked. She had lived among them eleven years and had thought herself alone in the way careful people are alone. She had been wrong about that. She had been wrong about a great many things, and the correction felt less like shame than like a door opening.

At the ridge the path joined the gorge track, and below her the river showed itself in lightning flashes — high, brown, angry, already swollen with the storm's first deliveries, roaring down out of the forbidden country where a man with two names was walking toward whatever waited at the source.

She thought of him sitting in her kitchen with his soup going cold, telling her about boot prints that arrived and never left. She thought of the sketch of his face in the official's hand, and how she had lied before she knew she was going to, the lie coming out of her smooth and instant as breath. That was the moment, she understood now. Not tonight, not the window, not the jump. The moment she lied for him, she had already chosen. Everything since was just the choice catching up with her.

"Corben Hale," she said aloud, to the rain, to the river, testing the false name and finding it thin. "Or whatever you are. You'd better be alive, because I have dried fish that tastes like punishment and I did not climb this mountain to feed it to crows."

Lightning lit the gorge from rim to rim, and in that white instant she saw, far below on the north bank, a tiny point of light moving upstream — a shuttered lantern, someone else walking the river in the dark.

And behind her, down the track she had just climbed, faint beneath the rain, the sound of hooves. Many. Moving fast.

The widow had said nobody flees upriver.

Apparently somebody was following anyway.
