## 13. The Valley That Remembers

Nadya Kesh felt them coming before she saw them—two heartbeats on the ridge trail, one stumbling, one steady. She had spent seventy-one years learning the valley's rhythms the way other people learned prayers, and the mountain had a way of announcing strangers: a stone rolled loose above the goat path, a crow lifting off the junipers in a straight alarmed line. She set down the basket of lentils she was winnowing and stood in the doorway of the long house, waiting.

They came out of the gorge mist an hour past noon. The first was a boy—no, not a boy, a man made thin by suffering, his left leg splinted with rail iron and birch bark, riding a litter of poles and canvas that the second man hauled with the harness of a draft animal. Nadya had seen men carry burdens before. She had never seen a man carry one the way this one did: not like weight, but like a debt.

They stopped at the boundary of the terraces, where the first wall of her people's oldest field rose out of the grass. The man with the litter set it down carefully, straightened, and looked up the valley. And Nadya watched his face do something she recognized from her own mirror, decades ago—the look of a person arriving at a place they had already been in their dreams.

She walked out to meet them. Not hurrying. Elders did not hurry. Hurrying was for people who believed the world might be taken from them, and Nadya had stopped believing that the day she buried her third child and planted an apricot tree on the grave.

"You are on Kesh land," she said in the valley tongue, and when he only blinked, she tried the trade speech of the lowlands, rough in her mouth after years of disuse. "Kesh land. You understand? You stand where you should not be."

"I'm sorry." His trade speech was better than hers. Lowland vowels, worn soft by years of hiding—she knew hiding when she heard it; the valley had sheltered hiders for six generations. "We needed water. My friend needs rest. We'll be gone by morning."

"Everyone is gone by morning," Nadya said. "That is what they all say. The tax collectors. The surveyors. The men with the paper." She looked at the litter, at the broken man inside it, whose eyes had opened and were watching her with the flat caution of an animal that had been hurt by hands. "And yet the valley is still here, and so am I, and it is nearly evening. You will eat. Then you will tell me why the river sent you."

The man with the litter—tall, dark-haired, a scar through one eyebrow, hands that had held tools of precision once and now held only rope—did not argue. That was the first thing she liked about him.

* * *

They ate in the long house, under the smoke-blackened beams her grandfather had raised. Nadya fed them barley and goat cheese and the last of the autumn honey, and she watched the tall one eat the way she watched everything: without appearing to. He ate like a man who had trained himself not to enjoy it, each mouthful functional, eyes always moving—doors, windows, the hills, the sky. A fugitive's table manners. The valley had seen his kind before.

The broken one, Reyes, slept almost at once, his fever bright in his cheeks. The tall one tried to stay awake beside him and lost the fight sitting up, his chin dropping to his chest, his hand still closed around the strap of the litter as if sleep were a country he could be deported from at any moment.

Nadya covered him with a wool blanket and went outside into the blue hour.

Her granddaughter Tamar was waiting by the shrine wall, as Nadya had known she would be. Fifteen years old and already the valley's sharpest eyes. "A lowlander," Tamar said. "With a broken man. From downriver."

"From the gorge."

"The gorge is forbidden. The land office posted it. New signs, red paint, last spring." Tamar's mouth did the thing it did when she was proud of knowing something the elders only suspected. "Grandmother. If the lowlanders are asking about the gorge again—"

"Then the paper has come back," Nadya said. "The paper always comes back."

She said it lightly, because Tamar was fifteen and deserved a light voice, and then she stood alone in the dark for a long time, listening to the river, which was already louder than it had been a week ago. The snowfields at the summit were letting go of winter. In eleven days, perhaps fourteen, the monsoon would arrive behind the meltwater like a creditor behind a debtor, and the river would rise, and the valley would do what it had always done: hold.

Unless, this year, the paper drowned it first.

* * *

In the morning the tall one was up before dawn, checking his friend's splint, and when Nadya came out he was standing at the edge of the terraces looking at the standing stones.

There were eleven of them, though a stranger would have counted six. They were older than the terraces, older than the long house, older than any name Nadya's people had ever written down—basalt pillars raised by hands so far back in time that the hands had left no other trace in the world. Her people called them the Grandmothers. Each one marked a grave, or a season, or a promise, and the six that stood above the ground marked the six places where the old flood had stopped, in the year of Nadya's grandmother's grandmother, when the summit snows had failed all at once and the river had come down angry.

The tall one was counting them. She could see it in the way his eyes moved, in the faint professional narrowing, the engineer's habit of measuring everything against everything else.

"You count six," Nadya said, coming to stand beside him. "There are eleven."

He didn't startle, which she gave him credit for. "The others are fallen?"

"The others are *placed*." She walked, and he followed, and she stopped at the third fallen stone—a slab of basalt half-buried in the hillside, its face worn smooth. "Lift the grass at its base. Carefully. It is old grass."

He knelt and did as she said, and Nadya watched his face as the stone's buried edge came into view. There was a chisel line across it, clean and deliberate, and below the chisel line, cut deep and filled with lead, a mark. A boundary mark. The land office's mark. The same mark, Nadya knew, that stood on stones all along the gorge and down into the lowlands, on paper maps in offices a thousand miles away, certifying where the valley ended and the government's flood scheme began.

Except the stone was not where the maps said it was. It had not been where the maps said it was for a hundred and forty years.

"You moved it," the tall one said. His voice had gone strange.

"My grandmother's grandmother moved it," Nadya said. "With her sisters, at night, in the year the first survey came. They rolled it downhill and buried it and planted the apricot that still stands there." She pointed to the tree, gnarled and heavy with last summer's fruit. "Every generation since, when the surveyors come, we move the stones a little. Downhill. Toward the river. Never far. A hand's width. A foot. Enough that when the lowland men measure their boundaries against their maps, the maps and the ground do not agree—and the ground is always blamed for lying."

The tall one stared at her. And Nadya saw the moment the arithmetic landed in him, the engineer's arithmetic, the numbers rearranging themselves behind his eyes.

"The grant," he said slowly. "The flood scheme. The boundary in the documents—"

"Is a boundary we have spent six generations making false," Nadya said. "Yes. You are not the first to discover that paper and stone disagree. You are only the first in a long while who seems troubled by it." She studied him. "You came up the gorge. You saw the signs, red paint, and you came anyway. So either you are a fool, or you are running from something, or—" and here she watched him very carefully— "you are running *toward* something, and the thing you are running toward has to do with the paper."

He said nothing. But he didn't lie, and that, in Nadya's long experience, was rarer than honesty.

* * *

She took him up the valley in the afternoon, past the terraces and the shrine wall, to the ruins.

Strangers never saw the ruins. That was the point of them. The valley's people had kept them for centuries the way other families kept a dishonor: quietly, completely, without discussion. The ruins sat in a fold of the hillside above the river meadows, half-swallowed by turf, and to an untrained eye they were nothing—bumps in the grass, a scatter of stones, the mountain doing what mountains do. But the tall one was not an untrained eye. She watched him stop at the lip of the fold and go still, the way a man goes still when the ground speaks a language he half remembers.

"Foundations," he said. "Dressed stone. Look at the coursing—that's not field wall, that's *masonry*. There was a town here." He was already moving down into the fold, crouching, touching, his broken friend and his hunted face forgotten. "A bridge footing—there, see the cutwater? And this—these are culverts. Graded. Someone engineered drainage for a settlement of—" he stood, turning, counting, and she watched the number grow in his face— "hundreds. Thousands. This valley was *full*."

"It was full before your government had a name," Nadya said. "Before it had a language. My people did not build it. We found it, the way you found it. The old ones who built it are gone so far back that even our oldest songs only remember their songs." She came down beside him and put her hand on a wall that had stood through every flood the river had ever delivered. "But we remember this: the water has never taken this fold. In the great flood year, the water stopped there—" she pointed to the sixth Grandmother stone on the ridge above— "and this ground stayed dry. The valley remembers where the water stops. The valley has always remembered."

He was quiet for a long time. Then he said, in a voice carefully emptied of everything: "The grant I've seen. The flood scheme. It puts the waterline above this fold. Above the ruins. Above your terraces." He looked at her, and for the first time he did not bother to hide what was in his face, and what was in his face was grief, arriving late for a crime not yet committed. "If the paper is true, all of this drowns. Your fields. Your long house. The graves."

"And your government's paper says the valley was empty when they measured it," Nadya said. "Empty, or nearly. A few scattered families, no permanent settlements, no claim worth the cost of rerouting a culvert. That is what the paper says. I have not read it. I do not need to. It is the same paper every time." She looked out over the ruins, the turf-covered streets where children who had been dust for a thousand years had run. "They do not erase us with soldiers anymore. Soldiers make stories, and stories travel. They erase us with *paper*. A line on a map. A boundary stone in the wrong place. A grant signed in an office by a man who has never been wet to the knee in this river. And then, one year, the water comes higher than it has ever come, and the lowland papers write about a natural disaster, and the valley is empty, and everyone agrees the valley was always going to be empty. That is how it is done. That is how it was always going to be done."

The tall one sat down slowly on the old wall, as if his legs had been taken from him, and put his face in his hands.

Nadya stood beside him and let the silence do its work. Below them the river ran loud and bright with snowmelt, and somewhere above, at the summit, the monsoon was assembling itself out of the warm sea air, patient as a debt.

"My friend," she said at last, "has a name?"

He looked up. "Mirabel," he said, and then, quickly, as if the word had escaped a cage: "That's—not the name on my contract. The contract name is—"

"I did not ask about your contract," Nadya said. "In this valley, a man's contract name is worth what paper is worth. His true name is worth what stone is worth." She held out her hand. "Mirabel. I am Nadya Kesh. My family has kept this valley for six generations, and we have kept it by being forgotten, and I am tired of it. Are you?"

* * *

They came down from the ruins at dusk, and Tamar met them at the shrine wall with the news, breathless from running the ridge path: two men in lowland clothes at the foot of the gorge, asking after a surveyor, showing a sketch. They had turned back at the red signs—but they had counted the valley's smoke, Tamar said, counted it twice, and written in a book.

Mirabel went white. Nadya watched him calculate—distance, pace, the litter, the broken leg—and watched him arrive at the same number she had already arrived at.

"They will come back," he said. "With more men. After the storm breaks, when the crews move in. Or before."

"Then there are things we must do before the water comes," Nadya said. "And one of them, I think, is yours." She looked at him, at this man who had carried a broken stranger up a forbidden gorge on his back, and she made the decision she had been circling for six generations of grandmothers, the decision they had all been too wise to make and too tired not to dream of. "You said you saw the grant. The paper with the boundary on it. Tell me, engineer—paper can be forged. Everyone in the lowlands knows paper can be forged, and no one can prove it. But a man who forges a document always leaves a mark. A habit of the hand. A date that cannot be true. Tell me: in all your walking up that river, following whatever it was you followed—did you find the mark? Did you find the proof?"

Mirabel Ishikawa looked at the shrine wall—at the flat stone in its center, older than the long house, older than the ruins, the stone her people had sworn on since before anyone could say what they were swearing to—and when he answered, his voice was very quiet, and it was the voice of a man stepping off a cliff he had been standing on for weeks.

"Not yet," he said. "But I know where it is."
