## 14. Torn Pages

The coordinates Reyes had given me were not numbers at all. They were a sentence.

*"Where the river turns its back on the sun."*

He'd repeated it three times while I strapped his splinted leg to the litter frame, and each time he'd added the same correction: *not where it faces the sun — where it turns away.* The distinction mattered, he said. The crew chief, a dry, precise man named Okonkwo who chewed coca leaf and spoke in decimals, had chosen the cache site the way other men chose passwords — something a stranger would get wrong on the first try and never get a second try at.

So I climbed the gorge's north wall in the last grey hour before dawn, watching for the moment the river below me swung west and put the sunrise at its back, and when that bend came — a hard elbow of white water against black stone — I stopped climbing and started looking sideways.

The shrine was nothing. That was the first thing I understood about it. A niche in the cliff face, chest-high, sheltered by an overhang of granite that had shrugged off ten thousand monsoons. Inside: a flat stone worn smooth by hands, a stub of juniper burned down to charcoal, a ring of small pebbles arranged with a deliberateness that made my skin prickle. Someone had been tending this place long before Okonkwo ever found it. The valley's people marked their dead here, or their debts, or their gods — I didn't know which, and standing there I felt like a man reading someone else's mail in their own kitchen.

The cache itself was tucked behind the smooth stone, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed string, tied with a knot I recognized because my father had used it on every parcel he ever shipped me from the coast. A surveyor's knot. Okonkwo hadn't hidden the pages from engineers. He'd hidden them *for* one — left them like a letter addressed to whoever was stubborn enough, or guilty enough, or angry enough to come looking.

I sat down on the ledge with my back to the rock and opened the oilcloth with fingers that wouldn't quite hold steady.

Fourteen pages. Torn close along the spine of the field journal I'd found sealed in the tunnel, the same paper, the same grid-ruled stock, the same cramped hand I'd spent two days convincing myself wasn't familiar.

It was familiar. Of course it was familiar. I'd learned to letter elevations under that hand. Aldous Vane had made me rewrite an entire bridge survey at twenty-two years old because my numerals leaned, and he'd stood over my shoulder saying, "A drawing is a promise, Ishikawa. Sloppy promises break."

The pages were promises. Broken ones, in his own writing.

* * *

I read them twice through before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and then a third time slowly, the way you'd read a confession from a man you loved.

Page one was routine — chainage notes, water levels, the ordinary arithmetic of a boundary survey up the source valley. Page two began to tilt. Vane's annotations started appearing in the margins, smaller than the body text, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the page beneath:

*"Stone re-cut. Original socket visible beneath new face. Lichen growth on original: minimum 40 yrs. On replacement: none. Whoever set this stone wanted it to look older than it is. Note date discrepancy vs. Grant 114-C."*

Grant 114-C. I knew the number the way you know the name of a disease running in your family. It was the land grant I'd pulled from the district archive in Kellwater, the one certifying the flood scheme, the one bearing Vane's signature as consulting engineer of record. The archive copy had been clean. Archivally, bureaucratically, beautifully clean.

These pages were what the clean copy had been built to bury.

Page five stopped my breath. The crew chief's hand — Okonkwo's, tight and decimal-precise — recorded the excavation of a second boundary marker, this one buried deliberately, its original position logged against three fixed triangulation points. And beside it, in Vane's margin script, in letters so small they were almost a whisper:

*"The grant is dated Year 12. This stone was moved in Year 9 to accommodate the new line. The grant describes a boundary that did not exist until after the grant was signed. Therefore the grant was backdated — or the boundary was forged forward. Either way, the document certifies a fiction. I signed a certification of a fiction. A.V."*

*I signed a certification of a fiction.*

He'd written it. Not implied it, not gestured toward it, not left it for inference the way he'd left everything else in his careful, deniable life. He'd written the sentence out, initialed it, and let it leave his possession — because Okonkwo had demanded the pages as insurance, and Vane, whatever else he was, understood leverage well enough to know that a blackmail cache only protected you if the truth inside it was real.

That was the part that sat in my chest like swallowed river stone. Vane hadn't confessed to the valley. He hadn't confessed to any authority. He'd confessed to a locked box in a cliff shrine, held hostage by a junior officer whose disappearance he may have ordered or permitted — I still didn't know which, and the not-knowing was doing its own quiet work on me.

Pages nine through twelve were the worst. They were hydrology.

Vane had run the flood model himself. I could see him doing it — the same tables he'd drilled into me, the same obsessive cross-checking, the same marginal habit of arguing with himself in pencil. The model showed exactly what the scheme would do: a retention basin behind a proposed dam at the gorge mouth, flooding the source valley to a designed depth of eleven meters during peak monsoon, protecting the downstream rail corridor and the towns on it from a hundred-year flood event.

And in the margin of page eleven, next to a figure for displaced population, Vane had written a single line:

*"4,100 souls. Relocation costed at 11 months' notice. Monsoon arrives in 7. Arithmetic does not care. I do. God help me, I do."*

Seven months. Seven months' notice for four thousand people, in a valley whose only road was a footpath, whose elders had no title deeds because their claim to the land predated deeds — predated paper, predated the office that held the paper. Eleven months was already a lie dressed as generosity. Seven was a rounding error applied to human beings.

I closed the pages and looked out over the gorge, and the river was just a river, silvering in the early light, making its indifferent noise. Somewhere below and behind me, Reyes lay on his litter under Nadya Kesh's granddaughter's watch, feverish but stable, memorizing nothing anymore because everything worth memorizing was now in my coat.

Vindication. I'd imagined what it would feel like for weeks — since the tunnel, since the camp with its boot prints arriving and none leaving, since the night in Sera Oduya's kitchen when she'd traded me soup for fragments and I'd heard myself say Vane's name out loud for the first time in six years. I'd imagined vindication as warmth. As relief. As the click of a mechanism finally seating true.

It felt like none of that. It felt like weight.

Because proof ends neutrality. That's the thing nobody tells you. While the evidence is missing, you can be a man who merely suspects, a man who keeps his head down, a man with a borrowed name and a low contract and a compass that points nowhere in particular. Suspicion is a private condition. It costs nothing and obligates nothing. But the pages were in my hands now, wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs, and every hour I carried them without acting, I became something with a name I'd been avoiding for six years.

An accessory. After the fact, and in advance of it.

* * *

Nadya Kesh met me at the valley's edge near midday, sitting on the boundary stone her people had been quietly moving for three generations — the original marker, the one Vane's margins described, hauled forty meters upslope and left to be "found" wrong by every survey party the government sent. She was shelling beans into a cloth, unhurried, and she watched me come down the switchbacks with the patience of a woman who had watched many men come down those switchbacks carrying less than they'd gone up with.

"You found it," she said. Not a question.

"I found it."

"And?"

I crouched beside the stone. My legs were shaking — not from the climb; I'd climbed worse. From the other thing.

"Your valley," I said. "In his own handwriting. Four thousand one hundred people, costed like freight. And the sentence *I signed a certification of a fiction*, initialed A.V."

She went on shelling. One bean, another. Then she stopped, and looked at me, and her eyes were the flattest, deepest thing I'd seen in this whole country — deeper than the gorge, older than the ruins her people lived among.

"Now you have it," she said, "and I have it, and the council will have it tonight. Do you understand what changes?"

"Everything," I said. "Or nothing. Depending on whether the men from the land office reach us first."

"Those are not the only options." She tied off the bean cloth with a motion so practiced it was invisible. "There is a third. You give the pages to my people, all of them, and we do what we have always done with things that must survive. We make copies. We scatter them. We put them in walls, in graves, in songs. A government can arrest a man. It has never once managed to arrest a valley."

"That's chapter nineteen of your plan," I almost said, and caught myself, because she didn't know she was living inside a book and I wasn't going to tell her. But the thought was real regardless: the council scene, the sealing of copies in three places, truth made durable. I could see it from here, the way you can see a bridge's far anchorage from the near one, and know the span between is the hard part.

"Tonight, then," I said. "The council."

"Tonight," Nadya agreed. Then she tilted her head, listening to something I couldn't hear yet — a bird changing its pattern, maybe, or just the accumulated instinct of seventy years in these mountains. "But you should eat first. And you should sleep. And you should know that the girl watching your engineer came down an hour ago to say there were horses on the far trail. Two riders. Moving fast, and not bothering to hide it."

The cold went through me the way the gorge wind did, straight up the canyon, no warning.

"Which trail?"

"The one that doesn't reach us before dark." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a chess player. "But it reaches the shrine before noon tomorrow. If they know to look for a shrine. Men from offices always know to look for shrines eventually — it's the first place anyone hides anything, including them."

I stood. The oilcloth packet pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Nadya," I said. "If they take these pages—"

"They won't." She picked up her bean cloth and rose, slower than I had, steadier than I would ever be. "But if they take *you*, the pages still go to the council. Decide now whether you can live with that arrangement, because by morning it may not be yours to decide."

I decided in about four seconds, which was three more than it deserved.

"Yes," I said. "That arrangement. Yes."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I told myself I would — ate what Nadya gave me, lay down in the granary loft she offered, closed my eyes with the discipline of a man who knows sleep is ammunition. But the pages were too loud. I'd read them three times on the cliff and twice more in the loft by the light of a rush lamp, and each reading peeled something back.

Here was the thing I kept circling and couldn't land: Vane's margins weren't the writing of a corrupt man. They were the writing of a man at war with his own signature. He'd certified the fiction, yes — but he'd documented the certification's fraud in the same notebook, in his own hand, knowing Okonkwo would hold it over him forever. That's not corruption. That's a man building his own gallows and hoping somebody honest finds the rope.

He'd taught me that drawings were promises. Maybe these pages were his way of keeping half a promise — the half that documents, if not the half that acts. Or maybe I was doing what I'd been doing since Kellwater: inventing excuses for him because the alternative was admitting that the man who made me had helped build the machine that erased a camp full of surveyors and was now erasing a valley.

Both things could be true. Both things probably were true. That was the discovery underneath the discovery, the one the pages forced on me whether I wanted it or not: Aldous Vane was not a villain, and that made everything harder, not easier. Villains you can fight. Mentors you have to grieve while you fight them.

Somewhere past midnight I took out my father's compass — the brass one, dented on the bezel, the one that had pointed uselessly at nothing since the day I'd found the dead team's camp, as if even the instrument had opinions about where I was going. I held it flat on my palm in the lamplight. The needle swung, settled, swung again, and finally stopped pointing at the gorge wall, pointing instead — for the first time in weeks — roughly south. Downstream. Toward Kellwater. Toward Sera Oduya, who had lied to armed men for a stranger without being asked, and who was somewhere behind me now either safe or not safe, and I had no way to know which.

Even the compass was telling me I'd passed the point where hiding was a direction.

Toward dawn I heard the horses. Not close — the far trail, as Nadya had said, a kilometer of air and two ridges away — but audible in the cold morning silence the way a train is audible before you see it: a rhythm, a weight, an intention. Two riders. I counted the hoofbeats the way I'd once counted wheel sets passing a milepost, and the count told me what I needed: they were pacing themselves. They expected a longer ride. Which meant they expected resistance, or distance, or both.

They knew where they were going. Offices always know to look for shrines eventually.

I rolled the pages tight, slid them into the oilcloth, and did the thing I should have done on the cliff: I split the risk. Half the pages — the five that mattered most, the ones with Vane's initialed confession and the population figures — went into a waxed tube I'd carried since Kellwater for map storage. The rest stayed in the oilcloth. One packet went inside my coat. The other went to Nadya's granddaughter, a girl of perhaps sixteen with her grandmother's flat unblinking eyes, who took it without a word and vanished into the terraces like water into gravel.

By the time the sun cleared the ridge, I was climbing back toward the shrine, alone, moving fast, carrying half the truth against my ribs and leaving the other half scattered across a valley that had been scattering truths for three generations.

Let them come to the shrine. Let them find fresh boot prints and a disturbed stone and a cold juniper stub, and let them spend their fury on an empty niche while the real evidence walked downhill on the legs of a girl who owned these mountains in ways no grant would ever describe.

I reached the ledge below the shrine as the hoofbeats rounded the final spur — close now, close enough to hear the tack creak, close enough to see dust rising off the trail through the pines. Two riders, as promised. I knew neither horse, but I knew the posture of the lead rider before I could make out his face: the forward lean of a man who has chased something for a long time and has begun to enjoy it.

I pressed myself into the shadow of the overhang, below the niche, and waited with my back against ten thousand monsoons of granite, and listened to them climb the last switchback, talking to each other in the low easy register of men who believe the outcome is settled.

Then the lead rider said a name.

Not mine. Not the borrowed one. Not Ishikawa, not the false name I'd signed contracts with in Kellwater.

He said *"Vane wants him alive,"* and then he said a word I hadn't heard spoken aloud in six years — my real name, my father's name, the name I'd buried with my career — spoken casually, correctly, by a stranger on a horse, in a place where no one alive should have known it.

The pages in my coat suddenly weighed nothing at all. Because the question was no longer whether I could protect the evidence.

It was who had sold me.
