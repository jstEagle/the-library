## 15. The Escort Returns

The shrine sat in a fold of the gorge wall like a cupped hand, three hundred feet above the river and reachable only by a goat track that Nadya's guide, a silent boy called Penn, climbed as if gravity were a rumor he'd chosen not to believe. Mirabel followed with the pages' coordinates burned into his memory—Reyes had recited them twice on the litter trail, eyes closed, lips moving like a man praying to arithmetic—and by late afternoon of the fourth day he stood before it.

It was smaller than he'd expected. A niche cut into living granite, older than any faith he could name, its carved lintel worn smooth by centuries of rain. Inside: a clay lamp long gone dry, a ring of stones that might have been offerings or might have been counting, and a flat slab at the back that looked like nothing until you put your fingers under its edge.

Penn watched him without comment. Below them, Sera had made camp with Reyes in a hollow where the smoke from their fire would disperse into the gorge wind. Nadya herself had come this far and no farther; she said the shrine belonged to her people's dead and she would not climb to it while strangers disturbed it. But she had sent Penn, and Penn had brought two others—broad-shouldered men who moved through scree like water through gravel—and Mirabel understood, without anyone saying it aloud, that they were not here for the view.

He worked the slab loose. It came free with a grinding whisper, and behind it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was a bundle the size of a prayer book.

His hands shook. He noticed it distantly, the way an engineer notices a vibration in a span that shouldn't vibrate—the part of him that observed was already separate from the part that trembled. He unwrapped the cloth on his knees in the dust.

Four pages. Torn edges matching the stubs he'd found in the field journal in the tunnel, fiber to fiber, a torn match across months and miles. And on them, in a hand he knew better than his own—

Aldous Vane's annotations. Marginal calculations in that precise, upright script, the same hand that had corrected Mirabel's first survey drawings twenty years ago, red ink then, pencil now. Dates adjusted. A boundary line shifted east by eleven hundred meters. And beside the new line, in Vane's own cramped notation:

*Grant instrument predates settlement record by 14 years. Stone cannot have been placed 1871. Whoever set it used the old flood-year benchmarks — see attached. This will not survive inquiry. Advise client accordingly.*

*Advise client accordingly.* As if forgery were a drafting error. As if a valley were a client.

Mirabel read the pages three times while the light went amber and then gray in the gorge. The fourth pass, he stopped reading and simply looked at the handwriting, because there was a grief in it that had nothing to do with the content—a man recognizing his father's voice in a stranger's confession. Vane had taught him that a line on paper was a promise made to everyone who would ever stand where the line fell. *We don't draw lines, Ishikawa. We draw consequences.*

He had drawn this one knowing what it buried.

"Down," Penn said quietly. Not alarmed—yet. Just the flat economy of a boy who had grown up reading weather and strangers both. "Men on the lower track."

* * *

Mirabel got the pages inside his coat, against his chest, and went down the goat track faster than was wise, loose stone singing away from his boots into the dark. From above, the gorge was all shadow and the river was a sound rather than a thing. He counted switchbacks. At the third he saw them: lantern light on the lower path, two lights, maybe three, moving with the unhurried discipline of men who knew exactly where they were going and had never once had to search for it.

They hadn't searched. That was the thought that turned his mouth dry. They had come upriver behind him the whole way—not chasing, *escorting*, the way you follow a survey stake to see where the line runs. He had been the instrument. Someone had let him find the coordinates, or had simply trusted that a man like Mirabel Ishikawa, given a torn journal and a guilty conscience, would do exactly this: climb to the truth and carry it in his own two hands, saving everyone else the trouble.

Sera had the fire kicked apart before he reached the hollow. She had Reyes half-upright against a boulder, the splinted leg propped on a rolled blanket, and she stood between them and the track with a camp hatchet held low against her thigh—not raised, not brandished, just present, the way a fact is present.

"How many?" she asked.

"At least two. Maybe three." He crouched by Reyes. "Can you move if we carry?"

Reyes laughed. It was a terrible sound, thin and cracked, the laugh of a man whose humor had been living off its reserves for weeks. "Ishikawa. Look at me. I've been carried for four days by people who owe me nothing. I'm done being cargo." His hand found Mirabel's sleeve. "The pages. You have them?"

"Yes."

"Then the arithmetic is simple, and I was always better at arithmetic than either of us liked to admit." He pushed himself straighter against the boulder. "You go up. The scree field west of here—it moves, doesn't it? Your friends know it moves."

One of Nadya's men, the one called Osten, materialized out of the dusk at the edge of the hollow. He looked at Reyes for a long moment, then at Mirabel, and said something in the valley tongue—short, questioning. Penn, arriving behind Mirabel, translated in a whisper: "He asks if the broken one is one of ours now."

"Yes," Mirabel said.

"No," Reyes said, at the same instant, and gripped Mirabel's wrist hard enough to hurt. "No. Listen to me. If I'm one of theirs, they'll fight for me, and these men with lanterns will stop counting sheep and start counting bodies. There are children in that valley. I've seen the graves of the last time your government came through with paperwork." His voice dropped. "You take the pages to Nadya. You let them take me. I'm a junior engineer with a busted leg who's been missing for a season—I'm a footnote. Those pages are the whole book. Don't you dare make me worth more dead than the truth is."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You left yourself years ago. You can manage this."

The lanterns rounded the last bend below the hollow.

* * *

There were three of them. Mirabel knew the lead man instantly—the land office official from Kellwater, the one with the sketch, though the sketch had flattered him. Up close he had a clerk's softness gone to leather, a face like a document kept too long in a damp drawer. His name, when he gave it, was Corvess. He pronounced it the way a man pronounces something he expects to be remembered.

Behind him, two escorts in plain gray coats with the particular stillness of men paid not to be described. Carbines slung, hands loose. Professionals.

Corvess surveyed the hollow with the mild disappointment of a landlord inspecting a tenancy. His gaze touched Sera, the hatchet, the boulder, Reyes, and finally Mirabel, and settled there with something almost like satisfaction.

"There he is," Corvess said. "The stray surveyor." He took a folded paper from his coat—Mirabel didn't need to see it to know it was the sketch, his own face rendered by someone who had studied it longer than an artist needed to. "You've cost this office a great deal of inconvenience, Mr.—well. We'll come back to names. Names seem to be difficult for you."

"He's hurt," Sera said. Her voice was level. The hatchet hadn't moved. "He needs a surgeon, not whatever you're selling."

"What he needs," Corvess said pleasantly, "is to be returned to the district for questioning regarding the disappearance of a government survey team. As does this gentleman—" a nod toward Reyes, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, "—whose miraculous survival will be a great comfort to everyone. The others, I regret to say, remain unaccounted for. Rivers are dangerous in this country."

"They weren't lost," Reyes said from the boulder. His voice rang off the gorge wall. "Your escort arrested our chief at kilometer thirty-one and drove the rest of us into the flood channel. I lay in a tunnel for nineteen days listening to the water decide whether to take me. So no, Mr. Land Office. Nobody here is going to help you lose us politely."

Something flickered in Corvess's face—annoyance, quickly filed away. "Delirious," he said to no one. "Exposure does that." Then, to his escorts: "Take them both. Gently. The office prefers its witnesses ambulatory."

Osten stepped out of the rocks.

So did Penn. And the second man, whose name Mirabel had never learned, rising from the scree above the track with stones already in his hands. Three of them against two carbines, on ground they had known since childhood, and for one suspended second the whole hollow balanced on it—Mirabel felt the balance physically, in his teeth, the way you feel a span take load.

"No," Reyes said loudly. "No—Osten. *No.*"

And then, absurdly, horribly, the thing that saved them was Reyes himself. He pitched sideways off the boulder—deliberately, Mirabel realized a heartbeat too late, throwing his weight onto the broken leg, screaming, real pain, real blood welling through the splint wrapping—and the escorts swung their carbines toward the noise, and Corvess snapped *"Don't shoot, you idiots, we need him breathing,"* and in that half-second of divided attention Osten whistled.

Two notes. High, then low.

The mountain answered.

* * *

Nadya's people had spent generations learning which stones wanted to fall. The scree field above the western track was old, unstable, and loved them for it—they had loosened its key stones years ago, Mirabel understood later, the way other communities maintained wells or walls, a piece of infrastructure nobody wrote down. Osten's whistle was the only permission it required.

The slope moved with a sound like the end of the world conducted politely. Not an avalanche—a *curtain*, tons of rock pouring across the lower track fifty meters downstream of the hollow, sealing it, dust boiling up white in the lantern light. The escorts flattened against the gorge wall. One of them lost his carbine to the scree. Corvess shouted something about the upper path, and Mirabel was already moving, hauling Sera by the wrist, Penn ahead of them, up and west, away from the shrine, away from the river, into the high ground where the lanterns couldn't follow and the horses couldn't exist.

Below, through the dust, he heard them get Reyes.

He heard it precisely: the scrape of a stretcher improvised from a coat and a branch, the escorts' clipped voices, and Reyes—God help him—talking, keeping his voice up, giving them a performance of delirium, rambling numbers and benchmark elevations so that anything he said near the pages would be buried in noise. Buying the last few seconds. Selling them his body so the truth could run.

Mirabel stopped at the top of the rise. Sera pulled at his arm. Penn pulled at the other. He stood there in the settling dust and watched the lanterns shrink down the far side of the slide, carrying Tomas Reyes toward the district, toward questioning, toward whatever word the land office used for the thing they'd done to the rest of the survey team.

"We can follow," Sera said. Her breath was ragged. "Four men, one stretcher, a broken road—we can—"

"And do what?" His own voice came out wrong, scraped. "Fight? With what? Get him back and lose the pages doing it?" He pressed his hand flat against his chest, against the oilcloth bundle, as if checking his own heart were still there. "He made the trade. He chose it. He—" The sentence collapsed. He stood in the dark hating every true thing he'd just said.

*Survival purchased with another man's capture.* The ledger of small debts, Sera had called it once, back in Kellwater, over soup. Well. The debts had gotten larger.

* * *

They reached Nadya's camp near midnight. Fires banked low, the valley people moving among them with the quiet efficiency of a household that had rehearsed bad news for a century. Nadya met Mirabel at the edge of the firelight. She looked at his empty hands, then at his chest, and something in her face eased and hardened at once.

"You have them."

"Yes."

"And they have the engineer."

"Yes." His throat worked. "He made them take him. He broke his own leg open on a rock to pull their guns off my people. Nadya—he did it so I could walk away with four pieces of paper."

Nadya was silent for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere a child cried and was hushed, gently, the way this valley hushed everything—grief included, grief especially.

"My grandmother," she said at last, "watched soldiers burn the records house in Harrow Ford. Everything her family owned in writing, gone in an afternoon. Afterward she told me: paper burns, but a witness walks. That is why we moved the boundary stones ourselves, generation after generation—because the paper was theirs, but the walking was ours." She looked at him steadily. "You are walking now, engineer. Where?"

Mirabel took out the pages. In the firelight, Vane's handwriting glowed faintly, the marginal calculations neat as scripture. *This will not survive inquiry.*

"It won't survive inquiry," he said. "That's what he wrote. He knew. He certified it anyway." Something was happening in his chest that he recognized from bridge failures he'd witnessed—one component after another passing its load to the next until the whole structure found a new shape or came down. Rage, arriving not as heat but as load-bearing resolve. "Inquiry won't survive *it*. Not if enough people hold copies. Not if it's sealed in three places, five places, not if every elder in this valley can recite the dates by heart. He built this lie to be unkillable by one man's arrest. Fine. Then we build the truth the same way."

Nadya nodded slowly. "The council sits in three days. You will speak to them."

"I'll speak to them." He fed one corner of the oilcloth to the fire, watching it curl, blacken, die. "But first I need ink and paper. There's someone who taught me everything I know about lines on paper. Before I deliver this to your council, he's going to have one chance to say it himself."

Sera, sitting down heavily beside him, handed him her notebook without a word. On its first blank page, by firelight, Mirabel Ishikawa began to write a letter to Aldous Vane—and for the first time since Kellwater, he signed it with his real name.
