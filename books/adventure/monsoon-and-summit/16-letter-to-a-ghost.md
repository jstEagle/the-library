## 16. Letter to a Ghost

The paper was the hardest thing to find.

That sounded absurd even to Mirabel as he said it aloud, alone in the shrine's shadow with the torn pages wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. He had crossed a flooded gorge on a rotted cable. He had carried a man with a shattered leg down three switchbacks of scree. He had watched Reyes taken at gunpoint and done nothing but run, and none of it had cost him what this cost: finding one clean sheet of paper in a valley that had been systematically stripped of anything that could hold a record.

Nadya Kesh solved it in the way she solved everything, which was to make the problem smaller until it disappeared. "The school," she said. "There are slates. But if you must write—" and she produced, from a locked box beneath her floor, three sheets of good rag paper and a stick of sealing wax, kept for letters that mattered. She did not ask who the letter was for. She had read enough of his face by now.

"You understand what you're doing," she said, not quite a question. She sat across the hearth from him, small and upright, hands folded like a woman waiting out weather. "You're giving him warning."

"I'm giving him a chance."

"Those are often the same thing." She turned her palms up. "In my experience they are almost always the same thing."

Mirabel looked at the blank page. The firelight made it glow faintly, like something already burning.

"He taught me everything I know about load paths," he said. "About how a bridge tells you it's dying before it dies. About reading steel the way you'd read a face. Twenty years I've been—carrying that. Like a compass." His father's compass sat heavy in his pocket; he didn't touch it. "If I send these pages to the council without giving him one chance to stand up and say it himself, then I'm just another man deciding someone else's story from behind a desk. That's what he did. That's what the land office does. I won't do it too."

"And if he uses the chance to have you killed?"

"Then I'll know," Mirabel said, "that the man who taught me never existed. Which is also worth knowing."

Nadya studied him for a long moment. Outside, the valley was making its preparations—the sound of stone being moved, deliberately, patiently, the way her people had been moving stones off false boundary markers for four generations. Somewhere below, the river ran high and brown with early melt, rehearsing for the monsoon.

"Write your letter," she said finally. "But write it in your own hand, and keep a copy. Not because you'll need it. Because a man should be able to look at his own words later and remember who he was when he wrote them."

He wrote by firelight, three drafts, burning the first two in the stove. The third came out shorter than he intended, because the truth, when he finally stopped dressing it up, was short:

*Master Vane —*

*I have the pages. You know which ones. I know what your annotations say, and I know why the crew chief kept them, and I know now that you knew he kept them.*

*I am going to deliver them to the council of the source valley within the week. Before I do, I am offering you the thing no one offered the survey team: the chance to say it yourself, publicly, before witnesses. Confess the forgery. Name the order it came under. Stand up before it is done to you.*

*You told me once that a structure fails at its weakest point, and that the engineer's whole duty is to find that point honestly, because the river always finds it anyway. The river is coming, sir. It finds everything.*

*Tell me where to bring the confession. I will come alone.*

*Mirabel Ishikawa*

He looked at his name for a long time. Four years since he'd written it anywhere. It looked strange on the page, like a stranger's handwriting that happened to match his own.

Nadya sealed it without comment and gave it to a boy of perhaps fourteen who, she explained, ran salt and lamp oil down to the trading post at Kilometer Thirty-One twice a month and could be trusted absolutely, mostly because he was paid in stories rather than coin and considered himself rich beyond measure.

"Kilometer Thirty-One has a post office?" Mirabel asked.

"Kilometer Thirty-One has a woman named Bree who collects mail for half the district and reads none of it, which makes her the most trustworthy person in the province." Nadya's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Your letter will reach the district capital in six days if the passes stay open. Less, if the monsoon front pushes people to move faster. Everything moves faster before a storm."

Six days there. Six days back, if Vane answered at all. Eleven days until landfall, by Mirabel's last calculation—and that number had been shrinking every time he looked at the sky. The arithmetic was bad. He'd known it was bad when he started writing. He wrote the letter anyway.

That night he slept badly, dreaming of a lecture hall twenty years gone, Vane at the blackboard with chalk dust on his sleeves, drawing the profile of a trestle pier and saying, in that dry voice that could make statics sound like scripture: *Gentlemen, the bridge does not care what you meant. The bridge cares what you built. Build accordingly.*

* * *

The reply came on the ninth day, which meant it had traveled faster than any legitimate post, which meant Vane had pulled whatever strings he still had, which meant—he made himself note this coldly—that Vane had received the letter and treated it as urgent.

It arrived at dusk, carried up from the trading post by the same boy, who delivered it with the gravity of a diplomat and then lingered hopefully until Nadya promised him the ending of a story tomorrow. The envelope was heavy stock, district-capital quality, addressed in a hand Mirabel knew better than his own: the sharp verticals, the engineer's drafting discipline transferred to penmanship, every letter a small act of construction.

He took it apart from the others, at the edge of the terrace above the gorge, where no wall was thin enough to listen through.

Inside were two sheets. The first was brief.

*Ishikawa —*

*You write like a man who has already decided and wants credit for the deciding. Very well. I will grant you your theater.*

*Come to the old line's last bridge. The trestle at Kilometer Forty. Come alone, come unarmed, and come before the storm closes the passes, because after the storm nothing we say to each other will matter and neither will either of us.*

*I will confess. On conditions you will not like. Bring the pages.*

*— A.V.*

The second sheet was not a letter. It was a single-page engineering memorandum, dated eleven years earlier, bearing the district land office seal and Aldous Vane's signature—a preliminary hydrological assessment of the source valley, recommending controlled inundation, citing downstream flood protection for a projected population of ten thousand four hundred. Mirabel knew the document. He had seen its final published version in the archive, the one with the boundary survey attached, the one with the forged grant underneath like a keel.

But this was the draft. And in the margin, in Vane's hand, in pencil, small enough that only someone who had spent years learning to read that hand would ever notice:

*Boundary figures supplied by L.O. Do not verify. Do not ask.*

Do not verify. Do not ask. Eleven years ago, his mentor had been handed falsified numbers, had recognized them as falsified, and had written them into a document that would drown a valley—and had chosen, in pencil, in the margin, to look away. Then he had signed it in ink.

Mirabel stood at the terrace edge until full dark, holding the memo, feeling the way he'd felt standing on a span once when a pier shifted under live load: that lurch of understanding that the thing beneath you was never what you thought, and had maybe never been what you thought, and that the collapse, when it came, would simply be the truth arriving late.

Hope and trap. He turned the phrase over all evening, testing it like bad steel. Every argument for hope: Vane had replied at all. Vane had confessed in writing, in effect—*do not verify* was a confession, was practically a signed admission, and sending it to Mirabel served no purpose except to show him the shape of the sin. A man planning murder did not enclose exhibits. A man planning surrender did.

Every argument for trap: come alone. Come before the storm. Bring the pages. Three instructions, and every one of them convenient for an ambush. The trestle at Kilometer Forty was a condemned structure at the end of a dead line, miles from any witness, reachable by one road—a road wide enough, Mirabel noted, for the land office convoy that had taken Reyes. They would want the pages back. They would want Mirabel closed the way the survey team had been closed: neatly, without tracks leading out.

And beneath both arguments, quieter than either, the thing he was ashamed of: *I want it to be hope.* He wanted to stand across from Aldous Vane and hear some version of events in which the man he'd followed for twenty years had been trapped, coerced, deceived—anything other than the margin note's plain meaning, which was that Vane had known, and signed, and spent eleven years building a career on top of the bodies of a drowned valley that hadn't drowned yet.

Sera found him there after midnight. She'd been back in the valley five days now, having walked out of Kellwater ahead of the men who'd meant to make her disappear, and she still moved like someone cataloguing exits. She sat down beside him on the cold stone without asking, wrapped a blanket around them both against the pre-monsoon damp, and read the letter over his shoulder with the ease of a woman who had long ago decided his privacy was a luxury the situation couldn't afford.

"Alone," she said.

"That's what it says."

"And you believe it?"

"I believe he means it," Mirabel said. "I believe he means it the way he's always meant things—which is that his meaning and the outcome don't have to have anything to do with each other."

Sera was quiet a moment. Below them the river muttered in the dark, higher than yesterday. "When the land office men came to my inn," she said, "showing their sketch of your face, I lied before I'd decided to lie. It came out of me like a reflex. And afterward I lay awake thinking: I have just bet my life on a man I've known for one winter, because of soup and a few bad nights' conversation." She pulled the blanket tighter. "So I won't tell you not to go alone. I'll tell you what I told myself. Being alone is a choice you get to make right up until the moment it becomes the thing that kills you. After that it's just a mistake with good posture."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true. You engineers—" she shook her head, but her shoulder stayed against his. "Go to your bridge. Meet your ghost. But understand that Nadya's people are going to be within a mile of that trestle whether you invite them or not, because they have spent four generations learning what happens to people who trust the land office's word about meetings. You can pretend you're alone. You don't get to actually be alone. That's the price of being someone people have decided matters."

He wanted to argue. He found he couldn't, partly because she was right and partly because some starved part of him had heard *someone has decided you matter* and gone quiet with it, the way a cold room goes quiet when a door opens onto warmth.

He spent the next day preparing, and preparation, for an engineer, was a form of prayer. He walked the lower gorge and memorized the approaches to Kilometer Forty: the dead line's embankment running along the north bank, the trestle itself spanning a narrows where the river compressed to white noise, the access road climbing from the south—the road the convoy would use. He assessed the trestle from across the water, binoculars borrowed from the school, and felt the old professional grief rise in him unbidden. It had been a beautiful structure once, eighty meters of timber and iron on cut-stone piers, his side of the province's ambition written in trusses. Now the piers were spalling, the iron was rust-bleeding down the timber like old wounds, and the whole span stood at perhaps fifteen percent of rated load—which meant it would hold two men talking and nothing heavier, and everyone involved in the coming meeting would know it.

Everyone. That word again. He caught himself doing it—thinking of Vane and the land office as separate parties, when the margin note suggested they had been one party for eleven years, and the question of the bridge was which half of that party would be standing on the deck when he arrived.

On the second evening, Nadya came to him with the weather.

"The front has entered the passes," she said. "My knees, the birds, and the government's own forecast agree, which is the first time in my life all three have concurred. Landfall in four days, perhaps five. Your meeting is the day after tomorrow." She looked at him steadily. "You understand what the timing means. If you go to that bridge and it goes wrong, there will be no one to carry the pages out afterward except us. If it goes right, the same is true. Either way, the pages leave this valley tonight. Copies. Three places, as the council decided. What you carry to the bridge can be the original or can be paper of equal weight. Only you will know which."

He understood. She was offering him the oldest trick in security and the kindest one: let the enemy take the prize and discover later it was ash. Let the ambushers win their ambush and hold nothing.

"No," Mirabel said. "The originals go with me."

Nadya raised an eyebrow, and for a moment he saw her recalculating him entirely.

"If I walk onto that bridge carrying blanks," he said, "then whatever happens there is theater, and Vane will smell it, and worse—I'll smell it. I'm offering him one real chance. A real chance means I can really lose. The pages, the confession, all of it, riding on whether a tired old man chooses ink over pencil one more time." He looked at his hands. "Besides. If they take the pages on that bridge, they'll have to explain where they got them. Stolen evidence is still evidence. Dead men can't testify, but documents travel."

"A dangerous philosophy for a man planning to possibly die."

"I'm a railroad engineer," Mirabel said. "Dangerous philosophies are the job."

Nadya left, and he sat with the letter and the margin note and the compass, and at some point in the dark he took the compass out and opened it, out of habit older than thought, and watched the needle swing and settle. North. It always found north. His father had bought it secondhand from a demoted stationmaster and told him: *A compass doesn't care whose son you are or what you've done. It points. Your whole life is deciding whether to follow.*

Four years of not following. Four years of a borrowed name and a bent posture and the careful, exhausting work of pointing nowhere. And now a letter from a ghost, summoning him to a dying bridge at the end of a dead line, with a storm four days out and an ambush probability he genuinely could not calculate, and the needle steady under glass saying the only thing it knew how to say.

He packed the pages against his chest, checked the oilcloth seams twice, and set out before dawn, alone in exactly the way Sera had predicted he wouldn't be—her silhouette visible for the first hour on the ridge above, keeping pace, refusing to be waved off, until the trail dropped into the gorge and took her from view.

The trestle at Kilometer Forty appeared through morning mist the way wrecks appear: gradually, then all at once, then impossible to stop seeing. Vane was already there.

His mentor stood at the center of the span, small in the fog, hands clasped behind his back in the lecture-hall stance, watching the river run beneath him with the expression of a man auditing a slow failure. He did not turn when Mirabel's boots rang on the deck planking. He simply spoke, and the voice reached across twenty years like weather.

"You came," Aldous Vane said. "I confess I gave it fifty-five percent."

"You under-rated me," Mirabel said, and walked out onto the span.
