## 18. Demolition Charge

The trestle at Kilometer Forty had been dying for thirty years, and I was the only one on either bank who knew exactly how.

I'd walked it twice in my first week in Kellwater—officially, contractually, with a clipboard and a borrowed name—rapping the timbers with a knuckle the way my father taught me before I could read. Dead wood answers with a sound like a held breath released. The deck answered everywhere. But decks are replaceable. What mattered was below: four granite piers rising out of the gorge's throat, and the fifth—the middle one—sitting wrong. Sitting the way a drunk sits against a wall. The spring flood two years back had undercut it, scoured a hollow under its upstream face, and nobody had ever come to look because nobody was ever going to run trains over this line again.

Except they were. That was the whole point of the grant. Flood crews, dredging barges hauled up in sections, stone and cement by the ton. Everything the valley's drowning required would cross this span or nothing would. Vane's arithmetic had a chokepoint, and the chokepoint had a name: Kilometer Forty.

I stood at the north abutment with Sera's hand gripping my sleeve and the torn pages pressed flat against my chest inside my coat, and I did what I had spent eleven years training myself never to do. I stopped calculating an exit.

"Tell me you have another way," Sera said. Her voice was steady but her eyes weren't. She had walked half the night through the gorge after escaping Kellwater, and she still looked less afraid than I felt.

"I don't."

"You always have another way. You told me that in the inn kitchen. You said engineers are people who refuse to accept there's only one road."

"I lied," I said. "Or I was younger. Both." I turned to face her fully. "There are nine men coming up that road behind us. Reyes is in their truck. Vane is somewhere ahead of us or behind us—I don't know which anymore, and not knowing is the same as dead. If we run, they take the pages off my body and the valley drowns in August and no one will ever know why. If we stand here, I can end this line permanently. Not damage it. End it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything I've been running from since I was twenty-six years old."

She didn't let go of my sleeve. "Say it plainly, Mirabel. Whatever name you're wearing today. Say what you're about to do."

I almost laughed. Almost. "I'm going to commit a crime," I said. "A large one. In front of witnesses. And then I'm going to stop being invisible forever, whether it works or not."

* * *

Here is what an engineer knows that a soldier doesn't: structures want to fall. Every bridge ever built is a negotiation with gravity, and the negotiators get tired. A healthy trestle holds itself up through geometry—load carried down through trusses into piers into bedrock, every member pulling its neighbor toward balance. A sick one is already falling. It's only been talked out of it, year after year, by paint and habit and the absence of anyone heavy enough to argue back.

Kilometer Forty was sick in three places, and I had catalogued all of them like sins.

First: the undermined center pier. Scour had eaten maybe a third of its footing on the upstream side. It was carrying load it was no longer shaped to carry, and it was doing so only because the load had never changed. Trains were gone; the bridge had forgotten what weight felt like.

Second: the second pier from the south end had a shear crack running diagonally through its capstone, old mortar patched over it in a hurry sometime in the last decade by someone who wanted the inspection report to read *pass*. I'd found the patch on my first survey walk and noted it in my log with a professional little frown, back when noting things was the most dangerous thing I did.

Third—and this was the gift, this was the thing I'd lain awake turning over for two nights since the shrine, since the rockslide, since Reyes's empty boots in the mud where the land office men had loaded him into their truck—the third was the abutment itself. The north abutment, the one I was standing on, was anchored into cut shale. Shale fails in sheets. It doesn't crumble; it peels, along bedding planes laid down a hundred million years ago, waiting patiently for someone to give them permission.

You could bring this bridge down with dynamite. I didn't have dynamite. I had something better, if better means crueler and slower and requiring more nerve: I had the bridge itself. A structure this decayed doesn't need to be destroyed. It needs to be *asked*, firmly, in the right order, at the right joint, and it will do the rest out of sheer eagerness.

All I needed was mass, applied suddenly, at the center span, from above.

And rolling down the gorge road behind me, at this moment, in a convoy of two trucks and a jeep, came nine men and one prisoner and approximately four tons of government steel.

* * *

Sera saw it before I explained it, because she was smarter than me in the ways that mattered. "You're going to make them chase you onto it," she said.

"I'm going to make them *want* to chase me onto it." I was already moving along the abutment, testing deck boards, choosing my path the way you choose words in a language you're about to speak in public for the first time. "They have Reyes. They think they have the situation contained—one fugitive engineer, one innkeeper who should be in a cell in Kellwater, one packet of papers that matters only if it reaches the valley council. They're confident. Confidence is a load rating. I intend to exceed it."

"And if they don't take the bait? If they shoot from the south bank?"

"Then I've lost nothing but the element of surprise, and I'll improvise." I hated saying it because it was true and improvisation was how men like me died. "But they won't shoot. Think like them for ten seconds—it's disgusting, but do it. They need me alive. Vane named me as his witness; killing me creates a martyr with documents already copied in three places. Nadya saw to that. No—they'll want to walk me off this bridge in handcuffs, quietly, and have me sign a retraction with a gun against my spine. They want the *performance* of my surrender. So they'll follow me out onto the span, at least partway, because that's where cornered men go when the far bank is blocked and the near bank is theirs."

"The far bank isn't blocked."

"It will be." I pointed south, across the gorge, to where the road climbed out of the river cut toward the valley. "Nadya's people. She sent word downriver at dawn—two dozen of them, stones, the same trick they used at the shrine but bigger. By now the switchbacks above the south approach are closed. The convoy knows it too; that's why they're hurrying. This bridge is the only way across the gorge for thirty kilometers in either direction, and they believe it's theirs."

Sera was quiet for a moment. Wind came up the gorge, smelling of wet granite and the distant green rot of the coming monsoon. Eleven days out, maybe ten now. You could feel the storm the way animals feel earthquakes—a pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the light.

"And you?" she said finally. "Where do you plan to be when your own trap closes?"

That was the question, wasn't it. That was the whole question, the one I'd been avoiding since the tunnel, since the camp, since the ledger of small debts Sera had collected from me one bowl of soup at a time. Every plan has a man-shaped hole in it where the planner is supposed to stand afterward, and most plans fail because the planner lies about the hole.

I didn't lie. "On the north third of the span," I said. "Past the cracked pier. When the center goes, the collapse propagates southward—crack first, then scour, then the rest follows like dominoes falling uphill. The north abutment is anchored in shale, and shale peels in sheets, and I'm going to be standing on the sheet that peels last." I heard how it sounded. I said it anyway. "It's a calculation, Sera. Not a prayer. There's a margin. It's thin, but it exists, and I've spent my whole life living inside margins thinner than this one."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be. It's supposed to be honest, which is the thing I owe you that I haven't paid yet." I took the compass out of my pocket—my father's compass, brass worn to gold at the edges, needle trembling toward a source it had been pointing at since chapter one of whatever this story was. "When this is done, if I'm standing anywhere at all, I'm walking upstream. All the way. And you're welcome to walk beside me, but I won't ask you to, because what comes next isn't hiding and it isn't heroism. It's just the bill arriving."

She kissed me once, hard, on the mouth, the way you'd stamp a document. Then she went to do the thing I'd asked her to do, which was climb to the high rocks above the north abutment with Nadya's signal mirror and become, for the next hour, the most important witness in the valley.

* * *

They came at four in the afternoon, when the gorge light turns the color of old paper and every shadow doubles its length.

I watched them from the shadow of the abutment's retaining wall: two trucks, a jeep, nine men, and Reyes—visible between the cab and the canvas of the lead truck, bound at the wrists, sitting upright with the terrible composure of a man who has decided to be dignified about whatever happens next. He caught sight of me on the rocks and did not smile, did not wave, did not do anything except turn his head slightly, once, like a man confirming a fact he'd already accepted. It was the bravest thing I saw all day, and the day contained a bridge falling down.

The senior land office man was called Dray. I'd never learned his name until Reyes told me in the drowned tunnel, and now I couldn't unknow it. He got out of the jeep with the unhurried movements of a man whose paperwork always arrived before his problems did, and he looked up at the rocks where Sera stood with her mirror catching the sun, and he made a decision I could see him making from two hundred meters away: he decided I was trapped, and that trapped men negotiate, and that negotiations were conducted by him.

"MIRABEL ISHIKAWA," he called up, using my real name like a weapon, which was the first honest thing anyone from the land office had done in months. "YOU ARE STANDING ON GOVERNMENT PROPERTY. YOU ARE IN POSSESSION OF STOLEN DOCUMENTS. COME DOWN AND THIS REMAINS AN ADMINISTRATIVE MATTER."

Administrative matter. As if Reyes's broken leg were a filing error. As if the crew chief who swallowed a key detail rather than surrender it had died of indigestion.

I stepped out onto the deck.

I made sure they saw me do it. I walked to the center of the north span—not far, forty meters of rotten timber between me and the abyss, but far enough that the deck flexed under my weight with a groan that carried across the gorge like a throat clearing before a speech. I put the torn pages down on a tie in front of me, weighted them with a stone, and stepped back.

"There," I shouted. "The original annotations. Vane's own hand. The boundary stone was cut in the year the grant claims it was surveyed, and the tool marks are machine-made, and machines like that didn't exist in this district for another six years. Your entire flood scheme rests on paper older than the pen that wrote it. Come and take it. It's right here. I'm right here. Everything you want is standing on this bridge."

Dray conferred with his men. I watched the conference the way I'd once watched gauge needles: looking for the reading, not the performance. Two of them started forward onto the deck. Dray waved them back and came himself, alone, hands visible, walking with the measured confidence of a man who believed bridges obeyed governments.

He was fifteen meters onto the span when Vane stepped out of the trees on the south bank.

I hadn't known he was there. Later—much later, in a cell, in a confession, in the wreckage of everything—he would tell me he'd driven ahead of the convoy specifically to watch, because some part of him needed to see whether the boy he'd made would really do it. But in the moment all I knew was that my mentor, my father-in-everything-but-blood, the man whose signature had bought a valley's drowning, was standing at the foot of the bridge I was about to destroy, and our eyes met across forty years and eighty meters of dead timber, and he shook his head.

One shake. Small. *Don't.*

I had imagined this moment a hundred times since the archive, since the grant, since the cold arrival of his signature on the page. In every imagining I had something to say to him. Standing on the span, I discovered I had nothing. There was no sentence in any language that could reach a man who had done the arithmetic and believed it. Ten thousand downstream lives against one valley of four hundred. He'd shown me that math when I was nineteen, on a different project, with chalk, and I'd admired it. That was the tragedy. Not that he'd fallen. That he'd never fallen—that the forgery was, for him, simply the price of the sum, paid in someone else's currency.

"Come back, Mirabel," he called softly, and the wind carried it to me whole. "Come back down. Stay small. Small men survive. I taught you that."

"You taught me load paths," I called back. "Everything else I taught myself."

Dray reached the pages. He bent to pick them up. And that was the moment, that exact moment—his weight on the center span, both trucks idling on the south approach, Vane watching, Sera's mirror flashing twice from the high rocks, the monsoon ten days out and patient as geology—

That was when I jumped.

Not off the bridge. Onto it. I dropped from the low ledge where I'd been standing onto the deck at the north quarter-point, both heels together, full weight, onto the one tie I'd chosen at dawn, directly above the shear crack in the second pier's capstone. It was a blow worth perhaps ninety kilograms delivered through half a meter of drop. Against a healthy pier, nothing. Against a pier already cracked, already patched, already tired—

The crack spoke.

I heard it through my boots before I heard it through the air: a report like a rifle, deep in the masonry, the sound of a hundred million years of bedding planes being introduced to the present tense. The patched mortar blew outward in a fan of dust. The capstone shifted. Maybe a centimeter. Maybe two.

And the center span, which had been holding its breath for thirty years, exhaled.

I ran. I ran north along the deck with the structure failing behind me the way I had calculated it would fail, in the order I had calculated, at nearly the speed I had calculated—crack first, the second pier letting go with a grinding roar, the load leaping sideways to the scoured center pier, which took the redistributed weight for approximately four seconds, which was three seconds longer than I'd promised Sera and one second longer than I'd privately believed. The hollow under its footing filled with the sound of granite finding out it was alone. Then it went. The whole middle of the bridge dropped as one piece, deck and trusses and Dray and the pages and all, folding downward into the gorge with a slowness that was worse than speed, the way avalanches are slow, the way verdicts are slow.

Dray did not pick up the pages. I understood that in the air between one stride and the next, watching the center span tilt away beneath the man: the pages were falling with him, fluttering loose from under their stone, white rectangles turning over the void like the world's coldest confetti. Whether he grabbed for them or for the truss I couldn't say. It didn't matter. The water in the gorge was brown with pre-monsoon silt, and it took everything without comment.

The failure propagated southward exactly as drawn: pier after pier, each one dragged down by its neighbor's fall, the deck unzipping toward the south abutment where the trucks sat. The drivers were reversing, screaming, headlights swinging wild through the dust. The lead truck made it clear. The second did not—the south approach deck sagged under it, tilted, and slid the last ten meters into the gorge with the vehicle still aboard. I never learned whether anyone was in it. I have chosen, some nights, to believe no one was. Other nights I do the arithmetic honestly, the way Vane would, and I understand why he went mad.

The north abutment held. The shale peeled as predicted—in sheets, slowly, a shudder rather than a slide—and I stood on the last sheet with my arms out and my heart going like a trip-hammer and watched eighty years of engineering die in eleven seconds.

When the dust cleared, the gorge was open sky. Nothing crossed it. Nothing would ever cross it again. The road ended at a gap, and the gap was mine, and the flood crews' barges and cement and stone were stranded thirty kilometers south of the valley they had been built to drown.

Vane was still standing at the south end. Alone now. The convoy was a scatter of men shouting at radios that had no answer. He looked across the new emptiness at me—at the small figure on the far edge of a broken bridge, breathing hard, alive, visible—and he did not curse me. He took off his hat. That was all. An old man's gesture, older than either of us: bareheaded before a thing larger than himself.

Then he turned and walked into the trees, and I understood I would see him once more, and only once.

* * *

Sera came down from the rocks with the mirror still in her hand and found me sitting on the abutment with my legs hanging over a drop that used to be a bridge. She sat beside me. For a while neither of us spoke, because there are moments when speech is just noise trying to get between a person and what they've done.

"They'll call it sabotage," she said eventually. "Treason, maybe. They'll hang a number on it."

"They'll call it what it is," I said. "I chose it. Outside the law, outside the contract, outside every version of myself I built to stay safe. I blew up my old life with the span. Both piers went down together—I made sure of that much."

"Reyes," she said, and her voice broke on the name, just slightly. "He was in the lead truck. It made it across. We'll find him."

"We'll find him." I took out the compass. The needle swung, settled, pointed upstream—toward the source, toward the council, toward the valley that had moved stones off boundary markers for generations because paper was never the only kind of record. "Ten days until landfall. The pages are at the bottom of a gorge, but I have the coordinates memorized, Reyes has them memorized, and Nadya's people cached copies at the shrine before the rockslide. Three places. No single arrest erases it now. That was always the design. I just had to survive long enough to build it."

Sera looked at the gap in the world where Kilometer Forty used to be, and then at me, and something in her face shifted—the suspicion that had lived beside her warmth since the first bowl of soup finally setting down its burden.

"What's your real name?" she asked. "Your actual one. Not for the council. For me."

I told her. Saying it felt like setting a bone.

At dawn we started walking upstream, toward the source, toward the assembled elders, toward the testimony that would make one man's truth into a valley's record. Behind us, the land office men were stranded with their dead convoy and their radios full of static. Ahead of us, the river rose, patient, and the storm came on behind it, and somewhere in the trees an old man was deciding what he would trade for his soul.

We had ten days. For the first time in years, I intended to use every one of them in the open.
