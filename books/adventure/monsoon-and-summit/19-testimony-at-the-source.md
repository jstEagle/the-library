## 19. Testimony at the Source

The council fire was lit at dusk, because Nadya Kesh's people believed that truth spoken in darkness had farther to travel. Mirabel stood at the edge of the circle with the oilcloth packet under his arm and felt the weight of it change by the minute—first paper, then stone, then something closer to a live coal.

The valley had turned out. Not all of them; the old and the very young stayed in the terraced houses above the river, but the firelight caught perhaps two hundred faces, and behind them the dark shoulders of the gorge, and above everything the smell of wet stone and coming rain. The monsoon was four days out. He could feel it in his teeth the way he'd once felt an unstable grade through the soles of his boots.

Nadya stood at the fire's edge and did not raise her voice. She had never needed to.

"We have been a valley that remembers," she said. "That is all we have ever been able to be. The grants come. The maps come. The men with instruments come. And we remember, and we move the stones at night, and we wait for the men with instruments to leave." She turned her head slightly, toward Mirabel. "This one did not leave."

Two hundred faces turned with her. Mirabel had stood before boards of inquiry before—three of them, after the Kessel Line collapse, before his name became a thing to be buried. He knew the physiology of judgment. This was not that. No one here was waiting to condemn him. They were waiting to hear whether the thing they had always known was finally written down somewhere they could not be made to un-know it.

He walked to the fire.

He had rehearsed a speech on the climb up from the shrine, and on the night after the rockslide, and in the grey hour before dawn when Reyes's absence sat in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed. The speech was gone now. What remained was simpler.

"My name is Mirabel Ishikawa," he said.

The words came out of him like a tooth coming free—pain and relief in the same motion, a hollow left behind that air rushed into. Six years. Six years of *Mr. Corren*, of *the surveyor*, of answering to syllables that belonged to no one. He said the name again because the first time his voice had almost failed. "Mirabel Ishikawa. Railroad engineer. I was licensed in the coastal districts. I lost my license because I told the truth about a bridge, and the men who built the bridge were better at telling stories than I was. So I came here to be nobody."

He held up the oilcloth packet.

"Inside are the torn pages of the government survey journal. Field notes. Boundary measurements. And annotations in the hand of Aldous Vane—my teacher, my mentor, the finest engineer I have ever known—admitting that the land grant for this valley is forged. Backdated. The boundary stone your people have watched for three generations was set *after* the grant it was supposed to prove. They wrote the paper first and then buried the stone and then dated the paper to make the stone a witness." He let that sit. "I have read the grant. I have read these pages. I am a licensed engineer—was a licensed engineer—and I will put my name to the finding: the grant is false, and the flood scheme it authorizes is not flood control. It is erasure. They intend to drown this valley and call it hydrology."

An old man near the fire—Mirabel had learned his name was Davor, keeper of the shrine—spoke without standing. "And your name. Will it hold? Names have been put to paper over this valley before. Paper burns."

"That is why I'm not giving it to paper," Mirabel said. "I'm giving it to you."

He opened the packet and read. He read for the better part of an hour, by firelight, while the valley listened: Vane's annotations, the chief's marginalia, the coordinates Reyes had carried in his skull for a month, the measurements of the ruins beneath the terraces that predated every grant in the district archive by four hundred years. When he finished, his voice was raw and the fire had burned down to a red heart.

Nadya came forward. She took the pages from him—not reaching, but holding out her hands until he placed them there—and she looked at the crowd.

"Three places," she said. "As we have always done. As the seed grain is kept. As the names of the dead are kept."

* * *

They worked through the night, and Mirabel worked among them, and it was Nadya's hands that directed everything.

Three copies. Not transcripts—Nadya did not trust a scribe's tired eyes at midnight—but the originals divided, each page photographed twice by the valley's one ancient camera, the negatives wrapped in waxed cloth and sealed in tin. One set went into the shrine above the gorge, into the cavity behind Davor's stones, where the pages had already survived one attempt at erasure. One set went downriver, sewn into the lining of a grain sack that would travel with a woman named Petra to her sister's house three valleys away, in a district where the land office's writ ran thin. The third set went up. Into the high caves where the valley's people had sheltered in the bad years, where Nadya herself would carry it, because she said—and Mirabel heard her say it to Davor, not for his benefit—"if they come for the valley, they will come for me first, and I would rather they find it with me than find it without."

No single arrest, she had told him. No single fire. No single flood.

Mirabel copied the pages a fourth time himself, in his own hand, and kept that copy in his shirt against his skin. He did not say so to Nadya. He suspected she knew.

Near dawn, while the tin was being sealed, she came and stood beside him at the shrine's mouth. The gorge below was turning from black to grey. Somewhere downriver, days away, the land office men were moving, and somewhere beyond them the storm front was moving faster.

"You gave your name to two hundred people tonight," Nadya said. "Do you understand what that does?"

"It makes me findable."

"It makes you *witnessed*." She looked at him with the flat patience she applied to everything, the patience of a woman who had spent her life watching water and knowing it would arrive exactly when it arrived. "A man alone with a document is a problem to be solved. A man whose name is known by two hundred people, whose testimony they have heard with their own ears—that man is a *record*. You stopped being a man tonight, engineer. You became an entry."

"I'd rather be a man."

"Yes," she said. "That is what everyone says. And then the flood comes anyway, and the ones who survive are the ones who became entries." She touched the sealed tin once, lightly, the way another woman might touch a child's head. "Reyes. The young one they took. When they took him, did he give them the coordinates?"

Mirabel's jaw tightened. "I don't know."

"Then assume he did, and assume they searched the shrine and found nothing, and assume that made them angry." She turned to go, then stopped. "The man who signed the forgery. Vane. Your teacher." She said the word *teacher* the way she said all words, without ornament. "You wrote him a letter. Davor told me."

"I offered him the chance to confess first. Before I delivered this."

"And?"

"A reply came yesterday. Three lines. *Come to the old line's last bridge, alone.*"

Nadya was quiet a moment. "Alone," she repeated. "Men who want to confess do not usually insist on the word alone. Men who want to bury you insist on it." She looked out at the lightening gorge. "But I have been wrong about men before. Once." She left him there with the tin and the dawn and the word *alone* ringing in his skull like a struck rail.

* * *

Reyes, they told him later, did not cry when they put him in the convoy truck.

That was not entirely true. He cried, briefly, at the moment the rockslide sealed the road behind the truck—cried because the slide meant Mirabel had escaped, and because escape was the one outcome he had been praying for and the one he could not witness. After that he was calm. He had a month of practice at being calm in the custody of men who needed him alive but not whole.

The land office men—Kroll, the wide one, and Vess, the quiet one who did all the talking—put him in a room in the district depot with a table, a lamp, and a pitcher of water, and Vess sat down across from him with the unhurried air of a man who had done this many times and had never once been in a hurry.

"Tomas Reyes. Junior engineer, second survey team." Vess opened a folder. "Your family is in the coastal districts. Mother, father, a sister at the teachers' college. Nice people, I'm told. I've never met them. I mention them only because the conversation we're about to have goes better when everyone understands the shape of it."

Reyes said nothing.

"You're going to tell me what the engineer—Ishikawa, we know his name now, isn't that something—what he has, where he's taken it, and who in this valley is holding copies. And then you're going to sign a statement, and then you're going to go home, and in a year this will be a strange story you tell at dinner." Vess poured water into two glasses, pushed one across. "Or we can have the other conversation. I don't like the other conversation. It's tedious for both of us and it ends with your sister's college hearing a rumor about her brother."

Reyes looked at the water. His leg, badly set and badly healing, throbbed in a rhythm he had learned to think around. He thought about Mirabel carrying him down the gorge on a litter made of survey staves and rope, day after day, never once complaining, feeding him first from a supply that was running out. He thought about the crew chief, Halloway, saying *memorize it, boy, paper burns, skulls are harder* the night before the escort took him away.

He thought about his mother, and the thought did what it was designed to do.

"Ishikawa has the pages," Reyes said. "The torn ones. Vane's annotations."

Vess smiled and wrote nothing down. "And where?"

"I don't know where he's taken them. He didn't tell me. He wouldn't." That was true. "But the copies—" and here Reyes made the decision that he would carry for the rest of his life, the decision that was either betrayal or the only loyalty left to him, and he made it the way Halloway had taught him to make every decision: by arithmetic. "The valley people keep everything three times. Shrine, downriver, high caves. That's their way. If you take one copy they'll still have two, and they'll know which one you took, and they'll move the other two."

Vess stopped writing.

"Three places," he repeated softly.

"That's all I know. I swear it's all I know."

Vess sat back. Outside the room's small window the district depot yard was grey with pre-storm light, and somewhere in that yard a radio was forecasting landfall in four days. Vess looked at the window for a long moment, and Reyes watched him do arithmetic of his own.

"Three places," Vess said again. "Then we don't need the pages. We need the people." He closed the folder. "Thank you, Mr. Reyes. You've been more helpful than you know."

They took the water glass away when he left. Reyes sat alone in the room with the lamp and understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that he had just told the land office how to make the valley's record into a list of arrests—and that the only thing standing between that list and two hundred names was a broken-legged liar's hope that they would believe three places was all there was.

He had not told them about the fourth copy. He did not know about the fourth copy. But he had gambled that a man like Mirabel Ishikawa would keep something back, and he sat in the lamplight and prayed to no one in particular that he had gambled right.

* * *

Sera Oduya reached the valley on the third day, on the last dry hour before the first outriders of the monsoon came up the gorge like a dark tide.

She had walked nine days on a route the town's hunters had drawn for her on a scrap of flour sacking, and she arrived at the terraces with her boots ruined, her hands cut from the scree, and a canvas bag containing every document the town of Kellwater had quietly collected against the land office for twenty years—tally sheets, tax demands, eviction notices, the small paper trail of a slow dispossession. The town had given them to her at the bridge at Kilometer Forty, all of them, pressed into her hands by men who had never once said aloud what they all knew.

She found Mirabel at the council fire, which was burning again in daylight now, in defiance of the rain just beginning to fall. He looked up and saw her and for a moment the engineer's careful face simply came apart, and he crossed the mud and took her by both arms as if to confirm she was load-bearing.

"You're supposed to be in Kellwater."

"I was detained," she said. "Briefly. And then I wasn't." She looked past him at the fire, at the elders, at Nadya Kesh watching her with an expression of frank assessment. "The land office has Reyes. The hunters saw the convoy on the lower road. And Vess is not coming to negotiate, Mirabel. I heard them in the depot before I got out. They're not coming for the pages." She set the canvas bag down in the mud between them. "They're coming for the people. Every name that heard yours. They mean to arrive with the flood and let the water do the paperwork."

The rain thickened. Above the valley the summit had disappeared entirely into cloud, and somewhere up in that cloud was the source of the river, and somewhere beyond the source was the storm front, and it was coming down on all of them at the speed of weather.

Mirabel looked at the canvas bag, and at Sera, and at the valley that had just made him a record—and understood that a record, to survive, sometimes needed someone willing to blow things up.

"The bridge," he said quietly. "The old line's last bridge is the only road in. Vane asked me to meet him there. Alone."

Sera read his face the way she'd read every face that ever sat at her bar. "And what are you going to do?"

He watched the rain come down the gorge toward the boundary stone, and he said: "I'm going to answer his letter."
