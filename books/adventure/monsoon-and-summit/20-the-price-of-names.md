## 20. The Price of Names

The news came to Kellwater the way all news came—late, bent, and carrying someone else's fingerprints.

Sera Oduya heard it first from the tinker, who had come up the valley road with his mule and his usual complaints about the toll at the ford, and who stopped mid-sentence when he saw the notice nailed to the district office door. She was carrying a crate of lamp oil across the square and she set it down in the dust without thinking about it, because the notice had Vane's name on it, and under Vane's name it had another name, and that name was not the one Mirabel Ishikawa had been living under for three years.

*Witness for the Crown: M. Ishikawa, civil engineer.*

She read it twice. Then a third time, because her eyes kept sliding off the word *witness* as if it were written in a language she'd only half learned. Around her the square went on with its morning—the baker's boy hauling his trays, two women arguing over the price of salt—but Sera stood in the middle of it feeling the ground tilt under her the way it tilts on a riverbank when the water takes a little more of it every year.

Aldous Vane had confessed. Publicly. In the capital, before the Commission of Inquiry, before reporters, before whatever machinery the land office used to grind men into paperwork. He had stood up in a room full of people whose whole profession was disbelief and said: *I falsified the grant. I backdated the boundary stone. The survey team found my forgery and I let them be silenced.* And then he had given them one name to hold against his own.

Not his accomplices'. Not the officials who had done the silencing while he did the signing. Just the witness. Just the man who could prove he was telling the truth.

"He's bought himself a cell," the tinker said beside her. He was squinting at the notice through spectacles thick as bottle bottoms. "And bought your friend a shield. That's the trade, isn't it? You can't arrest a man the Crown has named as its own witness. Not legally. Not cleanly."

Sera didn't answer. She was thinking about the night she had lied to two land office men with a sketch of Mirabel's face in their hands, and how she had done it without deciding to, the way you catch a falling cup without thinking—and how afterward she had lain awake wondering what it meant that a stranger's life had become hers to spend. Now here was an old man three hundred miles away spending his own life the same way. Without being asked. Without asking.

"Oduya," the tinker said. "You've gone white as flour."

"I'm fine," she said, which was a lie, and she picked up the crate and carried it back to the inn because her hands needed work or they would shake where people could see.

* * *

The inn was empty in the middle hours of the day, and Sera used the emptiness the way she used everything: carefully. She sat at the corner table where Mirabel had eaten his soup that first night, trading her fragments for his, and she laid out what she knew like a hand of cards.

He had gone upriver. That much the town knew, or thought it knew; Kellwater's silence was a kind of currency and everyone spent it discreetly. The land office men had gone upriver after him and had not come back down—at least not by the road, not with their convoy intact. There had been a rockslide in the gorge, the mule drivers said, a whole slope of it, timed like a held breath released. And there had been a bridge. Everyone in the district knew about the bridge, because the bridge at Kilometer Forty had stood for sixty years and now it lay in the river like a dead animal, its piers cracked open along seams no inspector had ever flagged, and the government crews sent to rebuild the access road were sitting on the far bank with nothing to do but write reports.

And now this. A confession. An arrest. A name spoken aloud in a courtroom by a man who had taught that name's owner everything he knew, and who had chosen, at the end, to teach him one thing more.

Sera turned her cup slowly on the table. She had never met Aldous Vane. She knew him only as Mirabel knew him—a voice in letters, a set of standards drilled into a young engineer until they became his spine: *measure twice, sign nothing you haven't walked, the line serves the country, not the country the line.* She had listened to Mirabel talk about him exactly once, late, after too much of her father's plum brandy, and what she remembered was not affection but something rawer—a boy's faith, still standing long after the boy should have outgrown it, the way a fence post stands after the field around it is sold.

That was why the confession would cost so much. Not the arrest. Arrests end. It was that Vane had waited until the last possible hour to be the man Mirabel had always believed he was, and had made sure Mirabel would know it, and would carry it.

"Grief," her mother used to say, "is just gratitude with nowhere to put itself."

Sera wiped the table that didn't need wiping and understood, finally, what the old woman had meant.

* * *

Reyes came down from the valley on the fourth day, riding in a cart borrowed from Nadya Kesh's people, his broken leg splinted and propped on a grain sack, his face thinner than she remembered and ten years older than it had any right to be. The valley folk brought him as far as the ford and no farther—they did not, Sera gathered, leave their boundary stones lightly even now—and it was Sera herself who helped him down from the cart, because the driver had a bad shoulder and because she wanted to hear it from him before anyone else dressed it up.

They sat in the inn's kitchen, away from the common room, and Reyes held a cup of tea in both hands like a man warming himself at a fire that might go out.

"They're calling it the Kilometer Forty Inquiry," he said. "Half the papers are carrying it. Vane's testimony runs four pages in the *Gazette*. Four pages, Oduya. I've read them until I can recite them." His mouth twisted. "He doesn't excuse himself. That's the worst part. He lays it out like a survey—bearings, dates, the original grant, the forged one, the difference in the stone's weathering that we found. He says, *I did this, and here is how you can check my work.* Like he's teaching a class."

"And Mirabel?"

"Named as witness. Which means—" Reyes shifted, winced at his leg, went on. "Which means the land office can't touch him. Not openly. They tried to have the valley council declared unlawful, you know, and the Commission threw it back in their faces, because you can't call a community illegal when its existence is the evidence. So instead they're doing what they always do. Filing things. Waiting. Building a case against him sideways—destruction of infrastructure, they're saying, about the bridge. As if a condemned trestle was a cathedral." He drank. "But a named witness of the Crown stands behind a wall even they can't knock down quickly. Vane knew that. He built the wall himself and then walked into the cell next to it."

Sera looked at her hands. Flour under the nails again, though she hadn't baked since morning. "Does Mirabel know yet?"

"The valley sent a runner the day the news came. And—" Reyes hesitated, and she saw him decide to give her the rest of it. "There's a letter. Vane wrote it from custody. One page. Mirabel got it two days ago." He watched her face. "I haven't read it. But I've seen a man read a letter like that before, and it isn't reading. It's being read."

"What does it say?"

"I told you. I haven't read it." He set down the cup. "But he asked me one question after, and I'll give you that much, because I think you've earned it. He asked me whether a man could accept a gift that cost the giver everything, or whether accepting it was just another way of taking."

Sera was quiet a long moment. Outside, the light was going amber on the mountains, and somewhere down the street the smith was closing up, hammer ringing once, twice, then nothing.

"And what did you tell him?"

"That I'm not the one to ask. I took the easy bargain myself—kept my head down, memorized my coordinates, survived." His laugh had no humor in it. "But I'll tell you what I think, innkeeper, since you fed me soup when I was nothing but a smell and a fever. I think the letter wasn't a gift. I think it was a lesson. The last one. Vane spent thirty years teaching him how to build things that hold, and now he's showing him what it costs when the foundation was rotten from the start. You don't get to accept that lesson or refuse it. You just get to build better than he did."

* * *

Mirabel came down from the valley himself eight days later.

She saw him from the kitchen window before she properly saw him—a figure on the valley road, walking, alone, with the particular gait of a man who has been climbing for days and whose body has stopped arguing about it. Even at that distance she knew him by the compass. He had taken to holding it as he walked, she'd been told, not checking it, just holding it, thumb over the face, the way another man might carry a stone from his father's grave.

He came into the inn through the back, into the kitchen, because he was still a man who entered buildings by the door nobody watched. He looked terrible. He looked, Sera thought, better than he had any right to look—cleaner somehow, as if the weeks upriver had scoured something off him that soap wouldn't have touched.

"You've seen the papers," he said. Not a question.

"Four pages in the *Gazette*," she said. "Reyes recites them in his sleep."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where one used to live. He sat down heavily at the kitchen table, and she put food in front of him without asking what he wanted, because that was the arrangement between them and had been since the first bowl of soup, and some arrangements don't need renegotiating.

For a while he ate and she worked and neither of them spoke, and the kitchen filled up with the ordinary sounds of an evening—water heating, wood settling, the town outside going dark window by window.

"He signed his confession with his left hand," Mirabel said at last. She stopped wiping the counter. "Did you know that? The right one shakes too badly now. Thirty years of drafting and the hand's gone, and he signed the most important document of his life with the wrong hand, badly, and the clerk made him do it twice." He stared at his plate. "He apologized to the clerk."

"Mirabel."

"I keep doing the arithmetic." His voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man walking a narrow gauge. "Ten thousand downstream, he said. That was always the number. Ten thousand lives against one valley, and the valley loses, and the arithmetic holds, and the arithmetic is monstrous, and I cannot make it stop being both true and monstrous at the same time. That's what he wanted me to inherit. Not the guilt. The equation."

"And what did you decide?"

He looked up at her then, and she saw that the weariness in his face had a floor under it now, something solid, and she understood that whatever the letter had contained, he had already answered his own question somewhere on the mountain, alone, and was only now telling her the shape of the answer.

"I decided the equation was wrong at both ends. You don't save ten thousand by drowning five hundred. You build the levee. You move the town if you must—you pay, you ask, you help them carry. There was always a third column in the ledger and he refused to look at it because looking at it meant the sin wasn't necessary, and if it wasn't necessary then it was just a sin." He pushed the plate away, half finished. "So no. I don't forgive him. But I'm going to testify for him. Both things. At the same time. I owe him that much and I don't owe him a spoonful more."

Sera came and sat down across from him, which she almost never did; the innkeeper's side of the table was a wall she maintained the way the valley maintained its stones. But tonight she crossed it.

"There's something else in the papers," she said. "Small item. Bottom of page six, where they bury things they want printed but not read." She slid the folded sheet across. "The Commission has ordered the original grant unsealed. Full provenance review. All the way back to the first survey." She tapped the paragraph. "And they've requested a certified engineer to authenticate the boundary evidence. On site. In the valley."

Mirabel read it. She watched his eyes move down the lines and stop, and stop again, and she saw the moment the words rearranged themselves from news into summons.

"They want me to walk the line," he said quietly. "Officially. Under my own name."

"It appears," Sera said, "that somebody up there agrees with you about the third column."

He sat very still. Through the window the peaks were going violet, and high up, where the river came down from its forbidden source, the snowfields caught the last light and held it like a promise. Somewhere in a cell in the capital an old man with a shaking left hand had traded everything he had for one clean fact entered into the record, and the record, Mirabel had taught her once, was the only thing that outlasted everyone who wrote it.

"When do they need me?" he asked.

"The warrant of request says within the month." She paused. "Before landfall season ends. Before the water comes down and moves the stones for them."

Mirabel folded the paper along its existing creases, precisely, the way he did everything, and put it inside his coat over his heart, where a man keeps the documents he intends to act on.

"Then I'd better start measuring," he said.
