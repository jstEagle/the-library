## 21. Landfall

The wind changed on the ninth day.

I felt it before I heard it—a shift in the pressure that made my ears ache the way they used to ache in the old tunnels, the way they ached when a bore was about to give. I was up on the ridge above the valley with a theodolite borrowed from Nadya's stores, checking the sightlines one last time, when the air went still and heavy as wet wool. Below me, the whole valley seemed to pause. The river ran low and clear over its stones, running like a man who doesn't yet know the letter has been posted.

Then, far to the south, a sound like a freight train that never arrives.

"Storm front," Reyes said beside me. He'd climbed the ridge on his mended leg, refusing the litter, refusing everything except the right to see it himself. He had a walking stick Nadya's grandson had cut for him and a stubbornness that had kept him alive in a drowned tunnel for eleven days, and he stood now with his weight on the good leg and his face turned south, reading the sky the way I once read gauge plates. "It'll hit the high country first. Twelve hours, maybe less. Then it comes down the gorge all at once."

"You sound certain."

"I've been counting since the shrine." He didn't smile. "It's what I had. Numbers and counting. When they took the others, I counted. When my leg broke, I counted. Now I count rain."

Below us, the valley was finishing its argument with the water. For three days the people had worked the defenses I had only drawn on paper—my old survey sheets, unrolled on Nadya's table and argued over line by line by men and women who had never read a contour map but knew every stone of this ground the way I would never know anything. They had cut the spill channel where I'd marked it, widened the old flood weir, stacked gabions along the lower terraces, and moved—stone by stone, in a slow procession that had gone on all night with lanterns—every movable thing above the line.

The line. That was the thing I kept coming back to, the thing I could not stop checking. The old boundary stone still stood where it had stood for two hundred years, a gray finger of granite at the valley's throat, its carved marks worn nearly smooth. Nadya's people had never moved it. They had only, generation after generation, moved the *other* stones—the false markers the land office set, the ones with the backdated grant behind them—nudging them downslope a hand's width at a time, so that on paper the valley shrank and shrank while the stone that mattered never moved at all.

The flood, when it came, would meet that stone. Everything depended on whether the water agreed with the granite.

* * *

We worked through the morning. I won't pretend I was calm. I checked the spill channel four times, walked the weir twice, and stood at the boundary stone so long that Nadya came and stood beside me without a word, the way you stand with a man at a bedside.

"You keep looking at it like it might lie to you," she said.

"It's a rock."

"It's the only honest thing the government never managed to forge." She touched the worn carving with two fingers, an old gesture, worn smooth as the stone itself. "My grandmother's grandmother fed it milk before the spring floods. I feed it tea. It has never once asked us for anything back. Can you say that of any signature you have ever trusted?"

I thought of Vane's signature, the elegant descending V of it, the signature that had taught me to draft and then signed away ten thousand people's ground. I thought of my own name, spoken aloud before the council five days ago—*Mirabel Ishikawa*—and how the valley had taken the name and simply written it into their record, no ceremony, no suspicion, as if names were only ever meant to be true.

"No," I said. "I can't."

"Then trust the rock." She turned to go, then paused. "The water is coming for us, engineer. It has been coming since before you were born. Today we find out if it remembers whose valley this is."

* * *

The first rain hit the high peaks at dusk.

We saw it from the valley floor—a gray curtain dropping over the summits, erasing them, and then the sound arriving minutes later, a vast low roar like the mountain clearing its throat. The wind came next, flattening the barley, tearing at the last of the defense works, and then the rain reached us and the world became water.

I have stood in monsoons before. I grew up with them, in the coastal districts, where the rain is a season and a rhythm and you plan your life around it the way you plan around the tides. This was different. This was the storm we had raced—Reyes and I, eleven days down a gorge with a broken man on a litter, the land office men a day behind us the whole way, the torn pages against my chest, the arithmetic of weather and pursuit compressed into a single countdown. We had won the race by four hours. Now the thing we'd been running from had caught up, and there was nothing left to do but stand and watch it work.

By midnight the river had tripled. I stood at the weir with a lantern that was more wish than light, watching the brown water climb the gauge stones I'd set myself, calling the readings to the men on the terrace, and every reading was a number I had already calculated and already feared. The channel held. The weir held. At two in the morning a gabion wall let go on the east bank with a sound like a door slamming in a giant's house, and for one black hour we hauled stone in the rain, forty people passing boulders hand to hand in the dark, and we rebuilt it before the water found the gap.

Sera worked beside me the whole time. She had arrived from Kellwater a week ago, soaked and furious and carrying a sack of dried fish and a letter she'd taken off a land office courier at considerable personal risk, and she had not once since asked me what we were doing here or whether it would work. She was not that kind of woman. She hauled stone, and she cursed the rain in the flat practical accent of the river towns, and when the gabion went she was the one who shouted the chain into order.

"If this fails," she shouted to me over the water, hauling a stone the size of her torso, "I want you to know I'm going to be extremely unpleasant about it."

"Noted."

"I keep a ledger of small debts, Mirabel Ishikawa. You owe me a great many bowls of soup."

"Put it on the account."

She almost smiled. Then the water surged, and there was no more talking.

* * *

Dawn came gray and blind, and the flood came down the gorge.

I had seen the numbers. I had drawn the hydraulics on Nadya's table and explained them until my voice gave out. Nothing I had calculated prepared me for the sight of it—the gorge simply filling, the river ceasing to be a river and becoming a moving lake, a brown sea walking upright through the mountains, carrying whole trees turned sideways like matchsticks, carrying boulders I could hear grinding against each other under the water like teeth.

The valley people stood on the high terraces in the rain and watched it come, and nobody ran. That was the thing I will carry to my own grave: nobody ran. Nadya stood at the front with her stick planted like a surveyor's rod. Reyes stood on his mended leg with his arm through mine, counting under his breath. Sera had my sleeve in her fist and did not appear to know it.

The flood hit the valley's throat at a few minutes past six.

The boundary stone took it first. I saw the water strike the granite finger and split around it—the old stone standing in the full weight of the gorge's anger, unmoved, the way it had stood through two hundred years of springs—and then the water poured into the spill channel we had cut, exactly where I'd marked it, exactly as calculated, and the channel took it and turned it, and the flood that should have drowned the lower terraces to a depth of four meters went instead down the old flood way and out across the stony flats where nothing lived and nothing needed saving.

The water stopped exactly where the old boundary stone stood.

Not a hand's width beyond it. Not a finger's length. The brown flood line ran along the granite like a rule drawn in ink, and on the near side of the line the barley stood bent but unbroken, and on the far side the valley's old flood plain did what flood plains do, which is drink.

For a long moment nobody on the terraces made a sound. The rain roared. The river roared. And then Nadya Kesh lifted her walking stick and struck it once against the stone beside her, a single dry knock, and the valley broke into a sound I had never heard before—not a cheer, nothing so simple—a long, low, rising noise, half shout and half song, the sound of ten thousand people who had been waiting two hundred years for a rock to be proven right.

Reyes was crying and counting at the same time. "Peak's passing," he said, voice cracking. "It's passing. Gauge says it's past the crest. It held. It *held*."

I stood at the boundary stone with the flood roaring a stone's throw away and felt something come loose in my chest that had been clenched since Kellwater, since the tunnel, since the day I first arrived under a borrowed name and decided that being nobody was the same as being safe. The water held the line. The line had held because people had refused, quietly, for generations, to let it move. And the papers—the grant, the signature, the whole elegant machinery of the lie—were sealed in three places now, in the shrine and the council house and the hands of a courier Nadya trusted, and no flood and no land office and no arrest could drown them all.

Sera's fist was still in my sleeve. I put my hand over it.

"You're smiling," she said, accusing.

"I'm an engineer. Something I built held."

"You didn't build the stone."

"No," I said. "But I built the channel. And for once in my life, I built it in my own name."

* * *

The flood took two days to fall. When it did, the valley went out to read the waterline the way other people read scripture—touching the silt mark on the boundary stone, walking the spill channel, finding the gabions we'd rebuilt in the dark still standing. Nadya's council met that evening in the long house, and for the first time the talk was not about survival but about what came after: the terraces to replant, the road to the low country, and—this was Nadya's doing, delivered with a look at me over the fire—a rail spur. A real one. An open one. A line surveyed in daylight, contracted in the valley's own name, to carry their barley and their record out to the world.

"Someone will have to survey it," she said, to the fire more than to me.

"I imagine so."

"They'd want a man who knows these mountains. A man who has already, as it happens, walked every contour." She turned then, and her eyes were the same dry gray as the boundary stone. "They'd want his real name on the contract. Not the borrowed one. The valley doesn't sign papers with ghosts."

The fire cracked. Outside, the storm was moving off east, and the air had that scoured, ringing quality it gets after a monsoon breaks—the whole world washed and new.

"I'd want to see the source," I said. "Before anything else. The river's source. I've followed it this far. I'd like to finish the survey."

"Then finish it," Nadya said. "The passes will be clear within the week."

Reyes, sprawled by the fire with his leg propped on a saddle, laughed for the first time since the tunnel. "He's going to climb it. I told you. The man found a compass pointing nowhere and couldn't leave it alone."

Sera leaned close, and under the council's talk she said, quietly, "You know they'll come asking. Vane's trial, the land office, all of it. They'll want the engineer who dropped the bridge."

"I know."

"And?"

I looked at the fire, and at the rain-washed dark beyond it, and at the compass in my hand—my father's compass, its needle finally pointing at something true.

"And I'll sign my name," I said. "It's mine again. I intend to get some use out of it."
