## 22. Monsoon and Summit

The contract was one page. That was the first miracle of it.

Mirabel Ishikawa read it twice at the table in the Kellwater land office—which was no longer the land office, but that was the second miracle—and set down the pen without signing.

"You'll want to read it a third time," Sera Oduya said from the doorway. "You're the type."

"I'm not the type anymore."

"You'll always be the type. It's why I let you keep the room." She came in carrying two cups of tea and set one at his elbow, close enough that the steam touched his wrist. "Read it again. Then sign it with the name on your father's compass case, and not the one on your old contract, and then we can both stop pretending I don't know both of them."

He read it a third time.

It was a simple document, simpler than anything he had ever been asked to sign in fourteen years of rail work. *The undersigned, Mirabel Ishikawa, licensed engineer, agrees to survey and map a proposed spur line from the valley of the source river to the junction at Kellwater, at the request and under the authority of the Kellwater Valley Council, for the purpose of connecting the valley's people to the lowland markets by their own consent.* No district seal. No land office letterhead. At the bottom, where the old contracts had carried the stamp of an office that had since been dissolved and three of whose officers had since been arrested, there was only the council's mark—a river line crossed by a mountain stroke, pressed in ink Nadya Kesh mixed herself from ash and river clay.

The paper was cheap. The authority behind it was the only kind he had ever fully trusted, and he had trusted it for less than two months.

"You're hesitating," Sera said.

"I'm savoring." He turned the page toward the window light. "Do you know how many contracts I've signed in my life? Dozens. Every one of them was a way of disappearing. Sign here, become the number. Sign here, become the man who does what the seal says. I signed the last one under a name I stole from a dead cousin, and I told myself it was survival, and it was, but it was also—" He searched for the word and found it had been waiting the whole time. "Convenient. Being nobody was convenient. Nobody gets arrested. Nobody gets hunted through a gorge. Nobody has to stand up in front of a council with his voice shaking."

"And now?"

"Now the paper has my real name on it, and the name is the whole point." He picked up the pen. "That's what I keep coming back to. Nadya said it the night of the testimony. She said, *a name is not a wound, it is a handle.* You can't carry water in a broken pot. You can't carry a debt without a name to owe it from."

"Is the debt paid, then?" Sera asked, and her voice was gentle in the way she was gentle, which was to say not gentle at all, only honest with its sleeves rolled up.

He thought about it honestly, because she deserved that and because he had spent enough of his life lying to people who fed him.

"The bridge is paid," he said. "The pages are paid. Vane—" He stopped. He had not said his mentor's name aloud in three weeks, not since the news had come down from the capital: Aldous Vane, confessed before a tribunal, stripped of his office, naming as his witness the engineer who had brought the forged grant to light. Naming him, and in the same breath asking the tribunal to note that the witness had written to him first, had given him the chance to confess on his own terms, and that the old man had chosen instead to summon him to a condemned bridge and argue arithmetic at him until the arithmetic fell down with the trestle.

Vane had taken the whole weight. That was the last thing he had ever taught Mirabel, and he had done it on purpose, and Mirabel would spend the rest of his life not knowing whether it was love or pride or both, which were, in a man like Aldous Vane, the same material poured into different forms.

"Vane is paid for," Mirabel said finally. "Whether he'd want to be, I don't know. The debt I mean is the other kind. The kind you owe the dead surveyors, and the valley, and—" he glanced up at her— "the innkeeper who lied to two men with a sketch of my face and then walked into a gorge after me with a mule and a rifle she'd never fired."

"You're welcome," Sera said. "Sign the paper."

He signed it. *Mirabel Ishikawa.* The letters came out steady, which surprised him. Fourteen years of writing a dead cousin's name had not, it turned out, worn the grooves of his own away. The pen knew the path. The hand remembered.

He blew on the ink, and outside the window the post-monsoon light lay across Kellwater like something newly washed—the town's tin roofs bright, the river running high and clean and loud, the air scoured down to its bones. Everything smelled of wet stone and cut grass. The storm had come, and the storm had lost, and the whole district was still quietly astonished by that fact, the way a man is astonished by his own pulse after a fever breaks.

* * *

Reyes was in the yard behind the inn, teaching Nadya's grandchildren to read a surveyor's chain, which was to say he was lying on a bench with his leg in its splint and shouting measurements while three children held a chain badly and argued about it.

"Forty-two links, not forty-one, are you measuring or guessing—there, that's it. Ishikawa! Tell them what a plumb bob is for."

"Telling the truth about which way is down," Mirabel said.

Reyes grinned. He had filled out since the gorge, color back in his face, the broken leg knitting straight under the valley bonesetter's care. He had wept the night of the testimony—Mirabel had seen it from the edge of the council fire, the junior engineer standing before the elders with his memorized coordinates and his survival shame, saying *I lived because I was small and they forgot me, and I am sorry, I am sorry*—and Nadya Kesh had answered him in front of everyone: *You lived to carry the memory. That is not nothing. That is the job.* He had slept for two days afterward, and woken up a different weight of man.

"The council approved the spur route," Reyes said, propping himself up. "I heard this morning. The low pass, not the gorge route."

"The gorge route would have saved six kilometers of grading."

"The gorge route goes through the shrine."

Mirabel nodded slowly. He had argued for the gorge route himself, two weeks ago, with the old engineer's reflex for the efficient line—and Nadya had walked him up to the shrine above the gorge, where the torn pages had waited in their stone cache through flood and pursuit and the fall of the trestle, and had said nothing at all, only stood there while he understood. Some things a line could go around. The map served the valley. The valley did not serve the map.

"The low pass," he agreed. "It'll cost more. It'll last longer. That's the whole trade, isn't it. It's always been the whole trade." He had spent his career, he realized, working for men who believed you could make the trade disappear—who believed you could drown a valley and call it arithmetic, backdate a stone and call it law. The spur would cost more because the truth cost more, and the truth was the only material that held.

"When do you start up?" Reyes asked. "The source survey."

"Tomorrow. I want one more day of maps."

"Take me."

"You can't walk to the gate."

"I can ride a mule and hold a stadia rod. Ishikawa." Reyes's voice dropped, went serious in the way it had learned to in the dark of the drowned tunnel. "I never saw the source. I was carried past it with a broken leg and a hood over my head, and I heard it, and that's all I know of it—a sound, through cloth. I'd like to see the thing I heard."

Mirabel looked at him for a long moment. "Day after tomorrow, then. We'll take it slow."

* * *

He spent the evening in his room at the inn, which was still his room, though he had stopped paying for it weeks ago and Sera had stopped pretending to charge him. The compass lay on the table where it had lain every night since the bridge. His father's compass. Brass case gone soft-edged with handling, the needle settling always, always to the same rest.

He had checked it against the surveyor's instruments a dozen times in the past weeks, out of an old habit he was only now ready to name. In the tunnel, on the river, in the gorge, he had trusted the dead team's bearing over his own father's needle, and the needle had never once disagreed. It pointed upriver. It had pointed upriver since Kellwater. He had told himself it was a defect, a bent card, a local pull of iron in the hills—anything but what it actually was, which was that his father had walked this district before him, thirty years ago, on the first survey, the honest one, the one whose line the forged grant had buried. He had found the old field notes in the council's recovered archive, his father's initials in the margin of the source valley pages, and beside them a note in a young hand: *Boundary stone here is older than any claim. Recommend the grant office be informed. They were not interested.*

His father had found it too. His father had been ignored, and had gone home, and had given his son a compass that pointed at the truth for the rest of its life.

Mirabel sat with that a long time, the lamp low, the river loud through the window.

Then he opened the compass case, and on the inside of the lid, in the space that had been blank his whole life—blank, he now understood, because his father had scraped the old engraving out and never replaced it—he took out a fine graver he had borrowed from the valley's clock-mender, and he cut two words into the brass.

*FOR THE LINE.*

Not his father's words. His own. The compass would go to no one after him, probably; he had no children, and the valley had its own marks to leave. But a tool should say what it was for. A man should too.

He closed the case. The needle spun once and settled, patient as ever, toward the source.

* * *

They left two days later, in the clear cold morning that follows the monsoon's retreat, when the whole world seems rinsed and hung out to dry.

Reyes rode the leading mule, splinted leg propped on a rolled blanket, a stadia rod lashed across his back like a banner. Behind him came two of Nadya's young people as chainmen—volunteers, the council's first employees, paid in salt and standing—and Sera, who had announced at dawn that the inn could run itself for a week, that Marta could cook, and that if Mirabel thought he was climbing to the source of the river without her he had learned less from the past three months than she had given him credit for.

"You're not on the contract," he said.

"Neither is the mule. The mule's coming."

They climbed through country the storm had rewritten—new channels cut through the scree, hillsides green past all believing, water standing in every hollow like coins. The old boundary stone stood where it had always stood, at the flood line, and the flood had stopped exactly at its base, to the hand's width, and no one in the valley considered this a coincidence, and Mirabel, an engineer, had run the levels himself and found that it wasn't. The valley's old defenses, the ones Nadya's people had maintained for generations in silence—terraces, diversion walls, a whole hidden hydrology of stones moved one at a time by people whose names were in no grant anywhere—had held. The paper had drowned. The stones had not.

He touched the boundary stone as they passed it, the way you touch a doorpost.

Above the stone the country opened, and by afternoon they could see the head of the valley, and by evening they made the last camp below the source itself—a spring-fed lake no wider than a threshing floor, ringed with white stones, giving off the river that ran down past the shrine and the gorge and the fallen trestle and the town and the sea. The water was so clear it seemed not to be there at all, only the lakebed, holding itself up.

They made camp without much talk. Sera built the fire. Reyes, propped against a stone, got out the field book and began, unbidden, to sketch the basin—the first page of the spur survey, the first line of the new map, drawn in a valley where every previous line had been drawn in secret or in service of a lie. Nadya's grandchildren would learn to read this page someday, Mirabel thought. Children would trace the contour lines with their fingers and learn that the map was made *for* them, by name, openly, by a man who signed his whole name.

He walked down to the water alone in the last light.

The summits across the valley stood bare of cloud for the first time since he had come to the district—every ridge sharp and present, snow on the far peaks burning orange, the air so clean it felt like a room with all the windows opened at once. Monsoon and summit. The two facts of the country, storm and stone, and for one season out of every year they were visible to each other, and everything in between lay washed and waiting.

Mirabel took out the compass and opened it and held it flat on his palm.

The needle did not strain. That was the thing he had never understood until now, standing at the place it had pointed to all along. It did not strain, or tremble, or pull. It simply lay there, aligned, at rest, doing with its whole small body the one thing it was made for. All those years he had thought of it as pointing *away*—away from Kellwater, away from the town, away toward some forbidden thing. It had never been pointing away from anything. It had been pointing home the entire time, and home had simply been upstream of everything he understood.

"Mirabel Ishikawa," he said aloud, to the water, to the summits, to his father wherever his father was. Just to hear it. Just to hear it in a place where the name cost nothing and meant everything.

The needle held steady.

Behind him, up at the fire, Sera laughed at something Reyes said, and the chainmen argued cheerfully about tomorrow's baseline, and the river began its long journey down toward all of it—the shrine, the stone, the bridge site where new piers would rise on honest footings, the town, the sea. He stood at the source with the compass in his hand and the contract in his pack and his name in his own mouth, and he understood that the survey had never really been of the land at all.

It was a map of what he owed. And starting tomorrow, line by line, he was going to draw the whole thing true.
