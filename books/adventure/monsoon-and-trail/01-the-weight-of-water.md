## 1. The Weight of Water

The river told you when the monsoon was coming, if you knew how to listen. Oscar Montrose had spent eleven years learning its language, and what it said now was *hurry*.

The Sengkang had gone the color of strong tea, swollen with rain from highlands he'd never see. Debris rode the current — branches, a drowned goat, half a stilt-house roof with a cooking pot still lashed to it somewhere upstream. He stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel*, one hand on the spokes, watching the water carry other people's lives past him at eight knots.

"Bar's moved again," Tam called from the bow, sounding the shallows with his pole. The old deckhand read the river better than any chart. "Sand's built up past the second bend. We'll want the east channel."

"You want the east channel," Oscar said.

"I want us not aground."

That was fair. The *Kestrel* drew too much water for her own good these days; her hull was weeping at a seam below the cargo line, and every trip she took on a little more of the river permanently. He'd patch her in Kajang, if the dry dock was free, if the price was right, if the monsoon held off another week. A long list of ifs, and Oscar had learned not to bet his life on any of them. Just his livelihood, which was nearly the same thing and cheaper to lose.

The cargo was rice and kerosene, machine parts and salt — the ordinary blood of the river trade — and behind it all, as always, the debt. Three hundred and forty dollars owed to Chua Brothers in Kajang, accruing interest like silt. His father's debt, technically. Everything was his father's, technically: the name Montrose, the shipping house that had rotted from the inside while old man Montrose drank himself respectable, the expectation that Oscar would come home and stand in the office and become a monument to respectability himself. Oscar had chosen instead to be a captain of one leaking boat with a crew of one and a half, running cargo ahead of the weather and one sandbar ahead of his creditors.

It wasn't a bad life. It required nothing from him but motion.

They made the second bend by late afternoon, riding the east channel through water the color of bruised sky, and that was where they found him.

A man on the mudbank, waving with both arms like a man signaling a rescue or surrendering to one. Thin as a heron, dressed in the rags of what had once been good canvas expedition gear, standing in the shallows with the water at his knees.

"Don't," Tam said.

"We're stopping."

"He could be anything. Sick, crazy, bait for river pirates—"

"He's drowning slowly." Oscar swung the wheel. "And we're the only boat stupid enough to be out here."

The man collapsed into the cargo well before they'd even finished hauling him over the gunwale, and up close he was worse than he'd looked from the water — sixty-odd years worn down to gristle, skin yellowed and hot as a stove top, lips cracked black. Fever. Bad one. He'd been out in the hills a long time, and the hills had sent him back with something eating him alive.

"Water," the man rasped, and then, when Oscar gave it to him, "Vann. Elias Vann." He said it like it should mean something. It didn't.

Oscar put him under the awning aft, out of the rain that had started coming down in fat, warm drops. Tam looked at Oscar over the man's body and didn't say *don't* again, because it was already too late for that word to do any good.

* * *

Fever is honest company. It doesn't perform. For two days, as the *Kestrel* pushed upriver against the rising water, Elias Vann burned and muttered and surfaced into lucidity only in fragments — an hour at dawn, twenty minutes at dusk — and in those fragments he talked.

He talked about jade.

Not the tourist trinket kind. Seam jade, boulder jade, the green stone that lay in veins in the mountains north of Kajang where the river narrowed and the maps went vague. Vann had been a prospector for thirty years, he said, most of them wasted, some of them with partners. He said the word *partners* the way a man spits out a fishbone.

"There's a seam," he told Oscar on the second dawn, propped against the cargo netting, eyes clear for the moment and burning anyway. "Two days' walk from the upper Kajang landing. I've walked it four times. I know where it lies like I know my own hands." He held up his hands. They shook. "Better than this, now."

"I'm a cargo man," Oscar said. "I haul rice. I don't haul rumors."

"It's no rumor." Vann fumbled inside his shirt and brought out an oilcloth packet, tied with waxed cord, worn soft as cloth from years against his chest. "Map. Survey notes. Assays. Enough to make a man rich enough to buy the river."

Oscar looked at the packet and felt the specific cold he always felt around other people's fortunes. Fortunes came with hooks in them. He'd watched men drown chasing gold in the shallows upstream; he'd watched his father drown slower, in an office, chasing a name.

"What do you want for it?"

"Not money." Vann laughed, and it turned into coughing, and the coughing left him gray. "There's a girl. Lena. My daughter. She keeps the clinic at the Kajang post." He stopped, gathered himself. "We haven't spoken in nine years. I chose the hills over her mother, and then the hills over everything else. She won't want anything of mine. I know that. But the map has to reach her. Not sold to her — given. What she does with it after, burn it, sell it, throw it in the river, that's hers. But it has to pass through her hands first. That's all I ask."

"And why me?"

"Because you were going there anyway. Because you owe nobody and nobody owes you — I've watched you two days, captain, you have the look of a man who owes nobody." Vann smiled with cracked lips. "And because you're the only fool on this river who stopped."

Oscar turned the oilcloth packet over in his hands. It weighed almost nothing. That was the trap of it — things that heavy always weighed almost nothing at first.

"I don't make promises," he said. "Promises are anchors. I deliver cargo. You want to pay freight, I'll take the packet to Kajang and hand it to whoever answers to Lena Vann. That's business. That I can do."

"That's not—" Vann started, and the fever took him again, dragging him under mid-sentence, and Oscar sat there holding a dead man's fortune and feeling like a man who'd picked up a rope without checking what was on the other end.

Tam found him there an hour later.

"Well?"

"He wants me to promise."

"To a dying man." Tam shook his head slowly. "You know what my grandmother said about promises? She said a promise is a debt you agree to before you know the size of it." He nodded at the packet. "That one looks big."

Oscar looked upriver. The rain had thickened into a steady gray curtain, and the water kept climbing the banks, patient as a creditor. Two more days to Kajang, maybe three if the channel shifted. Behind him, downriver, the monsoon would close the passes within the week, and whatever he didn't finish now wouldn't be finished until the dry season — months away, months of moored boat and mounting interest and sitting still, which was the one thing Oscar Montrose had built his whole life never to do.

Everything argued for hurrying. Everything except the man under the awning, breathing in shallow sips, burning through his last fuel.

* * *

Elias Vann died in the third hour of the fourth night, between one wave slap and the next, so quietly that Tam only noticed when he went to check the bilge pump and heard nothing from under the awning but the rain.

Oscar crouched beside him. In the lantern light the prospector looked smaller than ever, wrung out, finished, a man the river had finally accepted. One hand was closed around the empty space where the oilcloth packet had been — Oscar had taken it, meaning to give it back, meaning to discuss terms again, meaning a lot of things.

In the packet, beneath the map, Oscar found a letter. It was short. The handwriting started strong and deteriorated line by line, a man racing his own body.

*Lena. I know what I cost you. I'm not asking forgiveness — I've seen what asking gets a man. I'm asking delivery. Whatever I found out there belongs to you. Not because you want it. Because it was always yours, and I was too busy being a coward in the mountains to say so.*

Oscar read it twice. Then he folded it along its creases, exactly, the way you fold something you intend to keep, and hated himself a little for how naturally his hands did it.

He buried Vann at dawn on a high cut bank above the third bend, under a cairn of river stones, because the ground was too wet to dig deep and the river was rising fast enough to argue about even that. Tam said a few words in his own tongue. Oscar said none. He stood there while the rain came down and the current pulled at the bank below, eating it grain by grain, and understood with total clarity that he was now carrying something that was not cargo.

Cargo you could set down anywhere. This had his name on it.

"Kajang by tomorrow night," he said, turning back down the bank toward the boat. "Then we're done. We hand it over, we patch the hull, and when the water drops, we're gone. Upstream, downstream, wherever the river isn't."

Tam gave him a look. "Captain. You memorized that letter."

"So?"

"So a man who memorizes a letter is planning to keep it." Tam shrugged and waded out to the *Kestrel*. "Just saying what I saw."

Oscar followed him aboard and took the wheel, and the *Kestrel* leaned into the current, and the rain came harder, and somewhere down that gray corridor of water the trading post waited with a woman in it who wanted nothing from her father — and a man named Harrow, though Oscar didn't know the name yet, was already asking questions about a map.

The river carried him toward both.
