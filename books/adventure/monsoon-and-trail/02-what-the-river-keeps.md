## 2. What the River Keeps

The Kajang trading post announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke, river mud, and the sweet rot of fruit nobody had gotten to market in time. Oscar brought the *Sengkang Queen* in against the current with one hand on the wheel and the other holding a canvas-wrapped bundle that felt heavier than any cargo he'd ever carried.

Elias Vann had been dead four days. The river had taken the body at his request — "give me to the water, Captain, she's owed" — but the map stayed, wrapped in oilcloth inside Vann's shirt, pressed there like a second heart.

Oscar had tried to give it back three times. Once to the current, once to a passing patrol boat, once to the firebox of his own stove. Each time his hands stopped working. Not sentiment — Oscar didn't traffic in that. It was more like arithmetic. A dead man's last request was a debt, and debts were the one thing Oscar Montrose understood completely, having spent thirty-one years running from his father's.

Kajang was half trading post, half afterthought. A stilted row of shops facing the water, a mission house with a collapsed veranda, and at the end of the main walkway, a building with a faded red cross and a hand-painted sign: CLINIC — DR. L. VANN.

Vann. So the old man hadn't lied about where to find her.

He found her elbow-deep in a basin of boiled cloth, wringing out bandages with the efficiency of someone who'd learned that gentleness was a luxury. She was maybe thirty, sharp-featured, sunburned across the nose, with the particular stillness of a person who did not waste movement. Three patients lay on cots behind her. One of them wasn't going to see another week.

"You're not sick," she said without looking up.

"I'm looking for Dr. Vann."

"You've found her. If you're bleeding, sit. If you're selling something, leave."

"I'm delivering something." He set the oilcloth bundle on the cleanest corner of her table. "From your father."

The wringing stopped. When she looked up, her eyes were the flat gray-green of the river in dry season, and they were aimed at him like a weapon finding its range.

"My father," she said, "is dead to me. Has been for nineteen years. So either you're a very confused ghost, or you're carrying something from Elias Vann, in which case I'd thank you to put it down and walk back to your boat."

"He died on my deck Tuesday night. Fever. Malaria, I think, or something wearing malaria's clothes." Oscar kept his voice level. He'd rehearsed this on the two-day run upriver and it still came out wrong. "He asked me to bring you his map. Said you'd know what it meant."

"I know exactly what it means." She wiped her hands on her apron with terrible calm. "It means he found something worth more than a daughter, and when he got too old or too sick to dig it up himself, he remembered he had one. That's the whole man in a sentence, Captain. What did you say your name was?"

"Montrose. Oscar Montrose."

"Well, Mr. Montrose, you can tell the river I said no." She picked up the bundle — and here Oscar braced himself — turned to the small iron stove in the corner, opened its door, and held the map over the coals.

She hesitated.

It was only a second. Anyone else would have missed it. But Oscar had spent his life reading hesitation in men about to do something permanent, and he saw it: her thumb moving over the oilcloth, finding the weight of what was inside, some muscle memory of a girl who had once loved the man who'd wrapped it.

She shoved it in anyway. The oilcloth began to smoke.

"The stitching," Oscar said quickly. "There's writing under the stitching. He said — his words — 'she'll want to read it before she burns it.'"

Lena Vann pulled the bundle back out of the fire and beat the flames out against the table edge, and if she noticed what she'd just revealed about herself, she had the grace not to mention it.

* * *

She read it that night by lamplight while Oscar sat on her clinic steps pretending to repair a lantern he had no intention of repairing. Through the window he watched her unfold the map, watch her go still, watch her lips move over whatever her father had stitched into the lining in thread so fine it must have taken him weeks.

When she came out, her face had rearranged itself into something harder than before, which Oscar would not have thought possible.

"He wrote me a letter," she said. "Nineteen years of nothing, and he writes me a letter sewn into a treasure map like I'm eight years old."

"What does it say?"

"It says he's sorry. It says the river took everything from him except the thing he was best at finding, and he wants me to have it because I'm the only person he trusts." Her laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "Trusts. He left. He walked out on us when I was eleven, and now I'm supposed to be flattered that dying made him sentimental."

Oscar looked out at the dark water. "My father left me the opposite way," he said. "Stayed. Made me stand next to him every day of my life until I could recite his sins from memory. Sometimes I think leaving would've been kinder."

"Kindness isn't the point."

"No. It never is." He set down the lantern. "Look — I delivered the map. My part's done. I'll take on cargo tomorrow and be gone before—"

"You can't leave." She said it fast, and then slower, like she hated every word. "There were men at the post office yesterday. Downriver men, nice clothes, asking after a prospector named Vann. Asking whether anyone had come through carrying his effects. The one doing the talking called himself Harrow. Charming as Sunday. Bought drinks for half the dock workers."

Oscar felt the old familiar itch between his shoulder blades, the one that said *run*. "Partners?"

"That's what the dock man said. Former partners. And Mr. Montrose, the way he asked questions — that wasn't grief. That was inventory."

They stood in silence, listening to the river work at its banks.

"They'll come for you," Lena said. "You're the last man who saw him alive. You're the man carrying his effects."

"I'm good at not being found."

"You were found. By me, by them, by circumstance. Maybe stop being proud of it." She crossed her arms. "The monsoon closes the river in three weeks. Your boat looks like it leaks already. Where exactly are you planning to run?"

Every answer he had was a true one and none of them were good ones, so he said nothing, which she correctly interpreted as surrender.

"There's a room behind the clinic," she said. "Formerly a storeroom. You can sleep there and earn your keep hauling water, because my well rope is rotten and I'm tired of doing it alone. In exchange, when those men arrive — and they will arrive — we decide together what happens to that map."

"Why together? It's yours. Burn it tonight and be done."

"Because whoever Harrow sends will ask *you* first, and I need to know what you'll tell them." Her eyes held his. "And because I read the letter twice, and the second time I read it all the way through, and there's a line in it I can't get out of my head. He wrote: 'I know you'll hate me, and you should, but hate travels lighter than love and gets you to the same places.' Which is either wisdom or nonsense, and I'm too tired to tell which, and apparently so are you, or you'd be on your boat right now."

* * *

Harrow arrived nine days later, and the storm arrived with him.

Oscar saw the launch first — a sleek upriver craft with an awning and too much polish, sliding toward Kajang through water the color of steeped tea. Behind it, the western sky had gone the bruised purple that meant the monsoon was done announcing itself and ready to begin.

He was on the *Sengkang Queen* when the first gust hit, checking seams he already knew were bad, and he heard Lena shouting from the walkway: "That's him! That's Harrow — he's asking for you by name!"

By name. Which meant someone at Kajang had talked, which meant the clock had been running longer than either of them knew.

Oscar cast off. It wasn't strategy; it was reflex, thirty-one years deep. Get the boat moving, get the map's captain out of reach, sort the rest later. He had the *Queen* halfway into the channel when the storm reached down and closed its fist.

Rain came sideways. The wind drove him off the channel line and onto water he couldn't read, and the river — treacherous, patient, full of bars that moved every season — reached up and took its toll. He felt the hull open below the waterline with a sound like a door slamming in a distant room, and then the *Sengkang Queen* was settling, listing, drinking the Sengkang River the way she'd been threatening to for years.

He ran her aground on the bar rather than let her sink outright, and stood in the driving rain watching Harrow's launch turn back toward the post, driven off by weather, and understood with perfect clarity that he was stranded — boat broken, enemy close, monsoon arriving — on a sandbar within swimming distance of the one woman in the world who wanted nothing from him except the truth.

Lena was waiting on the bank when he waded ashore, soaked through, furious, and — he realized with some surprise — laughing.

"Your boat," she shouted over the rain.

"Is resting," he shouted back.

"Your boat is *drowning*, Mr. Montrose, and Harrow will be back the moment this squall lifts, and you have nowhere to go." Lightning whitened her face. "So welcome to Kajang. You live here now."

And Oscar Montrose, who had spent his whole life keeping the river between himself and every promise ever offered, stood on the flooded bank of the Sengkang with nothing seaworthy, nothing hidden, and nothing left to run on — and felt, beneath the fear, something dangerously close to relief.
