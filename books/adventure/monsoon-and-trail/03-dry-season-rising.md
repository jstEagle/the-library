## 3. Dry Season Rising

The rain came in the night like a debt collector, and by morning the Kajang trading post stood ankle-deep in brown water.

Lena woke on a cot in the clinic with her boots floating beside her and the smell of river rot thick enough to chew. Outside, the Sengkang had swallowed its banks whole. The dock was gone. The market stalls were gone. What remained of the post was an island of rooftops and stubborn trees, and moored to the clinic's porch post — riding the flood like it was born to it — sat Oscar Montrose's boat, listing hard to port.

She'd watched him work all yesterday afternoon, shirtless in the downpour, hammering canvas and pitch into the split along his hull while the water rose around his knees. He hadn't asked for help. She hadn't offered. They'd spoken maybe forty words since he'd carried her father's body off his deck three days ago, and most of those words had been about drainage.

Now she sat at the clinic table with the map spread out before her, weighted at the corners with brass instrument trays, and tried to understand what she was looking at.

It wasn't really a map. It was a palimpsest — Elias Vann's original survey, done in a careful draftsman's hand twenty years ago, and then decades of annotations crammed into every margin, every blank space, every inch of water he'd drawn and redrawn. Elevations crossed out and corrected. Channel markers that no longer existed. Notes in pencil so faint she had to tilt the paper toward the lamp to catch them.

*The river moves,* one note read. *The seam does not.*

Lena traced the contour lines with a fingertip. Her father had been precise. That was the thing she kept forgetting about him — the man who'd left, who'd sent nothing for fifteen years but a money order every monsoon like he was paying off a fine — that man had been *precise*. Every line on this map was a place he had stood. Every annotation was a day of his life she knew nothing about.

She hated how well she could read it. She hated that they drew contours the same way, that she recognized his shorthand because it was her shorthand, learned at his knee before he left, before she was old enough to hate him properly.

*Here.* His final note, in ink fresher than the rest, pressed hard enough to emboss the page: *Seam runs under the old ridge above Kajang bend. Forty feet of overburden. Easy dig.*

Lena's finger stopped.

Above Kajang bend.

She looked up through the clinic's salt-fogged window at the ridge behind the town — the green shoulder of land where the hillside children caught fireflies, where Mrs. Anwar grazed her two goats, where Lena herself walked every evening when the clinic closed and she needed not to think. The ridge. Her ridge. The only piece of this flooded, muddy, malarial place that had ever felt like hers.

*Easy dig* meant clearing it. Meant cutting the overburden away, forty feet of it, meant terraces and sluice boxes and tailings choking the creek that fed the town's well. Meant the firefly field stripped to gray gravel. Meant the goats gone, the path gone, the evening walk gone.

Meant her father, dead three days, reaching up out of the river to take the last thing she had.

Lena sat very still for a long time. The rain drummed the tin roof. Somewhere below, Oscar's hammer kept its slow, stubborn rhythm against the hull.

Then she began, methodically, to fold the map away — and stopped, because folded into its crease was a second sheet she hadn't noticed. A letter, unsent, addressed in her father's hand: *To my daughter, if she will read it.*

She didn't read it. Not yet. She put it in her apron pocket instead, against her hip, where she could feel its weight and pretend it wasn't there. There were things she'd do after the flood. There were things she couldn't do while standing in water.

* * *

Harrow came upriver at noon in a longboat flying no flag, carrying six men and a smile.

He arrived the way weather arrives — everyone felt the pressure drop before they saw him. By the time his boat bumped against the clinic porch steps, half the post had found reasons to be indoors, and the other half stood watching from doorways with their arms crossed.

He was handsome in the way of well-kept things: linen shirt dry despite the deluge, boots polished, hair silvering at the temples exactly where it flattered. He climbed the steps, shook water from his hat, and introduced himself to Lena as though they were at a garden party.

"Miss Vann. I had the honor of knowing your father. Brilliant man. We were partners, once — before circumstances separated us." His eyes went, without pretense, to the table where the map lay under the instrument trays. "I've come to settle his estate."

"The estate is a dead man and a debt," Lena said. "You're welcome to both."

"Charming." Harrow's smile didn't move. "And Captain Montrose? I understand he's been having hull trouble."

"He's busy."

"I'll keep it brief, then." He stepped closer, and the charm dropped away like a mask slipping its strap, and what was underneath was patient and cold and very sure of itself. "There are four of us who financed your father's last survey. Four men owed a return on twenty years. The jade under that ridge is worth more than this entire post, more than everything your father ever touched. I am offering you a fair share of it, Miss Vann. A generous one. All I require is the map, and your cooperation in locating the markers."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll locate them myself. It will simply take longer, and cost more people more than it needs to." He straightened his cuffs. "Your father made promises he couldn't keep. I'm here to collect on one of them. You may decide whether you're part of the payment."

He tipped his hat and went down the steps into the rain, and Lena stood at the window and watched his longboat slide away up the flooded street, and understood with perfect clarity that the offer had been a courtesy. The kind you extend before a siege.

* * *

She found Oscar that evening in the shallow water beneath his boat, a lantern hooked to the prow above him, caulking the seam by feel. He worked the way rivers ran — steady, unglamorous, indifferent to weather. When her shadow fell across him he didn't look up.

"Harrow was here," she said.

"I saw the boat." He drove another wad of pitch into the planking. "What did he want?"

"What he wants is obvious. But he made me a different offer first. Or rather—" she crouched at the waterline, arms on her knees, "—he told me what he'd offer *you*. Passage out. Downriver, past the bar, ahead of the closing season. Your boat repaired, your debts paid, your name cleared wherever it needs clearing. Everything you've been running toward for ten years, priced and gift-wrapped. In exchange for me and the map."

Oscar's hands went still against the hull.

For a moment the only sound was the rain on the water and the lantern creaking on its hook. Then he said, quietly, "Did you come to accept on his behalf?"

"I came to ask why you haven't already."

"Why haven't *you* burned the map?" he said. "You wanted to. First day. You told me so."

"Because I finally read it properly." She pulled the letter from her apron pocket and held it, unopened, between two fingers. "The seam isn't somewhere far away, Captain. It's under the ridge. My ridge. The one piece of ground in this whole drowned valley that I chose. If Harrow digs it, he doesn't just take jade. He takes the creek, the well, the hillside, all of it. My father's last gift to me is a hole where my home used to be."

Oscar looked at her then — really looked, the way he rarely let himself look at anything, as though stillness might cost him. Water dripped off the lantern. Downriver, lightning stitched the clouds above the bar that had split his hull, the bar that would close the river in nine days regardless of what anyone decided.

"So we're both being bought," Lena said. "You with a future. Me with a fortune. And the price of refusing is that we stay here, in a flooded post, on a leaking boat, waiting for six armed men to stop being polite."

"That's about the size of it."

"Good," she said. "Then we're clear."

She stood, and the letter was still in her hand, and she found she couldn't make herself burn it either. Instead she tucked it back against her hip and turned for the porch steps.

"Lena." Oscar's voice stopped her. He was looking at the ridge now, black against the storm light, the ridge he'd moored beneath without knowing what slept under it. "Nine days. If we're staying — and I haven't said we are — we'll need more than a patched hull and good intentions."

"No," she agreed. "We'll need the whole town."

Behind them, upriver, a lantern flickered where no lantern should be — high on the flooded bank, among the trees, watching. Harrow hadn't left. Harrow had landed his men, and the siege had already begun.
