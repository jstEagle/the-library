## 4. Monsoon and Trail

Oscar woke before the light, the way he always did on the river, but this morning the river had come to him. The trading post's lower walk was under a foot of brown water. The *Kestrel* strained at her lines, hull groaning against the swollen current, and somewhere out in the murk Harrow's launch idled with its engine ticking over like a held breath.

He'd known this dawn was coming for three days. He could feel it the way you feel a sandbar under a keel — not seeing it, just knowing.

The map lay rolled inside his oilskin, against his chest. He had told himself he was keeping it safe. That was a lie he told kindly. He was keeping it because letting go of things was the one skill he'd never learned, and Elias Vann had handed him the heaviest thing Oscar had ever carried: not paper, but a promise with a woman's name on it.

Lena was already awake. He could see her silhouette through the clinic's fogged window, moving among her cots, checking bandages by lamplight. She hadn't slept either. Neither of them had said it aloud, but they both knew what Harrow's silence meant. Men like Harrow didn't wait. They arrived.

Oscar sat on the *Kestrel*'s deck with his back against the wheelhouse and unrolled the map across his knees in the gray half-light. Vann's hand crawled over it — elevations, a fallen tree used as a marker, a bend where the water ran green even in flood. And there, in faded ink beneath the seam itself, an annotation Lena had deciphered two nights ago: *the jade lies under the old terrace ground. Do not dig deep. It will not forgive you.*

Vann had known. Had gone anyway. Had spent thirty years running from that knowledge, same as Oscar ran from his own name.

Oscar read it until his eyes ached. Then he read it again. He traced the route from the Kajang bend to the ridge line to the seep where the stone showed pale in wet weather. He made himself see it without the paper — made the map become memory, the way rivermen hold channels in their heads, every bar and snag and deep hole, no chart required.

When he was certain — when he could close his eyes and walk the trail blind — he went to the galley stove.

The paper caught fast. Vann's handwriting curled and went black, the ink flaring green for one strange second, as if the jade itself had objected. Oscar held it over the water and let the ash fall into the current, and watched it swirl away toward the sea.

It felt less like burning a map than cutting an anchor loose. Except this time he wasn't cutting himself free. He was setting the thing down so he could pick up something heavier and choose to carry it.

Behind him, a voice said, "You absolute fool."

He turned. Lena stood on the dock, arms crossed, soaked to the knee, watching the last flecks of ash.

"Or the only smart thing anyone's done on this river all year," Oscar said. "Harrow can't take what doesn't exist. Now it's just a story. Stories need witnesses."

"He'll take us instead."

"Then we'd better make sure he doesn't."

* * *

Harrow came at first light, of course. Men like him always did — they believed dawn belonged to them, that everyone else would be slow and bleary and grateful to be threatened politely.

His launch grounded on the flooded walk at 5:40, six men stepping down into shin-deep water, rifles slung but hands near them. Harrow himself came last, dry under a wide hat, smiling the smile that had charmed three river towns into giving him credit and two into giving him their boats.

"Captain Montrose," he called across the water. "I hear you've been burning correspondence."

Oscar met him at the top of the dock stairs, Lena beside him, and behind them, to Oscar's quiet astonishment, a dozen townsfolk — the clinic's patients, the post keeper, two fishermen with machetes, old Mrs. Anwar with a boat hook she'd clearly owned since before the war.

"You're standing on my dock," Oscar said. "Flooded or not, it's mine to keep."

"It's a trading post," Harrow said pleasantly. "Trading posts change hands. Everything does. That's rather the point of rivers." He spread his hands. "Give me the girl's cooperation and passage downriver unmolested, and I'll forget the whole unpleasant business. Refuse, and—" he gestured at the water rising around everyone's ankles "—well. The monsoon does my negotiating for me. I only have to wait."

"You're good at waiting," Lena said coldly. "You waited thirty years for my father to die."

"I preferred him alive. Dead men don't sign anything." Harrow's smile thinned. "The map, Captain. Last offer."

"The map's ash," Oscar said. "But I'll tell you what I do have. A memory. Mine. And a town full of people who've heard exactly what you did to Elias Vann's partners downriver — the ones who signed and still got ruined. Ask your own crew how much they trust you."

That was the crack, and Oscar hadn't even planned it. He'd seen it the night before, drinking with Harrow's boatmen at the post keeper's table while Harrow dined alone: men who'd been promised percentages for years and paid in rations. Men who knew the flood season better than Harrow ever would, because they were rivermen too, and rivermen know when a man is about to cut them loose the way they know a storm coming — by the smell.

At 6:15, as the river peaked against the pilings, Harrow's bosun put down his rifle, walked to the far end of the dock, and sat down facing the water. Two more followed within the minute. Harrow turned to shout something commanding and found himself addressing a launch with no crew and a dock with no fear in it.

What happened next happened fast and mostly without violence. The remaining three men looked at the water, looked at the mutineers, looked at each other, and decided the flood was a better employer than Harrow. One of them shoved the launch off the pilings so it drifted broadside into the current; Harrow scrambled aboard it alone, engine flooding, hat gone, shouting terms at a town that had stopped listening.

They didn't lynch him. They didn't have to. The river took the launch sideways into the submerged fence line above the post, and the current peeled it apart plank by plank with the patience only moving water has. Harrow made the high ground of the old shrine mound with nothing but his shirt, and by noon he was trading his boots to Mrs. Anwar for dry tobacco, and by evening he was asking the post keeper, very politely, whether the southbound packet took passengers.

Lena watched him go from the clinic window and felt — not triumph. Something quieter. Her father had spent his life outrunning what he'd broken; Harrow had spent his chasing it. Both of them ended up with empty hands. She intended to find out what filled hands otherwise.

* * *

The water took nine days to drop.

Oscar spent them on the *Kestrel* with his sleeves rolled, caulking the split strake that had nearly drowned them at the hidden bar, re-cottoning seams, replacing a plank he milled himself from a fallen meranti the flood had gifted to the post. Lena brought him coffee that tasted of bark and kept his bandages changed when the adze slipped. They talked about everything except the seam, and then, on the sixth day, sitting on the dock with their feet in the retreating river, they talked about nothing else.

"It's under the terrace," she said. "Under the village ground. My ground now, I suppose. Inheritance again." She laughed, bitter and tired. "My father left me a disease to cure and a treasure to refuse."

"Or a choice," Oscar said. "You could dig. You'd be rich by Christmas and hated by the dry season."

"And you could run. You always could. Boat's nearly sound again."

Oscar looked at his hands — scarred, rope-callused, forty years of tiller work in them — and realized he was waiting for the itch. The pull downriver. The feeling that anywhere was better than here, that staying was sinking.

It didn't come.

"I patched her to stay," he said. "Not to leave."

They staked the seam together the next morning, walking the trail Oscar now knew by heart — no map needed, which proved the burning right — up past the Kajang bend to the ridge line, to the seep where pale stone showed in the wet. Lena drove the boundary posts herself, four of them, and painted on each in white: *Protected Ground. By order of the Kajang Clinic and the Kestrel.* It wasn't law. It didn't need to be. It was a promise, witnessed by a whole town, and promises were the one currency Oscar Montrose had finally learned to spend.

On the last day of the floods, he moved the *Kestrel* to a mooring off the clinic dock — permanent lines, proper ones, the kind a man only rigs if he means to come back aboard tomorrow, and the day after, and the season after that.

Lena stood on the dock and watched him tie off, and said, "So the captain finally dropped anchor."

"Not anchor," Oscar said. "Mooring. Anchors are for leaving. Moorings are for coming home."

The river ran clear and low and steady, going about its business, and for the first time in his life Oscar Montrose let it go on without him.
