## 4. North Is a Lie

Sefa had learned three things about Wren Yarrow in the hour since the truce: they ate like a heron, they checked the cave mouth every eleven minutes, and they did not believe a single word she said.

She could work with that. Distrust was just curiosity wearing armor.

"Sit," she said, patting the flat stone beside the altar where the compass still lay, patient as a sleeping cat. "You're wearing a groove in the floor."

"I'm fine standing."

"You're wearing a groove in the *floor*, ranger. I can hear the sand grinding." She unrolled her satchel across the stone, laying out her tools with the ceremony of a street performer: a tin of wax-styluses, a folded square of oilcloth, a stub of candle she lit with a match struck on her thumbnail. "If you're going to accuse me of theft, at least have the decency to sit while I ruin your theory."

Wren did not sit. But they did stop pacing, which Sefa counted as a diplomatic victory, and leaned against the cave wall with their arms crossed in a way that said *I have nowhere better to be, which is the only reason you're still breathing.*

The tide was coming back in. That gave her maybe two hours before the sea sealed the cave mouth and made the conversation either very intimate or very final. She'd have preferred more time, but archivists learned early that truth kept badly, like fish.

"My name is Sefa Marun," she began. "I was raised in the Archive of Vell, third tier, cartography division. Which means I spent my childhood copying maps of places I was never allowed to go."

"Copying maps is a crime now?"

"Copying maps is a *profession*. Going to the places on them, that's the crime." She pulled a folded paper from the oilcloth and held it up. "The river-cities have an arrangement with the guild, you see. We keep their ledgers, their shipping manifests, their very respectable records of very respectable trade. And in exchange, we don't map the upper river. Two hundred miles of it. It's a blank space on every chart in Vell, and I do mean blank—inked over, deliberately, by hand, in every single copy. Do you know how much labor that takes? How many archivists, over how many years, sitting at desks, carefully erasing a river?"

Wren's eyes flicked to the compass on the altar. Just once. Sefa pretended not to see. People told you everything with their eyes if you were patient enough to watch.

"So no," she continued, "I didn't steal your compass. I *borrowed* it, which is different in the way that trespassing is different from burglary. I took it from a guild courier who took it from a dead man's effects, and I've been trying to work out what it is ever since. And then it pointed at *you*, and I confess I got curious."

"Pointed at me."

"Pointed at you the way an arrow points at a target." She tapped the altar. "May I?"

Wren hesitated, then nodded once, a concession granted like a tax payment.

Sefa picked up the compass and held it flat on her palm. The needle swung lazily, settled, swung again. It had been doing that since the moment Wren walked into the cave—quivering, restless, like a dog that could smell its owner two streets away.

"Every compass in the world points north," she said. "Magnetism, lodestone, the great iron bones of the world—there are a hundred explanations and I've copied all of them. But this one doesn't. Watch."

She turned in a slow circle, holding the compass level. The needle ignored her completely. It didn't waver, didn't spin, didn't so much as tremble. It pointed—unwavering, absolute—upstream. Inland. Toward the mountains that stood behind the drowned coast like a wall between the world and its own history.

Wren pushed off the wall. "It's broken."

"It's been 'broken' in exactly that direction for six weeks."

"Then it's broken *consistently*." Wren snatched it from her palm, turned it, turned it again, held it upside down as though gravity were a trick that could be caught out. The needle held its line toward the mountains with the serene stubbornness of a thing that had never once cared what north thought of it. "It pointed north before it was stolen. I've navigated by it for nine years. Nine *years*."

"And what did you navigate toward?"

"Water. Camps. The edge of the Flats. It pointed *north*."

Sefa leaned forward, chin on her fists. "Did it? Or did it point wherever you needed to go, and you called that north because the alternative was asking questions you weren't ready to answer?"

The look Wren gave her could have cured meat. Sefa smiled into it, because someone had to be pleasant in this cave, and it was clearly not going to be the ranger.

"Here's what I know," she said, and unrolled the map.

* * *

The map was her own work, which meant it was heresy, which meant she'd copied it three times and burned two. It showed the river from its mouth to its source—or rather, it showed the river from its mouth to the blank space, and then, in the blank space, in her own careful archivist's hand, everything the sealed records hinted at and the drowned ferryman's songs confirmed and one very drunk guild surveyor had once wept about into his cups.

"The river doesn't start in the mountains," she said, tracing it with a stylus. "Everyone says it does. The maps say it does. But the maps are wrong on purpose, and here's how I know." She tapped a series of small marks along the river's lower course. "Flow measurements. The Archive takes them every season—has for a century, quietly, because the guild likes to know exactly how much water it's selling. And the numbers don't work. The river at the mouth carries more water than the river at the last unsealed town can account for. A third more. Maybe more than that."

"So there's a tributary."

"There's no tributary. There are no tributaries. I've looked. What there is—" she drew a line upward, into the blank space, and her stylus hand was steadier than her voice, "—is a spring. Not a seep, not a snowmelt trickle. A *spring*. The kind of source that doesn't feed a river so much as *become* one. And a hundred years ago, the guild sealed it, and rewrote every map, and built a water trade on the difference."

Wren had gone very still. Sefa had seen stillness before, in interrogations, in the Archive's reading rooms when someone found their own family name in a ledger they shouldn't have been reading. Stillness like that wasn't calm. It was a person holding a door shut from the inside.

"That's a story," Wren said. "A good one. I'll grant you the needle's odd. But you're telling me the river-cities—the guild—the whole desert's water supply is built on a sealed spring and a century of lies, and the proof is a broken compass and a map you drew yourself."

"And a courier who was murdered for carrying this." She let that sit. "And a guild that sent riders to the coast within a day of my borrowing it. And you, ranger. You, who the compass has been pointing at since before you knew it existed. You didn't find me. I've been following it, and it led me to a desert ranger with no family name and no history and a relic they can't explain, and I'm supposed to believe that's coincidence?"

"I never said I had no history."

"You said you don't *remember* it. There's a difference, and it's the difference I'm interested in." Sefa rolled the map closed before Wren could look too long—some hooks you set gently. "The Archive has records, ranger. Old ones. Sealed ones. I've spent two years finding the seams in them, and every seam leads the same direction: upstream. Three keys, the oldest fragments say. Three compasses that point not north but *home*, to the spring's own bloodline. The guild has been hunting them for a century. They found two." She held Wren's gaze. "They're missing one."

The cave dripped. Somewhere out in the grey water, a gull said something rude.

"You're saying this is a key."

"I'm saying it's the only one left."

Wren took the compass back from the altar—slowly, the way you take back a thing that has changed shape while you weren't looking—and held it flat. The needle strained toward the mountains. Toward the blank space on the map. Toward, Sefa was nearly certain, whatever was left of a family that had gone to extraordinary lengths to make sure their child would never be found.

"I don't remember anything," Wren said quietly. It wasn't an argument. It was almost a confession. "Before the Flats. Before Corvane. There's nothing. I told myself it was the fever, the year he found me. People lose time to fever."

"People do." Sefa stood, brushing sand from her knees. "Fever doesn't erase a family sigil off a compass case, though. Have you looked at the underside?"

Wren turned the compass over. Sefa watched their thumb find the worn groove in the brass, the one worn too smooth to read by eye—and saw the exact moment they realized they'd been carrying a name they couldn't read for nine years.

"The guild sealed the spring," Sefa said gently, "and the spring, as far as I can tell from the fragments, was the *memory* of the river country. Songs. Names. Faces. The old ferryman sing about it—water that remembers. Seal the spring, and the past goes quiet. Erase the bloodline, and the key goes quiet. You don't have amnesia, ranger. You have a *seal*."

Wren's jaw worked. For a long moment they said nothing at all, and Sefa let them have the silence, because she'd learned that too—truth, like fish, needed time on ice before people could stomach it.

Then Wren looked up, and their eyes had gone flat and ranger-hard, the eyes of someone deciding what to do with a stranger who knew too much.

"Say I believe you. Say all of it's true." They held up the compass, needle straining at the mountains. "Then the guild isn't hunting you. They're hunting *this*. And every mile upstream we go, we're walking into their hands, and you're asking me to lead them there by carrying the one thing that opens the door." A pause. "Why would I do that?"

And there it was. The real question, the one Sefa had been turning over for six weeks in cheap rooms and stolen boats: *why her*, why this, why a third-tier archivist with ink-stained fingers had thrown away her whole life on a blank space on a map.

"Because I copied those maps for eleven years," she said. "And I got very good at pretending the blank space was nothing. And then one night I held a compass that pointed at a person instead of north, and I understood that the blank space isn't empty. It's *full*. Full of everything they took, and everything they're still taking, and every name they erased—and my whole profession is names, ranger. It's the only thing I've ever been good at." She shouldered her satchel. "The tide turns in an hour. The cave closes. And the guild's riders are maybe half a day behind me, which means we can argue on the way or argue in a cell. Your choice."

Wren looked at the compass. At the cave mouth, where grey light and grey water were beginning their slow argument. At her.

"You're certain about the riders."

"Certain enough to be leaving."

Wren exhaled through their nose—the sound of a person losing an argument with themselves—and slung their pack. "Upstream, then. But we do this my way. No fires, no roads, no names."

"No names," Sefa agreed, and then, because she was who she was: "Though for the record, mine's a good one. Sefa Marun. It means *she who keeps the accounts*. My mother had a sense of humor."

The compass needle held its line toward the mountains, unwavering, as they walked out of the cave and into the grey wash of the returning tide—pointing, with the patience of a thing that had waited a hundred years, at everything the water remembered.

And behind them, far down the coast road, dust rose where dust had no business rising.
