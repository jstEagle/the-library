## 6. The Ferryman's Ledger

The town was called Brackwater, or had been, once. Now the river had taken the ground floor of every building and left the upper stories standing like a crowd of people wading, patient, up to their knees. Sefa had grown up in the river-cities, where flooding was a season and drowning was a word, but Brackwater had made its peace with the water in a way that unsettled even her. Houses floated on barrels. Chickens lived on rooftops. A woman hung laundry between a bell tower and what used to be a tax office, and the laundry was dry, and the tax office was not.

"They just... live like this," Wren said, from behind her. Their voice had the flat carefulness Sefa had learned, in four days of traveling together, meant *I am not going to admit this frightens me.*

"It's cheaper than moving," Sefa said. "That's the whole philosophy of the river towns. The water's going to win eventually, so you might as well get a boat."

"I don't like water."

"You've mentioned. Twice. Once while standing in it."

"The river's fine," Wren said. "Rivers have edges. This—" they gestured at the drowned street, at the doorways that opened onto nothing but current, "—this is a river that's been lying about where it ends."

Sefa bit back the observation that Wren, a person with no memory, complaining about things that lied about their own boundaries, was almost too perfect to say out loud. She'd learned that too, in four days: some jokes you saved, the way you saved the last of your water.

They needed a boat. That was the simple version. The complicated version was that the river narrowed ahead into the Gullet—a stretch of current so fast the maps just said *good luck* in three different hands—and walking the banks meant guild territory, and guild territory meant riders, and riders meant Corvane's shadow stretching all the way from the coast to here, which Sefa had already confirmed by the burned waystation they'd passed at dawn. Someone was sweeping the river valley behind them, methodical as a comb through wet hair.

So: a boat. And in Brackwater, boats belonged to one man.

The ferryman's dock was a crooked thing built from three different wrecks, and the ferryman himself sat at the end of it like a piece of driftwood that had learned to smoke a pipe. He was old the way river things were old—not wrinkled so much as *worn*, smoothed down by decades of current until what was left was mostly eyes. He watched them pick their way along the dock without moving anything except those eyes.

"Ferry's not running," he said.

"It's the only ferry," Sefa said.

"That's why it's not running." He knocked out his pipe against the rail. "Water's high. Water's mean. And you two—" the eyes moved from her to Wren and back, "—you're being followed. I can smell guild soap from here. They wash their hands too much, guild men. Like the river's something they caught."

"We can pay," Sefa said.

"Everyone can pay. Money's the easiest thing in the world to get. That's why it's worth nothing." He leaned back, and the driftwood creaked. "I take stories. A good one, true, told whole. That's my fare. Been collecting them forty years. Got a story from a woman who walked the length of the river blind. Got one from a soldier who deserted twice from the same army. You got a story?"

Sefa opened her mouth, ready to spend the archivist's trick—half-truths arranged to look like whole ones—but Wren spoke first.

"No," Wren said. "We don't. Not one that's whole."

The ferryman looked at them for a long moment. Something in his face shifted, the way water shifts when something moves underneath.

"Well," he said quietly. "That's a story too, isn't it."

* * *

He took them out anyway. Sefa never did learn if that was generosity or something else, and she suspected the answer was the answer she'd been circling her whole life: the river did what it wanted, and people who lived on it long enough stopped pretending otherwise.

The boat was flat-bottomed and patient. The ferryman poled them through streets where the water ran green-gold in the afternoon light, past windows where people watched them pass and, uniquely for this journey, did not look away. In the fishing villages on the coast, Wren had been a stranger, and strangers got silence. Here, they got something worse. Here, people looked at Wren like they were *checking*.

"Left at the church," the ferryman said, poling them around a steeple that rose out of the water like a drowning man's last word. "Mind the bell rope. It's still attached."

"To what?" Wren asked.

"Nothing good."

They passed a market square where the stalls had been converted to fish traps, and a schoolhouse where the desks floated free, and Sefa watched Wren watching all of it with an expression she was starting a catalog of. This one was new: recognition without memory. The face of someone hearing a song they couldn't name, in a country they'd never admitted to visiting.

"You've been here," she said. Not a question. She'd stopped asking questions somewhere around the burned waystation; statements worked better on Wren, gave them less room to hide.

"No," Wren said.

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The face. Like the town owes you money."

Wren didn't answer, which from Wren was a paragraph.

The ferryman brought them at last to the far edge of town, where the river gathered itself and began to hurry—the Gullet, white water ahead, the sound of it like distant applause. He shipped the pole and let the boat drift in an eddy, a slow circle, and looked at Wren with those worn-down eyes for a long, long time.

"I know you," he said.

"You don't," Wren said. "Nobody does. That's rather the point of me."

"I poled this river forty years. I poled it the spring you were born." The old man's voice had gone gentle, which was somehow more alarming than if he'd shouted. "I poled the night they took you out of it."

The eddy turned the boat. Sefa felt the whole world tilt with it.

"What," Wren said, very evenly, "did you just say."

"Little one. Wet as a cat. Screaming like one, too." The ferryman smiled, and it was a sad smile, the kind that had been carried a long way. "Your father held you up like you were made of glass and told me to forget his face. I told him the river doesn't forget faces, and he said he knew, that's why he'd picked a ferryman, because ferrymen keep things even when they shouldn't." He tapped his own temple. "Forty years, I kept it. The spring-child, we called you. Those of us who knew there was a spring at all."

Wren had gone very still. Sefa had seen them still before—in the cave, at the coast, facing down guild riders—but this was a different stillness. This was a person holding a door shut with their whole body.

"My name," Wren said, "is Wren Yarrow."

"That's the name they gave you after," the ferryman said. "I'm sure it's served you well. But your mother's name was Meren. And your family's name was Solenne. And this river—" he put his hand flat on the water, on the current that had carried everything Sefa had ever studied, "—this river was your house."

* * *

They moored at the ferryman's house, which was the top floor of a granary, reachable by ladder, and he climbed it at seventy like a man who'd done it ten thousand times, because he had. Inside it was dry and dim and smelled of pipe smoke and old paper, and Sefa's archivist heart did something embarrassing in her chest, because the walls were lined with ledgers.

Dozens of them. Water-stained, swollen, their spines cracked, but *ledgers*—forty years of crossings, fares, names, dates, and between the lines, in a hand that started firm and went shaky and went firm again, the stories. The blind woman. The double deserter. Everything he'd ever collected, kept the way the river-cities' archivists were supposed to keep things, except he'd actually done it.

"You're an archivist," Sefa breathed.

"I'm a ferryman who writes things down." He shrugged. "Same thing, if you do it honest. Your lot made it a job so you could charge for it."

She couldn't even argue. She'd been trained by people who charged for it.

Wren stood in the middle of the room like someone who'd walked into a weather system. "You said they took me out of the river. Who's *they*? Who was my father? Why don't I—" Wren's voice did something it almost never did; it cracked, just at the seam. "Why don't I remember any of this?"

The ferryman looked at them both for a while. Then he went to the wall, and he didn't take down just any ledger. He took down one near the bottom, the oldest, its cover so water-swollen it had gone soft as cloth, and he set it on the table between them with the care you'd use on a sleeping animal.

"Fare's a story," he said. "Here's yours. I only kept the receipt."

He opened it to a page near the middle, and Sefa leaned in, and the archivist in her went quiet and reverent, because the page was a crossing record like all the others—*date, cargo, fare*—except the cargo line read, in the ferryman's younger hand:

*One man. One woman. One child, sleeping. Fare: the truth, to be kept until asked for.*

And beneath it, a family name, written the way the river-cities wrote the names of the old water-wardens, with the warden's mark beside it—a circle with a spring rising through it.

Solenne.

Wren touched the page. Sefa watched their fingers hover over the ink, not quite landing, the way you'd hover over a burn.

"Water-wardens," the ferryman said. "Last of them, your family. Keepers of the source. Your mother's people had tended the spring since before the river-cities had names. And then the guild sealed it, a hundred years back, and the wardens went from keepers to... reminders. Living proof the water used to run free." He looked up at Wren. "The guild couldn't kill what the water remembered. So they did something worse. They made the water forget you."

"The sealing," Sefa said slowly, and her training was shouting at her now, every archived rumor she'd ever filed under *folklore, unreliable*, "the sealing didn't just stop the water. Memory runs in the water. That's why the river-cities' oldest records are—why they're *poetic*, why they don't parse as data. The archivists thought it was style. It's not style. It's the water talking."

"Now she's got it," the ferryman said.

"Then why do I remember *nothing*?" Wren said, and the stillness was gone now, and underneath it was something that frightened Sefa more than any stillness had: Wren angry, Wren grieving, Wren a child again for one unguarded second. "If the water forgot me, fine, the water forgot everyone, but I'm a person. People remember their own mothers. I remember sand. I remember Corvane pulling me out of a river when I was—how old? Sixteen? Seventeen? And nothing before. Not a face. Not a *name*. You don't lose a whole childhood by accident."

"No," the ferryman said. "You don't."

The silence stretched. The river clucked against the granary's drowned foundations, patient, patient.

"Your father brought you downriver the night the guild came for the last of the wardens," the ferryman said. "He wasn't running from them, understand. He was running *ahead* of them. There's a difference. He'd made a bargain—I never learned with whom, and I've wondered forty years—to have the child's memory taken. All of it. Before the guild could find it and read it like a map." His eyes were very old and very sorry. "They didn't wipe you because they hated you, little one. They wiped you because you *were* the map. Every road to the spring was written in your head, and your father chose to burn the map rather than let them drive it."

Wren sat down. Sefa had never seen Wren sit down. Wren stood through everything—arguments, revelations, the entire concept of having a past—and now they sat, slowly, on the ferryman's bench, looking at the ledger like it might look back.

"He chose it," Wren said. "He chose for me."

"He chose to keep you alive and *invisible*. His words, near enough. I've carried them forty years too." The ferryman closed the ledger with a soft whump. "I'd have made the same choice. That's not comfort. It's just the truth, and truth's the only fare I take."

Outside, the light was going. Downriver, toward the Gullet, the white water's applause had changed its rhythm, and Sefa's ears—archivist's ears, river-girl's ears—caught what was underneath it: hooves. Many. Coming along the bank road through the dusk.

The guild had found the town.

"They'll search house by house," the ferryman said, calm as a man reading weather. "They always do. You've got until the water rises." He pushed the ledger across the table to Wren. "Take it. It's yours twice over—your family's name's in it, and your father paid the fare. Ferrymen don't keep paid debts."

"I can't read it," Wren said. "It's not mine. I'm not—"

"It's exactly yours. That's the whole trouble with it." The ferryman stood, and took down his pipe, and began, with the unhurried movements of a man who had ferried fugitives before, to douse the lamps. "Question is what you do with a name the guild spent a century trying to drown. That's not my story to tell. That's yours."

Sefa took the ledger, because Wren wasn't moving, and weighed it in her hands—light, water-swollen, the heaviest thing she'd ever carried. Below them, through the floor, she heard the first boot on the dock. Then the second.

"Back door's a window," the ferryman said, nodding at the river side of the granary, where a small boat waited at the sill like a dog that knew the routine. "The Gullet's running fast tonight. Fast enough to lose riders. Fast enough, if you're careless, to lose everything else."

Wren stood. Whatever had broken in them had been gathered up and put away, quick as a ranger hiding a fire—but their eyes, when they turned to Sefa, were not the eyes of the person who'd walked into Brackwater. They were the eyes of someone who had just learned they'd been *designed*—built, piece by piece, by other people's choices, into a shape that fit a lock.

"Sefa," Wren said quietly. "Tell me everything the archives know about the spring. Tonight. While we run."

She nodded. And as she passed the ledger into Wren's arms and turned for the window, she thought: *the compass points at people, not places—and if Wren is the map, then the guild doesn't need to find the spring.*

They just need to find Wren.

The first fist hit the door below.
