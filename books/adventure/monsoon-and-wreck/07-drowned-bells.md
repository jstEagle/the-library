## 7. Drowned Bells

The village of Lowmark had drowned on a Sunday, the ferryman told us, and its bells had rung until the water took them.

"That's not how drowning works," Sefa said. She was crouched at the river's edge, testing the current with two fingers like she was greeting an old dog.

"Everything about this place is not how things work," I said. "You get used to it."

I hadn't gotten used to it. Three days upstream from the coast and I still flinched when the river moved without wind. In the desert, water only moved if you made it move. Here it had opinions. It breathed against the banks in long slow swells, and at night it whispered under the floorboards of the ferryman's house, and once—this was true—I'd heard it say something that sounded almost like my name.

Sefa said that was a heron. Sefa said a lot of things with great confidence and no proof.

"Look," she said now, pointing across the brown-green water to where a stone spire broke the surface like a finger raised in permanent objection. "That's the tower. The ledger says the second key was kept in the bell chamber. The archivists sealed it there before the flooding."

"The archivists sealed a compass in a church and then let the church drown."

"They didn't *let* anything drown. The guild flooded it." She said it flatly, the way you'd report weather. "When the river-cities refused to hand over their records, Corvane's people broke the levees. Four hundred people lost their homes. The church was the one building they couldn't reach, because the priest sank the key in the bell chamber and the guild divers never came back."

"Why didn't they come back?"

The ferryman answered from his porch, where he'd been mending a net with the patience of a man who had outlived everyone he'd ever argued with. "Because the bells still ring down there. And a man who hears a bell underwater forgets which way is up." He squinted at me. "You'll want to go anyway. You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of someone the water remembers." He tied off a knot. "My father pulled three bodies out of Lowmark after the flood. Two of them were smiling. Mind you don't come up smiling."

* * *

We borrowed the ferryman's diving gear, which turned out to be a coil of oiled rope, two lead weights, and a gourd each for air that held maybe forty breaths if you breathed like a monk. Sefa assured me this was generous by river-country standards. River-country standards, I was learning, mostly consisted of things being worse than you'd hoped but better than you'd feared, provided you didn't panic.

"You don't have to come," she said, checking the rope knots for the third time. "I can dive alone."

"And leave me sitting in a boat above a drowned village full of guild secrets? No. If you're going to find my family's business, I'm going to be there when you do."

"It might not be your family's business."

"The compass is mine. The name in your map margin is mine. The ferryman called me spring-child." I pulled the lead weight belt tight. "It's all mine, Sefa. Even the parts I can't remember. That's what makes it mine."

She looked at me for a moment—she does that, looks at you like she's reading a page faster than you can write it—and then nodded. "Rule one: if the bells ring, you surface. Don't think about it. Don't finish whatever you're doing. Surface."

"And rule two?"

"Don't touch the fish."

"Why would I touch the fish?"

"You'll see."

* * *

The water took us the way sleep takes you—not all at once, but in stages, each colder than the last.

First the green murk where the light came down in slanted columns and turned our bodies into shadows of ourselves. Then the rooftops, soft with silt, chimneys standing in rows like grave markers. Then the street itself, running below us, doors open, a cart half-buried in the road with its handles still raised as though waiting for an ox that had swum away a century ago.

I had never seen a drowned place. I had never seen any place with so much water in it that the water won. In the desert we built low and dug deep and gave nothing to the sky. These people had built tall and proud and left their windows open to the morning, and the morning had eventually come in and stayed.

Fish moved through the streets in schools, silver and unhurried, parting around us without interest. A catfish the size of a dog watched from a doorway with the expression of a retired official. Everything was quiet in the way deep places are quiet—not silent, exactly, but holding its breath.

And then, faintly, from somewhere ahead: a sound.

Not ringing. Not yet. More like a hum, the way a wet glass hums if you run your finger around its rim. It came up through my chest, through the water, through the bones of the drowned houses, and it made the silt shiver off the rooftops in little gray ghosts.

Sefa pointed. The tower rose out of the gloom ahead, its belfry open to the water on all four sides, arched windows like eye sockets. Weeds hung from the stonework in long banners. And inside, dimly, I could see the bells—three of them, hanging in their yoke, furred with a century of river growth, so that they looked less like instruments than like enormous sleeping animals.

The rope between us went taut twice. *Are you all right.* I tugged back twice. *Yes.*

We rose along the tower wall, hand over hand, into the belfry.

The hum grew. It wasn't coming from the bells, I realized. It was coming from the water itself, moving through the bronze in some slow tidal breathing, and every time it swelled, the smallest bell—the third one, cracked clean down one side—shivered and gave off a single soft note, like a word spoken just below hearing.

The key was there. I knew it before Sefa pointed. Wedged in the crack of the broken bell, glinting faintly even through the silt—a compass, twin to mine, its case fused to the bronze as though the metal had grown over it deliberately. As though the church had swallowed it and was keeping it.

Sefa worked at it with her knife. I held the bell steady, my palms flat against cold bronze, and that was when the memory hit me.

* * *

Not a memory like remembering. A memory like drowning.

One moment I was in the belfry; the next I was four years old, or five, and the water was everywhere and it was *loud*, and I was falling through green light with my hair streaming and my small hands reaching for a brightness overhead that kept getting further away. Someone had been holding me. Someone had let go. There was shouting above the surface, muffled into whale-song, and the bells were ringing—ringing wildly, all of them, alarm-bells—and I was sinking past a window and seeing faces in it, other children pressed to the glass, mouths open, screaming silently, and I thought with a child's terrible clarity: *they can see me and no one is coming.*

Then hands. Strong hands, closing on my wrists, hauling me up through the green into the white noise of the world, and a voice saying words I couldn't hear then and heard now, clear as church bells, in a woman's voice:

"*Breathe, little spring. Breathe. Your mother will kill me if you don't.*"

I gasped.

The gourd flew from my mouth. My arms thrashed. The rope burned through my grip and the belfry tilted and spun and the hum became a roar and the bells—

The bells rang.

All three of them, one great rolling peal that went through the water like a hammer through glass, and every fish in the village turned at once toward the tower, hundreds of silver faces, and the catfish in the doorway began, horribly, to sing—or to make the sound a song makes when it's been underwater for a hundred years, a groaning chord that vibrated in my teeth.

*Surface*, some sane part of me said. Rule one. But my body had forgotten rules. My body was four years old and drowning again, and the green light was pulling me down, and the last thing I saw clearly was Sefa turning from the bell, her eyes wide, her mouth shaping my name into the water.

Then her hands closed on my wrists.

Strong hands. Closing exactly where the memory-hands had closed, and hauling me up through the green, and I thought, distantly, absurdly: *so that's how it felt from the other side.*

* * *

I woke on the rooftop of the ferryman's boathouse, coughing river onto good shingles, with Sefa kneeling over me looking furious and relieved in equal measure, which on her face meant simply furious.

"Rule one," she said.

"I know."

"You *rang the bells*, Wren. You touched the bell and the bells rang and everything in that village started singing. Do you know what sings down there? Eels sing. I did not need to know eels sing."

"I'm sorry." I sat up. The world rocked gently and settled. "I remembered something. When I touched it. Someone pulled me out of the water. When I was small. During the flood. A woman—she called me—" My voice caught on it. "*Little spring.*"

Sefa went very still.

"She said my mother would kill her if I didn't breathe." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and cracked. "Sefa, I remember dying. Or almost. I remember the water taking me and nobody coming, and then somebody coming. And I don't know her face. I know her *hands*. Isn't that a terrible thing? To keep someone's hands and lose their face?"

Sefa sat down beside me on the shingles. For a while neither of us spoke. Below us the river moved in its long slow swells, and the tower stood in it, and I could almost believe the bells had gone quiet—but the water still hummed, faintly, like a throat clearing before a story.

"When I was eight," Sefa said finally, "the archivists found me in a records barge during a storm. No family. No papers. They asked my name and I lied, because I'd already learned names get you claimed by whoever hears them first. So they gave me a new one and a desk and I spent ten years being grateful and useful and completely invisible." She glanced at me. "Until I found a compass in a sealed drawer that pointed at *people*, and followed it to a ranger in the Ashen Flats who'd built invisibility into an art form. And I thought: there's someone worse off than me. Maybe we're each other's evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

"That we existed before the guild decided we shouldn't." She stood, wrung out her braid, and held out her hand. In her palm lay the second compass-key, pried free at the last possible moment, dripping, its needle swinging lazily—and then, as I reached for it, snapping taut. Pointing. Not north. Upstream, deeper into the hills, toward the mountains.

Toward the same bearing as mine.

Two needles, agreeing.

"Well," Sefa said. "That's inconvenient."

"Why?"

"Because the third key-holder is out there, and both compasses are pointing at them. And because—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide, deliberately, to tell me the truth, "—because when the bells rang, Wren, they rang a pattern. Three-and-one. Over and over. It's an old archivist signal code. It means *heir present*."

The river whispered beneath us. Somewhere under the green, the eels sang.

"Heir to what?" I asked, though the compass in my hand, warm now despite the water, already knew.

Sefa looked at me with those page-reading eyes. "To the spring," she said. "Wren. I think you're heir to the spring."

* * *

That night the ferryman fed us eel stew—he insisted, grinning, that after today we'd earned a taste of the singers—and refused payment, which worried me more than the guild riders had.

"Debts," he said, waving off Sefa's coin purse. "You don't pay a man for pulling a drowning child from his own river. That's not commerce. That's just what hands are for." He looked at me a long moment, too long, the way old men look when they're checking a face against a much older one. "Your mother had hands like yours. Quick. Always reaching for things falling."

"You knew her?"

"I knew a woman who came through here the summer before the flood, asking the archivists to hide something small and important. Paid in stories." He shrugged, and turned back to his net, and I understood the audience was over. "River keeps what it's given. Go to sleep, spring-child. You've got mountains ahead, and the mountains have got the guild, and the guild has got a very long memory and very short mercy."

I lay awake in the loft above his kitchen, listening to the water whisper under the floorboards, and Sefa lay awake across the room, pretending not to, and finally she said into the dark:

"You owe me now. For the dive. That's how it works, right? Debts?"

"That's how it works."

"Good." A pause. "I've decided what I want."

"Sefa—"

"A story. When we're done. The whole truth, start to finish, your family, the spring, all of it. Told to *me*, first, before anyone else. Before the archives, before the guild, before the desert." Her voice was sleepy and absolutely serious. "That's my price. The first telling."

I stared at the rafters, at the water-stains spreading across them like maps of countries that no longer existed, and found—to my considerable surprise—that I wasn't afraid of the price.

"All right," I said. "First telling. You have my word."

"Good," she said again, and rolled over, and within minutes was asleep, breathing easy as tide.

I slept too, eventually. And I dreamed of green light, and strong hands, and a woman's voice laughing somewhere above the surface, saying *breathe, little spring*—and when I woke at dawn, both compasses were lying on the sill where we'd left them, side by side, their needles straining upstream like dogs at a leash.

And on the door of the loft, nailed there sometime in the night, was a strip of oilcloth with a guild seal pressed in black wax.

The message was short. It was addressed, by name, to Sefa.
