## 8. What the Guild Wants

The patrol caught Sefa at dawn, which she considered deeply rude, because dawn was when she did her best thinking and it was very hard to think with a boot on the back of her neck.

She'd heard the horses first—she always heard horses first, a habit from her archivist days when the only things that came galloping toward the archive were auditors—and she'd had perhaps forty seconds to decide whether to run or stay. Running would have meant leaving the camp, the map, and Wren sleeping ten paces away with their hand curled around a knife they didn't know they'd reached for. Staying meant this: wet grass against her cheek, a boot between her shoulder blades, and six guild riders arranging themselves around her like punctuation.

She stayed. She was already composing the lie.

"Well," said a voice above her, dry as ledger paper. "The archivist's little runaway. Out in the world. Unchaperoned."

Sefa turned her head as far as the boot allowed and got her first good look at the speaker: a woman in guild gray, mid-forties, with the particular posture of someone who had spent her life sitting in chairs that other people weren't allowed to sit in. Her insignia was three silver drops on a field of blue—Senior Warden, third rank, which meant she could order executions but had to file paperwork about them afterward.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Sefa said. "You know what I am, and I only know what you are, and what you are is a long way from the nearest guildhouse, chasing a girl through flood country at dawn. Which suggests urgency. Which suggests the guild is worried."

The boot lifted. Sefa took that as permission to sit up, and did so with as much dignity as a person can manage with river mud on her chin.

"Warden Hesk," the woman said. "And we're not worried. We're thorough. There's a difference."

"In my experience," Sefa said, "the guild uses *thorough* the way other people use *worried*. It's the same feeling in a nicer coat."

One of the riders snorted. Hesk did not look at him, but the rider's horse took a small, nervous step sideways, as if it had been kicked by the silence.

"Where is your companion?" Hesk asked.

"What companion?"

"The desert ranger. Tall. Unfriendly. Walks like someone trying to be a shadow and mostly succeeding."

"I travel alone," Sefa said. "I've always traveled alone. It's in my file. You wrote half of it."

"I wrote all of it," Hesk said, and something flickered across her face that Sefa, after eleven years in the archives, recognized as the expression of a person who has read a file more recently than the person it's about. "Which is what interests me. Sefa Vell. Apprentice archivist, third circle. Discovered cataloguing restricted materials. Escaped the river-cities with a restricted map and an unlicensed theory. And yet." Hesk crouched, bringing them eye to eye. "You didn't run toward the river-cities, where your theory would make you famous. You ran toward the desert. Toward a person. That's not the behavior of an archivist, girl. That's the behavior of a lure."

Sefa's stomach did something complicated. She kept her face still. In the archives they'd taught her that documents lie in two ways: by what they say, and by what they leave out. People were the same.

"I ran," she said, "because the guild was going to burn my research and me with it. I ran toward the one person who'd already been robbed by the people doing the burning. That's not a lure, Warden. That's an alliance."

"An alliance." Hesk straightened, and gestured, and one of the riders brought her a leather satchel that Sefa recognized with a cold drop in her chest—her own satchel, taken from her bedroll while she slept, which meant at least one of these six was very good at his job. Hesk opened it and drew out the map of the river's forbidden upper reaches. She didn't unroll it. She just held it, the way a person holds a thing they intend to make you want.

"Let me tell you what the guild wants," Hesk said. "Since you seem to enjoy knowing things. You found the sealed records. You know the river doesn't begin where the river-cities say it begins. You know the spring was sealed a century ago—not dried up, not lost. *Sealed.* By the guild's founders, with the river-cities' grateful cooperation, because a river that can be measured at its mouth can be taxed at its mouth. Do you know what happens if that spring opens?"

"Water," Sefa said. "Clean water. Memory-water, if the old records are right. Every settlement on the flats stops buying guild water. The river-cities stop paying guild tariffs. The whole architecture of—"

"*Dependence*," Hesk said. "Yes. You were always good at the vocabulary. Control the spring, and we control the desert. Not the desert's people—the desert's *thirst*. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire guild charter. And the river-cities—" she smiled, thin and correct— "the river-cities have enjoyed a hundred years of independence bought with a lie. Independence ends. Everything ends. It's in the charter."

"You've memorized the charter," Sefa said. "Have you read it? The first page says the guild exists to serve the water. Not to own it. *Serve.*"

"The first page is for apprentices." Hesk rolled the map closed. "Here is what happens now. You're going to tell me where the spring's gate is."

"Or?"

"Or we stop being thorough."

Sefa looked at the six riders. She looked at the horses, stamping at the wet grass, at the river glinting gray-gold through the trees, at the satchel in Hesk's hands. She thought about Wren, asleep ten paces away, or—no. Not asleep. Wren had heard the horses too. Wren was almost certainly in the reeds right now with a knife and an opinion, and if Sefa screamed or stalled too long or bled, Wren would come out of those reeds and die for a girl they'd known for three weeks.

So she did the arithmetic archivists do best. She weighed the lie against the truth and found the lie heavier, which was how you knew it would hold.

"Fine," she said, and let her shoulders drop, and let her voice go small. "Fine. You want the gate? I'll give you the gate. But not here, and not for free."

Hesk's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You're in no position to bargain."

"I'm in the *only* position to bargain," Sefa said, "because I'm the only person alive who's read the third-circle seals and lived. The gate isn't a door, Warden. It's a lock, and the lock is keyed to a route, and the route is in my head, because I burned the map the night I left the cities. You can kill me and spend thirty years re-deriving what I can tell you before lunch." She held up one finger. "The gate is at the confluence above the drowned towns, where the river forks under the old watchtower. But the fork only shows at low water, in the dry season, and the approach is through the narrows—your boats won't make it, your horses won't make it. You need me. You need me walking, talking, and reasonably unbruised."

It was, she thought, a genuinely excellent lie. It had a real place in it—the confluence was real, the watchtower was real, the narrows were real and genuinely impassable, which was the best kind of detail because it cost the listener effort to check. It had a flaw in it, too, a deliberate one: the dry season was three months away, and Hesk would notice that eventually, and by then—

By then, Sefa planned to be somewhere else entirely.

Hesk studied her for a long moment. Then she laughed, one short breath through the nose, and Sefa understood with a lurch that it hadn't worked at all.

"Oh, that's *good*," Hesk said. "The burned map. The keyed route. The impassable narrows. You've been rehearsing that since you heard the horses, haven't you?" She tossed the map into Sefa's lap. "Keep it. We already copied it. And here's the thing your excellent lie can't survive, girl: we don't need you to find the gate. We need you to find the *key*."

The morning went very quiet. Even the river seemed to lean in.

"The first key was the compass you stole from the ranger," Hesk said. "We've tracked it for years—it surfaced in a desert market two seasons back, and we let it sit, because a compass in a ranger's pack is a compass in the dark. But then a runaway archivist stole it, and ran *toward* the desert, and suddenly the compass was moving with purpose. Toward the coast. Toward the caves." She crouched again. "The guild has hunted the other two keys for a hundred years. Do you know how we finally found one? We didn't. *It* found *you*. The keys are drawn to each other, Sefa. And you—sweet, clever, traitorous you—have been walking the second key straight toward the third."

Sefa's mouth was dry. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Hesk agreed pleasantly. "But you're going to think about it. Every night. You're going to look at your ranger friend and wonder." She stood, and snapped her fingers, and the riders began to mount. "We're letting you go. Consider it an investment."

"You're just—letting me go."

"We're letting *you* go. The ranger we'll collect later, when the keys are together. That's the whole point of bait, girl. You don't eat the bait. You eat what comes for the bait." Hesk swung up into her saddle and looked down, and for one moment the thin correctness fell away and Sefa saw the thing underneath it, which was hunger, old and patient and enormous. "A hundred years we've waited. A century of measuring water at the mouth while the source sits locked. Your parents' generation built the lie. Mine maintains it. Yours—" she smiled— "yours is going to hand us the door. Run along."

They rode out upstream, single file, and the sound of them faded, and Sefa sat in the wet grass with her map in her lap and her heart going like a trapped bird.

* * *

She didn't go back to camp. Not immediately. She sat by the river and washed her chin and made herself think, because thinking was the one weapon nobody had taken from her, though the guild had certainly tried.

Bait. She had been bait. Not an ally, not a fellow fugitive—a lure on a line, and she hadn't even felt the hook, because the hook had been her own idea. *Run toward the ranger.* She'd thought it was her decision, the night she'd slipped out of the cities with the map under her coat. She'd told herself she'd chosen Wren because Wren had been wronged, because the compass was evidence, because an archivist needed a witness.

And all of it was true. That was the elegant part. The guild hadn't needed to steer her with lies. They'd just made sure the truth pointed where they wanted.

She thought about the compass. She thought about the moment in the cave when the needle had swung away from north and settled, trembling, toward the mountains—toward the spring. She had assumed it pointed at a place. Everyone assumed a compass pointed at a place; that was the entire social contract of compasses.

But Hesk said the keys were drawn to each other. Drawn to each other. Not to a place. To *holders*.

The first key had been in Wren's pack. Wren had carried it for years without knowing what it was—which meant Wren had never been drawn anywhere by it, which meant either Wren wasn't a key-holder, or Wren was a key-holder so thoroughly asleep that even the key couldn't wake them.

And the third key. The third key-holder the guild had hunted for a hundred years. The family that had sealed the spring—the family the guild had erased, scattered, *hidden*. A child smuggled out. A child made invisible. *Keep her invisible.* Sefa had read those words in a restricted file once, in a record so old the ink had gone brown, and she had filed it under *legends* because that was safer.

A child who couldn't remember. A child the spring's sealing had hollowed out, because the spring fed memory and its absence starved it.

Sefa stood up. Her legs were unsteady. Downstream, faint in the morning light, she could see the thread of their campfire smoke, and somewhere past it Wren was probably awake by now, probably worried, probably pretending not to be, because pretending was Wren's native language.

*It points at people, not places,* she thought, and her whole body went cold, because she'd felt it herself and never let herself say it: the compass had never pulled her toward the mountains. It had pulled her toward a person. She had told herself it was the spring. She had told herself a great many things.

She started downstream toward the smoke, fast, then faster.

Because if Wren was the third key-holder—if the guild had spent a century hiding the one person who could open the gate, and had just spent three weeks letting Sefa deliver that person to them—then every mile they walked upstream wasn't flight.

It was an escort.

And Sefa was going to have to decide, before the narrows, before the fork in the river, whether to tell Wren the truth: that the compass had never been pointing home.

It had been pointing at them all along.
