## 10. Rapids of the Unmapped

The river changed its mind about being a river the morning after the memory market.

Sefa heard it before she saw it—a low, continuous growl somewhere ahead, like the earth grinding its teeth. She'd grown up on the river-cities' canals, where the water was polite, where it moved when you told it to move and apologized for the inconvenience. This was not polite water. This was water with opinions.

"Tell me that's not what I think it is," Wren said, coming up beside her with the particular flatness they used when they already knew the answer.

"That's white water," Sefa said. "A lot of it."

"How much is a lot?"

She squinted at the gorge ahead. The river, which had spent the last three days wandering lazily between drowned farms and half-sunken orchards, suddenly remembered it had somewhere to be. The banks pinched inward. The water stacked itself into ridges and dropped away in steps, and the spray coming up out of the gorge caught the early light and threw it around like a festival.

"The archivists have a map of the upper river," Sefa said. "It's about four hundred years old. It ends—" she pointed at the growl, "—approximately there."

"Wonderful."

"They stopped mapping because the surveyors stopped coming back."

Wren looked at her. "That's the kind of sentence you should lead with."

"I'm leading with it now."

They stood at the edge of the last mapped pool, and Sefa did what she always did before bad water: she read it. Not the way the desert people read sand—though she was starting to suspect Wren did something similar, some quiet arithmetic of wind and grain—but the way an archivist reads a page. The river had a grammar. A standing wave meant a rock beneath, patient and permanent. A V of smooth water pointing downstream meant a channel, an invitation. Foam piled against a bank meant the current had decided something over there and it wasn't negotiable.

The grammar of this stretch was mostly shouting.

"There's a line," she said, tracing it with one finger. "Right side of the first drop, then hard left before the haystack wave, then whatever happens after that is a surprise."

"I hate surprises."

"You grew up in the desert. Your whole life is a surprise."

Wren almost smiled. It was small and gone fast, but Sefa had been collecting those lately, the way other people collected river-polished stones.

They had a raft. The ferryman had thrown it in with the ledger—*you'll need it, spring-child, and you'll need more than that*—and it was a good raft, lashed from river-cane and sealed with pine pitch, the kind of thing that forgave mistakes. Sefa had built rafts since she was six. She knew how they flexed, how they complained, how much abuse they'd take before they told you the truth about your choices.

What she didn't know was the desert. And over the past days she'd watched Wren do things with water that made no sense to her canal-trained eye—finding shade where there shouldn't be shade, reading a dry wash's history from a single bent reed, knowing which mud would hold a boot and which would swallow it. Desert instincts. Water instincts, but for water that was mostly absent.

She had a theory forming. It was not a comfortable theory. She kept it in the same pocket where she kept the guild message she hadn't shown Wren, and the pocket was getting heavy.

"We portage around the first section or we run it?" she asked, though she already knew what Wren would say, because Wren had spent nine days proving that the fastest way between two points was whatever way Sefa was already going.

"We run it," Wren said. "The guild's behind us. The river's ahead of us. I'd rather be wet than caught."

"That's the first sensible thing a desert person has ever said about water."

"I'm full of them. Don't tell anyone."

* * *

The raft hit the first drop and the world became noise.

Sefa had steered rapids before. She'd steered rapids her whole life. But the canals of the river-cities were architecture—water that had been taught manners by a hundred generations of engineers. This was water that had never been told anything, water that had been sealed away and forgotten for a century and had clearly spent the time working up a grudge.

"LEFT!" she shouted, and drove her paddle into the current, and the raft swung, and the haystack wave she'd promised them rose up green and enormous and put the entire river in their laps.

They came down the far side of it spinning, and the spinning was the problem, because the river didn't care which way they were facing. The next V-channel—she could see it, a tongue of smooth water threading between two black rocks—was coming at them sideways.

"Wren! The channel! Right of the split rock!"

Wren didn't look where she pointed. Wren looked at the *water*, and Sefa watched them do the thing she couldn't do: read the desert's oldest trick, the way wind and current lie to each other. Wren's eyes tracked the foam, the drift of a broken branch, the way the spray bent.

"Your channel's a lie," Wren shouted back. "There's an eddy behind the split rock—it's sucking back upstream. Aim left of where she's telling you to aim!"

Sefa trusted a lot of things—archives, arithmetic, her own reading of water—but she did not, as a rule, trust desert people's opinions about rivers. She aimed where her instincts said.

The eddy grabbed the raft like a hand.

They swung broadside into the current, and the river, delighted, began to roll them. Sefa felt the cane flex, felt the whole world tilt toward green water and white noise, and then Wren was there—not paddling, *leaning*, throwing their whole weight against the high side the way you'd lean into a sand-slope to keep it from sliding, reading the raft's balance the way they'd read a dune.

"Counterweight! Lean!" Wren bellowed, and Sefa threw herself flat against the far tubes, and the raft groaned, argued with the river, and came back down level.

They shot the rest of the drop sideways, which was undignified, and hit the pool below, which was a mercy.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The raft drifted in a wide, slow eddy. Water ran off Sefa's braid. Somewhere upstream, the rapids were already forgetting them.

"Your channel was a lie," Wren said finally.

"My channel was *archival*."

"Your channel tried to drown us."

"My channel was *documented*," Sefa said, "in a four-hundred-year-old survey, which is more than I can say for your eddy."

Wren wrung out a sleeve. "The eddy was real."

"The eddy was *lucky*."

They looked at each other, dripping, and Sefa started laughing first, and then—rare as rain—Wren laughed too, a real one, startled out of them, and the sound bounced off the gorge walls and came back twice.

* * *

The second time the river tried to kill them was two hours later, and it was not funny at all.

The gorge narrowed until the walls leaned overhead, and the water went from loud to *furious*, and Sefa's grammar failed her completely. There were no V-channels here, no invitations, just a brown churning chaos where the river dropped through a slot no wider than the raft was long.

"We can't run that," she said. "There's no line. There's no *anything*. That's not rapids, that's a meat grinder."

Wren stood in the bow, one hand on the cane, and did the thing Sefa had come to recognize as their whole self distilled: the stillness. Desert people had it, the stillness of creatures who couldn't afford to waste a movement. Wren's eyes moved across the chaos the way they moved across a horizon.

"There," they said. "See the foam line? It's not straight. It bends around something."

"Rocks."

"Rocks *or* a shelf. Either way, there's slower water hugging the left wall. It's a seam. Deserts have them—where two dunes meet, the wind can't decide, and there's a pocket of still air. Rivers do it too. You just have to look for where the water's arguing with itself."

Sefa looked. She looked until her eyes ached, and then she saw it: a thread of darker, smoother water, no wider than a hand, running along the base of the left cliff. A seam. A pocket where the river couldn't make up its mind.

"That's not a channel," she said. "That's a rumor."

"It's a rumor that keeps us alive. Paddle when I say."

They threaded it. It was the worst hour of Sefa's life and she'd once spent a week hiding in a guild archive from a man named Brakka who collected fingers. The seam wandered. It pinched. Twice it simply *stopped*, and Wren read the water ahead and called the paddle strokes, and Sefa—archivist, river-born, raised on water that behaved—put her blade exactly where a desert ranger told her to, because the desert ranger was right every single time.

Halfway through the slot, the seam died for real.

The current took the stern and swung it, and Sefa went over the side.

The water was cold in a way canal water never was—cold with altitude, cold with the mountains it had come from—and it had her instantly. It rolled her, found her boots, pulled. She'd been trained for this too; she tucked, she counted, she waited for the eddy that every drowning current eventually offers. The eddy didn't come. The slot just kept pulling, and the walls went by too fast, and her lungs started making their case very urgently, and she thought, with the strange clarity of drowning: *the archive is going to record that I died in unmapped rapids, and no one will even know where to put the marker.*

Then a hand closed on her braid.

Wren had gone in after her. She didn't see how—she'd learn later that they'd dropped the paddle and thrown themselves flat along the raft's edge and caught her by the hair as she swept past, which was either brilliant or insane depending on which of them you asked. Wren hauled, and the current fought, and Sefa got one hand on the cane lashing and pulled herself up the raft's side like the water was a debt she was refusing to pay.

She came out coughing, on her hands and knees, alive.

Wren knelt beside her, soaked, shaking—not from cold, she'd realize. From the other thing. "I've got you," they were saying, over and over, quiet, like a spell. "I've got you. I've got you."

And Sefa, gasping, thought: *someone pulled you out of a river once, didn't they. And you never got to say thank you.*

"You idiot," she rasped. "You absolute—your paddle. You lost the paddle."

"Priorities," Wren said.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar below the slot, where the river widened and remembered how to be gentle. Wren built a fire with the efficiency of someone who'd done it ten thousand times in places where a fire meant life, and Sefa dried her clothes and her braid and tried to stop replaying the moment the seam had died.

Twice, today. Wren had leaned them level in the haystack wave; Wren had pulled her from the slot. And before that—she ran the ledger of it in her head, the way she ran all ledgers—the eddy call, the seam call, the mud-reading, the shade-finding. Nine days on the river and the desert ranger had been saving her life in small installments the whole way, quietly, without ever once mentioning it.

She'd told herself she'd found Wren by chance. Thief in the market, stolen compass, chase to the coast. A clean story. Archivists loved clean stories.

It wasn't true. And the fire was warm, and Wren was watching the flames with that stillness, and Sefa found she couldn't hold the pocket shut anymore.

"I need to tell you something," she said.

Wren looked up.

"The day I took the compass from your pack—I told you I borrowed it because it points to the spring. That part was true. But I didn't find you by accident, Wren. I'd been tracking the compass for two years. The archivists had records of a spring-key, and the guild had records, and everyone was looking for an *object*." She turned her hands toward the fire. "But I had a fragment. A scrap of an old survey, pre-sealing. And in the margin, in the surveyor's hand, there was a note. It said the keys weren't made to find the spring."

Wren was very still. "What were they made for?"

"To find the *keepers*. The families who tended it. The compass doesn't point north, and it doesn't really point to the spring either—it points to spring-blood. To people. It's been pointing at *you* your whole life, Wren. That's how I found you. I wasn't following the compass." She swallowed. "I *am* the compass. I've been following its pull since I was old enough to read the margin note, and every road I've ever taken bent toward the desert, and I told myself it was research, and it wasn't. It was the key looking for its keeper."

The fire popped. Downstream, the river ran on, patient, carrying its light.

"So when I lifted it from your pack," Sefa said, "I wasn't stealing. I was answering a summons I didn't understand. And I let you believe the simpler story because the true one sounded like—" she searched for the word, "—like fate, and I don't trust fate. Fate is what the guild calls its plans."

Wren said nothing for a long time. Sefa had learned to read their silences the way she read water, and this one wasn't anger. It was something more tired and more tender.

"My whole life," Wren said finally, "I thought I was invisible because I made myself that way. Ranger discipline. Survival. And now there's a compass that's been pointing at me since before I can remember, and a guild that's been hunting me, and a smuggler who saved my life *for a reason*, and a river that knows my face." A short, unamused breath. "I was never invisible. I was *hidden*. There's a difference, and the difference is that someone else chose it for me."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. You're the first person who's ever pointed at me on purpose." Wren looked at her across the fire, and the firelight did something unguarded to their face. "The compass points at people. Then answer me this. Does it still point?"

Sefa reached into her shirt and drew out the compass. The needle swung—away from north, away from the mountains upstream, and settled, steady as a heartbeat, on Wren.

They both looked at it.

"Well," Wren said. "That's either very flattering or a warning."

"With keys and keepers," Sefa said, "it's usually both."

She meant it as a joke. It landed as a prophecy, because at that moment, downstream, a lantern flickered on the dark water—then a second, then a third, in the patient spaced rhythm of a patrol that knew exactly how to search a riverbank.

Wren pinched out the fire with their bare hand, fast as a dune-snake, and the dark took them both.

"Guild?" Sefa whispered.

"Worse," Wren breathed, watching the lanterns. "That's Corvane's spacing. He taught his riders to move like a tide."
