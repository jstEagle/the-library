## 12. Alone Upstream

The gorge did not want company. That was the first thing Wren understood about it, standing at its mouth on the morning after Sefa walked out of the rain and back downriver, out of their life as cleanly as a knife leaving bread.

The walls rose sheer on either side, red stone streaked black where a century of seep had painted its slow calligraphy. The river ran fast and green between them, loud enough that Wren's own thoughts came back to them secondhand, shouted over the water like gossip across a market square. There was no trail marked on any map Sefa had shown them — but then, the maps had stopped here on purpose. The river-cities had drawn their borders at the last ferry crossing and filled everything above it with a polite blank space labeled *unreliable terrain*, which was how bureaucracies said *we are lying to you*.

Wren tightened the straps of their pack, checked their water — three skins, and a river right there, though drinking from it felt strangely like eavesdropping — and stepped into the blank space.

Alone had been their native country for twenty years. They had made a discipline of it, an art even. You learned to be invisible in the desert the way sand learned to be still: by practice, by patience, by never giving the wind anything to catch. Walking alone up a gorge that didn't want them should have felt like coming home.

It felt like walking through a house where every room remembered someone else.

* * *

The herons found them before noon.

Wren heard them first — a sound like branches being argued over, high on the cliff ledges — and then saw them: grey-blue birds the size of children, standing in the shallows on legs like folded compasses, staring at Wren with the flat, patient disapproval of tax collectors.

There were nine of them. Then fourteen. They did not attack. They *escorted*. When Wren walked, they flew ahead and landed, one by one, along the bank like a picket line of offended clerks. When Wren stopped to drink, they stopped too, and stood there, and stared.

"All right," Wren told them, wiping their mouth. "I'm trespassing. I know."

The largest heron — a magnificent, disreputable-looking bird with a crooked crest and the eyes of a customs official — took two steps closer and opened its beak in what could only be described as a bureaucratic cough.

"I'm going upstream whether you like it or not," Wren said.

The heron stared. The other herons stared. Somewhere above, a raven laughed, which Wren chose to take as solidarity.

They traveled together for the rest of the day, this procession of one ranger and fifteen birds, and Wren — who had spent years perfecting the art of being unnoticed — found it deeply humiliating to be so thoroughly noticed by animals. But there was something else under the humiliation, something they only admitted to around the cold campfire that night: the herons were not guarding the river *from* Wren.

They were guarding it *for* Wren. Or at least, they behaved like sentries expecting someone. Like soldiers holding a gate for a relief column that was very, very late.

Wren fell asleep to the sound of the river and dreamed, for the first time in years, in color.

* * *

The trail died on the third day.

Not faded — *died*, abruptly, at a bend where the cliff leaned out over the water like a man craning to look at something interesting below. What had been a shelf of packed earth became a smear of scree, then a suggestion, then nothing. Below it the river ran white over drowned boulders, loud and hungry.

Wren stood at the edge and did the arithmetic every ranger learns young: fall distance, water speed, rock hardness, odds. The numbers came back insulting.

"Right," they said aloud, because there was no one left to hear them not say things. "Options."

Option one: climb. The wall above offered handholds the way a miser offers coins — grudgingly, and mostly worthless. Option two: swim the channel, which the white water had already voted against on Wren's behalf. Option three: go back, which was not an option, it was a surrender wearing a practical coat.

Wren climbed.

It went well for forty minutes, which in Wren's experience was exactly long enough for the desert to establish trust before collecting interest. A ledge that had held their weight decided, somewhere around mid-morning, to retire. It went all at once — no groan, no warning, just a soft *whump* of stone letting go of stone, and then Wren was falling, hands slapping at rock, boots skidding, the river roaring up at them with its mouth open—

—and caught themselves. Barely. Fingers hooked into a crack no wider than a knife, body swinging against the cliff like a door on a bad hinge, feet pedaling air over thirty feet of very enthusiastic water.

Wren hung there and breathed.

Above them, the herons — who had followed, because of course they had — lined up along the rim and looked down with expressions of profound, avian *I told you so*.

"Don't," Wren said through their teeth.

The lead heron made its bureaucratic cough.

"I'm fine."

Cough.

"I am *fine*—"

And then it happened. Hanging there, blood hammering, arms burning, close enough to the water to feel its breath — Wren *remembered*.

Not a flash. Not the usual smoke-and-ash impression that passed for their childhood. A whole moment, sharp as a snapped twig: small hands on warm stone. Being four, maybe five, hanging exactly like this over exactly this kind of water, and a voice above saying — laughing, actually, *laughing* — *"Up. Arms up, little fish. The river's not done with you yet."*

A woman's voice. Brown hands hauling them up by the wrists. The smell of crushed mint on those hands.

Wren hung from the crack and shook, and it wasn't the arms.

When they finally hauled themselves onto solid ground and lay flat on their back on the sun-warmed stone, chest heaving, the herons gathered around in a loose ring and stood there in silence, which was either concern or a committee meeting, and honestly with herons it could be both.

"That was my mother," Wren said to the sky. To nobody. To the birds. "That was my mother's voice."

The lead heron coughed once, softly, almost gently, and Wren laughed — a cracked, wet-edged laugh, but a real one — and got up, and kept walking.

* * *

By the fifth day, Wren had stopped pretending the memories were accidents.

They came now like weather. A bend in the river would smell of juniper and a whole afternoon would arrive uninvited: running barefoot on this exact stretch of bank, a man with grey in his beard teaching them the names of stones, *"This one's granite, little fish, and this one's stubborn, and this one—" tapping Wren on the nose "—this one talks too much."* A night sky seen from a ledge, constellations named wrong on purpose, a family joke worn smooth by use. The taste of honey in water. Always the water. Water sweet as light, water that tasted the way remembering feels.

Wren began keeping a list, scratched into the back page of their field journal, because a ranger trusts paper more than a head that has already lost everything once:

*Day 3. Mother's voice. Mint.*
*Day 4. Father. Grey beard. Calls me "little fish." I talked too much.*
*Day 5. Honey-water. The water tastes like remembering.*

It was the last entry that stopped them.

They sat down hard on a boulder in the middle of the trail and read it back three times, and then sat some more, while the herons waited uptrail with the collective patience of a bureaucracy that has all eternity.

Because it wasn't a metaphor. Wren knew metaphors; metaphors were how you survived a childhood with holes in it. This was literal. Every mile upstream, the memories came faster, clearer, fuller — and every mile upstream, the water changed. Down at the coast it had been river-water, plain and brown and honest. Here it ran clear over green-gold stone, and when Wren drank it, the world came in sharper. Colors deeper. Sounds layered. And behind the colors, *memories*, arriving like letters postmarked decades ago.

Wren thought about the drowned towns. The villages where old people had sung songs no one had taught them. The ferryman who had looked at Wren's face and known a name. The church bells under the lake that divers swore rang sometimes, though there was no wind down there and no rope.

And Wren thought about the Ashen Flats. About the settlements that had adapted to scarcity — the water rationed drop by drop, the people rationing something else too, something no one had ever named. The way whole generations out there seemed to have thin pasts. Shallow family trees. Grandmothers who couldn't recall their own mothers' faces, and everyone shrugging it off as *the desert, the heat, the hard life*.

Everyone forgetting, everywhere, and calling it normal.

"Oh," Wren said quietly. The word came out of them like a splinter leaving skin.

The spring didn't just feed the desert.

It fed *memory*. It was the well the region drank its own past from — and a century ago, the guild had sealed it, dammed it, piped it thin, and the thinning hadn't just dried the land. It had dried the *people*. The amnesia wasn't an accident that had happened to Wren. It was a drought, and Wren had been living downstream of it their entire life. Their forgotten childhood wasn't a wound.

It was a *bill*. One the guild had sent to everyone, and Wren most of all, because the guild had gone further with them — had taken a child of the spring and made sure she stayed invisible, stayed empty, stayed *safe and useless*, a key melted down so no one could ever find the lock.

"Keep her invisible," their father had said, handing them away. Wren had thought it was kindness. It was both. It was kindness and it was the guild's victory, all in one sentence, and the sentence had worked for twenty years.

Wren sat on the boulder until the light went long and copper, doing the arithmetic again — the other arithmetic, the one without numbers. If the spring fed memory, then opening it would give the desert back its past. And if the guild had poisoned the outflow — Sefa's shout in the cavern, still ringing — then telling that truth might burn whatever goodwill the truth earned. Some would forgive. Some wouldn't. Wren had known that since chapter one of this whole miserable, miraculous journey, back when the thief was a stranger and north was still true.

What Wren hadn't known, five days ago, was that they'd be making the choice alone.

They looked uptrail. The herons stood waiting, fifteen clerks of the water, and beyond them the gorge narrowed toward a wall of living green far off — roots, vast and dark, climbing the cliffs like a hand closing over something precious.

The compass was gone — dissolved to water in their palms at the gate, or nearly there; Wren still carried the empty brass case, warm as a held hand. But they didn't need a needle anymore. The pull was in their own blood now, getting stronger with every mile, the way thirst gets stronger, the way homesickness does.

Wren refilled their skins from the clear gold-green water, drank deep, and felt another decade of fog lift off their mind like heat-shimmer.

"Come on, then," they told the herons, shouldering the pack. "Let's go meet my family."

The birds lifted as one and flew ahead, escorting, and Wren walked on alone up the gorge — except that *alone* had quietly stopped being accurate. They carried a mother's voice, a father's joke, a name the ferryman had spoken, a girl downriver somewhere who had lied to protect them and broken their heart doing it, and fifteen indignant herons who refused to let them drown twice.

Ahead, the roots thickened. The air changed — sweeter, older, full of a hum Wren felt in their teeth rather than heard.

And threading up through the river's roar, faint and impossible, from somewhere ahead and below, Wren heard a voice they knew.

Sefa's voice. And she was not alone.
