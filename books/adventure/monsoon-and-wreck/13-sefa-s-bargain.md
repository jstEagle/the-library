## 13. Sefa's Bargain

The trouble with doubling back, Sefa decided somewhere around the third mile of retracing her own footsteps, was that you had to keep looking at your own footprints. They told on you. *Here went a fool,* they said. *And here she turned around and went back anyway.*

She crouched beside a bend in the river where the water ran shallow over red stone, and she looked at those footprints for a long moment before doing something about them. Then she walked backward in them for twenty yards, which was undignified and nearly cost her an ankle, but it made the trail read as though someone had come downriver instead of up.

Wren would have hated this plan. That was partly why she hadn't told Wren about it. The other part—the larger part—was that if she had told Wren, Wren would have insisted on coming, and then there would have been no one left to walk through the gate when it opened, and Sefa had not spent three years in the archives learning to read sealed ledgers just to watch the last key-holder get caught in a pincer because he was too stubborn to let a girl lie to some soldiers on his behalf.

*His*, she thought, catching herself again. She'd been thinking of Wren as *he* since the ferryman's ledger, since the name that wasn't Wren's name, since the whole slow unraveling of whatever Wren had been before the desert made him over. It didn't matter. He was still Wren. Names were just watermarks; the paper underneath stayed the same.

She checked the sun. Two hours, maybe three, before the guild patrol finished sweeping the false route she'd fed them at the ford—or failed to finish, which was worse—and came looking for whoever had fed it to them. Three hours to put distance between herself and Wren's trail, and then to let them find her, deliberately, in the most disarming way she could manage.

It was, she reflected, a terrible plan. It was also the only one she had. Archivists were trained to work with what survived, not what they wished had.

* * *

She found the patrol's outrider by accident, which was the best way to find anything: the man's horse was drinking at a side channel, and the man himself was crouched behind a boulder with his trousers around his ankles, performing the universal soldier's ritual of pretending he was not, at this moment, extremely catchable.

Sefa considered her options. Sneaking past felt wasteful—he was exactly the kind of low-ranking, under-informed, easily-flattered asset the archives had taught her to cultivate. So she sat down on a flat rock within easy sight of him, took out her travel bread, and began to eat it with the unhurried confidence of someone who owned the riverbank.

He emerged from behind the boulder approximately four seconds later, trousers sorted, sword half-drawn, wearing the expression of a man who has just realized his afternoon has gone wrong in a way he will never be able to explain to his sergeant.

"Morning," Sefa said pleasantly. "You'll want to boil that water before your horse drinks much more of it. There's a parasite upstream, comes down after the rains. Kills horses in about a week. Terrible business."

The man blinked. His sword drifted uncertainly between *raised* and *lowered*, like a compass needle between two magnets. "You're—" He squinted at her. "You're the archivist girl."

"I'm an archivist," Sefa corrected gently. "There's a difference. 'Girl' implies I can be dismissed." She tore off another piece of bread. "You're a long way from your patrol, and they're a long way from anywhere useful. Shall I tell you why?"

"Because you're going to come with me," he said, finding his footing at last, "and explain yourself to Sergeant Hesk."

"Because I'm going to come with you," she agreed, standing and brushing crumbs from her coat, "and explain myself to Sergeant Hesk, and then to whoever Hesk reports to, and I am going to save all of you about six days of marching into country that will kill you. And when I do, you're going to remember that I was polite about it."

The outrider stared at her. Somewhere downstream, a heron screamed its rusty-gate scream, and the man flinched.

"That's a fish eagle," Sefa said. "They sound like that. You get used to it. Mostly."

* * *

Sergeant Hesk turned out to be a broad, weathered woman with a voice like gravel in a drum and the particular patience of someone who had been lied to by professionals and amateurs alike and had learned to enjoy both. She received Sefa at the patrol's camp—a scatter of bedrolls under a rock overhang, a cookfire, three horses too tired to be dangerous—with her arms crossed and her eyebrows already skeptical.

"So," Hesk said. "The archivist. Who walked out of our custody at the ford two days ago after promising us the spring-child was headed for the Salt Steps."

"I did promise that," Sefa agreed. "And I've come to correct the record, because the correction is worth more to the guild than my credibility is worth to me." She held out the map she'd spent the morning preparing: the real survey of the upper gorge, accurate in every detail except one, the one that mattered. "Your scouts are reading old charts. The Salt Steps route hasn't existed since the flood year—it collapsed. Anyone following it ends up in a box canyon with no water and no exit. I've marked the actual passable route here." She tapped the map, tapping a valley that did not lead to the gate. "That's where the spring-child is headed. Slowly. He's injured—ankle, from the rapids. You could take him tomorrow. Or you could follow your current orders and arrive at a dead end the day after he's already through."

Hesk studied her the way a butcher studies a carcass. "Why?"

"Excuse me?"

"Why correct the record? You escaped us. Best thing you ever did, from where I sit. And now you've walked back into camp to hand us the man you've been traveling with." Hesk leaned forward. "Nobody does that for free. So either you're lying, or you've been paid, or you want something. Which is it?"

"All three," Sefa said, "in roughly equal measure, and only one of them matters to you."

Hesk's mouth twitched. It was almost a smile. "Go on."

"The guild wants the spring. Fine. The guild has always wanted the spring; my own teachers helped bury the records saying where it was, and I helped dig them up again, so believe me when I say I know the shape of that appetite better than anyone alive. But there are three keys, sergeant, and the guild has spent years hunting key-holders one at a time, and every year the desert towns drill deeper wells and the river-cities build higher levees and everyone gets more desperate and less reasonable. I don't want the war that happens if the guild takes the spring by force. Nobody sensible wants that war. So I'm selling you the cheap version: the key-holder, delivered pliant, before anyone has to burn anything."

"And what do you get?"

"A seat at the table," Sefa said. "When the spring is opened, someone has to decide who the water goes to. I intend to be in the room."

Hesk was quiet for a while. Around the campfire, the other soldiers pretended very hard not to listen, which meant they were listening to every word.

"You know what I think?" Hesk said finally. "I think you're the best liar I've met in nineteen years of soldiering, and I think half of what you just said was true, and I cannot for the life of me work out which half. So we're going to do this properly. You'll ride with us. You'll guide us. And if your map is wrong—if there's so much as a dry creek where you've drawn a flowing one—I'll personally introduce you to the consequences, and I promise you they're creative."

"Fair terms," Sefa said, and shook on it, and spent the rest of the evening memorizing the layout of the camp, the rotation of the watches, and the exact location of the sergeant's spare knife, purely as a professional exercise.

* * *

Corvane arrived at dusk on the second day.

She heard him before she saw him—not hoofbeats, though there were those, eight riders moving with the loose discipline of people who had done terrible things often enough that it no longer required effort—but the *change*. The camp tightened. The sentries stood straighter. Even the horses went quiet, ears forward, watching the trail. Animals, Sefa had learned in the archives' animal-handling annex, were excellent judges of character, and these animals looked like they were being introduced to their new landlord.

Then he rode in, and she understood.

The legends had him wrong in every particular that mattered. Not a scarred brute, not a gray wolf of the water roads. Corvane was handsome in a worn, agreeable way, forty-some years old, with laugh lines he had clearly earned honestly and eyes that used them as camouflage. He swung down from his horse like a man getting off a ferry to greet a friend, clapped Hesk on the shoulder, complimented the cookfire, asked after everyone's feet, and generally radiated the warmth of a favorite uncle—which was precisely, Sefa thought, the problem.

"You must be the archivist," he said, turning to her before Hesk had even finished introducing them. "Sefa. The one who reads drowned things." He smiled. It was a good smile. It reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "I've read your theft reports. Well—your *colleagues'* reports about your thefts. Very thorough. You catalogued what you took. Do you know how rare that is? Most thieves can't even be bothered to apologize."

"I didn't steal anything," Sefa said. "I *liberated* records that were being illegally suppressed. The cataloguing was simply good practice."

"There it is." He pointed at her, delighted. "That's exactly what the report said. Word for word, nearly. Sit. Eat. You look like you've been living on river fish and stubbornness."

She sat. She ate. The stew was genuinely good, and she resented it.

For an hour he talked about nothing—about the flood year, about a ferryman he'd known on the lower reaches, about the proper way to dry boots—and he talked *well*, warmly, with the easy intimacy of a man who remembered names and details and deployed them like small gifts. Twice he made her laugh. Once he made her forget, for almost a full minute, that she was sitting across a fire from the person who had orchestrated the disappearance of an entire family, the poisoning of a river's memory, and the century-long theft of the desert's birthright.

Then, without any change in tone at all, still warm, still smiling, he said: "Now. The third key."

And the temperature of the conversation dropped ten degrees, though the fire hadn't moved.

"You have it," Sefa said. "Or you know where it is. Your patrols swept the archive quarter two years ago; the third key's provenance chain ends in your requisition seals."

"It does," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "I have it. Lovely piece of work—brass and river-pearl, older than the cities themselves. And it's been pointing, for some months now, at a person rather than a place. Did your research include that? No? A pity. The archivists who hid the keys understood something the guild never has: you can't lock a door that opens for blood. You can only hide the blood." He stirred the stew with a piece of flatbread, unhurried. "The compass points at the spring-child. It has been pointing at him since before he had a name anyone remembers. Which means the guild has known where he was for years. We simply needed someone to bring him to the door. Someone he'd trust." Another smile, thinner now. "Someone like you, Sefa. Or someone like the smuggler who saved his life and has been collecting interest on it ever since. We had two lines cast into the same water. You were the bait. He was the hook. And here we all are."

The fire popped. Sefa kept her face still, which took genuine effort, because her stomach had just performed a maneuver usually reserved for river rapids.

"You're telling me this because—"

"Because I want to make you an offer, and I want you to understand that I'm making it honestly. I find dishonest bargains vulgar. Lies are tools, not currency." He set down his bowl. "You want to be an archivist. A real one. Recognized, funded, with access to the deep vaults and the right to publish. I can give you that. Charter, title, salary, protection—the full weight of the guild behind your name. In exchange, you give me the gate. Not the spring-child; I'll have him regardless, one way or another, and we both know it. The *gate*. Where it is, how it opens, what it costs. And you walk away rich, respected, and safe, having sold a secret that was always going to be sold—by me, if not by you."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you stay exactly where you are," Corvane said, "which is in my custody, on a charge of theft from the guild archives, carrying a forged map, having confessed to bargaining with a sergeant above your station. The penalty for the first is your hands. For the second, your freedom. For the third—" he shrugged, charmingly, horribly, "—well. Sergeants are creative too. Ask Hesk."

Hesk, at the edge of the firelight, looked away and found something to inspect on her boot.

Sefa made herself breathe slowly. *Buy hours,* she reminded herself. *Not win. Hours.* Every hour this charming monster spent negotiating with her was an hour Wren spent walking toward the gate with his bad ankle and his unbearable stubbornness and his compass dissolving somewhere ahead of him into plain water.

"I'll need time," she said. "To verify the charter. To write to my colleagues in the river-cities. To close out my obligations. A week."

"Three days."

"Five."

"Four," Corvane said, "and I'll throw in a horse, because yours is lame and I find cruelty to horses distasteful." He extended his hand across the fire. "Do we have a bargain, archivist?"

She looked at his hand. Warm, open, well-manicured, attached to a man who had drowned a family's history and called it stewardship.

"We have a bargain," Sefa said, and shook it, and his grip was exactly as warm as his smile, and exactly as honest.

* * *

They gave her a tent—small, but hers, with a guard outside whom she was apparently expected to regard as a courtesy. She lay awake on a bedroll that smelled of someone else's pipe smoke, staring at the canvas, doing arithmetic.

Four days. Wren had perhaps two on her, maybe less, if the ankle held and the guardians of the gorge were feeling merciful. The false route she'd sold Hesk bought him another day beyond that, if the scouts believed it, and Hesk half-believed it—Sefa had seen the doubt in her eyes, the butcher's squint, the professional respect of one liar for another. Half a belief might be enough. Might.

And Corvane knew. That was the part that kept her staring at the canvas. He'd told her plainly: she was bait, she had always been bait, the guild had cast her into the water years ago and waited. Which meant everything she'd done—the escape at the ford, the map, the return, the bargain—had happened inside a trap whose walls she'd only just now been allowed to see. He hadn't caught her doubling back. He'd *permitted* it. He'd probably enjoyed it.

But.

But traps were built for the animal you expected. And Corvane expected a key-holder who would trade his debt for his life, a girl who would trade her principles for a title, a gate that would open for greed or fear or blood spilled carelessly. He did not expect—could not expect, because no ledger recorded it, because the archives had burned it, because only two people in the world knew it—that the gate killed liars. That the spring judged intent. That the water itself had opinions.

Sefa rolled onto her side and watched the firelight move on the canvas wall, and thought about Wren kneeling at a gate of living roots with a compass that had become a handful of river, and about what the water did to men like Corvane when they came to collect.

Four days, she had promised him. Four days of her life, signed over with a handshake.

She intended to spend every hour of them buying Wren more.

Outside the tent, the guard shifted his weight, and somewhere up the dark gorge, faint and impossibly far, a heron screamed like a rusted gate—and Sefa closed her eyes and listened, and thought: *that's a fish eagle, you idiot, and it means the river is still running, and so is he.*
