## 14. The Gate of Roots

The gorge narrowed the way a throat narrows when it's about to say something important.

Wren had been walking since before dawn, following a river that no longer behaved like a river. It ran quiet here, almost apologetic, sliding over pale stone in sheets thin enough to read through. The cliffs on either side rose in terraces, and every terrace wore its own garden—ferns spilling from cracks that should not have held soil, moss so green it looked freshly invented. After eleven days of drowned towns and memory-haunted ruins, after herons with opinions and trails that collapsed out from under them like bad promises, Wren had expected the upper river to be wild.

It wasn't wild. It was *tended*.

That was worse, somehow. Wild things you could outrun. Tended things meant someone had wanted them this way, and whoever did the wanting had been gone a long time, and still the ferns kept growing exactly where they'd been told.

Wren stopped at a bend where the water pooled around three smooth boulders and drank, cupping handfuls that tasted of nothing at all—which was to say, of everything, because water with no taste is water that hasn't been argued with yet. The compass hung on its cord against their chest, and all morning it had been doing something Wren refused to name. Not spinning. Spinning would have been easy. It was *leaning*, the way a dog leans toward a door it knows opens onto dinner. The needle had given up on north days ago. Now it gave up on anything except forward, straining against its housing like a small caged animal that could smell home.

"Fine," Wren said aloud, to the needle, to the gorge, to whatever was listening. "You win."

The needle, being metal, said nothing, but it settled, satisfied, and Wren felt absurdly like they'd just signed something.

They walked on. The trail climbed away from the water and then returned to it, and returned again, each reunion more intimate than the last, until the path was less a trail than an argument between stone and root that human feet had been permitted to witness. And then, around a shoulder of rock draped in ivy so old it had gone woody, Wren saw it.

The gate.

It shouldn't have been beautiful. It was, by any honest accounting, a wall—a wall of living roots grown over and through and around a frame of dark stone, woven so densely that no light passed, no draft, no sound except the faintest hum, like a hive heard from across a field. The roots were thick as a man's arm at the base and fine as hair at the top, and they had grown in patterns: spirals, waves, a repeating chevron that climbed toward the crown of the arch like water climbing a reed. Someone had trained them once. Or someone had *asked* them, and roots that old do not distinguish between instruction and prayer.

And there, at eye height, set into the stone beneath the weave where the roots had parted politely to make room—

A sigil.

Wren knew it. That was the terrible part. They had never seen it before in their remembered life; they could account for every year of themselves back to a riverbank and a pair of strangers' hands and nothing before. And still they knew it, the way you know your own name in a dream. A circle, open at the bottom, holding three drops that fell upward instead of down. It was carved deep and worn shallow at once, as if a thousand hands had touched it and a thousand years had tried to erase it and neither had won.

Wren stood in front of it for a long time.

Their hand came up without permission. They watched it rise the way you watch someone else's hand, and it pressed flat against the stone beside the sigil, and the stone was warm. Not sun-warm. The gate faced north into permanent shadow. Warm like skin-warm, like something under the surface was circulating, patient, alive.

"You're mine," Wren said quietly. "Aren't you. You've been mine the whole time."

The compass chose that moment to answer.

It didn't swing. It didn't point. It *dissolved*—there was no other word for it. The brass casing went soft in Wren's hands, soft as tallow, and then it went liquid, and the liquid went clear, and before Wren could do anything but gape, the whole instrument—the case, the glass, the needle that had lied about north for a hundred years and told the truth about everything else for eleven days—ran through their fingers like water and soaked into the ground at their feet.

Gone. Eleven days of following it, and it hadn't been a thing at all. It had been a message wearing a disguise, and now that it had delivered them, it had taken off its costume and gone home.

Wren knelt and pressed their palm to the wet earth where it had vanished. Nothing. No metal, no glass, not a grain of grit that hadn't been there before. Just damp ground and the lingering sense of having been carried somewhere by something that had never intended to stay.

"Well," Wren muttered, standing, dusting off their knees with the particular bitterness of someone who has just lost their only heirloom twice. "I hope wherever you are, you're happy."

The gate hummed a half-tone louder.

* * *

There was a flat stone before the arch, knee-high, worn smooth as a kneeler in a chapel. Wren circled it twice looking for a lock, a seam, a hinge, a keyhole—anything that matched the shape of the instrument currently seeping uselessly into the topsoil. There was nothing. There was only the sigil above, and the humming, and the growing certainty that the gate was not locked so much as *holding its breath*.

Sefa would know what to do. Sefa always knew what to do; she had a filing system in her head for legends the way other people had one for receipts. But Sefa was two days behind, or ahead, or gone—Wren had walked away from her in the rain and she had let them go, and neither of them had said goodbye because goodbyes imply endings and what they had was more like a dropped stitch that both of them were pretending wouldn't unravel the whole sweater.

So Wren was alone at the door of their own childhood, and the door was humming, and the only tool they had left was themselves.

They thought about the ferryman's ledger. *Yarrow*, written in faded ink beside a date a hundred years old, and next to it, in a different hand, a single word: *spring-child*. They thought about the vial from the memory market, the taste of it like cold rain, the fragment it had returned: their mother's hands on a mechanism, sealing; their father's voice saying *keep her invisible*; the specific terror in both their faces, which Wren had understood only later was not fear of the guild but fear *for* her. They thought about the church tower, the bells, the moment underwater when a small brown hand had hauled them up through green light—and the older, deeper memory underneath it, the original drowning, the first rescue, the debt that had started everything.

Blood. The old stories all agreed on blood. *The caves only open for people the water remembers,* the elder had said, and everyone Wren had met since had repeated some version of it, and every version had sounded, until now, like poetry.

It wasn't poetry. It was instructions.

"Oh," Wren said. "Oh, you have got to be joking."

But the gate was not joking. Gates of this age rarely were.

Wren took out their knife—the desert ranger's knife, short, honest, the one possession that had never once pointed anywhere but at what needed pointing at—and considered their options with the gravity the situation deserved. Palm. Thumb. The pad of a finger. They had rationed water and memory in equal measure for eleven days; they supposed they could ration blood too.

They drew the blade lightly across the heel of their palm—not a wound, a signature—and pressed the hand flat against the stone beneath the sigil.

For three full seconds, nothing happened, and Wren had time to feel extremely foolish, and to compose several sharp letters to the authors of legend regarding the reliability of their instructions.

Then the gate exhaled.

There was no better word. The hum dropped away and what replaced it was a long, low rush of air, warm and green-smelling, like the breath of something enormous waking from a nap it had taken a century ago. The roots moved. Not violently—they *parted*, peeling back from the center of the arch in slow spirals, each strand lifting away from its neighbor with the courtesy of dancers, and behind them the dark stone showed itself to be a doorway, and behind the doorway was a passage sloping gently down, lit from below by a light that was not firelight and not sunlight and not any light Wren had a name for. It was the color of the water in the source pool, which is to say it was the color of remembering.

Wren stepped through, and the roots closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting, and the desert, for the first time in their entire invisible life, was on the other side of a door.

* * *

The passage went down in long, gentle switchbacks, and the walls of it were carved—not decorated, *carved*—with the whole history of the river in bas-relief. Wren walked slowly, because there was no reason not to, and because every few paces the carvings said something their heart already knew and their head was only now catching up to.

Here: the spring, unsealed, a pool ringed by figures dancing. Here: fields green to the horizon, canals like silver veins, towns fat and laughing along the river's length. Here: riders in guild colors, and the same pool ringed now by figures building a wall, and the wall growing panel by panel, decade by decade, while the fields above turned the color of ash. Here—Wren stopped here for a long time—a smaller scene, tucked into an alcove as if the carver had wanted it found only by someone who was looking: two figures pressing a child into the arms of strangers, and above them the upward-falling drops of the sigil, and beneath them words in a script Wren couldn't read but somehow, distantly, recognized the way you recognize a lullaby sung in a language you've forgotten.

*Keep her invisible.*

Wren put their hand over the carving. Their palm had scabbed neatly; the blood had been spent, the toll paid, the receipt accepted. Under their fingers the stone was cool now, spent, like a bell after the ringing stops.

"I'm here," they told the carving. "You did it. I made it. It took me twenty-some years and a smuggler and a thief and a very opinionated heron, but I made it."

The passage forgave the sentimentality by continuing downward, and Wren followed, because the alternative was standing in a hallway talking to rocks, and even Wren had limits.

* * *

The cavern opened around them the way a held breath opens.

It was vast—cathedral-vast, though no cathedral ever built had dared this ceiling, which arched up into darkness stitched with veins of the same unnamed light, so that the whole space glowed from within like a lantern made of geology. And at the center, fed by a thread of waterfall that fell from a crack in the high dark and caught the light and turned it into falling glass—

The pool.

Wren had imagined it a hundred ways in eleven days. They had imagined it huge and churning, a cauldron, a fountain, a wound in the earth. It was none of those things. It was small. It was calm. It was round and shallow-edged and deep at the center, and it lay utterly still, and its surface reflected not the ceiling above it but—Wren leaned closer, disbelieving—images. Faces. Moving, flickering, gentle images, like the pool was dreaming with its eyes open. A woman laughing. A town on market day. A pair of hands braiding a child's hair. The water was full of remembering, and it was not hoarding any of it.

This was what the guild had sealed. This was what Corvane had crossed a desert to own. Not power, exactly—or not only power. *Everyone's pasts*, floating free in a hole in the ground, available to anyone thirsty enough, and the guild had built a wall between the world and its own memory and called the wall protection.

Wren knelt at the edge. The stone there was worn into a smooth hollow, shaped by generations of kneeling, and the hollow fit their knees as if it had been measured for them, which, in a way that made their chest ache, it probably had.

They looked at their reflection in the pool and their reflection looked back, and for once it wasn't alone. Around their mirrored face, faint as breath on glass, moved other faces—the mother from the carving, perhaps; a father with their same stubborn jaw; a hundred ancestors Wren had no names for, standing in the water like an honor guard.

"Hi," Wren said to all of them. "Sorry I'm late."

Then they cupped their hands, and dipped them, and drank.

* * *

The memory came up through them like floodwater through a dry creek bed, and it did not come gently, and it did not come in order, and Wren had the presence of mind to be grateful for neither fact because gratitude requires leisure and this was not a leisurely experience.

They remembered the spring unsealed, and their mother's hands on the mechanism, and the smell of the water, and the sound of their father arguing with a guild envoy whose smile was a weapon. They remembered the night of the sealing—torches, shouting, the wall going up panel by panel while their parents wept into each other's shoulders, and the specific, unbearable detail that the guild had not stolen the water by force. They had *bought* it. Cheaply. From frightened people. And then they had poisoned the outflow downstream, slowly, so that the desert settlements would grow dependent, so that scarcity would become policy, so that no one would ever again think to ask what the wall was hiding, because everyone downstream was too busy surviving to look up.

They remembered being four years old, and the second compass-key being sewn into the hem of their coat, and their father saying *she'll never know it's there, and that's how she'll live*. They remembered the drowning—the real one, the original—not an accident but an escape attempt, a four-year-old slipping away from strangers' hands toward the one place she remembered being happy, and the river taking her, and a smuggler named Corvane hauling her out, dripping and furious and five years old, and Corvane saying, with a kindness he had spent decades converting into leverage: *one day I'll collect*.

They remembered the strangers. Kind, tired, honest people who had carried a child across the Ashen Flats and taught her to be no one, because being no one was the only safety the century offered, and who had loved her in the specific, restrained way of people guarding something explosive, and who had died ordinary deaths and been buried in ordinary sand, and who had never once told her, because telling her would have been the one cruelty worse than silence.

Wren knelt at the pool's edge and wept—not prettily, not in the measured way of someone performing grief, but in the ugly, grateful, catastrophic way of someone meeting their family all at once, a hundred years late, in the reflection of a puddle of light.

And when the weeping slowed, and the memories settled into place like sediment finding a riverbed, Wren sat back on their heels and did the arithmetic they had been avoiding for eleven days.

Restoring the spring would drown the adaptations. The desert settlements had learned to live on poison-thin water; they had rebuilt their lives around scarcity the way a body rebuilds around a wound. Open the flow, and the canals would flood, the salt-crops would rot, the villages that had learned to survive would learn, abruptly, that survival had changed the rules. Expose the truth, and the truth would hurt everyone it was supposed to help.

Corvane wanted the spring to control the water trade. Wren wanted the spring to tell the truth. And the spring, it turned out, wanted something else entirely—to be *remembered*, loudly, publicly, in every village square from the coast to the flats, so that no wall could ever be built over it again, so that the choice about the water belonged to the people who drank it.

That was a fight for another day. Possibly another year. Possibly with torches.

Wren stood, dazed, hollowed, refilled, and turned to climb back up the passage and start being a person with a past in earnest—

—and heard, echoing up the switchbacks from the dark below, thin and distorted and unmistakable, a voice they knew.

Sefa's voice. Screaming. Not at Wren—at someone else. At someone whose laughter Wren also recognized, because they had heard it once before, years ago, on the day a smuggler pulled a drowning child out of a river and smiled while he did it.

Corvane had her. Corvane was *here*, under the mountain, in the one place on earth Wren had thought was finally, finally safe—

—and the gate behind them had closed, and the roots had sealed, and the only way out was down, through the source, toward the sound of the only person left who had ever chosen Wren without being paid, tricked, or fated to.

Wren turned. The pool glowed. The voices climbed.

"Hold on," Wren said, to Sefa, to the dark, to nobody, knowing it couldn't hear. "Hold on. I'm coming."

And for the first time in their life, Wren Yarrow went *toward* the sound of danger—not because a compass pointed there, not because a debt demanded it, but because someone they loved was on the other side of it.

The passage swallowed the light behind them, step by step, and the screaming grew louder, and the water at Wren's back rose an inch, as if the spring itself were leaning forward to watch.
