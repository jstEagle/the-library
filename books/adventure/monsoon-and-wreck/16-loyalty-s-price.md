## 16. Loyalty's Price

Sefa had been tied to a horse for two days, and she had opinions about it.

Most of them were rude. A few of them were architectural — she had spent the first night redesigning the saddle from memory, adding a cantle that would actually support a person whose wrists were bound behind their back and who was therefore riding with all the grace of a sack of turnips. The rest of her opinions concerned Corvane personally, and she kept those behind her teeth, because she had learned in the river-cities that the most dangerous men were not the ones who punished insolence but the ones who *enjoyed* it.

Corvane enjoyed everything. That was the horror of him.

He rode ahead of her through the gorge in a coat the color of wet slate, humming. Actually humming. Some old river-song that Sefa recognized from the archive nurseries — a lullaby about the water rising, which was either sentimental or prophetic depending on how his week went. His riders flanked them in silence. Guild men did not chatter. They had been warned about her, she suspected. *The archivist talks,* someone had probably said. *Don't listen to her.*

Which was flattering, honestly. She'd been underestimated by better institutions than this one.

"Sefa." Corvane turned in the saddle without slowing, so that he could look at her while addressing her, which was a habit of men who liked an audience even when the audience was one bound girl on a borrowed horse. "You've been quiet since morning. I find I miss it."

"I'm conserving my voice," she said. "For screaming."

He laughed — a real laugh, warm as bathwater, and that was the trap of him too. "There she is. You know, I meant what I offered you. The archive. Your name on the door. A chair with your own key to it." He let the word *key* sit there between them like a coin on a table. "All you have to do is tell me where the gate is."

"The gate is wherever Wren Yarrow is," Sefa said. "I told you that already. The compass dissolved. The map is a person now. You're not hunting a location, Corvane, you're hunting a girl, and girls are famously difficult to file."

"Mm." He clicked his tongue at his horse and faced forward again. "Then we follow the girl."

They followed the girl.

The gorge narrowed around them as they climbed, the walls going from red stone to something older and darker, veined with pale roots that thickened as they rose until the rock seemed less carved than *grown*. Sefa had read about this in the sealed folios — the ones the guild had locked away three generations ago, the ones she had picked open at fourteen with a hairpin and a grievance. The roots were the gate. The roots were the lock. And somewhere above them, walking ahead of them, was the only blood in the world that fit the keyhole.

Wren didn't know they were being followed yet. Or — no. Sefa corrected herself. Wren knew. Wren always knew; that was the whole point of being a ranger. What Wren didn't know was that Sefa had fed the patrol a false route two days ago, buying hours, and that the false route had run out at dawn when Corvane caught her at the ford, and that every hour she'd bought had been spent, and now the ledger was empty and she was riding toward the gate as a hostage with nothing left to trade except—

Except the truth. Which was, she reflected, the one thing the guild had never learned to price correctly.

* * *

They found the gate at dusk.

It should not have been possible to miss it, and yet it nearly was, because the gate did not look like a gate. It looked like a cliff face that had been argued with for a hundred years and had won. Roots the thickness of temple pillars wove across the stone in a lattice, and woven through the lattice — Sefa's breath caught, because she had seen this sigil once before, pressed flat in wax on the oldest document in the forbidden folios — was the mark of the Yarrow family: a circle split by a line, like a seed cut open to show what lived inside.

Corvane dismounted. He did not hurry. That was the worst part of him, Sefa thought — the patience. Greedy men were supposed to be fast, grasping, obvious. Corvane strolled up to the roots the way a man approaches a shop counter, and put one gloved hand against the lattice, and waited.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. The roots did not care about gloves or gold or guilds.

"Open it," Corvane said, without turning around.

"I'm flattered," Sefa said, "but I'm more of a librarian than a locksmith."

He sighed, the way a patient teacher sighs, and nodded to one of his riders, and the rider took Sefa down off the horse none too gently and stood her in front of the gate with a knife resting against the side of her throat — not pressing, just *present*, the way weather is present. Corvane stepped back to give himself a view of both the girl and the gate, because he understood composition. He had always understood composition.

"Wren!" he called, and his voice went up the gorge and came back down changed, doubled, the way voices do in stone. "I know you can hear me. Rangers hear everything. It's your one redeeming quality."

Silence. High up among the roots, something shifted — a bird, or a shadow, or a girl deciding.

"You have something of mine," Corvane went on, conversational now, almost gentle. "A debt. Sixteen years old, accruing interest. You owe me your life, Wren Yarrow, and I have come to collect it in the only currency I accept." He gestured at Sefa with an open hand, a showman's flourish. "But I'm reasonable. I'll take the gate instead. Open it, walk away, keep breathing. Refuse, and I'll spend this one slowly, and then come find you anyway, because I always do. Ask anyone. My collections are famous."

Sefa felt the knife's edge hum against her skin when she swallowed, and she made herself swallow, deliberately, so that she would feel it, so that fear would be a fact instead of a fog. This was a trick she'd taught herself during her first interrogation at fifteen: name the fear precisely and it becomes information. *Knife. Throat. Man with excellent tailoring and terrible ethics.* Information could be used. Fog could not.

Above them, at the top of the root-lattice, a shape moved against the last of the light. Small. Deliberate. Coming down.

*No,* Sefa thought, with a ferocity that surprised her. *Don't. Don't you dare come down here for me.*

Because that was the thing about Wren Yarrow that Sefa had figured out somewhere around the drowned bells, somewhere between the second compass and the campfire where Wren had finally laughed — really laughed, ugly and startled, like a person remembering how — that Wren paid debts. All of them. To the last coin. Wren would walk down to this clearing with their throat already bared because a life was owed and Corvane had come to collect, and Sefa refused — *refused* — to be the invoice.

So she did the only thing left in her inventory. She talked.

"Before you waste your leverage," she said loudly, pleasantly, to Corvane, "you should know something about this gate."

"Sefa," Corvane said, warning in it, mild as milk.

"It kills liars." She let that land. She watched it land — saw the stillness go through the riders, saw Corvane's head tilt a degree, the degree of a man recalculating. "That's the whole design of it. That's why your guild sealed the upper river instead of taking the spring outright, a century ago. You couldn't. The gate opens to spring-blood and honest intent, and your founders had neither. There are records. I've read them. I've *catalogued* them, Corvane, with marginalia. Do you know what I do to documents? I index them. Everything you buried, I filed under *evidence*."

"That's a charming story," Corvane said.

"It's an indexed story," Sefa said. "Cross-referenced. You're welcome to test it. Put your hand back on the roots. Go on. Worst case, I'm wrong, and you look foolish in front of your men, and frankly you could use the humility."

She was lying, of course. Partially. The records said the gate opened to spring-blood; the part about liars was folk-creed, archive gossip, the kind of story archivists told each other over cold tea. But Corvane couldn't know that, and more importantly his men couldn't know that, and a hostage is only worth as much as her captor's certainty. So she stood there with a knife at her throat and planted doubt in the soil of twelve armed men, and watched it grow, and thought: *Grandmother would be proud. Grandmother would also ground me.*

And then, because a moment bought is only useful if you spend it —

"WREN!" she shouted, up at the roots, with everything her conserved voice had left. "THE GUILD POISONED THE OUTFLOW! YEARS AGO! BEFORE THE SEALING! THE DESERT ISN'T DYING OF THIRST — IT'S DYING OF *POISON*! THE RIVER-CITIES DIDN'T HOARD THE WATER, THEY FLED THE WATER! CHECK THE LEDGER! CHECK THE FERRYMAN'S LEDGER!"

The clearing went very quiet.

Even the knife-holder faltered, the blade drifting a finger's width off her skin, because men will forgive a hostage for struggling but not for making sense.

Corvane turned to look at her fully for the first time, and the warmth went out of his face all at once, like a lamp snuffed, and what was underneath was worse and older and much more tired. "You know," he said, "I had such hopes for you. The archive. The chair. Your name in gilt lettering. And instead you stand there doing the one thing I cannot allow." He raised his voice to the roots, to the darkening gorge, to the shape that had frozen halfway down the lattice. "Whatever she told you, girl, consider the source. Archivists embroider. It's a occupational disease."

"It's an occupational *duty*," Sefa snapped. "Embroidery is decorative. Documentation is load-bearing. And I'll tell you what else is load-bearing—" she twisted slightly against the rider's grip, enough to see the roots, enough to speak straight up at the descending shape, "—Wren, listen to me. Whatever you remember about why you were hidden, whatever the vial showed you, it's incomplete. Your parents didn't seal the spring to punish the desert. They sealed it because the guild had already fouled the outflow and blamed the sealers for the drought. The lie isn't that the spring exists. The lie is *which side poisoned whom*. The truth you're carrying isn't just yours. It belongs to every village that buried a child thinking the water-gods were angry."

She was crying now, she realized, distantly, the way you notice rain on someone else's window. She didn't stop talking. Archivists don't stop talking. It's the whole job.

"And if you open that gate to save me," she said, quieter, and this part was not strategy, this part was just true and it hurt going out, "then Corvane wins without lifting a hand, and I will haunt you, Wren Yarrow, I will haunt you *thoroughly*, I will reorganize your pack every night for eternity—"

"*Enough*," Corvane said, and the word cracked like ice.

He moved fast now, finally, the patience burned off. He crossed the clearing to the roots, dragged Sefa with him by the arm — the knife-rider scrambling to keep up — and pressed her back against the lattice so that the pale roots framed her like a saint in a bad painting, and he looked up at the shape coming down through the weave, and his voice dropped into something low and certain and horribly kind.

"Come down, spring-child. Come down and open your family's door. Do it, and the archivist lives, and I give you my word on the guild's seal — and my word, whatever else may be said of me, has never once been broken." He smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the gorge. "Refuse, and I'll make sure she watches the desert drink poison for another hundred years, and knows, every day, that it was her mouth that told you the truth too late."

The roots hummed against Sefa's spine. She felt it — a vibration, faint, like a struck bell heard through a wall — and she understood suddenly, with the particular clarity of a person standing inside the mechanism, that the gate was listening. That it had been listening the whole time. That every word spoken in this clearing was falling into it like sediment into a riverbed.

And she thought, with a wild, giddy, terrified hope:

*It doesn't kill liars. But maybe it remembers them.*

High above, the shape stopped moving. In the blue last light, Sefa could see her now — Wren, small against the vast weave, one hand flat against the roots, the other closed around nothing, around the ghost of a compass that had become water in her palm. Watching. Deciding.

Below, Corvane waited with his hand fisted in Sefa's collar and his patience gone to ash, and behind his eyes Sefa could see the arithmetic running — the debt, the key, the girl, the gate — numbers that had never once included the possibility that the spring-child might answer him with the truth.

The roots tightened, very slightly, around them both.

Like a held breath.

Like a door, deciding whether to open — or to close forever on the hand that reached for it.
