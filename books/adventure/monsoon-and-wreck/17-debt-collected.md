## 17. Debt Collected

The gate of roots stood open behind me like a mouth full of green teeth, and I had just enough time to think *I should have closed it* before the torchlight came around the bend of the gorge.

They came in single file along the ledge—six riders afoot, horses left below at the last ford, and at their head a man walking with the unhurried gait of someone who has never once been late for anything that mattered. Corvane. The smuggler. The savior. The man whose name I had carried in my chest for eleven years like a stone I'd mistaken for a heart.

He looked older than the legend. Leaner. His hair had gone silver at the temples in a way that was almost elegant, and his coat—dark river-cloth, water-shedding, absurdly fine for a man who'd spent the morning climbing a gorge—was buttoned to the throat against the spray. He smiled when he saw me. It was a good smile. That was always the problem with Corvane. Everything about him was good, right up until it wasn't.

"Wren Yarrow," he said, and my name sounded strange in his mouth, formal as a summons. "You've made excellent time. I'd have wagered on you reaching the spring by month's end. You've beaten my estimate by nine days."

"I didn't have an estimate," I said. "I had a compass."

"And where is your compass now?"

I held up my empty hands. "It became water."

His smile didn't falter, which told me everything. A man who could hear that—the metal of a hundred-year-old key dissolving in a desert ranger's palms—and not blink was a man who had already decided how this story ended.

Behind him, two of his riders dragged something forward. I knew the shape of her before I saw her face. Sefa, wrists bound with river-cord, hair plastered to her cheek, one boot missing entirely. She looked, I thought with a small fierce pang of pride, absolutely furious about the boot.

She caught my eye and shook her head once—a tiny motion, deliberate, meant only for me. *Don't.* Don't do whatever you're planning. Which was unhelpful, because I hadn't been planning anything, but now of course I was.

"Corvane." I stepped forward, away from the gate, putting my body between him and the opening. "Let her go."

"Let her go?" He laughed, and it was a warm laugh, genuinely warm, and I hated how much I remembered loving that laugh. "Wren. You misunderstand the arrangement. There is no arrangement. There never was an arrangement. There is a debt."

The word landed in the gorge and echoed off the wet stone. *Debt. Debt. Debt.*

"You pulled me out of a river," I said. "Eleven years ago. You pulled a drowning child out of the water and you fed her and you taught her the flats and you let her believe, all this time, that it was kindness."

"It was kindness," he said mildly. "And it was investment. Both things can be true. I've never understood why people find that so distasteful. A farmer is kind to his field and expects a harvest. Nobody calls the farmer a monster."

"A child isn't a field."

"No," Corvane agreed. "A child is better. A child can walk."

* * *

Here is what I want to say about the next few minutes, and I say it knowing how it will sound: I was calm. Genuinely, deeply calm, in the way of a person who has finally run out of futures to worry about and is left standing in the present tense with nothing to do but look around at it.

The gorge was beautiful. I noticed that. The walls went up and up, streaked with mineral bloom, and the river ran loud below us, and the air smelled of wet stone and green growing things—the smell of the source, drifting up from the open gate behind me like bread from an oven. Somewhere upstream a bird was making an idiot of itself, some kind of territorial shrieking fit, and part of my mind filed it away as *heron, chapter twelve, still holding his grudge.*

Corvane walked toward me across the flat shelf of rock. His riders spread out with practiced ease—not surrounding me exactly, because there was nowhere to go with the gate at my back, but arranging themselves the way men arrange themselves when they want you to feel surrounded without the indignity of admitting it.

"Do you know why I saved you?" he asked. He stopped six feet away. Close enough to talk. Close enough, if it came to it, to reach.

"Because you were there," I said. "Because the water was high and a kid went under and you happened to be the kind of man who jumps."

"That's the version you needed," he said. "And I let you keep it, because it kept you alive. But here's the true ledger, Wren, since we're settling accounts tonight. Eleven years ago I pulled a drowning girl out of the Ashwater, and she was clutching something. A compass that wouldn't point north. I noticed. I notice everything; it's the trade I'm in. And I did the arithmetic every smuggler does: *what is this worth, and to whom?*"

He tilted his head, and the torchlight moved across his face like weather.

"The answer took years. The guild's old records, the sealed archives, the stories the river-cities whisper and deny. Three keys. One spring. A century of lies built on top of a locked door. And the door doesn't open for force—it opens for blood. For a spring-child. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I didn't save a child. I saved the only living thing on this earth that could give me the water trade entire. Every kindness since—the training, the trust, the freedom, the long leash—all of it was watering a crop."

Sefa made a sound behind him. Not a word. Just a noise, low in her throat, the noise a person makes when they are being physically restrained from committing violence and are losing.

"You could have asked," I said. "All those years. You could have told me what I was. You could have shown me the gate and said *this is your family's door, will you open it?* And maybe I'd have said no. But you'd have asked."

"And if you'd said no?"

"Then no."

Corvane sighed, and it was almost tender. "You would have said no. Of course you would have. You're your mother's daughter down to the bone—I've read enough of the old records by now to know exactly whose stubbornness I was up against. No, Wren. People don't hand over kingdoms when you ask nicely. They hand them over when the alternative is worse. When the debt comes due and the interest has compounded past anything they can pay. That's not cruelty. That's just how large transactions work. Ask any bank."

"I have met banks," Sefa said from the ground. "They're nicer than you."

"Archivists," Corvane said fondly, without looking at her. "Always footnoting."

* * *

"So," I said. "The debt."

"The debt." He drew his knife.

I want to be precise about this moment, because precision is all I have left. The knife was not dramatic. It was a working blade, plain-hilted, the kind of knife a man uses for cutting rope and cheese and, apparently, the occasional debt. He held it loosely, at his side, the way you hold a tool you've used ten thousand times.

"Your life," he said. "Owed since the day I pulled you from the river. That was always the shape of it, wasn't it? You've known. Some part of you has always known. Why else would a person spend eleven years being invisible? Why else would a ranger who can track a lizard across hot stone refuse to be tracked, refused to be *known*, refused to owe anyone anything except the one debt too large to see over the top of?"

He was right, and I hated him for it, and I hated myself more for the relief that came with being hated correctly.

"You want the spring," I said.

"I want what the spring buys. Control of the water. Every cistern from the coast to the deep desert, every well, every rain-catch, every life that drinks. One hundred years ago your family locked the source because the cities above were drowning in plenty while the desert died of thirst, and the compromise was scarcity for everyone. Noble. Ruinous. Over." He spread his hands. "I'm not a villain, Wren. I'm a correction. The desert needs the water. I am simply going to be the one who decides where it goes."

"And the deciding," I said, "is the whole point."

"The deciding," he agreed pleasantly, "is the profit margin."

Behind him, Sefa had gone very still, in the way she went still when she was calculating. I'd seen it on the raft, in the flooded town, in the memory market when the vial-seller tried to shortchange us. Stillness was her battle stance. Whatever she was computing, it had a lot of variables.

"So here is what happens now," Corvane said. "You open the inner vault. The gate is open, yes, but the source itself sits behind the old seal-stone, and the seal answers to a spring-child's blood freely given—freely, Wren, the records are very insistent on that point, and I have learned to respect the records. You give your blood. You speak the opening. My people take custody of the flow. And your debt—" he raised the knife, and the light ran down it like water "—is collected. Cleanly. Honorably. In front of witnesses. You die having paid what you owed, and I will carve it on your marker myself: *she paid in full.*"

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I start with the archivist." He didn't even turn to look at Sefa, which was somehow worse than if he had. "And then I work outward through everyone you've ever been kind to, and I know exactly how many people that is, because I taught you to count them."

* * *

I knelt.

I felt Sefa's shout catch in her throat—felt it rather than heard it, the way you feel a door slam three rooms away—and I knelt on the wet stone of the shelf with the gate breathing green and cold behind me, and I bowed my head forward, and I said:

"Then collect it."

The gorge went quiet. Even the idiot heron stopped.

Corvane came closer. I heard his boots on the stone, measured, unhurried. He set the blade against my throat, and it was cold, colder than the spray, cold as the bottom of the river that had nearly taken me when I was seven years old.

And here is the thing I need you to understand, the thing I did not fully understand myself until the steel touched skin: I was not surrendering. I was fishing.

Because I had drunk from the pool. Last night, alone, in the dark of the source cavern, I had cupped the memory-water in my hands and drunk until I remembered everything—and one of the things I remembered, one of the things my mother said with her hand pressed flat against the seal-stone while the guild hammered on the far side, was this: *the spring judges intent. The water knows why you come. It always has. That is the whole design, and it is the only lock that cannot be picked, because greed cannot pretend to be thirst for longer than it takes the first mouthful to swallow.*

My mother had built the trap a hundred years before I was born. I was simply the bait that had finally grown up.

So I knelt, and I offered my throat, and I waited to see whether Corvane was quick enough to feel the floor move under him.

He hesitated.

It was a small hesitation. A flicker. The blade stayed against my skin but the arm behind it went uncertain, the way a man's arm goes uncertain when the arithmetic he's been running for eleven years suddenly returns a number he doesn't recognize. Because here was the flaw, the beautiful, catastrophic flaw in his ledger: a dead key-holder opens nothing. The seal wanted blood *freely given*, given *living*, given by a hand that chose it—and if he cut my throat now, on this stone, with my consent manufactured by his knife, the spring would read the whole transaction for exactly what it was. Theft. Murder dressed as collection. And the water would know.

"You hesitate," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Why do you hesitate, Corvane? Collect. The debt's on the table. I'm kneeling. I'm offering. Isn't this what you crossed the desert for?"

"Quiet," he said, softly, and the softness was the sound of a man thinking very fast.

"Or is it that the debt was never really mine to pay?" I lifted my head, slowly, so the blade rode my skin but didn't bite. "Is it that you need me alive? Is it that the great collector of debts has discovered his prize asset is also his single point of failure? You built everything on me, Corvane. The training. The leash. The long game. And now the game requires a dead woman who must remain living, a free gift that must be coerced, a willing hand that must be forced. Your ledger doesn't balance. It never balanced. You just never got far enough downstream to see the waterfall."

"Get up," Corvane said.

"No."

"*Get up.*"

"You'll have to kill me to make me get up," I said, "and killing me costs you the spring, and we both know it, and now your riders know it, because I just said it out loud in front of them."

That was the moment. I felt it in the stone under my knees—a tremor, faint, rhythmic, like a heartbeat coming up through the mountain. The water in the gorge below changed its note, dropping half a tone, the way a river does when something upstream has opened. Mist began to rise off the surface in slow ropes, climbing the cliff walls, and the mist was warm and it smelled of green places and old summers and my mother's kitchen, smells that do not belong in a gorge, smells that belong to a spring deciding something.

"What is that," one of the riders said. Not a question. A plea.

"The spring," I said, "is listening."

Corvane turned toward the gate, and I watched his face do something I had never seen it do in eleven years of knowing him, first as legend, then as savior, then as betrayer, and now as simply a man standing in a place that was about to tell the truth about him. His face *flickered*. Confidence, calculation, charm—and underneath, briefly, horribly, fear.

The mist thickened. It coiled around his boots. And in the mist, unmistakably, shapes: faces in the vapor, dozens of them, hundreds, the faces of everyone whose water he had ever rerouted, every village that had gone dry so his ledgers could stay fat, every season of thirst he had priced and sold. They weren't accusing. That was the terrible part. They were just *looking* at him, with the patient attention of water, which has all the time that has ever existed.

"Corvane," I said, still kneeling, still with his knife at my throat, and I confess that what I felt then was not triumph. It was something sadder and larger. Pity, maybe. Or the beginning of it. "Take the blade off my throat. Take it off, and step back from the gate, and I will ask the spring to be gentle with you. I can't promise it will listen. But I'll ask. That's more than you ever gave anyone."

The water rose.

Not fast. Not violent. It rose the way truth rises in a room where it has been locked out for a century—up through the stone, up through the seams, gleaming black and silver in the torchlight, climbing the walls of the gorge in sheets, and the faces in the mist grew clearer, and one of the riders dropped his torch and fled, and another followed, and Corvane stood his ground because Corvane always stood his ground, it was the only prayer he knew.

"Wren," Sefa said. She had gotten one wrist free—I never found out how, though I suspected her hairpin and would have married the hairpin if asked—and she was on her feet, bound hand half-raised, staring past me at the rising water with her eyes enormous. "Wren, the cavern. Look at the cavern."

I turned.

The gate of roots was glowing. Light poured out of it, gold-white, the color of the pool itself, and the sound coming up from below was not water anymore. It was voices. Hundreds of them, layered, murmuring, the sound of a hundred years of stolen memories all trying to come home at once—and beneath the murmur, gathering strength, a single clear note like a struck bell, like a bell underwater, like—

"Drowned bells," Sefa whispered. "It's the bells. The whole river remembers at once, and it's coming *here*."

Corvane's knife hand dropped to his side. He looked at the light, and the faces, and the water climbing his fine dark coat, and he said, quietly, in the voice of a man reading his own obituary in someone else's handwriting:

"What have you done?"

"Nothing," I said, getting to my feet at last, my knees wet, my throat unbroken. "I knelt down. That's all I did. I gave the spring a clear view of you."

The first wall of water came around the bend of the gorge like a hand coming around a door.

And Sefa ran toward it.
