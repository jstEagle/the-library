## 19. What the Water Carried

The river had changed its mind about us.

I noticed it first at the shallows below the gorge, where the water ran over pale stone the way it always had—except it wasn't pale anymore. It was clear. Not desert-clear, which is a polite word for thin. Clear like glass held up to the sun, clear enough that I could count the pebbles on the riverbed and see my own face looking back at me from the surface, and for once the face looking up didn't feel like a stranger's.

Sefa crouched beside me and cupped a handful of it, letting it run through her fingers.

"It's carrying something," she said. "Can you feel it?"

I could. Drinking from that water was like hearing your own name spoken in a crowded room—you turned before you could stop yourself. A mouthful of the river and suddenly I remembered the smell of bread my mother used to bake, a bread I had no name for, in an oven I'd never seen. It passed through me like weather and left something behind.

"Small things," I said. "It carries small things."

"Small things are how everything starts." Sefa grinned at me, and there was river-mud on her cheek and she hadn't noticed, and I decided not to tell her, because some things you carry just because they make you happy to look at.

We walked downstream for three days, following the water as it widened and slowed, and everywhere we went, the waking had already begun.

* * *

The first village was called Brack's Landing, or had been, once. The guild's maps called it a ruin. It was not a ruin. It was forty people living in the ribs of drowned houses, fishing the slow channels where streets used to be, and when we came down the bank they met us with spears until an old woman tasted the water, spat it out, and started crying.

Then she started singing.

I didn't know the song. But every person in that village older than fifty did, apparently, because within a minute half of them were singing too—a rowing song, Sefa whispered, from before the sealing, a song the archivists had recorded as lost—and they sang it badly and out of tune and one man kept forgetting the third verse and starting over, and nobody cared. The spears came down. Somebody put a bowl of fish stew in my hands and told me to eat before I fell over, in the tone of someone who has raised six children and recognizes hunger on sight.

That night, sitting by their fire, I watched a fisherman hold his wife's face in both hands and say, "Your name is Odile. Your name is Odile," over and over, like a man saying grace, because the fever years had taken her memory of herself and the river had just handed it back.

"She used to sing to me," he told me later, by way of explanation, though no explanation was needed. "I'd forgotten her voice. Now I can't get it out of my head. It's wonderful. I may never sleep again."

I laughed, and it surprised me, laughing. There had been a time—not so long ago, though it felt like another life, because it was another life—when I would have stood at the edge of that firelight and been invisible. That was the whole art of me: Wren Yarrow, ranger of the Ashen Flats, a person shaped like a gap in the conversation. My father's last instruction, carried across twenty years and a stolen memory: *keep her invisible.*

Well. The river had other plans, and rivers generally do.

* * *

By the fourth village, they were waiting for us.

Word travels faster than water when the water is good news. They'd heard there was a spring-child come down from the source, and they'd heard the spring was open, and they'd heard—I felt this one arrive like a change in wind—that the reason the water had been thin for a hundred years was not drought, not fate, not the desert's natural temper, but poison. Deliberate, upstream, human poison. Guild poison.

And they'd heard that the person standing in front of them was the daughter of the family who had sealed the gate to keep the spring safe, and had let the world believe the water simply died rather than hand it to the men who would have made a weapon of it.

So I told them. All of it, standing on the levee at Duskmere with the whole village below me and Sefa at my shoulder, and I will not pretend my voice was steady, because it wasn't. I told them about the guild's century of theft dressed up as scarcity. About the outflow channels my parents had found laced with mineral salts to keep the memory out of the water even where it flowed. About Corvane, and the debt, and the compass, and the girl beside me who had stolen—or borrowed; we were still litigating the verb—the only thing I owned. About my own name, which was not Wren Yarrow, and my own childhood, which I had lived without keeping.

"You drank this water your whole lives," I said, "and it gave you what it could. Songs. Faces. Small mercies. It would have given you everything, if not for us—for my family's war and the guild's greed and my silence. I'm not asking you to forgive that. I'm telling you because you've been lied to long enough, and I was one of the lies."

Silence. The bad kind, the kind with weight.

An old man near the front—Duskmere's ferryman, I learned later, though his ferry had been a rope-and-plank affair since before I was born—spat into the river and said, "My son died in the thirst years. Forty-one of them, the year the wells failed. You're telling me that didn't have to happen."

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly what I'm telling you."

He looked at me a long moment. "Then I'll hate you a while," he said. "Ask me again next season." And he turned and walked off, and I let him, because that was more honesty than most people manage at a funeral, and grief deserves room to be ugly.

But an old woman pushed through the crowd after him, took my hand, and pressed something into it: a river-stone, smooth and grey, worn round. "My mother told me about the spring-children," she said. "Told me they'd come back when the water did. I thought she was making it up to comfort me. Turns out she was making it up to comfort *herself*, and both of us were right." She squeezed my hand hard enough to hurt. "You were a child. Children don't owe for their parents' wars. Whoever told you different was selling something."

*Some forgave,* I wrote in my journal that night, by firelight while Sefa slept. *Some didn't. Both were right.*

* * *

Not every village got the story gently. In Harrow Ford, a man threw a cup of river water in my face—which, given what the water does now, meant I spent the next hour remembering a grandmother I'd never met, her kitchen, her terrible singing, her habit of calling everyone "duck," including the goat. I sat on the levee steps wiping my eyes and couldn't tell Sefa whether I was crying from grief or from being called *duck* by a dead woman's memory.

"Both," Sefa said comfortably. "Grief and love come in the same bucket. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone where?"

"River-cities. We're very wise. It's the damp."

Harrow Ford's headman was less gentle than his water. He listened to all of it, arms crossed, and then said: "So the desert starved while the spring ran fat behind a gate. And now you've opened it, and the desert will drink, and the river-cities will drink, and everyone will live happily ever after except the hundred years of people who didn't." He looked at me the way you look at weather you can't argue with. "What do you want from us, spring-child? Absolution isn't mine to give. I just want to know what happens when the desert comes down here thirsty and finds out who kept the tap shut."

"I don't know," I said. It was the truest thing I said all week. "But I'd rather stand between them with the truth than hide behind a lie. Lies are how we got here."

He grunted. Then he fed us dinner anyway, because that's what river folk do—they'll curse you and set a place for you at the same table, sometimes in the same breath. His wife made a flatbread with river-cress that I think about still, at odd moments, the way you think about a kind word someone said years ago.

* * *

It was in Harrow Ford that I understood the thing I'd been circling for weeks.

Belonging isn't a debt. I had spent my whole life believing it was—that I owed Corvane my life, that I owed my parents my silence, that I owed the desert my invisibility, one great ledger of obligation with my name at the bottom of every column. Debts can be collected. That's what makes them debts. And anything that can be collected can be taken away.

But the old woman with the river-stone hadn't offered me forgiveness as payment for anything. The ferryman at Brack's Landing hadn't sung his broken song because anyone deserved it. The fisherman saying *your name is Odile* wasn't settling accounts. The water doesn't remember because it should. It remembers because it wants to. That's the whole nature of it.

Nobody owes me a place. That used to terrify me. Standing on the Harrow Ford levee, watching the clear water slide past under a sky going soft and orange, it finally felt like the opposite of terrifying. If belonging is offered, then it can be accepted. And I was, at long last, spectacularly good at accepting things. I'd had practice. A girl stole my compass and I accepted her. A river flooded my past back into me and I accepted that too, mostly, on good days.

"What are you smiling at?" Sefa asked.

"The water," I said. "It carried all this. Every song, every face, every terrible flatbread. A hundred years underground behind a gate and a lie, and it comes out still carrying gifts."

"That's water for you." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Stubborn. Keeps what matters."

"So do you."

"I know," she said. "It's my best quality. That and the mud on my face, which, by the way, has been there since Brack's Landing, and you *knew*, and you said nothing—"

"Some things you carry," I said, "just because they make you happy to look at."

She stared at me. Then she laughed so hard she had to sit down on the levee, and the headman's wife, passing with a basket, looked at us both and shook her head fondly, the way people do at children and fools and other beloved disasters.

* * *

We might have stayed in Harrow Ford a week, eating cress bread and teaching the children desert songs in trade for river ones, if the rider hadn't come.

He came down the north road at dusk, horse lathered, guild grey gone dusty and travel-worn, and I had my knife halfway drawn before I saw that he wasn't riding like a soldier. He was riding like a messenger who has been riding for days and hates every mile of it. He swung down, saw me, and visibly revised whatever speech he'd prepared.

"You're the ranger," he said. "Yarrow. Or—" he squinted— "whatever your name is. The spring-child."

"Depends who's asking."

"Nobody, anymore." He pulled a folded paper from his coat, and I watched his hand shake, and I knew before he spoke. "The guild's done. Finished. Corvane's captains scattered the day the flood hit the lower stations—half of them woke up remembering why they joined and hating themselves for it, the other half just ran. There's no one left to give orders. There's barely anyone left to disobey them." He held out the paper. "This was posted at every crossing on the river. Thought you should see it."

I unfolded it. The guild seal was stamped crookedly, the way seals get stamped by people who no longer believe in what they're stamping. The notice declared the guild dissolved, named Corvane as a fugitive from his own organization, and then—this was the part that stopped my breath—it listed, in careful clerk's handwriting, every crime the guild had buried for a century. The theft of the spring. The poisoning of the outflow. The names of the families who had hidden the truth, and the names of the families who had kept it.

At the bottom, in fresher ink, someone had added a line:

*The full record is being assembled at the river-cities, by the Archivist Sefa, who asks that anyone holding guild documents bring them forward, no questions asked.*

I read it twice. Then I looked up at Sefa, who was reading over my shoulder, and whose face had gone carefully, deliberately blank in the way it does when she's planning something enormous and wants to watch me figure it out first.

"When," I said, "exactly, were you going to mention that you're founding a free archive?"

"When you asked," she said serenely. "You're asking now."

"You sent word ahead. Downriver. While I was busy being spat at and forgiven."

"I sent word ahead," she agreed, "because somebody has to write all of this down—all of it, the guild's crimes and your family's sacrifice and the ferryman's song and the old woman's stone and every cup of water thrown in your face—so that no one ever gets to unremember it again. That's what archivists are *for*, Wren. We're the part of the river that walks on land." She paused. "Also I need a partner. The job description says so. It's very specific. 'Must be stubborn, must be good at accepting things, must tolerate mud.'"

I looked at the notice. At the crooked seal. At the road running south, toward the river-cities, toward whatever came next. Behind me the water slid past, clear and patient, carrying its small cargo of songs and faces toward villages that were learning, one memory at a time, how to be whole.

"Well," I said, folding the notice and tucking it inside my coat, next to where a compass used to live. "I suppose someone should keep you honest."

"Someone should," Sefa said, and held out her hand, and I took it, and we turned south together as the rider remounted and clattered off to spread the news, and behind us the river kept talking in its low bright voice, saying everything it had carried a hundred years to say—

—and ahead, on the far bank, a lantern flickered in the dusk where no lantern had been an hour before, and a figure stood watching us, and I knew the walk, and the coat, and the way the man held his shoulders like a man deciding something.

Corvane had come downstream. And he was walking toward us with his empty hands open.
