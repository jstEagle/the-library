## 20. The Archivist's Oath

The river-cities had a smell Sefa had forgotten she loved: wet stone, lamp oil, and the particular green tang of algae that the guild's sanitizers spent a fortune pretending didn't exist. She stood on the Tarbridge in Vell, the oldest of the three cities, watching barges slide beneath her feet, and thought: *I could have had this.*

That was the thing about Corvane's offer. It hadn't been a bribe, exactly. Bribes were crude. What he'd offered her in that lantern-lit hold was *legitimacy*—a seat at the archive, her name inked into the registry, the title she'd been chasing since she was nine years old and first pressed her nose against the sealed doors of the Restricted Stacks. *Archivist Sefa of the River-Cities.* He'd said it like he was handing her a key.

And she had wanted it. That was the part she refused to lie to herself about. She'd wanted it so badly her teeth had ached.

She wanted something else more.

"Sefa!" A voice cut across the bridge traffic. "Sefa, the ink is *congealing* and the apprentice is crying and I need you to tell me which of these is the real charter and which one is the forgery the guild planted, because honestly at this point they both look equally guilty to me."

Sefa turned. Doss, her deputy—self-appointed, unanimous vote of one—came pelting across the Tarbridge with an armful of documents and the expression of a man who had made peace with chaos and simply moved in with it. Behind him trotted the apprentice, a ferocious girl of thirteen named Pell, who was not crying but had clearly been crying recently and would deny it under torture.

"The forgery," Sefa said, "will have the guild's watermark in the lower corner. It's subtle. A wave inside a circle."

Doss squinted. "This one has a wave inside a circle."

"That's the forgery."

"This one has a wave inside a circle *and* it's on nicer paper."

"They spent more on the lie than the truth. That's the whole guild in one sheet of paper, Doss."

He grunted, filed that away somewhere, and handed her the real charter. The Free Archive of the River-Cities. Three words the guild had spent a century making illegal, and now they were written on a document with a wax seal Sefa had made herself, using a button and a candle, because budgets were what they were.

The archive itself occupied a decommissioned tide-mill at the city's edge—a building the guild had abandoned because the millwheel no longer turned and the lower floors flooded twice a day. The guild had seen a liability. Sefa had seen a building that *remembered the tide on a schedule*, which was more than she could say for most archivists she'd trained under.

They'd been open eleven days.

In eleven days, people had brought them: a guild ledger of water-ration manipulations; a fisherman's log proving the outflow had been poisoned decades before the drought the guild blamed; three separate confessions from retired clerks who'd each assumed they were the only one with a conscience and had been too frightened to check; and one very confused man who'd brought a jar of pickled eels, which they'd kept, because turning away donations set a bad precedent.

And today—the last of it. The story Sefa had been waiting to write down.

* * *

Wren arrived at the tide-mill an hour before the tide did, which Sefa had learned was typical of them. Desert people treated water the way sailors treated weather: with respect, with schedules, and with a low-grade suspicion that it was personally out to get them.

"You're early," Sefa said.

"You're surprised."

"I'm never surprised by you anymore. I've downgraded to *mildly bemused*. It's a healthier place to live."

Wren came in out of the light, blinking, and stood in the archive's main room the way they always did—like a ranger entering unfamiliar territory, cataloging exits, assessing threats. The exits were two doors and a window. The threats were Doss, Pell, four thousand documents, and a cat named Ledger who had adopted the building and considered himself senior staff.

"Sit," Sefa said. "Please. I need your permission for something, and I'd rather you were sitting down when I ask, because you go very still and very desert when you're startled, and it makes me nervous."

Wren sat. Ledger immediately colonized their lap with the entitlement of a creature who had never once been refused. Wren, who could out-stare a heron, was defeated in four seconds.

"The story of your family," Sefa said. "The sealing of the spring. Your parents. What they did, and what was done to you. I want to write it down. All of it. In the archive, in the open record, with your name attached—not the spring-child's, *yours*. Wren Yarrow. And I won't do it without your blessing. Not one word."

The room was quiet except for the millwheel, which groaned on its axle like an old man asked to move furniture.

Wren looked down at the cat. Sefa watched them do the thing they did—reaching for the silence where memory used to be, and finding, these days, that the silence had been filled back in. It had to be strange. Sefa had spent her life around records; she knew that a restored document was never quite the same as an intact one. The creases stayed.

"You'll write it true?" Wren said. "Not kind. True. Even the parts where my parents were frightened and made choices I might not have made. Even the part where the desert settlements have a right to be angry at what the sealing cost them."

"True," Sefa said. "I've built an entire institution on it. Well. An institution and a tide-mill. The institution is mostly aspirational."

"And the parts where I—" Wren stopped. Started again. "The parts where I didn't know. Where I spent years chasing a compass and calling it grief when I didn't even know what I was grieving."

"Those are my favorite parts," Sefa said, and meant it, and watched Wren understand that she meant it. "Because that's the lie the guild told best, Wren. Not that the spring didn't exist. That forgetting was *safety*. That a person could be better off not knowing their own name. I want the record to show what that costs. What it cost you."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Then: "You have my blessing. All of it. My parents' names were Ilsa and Tam Yarrow. Write them where people will say them out loud."

"They'll be the first names in the open record," Sefa said. "Before the guild's. Before Corvane's. Before mine."

"That last one I insist on," Wren said, and the ghost of a smile crossed their face—the rare one, the one Sefa had learned to treat like a solar eclipse: noteworthy, not to be stared at directly, best witnessed in company.

* * *

She wrote for three days.

Doss brought her tea until she threatened him with the pickled eels. Pell sat across the table copying documents in her fierce, careful hand, and every so often would read a line of what Sefa was writing and go quiet, and once said, "They gave you their *child*," about Wren's parents, in the voice of a girl who was taking notes for a different reason than the assignment.

On the third evening, Sefa read the finished account aloud to the room—Wren, Doss, Pell, Ledger (asleep, but present in body), and eleven visitors who had come in from the rain and stayed. She read about the spring and the sealing. About the compass-keys. About a child carried out of a drowned village and told to be invisible, and about a ranger who spent a lifetime being no one, and about the day the water remembered them anyway.

When she finished, nobody clapped. That was right, she thought. Some stories aren't for clapping. An old woman in the back simply said, "I knew an Ilsa Yarrow. She mended nets in Marrow's Reach. I always wondered where she went." And then she cried a little, and Pell put a cup of tea in her hands, and that was the archive working, Sefa thought. Not the documents. The tea.

Afterward, Wren walked the lower floor with her, where the water came in with the evening tide and lapped, gentle and scheduled, at the base of the shelves.

"You could still have it," Wren said. "Corvane's offer. The registry would take you now, after all this. You'd be the most famous archivist in a generation."

"I considered it," Sefa said. "For nearly four seconds. Then I remembered that Corvane once tried to drown me in my own bargain, and I'm told that's frowned upon in professional registries."

"He's in custody."

"He's in *Vell* custody, which is a revolving door with excellent hinges. He'll charm his way out by spring. He always does." She watched the water rise around the shelf-legs, marked in careful painted bands by the previous, sensible owners. "Besides. He offered me recognition *from the guild*. I don't want to be recognized by the people who buried the truth. I want to be the person who dug it up. Different job. Better hat."

"You don't have a hat."

"I'm going to get one. An archivist's hat. Doss is terrified."

Wren laughed—short, surprised out of them, the best kind—and then went quiet in the way that meant there was something underneath.

"Sefa. The debt."

"Ah."

"I've been thinking about it. What I owed Corvane. What I owed you. I've been keeping accounts my whole life because it was the only way I knew to belong to anything—owe enough, and people keep you around." Wren turned to face her, and the tide-light moved on their face. "I'm done with debts. I don't want to owe you and I don't want you to owe me. I want—" A pause. The desert stillness, and then, deliberately, the choice not to be still. "I want a partnership. Chosen. Renewed whenever we both say so, and dissolved the moment either of us doesn't. No ledger. No interest accruing. Just—two people who keep choosing each other."

Sefa found, to her considerable annoyance, that her eyes were doing something. "That's the least archivally sound contract I've ever heard. No terms? No conditions? No clause for what happens if one of us gets eaten by a heron?"

"We'll improvise."

"We'll *improvise*." She wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, as if dealing with weather. "Fine. Yes. Obviously yes. But I'm writing it down anyway, because I'm me, and the record will show that on this date, in a flooded mill, Wren Yarrow proposed a contract with no terms and I accepted it, which future historians will cite as proof that the collapse of civilization began in the river-cities."

"Let them," Wren said. "Historians need the work."

They shook on it, formally, absurdly, standing in rising water, while a cat watched from a dry shelf with the expression of an entity who had seen empires fall and remained unimpressed.

* * *

The oath came a week later, because Pell pointed out that a free archive needed more than a charter—it needed an oath, something people could say out loud, something that would outlive them. Sefa wrote it on the back of a guild requisition form, for the symbolism, and read it to the room on the morning the first official archivists—Doss, Pell, and two of the retired clerks with consciences—stood to swear it.

It was short. Sefa had fought her own instinct toward eloquence and won, barely.

"*I will record what is true. I will record it whole, including what shames me and mine. I will not trade truth for comfort, title, or safety. I will leave the record open, so that those who come after me may correct what I got wrong, which will be a great deal.*"

Doss cried. Pell pretended Doss was crying. The old clerks said it like they'd been waiting forty years for permission.

Wren stood at the back, and when it was done, they came forward and did something Sefa hadn't expected: they took the oath too. Not as an archivist—as a witness. "I'm not keeping records," Wren said, a little sheepishly, aware of the whole room watching. "But I'll hold to it. Truth over comfort. Even out there." A nod toward the east, toward the desert, toward the Ashen Flats where a ranger's work was waiting, and where a great many people were going to want answers about the guild's poisoning, and where Wren intended to give them, whatever it cost.

"You're leaving soon," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen it in how they'd started oiling their pack straps again.

"Soon. The desert deserves the truth from someone who was there. And—" the almost-smile, "—I'm told the river runs all the way to the flats now. Clear water. Small memories coming back. Songs. Faces. A ranger could do worse than follow it home."

"Take the new compass."

"You made me a compass."

"I made you a *terrible* compass. The needle points wherever you decide. Pell says it's not a compass at all, it's a metaphor with a glass cover. I say metaphors are underrepresented in navigation." She pressed it into their hand—brass, tide-marked, the needle resting at peace because it had no master but Wren. "So you stop looking for north. North was always a lie anyway. You told me that. Chapter and verse."

Wren closed their hand around it and looked at her for a long moment, and Sefa understood that this was the goodbye—not forever, never forever, not for people who had chosen each other without terms—but the kind of goodbye that meant *the road splits here, and I trust the road, and I trust you on yours.*

"Write it all down," Wren said. "Even the parts about me that are embarrassing."

"Especially those. I've already started. There's a whole section on the heron incident."

"That heron started it."

"The record will show," Sefa said, "that the heron was defending a nest, and you escalated."

Wren laughed and shouldered their pack, and walked out of the tide-mill into the morning, east along the river road, getting smaller, until the light took them.

Sefa watched until they were gone. Then she went inside, sat down at her table, opened the ledger to a fresh page, and wrote the date, and beneath it: *The river runs clear. The archive is open. Everything is recorded.*

Pell leaned over her shoulder. "What do we do now?"

Sefa looked out at the water rising, right on schedule, faithful as a promise. Somewhere east, a desert was learning to trust the rain again, and somewhere in Vell, a cell door with excellent hinges was about to swing open, and somewhere in the Restricted Stacks of the old guild archive—she was almost certain, because Pell had found a reference and Pell was never wrong—there was a fourth key the guild had never accounted for.

"Now?" Sefa said, and dipped her pen. "Now we find out what else they buried."
