## 21. Compass Points Home

The Ashen Flats had not changed, which was, Wren decided, the most remarkable thing about them.

A year ago—or a lifetime ago, depending on how you counted—Wren had crossed this same stretch of cracked earth with half a waterskin, a stolen name, and a compass that lied to them about north. Now they crossed it with a full canteen, a name that fit like a coat tailored to their shoulders, and a compass that didn't point anywhere at all unless Wren told it to.

Sefa had made it herself. She'd spent three weeks on it, hunched over the worktable in the free archive, soldering and swearing and once, memorably, setting a small fire she insisted was "controlled." When she finally handed it over, the case was river-pearl and desert brass, and the needle sat perfectly still until Wren touched it.

"It points wherever you decide," she'd said. "That's the whole trick. The old compasses pointed at what you'd lost. This one points at what you choose."

Wren had tested it that first night, lying under the stars outside the archive, and asked it, silently, *home*. The needle had swung—not north, not toward the spring, not toward any of the places that had claimed pieces of them—and come to rest pointing at the sleeping archivist beside the fire.

Wren had decided not to tell her that. Some ammunition was too dangerous to surrender.

Now the needle lay quiet against their hip, patient as a sleeping cat, and the Flats opened ahead of them in every direction: pale, vast, indifferent. A ranger's country. A country that had taught Wren everything about survival and nothing, until recently, about living.

The first village was Ferrow's Rest, three days in. Wren had passed through it a dozen times in their invisible years—slipping along the fence line, trading for water with their hood up, speaking only when spoken to and then as little as possible. *Keep her invisible.* Their father's words, recovered from a bottle of memory and a drowned ledger and a spring that remembered everything. Wren had worn that invisibility like armor for so long they'd forgotten it was a cage.

This time they walked in through the front gate.

The well-keeper, an old woman named Dessa with forearms like braided rope, looked up from her buckets and squinted.

"Ranger Yarrow," Wren said, and offered their hand.

Dessa took it. Held it a moment longer than necessary. "Heard about you," she said. "The spring-child. The one who opened the water." Her face did something complicated—pride and grief and suspicion all fighting for the same real estate. "Heard you also told us the truth about what the guild put in our wells."

"I did."

"My husband drank that water for thirty years." Dessa set down her bucket. "He forgot our wedding song. Forgot my face, near the end. Thought I was his sister." She looked at Wren, and Wren made themselves hold the look, because running from it was the old life, and the old life was over. "The new water brought the song back. He hums it in his sleep now. He still doesn't know my face." A pause. "But he's *learning* it. Every morning, a little more. So."

She thrust the bucket at Wren. "Water's free. Always should have been. Sit, eat, and tell me if the river-cities are really as soft as they say."

"They're worse," Wren said. "They put honey in *bread*."

Dessa made a sound that might have been disgust or might have been the laugh she was hiding, and that was how Wren learned that belonging, offered rather than owed, could start with a bucket of water and a bad joke about honey.

They stayed two nights. They fixed Dessa's sagging gate, because some habits were load-bearing. When they left, three children followed them to the edge of the village, and one of them—a girl with sand-colored braids—asked if the stories were true, that a ranger had made the desert remember.

"Close enough," Wren said. "The desert remembered on its own. I just unlocked the door."

"Can I be a ranger?"

"You can be anything," Wren said, and then, because honesty was a discipline now, not just a weapon: "Anything costs something. Make sure you know the price before you pay it."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a guild negotiator. "Okay," she said. "Can I have some of your jerky instead?"

Wren gave her the jerky. It felt, oddly, like the more important transaction.

* * *

The Flats gave way to scrubland, and the scrubland to the first shy green, and on the ninth day Wren crested a ridge they had never seen before but recognized anyway—the way you recognize a face from a photograph you studied too long.

Below, the river ran.

Not the drowned, poisoned trickle Wren had followed upstream through ruined towns and memory-haunted bells, but a living river, wide and quick, its banks stitched with reeds and the small industrious sounds of things growing. A year of unsealed water had done what a century of guild engineering could not. The drowned towns downstream were waking. The desert's edge was moving, inch by cautious inch, toward the green.

And at the river's source, high and hidden in the fold of the mountains, the spring.

Wren's parents had died there. That was the shape of it now, remembered whole: their mother sealing the gate with the last of her strength, roots over stone, buying the desert a century of slow forgetting so the guild couldn't take the water and the memory both. Their father pressing the compass into a stranger's hands. *Keep her invisible. Keep her safe. She'll forgive us someday, or she won't, but she'll live.*

*She'll forgive us someday, or she won't.*

Wren had come to find out which.

They made camp that night at the ridge's edge, and before sleep took them, they touched the compass and thought, deliberately, *them*. The needle turned in its case and settled, pointing down into the dark toward the spring, and Wren slept without dreams for the first time in a week, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

The gate of roots still stood.

It had closed forever behind them on the day of the flood—that was the spring's final gift and final judgment, the door shut so that no guild, no army, no desperate desert settlement could ever claim the source again—but the roots had kept growing, as living things do. They had climbed the stone arch in the year since, threading it with new green, and someone—Wren stopped, and looked, and their throat went tight—someone had been tending them.

There were offerings at the gate's foot. Small ones. A clay cup of water, fresh. A string of dried peppers. A child's carved wooden bird, its paint faded by sun. And tucked into a knot of root, carefully, the way you'd leave something for a shrine you weren't sure was listening: a scrap of paper, ink bleached but legible.

*For the spring-child. Thank you for my sister's song. — T. of Ferrow's Rest*

Wren stood there a long time.

They had expected the grave to be hard. They had expected grief. What they had not expected was this—that their parents' sacrifice had not vanished into the sealed dark with the water. It had leaked out, the way water does, the way memory does. It had become a song returned, a face half-remembered, a wooden bird left at a gate by someone who had never met them and loved them anyway.

*You're known here,* the offerings said. *You were never invisible. We just didn't know your name.*

The graves were a hundred paces on, at the spring's outflow where the first true water of the river spilled over mossy stones. Wren's mother and father lay side by side under a cairn of river rock, and the sigil on the headstone—the same one that had marked the gate, the same one that had lived, unknown, on a compass Wren had carried through the desert for years—was worn soft by weather and, at its base, brightened by the touch of many hands.

People came here. People had *always* come here, Wren realized. The guild had sealed the spring and buried the story, but the story had gone underground the way water does, and surfaced here, at this cairn, in this green place the maps called nothing.

Wren sat down in the grass.

"Hello," they said. It came out rough. They cleared their throat and tried again. "Hello. It's—it's Wren. It's also—" and they said the name from the ledger, the name from the ferryman's book, the name their mother had whispered over a cradle at the spring's edge, and saying it out loud, here, in this place, felt like setting down a pack carried so long the shoulders had grown around it.

The river made its small sounds. A heron—territorial, Wren noted with the ghost of a smile, some things were eternal—regarded them from a stone with the affronted dignity of a landlord.

"I spent my whole life being angry at the wrong things," Wren said. "Angry at the desert for being hard. Angry at myself for not remembering. I didn't know there was anyone to be angry *at*." They picked a stem of grass, folded it, unfolded it. "And then when I did know, I thought the anger would be the whole of it. That I'd come here and it would be all grief, all the way down."

They looked at the cairn. At the wooden bird. At the cup of water, catching the light.

"But there's this too. There's all of *this*. You didn't just save me. You saved—" Wren gestured helplessly at the river, the green, the world downstream where a woman named Dessa was relearning her husband's face one morning at a time. "All of it. And I spent a year thinking I had to choose between the truth and the desert, and the truth turned out to *be* the desert. Turned out to be the river too. Turns out they were never separate countries. I just didn't have a compass good enough to see it."

The needle of the new compass, Wren noticed, had not moved. It didn't need to. It was already pointing where Wren had decided, long before this moment, that it would.

"I forgive you," Wren said. "I think I forgave you somewhere around the rapids, actually, which was a terrible time to do it, I was very busy nearly drowning. But I wanted to say it here. Where you could—" They stopped. Started over, plainer. "I wanted to say it out loud, once, in a place you might have loved. And then I'm going to go home, and I'd like it very much if you considered that home to be wherever I say it is. Because I have a compass now that works that way. You'd have hated it. You'd have loved it. Both."

A breeze came down off the mountain, moved through the roots of the gate, and stirred the grass around the cairn, and Wren—ranger, skeptic, survivor of guild ambushes and underwater bells and one truly catastrophic rafting incident—chose, deliberately, to believe it was an answer.

They stayed until the light went gold. They told their parents about Sefa, which took a while, because Sefa took a while. They told them about the archive, and the flood, and the moment Sefa had pulled Corvane—*Corvane*, of all the drowning men in all the world—out of the memory-water, mercy offered to the man least entitled to it, and how Wren had understood in that moment that they were looking at the finest person they had ever known, and how it had taken them another four months and one controlled fire to say so out loud.

"I told her, eventually," Wren said. "What the compass pointed at, that first night. She laughed until she cried and then she said, 'I knew, I made it, I calibrated it.'" A pause. "She didn't know. She absolutely didn't know. But I love that she pretended."

The heron had given up on them and gone fishing. The first stars were out over the ridge.

Wren stood, brushed off their knees, and pressed their palm flat against the headstone, over the worn sigil.

"Goodbye," they said. "Not forever-goodbye. Just—the kind where you stop carrying someone and start *visiting* them. I'll come back when the monsoon breaks. I'll bring her. She'll want to catalogue the roots."

They walked back down through the gate of living green, and at its foot they added their own offering to the shrine: the old compass. Or what remained of it—the case, at least, the metal long since gone to water in their hands at the gate. They set the empty pearl-and-brass shell beside the wooden bird, where it caught the last light like a small closed eye that had finally, finally finished watching.

* * *

Sefa was waiting at the ridge camp when Wren came up the trail in the blue hour, a fire lit, tea going, her boots off and her notebook on her knees in the manner of a woman who had been writing about Wren's probable emotional state and was now going to pretend she hadn't.

"You were gone a while," she said, too casually.

"I was saying goodbye."

"Good goodbye?"

"The best one." Wren sat down, accepted tea, and looked out over the darkening Flats, where the first faint green was creeping along the old dry washes like a rumor no one wanted to jinx by repeating. "The graves are under a cairn. People leave things there. A bird. A cup of water. Someone from Ferrow's Rest left a note about a song."

Sefa's pen had gone still. "The archivist in me is going to want to catalogue every single one of those offerings," she said. "Fair warning."

"The archivist in you can catalogue them next time. We're coming back when the rains come."

"Next time," Sefa repeated, tasting it. She closed the notebook. "That's the second time you've said *we* today. I'm keeping count. We're at eleven."

"Twelve. You missed one."

"I never miss one." But she was smiling, and the fire was warm, and out in the dark the Flats lay quiet under the stars—the same stars that had watched Wren cross them invisible, hungry, and alone, and that would, if the archivists' rain-charts were right, watch the first true monsoon in a generation come up over them before the year turned.

Wren touched the compass at their belt, out of habit, out of ceremony, out of the simple pleasure of touching a thing that had never once lied to them.

"Where to?" Sefa asked. It was the ritual question now, the nightly one. *Where does it point, ranger?*

Wren looked at the fire, and the woman beside it, and the country below that was learning, drop by drop, to remember itself.

"Home," Wren said.

The needle didn't even have to move.
