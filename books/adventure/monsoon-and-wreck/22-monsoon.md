## 22. Monsoon

**WREN**

The first drop hit the ridge a full minute before the rest of the sky followed it down.

Wren felt it land on the back of their hand—a single cold coin of water—and for a moment they did nothing at all. They just stood there, ranger's stillness, the way they'd learned to stand in the Ashen Flats when a dust storm was deciding whether to come. Waiting. Listening.

Then the second drop. The third. A fourth landed square on the compass at their belt—the new one, the one Sefa had made from river-copper and stubbornness—and slid down the casing like it was greeting an old friend.

"Here it comes," Sefa said beside them.

"Here it comes," Wren agreed, and then the monsoon arrived, all at once, the way a year of patient work arrives all at once, and the desert began to drink.

It was not a gentle thing. Wren had imagined, in the year since the spring opened, that rain would be gentle—that anything so long withheld would fall softly, apologetically, like a gift handed over with both hands. But the monsoon came down like it had somewhere to be. It hammered the ridge, sheeted off the rocks, turned the dry wash below into a brown, roaring ribbon in the space of ten breaths. Thunder rolled up the valley and the valley rolled it back, and somewhere in the flats below, Wren knew, every cistern in every village was being opened to catch the sky.

They were laughing before they realized it. Sefa was too—Sefa, archivist of the free archive, keeper of everything the guild had buried, currently soaked to the ribs and grinning like a girl who'd grown up reading about rain in a dry room and had finally, *finally* been introduced to the original document.

"You're getting water in your boots," Wren shouted over the noise.

"I'm getting water in my *soul*," Sefa shouted back. "There's a difference. Write it down."

"I don't write things down. That's your department."

"Someone has to be the responsible one."

"You're the least responsible person I've ever—"

The rest was lost to thunder, which was probably just as well, because Wren had a whole list prepared and none of it was true. Sefa was, by any honest measure, the most responsible person Wren had ever known. She'd spent a year building the archive from three salvaged ledgers and a borrowed cellar into an institution that the river-cities actually respected. She'd recorded everything—the guild's century of theft, the poisoning of the outflow, Wren's family's sacrifice, all of it told with Wren's blessing and in Wren's own words, read back to them twice so they could correct the details. She'd even recorded the parts that made Wren look like an idiot. Especially those. She said an archive that flattered its subjects was just gossip with a roof.

Wren had argued. Wren had lost. Wren had, privately, never been happier to lose an argument in their life.

Below them, the flats were changing color. Wren had spent eleven years learning to read that land—the way cracked clay went from white to grey to the bruised purple that meant water underneath, the way the ghost-grass came back overnight after even a lie of a rain. They knew it better than their own face, which for a long time had been the only thing they knew better than their own face. Now, watching the first real water pool in the low places in a generation, they realized they were reading it the way you read a letter from someone you love. Not for information. For the sound of the voice.

A year ago, they hadn't known they had a voice of their own to listen for.

"Do you remember," Sefa said, quieter now, rain streaming off her chin, "when you told me the desert would hate you? When we were coming down out of the gorge and you were doing that thing where you list all the ways everything ends badly, in order, like a shopping list?"

"I don't do that."

"You do it alphabetically, Wren. You once got to 'F.'"

"F is a reasonable letter to stop at."

"The desert doesn't hate you." Sefa nodded at the flats, where, far off, lamps were coming alight in the villages one by one—not emergency lamps, not flood-warning lamps. Celebration lamps. "Look at that. That's not hate. That's a town that knows exactly whose fault the water is."

Wren looked. The lamps were spreading, village to village, the way the water was spreading, and they knew—because they'd spent a year walking those villages and saying the hard things out loud—that in some of them, people were saying Wren's name tonight. In some of them, people were saying it the way you say the name of someone who brought rain. In a few—the salt-town of Harrow Flats, where the guild's poisoning had hit hardest and the forgiveness had come slowest—they were saying it in other tones, and Wren had learned to let those tones exist without flinching. Some things you didn't get to be forgiven for. Some things you just got to keep showing up for, year after year, until the showing-up became its own kind of answer.

That, too, was something Sefa had written down. Wren had corrected the details anyway.

"Come on," they said. "We should get down there before the wash cuts the trail."

"What, and miss the view?"

"Sefa."

"I'm coming, I'm coming." She pulled her hood up, which was a gesture of pure optimism given that the hood had been decorative for the last ten minutes. Then she paused, and her voice went soft in the way it did when she was about to say something she'd been carrying for a while. "Hey. Wren."

"Mm?"

"The compass. Check the compass."

Wren frowned, but unclipped it. The old compass—their mother's, the spring-key—had dissolved in their hands at the gate, metal becoming water, and Wren had mourned it like a limb. The new one was Sefa's work: river-copper, a face of smoked glass, and a needle that pointed wherever Wren decided. That had been the joke at first. *A compass for someone who finally knows where they're going.* But Wren had discovered, over the year, that it wasn't a joke at all. It was a discipline. Every morning they chose a direction, and the needle swung to honor it, and every morning the choosing got a little easier.

They looked down at it now, rain drumming on the glass.

The needle pointed at Sefa.

Wren stared at it. Sefa stared at it. The rain came down between them, and the flats glittered below, and the needle did not waver, because Wren's decision, apparently, had been made some time ago without consulting them.

"That's not—" Wren started.

"It's a very well-made compass," Sefa said, with tremendous dignity.

"You calibrated it to point at—"

"I calibrated it to point at whatever you decide. That's what I said. That's what I *built*." Her ears had gone red, which the rain did not adequately explain. "If it's pointing at me, that's a you problem. Take it up with the manufacturer."

"The manufacturer is standing in front of me getting rained on."

"Then take it up with the rain."

Wren looked at the needle. Looked at her. Thought about the girl in the tidal cave who'd borrowed a compass because it was the only thing that pointed to the truth, and the archivist who'd built a free archive out of a cellar and spite, and the person who'd pulled them out of a flooded church tower and refused, ever after, to call it a debt—*debts are how the guild keeps people, Wren, we don't do that, we do favors, we do breakfast, we do showing up*—and thought about the fact that they had, without ever saying it in words, been deciding this direction every single morning for a year.

"Huh," Wren said.

"'Huh,'" Sefa repeated. "The celebrated novelist of the flats, everyone. The ranger who faced down Corvane. 'Huh.'"

"Give me a moment. I'm deciding."

"You've had a year."

"I work slowly," Wren said. "I'm thorough."

And then, because the rain was coming down and the lamps were coming up and life was short even now, especially now, they kissed her—there on the ridge, in the first monsoon of the new world, with the compass between them pointing very firmly at the both of them, which Sefa would later insist was a calibration error and Wren would later insist was the single most accurate reading the instrument had ever produced.

The thunder, when it came, sounded approving.

* * *

**SEFA**

The village of Low Crossing had put out lanterns on every pole, which meant that by the time Wren and Sefa came down off the ridge, the whole town looked like it was floating on a raft of small gold suns. The wash had become a proper river, brown and joyful, and children were out in it already, shrieking, in defiance of every adult within earshot, which was the traditional and correct way to greet the first monsoon.

"They know we're coming," Sefa said.

"Everyone knows we're coming. You published the route in the archive bulletin."

"It was a *field schedule*, not an announcement."

"You titled it 'Field Schedule (Everyone Come).'"

"That was the printer's error."

"The printer is your cousin."

"He's a very enthusiastic cousin." Sefa squelched along beside Wren, boots full of rain, heart full of something she refused to name in front of witnesses, especially the witness responsible. "Besides, it's not for us. It's for the water. We're just traveling with it."

That was true, and she was proud of it. The archive's great project of the year—the one she'd argued for in front of the river-cities' council until three of them resigned and one of them threw a shoe—had been the Memory Roads: a survey, village by village, of what the restored water actually did. Because the spring didn't just feed the desert. It fed memory. And the water that came downstream now carried small things in it—a song someone's grandmother had sung, the smell of a doorframe, a face you'd lost—returning them gently, the way you'd return a borrowed coat. Not everything. Not the big griefs. The spring had judgment, and its judgment was merciful in a way the guild's had never been.

The guild itself was mostly gone. Corvane had survived the flood of his own worst memories—Sefa had pulled him out, and had never once regretted it, though she'd had nightmares about his face underwater for months—and had been tried by the river-cities in a courtroom so packed they'd had to open the windows. He'd listened to the whole ledger read aloud, his whole century, and at the end he'd asked for one thing: that his name be recorded accurately, with no embellishments. Sefa had granted it. It was, she'd told Wren, the cruelest sentence available. He served his years in the archive itself, under supervision, cataloging the records of everything he'd tried to bury. He was, infuriatingly, extremely good at it.

"Ranger Yarrow!" someone shouted from the lantern-light, and then, in chorus, from a dozen throats: "Ranger Yarrow! Archivist Sefa!"

Wren flinched—just barely, just the old flinch, the invisibility reflex—and then, Sefa saw, deliberately un-flinched. Stood straighter. Lifted a hand.

It had taken most of the year. The first months, Wren had traveled the villages with their hood up, saying the hard truths in a voice pitched not to carry, as though the truth were something you could apologize for quietly and be on your way. But the desert had refused the arrangement. The desert had wanted names. And Wren had learned—slowly, alphabetically, one letter at a time—that being known was not the same as being trapped. That a name could be a door.

"Yarrow!" A small shape detached itself from the crowd and barreled into Wren's legs: Tam, the ferryman's granddaughter, aged nine and built entirely of momentum. "Yarrow, the wash came all the way to the mill! Grandpa says it hasn't done that since he was little! He says you have to see it!"

"Does he," Wren said, hoisting her onto their shoulders with the ease of long practice. "And does your grandpa also say you should be out of the rain?"

"Grandpa says rain is for being in. He says dry people are suspicious."

"Your grandpa," Sefa said, "is a very wise man who owes me a story."

"He says you owe *him* a story. He says the ferryman's ledger goes both ways."

"The ferryman's ledger," Sefa said solemnly, "absolutely goes both ways."

They made their way into the lantern-light, and the village closed around them—not like a crowd, Sefa thought, but like water around a stone, easily, without effort, as though they'd always belonged in the current. An old woman pressed a cup of something hot into Wren's hands. Someone had already taken their soaked packs and hung them by the communal fire without asking, which was the desert's way of saying *you're staying, we've decided, don't argue.* A man Wren had once arrested for water-hoarding—back when hoarding was a crime instead of a memory—shook their hand and said, "Ranger," and the word had no weight in it at all except welcome.

Sefa stood at the edge of it for a moment, watching, the way she always did for a moment before entering. Archivist's habit. Record the scene before joining it.

Wren, mid-laugh at something Tam had shouted in their ear, looked up and found her across the firelight. Held out a hand. Didn't say anything, because nothing needed saying, and also because Tam was yelling.

Sefa went. She'd been going, she realized, since a tidal cave on a drowned coast, when a soaked desert ranger had looked at a compass pointing at the mountains and said, in a voice of pure betrayed disbelief, "*North is a lie?*"

She'd known then. She'd just had to let them catch up.

* * *

**WREN**

Later—much later, after the stories and the stew and after Tam had fallen asleep against Wren's shoulder and been carried off by her grandfather, who winked at Wren with a knowingness that Wren chose not to investigate—Wren stood at the edge of the village where the new river ran past, and listened to it.

The rain had softened to a steady, generous whisper. The wash—no, the river, it was a river now, it could stop pretending—moved dark and quick under the stars that were reappearing between the clouds. Somewhere upstream, at the source, the spring was running open for the first time in a century, and every drop that passed Wren now had been there. Had passed over the roots of the gate. Had touched the pool where their mother had stood and their father had made his terrible, loving arithmetic.

Wren had gone back once, in the spring, to the grave at the water's edge. Two stones, side by side, names carved deep: *Yarrow*, and beneath it, in their mother's hand, copied from the family ledger, *Keepers of the Source.* Wren had sat there a long time. Had said the things that needed saying—*I'm angry, I understand, I forgive you, I don't forgive you, I'm going to be all right, look at the rain*—and had left the new compass on the stone overnight, pointing at the grave, so that for one night at least, the needle held still on home.

In the morning it had pointed at the road back to the river-cities, where Sefa was waiting, and Wren had understood that this was not a contradiction. Home was a direction you chose daily. Some mornings it was a grave. Most mornings it was a person.

"You're doing the standing-still thing," Sefa said, arriving at their side with two cups of the hot something and handing one over.

"I'm listening to the river."

"You're doing the standing-still thing *emotionally*. I've catalogued the difference."

"You've catalogued me?"

"Volume three," Sefa said serenely. "Restricted access. Very restricted. Basically just me."

Wren drank the hot something—it tasted like rain and honey and a village deciding to be kind—and watched the water go by. "Do you ever think about what it would've been," they said, "if you'd never picked up the compass in the cave? If you'd just... left it on the altar and walked away?"

"Every day."

"And?"

"And then I wouldn't have you." She said it simply, the way she recorded facts. "And there'd be no archive, and the spring would still be sealed, and Corvane would still own the water, and I'd be an archivist of lies with a very tidy shelf. It's not a difficult calculation, Wren. I'm an archivist. I ran the numbers."

"The compass pointed at me before you ever met me," Wren said. "You told me that. It points at people, not places."

"It does."

"So what's it pointing at now?"

Sefa smiled, and the lantern-light caught her, and the river went on carrying its cargo of small remembered songs down through the sleeping desert, village to village, lamp to lamp, all the way to the sea.

"Same thing it's always pointed at," she said. "Home."

Below the ridge, in the flats, in the drowned-and-risen towns, in every place the water touched, people were waking to the sound of rain on roofs that had forgotten the noise, and children were learning a word their grandparents had only read about, and somewhere a bell that hadn't rung in a hundred years was ringing, and the river remembered.

And so did they.
