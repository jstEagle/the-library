## 2. What the River Kept

Kestrel found her the way he found everything on the river—by asking questions in the places questions cost money.

The assay office in Copperline sat at the end of Front Street like a stubborn tooth, wedged between a saloon that smelled of spilled beer and old smoke and a dry-goods store whose windows had gone gray with road dust. He could see her through the glass before he ever pushed the door: a woman bent over a balance scale, weighing something small and glittering with a patience that looked more like contempt than care.

The bell above the door announced him. She didn't look up.

"Office closes at five," she said. "Whatever's in that sack, it can wait until morning."

"It's not ore."

"Then it's worse. Men who bring things that aren't ore always want something." She finished her weighing, wrote a number in a ledger with a hand that didn't tremble, and only then raised her eyes. They were gray as river fog, and they went through him like a current through a net—sorting, discarding, keeping. "You're the boatman. Halloran."

"You know me."

"Everybody knows you. Twenty years of pushing freight past this town and never once stopping. My father drank with you twice and called you the politest ghost on the Kessler." She set down her pen. "So why does a ghost come ashore?"

He had rehearsed this on the walk up from the landing. He had planned to be gentle about it, to lead her toward it the way you lead a horse toward a bridge it doesn't trust. But standing under her gaze, all the careful words went out of him like heat through a cracked window.

"Your father's dead," he said. "Shot on my boat, two nights ago. I buried him at Miller's Bend."

The change in her was not what he expected. She didn't cry out. She didn't go pale. Something in her simply went *still*, the way water goes still over a deep place, and Kestrel understood that this was a woman who had learned early that the world took things and gave no receipts.

"Show me," she said.

He unfolded the map on her scale-table, smoothing the creases with a hand that wasn't quite steady. "He made me swear I'd bring it to you. He was—there wasn't time for much else."

Ada Wren bent over the paper. He watched her take in the contour lines, the creek forks, the little cairn symbol at the head of the western draw—and then he watched her eyes stop, and narrow, and travel to him.

"Whose hand is this?"

"That's what I came to ask you. I know it."

"Know it *how*?"

"Because it's my father's." The words came out of him rough, like stones dragged off a bar. "Thomas Halloran. He drew survey maps for the Meridian Company until they ran us off our claim and put his name on every wanted board in the valley. He's been dead nineteen years. I'd know that hand anywhere. He made me practice my letters copying it."

For a long moment the assay office was silent except for the tick of the stove cooling and, somewhere out on the street, a team jingling past in the dark.

"Sit down, Captain Halloran," Ada said at last, and her voice had changed—not softer, exactly, but lower, the way a river goes quiet before it takes a bend. "And start at the beginning. Because my father left in the spring saying he'd found something that would buy us a ranch in Nevada, and he came back in the summer saying nothing at all, and he wouldn't look at the mountains for a month. I want to know what my father found that he was afraid of."

* * *

They talked until the stove went cold and the lamp burned low, and what they built between them, piece by piece, was a shape neither of them liked.

Kestrel told her about the claim on Kettle Creek—his father's stake, filed legal and proper, and how the Meridian men had come the following winter with papers Kestrel was too young to read and guns too old to argue with. How his father had ridden to the county seat to contest it and had been found, three weeks later, at the bottom of a cutbank with a broken neck and his saddlebags empty. How the coroner had called it a fall. How his mother had lasted two more years and his father's name had lasted considerably less.

Ada told him about her father's years with Meridian—Elias Wren had been a locator man, walking the high country for the company, staking what they told him to stake. And how, in his last sober years, he'd taken private contracts instead, and how the money had gotten strange, and how once, drunk on Christmas whiskey, he'd said to her: *There's ore in that range that would make Meridian look like a church collection plate, and the man who found it first is in the ground.* She had thought it was just drunk talk. She had thought that about a great many things.

"Your father found the lode," Ada said slowly. "Nineteen years ago. On the claim they took from you."

"And mine found it too, or found them finding it, and they killed him for it." Kestrel stared at the map. In the lamplight his father's letters seemed almost to move, the old familiar slant of them, the way he'd crossed his sevens. "Twenty years I've run this river telling myself the past was sunk. That it was under the water and the water keeps what it keeps."

"The river keeps everything," Ada said. "It just doesn't always give it back."

* * *

He smelled the smoke before he saw the fire.

They had talked until past midnight, and Kestrel had gone down to the landing to sleep aboard the *Marguerite*—his boat, his father's boat, the one thing the Meridian Company had never managed to take from him. He was still a quarter mile from the water when the smell reached him, tar and pitch and burning spruce, and he broke into a run along the frozen wheel-ruts of the landing road.

The *Marguerite* was a column of orange light against the black river.

She burned from the inside out, flames standing up out of her hatch like something being born of her, and the heat of her reached his face from thirty yards and drove the cold out of his bones and put something colder in their place. Two figures were moving away along the bank, unhurried, and one of them turned at the sound of his boots and stood a moment in the firelight—a big man, beaver hat, a face Kestrel didn't know and would know forever now. The man touched his hat brim, almost politely, and the two of them went on into the dark.

Kestrel stood until the heat became unbearable and the *Marguerite*'s stern settled, hissing, into the shallows, and the river took her the way it took everything—without comment, without mercy, keeping what it kept.

Ada found him there at dawn, sitting on a bollard with his hands hanging between his knees, watching the blackened ribs steam in the gray light.

"They didn't even run," he said. "They wanted me to see."

"Then they're afraid of what you're carrying." She sat down beside him on the frozen timber, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her through two coats, and put something in his hand: a canvas rucksack, packed tight. "Dried meat, hard bread, a compass, and my father's pistol, which I expect you know how to use better than I do. The pass closes in nine days. Meridian knows it too, which means they'll be watching the wagon road and the low trails."

"So we go high."

"We go high." She stood and looked up at the range, where the peaks had put on their winter white overnight, bright and indifferent as judgment. "Captain Halloran, I want to be clear about something. I don't trust you. I've known you one day and you've brought me a dead father and a burning boat. But my father trusted yours, and your father's handwriting is on that map, and somebody burned twenty years of my family's living to a waterline in one night. So I'm coming with you—not for you, and not for the silver."

"For what, then?"

She looked at him, and the fog-gray eyes had banked fire in them now.

"For the truth. All of it. Whatever it cost our fathers, I mean to see it paid back." She shouldered her pack and started up the landing road, toward the mountains, and did not look back to see if he followed.

He followed.
