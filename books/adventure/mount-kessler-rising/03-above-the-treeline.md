## 3. Above the Treeline

The storm came down on them like something with intent.

Ada had watched weather her whole life—assay clerks learn to read the sky the way rivermen read water—but she had never seen a front move this fast, this low, this determined. One hour the peaks above them were clean and blue-edged against a hard October sun. The next, a wall of gray stood over the saddle to the north, swallowing the ridgeline whole, and the wind that preceded it carried snow in its teeth and a smell of iron and coming dark.

They had been climbing for two days out of Copperline's shadow, following Wren's map through country neither of them knew well enough to love. The trail was an old one—a freight road from before the railroad, cut by men whose names had worn off every marker—and it switchbacked up through stands of lodgepole gone copper with frost, past talus slopes where the rock sang when the wind moved it. Kestrel walked ahead, favoring his left knee, which had never forgiven him twenty years of wheelhouse floors, reading the map by lamplight in the lee of whatever stone they could find at night. Ada walked behind with her father's rifle slung across her back and her hands jammed in her coat, doing sums in her head: distance to the shaft, days of food, hours of light.

And now the sky was closing its ledger.

"We won't make the pass before it hits," she said, catching up to him at the bend where the trail left the trees. "Not both of us, not with full packs."

Kestrel didn't answer right away. He was looking north, into the wall of weather, and Ada saw something in his face she'd learned to recognize on the river: the look of a man measuring a current he couldn't afford to misjudge.

"The map says there's shelter," he said finally. "An old powder house, half a mile off the trail below the saddle. Your father marked it."

"Marked it twenty years ago."

"Stone doesn't rot." He turned. "We push for it. If we're caught in the open, we're dead, and if we're dead the map dies with us, and everything your father bled for dies too."

She hated that he was right. She hated more that he said it like a man who had already decided to die carrying it if it came to that.

They made the powder house as the first true snow came down—fat flakes, dry as ash, falling straight and silent, the kind of snow that meant business. The structure was half-collapsed, roofed in rotting shakes, but the walls were double-laid stone and the door still swung, and inside there was straw gone to dust and a hearth blackened by other men's fires, decades old. They got a flame going with shavings and Kestrel's oilcloth-wrapped matches, hung the wettest of their gear near it, and ate cold beans out of the can while the storm found its voice outside and began, very slowly, to bury the world.

It was there, in the firelight, that Ada asked him about his father.

She hadn't meant to. It came out sideways, the way real questions do. He was turning the map over in his hands, studying the reverse, where Elias had penciled elevations and bearings in a cramped, confident hand—and beneath those, faded almost to nothing, older notations in a different hand entirely. Rounder. Slower. A hand that had learned letters from a Bible and a ledger book.

"That's him," Kestrel said quietly. "That's my father's writing."

"You're sure?"

"I'd know it anywhere. He wrote me letters from the camps. Every one started *Dear boy* and ended *keep your boots dry.*" His thumb moved across the faded ink without touching it. "He surveyed this ridge. Before I was born. Before Meridian ran the Hallorans off their claim and put the word *thief* on his name in every assay office from here to the coast."

Ada looked at him across the fire, and what she felt wasn't pity. It was recognition—the specific, cold recognition of one person who had grown up inside a lie built by other people. Her father had left Copperline when she was nine and come back twice a year with ore samples and no money and stories he told only after she was asleep. She had thought him a failure. A dreamer. A man who chased silver the way some men chase card games.

Now she knew he had been chasing testimony.

"They'll be behind us," she said. "Meridian's men. They burned your boat; they won't stop at that."

"No," Kestrel agreed. "They won't."

He banked the fire low, the way rivermen do, and took first watch, and Ada slept badly in the straw with the rifle under her arm and the storm howling at the stones like something trying to get in.

* * *

Morning came white and blind. The storm had passed overnight, leaving two feet of new snow and a sky so clear it hurt, and they broke camp in the blue hour and climbed toward the saddle with the sun rising red on their backs. The trail above treeline was a memory of itself now—wind-scoured rock, cairns buried, the world reduced to white and gray and the map's small confident lines.

They were a quarter mile below the saddle when the shots came.

The first one missed high, cracking off the rock above Kestrel's head, and the second didn't miss. Ada heard the impact before she understood it—a sound like an axe going into wet wood—and then Kestrel staggered, dropped the map case from his shoulder to his hand, and kept walking. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that undid her: he kept walking. Bent, bleeding, one arm clamped to his side, he turned and shouted at her—not words she could catch at first, just sound, urgency—and pointed up-slope to where the trail bent around a rib of granite toward the mine workings.

"Go," he said, when she reached him. "Don't stop for me. Go."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're not. You're carrying it." He pressed the map case into her hands. "I'll follow. I always follow."

Three men had come out of the rocks to the east, dressed for winter, moving with the unhurried competence of professionals. Ada knew what they were. Everyone in the valley knew what Meridian's quiet men were for. She got Kestrel moving uphill, half-carried him behind the granite rib, and then did the arithmetic she'd been doing all her life with numbers and did it now with stone.

Above them, where the trail crossed the base of a scree field, the slope was loaded. All autumn's freeze and thaw had loosened it, and the storm had greased it. If it came down, it would come down across the only line of approach, and it would keep coming for a long time.

She climbed. Bullets spanged off the rock around her—one struck close enough to sting her cheek with grit—and she reached the base of the scree, set her shoulder against the largest stone she could move, and pushed.

The mountain answered.

It started as a whisper, a few rocks rolling, and became a rush, and became a roar, a white-and-gray avalanche of broken stone pouring across the trail below her, burying the approach, choking the draw with a sound like the end of the world. Dust rose in a column. When it settled, the three men were nowhere, and the slope where they had stood was a fresh scar of rubble forty feet deep.

Ada sat down on the rock and shook. Then she went down the rib to where Kestrel lay against the granite, pale as the snow, blood freezing black along his ribs, and she did what her father had taught her with a wound like that—packed it, bound it, got him warm—and together, slowly, each carrying the other by turns, they made the last mile to the mine.

* * *

The adit was exactly where the map promised: a black horizontal mouth in the mountainside, timbered in spruce, drifted shut with snow except for a gap at the top they could crawl through. Inside, the cold was a different order of cold—the kind that lives underground and never leaves—and their lantern threw shadows that seemed older than they were.

Kestrel could barely stand. Ada propped him against a timber and went ahead with the light, twenty feet, thirty, until the tunnel opened into a stope where the vein had been worked and abandoned mid-argument with the rock.

The samples were stacked in a wooden crate like a man's unfinished sentence. Galena and quartz, heavy with silver, each piece chipped and numbered in pencil—in *two* pencils, she realized, two hands, two men, years apart. Her father's last trip. And beside the crate, wrapped in oilcloth and frozen into a block of ice against the wall, a journal.

They thawed it over the lantern glass, page by page, careful as surgeons. It was Kestrel's father's hand throughout—*Dear boy* was nowhere in it, but the roundness was, the patience—and it was a surveyor's record and then, halfway through, something else. A record of what he'd found. Of what Meridian's superintendent had offered him for it. Of what was said when he refused.

Of the night four men came to the Halloran cabin.

Ada read the pages aloud to Kestrel because his eyes were failing him in the cold and his hands were too hurt to hold the book, and hearing his own father's voice come off those pages—flat, factual, unanswerable—was more than either of them had prepared for. The frame-up was written down. The theft invented. The payment offered and refused. The names of the men who came, two of whom, Ada realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature, still drew Meridian wages.

At the back of the journal, folded into the binding, was a letter. Elias Wren's hand. Dated three weeks ago. Addressed to *the valley*, to whoever would read it, and beginning: *I am dying of what they gave me, and I want it known that Thomas Halloran died honest.*

Kestrel knelt in the frozen stope with the letter in his ruined hands, and Ada watched a man grieve and be freed in the same minute. Twenty years of being nobody's son, of wearing a name like a brand, and here in the cold and the dark was the proof that the name had been stolen from him, not earned by him—that his father had died protecting a lode rather than selling a lie.

"He knew," Kestrel said. "All those years. He knew what they'd done and he stayed anyway, and he wrote it all down so someone would—" He stopped. Swallowed. "So I would find it."

"So we would," Ada said.

Outside, the light was going. And far below them, faint but unmistakable through the drift-choked mouth of the adit, came the sound of men digging—shovels on stone, voices in the white silence—because a rockslide buries a trail, but it does not bury a purpose, and Meridian's men were already climbing.
