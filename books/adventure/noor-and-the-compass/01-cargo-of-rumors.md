## 1. Cargo of Rumors

The strip at Kettle Landing was four hundred feet shorter than the one in Freya Fairweather's head, which was how she liked her airstrips: just long enough to be a rumor of themselves. She came in over the black spruce with the wheels already half down, one hand on the yoke and the other on the coffee thermos wedged between her knees, because a pilot who couldn't land with coffee in reach wasn't a bush pilot, she was a commuter with delusions.

The Cessna settled onto the gravel with a sound like someone shaking out a rug. Dust rose behind her in a brown curtain, drifted over the fuel drums, the leaning hangar, the sign that read KETTLE LANDING — POP. 61 in paint so faded it might have said 16 or 610 or nothing at all. A dog lifted its head from under the porch of the trading post, considered barking, decided against it.

Freya cut the engine and sat for a moment in the ticking heat, doing what she always did after a landing: counting exits. Wind from the northwest, strip usable, lake to the east if the gravel went soft. Two roads out of town, both of them more suggestion than road. It wasn't fear. It was inventory. You kept track of your ways out the way other people kept track of their savings, and if you did it long enough you stopped noticing you were doing it at all.

She climbed out into air that smelled like diesel and fish guts and, underneath, the green cold breath of the spruce swamp that had been patiently eating this town for forty years. Kettle Landing had been born for a mine that closed before Freya was born, and everything since had been the town politely declining to admit it.

"Meds?" called Marta Okonkwo from the porch, not moving from her chair. Marta ran the post office, the clinic storeroom, the radio, and, as far as anyone could tell, the entire municipal government of Kettle Landing, population 61, give or take whoever was currently hiding from somebody else.

"Crate marked with the red cross," Freya said, hauling the door open. "Insulin, amoxicillin, and the good cough syrup, which I want it noted I did not sample."

"Noted," Marta said dryly. "You're getting a reputation anyway. Three towns now say you talk too much."

"I haul gossip the way I haul freight. Somebody has to."

Marta came down the steps at last, joints announcing each one like a church bell with opinions. Together they wrestled the crate off the cargo floor and set it in the shade of the hangar, where the insulin could pretend the day wasn't happening. Freya had learned medicine runs the hard way: you didn't leave penicillin in a hot plane any longer than physics allowed, and physics allowed less every year, like everything else.

"You eating?" Marta asked.

"I'm flying."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one." Freya patted the fuselage. "I've got a pickup at Two Rivers by four, and if I'm late, Ray Delgado starts telling people I've crashed, and then his wife calls my broker, and then my broker calls me, and honestly the chain of custody on my reputation is longer than the supply chain on this insulin."

Marta looked at her the way old women look at young women who are lying about being fine, which is to say kindly and without any intention of letting it go. "You slept here last time in June. Before that, October. You know what the pattern says?"

"The pattern says I'm efficient."

"The pattern says you're running." Marta squinted up at her. "From what, I couldn't say. But nobody flies circles around a country this size unless they're drawing a shape around something they don't want to land inside of."

Freya smiled, because smiling was cheaper than answering. "I'll tell you what I heard in Fort Nelson, if you tell me who's been asking about me."

"Nobody's been asking about you," Marta said, and then, after a pause that lasted exactly one heartbeat too long, "except the man at Calloway's office."

* * *

There it was. Freya felt it land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well — that particular drop, the one with no bottom audible. Calloway. Even the name had a texture, oiled and slightly warm, like a handle you weren't sure you wanted to grip.

"What about him?" she asked, keeping her voice at cargo-manifest neutrality.

"He sent a letter. Came up on the mail plane Tuesday." Marta reached into her apron pocket and produced an envelope, cream-colored, heavy stock, absurdly formal for a place where most correspondence was written on the backs of fish manifests. "Said to hold it till you came through. Said you'd come through. Which, he was right, so there's that."

Freya took it without opening it. She held it the way you'd hold a live grenade with the pin still in — respectfully, and with a plan to put it down soon.

Calloway ran money and favors out of a second-floor office in Fort Nelson above a barbershop that never seemed to have customers, only men waiting. He'd lent her eleven thousand dollars two winters ago when the engine on her old Cub threw a rod and every bank in the territory had looked at a single woman with a bush plane and a debt history and said, in chorus, no. Eleven thousand dollars. She'd paid back six. The interest moved like weather — you couldn't see it happening, but afterward everything was different.

What she'd never told anyone, including herself, in any language she'd be willing to repeat aloud, was why she'd needed the plane running *that week*. Why she'd taken the loan from a man everyone warned her about rather than sell the compass.

The compass sat in her flight bag right now, wrapped in a wool sock, riding behind the seat where it had ridden for twenty years. Brass case, glass gone cloudy, needle steady as a sermon. Her mother's. The one thing left behind when Maren Fairweather walked out of a rented house in Watson Lake with one duffel bag and no forwarding address, leaving a seven-year-old girl and a husband who drank himself dead within three years of her going.

Freya had been offered four hundred dollars for that compass by a collector in Whitehorse, once. She'd laughed in his face, and then she'd gone out to the truck and shaken for ten minutes, and she still didn't know which of those things was the truer feeling.

"You going to read it?" Marta asked.

"Eventually."

"In this decade?"

"Maybe the next one." Freya slid the envelope into her jacket, unopened, which was its own kind of answer. "Look, if Delgado's freight is ready, point me at it. And Marta — the thing I heard in Fort Nelson—"

"You were going to trade me that."

"I was." Freya leaned against the strut, watching dust settle back onto the strip. "There's a prospector been telling anyone who'll buy him a drink that there's a river source up past the divide that doesn't show on any government map. Says the water comes out of the ground warm. Says there's gold in it, but that's prospectors for you — everything's gold until it's a story, and then the story's worth more."

Marta snorted. "Elias Vane."

Freya blinked. "You know him?"

"Everybody knows Elias Vane. Or knew him. He used to run traplines out of here before—" Marta waved a hand, a gesture that contained twenty years of small-town history in a single dismissive arc. "Before whatever happened. He's got a granddaughter, you know. Noor. Works the cannery docks at Grayling in season. Sharp as a filleting knife, that girl, and twice as likely to cut you if you handle her wrong."

"Huh," Freya said, filing it away the way she filed everything — in the part of her mind that never threw anything out, which was most of it.

"And Freya." Marta lowered her voice, though the dog was the only other witness and the dog was loyal to a fault. "That river story. Calloway's boys have been asking after it too. Buying drinks for anyone who'd talked to Vane. That's usually how you know there's something under a rumor. When the fixers start fishing in it."

Freya stood very still for a moment. This was the thing about gossip, the reason she hauled it alongside the insulin and the rifle cartridges and the wedding cakes: gossip was cargo that weighed nothing and could sink you. Somewhere north of here, an old man was drunk-talking about a river that shouldn't exist, and somewhere south of here, a man with oiled hands was paying to hear about it, and Freya Fairweather was standing exactly on the line between them holding an unopened envelope.

"Well," she said lightly, "sounds like a story that'll keep."

"Everything keeps," Marta said. "That's the trouble with this country. Nothing rots fast enough."

* * *

Delgado's freight turned out to be nine crates of canned peaches, a snowmobile track, and a crib — flat-packed, Swedish, destined for a family at Two Rivers whose baby was due in August and whose father had apparently decided furniture was a problem for future him. Freya loaded it all with the economy of long practice, every crate finding its center of gravity like it had been measured for the space, which, after enough years, it effectively had. Her plane was a body and she knew its bones.

Ray Delgado watched her work from the tailgate of his truck, chewing on a grass stem. "Heard you're going up the Grayling side," he said.

"I'm going wherever the manifest says."

"Manifest says Grayling, does it?"

"It says a lot of things, Ray. None of them your business, all of them insured."

He grinned and spat. "Vane's been camped up on the ridge trail past Grayling. If you're flying that way, maybe keep an eye out. Man's seventy if he's a day, and pride doesn't keep you warm up there."

Freya noted it. Another entry in the ledger of things she noticed and would deal with later, or wouldn't. Her mental ledger was a lot like her financial one: long, complicated, and structurally dependent on her never stopping moving long enough to audit it.

She fired up the engine at 3:40, gave Marta a wave that Marta returned with one hand while holding the clinic clipboard in the other, and rolled down four hundred feet of gravel toward the spruce. The Cessna lifted off heavy, wallowed once in the heat shimmer off the strip, found its legs, and climbed.

And there it was below her, shrinking: Kettle Landing, population 61, dropping away like a coin out of a pocket. Freya felt the familiar lift, the lightness, the specific pleasure of a town becoming a map feature. Some pilots flew for the money. Some flew for the view. Freya flew for the moment the ground let go of her — that clean severance, that permission to be nowhere in particular.

It occurred to her, somewhere around eight hundred feet, that this was not a healthy relationship with geography. It occurred to her further that she had noticed this before, many times, and that noticing had changed nothing, and that this was probably the definition of a habit versus a choice.

She leveled off at 4,500, pointed the nose at Grayling, and let the autopilot of routine carry her. To the west the Rockies stood up in blue-gray ranks, still wearing snow in their folds like old men wearing hats indoors. Somewhere past those folds was a river that wasn't on maps. Somewhere closer was an envelope in her jacket that she was going to have to open eventually, because Calloway did not send letters for his health.

At 4:15, over the Grayling flats, she saw it: a thread of smoke rising off the ridge trail, thin and wrong, the kind of smoke that meant a signal fire rather than a cook fire. Cook fires made fat smoke. Signal fires made desperate ones.

Freya rocked her wings, dropped low, and looked.

A man stood in a clearing beside the trail, waving both arms over his head, staggering a little as he did it. Old. Alone. His parka was torn at one shoulder and dark at the sleeve, and even from three hundred feet up, even through a windshield spattered with the summer's first bugs, Freya understood what the dark was.

She should keep flying. She had a schedule, a manifest, a reputation built entirely on the fact that things arrived when she said they would. Stopping meant landing on a trail cut, meaning a slope, meaning wind she couldn't see, meaning an hour lost and a favor spent and a story acquired, and stories, Marta had told her just this afternoon, were the cargo that sank you.

But the man was waving like a man who had been waving for a long time. And Freya Fairweather had spent twenty years perfecting the art of not stopping, and she knew, with the certainty of someone who has examined her own machinery many times without ever fixing it, that the art was starting to cost more than it paid.

She circled once to read the wind coming down off the ridge — quartering tailwind on approach, gusting, ugly, doable. She checked the strip of gravel beside the creek twice, then a third time, because the first two times she'd been looking for reasons not to.

Then Freya Fairweather, who never stayed anywhere, who owed eleven thousand dollars to a man with soft hands, who carried her mother's compass in a wool sock behind her seat and her mother's absence in a place no sock could reach — Freya put the flaps down and went in after the smoke.

The landing was rougher than she'd have liked and better than she'd deserved. The gravel held. The plane stopped with thirty feet of clearing to spare.

The old man fell to his knees in front of her propeller wash, and when she got the door open and stepped down into the smell of blood and pine, he was still conscious, barely, and he was clutching something to his chest wrapped in greasecloth, and he was saying one word over and over, hoarse as a crow:

"Noor. Noor. Noor."

Freya knelt in the gravel beside him, and some instinct older than her caution told her that her schedule had just died, and that whatever was wrapped in that greasecloth was going to follow her for the rest of her life.

"Who's Noor?" she asked.

"My granddaughter," Elias Vane said, and smiled with bloody teeth. "And I need you to fly, pilot. I need you to promise."
