## 2. The Dying Prospector

The trouble with small towns is that they're small. Word travels at the speed of gossip, which in a place like Kettle Landing is roughly twice the speed of sound, and by the time I'd finished my coffee at the Bluebird Café that morning, three separate people had told me about the man on the ridge trail.

"Heard he came down off the high country half-dead," said Doris, who ran the café and considered herself the town's central nervous system. "Old prospector type. Shot, they say."

"Who shot him?"

"That's the interesting part, isn't it?" Doris refilled my cup without asking, which in Kettle Landing was both hospitality and surveillance. "Nobody knows. Doc Winters has got him at his place. Won't let anybody in but the nurse."

I should have finished my coffee and flown out. That was the plan — medicine crates to Two Rivers, then north with a load of engine parts for a man who paid late but paid. I had a schedule. I always have a schedule. Schedules are how people like me stay honest: if you never stop anywhere long enough for your debts to catch up, you can pretend you don't have any.

But there was something in the way Doris said *nobody knows* — the way she leaned on it like a woman leaning on a broom she has no intention of using — that made me sit another ten minutes. And then another twenty. And then I gave up pretending and walked the four blocks to Doc Winters' house, because apparently I'm the kind of person who walks toward gunshots instead of away from them, a fact about myself I've been trying to unlearn for thirty years without success.

* * *

Doc Winters answered the door himself, a big man gone soft at the edges, spectacles fogged from the heat of the house. He looked at me the way doctors look at people who are about to become their problem.

"Freya Fairweather," he said. "Doris called you."

"Doris mentioned it."

"She mentioned it to everyone within earshot of her radio." He stepped aside anyway. "You can have five minutes. He's asking for a pilot, specifically. Wouldn't say why. He's not going to make old bones, Freya, so whatever he wants, decide fast."

The room smelled of woodsmoke and iodine. Elias Vane lay in the bed under a quilt that had been mended more times than it had been washed, and my first thought was that he looked less like a dying man than like a man who had died some time ago and simply hadn't gotten around to lying down. His face was weathered down to the grain, all ridges and crevices, and his hands — propped on top of the quilt because they couldn't be tucked under it without pain — were the hands of someone who had spent sixty years arguing with rock and losing slowly.

His left foot was wrapped to the knee. Frostbite, black at the toes. And when he shifted to look at me, I saw the bloodstain on his side, low, below the ribs, where somebody had put a bullet through him and left him to freeze as a courtesy.

"You fly," he said. Not a question. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel.

"I fly."

"They said there's a woman. Bush pilot. Fairweather." He said my name like he was testing whether it would hold weight. Something moved behind his eyes — recognition, or its cousin — and then it was gone, packed away somewhere deep. "Good. Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"Sit down," he said again, with the mild ferocity of a man who had run out of time to be polite, and I sat, because you do.

Up close he smelled of spruce smoke and old sweat and something chemical, kerosene maybe, the smell of men who live alone with machines and fire. His eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and they didn't wander around the room the way most people's do. They stayed on me. It was like being looked at by a survey instrument.

"You're hurt bad," I said, stating the obvious, because sometimes the obvious is the only thing worth saying.

"I'm hurt done," he corrected. "Doc's kind. Doc's wrong. I know what a gut shot looks like after four days walking on it. You get infected, you get fever, and then you get quiet." He smiled briefly, and it was the worst smile I'd seen in years, because it was entirely at peace. "So we haven't got long, you and me, and I've got one thing to trade and you're the only one here who can carry it where it needs to go."

"Where does it need to go?"

He reached under the pillow — slowly, the movement costing him something — and brought out a bundle wrapped in greasecloth, tied with wire. It was the size of a thick paperback book and heavy for its size. He held it out on his palm like an offering at an altar nobody attended anymore.

"What is it?"

"A map."

"I figured that part."

"Not just a map." His hand trembled; the bundle trembled with it. "A map to the source of the Kestrel River. The upper gorge. Country no plane's cleared into, country the syndicate keeps closed, country that opens maybe eleven days a year when the glacier calves and the ice dam lets go." He watched my face while he said it, watching for the flicker, and I'll admit there was a flicker, because every pilot in the interior has heard rumors about the upper Kestrel and every rumor ends with *and nobody comes back*. "I found something up there. Thirty years I kept my mouth shut about it. Then somebody decided my mouth needed closing permanent, and now I've got maybe a day, and the map needs to go to my granddaughter."

"Noor," I said, before I could stop myself.

Something went through his face then, quick as a fish under ice. "You know her name."

"It's a small territory. Names travel."

"Noor Vane," he said, and now the name wasn't being tested, it was being carried, hauled up from somewhere deep in his chest like water from a well. "Cannery docks at Port Anselm. Seventeen years old. Her mother works the line, her father—" he stopped, swallowed. "Her father's none of it. Point is, she doesn't know me. Doesn't want to know me. And she's right not to."

"Why's that?"

"Because I left." He said it flatly, no decoration, the way you'd report a mechanical failure. "Thirty-one years ago I walked out on her mother, who was my daughter, and I told myself I was doing it for good reasons, and the reasons were even true, and it didn't matter, because leaving is leaving. A girl grows up with a hole where her grandfather should be, and then one day a stranger shows up saying the grandfather sent me, and what's she supposed to think?" His gray eyes fixed on mine. "She's supposed to think it's a trick. She's right. Most things that come wearing a gift are tricks."

"So why send anything?"

"Because it isn't a gift." He pushed himself up against the headboard, and the effort turned his lips gray, and I started forward and he waved me off with the hand holding the bundle. "It's a debt. There's a difference. A gift you give because you want to. A debt you pay because you owe. I owe that girl the truth about what's at the top of that river, and I owe it to about two hundred other people who aren't here to collect, and I am out of time to pay any of it myself."

Outside, a dog barked twice and quit, as if even the dogs of Kettle Landing understood the gravity of the moment and didn't want to interrupt.

"Who shot you?" I asked.

"Men in a boat with no name on it. Survey crew, they said. Courteous." The word came out of him like a splinter. "One of them asked me real polite where I'd been all these years, and I told him nothing, and he shot me anyway, which tells you everything about courtesy. They wanted to know if I'd talked. If I'd written anything down. I hadn't, then. Now I have." He looked at the bundle. "Which means whoever finds this gets found too. You understand? This isn't treasure, Miss Fairweather. This is a lit fuse."

"And you want to hand it to a seventeen-year-old."

"I want to hand it to *you*, to carry to her. There's a difference there too." He was breathing harder now, short pulls, a man rowing against a current that was winning. "You've got a plane. You've got no attachments — I asked around, everybody says you land, you unload, you're gone before the coffee's cold. Nobody owns you. Nobody can lean on you. That's worth more than courage, in the country this map goes to. Courage gets people killed. Nothing-to-lose keeps them alive."

"That's not true," I said. "Nothing-to-lose gets plenty of pilots killed. I've buried a few."

"Then bury one more." He laughed, a dry crackle like paper burning. "No. Listen. Listen to me, because I'm going to ask you for something and I need you to hear all of it before you answer."

He laid the bundle in my hands. It was heavier than it looked, dense, and through the greasecloth I could feel the edges of oilskin beneath, layers of protection, a man who had spent decades making sure one piece of paper survived everything the world could throw at it.

"Find Noor Vane at the cannery docks in Port Anselm. Tell her what I found at the source of the Kestrel. Don't tell her I loved her — she won't believe it, and she shouldn't, love that stays silent thirty years isn't worth the word. Tell her what I *did*. Tell her I saw it. Tell her I wrote it down so it couldn't be drowned twice." His grip closed over my wrists, surprisingly strong, the strength of the nearly dead, which is a real thing and stronger than the living suspect. "That's the whole trade. The map for the promise. Say it back to me."

"Elias—"

"*Say it back.*"

I looked down at the bundle in my lap, and at the man attached to the voice, and I thought about schedules, and Calloway, and the particular arithmetic of my life where every favor taken becomes a hook set. I thought: walk away. I thought: you've walked away from harder things than a dying man's last wish, you've built a whole aircraft out of walking away, it flies beautifully.

And then I thought about a girl on cannery docks learning from a stranger that her grandfather had died with her name in his mouth, and that nobody had bothered to tell her, and I felt something in my chest turn over like an engine catching on a cold morning.

"All right," I said. "Here it is. I'll find Noor Vane at the cannery docks in Port Anselm. I'll tell her what you found at the source of the Kestrel River. What you saw, what you wrote down, why it couldn't be drowned twice. I won't dress you up as somebody you weren't. That's the promise."

The tension went out of him all at once, the way air leaves a tire, and he sank back against the headboard looking suddenly smaller, suddenly older, suddenly like exactly what he was: a man with hours left and a lifetime of accounts he couldn't balance.

"That's it," he said softly. "That's all I needed. Thirty years I carried that thing up and down mountains, through winters, past men I knew were watching, and all it ever needed was somebody stubborn enough to keep a promise." He studied me. "You will. I can tell. You've got the look of a person who hates owing more than she hates danger."

"You don't know me."

"Don't I?" And there it was again, that flicker behind his eyes, that almost-recognition, gone before I could net it. "Fairweather," he murmured. "Knew a Fairweather once. Woman named Maren. Came through the high country years back, asking after the river. You wouldn't know anything about that."

My hands went still on the bundle.

"My mother's name was Maren," I heard myself say. "She flew out of Kettle Landing. Twenty-two years ago. She never came back."

Elias Vane looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face made him close his eyes briefly, as if in pain that had nothing to do with the bullet.

"Well," he said finally. "Well, then. Maybe that's why the map picked you. Things up that valley have a way of picking their own." He was fading now, words coming slower, spaced further apart, a radio losing signal. "Go on, then. Go find my girl. Tell her—" his breath caught, held, released, "tell her the old man finally came through. Late. But he came through."

I stood. At the door I looked back. He was staring at the ceiling with his ruined hands folded on the quilt, and he wasn't seeing the ceiling. Whatever he was seeing, it was green water and high country and a long way upstream.

He died the next morning, Doc Winters told me later, quietly, between one breath and the next, like a man stepping off a trail he'd finished walking.

* * *

I loaded the medicine crates at dawn and did not fly out at dawn, which was unlike me, and everyone noticed, which was also unlike me to allow. I sat in the Bluebird with the greasecloth bundle in my flight bag at my feet, feeling its weight through the canvas like a second heartbeat, and I drank two cups of coffee I didn't taste.

Port Anselm was ninety minutes south, over the pass, along the coast. Cannery season. A girl on the docks who didn't know her grandfather existed, or wished he didn't.

Doris refilled my cup a third time and studied me with the frank appraisal of a woman who has watched every departure this airstrip has ever produced.

"You're still here," she observed.

"I'm still here."

"Huh." She set down the pot. "Must be something heavy in that bag."

"There's a map in the bag, Doris."

"Mm." She nodded, unsurprised, unimpressed, taking it in stride the way small towns take everything in stride, which is to say completely and immediately and forever. "Maps are heavy. Heavier than they look." She topped off my cup to the brim, an act of either kindness or sabotage. "Whatever it is, honey, I'd decide quick whose problem it's going to be. Because a thing like that doesn't stay a secret in a town this size, and it doesn't stay yours unless you make it yours."

She was right, of course. Doris was always right, which was the problem with Doris.

Because before I'd finished that third cup — before I'd even fired up the engine — the radio crackled, and a voice I knew too well came through the static, smooth as river ice, asking if I was still in Kettle Landing, and saying he had a business proposition, and saying the word *debt* in a tone that made it sound like a hand on the back of my neck.

Calloway.

I looked at the flight bag. I looked south, toward the coast, toward a girl on the docks who was about to have her whole life rearranged by a stranger with a greasecloth bundle and a promise she'd made to a dying man.

Then I keyed the mic, and lied to Calloway about my heading, and flew south anyway.

Some promises, it turns out, are heavier than debts. Nobody warns you about that. Nobody can.
