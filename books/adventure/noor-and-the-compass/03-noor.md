## 3. Noor

The thing nobody tells you about gutting fish for a living is that your hands learn the work so well they stop belonging to you. By the third summer at the cannery, mine could open a salmon, strip the spine, and flick the roe into the bucket without my eyes ever leaving the water. Which was convenient, because the water was the only interesting thing in Kettle Harbor, and I'd been watching it for signs of my grandfather for six years.

Not signs he was coming back. Signs he was dead. There's a difference, and I'd made my peace with wanting the second one.

"Noor." The foreman, Dree, stood at the end of my line with her clipboard held like a shield. "Boat's coming in. Not a tender."

"Then it's not my problem."

"It's a bush plane. Landed on the strip an hour ago. Woman pilot, asking after Elias Vane's people." Dree lowered her clipboard. "You're his people."

"I'm his granddaughter. It's not the same thing." I flipped another fish, opened it, stripped it, flicked. "His people are whoever he's currently lying to."

Dree didn't argue. Dree had learned years ago that arguing with me was like arguing with the tide — you'd get wet and the tide wouldn't care. She just left the clipboard on the sorting table, which was her way of saying *you'll want this later*, and went back up the ramp.

I kept gutting. That's the honest version. I kept gutting for a solid four minutes, hands doing their borrowed work, while my chest did something that wasn't borrowed at all and that I refused to name.

Elias Vane. My grandfather. The man who taught me to skip stones and read water and then, when I was eleven, walked out of our house in Fort Alder with a duffel bag and a promise to be back before freeze-up. He'd made that promise to my mother, who was dying — slowly, in the way of bad lungs, but dying — and he'd missed the funeral by nine days. Nine days. I'd done the math so many times the numbers had worn smooth as river stones. Nine days. Whatever he'd been chasing upriver had been nine days more important than his daughter's last breath.

So no. I didn't mourn him. Mourning was for people who deserved the grief you spent on them.

The plane, when I finally stomped up to the strip, was a beat-up de Havilland Beaver with a cargo door that hung crooked and a paint job the color of an old bruise. The woman leaning against its strut was maybe thirty, tall, sun-browned, with the kind of stillness you only get from spending a lot of hours alone in small loud machines. She watched me come up the gravel like she was reading water — looking for the rocks under the surface.

"You're Noor," she said.

"Depends who's asking and why."

"Freya Montrose." She didn't put out a hand, which I appreciated. People who put out hands first are usually trying to buy something cheap. "I flew your grandfather out of the country three weeks ago. I found him two days ago on a ridge above the Sleet, half dead."

The fish smell was on me — it was always on me — and I was suddenly, furiously aware of it. "Found him."

"Shot. Frostbitten. He'd been walking on a leg that wasn't working for a long time." She said it flat, no drama in it, and somehow that made it worse, because drama you can dismiss. Flatness you have to sit inside.

"Shot by who?"

"He wouldn't say. He was more worried about the time." Her eyes didn't leave mine. "He died yesterday morning. At the mission in Copper Bend."

The water in the harbor did its usual thing, slapping the pilings, indifferent. Somewhere behind me a gull screamed like it was being murdered, which gulls always did, and for once I understood the impulse.

"Okay," I said.

"That's it? Okay?"

"What do you want me to say? He was a stranger who shared my last name." My voice came out steady, which surprised me, because my hands had gone cold inside my rubber gloves. I stripped the gloves off and shoved them in my coat pocket so she couldn't see them shaking. "I'm sorry he's dead the way you're sorry when a stranger's dog dies. It's sad. It's not mine."

Freya Montrose looked at me for a long moment. Then she nodded, slow, like I'd confirmed something. "He said you'd say something like that. He said, *tell her anyway. She'll pretend she doesn't care, and then she'll ask questions.*" She almost smiled. "He wasn't wrong about either half, was he."

"I'm not asking anything."

"You will."

* * *

I didn't ask anything for two more days, which I counted as a moral victory. She stayed in Kettle Harbor — the only rooming house had one room and a landlady who charged by the rumor — and I went to work and gutted fish and did not think about Elias Vane, except in the gaps, which turned out to be everywhere. He was in the way I held a filleting knife. He was in the way I read the tide tables without thinking. He was in the stones I couldn't stop skipping off the breakwater at lunch, one after another, until my arm ached.

On the third day she came down to the docks at shift change and sat on a bollard across from my station like she had all the time in the world, which bush pilots don't, which meant it was a performance, and I hated that I could read it.

"Your grandfather gave me something," she said. "Before he died. He traded it to me for a promise."

"I'm not interested in his junk."

"It's a map."

My hands stopped. Just for a second. I made them start again. "He drew maps on napkins for tourists. Half of them were lies and the other half were wishes."

"This one's on greasecloth. It's the Sleet headwaters — the country above the gorge, the part no plane's cleared to enter and no boat's stupid enough to run." She reached into her jacket and pulled out a bundle wrapped in oilskin, but she didn't open it. She just held it. "He said there's something at the river's source. Something he found. Something somebody shot him over."

"Then somebody should've shot the map too. Would've saved everyone the trouble."

"Noor." She leaned forward, elbows on her knees. "He was shot through the shoulder and he walked — *walked* — eleven miles down a ridge trail in freezing rain to get to a radio. He could have asked for a medevac. He could have asked for anything. What he asked for was a bush pilot who owed him a favor, and what he traded with the favor was this map and one sentence."

I gutted a fish. Opened, stripped, flicked. My hands were steady again, or lying about it convincingly.

"What sentence?" I asked, and hated myself for it, and kept going anyway because the words were already out and you can't unthrow a stone.

"He said: *Tell my granddaughter Noor what I found. Not the syndicate. Not Calloway. Her.*"

The roe bucket was full and I stared into it like it held an answer. Salmon roe looks like a thousand small orange moons, all packed together, all going nowhere unless somebody carries them upstream. I'd never thought about that before. I thought about it now, and I hated that too.

"He left," I said. "When my mother was dying, he left. Nine days. He missed it by nine days."

"I know."

"You don't know anything. You knew him three weeks."

"I know he talked about you." She said it quietly. "Delirious, mostly. But even delirious, it was your name. Not your mother's. Yours. He kept saying you could read water better than him by the time you were twelve, and he said it like it was the one thing in his life that had gone right."

"Then his life was even sadder than I thought." I yanked the last fish off the table and stood, and my chair went over backward with a bang that made the whole dock look at me, and I didn't care. "You want to know what he found? He found out that leaving is easy. He found it out twenty years ago when he walked out on my grandmother, and then he found it again when he walked out on my mother, and I bet the view from that ridge was real pretty, because he'd gotten so much practice."

"He was trying to come back."

"People are always trying to come back. It's the leaving they're good at." I picked up my chair, set it down, picked it up again because my hands needed a job. "You flew all this way to hand a dead man's map to a girl who gutted fish through her mother's funeral. You want gratitude? Go find a family that got the whole body."

* * *

But that night I couldn't sleep, and the map was all I could see — not the map itself, I hadn't looked at it, but the shape of it, the oilskin bundle, the way Freya Montrose held it like it was warm. I got up in the dark and sat on the breakwater with my boots dangling over the black water, and I did the arithmetic I always did, the nine days, and then a new arithmetic on top of it: three weeks ago he'd flown out of the country. Three weeks. My mother had been dead six years. So what had he been doing for six years that was worth missing the funeral and worth getting shot over and worth his last breath being spent on my name?

That was the question, and questions were the one inheritance he'd actually left me. He used to say, *the river tells you where the rocks are by how it breaks around them.* Everything breaks around something.

I found her at dawn, loading the Beaver. She saw me coming and didn't say anything, just kept coiling rope, letting me arrive at my own speed, which I grudgingly admitted was smart.

"I want to see it," I said. "The map. Not because I care. Because if some syndicate shot my grandfather, I want to know what they were afraid of, and I want to be the one who decides what happens to it. Not you. Not Calloway — yes, I know who Calloway is, everyone on this coast knows who Calloway is. Me."

Freya set the rope down and looked at me with that water-reading stare, and then she unwrapped the oilskin right there on the gravel, and there it was. The Sleet headwaters, drawn in a hand I knew — I'd know it anywhere, that cramped left-slanting script that used to label my childhood, *good skipping stone, this one* — and marked in three places with a symbol I didn't know, a circle with a line through it like a struck-through zero.

And there, in the corner, in that same handwriting, smaller than everything else:

*For N. She'll know what to do with the water.*

I looked at it for a long time. The gulls screamed. The harbor slapped its pilings. My chest did the thing I'd been refusing to name for three days, and this time it had a name, and the name was grief, and it arrived exactly the way everything in my life arrived — as anger, wearing grief's coat, or the other way around, I couldn't tell anymore.

"He should have given it to me himself," I said.

"Yeah," Freya said. "He should have."

I folded the oilskin back over the map with hands that finally, finally felt like mine. "So where's the source," I said, "and how fast can this thing fly?"

Freya Montrose smiled like a woman who'd just watched a river break around a rock she'd predicted all along. "Fast enough," she said. "But we've got a problem. Somebody's already asking about the map — and he's asking through me."

The name she said next made the morning go cold: Calloway. And the way she said it told me the problem wasn't behind us. It was sitting in the cockpit, and it had been the whole time.
