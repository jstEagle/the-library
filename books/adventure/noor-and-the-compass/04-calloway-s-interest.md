## 4. Calloway's Interest

The trouble with owing a man like Calloway is that he never sends a bill. A bill you can pay, argue with, or lose in the seat cushions of your plane. What Calloway sends is an invitation.

Mine came on a Tuesday, folded inside a crate of spark plugs I hadn't ordered, written in pencil on the back of a fuel chit from a strip I'd flown out of three years ago. *Come to dinner. Thursday. Wear something you can burn.* That last part was his idea of humor. It worked, which was worse.

I told myself I could refuse. I'd refused him before — twice by radio, once in person, once by simply being airborne when he landed at my strip and waited four hours for me to come back. But there's a difference between refusing a man and refusing a debt, and Calloway had spent nine years making sure I understood which one I'd be doing.

Here's how the arithmetic went, for anyone keeping score, which Calloway certainly was:

Nine years ago I came north with a wrecked Cessna 180, no money, no family, and a name I'd half made up. Calloway owned the only hangar within two hundred miles that would extend credit to a woman nobody vouched for. He fixed my engine. He fronted my fuel. He did it smiling, and he did it without papers, because Calloway understood something about paper: it's only useful if you intend to collect legally. He didn't. He intended to collect however collecting became convenient.

The number he quoted me grew the way numbers do when they live in a man's head instead of a ledger. Interest compounded on interest. Favors got folded into the principal — the time he "found" me a medical run when I was desperate, the winter he let me park free, the season he bought my mail contract out from under me and leased it back at terms I pretended were generous. By this spring the debt was whatever he said it was, whenever he said it was due, and I had learned to fly light and sleep shallow.

So Thursday found me walking up the gravel path to his house above the river bend, wearing my good flannel, which was also my only flannel, and carrying a bottle of rye I'd paid for with money I needed for tundra tires. The house was the biggest thing in town that wasn't a mountain — timber-frame, glass-fronted, built by a man who wanted everyone who flew past to know exactly where he lived. His floatplane sat on the water below like a dog waiting at the end of a chain.

He opened the door himself. That was part of the performance. Calloway liked to be seen doing his own hospitality, as if the absence of staff proved he was still a regular guy and not what he actually was, which was the closest thing the interior had to a government.

"Freya Montrose." He said my name the way you'd set down a card you intended to play later. "You look thin. Flying agrees with everyone except the people who do it."

"I eat fine," I said, and handed him the rye.

He looked at the label, looked at me, and smiled the smile I'd flown nine years trying not to earn. "Come in. Moose stew. You'll hate it."

I hated it. It was excellent.

* * *

Dinner was the two of us and a silence he filled the way he filled everything — comfortably, at his own pace, with the assumption that the room belonged to him. He talked about the season. About the syndicate's new survey crews working the upper watersheds, helicopters moving equipment in pieces over ridgelines that used to be nobody's business. About a judge in Anchorage who owed him. About a widow in Fort Reliance whose trapline he'd bought for less than her husband's funeral cost, and how she now cooked for his crews and called it fair.

I ate and listened and kept my face the way pilots keep their faces in weather: nothing showing, everything noted.

It was nearly over — plates cleared, second pour of the rye, the window gone black against the river — when he said, casually, the way a man drops a hook through ice:

"Elias Vane."

I didn't move. Moving is information. "Never heard of him."

"No?" Calloway turned his glass a quarter turn on the tablecloth. "Funny. Because a friend of mine runs the trading post at Kettle Landing, and Tuesday morning a frostbitten old prospector came off the ridge trail shot through the shoulder, and Tuesday afternoon a bush pilot with your tail number bought every bandage in the building and flew out heading northwest." He set the glass down. "And Wednesday morning my friend found a greasecloth wrapper in the stove ash behind Vane's cabin. Burned. Empty. Whatever was wrapped in it left in somebody's airplane."

The stew turned to lead in my stomach. I'd burned the wrapper myself, back at my strip, because it seemed tidy at the time. Nine years of careful flying, undone by housekeeping.

"You've got eyes everywhere," I said.

"I've got eyes where things are worth seeing." He leaned back. "Vane's been in these hills thirty years. Everyone thought he was chasing color. Turns out he was chasing something else, and now he's dying of it, and the map to it is sitting in your cargo pod, or your mattress, or wherever you keep the things you don't want found. And I am sitting here wondering why a pilot who owes me more than her plane is worth didn't mention it."

"Because it isn't yours."

"Neither is the air under your wings," he said pleasantly, "and yet here we both are, breathing it."

There it was. That was always the shape of a conversation with Calloway: he started reasonable and ended somewhere you couldn't follow without bleeding.

* * *

He laid it out like a man dealing cards he'd already counted.

Whatever Vane found at the source of that river — gold, a strike, a lost claim, something the syndicate's helicopters were crawling the watershed looking for — Calloway wanted it brought back to him. Not sold to him. Brought back. There was a difference, and the difference was ownership.

In exchange: the debt. All of it. Every invented dollar, every compounded favor, wiped clean. He'd even write it down. On paper. Signed. He almost laughed saying it, because we both knew the joke — the one time Calloway offered paper was the time the paper didn't matter.

"And if I say no?"

"Then you owe me what you owe me, and I'll need to start thinking about collection." He let that sit. "Freya. I like you. That's not sentiment, that's inventory. You're the best stick-and-rudder pilot in the territory and you're the only one who doesn't drink before noon. I'd rather keep you flying than sell your plane, and I'd rather have you grateful than gone. But gratitude is a choice, and so is the other thing."

I should tell you what the other thing was, because I spent the whole flight home turning it over. Collection, with Calloway, wasn't repossession. Repossession is a business transaction. Collection, for him, was pedagogy. He'd take the plane, sure — but first he'd make sure everyone from Kettle Landing to the coast knew exactly what happened to pilots who crossed him, and he'd do it slowly enough that the lesson stuck, and politely enough that no law ever got a handle on it. I'd seen it done once, to a trapper named Ostrander who shorted him on furs. Took Ostrander two years to lose everything, and by the end he was thanking Calloway for the charity work. That was the part that lived in me like a splinter: not the losing, but the thanking.

"So that's the offer," he said. "One trip. Bring back what the old man found, and you're free. Free, Freya. You could point that plane anywhere and never look at this river again."

For about four seconds, I admit it, I wanted it. Freedom held out by the man who owns your cage has a shine to it that honest freedom never gets. Fly one errand, land rich in the only currency that mattered, done. Nine years closed like a book.

But I'd sat with Elias Vane while he bled into his own coat, and I'd heard the promise come out of my own mouth, and here's the thing about promises made to dying men: they're the only kind that can't be renegotiated. He hadn't asked me to bring back treasure. He'd asked me to find his granddaughter and tell her what he saw. There was no version of hauling that story back to Calloway's dining room that wasn't selling it, and some things you don't sell even to buy yourself.

"No," I said.

Calloway nodded slowly, like I'd confirmed something he'd already bet on. "May I ask why?"

"Because it isn't mine to give you. It belongs to a girl in Fort Reliance who doesn't know yet that she owns it."

"A girl." He tasted the word. "Family?"

"Not mine."

"That's a shame." He stood, collected our plates like any host, carried them to the counter, and came back drying his hands. "Because here's my problem, Freya. I believe you're telling the truth. Which means somewhere out there is a map, or was a map, and a girl, and a valley full of whatever Vane died of — and I'm a patient man, but patience isn't the same as waiting. The syndicate's crews will find that country eventually. Helicopters don't respect grief or promises. So either you bring it to me first, or someone brings it to them, and either way I learn what's up that river." He smiled again, and this time I finally understood the smile, and understanding it was worse than fearing it. "Unless, of course, you get there first and decide there's nothing worth bringing back. In which case — well. I'd want to hear that from you in person."

"Do you hear yourself?" I said. "That's not an offer. That's a leash with a bow on it."

"It's collateral," he said. "You've been collateral for nine years, Freya. I'm just telling you what you already are." He walked me to the door, and on the step, in the cold, he shook my hand — firm, warm, almost fond. "Fly safe. Seriously. I mean that. Dead pilots settle nothing."

I walked down to the water and untied my canoe and did not look back, because looking back is information too, and I'd given him enough for one night.

* * *

The paddle back across the bend took twenty minutes and lasted about a decade. The river was flat, black, running slow under a sky with no moon in it, and every stroke I made was loud as a confession. I kept replaying it — the burned wrapper, the informant at Kettle Landing, the word *collateral*, delivered like a gift. He'd known about Vane within a day. Which meant he'd known about Vane before Vane stumbled off that ridge, or he had someone close enough to my life that my whole route was a page he could read. Both possibilities felt the same from inside a canoe: like flying with a hole in the fuselage you haven't located yet.

By the time I grounded the canoe at my strip, I'd stopped being afraid and started being practical, which is my version of panic. Practical said: he'll wait a week, maybe two, then stop asking nicely. Practical said: the syndicate's helicopters are already in the watershed, and Harkness — I'd heard the name from a fuel hauler, a survey boss, courteous, they said, the dangerous kind of courteous — Harkness wants the same country Calloway wants, and neither of them knows the other is hunting, and I was the only living soul holding the thread that connected all of it.

Practical also said: you promised a dying man. The girl comes first. Everything else is weather.

I sat in the dark on the gravel with the compass in my hand — my mother's compass, the brass one I'd carried since I was six and never once trusted, because it pointed true north about half the time and pointed somewhere private the rest — and I thought about what Calloway had really bought with his dinner. Not information. Leverage. He didn't need the map. He needed me to know he knew, so that every mile I flew now, I'd be flying for him whether I admitted it or not.

Fine. Then I'd fly like a woman with a tailwind and a grudge.

At dawn I fueled the Cessna full — tanks, spare drums, the works — and filed a float plan that lied about my destination, which is illegal, and necessary, and the first dishonest thing I'd done in nine years that I didn't feel bad about. In the cargo pod went rope, raft kit, the medicine crate I'd been meaning to deliver to Fort Reliance anyway, and a folded chart of the upper river with a grease-pencil line on it that no dispatcher was ever going to see.

Fort Reliance first. Find the granddaughter. Tell her what her grandfather saw.

And if a certain fixer with a glass house on the river bend wanted to know what I found up there — well. He knew where I lived.

He'd just have to catch me airborne.
