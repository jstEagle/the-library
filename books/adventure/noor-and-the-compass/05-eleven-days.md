## 5. Eleven Days

The cannery office smelled like diesel, fish oil, and the particular despair of a man who'd been doing paperwork for thirty years. Mr. Okonkwo — everybody called him Okie, including, I suspected, his mother — sat behind a desk buried under manifests and said, "You want a raft. In April."

"I want two rafts," Freya said. "And rope. And whatever you've got in the way of dry bags that don't leak."

Okie looked at her over his glasses the way teachers look at students who have just announced they intend to lick a light socket. "The upper gorge doesn't open until calving season. You know that. Everybody knows that. The river comes up maybe forty feet of vertical in the narrows when the glacier starts shedding."

"Eleven days," Freya said. "Give or take."

"Give or take what? Your life?" He pulled a manifest toward himself as if paperwork could protect him from us. "Last year the Kellermans tried it early. Found their raft wrapped around a rock at Mile Nine. Took the coroner three weeks to get them out, and he billed the family for the helicopter."

I stood by the door with my arms crossed, doing my best impression of someone who was only there under protest. Which was true. Mostly. My grandfather had died on a ridge trail with a bullet hole in him and a map wrapped in greasecloth, and now there was a bush pilot in my kitchen telling me the map led somewhere no plane was cleared to fly, and I had said — out loud, with witnesses — that if anyone was going to find out what Elias Vane found, it would be me.

I'd had about nine hours to regret this.

"Eleven days is enough," Freya said, in the tone of a woman who had decided something and would not be un-deciding it. "If we put in at the old logging ramp and make good time."

"Make good time." Okie took off his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Miss Fairweather. I've known you since you were hauling crates for your uncle. You are many things. Careful is not one of them."

"That's slander," Freya said. "I'm extremely careful. I've just never crashed anywhere I couldn't take off from again."

He looked at me then, over her shoulder, and I got the full weight of an adult man's silent plea: *talk some sense into her.* And I understood, in that moment, that I had already become the responsible one in this operation, which felt like being promoted to captain of a sinking canoe.

"She's coming too," Freya added, jerking a thumb at me, before I could decide whether to be sensible or not.

"I'm standing right here," I said.

"Then say something useful."

What I said was: "The narrows drop through a basalt chute below the confluence. If the water's up, there's a seam on river-left where the current folds back on itself. You can ferry across it if you edge out slow, but if you punch straight into the fold it'll hold you against the wall and drown you politely while you think about your choices."

The office went quiet. Okie's eyebrows climbed. Freya turned and looked at me properly for the first time since the airstrip — not at the cannery kid, not at Vane's granddaughter, but at me, Noor, seventeen years old, wearing rubber boots with a hole in the left toe.

"Where'd you learn that?"

"Reading water is the only thing my grandfather ever taught me that was worth the tuition." I kept my voice flat. "We ran rivers every summer until I was fourteen. Then he decided family was optional and stopped calling. But the water didn't change. Water never changes. People do."

Freya held my gaze a second longer than was comfortable, then nodded once, like a door closing on a decision. "She reads water better than any pilot I know," she told Okie. "Including me. Pilots read water from eight hundred feet. She reads it from inside it."

"Pilots also live longer," Okie said, but he was already pulling a key ring off a nail behind him, which meant he'd surrendered. "Two rafts. The cataraft frame's got a cracked crossbar — you'll want to lash it. Rope's on the wall, count what you take. And if the coroner bills anybody, it's going on your tab, Fairweather, because I know for a fact you don't have one worth seizing."

* * *

We spent the next two days getting ready, and I learned three things about Freya Montrose in that time.

One: she checked everything twice. Straps, seams, fuel lines, the stitching on the dry bags. For a woman who flew into country no plane was cleared to enter, she prepared like someone who expected the world to betray her specifically.

Two: she talked to her plane. A de Havilland Beaver called *Juneau*, tail half silvered with patched aluminum, and she patted the cowling and muttered to it — "come on, old girl, one more ugly landing" — in a voice she probably thought I couldn't hear.

Three: she avoided the subject of my grandfather unless I brought it up, and when I brought it up, she answered everything except the thing I actually wanted to know.

"Tell me again how he looked," I said. We were in the hangar, lashing the cracked crossbar with aircraft wire, because apparently rafts built from airplane parts were a normal thing in Freya's world. Night had come down outside. The heater ticked.

"Gray," she said. "Frostbite up both hands. The shot went through his left side, low. He'd walked a long way — his boots were done, he'd wrapped them in bark strips."

"Did he say anything? About me? About my mom?"

Freya's hands paused on the wire, then kept twisting. "He asked if I'd fly. That was the first thing. Not help — fly. Like he knew time mattered more than comfort." She tied off the wire and bit the end clean. "And he made me promise. Before anything else. Before I even saw the map. He said, *tell my granddaughter what I found.* Not show. Tell. Like the finding was dangerous and the telling was the safe part."

"And you agreed. To a dying stranger. Just like that."

"Just like that." She shrugged, but it wasn't casual; it was the shrug of somebody carrying something heavy and pretending it was a jacket. "Some promises you make because you mean them. Some you make because the man asking is dying and there's nobody else in the world to ask. Those are the same promise, mostly."

I wound the wire spool shut harder than necessary. "He left. You know that, right? Twelve years ago. Mom cried for a year and then got hard, and I grew up watching her check the mail like it might apologize. And now he dies and sends a stranger instead of a letter, and I'm supposed to run a river for him."

"Noor—"

"He didn't even leave the map *to* me. He traded it to you. For a ride he never took." I heard my own voice crack on the last word and hated it. "That's the part that gets me. He spent his last breath haggling."

Freya was quiet a moment. Then: "Or he spent his last breath making sure it went to you and not to whoever shot him. There's a difference between haggling and hiding something where the wolves can't smell it." She stood, brushed shavings off her knees. "Get some sleep. We fly at first light, weather permitting, and weather never permits, so we fly anyway."

* * *

First light came gray and mean, the kind of dawn that looks like it lost an argument with the night. We loaded *Juneau* on the gravel strip behind the cannery while the gulls screamed their opinions, and I will admit — privately, forever, to no one — that my stomach did something complicated when the wheels left the ground.

Not fear exactly. Something adjacent. The town shrinking under us, the cannery roofs going small and tin-colored, my whole life compressing into a place you could cover with a thumb. My mother was at her shift; I'd left a note that said *gone to settle Grandpa's affairs, back in two weeks,* which was technically true the way a knife is technically a spoon.

"You okay back there?" Freya called over the engine noise.

"Lying," I called back, and she laughed, and somehow that made it better.

We flew north along the river valley, and I'll say this for bush pilots: they see the country honest. From the air the river was a brown-green braid unwinding through spruce and muskeg, splitting and rejoining, patient as anything. Freya flew it the way other people drive a familiar road — tilting a wing to look at a bend, muttering "still there" at a gravel bar like an old friend.

Then the valley narrowed, and the mountains came in close on both sides, and the river went from braid to blade.

"There," Freya said, and pointed.

The gorge opened below us like a wound. Walls of black basalt, close enough together that the river filled them edge to edge, white water stitched all the way down its length. Even from the air, even with the engine roaring, I could feel it — that pull water has, the gravity of moving things.

And above it all, at the head of the valley, the glacier.

It came down between two peaks like something poured and frozen mid-pour, blue in its crevasses, dirty gray on its face, and even at this distance I could hear it — a deep, irregular cracking, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"Calving's started," Freya said. "Early this year. That's our window opening."

I leaned toward the window, reading the water below us the way I'd been taught — the way Elias Vane taught me, back when teaching me was still something he wanted to do. The gorge ran high. Higher than normal for the season. You could see it in the color, that milky glacial green, and in the way the rapids stacked up against the walls without the usual gravel showing through.

"It's running big," I said. "Look at the seam lines. See how the current piles against river-right below the second bend? That's a lateral wave building. When the glacier lets go up top, that whole chute's going to double."

"So we're early or we're late."

"We're right on time," I said. "Which is worse. Eleven days means the water's rising the whole time. Every day we go upstream, it gets harder. Every day we come back down—" I did the math and didn't love the answer. "We can't dawdle."

"Dawdling's not really in the flight plan." Freya banked toward a wide gravel bar maybe six miles short of the gorge mouth — pale, long, flat, the kind of landing strip God leaves lying around for people who pray to propellers. "That's our put-in. Or crash-in, depending."

"Optimist."

"Realist with good insurance."

She brought *Juneau* down onto the bar in a landing so smooth I only knew we'd touched ground because the sound changed, and we unloaded rafts and rope and the medicine crate — the one piece of cargo she refused to explain, strapped amidships like a passenger — and then she did something I didn't expect.

She handed me the throw rope and said, "Show me."

"Show you what?"

"The fold. You talked about it at Okie's like you'd swum it. Walk me through it." She nodded downstream, toward the distant white noise of the gorge. "I've flown that chute forty times. I've never run it. You've run water like it your whole life. So walk me through it, and don't dumb it down. I hate being dumbed down to."

So I stood on the gravel bar with a coil of rope in my hands and the wind coming down-valley cold off the ice, and I taught a grown woman — a celebrated pilot, a legend of the interior, a person who owed money to frightening men — how to read a river.

"Ferry angle," I said, dragging a line in the gravel with my boot heel. "You never point downstream. You point forty-five degrees across, let the current do the work. The river's always faster than you. Fight it and it wins. Agree with it and it carries you."

I drew the chute. The constriction. The fold on river-left where the current doubled back on itself.

"Here. This eddy fence. It looks calm. It's lying. Anything that crosses that line gets sucked upstream into the recirculation and held. Boats have sat in that fold for hours, spinning, while the people in them figured out they couldn't paddle out." I looked up at her. "You don't cross the fence. You respect the fence. You edge along it, you feel for the seam, and when the seam softens — and it does soften, every few minutes, the river breathes — you go. Fast. Committed. No halfway."

Freya crouched over my drawing in the gravel for a long moment. Wind pulled at her jacket. Somewhere up-valley, the glacier boomed, once, like a door closing in a very large house.

"You got all this from summers with a man who walked out on you," she said. Not cruel. Just checking.

"I got it from the water," I said. "He was just the one who made me look."

Something passed over her face then — recognition, maybe, or grief wearing recognition's coat. She stood up and stuck out her hand, rough palm, pilot's calluses.

"Partners, then," she said. "Not passenger and pilot. Partners. You read the water, I keep us alive in the air and on the ground, and neither of us dumbs anything down."

I shook it. Her grip was strong and her hand was cold and I thought, absurdly, *this is the first adult who's ever asked me for something instead of telling me.*

"Partners," I said. "But I'm navigating the gorge, and if you argue with me in the rapids, I'm allowed to say I told you so."

"Deal. And if we die, I'm allowed to haunt you."

"Fair."

We worked until dark, rigging the cataraft, lashing the frame, waterproofing the medicine crate with tarp and wire. The glacier rumbled all evening, regular now, like breathing. Like something enormous waking up.

I fell asleep in the plane's shadow with the river talking to itself fifty yards away, and dreamed of my grandfather's hands — younger than I remembered them, steady on a paddle, pointing at a seam in the current and saying, *there, Noor, see it? Right there. The river tells you everything if you stop trying to tell it.*

I woke at gray light to Freya shaking my shoulder, and her face was wrong.

"Not the weather," she said, before I could ask. "Look downstream."

At the mouth of the gorge, six miles off, where the morning mist hung over the water — there was a boat. Small, motorized, running dark, nosing up the channel toward the narrows.

Nobody runs the gorge at calving season. Nobody runs it at all, not anymore. Except us, and we hadn't launched yet.

"Somebody beat us to the window," Freya said quietly.

I stood there with sleep still in my eyes and the rope coiled cold in my hands, and watched the little boat dwindle upriver into the white noise of the narrows, and felt the eleven days start ticking down like water through my fingers.
