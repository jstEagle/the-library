## 6. The Compass That Lies

The compass had been lying to me for three hours before I admitted it.

I want to be clear about what kind of lying this was, because compasses, in my experience, are honest to a fault. They're the only instruments I've ever trusted completely — more than radios, which gossip, more than fuel gauges, which flatter, more than men, obviously. A compass points north. That's the whole contract. You don't have to like what it tells you, but you can build a life on it. I had, more or less. Twenty years of flying cargo and rumor across the interior, and the one thing that never changed on me was that little brass needle swinging in its glass coffin, faithful as a dog.

At 0940, somewhere over the divide with the valley mouth still forty minutes out, it pointed south.

I noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you know by heart — a small cold tick at the base of the skull before the conscious mind catches up. I was running the trim, drinking terrible coffee from a dented thermos, and Noor was asleep in the right seat with her boots on the firewall and her grandfather's map folded into her jacket like a letter she wasn't ready to read. The needle sat there, steady and serene, aimed at the tail of the airplane.

"Very funny," I told it.

The needle did not laugh. Compasses never do. That's part of the contract too.

I did what any sane pilot does when an instrument misbehaves: I ignored it, then checked it against everything else, then ignored it again with more conviction. The GPS agreed with the sun, which agreed with the terrain sliding under the wings, which all agreed we were heading north-northwest up the spine of the country, toward a river valley that appeared on exactly none of my charts and appeared, in a grease-stained rendering in Noor's jacket, as a single confident line drawn by a dying man. Everything agreed. Everything except the compass.

I tapped the glass. The needle shivered, considered, and swung back to south like a man returning to a barstool.

*Deviation,* I told myself. *You're hauling a crate of magnets in the back, you genius, you've been flying with a load of medical supplies and somebody's ore samples for two days.* That was the comfortable explanation and I wore it like a coat two sizes too small. Because I knew what was in the cargo bay — bandages, penicillin, a case of insulin packed in dry ice, and the medicine crate we'd salvaged nothing from yet because we hadn't crashed yet, that came later — and none of it was magnetic. I'd packed it myself. I pack like a woman who expects to be audited by God.

So I flew on, and the compass pointed south, and I did not tell Noor, because she was seventeen and grieving and had already decided I was the most reliable thing in her life, a burden of trust I was carrying the way you carry a full cup of hot coffee across a moving deck — carefully, and with a growing certainty that it would end badly for someone.

* * *

The valley announced itself before I saw it. The country ahead had been folding up all morning — ridgelines stacking like dropped cards, timber climbing the slopes in dark ranks — and then the land simply opened, a cleft running east to west with a river in its floor the color of gunmetal and milk. Glacial flour. Water that had been grinding rock since before anyone drew a map of it. The calving season had done its work; the upper gorge was open, Elias Vane had sworn it on what was left of his life, and from eight thousand feet I could see he hadn't lied about that, at least. The river ran wide and brown-green and unhurried, the way rivers do when they're pretending to be harmless.

"Freya." Noor's voice, sleep-rough. She'd woken the way she always did, instantly, no transition — one minute a kid asleep against the window, the next a sentry. "That's it?"

"That's it."

"It doesn't look forbidden."

"Nothing does from up here. That's the whole trick of altitude. From up here, everything looks survivable." I banked us gently along the north wall so she could see down the length of it. "Give it an hour. It'll get opinionated."

She leaned into the glass, and I watched her watch the valley, and I felt the particular ache I'd been feeling for two days now, which was the ache of a woman who had spent her whole adult life making sure nobody depended on her, discovering that it was too late for that. She had her grandfather's jaw. She had his way of looking at a landscape like it owed her an answer. And she had the map, and I had the promise, and between us we had a fuel plan with a margin so thin it qualified as a philosophical position.

I glanced at the compass. Habit. Twenty years of habit.

It was pointing at Noor.

I want to describe this precisely, because I've turned it over so many times since that the edges have gone soft, and I don't want to soften it here. The needle wasn't spinning. It wasn't erratic. It was *aimed* — steady, settled, the way it pointed north on every calm day of my life — and what it was aimed at was the seventeen-year-old in my right seat, at her chest, at the map folded inside her jacket. South had been a phase. South had been the valley, maybe, or something in it. Now it had found a new true north and it was sitting three feet from me with her boots on my firewall.

"Freya." Noor hadn't turned her head. "You've got a face on."

"What kind of face?"

"The kind my mom used to make when the phone rang late." She turned, and her eyes went to the compass on the panel — because of course they did, she was Vane's granddaughter, she missed nothing that mattered — and then back to me. "That's your mom's, right? The one you said she left."

"I said she left it behind. Along with everything else." I kept my voice level, pilot-calm, the voice I used on nervous passengers and my own reflection. "It's a good compass. Was. It's acting up."

"Acting up how?"

I could have lied to her. I'd had plenty of practice lying to passengers — *that's just turbulence, that smell is nothing, we're absolutely going to make it* — and lying to Noor would have been easy, and it would have been the beginning of the end of whatever we were building in this airplane. So I told the truth, or the part of it that fit between here and the gravel bar I needed to find.

"It stopped pointing north about an hour out. First south. Now—" I tapped the glass. The needle didn't so much as tremble. "Now that."

Noor looked at the needle. She looked at herself, as if checking. Then she did the thing that made me understand Elias Vane hadn't just left his granddaughter a map — he'd left her a mind, and it was a good one. She didn't laugh. She didn't say *that's creepy* or *planes do weird stuff.* She said:

"When did it change?"

"Change?"

"From south to me. Something happened, or we passed something. Instruments don't get bored, Freya. They respond." She was already twisting in her seat, looking back along our track, at the ridgeline we'd crossed maybe ten minutes ago. "What was back there?"

"Rock," I said. "Spruce. A glacier you can't see from this side. Twenty miles of nothing that's on no chart I own." And under that, in the basement of my voice where I keep the things I don't say: *my mother.* Because that's what was back there. That's what had always been back there, in this direction, in every direction I'd ever refused to look. Maren Fairweather had walked into country like this twenty years ago with a pack, a rifle, and the twin of the compass on my panel, and had not walked out, and I had built an entire flying life on the principle that the past stays put if you keep your airspeed up.

The needle held on Noor, patient as a held breath.

* * *

We dropped into the valley mouth through a layer of cloud that behaved less like weather and more like a curtain being drawn for us specifically, which I did not like, because weather is supposed to be indifferent. The walls rose on either side — granite going up and up into the gray, streaked with the white scars of avalanches, timber clinging to every argument of flat ground — and the river widened below us, braiding and re-braiding around gravel bars like a man changing his mind.

I'd flown into a lot of places I wasn't cleared to be. That's most of the job, out here. But there's a difference between illegal and *unwritten*, and this valley was unwritten. No chart. No strip marked. No radio traffic, no beacon, no fuel, no witness except the kind that doesn't talk. Flying into it felt less like breaking a rule and more like reading someone else's mail.

The compass, I noticed, had gone back to pointing south. Straight down our track. Up the valley. Toward the source.

I unclipped it from the panel — a two-second job, a screw and a mount, done a hundred times for maintenance — and put it in my jacket pocket, and told Noor the mount was loose.

"Uh-huh," she said, in the tone of a person filing something away for later, and I remembered that she'd grown up around men like her grandfather, men who lied by omission as a love language, and that she had survived it by keeping receipts.

* * *

I want to talk about my mother for a minute, because she was in the cockpit with us the whole way in, the way she'd been in every cockpit for twenty years, and I've never once said her name out loud in this story and I'm not going to start now, but she was there.

She left when I was nine. That's the version I tell. The true version has more weather in it. She was a bush pilot too — that's the part nobody believes, that there were women out here doing it before it was fashionable, running supplies and patients and the occasional fugitive into country that maps treated as a rumor — and she was good, better than me if I'm honest, and one September she filed a flight plan into the kind of valley that doesn't get flight plans, and she didn't come back. No wreckage. No distress call. Nothing. The search went eleven days, which was generous, and then the interior did what the interior does, which is close over the hole like water.

They gave me her effects in a cardboard box. The compass was in it. No note. No explanation. Just the brass and the glass and the needle, pointing north, faithful as ever, as if to say: *this still works. Whatever happened to me, this still works.*

I learned to fly on her memory and her instrument, and I told everyone — told myself — that the compass was just a compass, that keeping it was sentiment and nothing more, that a woman who never stays anywhere can't be abandoned because you can't abandon a moving target. Twenty years I flew with it. Twenty years it pointed north. And in all that time I never once flew this direction, into this country, and I want it on the record that I told myself that was coincidence. That the interior is big. That a pilot goes where the cargo goes.

The cargo, this time, was a dying man's promise and a girl with her boots on my firewall, and the compass had stopped pretending.

Because here's what I hadn't told Noor, what I'd barely told myself: the needle hadn't started misbehaving when we crossed the divide. It had started the night Elias Vane died. I'd been sitting with the map, studying it by lamplight in the shack at the strip, and the compass had been sitting on the table beside it, and at some point I'd looked down and the needle was swinging — slow, wide arcs, like a searchlight, like something looking for something. I'd blamed the lamp. I'd blamed the ore samples in Vane's pack. I'd blamed everything except the obvious, which is that the map and the compass had come from the same country, and one of them was calling the other home.

*The past, tapping on the windshield.* That's what it was. Not breaking in. Not yet. Just tapping — politely, the way a stranger taps on glass at night — asking to be let in.

* * *

The gravel bar was where Vane's map said it would be, a pale comma in the river's sentence, long enough for a plane if you were good and short enough that you'd better be. I set up the approach with the wind quartering off the glacier and Noor calling the water for me — she read that river like text, calling the channels, the shallows, the color changes where it went from knee-deep to over-your-head, and I'll say now what I didn't say then: I've flown with bush pilots who had thirty years on her and couldn't read moving water half so well.

"Left side's braided out," she said. "Touch down long if you have to. There's a channel cuts through the middle bar — you'll see the gravel's darker, that means it's wet, that means it's deep enough to eat a wheel."

"Where'd you learn that?"

"Cannery docks. You learn what water's doing or the water learns it on you." A pause. "Touch down past the driftwood line. There's a logjam at the top end."

I touched down past the driftwood line. The tires kissed gravel, the tail came up, and we rolled out in the kind of long shallow valley-bottom silence that makes you whisper even though the engine's still turning. I shut her down. The prop ticked as it cooled. The river said what it always says, which is everything and nothing.

And in my jacket pocket, against my ribs, I felt it — not heard, not saw, *felt* — the compass settle. The needle going still. Pointed south. Pointed up the valley. Pointed, with a patience I found worse than any spinning, at whatever was waiting for us at the source of that river.

Noor was already out on the gravel, map in hand, hair full of glacier wind, looking up-valley the way you look at a door you're about to knock on.

"Freya," she called. "There's smoke. Way up. Past the gorge." She shaded her eyes. "Somebody's up there. Somebody's *home*."

I stood in the propwash with my hand over my jacket pocket, over the brass and the glass and the lying needle, and I thought about a September twenty years gone, and a woman who flew into a valley with no name, and a cardboard box with no note in it.

"Then we'd better go say hello," I said, and hated how steady my voice came out.

Because the needle hadn't been pointing at Noor, I understood suddenly. It had been pointing through her. And whatever it was aimed at, up there in the smoke, had been waiting for me a very long time.
