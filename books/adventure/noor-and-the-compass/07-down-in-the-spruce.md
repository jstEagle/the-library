## 7. Down in the Spruce

The engine coughed twice before I understood what the sound meant.

I'd been around boats my whole life — cannery docks, gillnetters, the old two-stroke on Dad's skiff that would run fine all summer and then die on a Sunday just to spite him. I knew what a sick motor sounded like. I knew the difference between a cough and a stutter and a death rattle. So when the engine of Freya's little bush plane went *cough-cough* and then settled into something that sounded like a man trying to swallow gravel, I knew exactly what we were in for, and I hated that I knew it.

"Tell me that's normal," I said.

Freya didn't answer right away. She was doing things with her hands — small, fast, precise things, mixture knob, throttle, carb heat — like she was playing an instrument only she could hear. Her face was calm in a way that scared me more than panic would have. Panic means there's still time to be scared. Calm at altitude over country with no airstrips for eighty miles means the person beside you has already done the math.

"Noor." Her voice came out level, almost gentle. "I need you to do something for me."

"What."

"Look out the window and tell me if you see water."

I looked. The valley floor below us was a braid of silver channels splitting and rejoining through spruce and muskeg, glittering like someone had dropped a necklace from the sky. Water everywhere. Too much of it, all of it moving, none of it straight.

"Lots," I said. "All of it looks angry."

"Good girl. That's the river braiding out before the gorge. It slows down down there. Gravel bars." She exhaled through her nose. "Here's what's going to happen. The engine is going to quit — maybe in thirty seconds, maybe in three minutes, I can't tell you which. When it does, I'm going to put us down on one of those bars. It'll be rough. It might hurt. Your job is to get your feet back on the floor, lean forward over your knees, cross your arms over your face, and stay like that until I say we're stopped. Can you do that?"

"Yes," I said, because the alternative was saying no, and no wasn't an option anymore.

"Then that's the whole job. Feet back, head down, arms crossed. Nothing else matters."

I did it. And here is the thing I will never tell anyone who wasn't in that seat: between the moment the engine finally gave up — a long, grinding silence, the sudden terrible loudness of wind against the struts — and the moment the wheels touched, I wasn't afraid. Not really. There was no room for it. Fear needs space to spread out in, and my whole world had contracted to the size of my own body: knees bent, arms crossed, breath held, waiting for the impact the way you wait for a wave to break over you when you've decided not to fight it.

What I felt instead was something colder and stranger. A kind of clarity. I thought about my grandfather — Elias Vane, the man whose map was zipped into the breast pocket of my jacket, the man I had refused to cry for at his funeral because crying would mean he'd mattered, and mattering was what he'd done to us by leaving. I thought about how he'd died shot and frostbitten on a ridge trail, trading his last possession to a stranger for a promise. And I thought: *if we live through this, I'm going to finish it.* Whatever he found. Whatever it cost him. Whatever it cost us.

The wheels hit.

It was worse than I imagined and better than I feared, which I've decided is the definition of surviving anything. The first touch was hard — a slam, a bounce, the world tilting — and then we were down among the stones and the plane was slewing sideways and everything was noise. Gravel sprayed up past the windows like thrown handfuls of rice. Something tore. We spun, or half-spun, the tail swinging around, and then there was a second impact, softer, deeper, the nose dropping as the wheels found softer ground or gave up entirely, and then—

Stopped.

Silence. Real silence, except the tick of hot metal and, somewhere behind me, my own breathing coming back online in ragged installments.

"We're down," Freya said. "We're stopped. You can look up now."

* * *

The Beaver was dying on a gravel bar in the middle of a braided river valley, and she was not going to fly again. I knew it the way you know a fish is dead even while its tail is still slapping — some knowledge arrives whole, without argument.

Her nose was buried to the prop hub in soft shingle. One wingtip was folded back like a broken finger. The left main gear had torn half off and lay under the fuselage at an angle that made my stomach hurt to look at. Fuel — what was left of it — was seeping slowly from a weeping seam near the wing root, smelling like money and bad decisions.

Freya stood next to it for a long time with her hand flat on the cowling, the way you'd rest your hand on a horse you were about to have put down.

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it more than I expected to. It was her plane. Planes are not machines to people like her; they're legs.

"Don't be." She said it briskly, already moving, pulling the cargo door open. "She got us down in one piece, both of us walking. That's all I ever ask of an airplane, and most of them don't manage it." Then, quieter, mostly to herself: "Engine's been telling me for a month. I kept telling her I couldn't hear it yet."

"You knew?"

"I suspected. There's a difference." She glanced at me, and there was something underneath the wry tone — guilt, maybe, or the shape of it. "If I'd known for certain, I'd have turned back. I want that on the record."

"It's on the record."

"Good." She hauled a duffel out onto the gravel. "Now help me strip her. Everything useful comes off this airplane in the next hour, because after that we're going to stop looking at her, and once we stop looking at her we'll start feeling sorry for ourselves, and self-pity out here is a luxury that kills."

So we stripped her.

I learned more about Freya Montrose in that hour than in all the days since she'd walked into the cannery office holding my grandfather's map like it was a live thing. I learned that she packed a plane the way other people pack a lie — everything in its place, nothing wasted, every strap and crate accounted for. I learned that she owned three changes of clothes and forty feet of good rope and considered the ratio correct. I learned that under the pilot's seat, wrapped in oilcloth, she carried a raft kit: two rubberized fabric bundles, folded tight, that bloomed into inflatable rafts when she worked the foot pumps with the grim efficiency of a woman inflating a grudge.

"Rafts," I said. "You carry rafts."

"I carry rafts. Everyone who flies this country carries rafts. The question isn't whether you go down — it's whether you go down somewhere you can walk out of." She knelt and cinched a valve cap. "This is not somewhere you walk out of. Upstream is the gorge. Downstream is sixty miles of braids and deadfall before the first cabin. So we don't walk. We float."

"Float where?"

"Downriver? No." She shook her head, and I saw the map in her mind the way I was starting to see it in mine — the greasecloth drawing, Vane's cramped annotations, the eleven-day window the calving season opened in the upper gorge. "Downstream takes us away from everything. The source is upstream. Which means the river that beat our engine—" she nodded at the chattering water beyond the bar "—is also the road. We go up. By water where we can, along the bank where we can't."

"Upstream. In rubber rafts. Against current."

"With paddles," she said, "and rope, and a certain amount of swearing. Yes."

I should have argued. Some part of me, the part raised on dock talk and disaster stories, wanted to argue — wanted to say *this is insane, turn around, build a signal fire, wait for someone to come find us.* But nobody was coming to find us. That was the part I kept circling back to as we worked. We had flown into country no plane was cleared to enter, chasing a map that a mining syndicate would burn towns to possess. If Calloway's people or Harkness's people found that wreck first, they wouldn't mount a rescue. They'd mount a search. And whatever they were searching for, we were carrying it.

There was only forward. I understood that now, standing knee-deep in glacial meltwater hauling a medicine crate toward the treeline, my boots soaked through and my hands going numb. Forward wasn't courage. Forward was just the only direction with air in it.

* * *

By late afternoon we had a camp of sorts tucked into the spruce at the top of the bar: the two rafts rolled and lashed as bedrolls-and-lifeboats both, the rope coiled, the rifle, the tarp, a battered pot, the food bag, and — sitting apart from everything else, because Freya insisted it sit apart — the medicine crate.

"The medicine crate," I said. "You're still dragging that thing."

"That crate is the reason I was flying this route at all. Insulin and antibiotics for the clinic at Kettle Landing. There's a woman there, Ada, diabetic, and a kid with a chest infection, and if those vials freeze or cook, the insulin dies." She said it simply, without drama, but I watched her check the crate's position relative to the fire pit the way other people check their wallet. "Cold night coming. I need to keep it from freezing without cooking it. Which means it sleeps between us, inside the shelter, not near the fire."

"So we're still delivering it."

Freya looked at me like I'd asked her if we were still breathing. "Of course we're still delivering it."

"Freya. The plane's dead. We're a hundred miles off any route. We're floating *upstream* toward a glacier with a syndicate behind us and a map in my jacket, and your concern is the insulin."

"My concern is the promise I made when I took the contract." She fed a branch into the small fire she'd allowed herself, shielded by a wall of green spruce boughs so the smoke wouldn't stand up and announce us. "Look — I know how this sounds. I know what it costs us, weight and worry and a few pounds of cargo. But I'll tell you the truth about myself, Noor, since we're apparently going to die together: I have spent my whole life leaving places. Every town I've ever landed in, I've treated like a parking spot. Take on fuel, deliver the goods, go. Never owe anybody, never stay anywhere long enough to be owed. It's a way of living that feels like freedom right up until you crash in the middle of nowhere and realize you have no one to call and nowhere to call them from."

I didn't say anything. The fire crackled. Somewhere across the river a wolf tested its voice and thought better of it.

"This crate is the first thing in years I've carried that somebody specific is waiting for," she went on. "Not a client. A person. Ada, with her insulin. So yes, the crate comes with us. Not because it's practical. Because if I abandon it, I become exactly what I've been running from being, and I'd rather drown."

That landed somewhere in me and stayed there. Because I recognized it — the shape of it, anyway. My grandfather had left us, and I had spent seventeen years building my whole self around that absence, around the decision that love was a thing men did with their backs turned. And here was this woman I'd known four days telling me that the entire difference between running away and carrying something was whether you kept holding it.

"Fine," I said. "The crate comes."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'm just noting that you're sentimental under all that competence, and someday I'm going to use it against you."

She laughed — actually laughed, short and surprised, the first real one I'd heard from her. "Duly noted."

* * *

Dark came down early through the spruce, the way it does in narrow valleys, like a lid closing. We ate cold beans and hardtack because Freya wouldn't risk a bigger fire, and we laid out the watch schedule — two hours each, she said, though I suspected she planned to cheat and take more than her share, and I resolved to catch her at it.

I took the first watch, sitting with the rifle across my knees at the edge of the trees, watching the gravel bar and the dead plane gleam faintly under a sky full of indifferent stars. The river ran past in the dark, making its endless arguments with the stones. Cold crept up through my boots. Behind me, Freya breathed slow and even, one hand resting on the medicine crate even in sleep, like a dog sleeping on a porch step.

And I sat there and let myself feel it — all of it, finally, the fear arriving late the way fear does when there's finally room for it. Fear of the water tomorrow. Fear of the men who might follow. Fear that the map in my jacket was leading us to nothing, that my grandfather's last act had been to hand a stranger a treasure map to his own regret. Fear, sharpest of all, that when we reached the source, whatever he'd found there would explain why he'd chosen it over us, and that the explanation would be worse than the mystery.

But sitting with it, in the dark, with the cold working into my bones and the river talking its one long sentence, something shifted. The fear didn't leave. It changed state. It condensed, the way fog condenses into water, into something denser and more usable.

Resolve, I guess you'd call it. Though that sounds too clean. It was more like anger that had finally found a job.

My grandfather had traded his map for a promise. Fine. I was the promise. I was what he'd bought with his last breath, and I decided, sitting there on watch in the spruce dark, that I was going to be worth the price. Whatever waited upstream — the gorge, the syndicate, the source, the truth about why Elias Vane had built dams instead of building a family — we were going to reach it, and I was going to look at it with my eyes open, and I was going to come back and say it out loud. To whoever needed to hear it. Even if what it said was ugly. *Especially* if what it said was ugly.

Near the end of my watch, I heard it — a sound I'd been half-listening for without admitting it. Far downstream, thin across the miles of braided water: engines. More than one. Low, steady, patient, the sound of boats working upstream against the current in the dark.

I woke Freya with a hand on her shoulder, careful, the way you wake someone when the news is bad.

She came up out of sleep instantly, fully, no grogginess at all, which told me everything about how many times she'd done this before. I pointed downstream. We listened together. The engines grew no closer and did not fade — holding station, maybe, or crawling, searchlights off, running dark.

"They're early," Freya whispered.

"How early?"

"Too early to have followed us. Which means they were already on the river." She was quiet a moment, and then she said, in a voice stripped of all its wryness: "Noor. First light, we're on the water. Everything loads tonight."

Everything loaded that night. And as I lay down beside the medicine crate with the engines murmuring far off in the dark like something digesting, I understood that the crash hadn't stranded us at all.

It had just made the choice obvious.
