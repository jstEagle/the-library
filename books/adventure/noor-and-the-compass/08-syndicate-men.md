## 8. Syndicate Men

The rain started an hour after the crash and didn't so much fall as settle in, the way a debt settles in — patiently, everywhere at once. By mid-afternoon Noor and I had the tarp strung between two spruce over what remained of my fuselage, and by late afternoon we had stopped pretending we were going to fix anything. The propeller was a modern sculpture. The engine had swallowed gravel the way Calloway swallowed promises: completely, and with no intention of giving any back.

"We could walk out," Noor said, for the fourth time.

"To where?"

"Anywhere that isn't here."

"That's not a place," I said. "I've checked."

She gave me the look she'd been giving me since the gravel bar, the one that said she'd already decided I was tolerable and was reserving judgment on whether I was useful. Seventeen-year-olds are excellent auditors. They don't extend credit.

We were salvaging when the boat came.

I heard it before I saw it — the low diesel burble of an outboard being run quiet, which is itself a statement. Nobody runs quiet because they're lost. Lost people holler. Quiet engines belong to men who want to see you before you see them.

"Company," I said, and put myself between the river and Noor, which was stupid, because a girl who'd grown up on cannery docks could read water better than I ever would, but some instincts don't consult the evidence.

The boat slid around the bend of the gravel bar: a gray inflatable with a rigid hull, the kind survey crews use, the kind that costs more than my plane's annual inspection. Three men in matching rain gear. A fourth at the stern, and he was the one my eyes went to and stayed on, the way your tongue goes to a cracked tooth.

He stepped ashore without asking permission, which told me everything before he said a word. He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, wearing a rain jacket that had never once been rained on in anger. His boots were dry. In this country, dry boots are either a miracle or a budget.

"Ladies," he said. "Terrible business." He looked at the wreck with the mild professional interest of a man appraising a horse he intended to buy cheap. "Mr. Harkness," he added, and offered his hand as though we'd been introduced by mutual friends.

I didn't take it. He let the hand hang there for a moment, unhurt, unbothered, then folded it away like a man putting a pen back in his pocket. That was my second piece of information. Men who need your handshake get flustered when it doesn't come. Men who don't need it just wait.

"Noor Vane," Harkness said pleasantly, turning to her. "And you'd be Freya Fairweather. The pilot." He pronounced *the* like it was a title. "Elias Vane's granddaughter. We've been looking for your grandfather, Miss Vane. Concerned parties."

"He's dead," Noor said flatly.

"Yes. So we understand. An accident, we're told." His voice did something small and terrible there — it stayed perfectly level while implying, gently, that accidents in this valley came with paperwork. "You have our sympathies. And our curiosity. A man doesn't walk into country like this without walking out of somewhere."

"He walked out of everywhere," Noor said. "That was his whole personality."

Harkness smiled. It was a good smile. It was the kind of smile they teach you in negotiation seminars, warm at the edges and load-bearing in the middle, and I watched him deploy it on her and watched her not flinch, and I thought: good. One of us has sense.

"And you, Miss Fairweather." He turned to me fully now, and I felt the weight shift, the way it does when a room's attention reorganizes itself around you. "Calloway Air Freight. You fly for Calloway."

"I fly for myself," I said. "Calloway just owns the paper."

"A distinction without much difference, from where I sit." He glanced at the wreck again. "Which is a shame about the plane. Calloway will want it written up. Insurance, salvage, all that. He'll want to know where you are, too. Men like Calloway always want to know where their assets are."

"I'm not an asset."

"Everyone's an asset," Harkness said, kindly, the way you'd tell a child the stove is hot. "Some are just poorly managed."

Noor made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter were capable of contempt.

Here's what I knew, standing in the rain with my hands empty and my plane dead behind me: Harkness hadn't found us by accident. My flight plan had died with the radio somewhere over the divide. Nobody knew where we went down except the river, the ravens, and whatever syndicate net covered this valley like a seine. Which meant they'd been watching the gorge already. Which meant Vane's map wasn't a rumor to them. It was a leak.

"You're a long way from anywhere, Mr. Harkness," I said.

"I'm exactly where I intend to be." He tipped his head toward the river. "We're running a survey. Mineral claims, hydrology, the usual. This valley's going to be very valuable in ten years, and we like to know our ground." He said *our* the way kings used to say *my*. "Now. You two are stranded, injured possibly, certainly wet, and eleven days' walk from the nearest thing resembling help. We have a boat, radios, food, dry socks." He paused, letting the inventory land. "We're heading downstream. You're welcome aboard."

Downstream. Toward the dam. Toward the reservoir. Toward whatever waited at the bottom of this valley with its lights off.

"It's generous," I said.

"It's practical," Harkness corrected. "Generosity implies sentiment. I'm told the weather's turning. Snow above two thousand feet by Thursday. The muskeg's already soft. Two women alone in bear country, in calving season, with no aircraft and no radio — well. I'd call it arithmetic, not charity."

He was right, and that was the trap of him. Everything he said was true. That's the mark of the genuinely dangerous ones; liars you can catch, but a man who tells you the truth in service of a lie is a different animal entirely. The ride downstream was real. The dry socks were real. And at the end of the ride was a room with a door that locked from the outside, and questions asked by men whose job was answers, and after that — after that, I suspected, was a river with two more bodies in it, because Elias Vane had died of a bullet and not of cold, and bullets in this valley had owners.

Noor was watching me. She couldn't know what I knew — I hadn't told her the full shape of it yet, the shot shoulder, the way Vane had gripped my wrist and said *they'll come, girl, they come quiet* — but she'd read the same man I had. Cannery docks teach you to size up buyers.

"We appreciate it," she said, sweet as syrup, "but we've got shelter, supplies off the plane, and my grandfather taught me this country. We'll manage."

"Your grandfather," Harkness said, "is dead in a snowbank with a hole in him. Forgive me if I don't rate his curriculum highly." Then, catching himself, the smile again, smoother: "But of course. Independence. It runs in families out here, I'm told." He reached into his jacket and produced a card — an actual card, laminated, absurd in the middle of the wilderness — and held it out to me. "If you change your mind. Or if you find anything. Documents, maps, personal effects of Mr. Vane's. Anything at all. There's compensation. Substantial compensation. Calloway holds paper on you, I believe? We buy paper."

There it was. Laid on the gravel bar like a lure, gleaming, barb hidden. He knew about the debt. Of course he knew about the debt. Calloway's ledger was probably photocopied in three offices by now.

"We'll keep walking," I said.

"Downstream?" he asked, and there was a flicker under the courtesy, quick as a fish turning.

"Up," Noor said.

Harkness looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face recalibrated — I watched him move her, mentally, from *incidental* to *relevant*, and I wished I could undo it. "Upstream's closed," he said mildly. "Calving season. The gorge floods. Ask any pilot."

"I'm asking one," Noor said, jerking her thumb at me.

"She crashed," Harkness observed.

"She landed," Noor said, "aggressively."

For a half-second — I'd swear to this later, under oath if it came to that — Harkness enjoyed it. There was a flicker of genuine amusement, a man appreciating craftsmanship even when it pointed at him. Then it was gone, filed away, and the courtesy resumed like a curtain drawn across a window.

"Well," he said. "The offer stands. We'll be on the river through the week. If you tire of the muskeg — or of each other — signal us. Any fire on the bank, we'll stop." He turned to go, then paused, and delivered the last of it almost gently, which was somehow worse than if he'd snarled. "Miss Fairweather. Your mother flew these valleys too, didn't she? Maren Fairweather. Lovely name. We have records. Companies always do."

The rain filled the silence where my answer should have gone.

He waited, patient as erosion, and when I gave him nothing he nodded as if I had, and stepped into the boat, and the engine woke, and the gray hull slid back around the bend and dissolved into the gray river and the gray day until I couldn't have sworn it had ever been there at all.

* * *

Noor didn't speak for a full minute. She stood with her arms crossed, watching the empty bend, and when she finally turned to me her jaw was set in a way I was starting to recognize as inherited.

"They knew Grandpa's name before we said it."

"They knew mine too."

"They knew your *mother's* name." She kicked a stone into the river, hard, unsatisfying. "Who talks like that? Who shows up in the middle of nowhere with laminated cards?"

"Men who've never once been lost," I said. "It's a specific pathology."

"This isn't funny."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. It's also not surprising. Vane told me they'd come. I didn't listen hard enough."

"What else did he tell you?"

I crouched by the wreck and started pulling the panel off the cargo bay, mostly so my hands had something honest to do. "That what he found at the source would burn the people upstream to the waterline. That he'd spent twenty years being sorry. That his daughter — your mother — shouldn't learn it from strangers." I glanced up. "And that they come quiet."

"So we go loud?"

"We go *now*," I said. "Tonight. Before they decide polite didn't work."

Noor looked at the sky, reading it the way I read instruments. "Rain till dark. Then it clears. Moon comes up around eleven."

"Then we move at ten and pray for cloud."

"With what? We've got a raft, forty feet of rope, a crate of antibiotics, and your charming personality."

"And a compass," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because her eyes went straight to my jacket pocket, and I remembered — vividly, the way you remember saying the wrong name at a wedding — that I hadn't told her about the compass either. About how it had spun near the valley mouth. About how it had pointed south, and then, impossibly, at her.

"The one that lies," she said. "Yeah. I've seen you checking it. It points at me sometimes. Want to explain that, or are we saving material for the rapids?"

"Saving it," I said. "Trust me, it keeps."

"Everything with you keeps," she muttered. But she let it go, which was its own kind of trust, and started breaking down camp with the brisk economy of someone who'd been leaving places her whole life too. Different docks, same motion. I recognized it and didn't say so. Some kinships you don't name out loud.

* * *

We loaded the raft in the last gray light: raft, rope, medicine crate wrapped in the tarp, the emergency kit I'd pulled from the wreckage — flares, a hatchet, a battered stove, three days of food I stretched in my head to five. I left the ELT beacon. That was a calculation. The beacon would bring rescue, and rescue would bring Harkness, and Harkness's version of rescue came with zip ties. Better to be missing than found wrong.

The compass sat in my pocket like a coal.

I took it out once, in the blue hour, while Noor cinched the last lashings. North was steady. North was fine. But when I angled it toward the upper valley — toward the source, toward whatever Vane had seen and my mother had stayed for — the needle swung, slow and certain, like a dog turning toward a whistle only it could hear. Pointing upriver. Pointing home, or pointing at the thing in me that wanted one.

"Put it away," Noor said quietly. She'd seen. Of course she'd seen. "Whatever it's doing, we navigate by water. Water doesn't lie."

"Water just drowns you honestly," I said, and pocketed it, and she almost smiled.

We launched at ten-forty under a lid of cloud, paddling the slack eddy along the true bank, away from the channel where a gray boat might run searchlights. The river talked to itself in the dark, that low gravel mutter, and every log we passed looked, for one bad second, like a boat. By midnight my shoulders were on fire and Noor was steering us through water she read like print, calling shifts in current before I felt them — *rock, left side, ferry right* — and I understood, not for the first time since the gravel bar, that the river had promoted her and I was crew.

We made six miles and hauled out on a shelf of gravel under alder, and I was so tired I lay down in the wet and considered it a bed.

That's when we heard it. Faint, downstream, threading up the valley through the rain-washed air: an outboard, running quiet, coming up.

They weren't waiting for us to signal. They were coming to collect.

Noor heard it too — I watched her sit bolt upright, dock-kid ears, twenty years sharper than mine. "One boat," she whispered. "Maybe two. Moving slow. Sweeping."

"They'll pass this bank within the hour."

"Then we're not on this bank within the hour." She was already rolling the tarp. No panic in her, no drama. Just that flat, furious competence, grief converted directly into motion. I thought of Vane dying on the ridge trail with frostbitten hands and a bullet in him, trading his last possession for a promise, and I thought: this is what he bought. Not the map. Her.

We pushed off into the dark and left our own campfire-less cold behind, and I won't pretend I wasn't afraid. I was afraid the way you're afraid in a dream where the floor keeps tilting. Behind us, men with radios and rifles and a laminated card with my name on it. Ahead of us, eleven days of gorge, a glacier deciding when to open the door, and a drowned town at the end of it with my mother's ghost — or worse, my mother — standing in the shallows.

Somewhere past two in the morning, running the raft through a shallow braided section with Noor poling and me hauling us over a gravel spine by main strength, my knee found the compass in my pocket, and I noticed the needle had stopped pretending.

It wasn't pointing north. It wasn't pointing at Noor.

It was pointing upstream, dead ahead, steady as a fact, into the black throat of the valley — and for the first time since the ridge trail, since Vane's blood freezing on the greasecloth, since Harkness said my mother's name in that pleasant, filing-cabinet voice, I felt something other than fear.

I felt summoned.

"Harkness thinks he's hunting us," Noor said, into the dark, not looking back at the sound of the engine below us.

"He is," I said. "He just hasn't figured out what's hunting *him*."

The river carried us north, and the compass carried us somewhere older than north, and behind us the searchlights swept banks we'd already given the slip — for tonight. Eleven days, Vane's map had promised. The glacier was counting. So, now, were they.
