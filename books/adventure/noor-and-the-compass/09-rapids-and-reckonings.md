## 9. Rapids and Reckonings

The gorge announces itself before you see it, the way a fight announces itself in a bar — a change in the air, a tightening somewhere behind your sternum. The river narrows from a lazy quarter-mile of braided gravel to a single green throat between walls of granite, and the sound comes up through the soles of my boots before it reaches my ears: not water anymore but machinery, something enormous and indifferent running day and night whether anyone lives or dies inside it.

Freya stands at the edge of the eddy where we've pulled the rafts to scout, binoculars up, saying nothing. That's how I know it's bad. Freya Montrose talks the way other people breathe — constant, ambient, mostly about cargo or weather or the moral failings of men named Calloway. Silence from her is like a lighthouse going dark.

"Well?" I say.

"Well," she says, lowering the binoculars with the deliberate calm of a woman deciding not to scream, "the good news is there's a line through it. The bad news is the line goes left of the fang, right of the hole, and if you miss either one, the river folds this raft around that rock like it's wrapping a present for nobody."

"Which rock is the fang?"

"The one that looks like it wants to eat us."

I look. There are about forty rocks that want to eat us. I pick the biggest one, the black snaggletooth in the middle of the chute with white water peeling off both sides of it like fur on a scared cat. "That one?"

"That's the one."

"Okay." I spit over the side, which Grandpa Vane taught me means the river knows you're serious. It might also just mean I have a habit I can't break. "I'll call the water. You row."

"You've been on a raft twice."

"I've been reading water since I was six, Freya. Cannery docks. Log booms, tide rips, the whole mess. You fly over rivers. I grew up underneath them, waiting for them to give back what they took." I don't say what they took. She doesn't ask. We're both carrying our dead quietly this morning, like stones in pockets we keep pretending aren't there.

She looks at me for a long second — the look she does when she's recalibrating her opinion of me, which happens maybe once a day and always surprises her — and then she says, "Fine. But if we die, I'm telling everyone it was your idea."

"It *was* my idea."

"I know. That's why it's funny."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about white water: it doesn't feel like falling. It feels like being *read*. The river takes the raft in its hands and turns it over, looking for flaws, and every flaw you have — every hesitation, every half-second of doubt — gets found and reported back to you at a volume you can't ignore.

The first mile is a blur I remember in pieces. Freya's shoulders working the oars like they owe her money. My own voice shouting "left! HARD left!" and hearing it come out of me in a register I didn't know I owned. Cold water hitting my face in fists. The walls of the gorge going by close enough to touch, striped with two hundred years of high-water marks, and me thinking, absurdly, *somebody should catalog those* — because apparently even mid-terror my brain wants to make lists.

Then the fang.

We come into the chute fast, faster than the scout made it look — everything looks slower from shore, the way fights look slower to people watching from outside the bar — and the hole below the fang has grown since yesterday. Snowmelt. A warm night up on the icefield, and now there's an extra foot of water in the river with nowhere polite to go. The tongue that was supposed to carry us left of the rock has shifted right. I see it half a second before Freya does, because I'm not rowing, I'm only looking, and looking is the whole job.

"RIGHT," I scream. "GO RIGHT, IT MOVED—"

She rows. God, she rows. Two strokes that would shame a regatta crew, and the raft slides right of the fang with maybe a paddle-blade's width to spare, and I have time to think *we did it* —

— which is exactly when the river decides we haven't suffered enough.

Because the move right puts us too wide for the exit channel, and the current grabs the bow and swings it toward the wall, and there's a seam of recirculating water along the granite there, a little eddy fence, nothing dramatic, except that when a raft's tube hits an eddy fence sideways at eight miles an hour, the current keeps pushing the boat while the fence holds the tube, and the boat starts to fold. Wrap, they call it. The river wraps your raft around the obstacle like a glove around a hand, and then it holds it there, thousands of pounds of pressure, forever, and you get to swim out and watch your entire expedition become garbage stapled to a rock.

The bow tube kisses the wall. Starts to bend.

I don't think. Thinking is for people with time. I do the thing I've done ten thousand times on the docks without ever calling it anything — read the force, find the direction it wants to go, and get ahead of it. There's a high-side maneuver; Freya probably knows it from some guide school, but knowing a thing and doing it in the half-second you have are different animals. I throw myself across the gear pile onto the folding tube, full weight, all five-foot-four and whatever the cannery has built onto me, and I start hauling the rope we'd tied as a flip line — slack loop, pull, the tube lifting, the water underneath it roaring like it's personally offended—

"NOOR—" Freya's voice, cracked open.

"PULL THE OARS, GET US OUT OF THE SEAM—"

And she does, and between her prying with the oars and me standing on the folding tube like an idiot surfing a dying whale, the bow pops free. The raft straightens. The river, having lost the argument, spits us into the pool below the rapids with a contemptuous little swirl, and we drift there in sudden, ringing silence, both of us breathing like the air owes us an apology.

My hands won't close properly. I look down at them — scraped, white-knuckled, shaking — and I start laughing. I can't help it. It comes out of me like water out of a cracked pipe.

Freya stares at me. Then she starts laughing too, that rusty-hinge laugh she almost never lets out, and for a full minute neither of us says anything true or useful, we just sit in the middle of a pool in a granite gorge laughing at the fact that we are alive and wet and stupid and fine.

"Okay," she finally manages. "New rule. You call the water, I row, nobody argues."

"Wasn't planning to argue."

"I argued with Calloway once. He won on points." She shakes her head, wrings out her braid. "That was — Noor, that was the best boat handling I've ever seen from someone who isn't paid to do it."

"Grandpa taught me knots. The docks taught me the rest." I say it lightly, and then, because the adrenaline has apparently dissolved whatever wall I usually keep that name behind, I hear myself add: "Before he stopped teaching me anything at all."

* * *

We camp that night on a bench of gravel above the river, under spruce so old and thick the sky comes down to a handful of stars. Freya builds a fire with the efficiency of someone who has slept alone in a lot of places, and I dry my socks, and the silence sits between us getting heavier, until I decide I'm tired of heavy.

"So," I say. "You going to tell me why a bush pilot who owes a man like Calloway money agrees to fly a stranger's granddaughter into a closed valley? Because I've done the math. This trip costs you more than the debt. Something else is paying you."

Freya feeds a stick to the fire and watches it catch. For a moment I think she'll deflect — she's good at deflecting, she could deflect a punch thrown at someone else — but the river took something out of her today, same as it took something out of me. Walls don't survive white water. They get rebuilt later, but tonight, for a few hours, they're down.

"Calloway holds paper on my plane," she says. "Not a loan, exactly. More like — you know how a pawnshop works? I put up the de Havilland three years ago when the medical contract went bust, and he's been kind enough to keep extending the terms in exchange for favors. Favors that keep getting less small." She turns the stick. "He found out about the map within a day. He always finds out. And when I told him no, he smiled at me in a way I haven't been able to shake. Like I wasn't a debtor. Like I was bait he'd already set out and was just waiting to see what came sniffing."

"So this trip is you running from him."

"This trip is me doing the one thing he asked me not to do. Which felt healthy." A crooked smile. "But that's not the real answer to your question. The real answer is the compass."

She digs it out of her jacket — brass, old, the glass scratched soft as sea ice — and holds it in the firelight without opening her hand all the way, the way you hold something that might still hurt you.

"My mother's. Maren Fairweather. She flew, too — mail runs, medevacs, the kind of flying nobody thanks you for. When I was nine she flew into the interior and never came out. No wreckage. No mayday. Nothing. Officially she's a missing pilot. Unofficially—" she shrugs, and the shrug costs her something, I can see it, "—unofficially everyone decided she ran off, because that's easier than the alternative, and because my mother was the kind of woman people liked to believe things about."

"And the compass?"

"Wouldn't stop pointing south after she vanished. South, away from everything. I kept it anyway. Pilots are superstitious idiots; we keep broken instruments like other people keep photographs." Her thumb moves over the case, unconscious, the way mine moves over my grandfather's knife. "Two days ago, near the valley mouth, it started spinning. And then—" she stops. Restarts. "It pointed at you. At your chest. Steady as a church."

I stare at her. "At *me*."

"At you. Which is impossible, and also which happened." She closes her fist around it. "So when you stood on that dock hating your grandfather, and I thought about a girl who might know something I need to know, and a valley I've been orbiting my whole life without meaning to — yeah. The math worked out different than it looked."

I turn that over. It should frighten me, and part of it does, but mostly what I feel is a strange, unearned relief — the relief of learning your weirdness has company. "Your mother disappeared into the same country my grandfather was sneaking into for thirty years," I say slowly. "Small river."

"Rivers are never small," Freya says. "They just run underground for a while."

I feed the fire. Might as well pay for my ticket. "Elias Vane left when I was four," I say. "Mom wouldn't talk about him. Just — 'he found something upriver and it mattered more than we did.' Every couple years a package would come. Caribou jerky, a fishing lure, once a book on glaciology with notes in the margins like a madman's diary. Mom burned the last one unopened. I fished it out of the stove. Half of it survived." I look into the flames. "I told you I refused to mourn him. That was true right up until Freya Montrose landed at the cannery dock with his map and a bullet-hole story. Now I don't know what I'm doing. Grief for a man who chose a river over me is a stupid emotion and I'm having it anyway."

"He didn't choose the river," Freya says quietly. "Whatever he found, it scared him enough to spend thirty years keeping it secret and one night bleeding out trying to pass it on. Men like that don't leave because they love something else more. They leave because they think staying will get the people they love killed." She pauses. "Doesn't make it okay. Doesn't even make it forgivable. But it makes it *explicable*, and explicable is where forgiveness usually starts, if it starts anywhere."

"When did you get wise?"

"Somewhere over the years between crashes." She grins. "Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation as a flake."

I laugh, and then the laugh catches on something, and I hear myself say the thing I've been circling since the dock: "I used to dream he'd come back. Standing on the dock, every floatplane, every season, I'd check the registrations. Fourteen years of checking registrations, Freya. And then you showed up instead, holding his map, and I got so angry I couldn't see straight — because you were the closest thing to him I'd ever gotten, and you weren't even him."

"Noor—"

"But here's the thing." I pull my knees up, look at her across the fire. "You came. He never came. Whatever your reasons — the compass, the debt, the running — you pointed your plane at a seventeen-year-old cannery worker and said *your grandfather wanted you to know*, and then you let me come along, and today you trusted me with both our lives in that gorge. So." I stick my hand out, palm up, the way you settle things on the docks. "Partners. Not passengers. Partners."

She looks at my hand for a second like it's another piece of moving water she needs to read. Then she takes it. Her grip is pilot-calloused and warm.

"Partners," she says. "Fair warning: I snore, I hoard chocolate, and I lie about fuel reserves."

"Lie about them how?"

"Optimistically."

* * *

I wake once in the grey before dawn, cold seeping up through my sleeping bag, and for a moment I can't remember where the fire went. I sit up. Freya is awake too, sitting cross-legged, and she's holding the compass flat on her palm, and the needle isn't pointing south.

It's pointing upstream. Up the side channel, past the mouth of the gorge, into the dark where the walls pinch together and the water goes quiet in a way that quiet water in this country shouldn't.

"Freya," I whisper. "What's it doing?"

She doesn't answer for a long moment. When she does, her voice is steady, but her eyes aren't.

"It's pointing at something," she says. "And I think it's pointing at my mother."
