## 10. What the Compass Wants

The compass had been lying to me for three days, and I'd been letting it, because a compass that lies is at least company.

It sat in my shirt pocket where my mother's had sat, or where I imagined my mother's had sat, since the only person who could confirm the habit had walked out of a rented cabin in Fort Nelson in 1979 and left a compass, a hairbrush, and a note that said *gone to see a thing, back soon*. Back soon. Twenty years and counting. If punctuality was hereditary, I was doomed too.

"Your needle's doing the thing again," Noor said. She was kneeling at the gravel bar, cinching the raft's lashings with the particular violence of a teenager who has decided that if she can't strangle her grief, she'll strangle a knot instead.

"It's doing a lot of things lately."

"It's pointing at you."

I looked down. The needle, which had spent the morning sulking toward the southwest — away from the river, away from the source, away from every sensible heading — had swung a slow quarter turn and come to rest against the glass, quivering, aimed at my sternum like a tiny brass accusation.

"That's a malfunction," I said.

"That's a choice," Noor said. She stood, shouldered her pack, and looked up the valley the way she'd been looking up valleys since we lost the plane: like the country owed her something and she intended to collect. "Which way?"

I should have said downstream. The source was upstream, the gorge was upstream, the eleven days were bleeding out of our allotment like a punctured fuel line, and everything Elias Vane had died for lay somewhere above the next bend. But the needle was pointing south now, off my chest and into a side channel so narrow the spruce leaned over it like conspirators, and I felt the pull in my own chest, a hook behind the breastbone, and I heard myself say:

"That way."

Noor looked at me. Seventeen years old, frost-nipped, furious, and sharper than any instrument I'd ever carried in a cockpit. "The compass says that way."

"The compass says that way."

"The compass has been wrong since the valley mouth."

"I know."

"And you're going to follow it."

"I'm going to follow it." I heard how it sounded. I heard how it *was*. "Noor, when we get to the source, there's going to be a thing Elias found, and there's going to be syndicate men behind us, and there's going to be maybe four days of open water left, and I am standing here telling you we're taking a detour because a dead woman's compass is pointing at a creek. You have every right to—"

"Shut up," she said, and pushed the raft into the water. "I came for the truth. If the truth's up a creek, we go up the creek."

I have flown with bush pilots who could read a glacier's mood from the sound it made at three in the morning, and I have never met anyone who read people the way that girl read me. She didn't forgive the detour. She simply declined to make it a question.

* * *

The side channel wasn't on Vane's map. I checked twice, holding the greasecloth wrapping up to the grey light while Noor held the raft against the current with one arm and an expression of infinite patience, which on her meant she wanted to hit me. The channel split off the main river through a screen of alder, and where it split, the water ran clear — glacial flour downstream, but here, cold and clean as glass, the color of the sky before weather.

The needle didn't waver. It pointed up the channel the way a dog points, rigid with intent, and the closer we pushed, the more I felt the hook behind my breastbone tighten. I want to be careful how I describe this, because I'm a rational woman. I fuel by weight. I calculate crosswinds. I do not believe that objects want things.

But the compass wanted.

It wanted the way a wound wants pressure. It wanted the way an empty house wants someone to come home. Every meter upstream the pull got heavier, and by the time the channel shallowed to shin-depth and we had to drag the raft, my hands were shaking, and Noor kept looking at me sideways and not asking, because she was learning my tells the way I was learning hers, and my tell was silence.

"Whose was it?" she asked finally, when we stopped to breathe among the alders.

"My mother's."

"You said she left."

"She left." I wound the lashing rope around my fist, unwound it. "Left a note. Left the compass. Left me with a woman named Dot who ran a floatplane base and believed children should be seen at mealtimes and useful otherwise." I shrugged, the shrug I'd been doing for twenty years, the shrug that had worn a groove in my shoulders. "I kept the compass because it was the only thing she left that pointed anywhere."

"And now it points at you."

"And now it points at me."

Noor was quiet for a while, hauling the raft over a submerged log with the economy of someone who'd been working docks since she was ten. Then: "My grandfather left too. Everyone acts like it's a mystery, why people leave. It's not a mystery. People leave because staying costs more than they can pay." She looked at me. "The question is what they were paying *for*."

I didn't have an answer. The compass did. It dragged us on.

* * *

Near noon the channel opened into a hanging valley, a bowl of green held in the arms of the ridge, and the pull became so strong I stopped pretending I was leading. The needle pointed to a shelf of rock above the outflow, where a cairn stood against the sky — a man-high stack of flat stones, lichened grey, old enough that two of the lower stones had sunk into the moss like teeth into gum.

Somebody had built that cairn by hand. Somebody had carried each stone.

I climbed to it with the compass in my fist and my heart going like an oversped engine, and Noor watched from below, and I put my hand on the top stone, and the needle spun once, twice, and lay still — pointing down, into the cairn, into the heart of it.

"Freya," Noor called. "What is it?"

"A cairn," I said. My voice came out strange. "Trappers build them. Surveyors. It doesn't mean—"

It meant. I knew it meant, the way you know a runway is too short before you've done the arithmetic. I started pulling stones.

The top ones came away easy. The lower ones had been set with care, mortared with moss and time, and I dug them out with my fingers going numb, and Noor climbed up and dug beside me without a word, and near the center, wrapped in oilcloth inside a tin that had rusted lace-thin, we found a journal page.

One page. Folded twice. The handwriting slanted left, quick and sure, the handwriting I had spent twenty years trying to remember and could only ever reconstruct in fragments — the way the *f* crossed itself, the way the *r* ran into the next letter like a person in a hurry to say something.

I sat down on the cold rock and unfolded it and read my mother's words for the first time since I was nine years old.

* * *

*If you have found this, then the compass has done its work, and I am sorry, and I am glad, and I don't know how to be both at once.*

*They will tell you it's a compass. It isn't. It was made here — in this valley, by a man named Oskar Lindqvist, who machined it from dam-site steel in the winter of '31, after the town burned. He made three. One he kept. One he gave to a woman who lost her sons in the flood. One he gave away to a stranger passing through, because he said grief should travel, it does more good moving than sitting.*

*It does not point north. It never has. It points to what you've lost. That is the whole of its mechanism and the whole of its cruelty. Oskar said he built it as a joke, then spent the rest of his life unable to laugh. The needle finds the heaviest thing in a person — the thing they carry — and it pulls.*

*If it brought you here, then either you have lost something in this valley, or you are carrying something that belongs here. Both, probably. That's how it worked on me.*

*I stayed. You will want to know why, and I can't tell you on this page, because this page is only the hook, and the fish hasn't been caught yet. Come to the source. Come to the water. I left the rest where the river keeps its records.*

*And Freya — if it's you, if it's my girl with the compass in her pocket — I did not leave because of you. I left because of me. That is not an excuse. It is only the truth, and the truth is the only thing I have ever had enough of.*

*The needle points at what you've lost. But what you've lost is not always gone.*

*M.*

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. The light moved. The river talked its one long sentence. Somewhere below us a dipper worked the shallows, bobbing like a small brown thought.

Noor read it over my shoulder. I felt her breath stop when she got to the signature, and I felt her look at me, and I could not look back. I was looking at the word *Freya* in my mother's hand, and the word was doing something to me that twenty years of Dot's casseroles and a thousand nights in a hundred bunkhouses had not managed: it was making the rootlessness hurt. All those years I'd told myself I didn't stay anywhere because I liked it that way. The truth was I had never once landed anywhere that my mother's leaving hadn't already cratered, and I'd been flying circles around the crater my whole life, calling the circling freedom.

"She's alive," Noor said.

"Don't."

"The page says—"

"It says a lot of things. It says a dead man built a compass that points at grief, Noor. It says my mother machined steel in 1931, which would make her a hundred and ten. People write things in journals. People write *back soon* on kitchen tables." My voice cracked on the last word and I hated it and kept going. "I have built my whole life on not hoping. It's the only load-bearing wall I've got. You don't get to come along on a raft trip and knock it out."

Noor took the page out of my hands — gently, which was somehow worse — and read it again, top to bottom, the way she read water, looking for the rocks under the surface.

"She knew you'd come," she said. "She wrote to you. Twenty years ago she wrote to you and she put it in a cairn and she trusted the compass to bring you, and it did." She held the page out. "That's not a load-bearing wall, Freya. That's a door."

"The compass points at what you've lost," I said. "It's pointing at me. It's been pointing at me since the valley mouth. What does that make me?"

Noor didn't flinch. "It makes you the thing somebody lost," she said. "Congratulations. Now you know how the rest of us feel."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and I let it, because there was no one up there to see a celebrated novelist and competent bush pilot come apart on a rock shelf, only a seventeen-year-old girl who had buried a grandfather that morning in her heart and was now watching his map's courier fall apart over a dead woman's handwriting. She put her hand on my shoulder. It was a dockworker's hand, rope-hard, and it stayed.

"The source," she said. "She said come to the source. Where the river keeps its records." She looked up the valley, where the glacier showed its blue teeth between the ridges, and her jaw set the way it set when she'd already decided. "Four days of open water. Maybe less."

"Maybe less," I agreed. I folded the page along my mother's folds and put it in the tin and put the tin inside my jacket, over my heart, where the compass had been, because the compass was in my hand now and the needle had gone strange again — not pointing south, not pointing at me, but up-valley, steady as a runway light, at the source.

"Freya." Noor's voice had changed. I looked up.

She wasn't looking at the compass. She was looking down the channel, back the way we'd come, where the alders stood thin against the grey water.

"There's a boat," she said. "Motor. Two men. They just turned into the side channel."

The needle quivered. The pull in my chest, the hook, the wanting — it all went suddenly quiet, the way the air goes quiet before a squall, and I understood that hope and danger had arrived in the same hour, riding the same current, the way they always do in this country.

"Raft," I said. "Now. Water."

Noor was already moving.

We hit the current with the cairn at our backs and my mother's page against my heart and the compass dragging us forward into the last four days of the open gorge — and behind us, patient as a debt, the sound of an outboard engine filled the narrow channel, and began to gain.
