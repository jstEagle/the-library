## 11. Harkness Closes In

I used to think fog was just weather. Now I know it's a negotiation — the river offering to hide you, and you deciding whether to trust a thing that has already tried to kill you twice today.

We'd put in below the second gorge at first light, Freya on the oars because she claimed her arms were longer and I claimed that was the only lie she'd told me all trip. The morning came up gray and close, mist sitting on the water like it had paid rent. Somewhere behind us — and I want to be clear that "behind" is a word I had stopped believing in — were two boats with outboard motors and men who said *good morning* like a threat.

It started as a sound. That's always how it starts. Not a sight, not a shape, just a sound under the river's noise: a low, even thrumming, patient as a heartbeat. Freya's head tilted the way a dog's does.

"Tell me that's a waterfall," she said.

"It's not a waterfall," I said. "Waterfalls don't idle."

She turned us toward the north bank without saying anything else, which was its own kind of conversation. We slid into the shallows where the spruce crowded down to drink, and I watched the fog through the trees while she shipped the oars and went quiet in the way she goes quiet — completely, like someone turned off at the switch, then suddenly loud again when it matters.

The boats came around the bend maybe four minutes later. Two of them, aluminum, gray as the water, each carrying three men in rain gear that cost more than my grandfather's entire estate, which was a rifle and a grudge. They weren't searching. Searching has a rhythm to it — sweep, pause, sweep. These boats moved like they knew something. Like they were reading our wake.

"They're following our line," Freya murmured. "Downstream side of the current seam. That's ours."

"That's *our* ours?"

"That's the line I chose yesterday because it doesn't wrap you on the rocks. Nobody picks that line by accident." She looked at me with an expression I can only describe as professional offense. "Somebody's reading water the way I read water."

"Is that bad?"

"Noor, there are maybe six people in this territory who read water like that, and one of them is rowing this raft."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Mr. Harkness by then, which was more than I wanted: he was courteous the way ice is courteous. He'd found our crash site on day one, helped us right the medicine crate, offered us a ride downstream in a voice like warm coffee, and meant every word of it except the ones that mattered. When we slipped out of his camp that night through the muskeg — me up to my ribs in bog water, Freya whispering coordinates like prayers — he hadn't chased. He'd smiled. I could feel the smile from two hundred yards away, the way you feel weather change.

Now the smile had motors.

"We can't outrun them," Freya said. "Outboards do twelve knots. We do whatever the river feels like doing, which today is six."

"So we don't run."

"So we don't run," she agreed, and there was something almost cheerful in it, which frightened me more than the boats did. "We vanish. Fog's thick enough to hide a mountain. We go to the bank, we drag the raft into the trees, and we let them chase the river for a while."

"You've done this before."

"I've been chased before," she said. "Different reasons. Same math."

We dragged the raft up a gravel shelf and into the spruce shadow, flipped it, and lay under it like children hiding from a game that wasn't a game. The medicine crate went deeper in, wedged between roots. Freya pulled the compass from her jacket and held it flat on her palm, and I watched the needle swing — south, east, at me, back south — like it was nervous too.

"It's never done that near people before," she said quietly.

"Maybe it knows them."

Freya looked at me sideways. "You're getting creepy. Good. It'll keep you alive."

* * *

The boats slowed across from our bank. Voices carried over the water, flattened by fog so they seemed to come from everywhere, which is a trick fog plays and I hated it personally. A man's voice, unhurried, educated: Harkness himself.

"—know they're in this reach. The current seam, the gravel bars. Ms. Vane flies like she rows; she'll have taken the inside line. Fan out at the next fork."

They knew my name. Not Freya's — mine. *Ms. Vane.* My grandfather's name in a stranger's mouth, spoken like inventory. Something went hot and cold in me at the same time, which I'm told is impossible, but grief does impossible things to your plumbing.

They beached. Boots on gravel, radios crackling little wet syllables. Six men spread along a bank we were lying twenty feet from, separated by spruce trunks and fog and the fact that neither of us had breathed in what felt like a fiscal quarter.

A flashlight beam walked through the trees. It crossed the overturned hull of our raft — I watched the light touch the wood, slide along the keel, catch on a rope end — and kept going, because the fog was doing its job and the forest is full of things shaped like rafts if you're in a hurry.

Then, closer than anything should have been, a voice directly above us: "Tracks here. Fresh. Going upslope."

And another voice, dry as paper: "Those are bear tracks, Ostrander. Four days old at least."

Pause.

"How can you tell?"

"Because I've been walking behind bears for thirty years and behind idiots for forty. Come on."

I will spend the rest of my life wondering whether that man saw us and didn't care, or didn't see us at all, or was the kind of person who lies beautifully just to watch what happens. Freya's hand found my wrist under the hull and squeezed once: *wait*. Her pulse against my skin was slow. Mine was trying to exit through my throat.

The boots faded. The boats left — or pretended to leave, engines fading downstream in a way that sounded rehearsed.

"He'll leave a watcher," Freya whispered after a long time. "He's too good not to."

"Then what do we do?"

"We wait until dark, and then we do the stupidest brave thing available."

* * *

We waited eleven hours under that hull. I want that recorded somewhere official. Eleven hours of cold seeping up through gravel, of sharing half a strip of dried fish and pretending it was dinner, of whispering plans and abandoning them, of listening to the river run its endless commentary. At some point Freya told me about her mother — not the whole story, just the edge of it, the way you show someone the border of a country you're not ready to enter. A compass that pointed wrong. A woman who flew north one spring and became a rumor.

"She'd have liked you," Freya said. "Stubborn. Bad at staying safe."

"I'm excellent at staying safe. I'm just bad at being talked out of things."

"Same thing, wearing different coats."

At full dark we moved. And here is where I confess the stupidest brave thing found us faster than we found it, because the stupidest brave thing was a hole in a hillside, breathing.

We smelled it before we saw it — musk and old fish and animal certainty. Freya froze, and I nearly walked into her spine. Ahead, maybe fifteen feet up the bank, a black opening in the root mass of a fallen spruce, wide as a doorway, deep as a bad decision.

"Bear den," she breathed.

"Winter's over. It might be empty."

"It might also contain a bear."

"Yes," I said, "but the syndicate definitely contains six men with rifles and a man who says my name like a receipt. I know which room I'd rather knock on."

Freya stared at me. In the dark I couldn't see her face, but I heard her exhale through her nose, the laugh she keeps in prison.

"You're going to get us killed."

"Noted. Get us killed *quietly*."

* * *

The den was empty. Or mostly empty — it had the archaeology of a bear's winter: compressed grass, a hollow worn smooth, a smell that reorganized your priorities. We crawled in past the entrance turn, which exists specifically so that anything coming in has to announce itself, and settled into the dark with the raft rope tied to my ankle and Freya's knife open on her knee.

Outside, the fog thickened until the world ended three feet from the door. And then the searchlights came.

I had imagined searchlights my whole life from movies, and the movies lied. Real searchlights aren't dramatic white blades. They're soft, wandering, almost gentle — a pale bloom sliding through fog, touching the spruce, sliding off, returning. They made the forest look like the bottom of the sea. They made our den look like nothing at all, and then, worse, they made it look like exactly what it was, and I pressed myself into the bear-shaped hollow and thought: *if a beam catches the entrance, we are two people in a hole with nowhere to be.*

Voices again, closer now, on the bank above us. Radios. Once, footsteps directly overhead, gravel crunching in a rhythm I memorized despite myself, because I decided that if I died I wanted to die knowing the exact beat of the song.

"River's empty to the fork," someone called.

"Keep the fork covered. They'll come to us. People always come to water."

Harkness's voice, farther off, unhurried, terrible: "No fires tonight. If they're on this bank, cold will do half our work. Patience, gentlemen. The river delivers everything eventually. That's rather the point of rivers."

I lay in the dark and did arithmetic instead of screaming. Eleven days of calving season. This was night five. Six nights left before the gorge sealed and the syndicate could stop hunting because geography would do their killing for them. Six nights, one holed raft — because yes, during the drag-up through the alder, before the boats appeared, we'd torn a gash in the starboard tube that Freya had patched with tape and prayer and I had agreed to forget about until morning — and one ledger of names waiting upstream in a drowned town, and one woman up there keeping it, though I didn't know yet whose mother she was. I knew only that Elias Vane had sent us. That the name *Vane* was worth motors and searchlights and patience measured in nights.

That was the moment the terror turned over. I felt it happen, physically, like a boat coming about. Fear is just information arriving too fast. Somewhere between the fourth hour in the den and the fifth, the information finished arriving, and what was left underneath it wasn't fear anymore. It was direction.

They wanted us gone. Therefore we mattered. Therefore the old man had been right, and the town was real, and the names were real, and every hour those men spent sweeping a fogbank for two women in a bear den was an hour they weren't spending erasing anything else.

"Freya," I whispered, when the lights had swept away downstream and the bank above us had gone quiet enough to hear the frost forming.

"Yeah."

"When we get out of this, I'm going to learn to fly."

A long pause. Then, dry as the old tracker's voice: "That's the fever talking."

"It's not."

"It's always the fever talking. Go to sleep, Vane. We move at first fog."

* * *

First fog came late and stayed all day, which was the best news of the trip so far. We eased out of the den at gray dawn, stiff as folded cardboard, and Freya examined the raft patch with the expression of a surgeon reviewing a resident's stitches.

"This holds until the next big water. Maybe."

"Maybe is a word I've grown fond of."

"Don't. Maybe is how planes end up in rivers." She ran her thumb along the gash. "We need real patch material. There was a cache — Vane's cache, he marked it on the map, a day's walk up the side channel. Sealant, spare fabric, food in tins."

"The map he gave you."

"The map he gave me." She looked at me over the raft, and something passed between us that had weight and edges. His map. My grandfather's handwriting, which I had never seen until three days ago and now recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall. "We're a day off the main channel. Off the seam they're watching. It costs us time we may not have."

"Or it buys us a raft that floats."

"Or that." She rolled the map open against her knee, and the compass sat on top of it, needle swinging its slow wrong arcs, pointing not north but — I followed it, really followed it for the first time — up the side channel. Toward the cache. Toward whatever else was up there, waiting in the fog, patient as a heartbeat.

"Your grandfather," Freya said slowly, "marked that cache beside a cairn."

"So?"

"So the compass has been pulling toward that side channel since the valley mouth. I thought it was broken." She looked up, and for the first time since the crash, since the boats, since the den, Freya Fairweather looked genuinely afraid — not of the men, not of the river, but of a small brass instrument sitting on a greasecloth map. "It isn't broken, Noor. It's navigating."

Toward what?

Toward what, I asked myself, all through that gray morning as we dragged the patched raft up the side channel, walking it through shallows, hauling over gravel, the fog closing behind us like a curtain drawn by careful hands. Behind us, faint and getting fainter, the outboards idled at the fork, guarding water we no longer needed.

Ahead, the compass pointed, steady now, certain, like it had finally gotten permission to say what it had been trying to say for twenty years.
