## 12. The Drowned Town

The river changed its mind about a mile before we saw why.

For two days it had been all business — gravel bars, deadfall, the flat gray hurry of water that knows where it's going. Then, just past a bend where a slide cone ran down to the bank like spilled sugar, the current slowed. Slowed, then softened, then went glassy and green and utterly indifferent to us, as if we'd paddled out of a river and into a held breath.

Noor noticed it before I did. She always noticed water first; I noticed engines and exits. She let her paddle rest across the gunwales and said, "It stopped."

"Rivers don't stop."

"This one did." She dipped two fingers over the side. "Cold underneath. Warm on top. That's lake water. That's *deep* lake water."

She was right, which was becoming a habit I'd have to break her of if we both intended to survive this trip with our egos intact. The valley had opened without our noticing — the walls stepping back, the spruce giving way to pale meadows of fireweed growing straight out of drowned ground — and ahead of us the water widened into something that had no business being here. A reservoir. Behind it, maybe four miles as the raven flew, a concrete wall sat across the gorge like a scar that hadn't decided how to heal.

The syndicate's dam. We'd been hearing about it for days the way you hear about a bad tooth — Harkness's survey boats, the no-entry signs Vane's map had warned us about, the helicopter that had passed overhead twice yesterday, close enough to read the registration through my binoculars. We'd been running toward the thing everyone told us to avoid, because the thing everyone tells you to avoid is usually the thing worth knowing about.

But neither of us had expected the rooftops.

They started small. A chimney, standing alone out of the water like a man up to his neck. Then a ridge line, blackened timber, the gable end of a house with its window frames still square and empty. Then another. And another. An entire street of them, running down into the green water at an angle, as if the town had lain down to sleep and the water had come up over it gently, without waking anyone.

Noor stood up in the raft. I told her to sit down. She didn't, which was also becoming a habit.

"What is that?" she said. "What *is* that?"

I didn't answer, because I was doing the thing I do instead of answering, which is looking. And what I was looking at, once my eyes sorted themselves out, was a town. Not ruins — towns don't ruin underwater, they just wait. A church steeple, leaning. A water tower on legs, rusted to lace. The main street's lampposts, still in a row, their heads just breaking the surface so that on a calm morning they'd look like a line of men fishing. Fences running off into deep water, following property lines nobody had surveyed in decades. A schoolhouse, I'd have sworn, judging by the bell tower and the flagpole stub beside it.

A whole town. Bought, burned, and drowned.

"Noor," I said quietly, "sit down and paddle."

She sat down and paddled. She was furious about it, but she did it, and I've known grown men with thirty years of bush time who couldn't manage both halves of that sentence.

* * *

We made for the high ground on the north shore, where an old road cut — you could still see the grade of it, climbing away from the waterline like a stairway to nowhere — and pulled the raft up into the alder. I don't know what I expected to feel. Something clean, maybe. Outrage is a tidy emotion when it's somebody else's story. You can carry it around like a stone in your pocket and take it out and look at it whenever you want to feel righteous.

Standing on that shore, I felt none of it. What I felt was colder and more personal: recognition.

Because I grew up in towns like this one. Not drowned — abandoned, which is drowning by other means. Company towns. My mother hauled me through six of them before she hauled me through none of them, and every one had the same architecture: the mill or the mine at the center, the company store, the rows of identical houses rented at prices calculated to keep you exactly broke enough to stay. And every one of them died the same way, too — not when the ore ran out, but when the men who owned the ore decided the people were costing more than the people were worth.

So when I looked at those rooftops under the green water, I wasn't looking at a stranger's crime. I was looking at the family business.

We walked the old road grade up onto the bench above the waterline, and there the town came back to life in the worst way. Foundations. Fence posts. A garden gone to fireweed and wild raspberry, still fenced, still planted in rows — someone had meant to come back. A child's swing set, the chains fused with rust, the seats long gone. And everywhere, everywhere, burn scars. Not wildfire burn — wildfire doesn't burn in rectangles. House foundations ringed with char that had soaked into the soil twenty years deep, so that each homesite lay inside its own black halo, like a candle burned down to the plate.

Every house. Burned. Every single one.

"They burned it," Noor said. It wasn't a question. She'd stopped asking questions somewhere back along the shore and started arriving at conclusions, and I found I preferred her conclusions to most people's questions. "Before the water. They burned the town and then they flooded it."

"Flooding hides the burning," I said. "Fire leaves ash. Ash can be dated, sampled, mapped. Water just — covers. Twenty years of green water and silt, and there's nothing left to test." I crouched at the edge of one of the halos, turned over a chunk of fused glass that had been a window, I supposed, in a life. "Except they made a mistake."

"What mistake?"

"They left the steeple up." I pointed. Above the waterline, on a knoll the engineers had apparently judged not worth blasting, the church still stood — roofless, but standing, its frame silvered and sound. "Sentiment, probably. Or the foreman liked the way it looked. Either way, it's the one building in this valley that got to keep its memory."

We climbed to it. Of course we climbed to it. You can put a town underwater, but you cannot stop people from walking uphill toward a church; it's bred into the species deeper than fear.

Inside, under the open sky where the roof used to be, the floorboards were rotted through in patterns like lace. Grass grew in the aisles. And on the back wall, protected from the weather by the one wall that still stood whole, someone had written names.

Dozens of them. Chalk, mostly faded to ghosts; some re-traced in charcoal by hands that came later, pilgrims maybe, survivors' children, people who remembered. Names, and beside some of them dates, and beside a few of them — the ones that stopped my breath — small annotations in a careful, schoolteacher's script:

*Elias Vane, witness.*

Noor made a sound I'd never heard from her. Not a word. Something below words, in the register where grief keeps its workshop.

"That's him," she said. "That's his name. Why is his name on a church wall in a drowned town?"

I touched the chalk ghost of it. Elias Vane. The dying man on the ridge trail, frostbitten and shot, who'd traded me a greasecloth-wrapped map for a promise about his granddaughter. I'd spent eleven days assuming he was a prospector — a greedy old fool chasing color in forbidden country, shot by a claim jumper, done in by his own obsession. That story had fit neatly in my head, the way stories do when you haven't looked too hard at them.

Prospectors don't get their names chalked on church walls.

Witnesses do.

* * *

Here's what I knew, assembled from pieces, the way you assemble anything out here — badly, at dusk, with cold hands.

Vane's map hadn't marked gold. I'd studied it enough times to have it memorized, and there were no claims staked on it, no X marks, none of the feverish notation of a man who thinks he's rich. What it marked was a route. A route to the source, yes — but annotated, in Vane's cramped hand, with things no prospector bothers noting: *old road, passable to km 9.* *Church knoll, high ground.* *Graveyard above flood line.* He'd mapped the town. He'd mapped it the way you map something you're afraid will be erased — every landmark, every refuge, every place a record could survive.

And he'd been shot. Not by claim jumpers — there was nothing to jump. Shot by someone who wanted him quiet, and had failed, and would presumably try again, because the syndicate doesn't hire quitters. Harkness's crew hadn't been chasing us, back in the muskeg. Or rather — they'd been chasing us, but we were collateral. They were chasing the route Vane had kept alive for twenty years, the thread that led to whatever record existed of what happened here.

What happened here. I stood in the church and made myself say it plainly, because euphemism is how crimes like this win:

A company bought a town. The town struck — miners, the union local, the whole valley, over wages or safety or both; the details were underwater now, but the shape of it was legible in every burned foundation. The company couldn't buy them out — strikers with a grievance are stubborn, and grievances multiply. So the company did what companies do when the arithmetic turns ugly. There'd been a night. There'd been men with rifles and kerosene. There'd been a massacre dressed up afterward as an accident, a fire, a tragedy — and then even the tragedy was too much to leave standing, so they built a dam, and the reservoir rose, and the town went under, and the maps were redrawn, and within five years there was no town on any map at all. Just a lake with a power station at its foot, generating electricity for cities three hundred miles away that had never heard the names chalked on this wall.

And Elias Vane — young then, a dam worker maybe, or a miner, or both — had seen it. Had survived it. Had spent the rest of his life carrying the record and the route, keeping the thread unbroken, until the syndicate finally caught up with an old man on a ridge trail and put a bullet in him. Too late. Because he'd already handed the thread to me.

To me. A bush pilot with a debt and a compass that lied. The universe has a sense of humor, and it is not a kind one.

"I don't understand," Noor said, and her voice had gone very flat, which with Noor was the storm warning. "He never said anything. Twenty years I knew him — holidays, twice, maybe three times. He sent postcards with fish on them. *FISH,* Freya. He had a whole lifetime to tell us this and he sent postcards with fish on them."

"He was protecting you."

"Don't." She said it fast, like slapping a hand away. "Don't you dare soften him for me. Everyone softens him. Mom does it, 'he did his best,' my aunts do it, 'men of that generation.' He walked out. Whatever saint act this is—" she gestured at the chalked names, at the drowned street below us, and her arm shook, "—it doesn't buy back Christmases. It doesn't explain why *we* weren't worth the truth and some stranger pilot was."

"He tried strangers first," I said. "Strangers shot him."

That landed. I watched it land — watched the fury hit the fact and bounce, hit it again, find no purchase. She turned away and stood at the church doorway looking down at the water, and I gave her the only gift I know how to give anyone, which is silence.

* * *

We made camp on the knoll above the graveyard — Vane's map had promised it, and there it was: a fenced acre above the flood line where the town had buried its dead for sixty years before the company buried the town itself. The headstones were readable, barely. Families, whole families, rows of the same surname. And near the gate, newer stones, granite, machine-cut — these ones the syndicate had permitted, a token cemetery for whoever they'd allowed to be publicly mourned. The dates on the new stones clustered. One week. Eleven names in seven days, all "accidents."

Eleven names. I counted twice.

Below us the reservoir lay flat and green and patient, holding its street of rooftops like a hand closed over coins. Somewhere under that water was everything — the union hall with its minutes books, the school with its registers, the houses with their photographs and letters and christening gowns, the whole paper body of a community, drowned on purpose so that no one could ever prove it had existed. Except it had existed. Except a dying man had spent twenty years making sure the proof had a route, and a seventeen-year-old girl who hated him was sitting at my fire eating beans out of the same pot, and the proof was now in two heads that the syndicate didn't own.

That's the thing about drowning a town. You can drown the evidence. You can't drown the witnesses' habit of telling the truth. It floats. It always floats. It washes up on shores twenty years later in the form of a chalked name on a church wall, and a bush pilot who should have minded her own cargo stands up in the shallows reading it.

Noor spoke first, after a long time. "There'll be a record," she said. "He wouldn't have mapped the route unless the record was at the end of it. The source. He said the source. 'Tell Noor what I found at the river's source.'" She looked at me across the fire. "It's not gold, is it. It was never gold."

"It was never gold."

"It's the ledger." Her voice cracked on the word and steadied. "Names. All of them. The ones on the wall and the ones that aren't — the ones they disappeared so completely there's not even a stone. Someone wrote them down. Someone kept them. And it's upstream, past the dam, past Harkness, in the one place they can't dig or dredge because the whole world's watching the calving season." She poked the fire like she was stabbing it. "He sent *me.* He could've sent anyone and he sent me, and I'm going to be angry about that for the rest of my life, and I'm going to do it anyway."

I believed her. God help me, I believed her completely, and it scared me more than Harkness did, because fury like hers gets people killed, and I'd started — against every rule I had — to care whether this one lived.

Then the light changed.

Not sunset — something else. A pulse of white swept the treetops across the water, swung, swept again. Searchlight. From the dam crest, four miles off, a beam was walking the shoreline in slow, deliberate arcs, the way a man walks a property line with a shotgun. And beneath it, faint across the still water, I could hear generators. Big ones. More than a dam needs at night.

They knew we were here. They'd always known. Vane's route led exactly where they least wanted anyone to stand, and we were standing in it, cooking beans over a fire on the high ground of a murdered town, lit up against the dark like an accusation.

Noor looked at the light, then at me, and there was no fear on her face. There was something worse. Recognition.

"They're still guarding it," she said. "Twenty years, and they're still guarding it. That means it's still true."

Down in the green water, the rooftops waited. Upstream, past the dam, past the searchlights, past whatever Harkness was bringing up the valley behind us — the source. And between us and it, starting tomorrow at first light, the last stretch of country no plane was cleared to enter and no witness had crossed in twenty years.

I banked the fire low. Some promises, it turns out, you make to a dying stranger and only understand at a drowned church wall.

Tomorrow we'd go see what the river had been holding all this time.
