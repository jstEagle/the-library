## 13. Halfway Point

I learned to read water before I learned to read words. Grandpa taught me that much, at least — how a river lies with its surface and tells the truth with its seams, how the smoothest stretch of current is usually the one trying to kill you. He'd crouch on the bank of whatever creek we were camped beside and point with a stick. *See that V? That's a rock under there. See that boil? Don't trust it.* I was six. I thought he was teaching me about rivers.

He was teaching me about himself.

So when Freya spread the map out on the gravel bar that morning and I looked past it — really looked, downriver, at what was left of Alder's Bend sitting forty feet under green water like a held breath — I understood the lesson I'd been getting my whole life. The smooth surface. The rock underneath.

My grandfather helped build the dam.

* * *

It came out sideways, the way these things do. We'd been walking the shoreline since dawn, following the drowned fences — fence posts standing up out of the reservoir like the stubble on a drowned man's face — and Freya had gone quiet in the way she goes quiet when she's deciding whether to tell you something. She's bad at hiding things, Freya. She thinks she's good at it. She does this thing where she gets very busy with her hands, checking straps, re-rolling the map, fussing with the compass she pretends isn't in her pocket. Her mother's compass. The one that doesn't point north. But that's another lie I hadn't gotten to yet.

"Your grandfather," she said finally, to the fence post, not to me. "Elias. He wasn't just a witness."

The water lapped against a rooftop. An actual rooftop, shingles still on it, a chimney breaking the surface like a periscope. A town called Alder's Bend, which had existed for sixty years and then, one autumn, had been bought, burned, and flooded by the Meridian Syndicate so that nobody would ever again be able to stand in its streets and count who was missing.

"He surveyed for them," Freya said. "Early days. Before the strike, before the massacre. He picked the dam site. He ran the levels." She said it fast, the way you pull a bandage. "And then he saw what they did with it, and he spent twenty years carrying the other thing instead. The ledger. The names."

I didn't say anything. That should have been warning enough for her. When I go quiet, it's not calm. It's pressure building behind a weak seam.

Because here's what I knew, growing up: Elias Vane was a man who left. He left my grandmother with three kids and a leaky roof. He left my mother with a story about prospecting, about striking it rich someday, about sending for them. He missed birthdays, funerals, my grandmother's whole slow dying. And every few years a letter would come, postmarked from nowhere in particular, full of rivers and weather and never once the word *sorry*. My mother read them aloud at the kitchen table in a flat voice, like reading a shipping manifest, and then she'd put them in a shoebox, and the shoebox went under the bed, and that was our whole relationship with Elias Vane.

I built my version of him out of that. A selfish old man chasing gold because gold was easier than family. It was a workable theory. It fit everything. I could be furious at it cleanly, the way you can hate a storm — no ambiguity required.

Now Freya was handing me a different man. A man who surveyed the drowning of a town. Who stood on the dam crest while they burned the houses below — or close enough to it — and then chose, for twenty years, to write letters about weather instead of coming home and saying *I helped kill those people and here are their names.*

"You're defending him," I said.

"I'm explaining him—"

"You're *softening* him." My voice came out strange, too level. "You've been doing it since the ridge trail. 'He was dying, Noor.' 'He was crying, Noor.' 'He held your name like it was the last warm thing he had, Noor.'" I heard myself doing an impression of her and hated it. "'Tell my granddaughter what I found.' You know what he found? He found a way to make his guilt somebody else's errand. He handed a stranger a map and a promise and died clean, and now I'm up here eating river water and running from men with guns, and everyone keeps telling me what a burden he carried. He *built* the burden. He laid the foundation. He ran the goddamn levels."

Freya took a breath. I watched her decide not to use it.

"Noor—"

"And you *knew*." That was the part that actually hurt, once I got through the anger to the wiring underneath. "You've known since the crash site, haven't you. Since Harkness. Since the gorge. You knew what he'd done and you let me walk around thinking my grandfather was just a coward with a gold pan, because the truth was worse and you were managing me. Like I'm cargo. Like I'm one more thing you have to deliver without damage."

"That's not—" She stopped herself. Started over. "Okay. Some of that's fair."

"Don't do that either."

"Do what?"

"The thing where you're reasonable at me. You're very good at being reasonable. It's your whole personality. You weigh things, you hedge, you keep one boot out of every room you're standing in. You've been flying away from places your entire adult life and you want me to believe you stayed honest with me out of some bush-pilot code?" I was crying now, which annoyed me, because I'd planned to do this cold. "You don't get to be the trustworthy one. You owe Calloway. You told me that yourself, night one, drunk on adrenaline after the rapids — you owe a man you won't even describe, and you flew us into forbidden country anyway, and now my grandfather turns out to be one of the men who flooded a town full of people, and you're standing there going *some of that's fair* like we're negotiating a freight rate."

The reservoir moved against the chimney. Somewhere under all that green water was a school. A church. A dance hall where people had probably been dancing the week before the trucks came. I thought about the names in the ledger — no. I hadn't seen the ledger yet. I only knew it existed, the way you know a body is under the ice by the shape of the snow. Freya had told me enough. Enough to know my grandfather's handwriting was somewhere in it, and not just as a witness.

"We split camps," I said.

"Noor."

"We split camps or I take the raft and half the food and you can explain to Harkness why there's only one of us." I heard how that sounded — like I was threatening to hand myself to the syndicate out of spite — and honestly, at that moment, the arithmetic of danger meant nothing to me. That's the thing nobody tells you about fury. It doesn't make you brave. It makes you bad at math.

Freya looked at me for a long moment. Wind off the water pushed her hair across her face and she didn't fix it. Then she nodded, once, like a pilot accepting weather she can't argue with.

"Okay," she said. "Split camps. We stay within shouting distance — not because I'm managing you, but because if Harkness's boats come up this shore, one person alone is a body and two people are a fight. That's not a negotiation. That's physics."

"Fine."

"And you take the flare gun."

"I don't want your—"

"*Take the flare gun,* Noor." First time her voice had any weight in it all morning. "You can hate me and carry the flare gun. Both fit in one pair of hands. I've checked."

She started gathering her share of the gear, and I stood there hating how competent she was even at being left, how she divided the rope without being asked, how she moved the medicine crate closer to my pile because I was the one more likely to need it, and how she did all of it silently, without one ounce of the wounded dignity she would have been entitled to, which somehow made it worse. If she'd fought me, I could have fought back. Instead she just made room for my anger like making room for a third passenger, and I wanted to throw the compass in the reservoir.

I didn't. Not yet. But I thought about it hard enough that I'm writing it down.

* * *

I walked the shoreline until the light went long and coppery. Picked a camp in the elbow of a collapsed boathouse, above the high-water line, with sightlines down the reservoir in both directions — Grandpa's habits, drilled into me so deep they operate without my permission. I ate half a ration bar and gave the other half to the lake, which felt ceremonial and stupid and necessary.

Here is what I couldn't say to Freya, standing on that shore with my arms crossed:

She was right.

Not about everything. About the shape of it. Because I'd done my own softening, hadn't I. Two weeks ago my grandfather was a stranger who wrote letters about weather. Then he was a dying man with frostbitten hands who traded a map for a promise, and I let that man into the boat with me, and somewhere between the gorge and the drowned rooftops I had quietly rebuilt him into *the grandfather who was coming back to us*, the one whose last act was reaching toward his granddaughter, and I needed that version so badly I'd stopped asking questions. Freya answered one question I hadn't asked, and I lit her on fire for it.

But knowing I'm unfair doesn't stop me from being angry, and being angry doesn't stop the truth from being true, and the truth has a specific gravity that pulls everything downhill toward it. Elias Vane surveyed the dam. Elias Vane ran the levels. Somewhere in a green drowned street, under the water I was drinking boiled from, were the foundations my grandfather set with his own instruments, his own hands, his own careful six-year-old-teaching eyes.

He taught me to read water. He must have known, even then, what was under the smooth parts.

I sat up most of the night listening to the reservoir. It makes a sound, a big impounded river. Not like a river. Rivers talk; lakes *hum*. A low pressure through the whole body of it, the drowned creek still trying to run its old course down there in the dark, pushing against forty feet of stolen sky. I used to think that sound was peaceful. Now it sounds like a town trying to finish a sentence.

Around what I guessed was two in the morning — my watch drowned with the plane, so everything is guessed now — I heard something downshore. Not a boat engine. Not Harkness's crew; they travel loud, they travel certain, generators and winches and men who've never once worried about noise carrying. This was quieter. A rhythmic knock, knock, knock, patient as a woodpecker but wrong for a woodpecker — too regular, too deep, coming off the water itself.

I lay flat at the edge of the boathouse slab and looked.

Far out, near where the old church steeple broke the surface, there was a light. Small. Oil-colored, not electric. It swung gently, anchored to something, and beneath it — I could barely make this out, and I read water, I'm telling you I *read* water — there was a shape that wasn't a boat. Low. Still. Sitting on the reservoir like it had grown there, like it had been there longer than the water.

A line paid out from it into the dark. Fishing, maybe. At two in the morning. Over a drowned town.

Knock, knock, knock. Something tapping on the inside of a hull. Or someone signaling across water the only way you signal when you don't dare show a flame — the way you'd signal if you'd been alone out here for twenty years, keeping a record, waiting for somebody the compass might finally send.

I should wake Freya. Shouting distance, she said. Physics, she said.

I lay there with the flare gun in my lap and did not call out, because some animal part of me understood that whatever was knocking out there on the black water had waited twenty years, and could wait four more hours, and that if I woke Freya, this would become *ours* — and right now, tonight, in the wreckage of everything I believed about my grandfather, I needed one thing in this valley that was mine alone.

The light swung. The knocking stopped.

Then it started again — and this time, I swear this, it wasn't random. Three knocks. Pause. Three knocks. Pause. The rhythm of a name being spelled out, slow, across forty feet of water and twenty years of silence.

K-N-O-O-R.

Somebody out there knew my name.
