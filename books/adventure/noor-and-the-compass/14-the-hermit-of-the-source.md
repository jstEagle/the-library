## 14. The Hermit of the Source

The outflow of the reservoir ran cold enough to ache in your teeth, and it ran loud — a constant gray argument between the dam and the valley, water that had been sitting on a drowned town for twenty years and still hadn't made its peace with leaving. Noor and I had come up along it for two days since the bear den, moving at night, sleeping badly, eating the last of a tin of pilot crackers and half a jar of peanut butter we'd salvaged from the wreck with our lives. My hands were still scabbed from the rapids. Her boots had given up pretending to be waterproof somewhere around mile forty.

We smelled woodsmoke before we saw anything else.

That alone was strange. This far up, past where the syndicate's survey stakes stopped being fresh and started being theoretical, nobody built fires. Harkness's men burned propane in their boats and diesel in their generators and left no trace they'd ever been human. A woodsmoke smell meant somebody who'd been here long enough to cut wood, split it, stack it against weather. Somebody who intended to stay through winter. Nobody stayed through winter here. The maps said so. The syndicate's exclusion notices said so, nailed to trees in three languages, all of them ending in the same threat rendered in the same bureaucratic font: *authorized personnel only.*

But smoke doesn't lie about itself the way signs do. We followed it down to the water.

There, on a gravel shelf above the outflow, sat a cabin so small it looked like it had been assembled by argument — one wall slightly longer than the other, a roof patched with tin in two different colors, a chimney of river stone mortared with what appeared to be patience. Next to it, a fish rack. And under the rack, working a gutting knife along a grayling with the unhurried precision of someone who had done this ten thousand times, an old woman.

Gray hair braided back tight. Hands like tree roots. A canvas coat gone the color of the gravel she sat on, which is either camouflage or twenty years of not caring, and I suspected both.

Noor spotted her first. "Somebody's home," she whispered, which was absurd, because there was nothing within fifty miles to be home *to*, but the instinct held. We crouched behind a windfall spruce like two teenagers casing a farmhouse, which, given that one of us was seventeen and the other was flying a plane illegally into a sealed valley while owing money to a man who smiled like collateral, was maybe the most honest description of us available.

I should have turned around right then. I want to be clear about that. Everything in me that had kept me alive for twenty years — the part that read weather, read rooms, read the specific silence of a place that didn't want visitors — that part looked at the cabin and said *walk away.* But the compass was in my breast pocket, and it had been dragging us up this side channel for two days like a dog pulling a leash, pointing not north, not toward the source, but at this exact bend of gray water, and I had learned over the last week that when that needle insisted on something, it was usually insisting on something I'd spent my whole life refusing to look at.

So instead of walking away, I did the thing bush pilots do when they land uninvited at a stranger's strip: I walked out in the open, slow, empty-handed, and announced myself like a decent person.

"Ma'am," I called. "We're not syndicate. We're just passing through, and we're hungry."

The knife paused mid-stroke. The old woman looked up, and her eyes did something I can only describe as taking inventory — not of our weapons, because we barely had any, but of our faces. She looked at Noor first, catalogued her, set her aside. Then she looked at me.

And the world tilted.

I don't mean I felt dizzy. I mean the ground changed identity underneath me, the way a runway changes identity when you realize you've landed at the wrong field. Because I knew those eyes. Not from photographs — my mother left almost nothing, and what little she left, Calloway's people had eventually gotten their hands on anyway. I knew them from the mirror. From the bathroom mirror in a dozen rented rooms, from the cockpit mirror I used to check for fatigue before night flights, from every moment in twenty-six years when I'd caught my own reflection and thought, vaguely, *I have someone's face and I don't know whose.*

She had my jaw. She had the crease between the brows that I'd always blamed on squinting into sun-glare, except hers hadn't been earned by glare, it had been earned by twenty years of watching water. She had my hands, if you aged mine forty years and gave them calluses I hadn't lived long enough to grow.

"Freya," she said.

Not a question. That was the worst part. Not *Freya?* with the hopeful question mark of someone guessing. Just *Freya,* flat and certain, the way you say the name of someone you've been expecting.

I stood there in the gravel like a mannequin someone had forgotten to pose. Twenty years. Twenty years of building a story about her — that she was dead, that she'd run off, that she'd been taken, that she'd never wanted me, that she'd wanted me too much and couldn't bear it, every version rotated through the carousel depending on how bad the year had been. Twenty years of carrying her compass and telling myself it pointed north like a normal object, because admitting otherwise meant admitting she'd known something about loss that I was only now learning at the bottom of a gorge.

And she had been *here.* Upstream of everything. Fishing the outflow of a drowned town, keeping its records, feeding herself off grayling and a garden the size of a blanket, waiting — or not waiting; I couldn't tell yet whether the certainty in her voice was hope or simply arithmetic — for the day her daughter came walking down the bank.

"You're Maren Fairweather," I said, and my voice came out strange, flattened, like radio transmission from a plane losing altitude.

"I'm your mother," she said. "The other thing is a detail."

Noor, crouched behind the windfall, made a sound I will remember longer than any engine failure. It wasn't a gasp. It was more like a hiccup of disbelief, the noise a person makes when reality reaches through the keyhole and rearranges the furniture. Then she stood up, because whatever protocol governed hiding from strangers clearly didn't survive meeting someone's resurrected mother, and stared at the old woman with her mouth open.

"That's—" she started, and stopped, and started again. "You're the hermit. People talk about you. Downriver, at the last cannery, there were stories. An old woman above the falls who trades fish for salt and won't say her name."

"I've had practice not saying my name," Maren said, dry as the gravel. "It got easier."

* * *

She fed us before she explained anything, which told me more about her than any confession could have. Grayling cooked over alder coals, flatbread on a skillet older than both of us combined, tea brewed from something piney that Noor identified as spruce tips and I identified as *better than anything I'd eaten since the wreck.* We ate sitting on split-log stools at a table scarred with knife marks, and Maren watched us eat with an attentiveness that was either love or surveillance, and after twenty years I suppose it was both.

"You look like him," she said to Noor, quietly, once the bread was gone. "Elias Vane."

"You knew my grandfather?" Noor's anger surfaced instantly, that flint-strike temper she carried like a sidearm. "He never mentioned—"

"He wouldn't have." Maren turned her cup in its saucer, a small circular motion, like stirring without tea. "Elias and I knew each other a long time ago, before either of you was born. Before the town drowned. He was a different man then. Or he was the same man, and the difference is what the valley did to him."

"The valley," I repeated. Not a question either. I was doing a lot of that — repeating words, holding them in my mouth like stones I wasn't ready to swallow or throw.

Maren looked at me then, fully, and I braced for the interrogation I'd rehearsed my whole life in my head. *Why did you leave. Did you write. Did you think of me. Was there someone else.* The questions every abandoned kid keeps loaded behind their teeth for decades, waiting for a target.

Instead she said: "Before I answer anything, I need to show you something."

She stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and went inside the cabin. Through the doorway — the door was open, propped with a stone — I could see the interior: one room, immaculate in the way of places organized by obsession rather than comfort. Shelves. Oilcloth bundles. And on the wall, hanging from a peg, a compass.

Not mine. Mine was in my pocket, and I could feel it through the fabric, feel it straining, the needle swinging toward the wall like a compass rose finally finding its true center. Hers hung there, brass case gone dark with handling, twin to mine down to the dents. I knew the dents. I'd grown up believing the dents on mine were from being dropped on a dock in Valdez. Looking at hers, I understood the dents were inherited. They matched. They matched *exactly*, the way scars match when two objects were made together, by the same hand, in the same hour, for the same purpose.

"There were two," Maren said. "My father made them. He was a machinist in the town — Corvane, it was called, before your syndicate bought it, burned it, and put a lake on top of it. He made the pair the winter before the strike. One for himself, one for me. They don't point north. They point at each other. At what matters. At what you've lost and can't afford to forget."

"And mine?" I said. "How did mine end up with me?"

She came back to the table. She sat down across from me, and for the first time since I'd recognized her, she looked away — out the window, at the water, at the surface that covered four hundred people and a hundred years of a town's life.

"Because I sent it to you," she said. "Twenty-two years ago. You were four."

"I never got a compass."

"No," she agreed. "You got it fourteen years later, in a box with no return address, after I arranged for it to reach you through a chain of people I trusted and one I shouldn't have. Calloway had his hands in that chain. Which means Calloway has known, for fourteen years, that the compass existed, that it was yours, and that whoever held it could find this valley." She turned back to me, and her eyes were steady and terrible. "Your debt was never about money, Freya. It was bait. It was a leash. He's been walking you toward this lake your entire adult life, and he's close enough now to taste it."

I heard Noor inhale beside me. I felt my own pulse do something complicated in my throat. But mostly — mostly, in that moment, I felt the smaller, crueler thing underneath the conspiracy, the thing I'd flown a stolen route into a forbidden valley to avoid feeling:

*She chose this. She chose the records over me.*

That was the thought. It arrived whole, pre-loaded, twenty years in manufacture, and it detonated quietly at a scarred wooden table while the woman who abandoned me poured tea like nothing was happening. I didn't say it. I've spent my life not saying things; it's practically my profession. But she saw it anyway. Mothers see it. That's the trap of mothers — you can hide a body in muskeg, hide a plane in fog, hide yourself inside a debt, and the one person you cannot hide from is the one who left, because she knows exactly which doors you'll bolt.

"You're angry," she said. Not defensive. Diagnostic. "Good. Anger means you expected better. I did too, once."

"Don't," I said. "Don't manage me. Don't do the wise-elder thing where you reframe my abandonment as a journey with lessons. I flew a plane into a rock canyon partly because of you. I've spent twenty years not staying anywhere because staying felt like something that gets taken away. That's not a lesson. That's damage. You did it. Own it plainly or I'm walking back down the bank."

For a second she just looked at me, and I watched something cross her face that I recognized from my own mirror — the expression of a person deciding whether to defend themselves or tell the truth. Defending yourself takes less time. Telling the truth costs everything and buys one thing: being believed.

She told the truth.

"I stayed because four hundred people were erased and someone had to keep their names," she said. "I left you because keeping them meant vanishing, and I told myself a four-year-old would forget me faster than a twelve-year-old would forgive me, and I was wrong about the math, and I have paid for it every single morning for twenty years by waking up alone in a cabin the size of a coffin with your face looking back at me from the water bucket." Her voice didn't break. It did something worse — it stayed level, level as the drowned rooftops. "I am not asking you to forgive me at this table. I'm asking you to hear the ledger before you decide anything. There are names in it. Elias Vane's among them. So are yours and Noor's, in the margins, in my handwriting, because I wrote you into the record even though I had no right to be writing anything but the dead."

Noor made a sound — half sob, half laugh, the specific noise of a seventeen-year-old discovering that grief has layers like geology. "You wrote *us* in?"

"Every year," Maren said. "On your birthdays. Freya, I logged your height, your school years as best I could learn them from traders, the planes you flew — yes, I know about the De Havilland, word travels upstream faster than you'd believe. Noor, I logged the cannery seasons, the winters your grandfather didn't come, the years your mother worked doubles. I couldn't raise you. I refused to lose track of you. Those were the only two options the valley gave me, and I took the coward's half of both."

* * *

Afterward, we went outside, because the cabin had become too small to hold three people and twenty years, and the light on the reservoir was doing that late-evening thing where the green water goes to glass and you can almost — *almost* — see the rooftops.

Maren showed us the ledger. Oilcloth, wax-sealed, heavier than it had any right to be. She didn't open it. She set it on the fish-cleaning table between us and put her hand flat on top of it, and said, "Tomorrow. Tonight you eat, and sleep under a roof for the first time in nine days, and in the morning I'll show you why the syndicate has killed to keep this book underwater in every way a book can be underwater."

Then, while Noor washed the plates in the outflow — the girl had appointed herself crew within minutes, some instincts run deeper than blood — Maren stood next to me at the rail of her porch, such as it was, and said the thing I'll carry out of this valley no matter what happens to the rest of us.

"You still carry it wrong," she said, nodding at my breast pocket. "The compass. You keep it over your heart like it's a keepsake. It's not a keepsake. It's an instrument. It's been trying to get you here since the day it reached you, and now that you're here, it's going to start showing you what it shows everyone who holds it near the water." She paused. "Including, if you let it, the life you could have had. Be careful with that. It's the most honest liar you'll ever meet."

Behind us, down the bank, a sound crossed the water — low, rhythmic, mechanical. Diesel. Somewhere below the falls, engines were idling where no legal engine had any business idling, and the evening air carried them upstream the way it carries everything.

Maren heard it too. She didn't flinch. She picked up her gutting knife, tested the edge against her thumb, and said, calmly, the way you'd note a change in barometric pressure:

"They're early. We have until dawn, maybe. Freya — tomorrow you were going to learn why I stayed." She looked at me, and my own eyes looked back at me out of her face, twenty-six years older and infinitely harder-won. "Tonight, you're going to learn why they're coming."
