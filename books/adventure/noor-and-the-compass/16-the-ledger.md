## 16. The Ledger

The morning Maren Fairweather handed me the ledger, the lake was so still it looked like someone had poured glass over the rooftops of a town and forgotten to tell anyone underneath.

I'd been up since before light, which wasn't unusual — cannery habits die hard, and my body still woke at four-thirty expecting a conveyor belt of salmon heads. But this morning I woke because Freya's mother was already at the fire, stirring a pot of fish soup with the patience of a woman who had spent twenty years learning that everything worth doing takes longer than you think.

"You're up," she said, like I'd done something remarkable instead of just failing to sleep.

"Your soup smells like a lie," I said, because I'm seventeen and grief makes you cruel in small ways before it makes you honest in large ones. "It smells like someone decided to be hopeful about breakfast."

Maren laughed — actually laughed, a dry sound like wind through spruce needles — and ladled me a bowl anyway. "My daughter says worse before coffee. You'll do fine."

That was the thing about Maren. Everyone else on this river treated me like cargo that happened to have opinions. Freya treated me like a co-conspirator she hadn't fully vetted. Maren treated me like a person who'd been carrying something heavy for a long time and deserved to set it down, even if she didn't know yet what I'd been carrying or how heavy.

I ate the soup. It was good. Hopeful, even. I hated that a little.

* * *

After breakfast, Maren went into her cabin — a low sod-roofed thing built against the outflow channel, half fishing hut, half archive — and came out with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with what looked like fishing line but was probably older than both of us put together.

She set it on the flat rock by the fire and didn't say anything for a long moment. Just looked at me the way my grandmother used to look at me when she wanted me to understand something without having to explain it, which is a skill I've decided only belongs to women who've outlived their explanations.

"Noor," she said finally. "Your grandfather kept records."

I knew that. Sort of. I knew Elias Vane as a man who sent money orders that arrived late and postcards that arrived never, a man whose handwriting on birthday cards looked like it had been written by someone shaking. I knew he'd left my mother before I was born and left my mother's mother before that, and I knew that in our family, "Elias" was less a name than a weather pattern — something that blew through every few years, wrecked things, and moved on.

What I did not know was that he'd spent the last years of his life keeping accounts.

Not gold accounts. Not claims or stakes or any of the things a prospector is supposed to keep accounts of.

People.

"He was there," Maren said quietly. "When they flooded it."

There being the town under the lake. The one I'd seen from the ridge two days ago — rooftops like drowned teeth, a church steeple poking through green water like a finger accusing the sky. The town the syndicate bought, burned, and buried, along with everyone in it who wouldn't sell.

"He helped build the dam." Maren said it without flinching, which took a kind of courage I didn't have yet. "He was young and broke and they paid cash. And then he watched what the cash was for. And instead of running, he stayed close for forty years, and he wrote down every name."

Every name.

I sat with that for a second, and then I did what I always do when something doesn't fit inside me: I got angry. Anger fits easier. Anger has edges.

"So he wrote names," I said. "Great. He wrote names while people drowned. He wrote names while families got burned out. He wrote names while my grandmother raised a baby alone and told everyone her husband was dead because it was easier than telling them the truth, which was that he was building a dam for the men who killed a town."

Maren didn't defend him. That surprised me more than anything. I expected her to — old people defend each other reflexively, like it's a union rule. Instead she said:

"Yes. All of that is true. And none of it cancels the other part. Both things are in the book."

Then she untied the oilcloth.

* * *

The ledger was not what I expected. I expected grand — leather, gilt, something that looked important enough to justify forty years of obsession. What I got was a school composition book, the cheap kind, marbled cover gone gray with damp and handling, spine cracked, held together at the back with copper wire twisted through punched holes.

Forty years of names in a five-cent notebook.

The first page was dated the summer of the flood. The handwriting was shaky — not old-shaky, young-shaky, drunk-shaky, terrified-shaky. A man writing fast because he thought he might be killed for writing at all.

And the first entry wasn't a name. It was a sentence.

*I helped them do this.*

Four words. Underlined twice.

I read it three times. Each time it landed somewhere different — first in my chest, then in my throat, then somewhere lower, in the place where I keep all the things I haven't decided how to feel about yet.

Because here's what nobody tells you about forgiveness: it isn't a door you walk through. It's a room you have to dismantle brick by brick, and every brick has a name on it, and some of those bricks are your own.

I turned the page.

Names. Columns of them. Some with ages. Some with descriptions — *left-handed, played fiddle, owed me eleven dollars.* Some with little marks beside them that I later understood were Maren's additions, cross-referenced over decades: confirmed, survived, unaccounted.

Unaccounted. That word again. The word that had been following me up this whole river like a dog I couldn't shake.

There were children in the columns. There were infants listed with no age at all, just *baby girl, born on the trail south, mother's name unknown.* There was a whole family — seven entries, same surname — with a single note beside the last one: *the dog made it out. Ran north along the rail grade. Never found the rest.*

I read until my eyes burned. I read past the point where reading stopped being information and started being weight. Somewhere around the third section — the syndicate's own men, listed too, because Elias Vane apparently believed a record should be complete even when completeness hurt — I realized my hands were shaking.

Freya appeared at some point. I don't know when. She'd been checking the raft lines downstream and came up quiet, the way she does, and stood behind me without saying anything, and I could feel her there the way you feel weather coming.

She didn't touch the book. She watched me read it, and after a while she said, very softly, "Take your time."

Which is pilot language for *I know exactly what you're holding and I'm not going to make it worse by hovering,* and I loved her for it and also wanted to throw her in the lake, which I've learned is just what love feels like at seventeen.

* * *

Here's where I have to be honest about the middle part, the part I'd skip if I could.

Near the back of the ledger — past the names, past the flood counts, past the pages where Elias had copied out, in careful block letters, the syndicate's internal correspondence that he'd stolen over the years — there was a confession.

Not four words this time. Pages of it. Written later, the handwriting steadier, dated maybe fifteen years after the flood. My grandfather, sitting somewhere alone for what must have been years, writing to a granddaughter he'd never met.

To me.

He'd known about me. That was the first blow. He'd known my mother was pregnant when he left the coast, and he'd known my name — Noor — because my grandmother had sent word through a fish buyer who owed him a favor, and he'd written it on the inside cover of this notebook like a man writing a prayer he didn't believe would be answered.

*Noor. If anyone ever brings you this book, it means I ran out of time to be sorry in person.*

I had to stop there. I actually had to stand up and walk to the edge of the outflow and look at the water moving, because water moves, and I needed to watch something in the world do the thing I couldn't.

Freya followed me. She didn't say anything. She just stood there, an arm's length away, close enough to grab if I went in, far enough that I could pretend she wasn't.

"I hate him," I said to the water.

"Okay," Freya said.

"I hate that he knew my name. I hate that he wrote it down. I hate that he thought about me for forty years and never once got on a boat."

"Okay," Freya said again, and didn't add anything, and that was the most respectful thing anyone had ever done for me.

But here's the part I can't skip either, the part that ruined me:

A few pages further in, Elias had written about the day of the flood itself. About standing on the dam crest with the other laborers while the water rose, and about a woman on a roof — he gave her name, one of the names in the columns, a fiddle player's wife — who looked up at the men on the dam and didn't scream. She raised her hand. Not a fist. An open hand. Like she was taking attendance.

*Like she was counting us,* Elias wrote. *Making sure we'd remember we were seen. She counted us, and I have been trying to be worth the count ever since, and I have failed more days than not, but the book is the count now. The book answers her hand.*

I read that standing in the shallows with wet boots and a burning face, and something in me cracked in a way that wasn't breaking.

Because I understood it suddenly. All of it. The money orders that came late. The silence. The forty years of staying near a thing he helped destroy, writing names in a notebook that cost five cents, because he couldn't undo a single thing and he knew it, and the only penance available to a coward who woke up was arithmetic.

He wasn't asking to be forgiven in those pages. I looked. I read it twice looking for the ask, and it wasn't there. He was asking to be *counted*. To be entered in the record alongside the people he'd wronged — guilty, named, present — so that the record would be true.

And the record should be true. That was the whole point of everything Maren had given her life to. The truth doesn't work if you sand off the ugly parts. A ledger with the guilty man's name missing isn't a ledger; it's a lie with neat handwriting.

So I went back to the fire, and I picked up the pencil Maren offered me — a stub, worn flat on one side — and below his confession, in my handwriting, seventeen years old and furious and certain, I wrote:

*Counted. — N.V., granddaughter of E. Vane, who was guilty and stayed anyway.*

And then I forgave him.

Not because he earned it. Not because forty years of names balances a flooded town — nothing balances that, nothing ever will, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. I forgave him because I finally understood that the anger was a room I'd been living in, and he'd been paying rent on it his whole life without knowing the address, and I was tired, and the river was long, and there were names in that book that needed mouths to carry them, and mine was the newest mouth available.

Grief resolved into purpose, Freya calls it. She said it last night watching me write, and I told her she sounded like a fortune cookie, and she said "fortune cookies are just small prophets with bad luck," and I laughed for the first time in two days, and that felt like forgiveness too.

* * *

We spent the afternoon doing the thing that mattered more than my feelings, which was: memorizing.

Maren laid it out plainly over the soup she refused to let go cold. "Paper burns. Paper floods. Paper gets confiscated by men like Harkness, who are very good at confiscating paper. The book survives if it lives in people."

So we divided it. Maren took the first third — she'd had decades, and it showed; she recited names like liturgy, unhurried, infallible. Freya took the middle sections, and I watched her do it with the same terrifying focus she used on weather and fuel loads, mouthing names while staring at the fire, testing herself, getting one wrong out of two hundred and starting over.

I took the confession and the final third, and I read them until I could recite them with my eyes closed, which I did, out loud, on the rock by the water, while the lake held its drowned rooftops perfectly still beneath me like an audience.

Somewhere in there, Maren said the thing I'll keep:

"They flooded a town to erase it. So we become the thing that can't be flooded."

I wrote that down too. In the margin. In pencil. Let Harkness confiscate that.

By dusk I knew I'd never lose them — the names, the sentences, the open hand on the roof taking attendance. They weren't in the book anymore. They were in me, wired in the way cannery work wires things into you: repetition until the hands know it blind.

And I was almost — almost — at peace. Sitting there with a five-cent notebook full of ghosts, a grandfather counted, a purpose loaded into memory like rounds into a magazine.

That's when Freya came up from checking the raft lines for the second time that day, moving faster than she'd moved since the crash, and she wasn't carrying firewood.

She was carrying her binoculars, and her face had the specific color of a pilot who has just seen something on the water that shouldn't be there.

"Boats," she said. "Two of them. Running dark, up the outflow channel. Harkness didn't come for the map."

She lowered the glasses and looked straight at me, and the words landed like a dropped engine:

"He came for the book."
