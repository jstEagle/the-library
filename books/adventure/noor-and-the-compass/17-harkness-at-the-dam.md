## 17. Harkness at the Dam

The dam house sat on the north abutment like a tooth that had grown in wrong — a squat concrete block with windows too small and a door too heavy, built by men who expected the valley to resent them. The valley did. You could hear it in the water, a low grinding complaint beneath the spillway, the sound of a river being held against its will.

Maren had warned us about the dam house. She'd said it the way she said most things, which was sideways, over her shoulder, while doing something else with her hands. *They kept the paymaster's office there. Back when there was a paymaster.* She'd been gutting fish at the time, and she didn't look up, and I should have understood that silence was her loudest voice.

Noor and I had come down from the ridge at first light, planning to skirt the reservoir's eastern shore and put distance between ourselves and Harkness's boats before he found our cold camp. We were maybe four hundred yards from the dam when Noor stopped walking and said, "Smoke."

Not woodsmoke. Engine smoke, thin and blue, rising off the dam house roof like a flag of occupation. And then, because the morning was flat calm and sound carries across water like gossip carries across a cannery dock, we heard the generator kick in, and under it — voices. More than two. More than three.

We crouched in the alders and did the arithmetic neither of us liked.

"He got here ahead of us," Noor whispered. "How?"

"Because he has boats and fuel and a radio, and we have a raft with a patched hull and my knees." I said it lightly, because that's what I do when the ground opens under me. Wit as ballast. "Because he's been running this river longer than we've known it existed."

"No." Noor's jaw was set in a way I'd learned to recognize. It meant she'd already done a calculation I hadn't. "He knew where Maren was. He went straight for her."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to argue. And then I remembered every courtesy Mr. Harkness had extended us at the crash site — the offer of a ride downstream, the folded map, the way he'd said *Miss Fairweather* like a man handling something he intended to own — and the argument died in my throat with its boots on.

We watched the dam house for an hour. Long enough to count silhouettes: six, maybe seven men moving behind the small windows. Long enough to see a figure led out onto the crest of the dam and back again — white-haired, unhurried, hands not bound, which somehow made it worse. A prisoner who isn't tied is a prisoner who's been told exactly what happens if she runs.

Long enough to understand that we hadn't been chasing the source.

The source had been bait, and we'd swallowed it whole, and everything since the gravel bar had been a long slow reel toward this concrete tooth.

* * *

I want to say we planned something clever. We did not. What we had was: one raft, one rifle with nine rounds, one girl who could read water better than any man on this river, one compass that pointed at grief, and one mother I'd met properly only days ago and had twenty years of things to say to. Against that: seven armed men, a fortified position, and a syndicate that had drowned a town to bury its crimes and would clearly drown whatever else it needed to.

"We go in at dusk," Noor said. "Through the outflow channel. It's loud there — the turbines eat sound. I can get us under the catwalk."

"And then?"

"Then you do the talking thing you do. The thing where people tell you things they shouldn't."

"That's not a plan, that's a personality flaw."

"It's worked so far," she said, and looked at me with Elias Vane's exact gray eyes, and I understood that somewhere between the rapids and the bear den and the ledger full of names, I'd stopped being the adult in this arrangement. I'd become the one who talked. She'd become the one who decided.

Dusk came down purple and cold. We paddled the last stretch by hand, no strokes, just letting the outflow current carry us into the churn below the dam where the water roared itself hoarse and nobody on the crest could hear a thing over it. Noor read the boils and eddies like a page of large print, steering us with two fingers on the gunwale, and we came in under the concrete shadow wet to the waist and shaking, and climbed a maintenance ladder whose rungs bit through my gloves.

The catwalk ran along the dam's downstream face. From there we could see into the dam house through its one lit window, and what we saw rearranged the evening entirely.

Maren sat at a table, upright, hands folded, a cup of coffee untouched in front of her. Across from her sat Harkness, coat off, sleeves rolled, pouring a second cup with the care of a man who believed hospitality was leverage. Two guards stood by the door. Through the open outer door we could see more men on the crest, and a boat tied at the reservoir-side landing.

And moored beside it, riding low and familiar, a black-hulled riverboat with a gold stripe.

I knew that boat the way you know a scar.

"Calloway," I said, and my voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's.

Noor looked at me. "Who's Calloway?"

"The man I owe." I heard myself laugh, one dry note with nothing under it. "The man I've spent three years hauling cargo to pay off. The reason I never land anywhere twice."

"You said he was a fixer. A lender."

"He is." My hands had gone cold in a way the river hadn't caused. "He's also, apparently, a buyer."

* * *

Here is what I had told myself about my debt, all these years, because a person needs a story to carry a weight: that I'd borrowed hard money in a bad winter, after my plane went down the first time, after my mother had been gone eight years and I had nothing but a compass and a habit of leaving. That Calloway had been decent about it, in his way. Flexible terms. A smile like a handshake. Interest that moved around the way interest does when the lender writes the numbers.

And here is what was actually true, standing on a catwalk watching him climb out of his boat with a document case under his arm: he had never once asked me for the money back. Not once in three years. He'd asked for favors. Runs to towns off the charts. Cargo I didn't inspect. Questions about who was asking questions where.

A debt you're never allowed to settle isn't a debt. It's a leash.

Harkness met Calloway at the dam house door. They shook hands like equals, which was the most frightening thing I'd seen all day, and I'd seen a glacier calve. Then they went inside, and the window framed them at the table with Maren between them like a coin being argued over, and Noor gripped my sleeve and said, "We can't hear anything."

"I know."

"There's a vent pipe on the roof. Old buildings like this — the generator room vents through the office wall. My grandfather taught me that trick. Listen through metal."

Of course he had. Of course Elias Vane, who had helped build this dam and then spent thirty years trying to unbuild what he'd helped make, had taught his granddaughter how to eavesdrop on the men who owned it. Grief and inheritance arrive in such odd packages.

We went up the outside stair on the reservoir side, quiet as weather, and Noor pressed her ear to the vent cap and then pulled me down beside her, and the voices came up through the metal thin and clear as radio.

"—appreciate the difficulty of your position," Harkness was saying, in that courteous tone he used like a scalpel. "But the fact remains that your pilot located the records. Your investment. Your return."

"My pilot," Calloway said, "has a talent for finding things. It's why I keep her in hock." A pause, and I could hear the smile in it, the smile I'd mistaken for kindness in a bad winter. "You have no idea what she cost me to aim. Three years of patience. Do you know how patient I am, Mr. Harkness? I let her bury her own mother's trail. I let her fly past this valley twice without knowing why the compass in her pocket wouldn't sit still. All I had to do was wait for someone else's dying old man to hand her a map."

My ears filled with a sound like the river. Noor's hand found mine and squeezed hard, whether to hold me still or hold me together I couldn't say.

Inside, Maren spoke for the first time. Her voice was steady, older than the recording of it I'd carried in my head for twenty years, and absolutely unafraid. "You're both discussing me as though I'm inventory."

"You are holding inventory," Harkness said. "The ledger."

"The ledger holds names," Maren said. "Seventy-one of them. Men who struck for a wage they'd earned, and the day they were fired, and the day the fires started, and the day the water came up over their church. You can buy paper. You cannot buy what I've memorized, or what my daughter's young friend has memorized, or what the descendants scattered along this coast remember in their bones. You are forty years too late to buy this, Mr. Harkness. Your father's generation managed the drowning. Yours only manages the forgetting. And forgetting is losing ground every year."

There was a silence with violence in it.

Then Calloway, gently, reasonably, the voice of a man offering a woman a chair: "Mrs. Fairweather. Nobody wants a tragedy. We want a transaction. The ledger, and any copies, and your continued — retirement. In exchange, your daughter walks away owing nothing, breathing free, able to land wherever she likes for the rest of her life. Isn't that what you'd want for her? A place to stop?"

And God help me, some traitorous cellar of my heart leaned toward it. A place to stop. Twenty years of airstrips and borrowed bunks and never unpacking past the second day, and here was the price of rest, itemized: my mother's silence, seventy-one names, a girl sitting next to me who'd earned the truth with her skin.

Noor was already moving. She took the rifle off her shoulder, checked it with hands that didn't shake, and mouthed at me: *the door.*

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way bad weather happens fast, and afterward I could never fully account for the order of events, only the fact of them.

Noor went down the outside stair to the dam house door and kicked it open — kicked it, seventeen years old, five foot four, furious in the specific way of a girl who has read her grandfather's confession aloud to the sky — and shouted "Drop it!" in a voice that carried the authority of someone much larger, and both guards turned, and I came down the stair behind her with the rifle I'd taken from her because she'd insisted I take the rifle, and I put myself in the doorway with the muzzle up and said, in my best cargo-negotiating voice:

"Evening, gentlemen. Terrible time of year for a meeting."

Chaos, briefly. Harkness rising, reaching inside his coat. One guard lunging for Noor and catching her elbow; her free hand going to his wrist and twisting with a dockworker's economy until he yelped and let go. Calloway not moving at all, just watching me over the rim of his coffee cup with an expression of genuine, almost fond disappointment, like a teacher watching a promising student fail the final.

"Freya," he said. "Sweetheart. Put it down. You're pointing a gun at your own future."

"She's pointing it at yours," Maren said, and stood up, and for a moment the room reorganized itself around her — around this small white-haired woman in a fish-gutting jacket whom six armed men had thought was inventory. "Both of yours. Because while you've been negotiating, my daughter's friend has been reading that ledger for eleven days, and I have been carrying it in my head for twenty years, and there are four hundred people on the coast who get letters from me every season. Kill us all and the names still come out. That is the entire failure of your industry, gentlemen. You keep trying to kill information, and information has already left the building."

Harkness had his pistol half-drawn. He looked at Maren, then at me, then — and this was the moment I'll remember, the moment the trap showed its true shape — he looked at Calloway, waiting. Waiting for direction. Because Harkness was the muscle, the syndicate's courteous face, but the money, the patience, the three-year leash on a bush pilot's neck —

That was Calloway's.

"Put the gun down, Freya," Calloway said again, softly. "Here's the arithmetic. You shoot, Maren dies, the girl dies, and you spend the rest of your life running from men worse than me. You don't shoot, and everyone leaves this room rich. The ledger for the debt. Clean slate. You can finally stop."

"Stop," I repeated. The word tasted like every airstrip I'd ever left at dawn.

"Yes."

"That's what this was." My voice cracked and I let it. "Three years. The flexible terms. The runs you never explained. You weren't lending me money. You were aiming me. Like a compass."

Calloway smiled, and for the first time I saw what had always been underneath the smile, which was nothing at all. "You were the best instrument I ever bought. And you flew yourself right to it. I never even had to point you at the valley. Vane did that. Grief does that. People walk straight toward what they've lost, every time, and all a patient man has to do is stand where they're headed."

Beside me, very quietly, Noor said: "That's not what the compass does."

Everyone looked at her.

"It doesn't point at what you lost," she said. "It points at what you haven't faced yet. There's a difference. My grandfather figured it out too late. You—" and she looked at Calloway with those gray eyes, "—you're going to figure it out in about ten minutes."

Outside, far upstream, the glacier made a sound.

I'd heard calving before — the boom rolling down a valley like the sky clearing its throat. This was different. This was continuous, a long tearing roar, and the floor of the dam house shivered, and the coffee jumped in its cups, and every man in the room except Maren and Noor turned toward the window.

Maren didn't turn. She looked at me across the room, twenty years of silence in her face, and said, "Freya. The gates. If the surge comes down the gorge, this dam takes it head-on. Those men in the control room—" she nodded toward the inner door, behind which the generators droned, "—will open the spillway or the whole structure goes. And if it goes, everything your friend Calloway bought and paid for goes with it."

The tearing roar swelled. Dust breathed out of the light fixtures. Somewhere on the crest, men were shouting.

Calloway set down his cup with a small precise click and said, "Then I suggest we conclude our business quickly—"

Noor hit the lights.

And in the dark, with the mountain coming apart upstream and the river gathering itself to take back everything that had been taken from it, I felt the compass in my pocket swing hard against its housing — south, then east, then dead steady, pointing at the control room door, at the gates, at the one thing in this valley that hadn't yet chosen a side.

The next ten minutes were going to decide who drowned and who didn't.

I ran for the door.
