## 18. Calving Season

The glacier gave us no warning. That's the thing nobody tells you about ice — it doesn't announce itself like weather does, doesn't build a gray wall on the horizon and give you time to argue about it. It just cracks. A sound like the world clearing its throat, and then a piece of mountain the size of a church falls into the water.

I was on the outflow bank with my hands in the shallows, rinsing our last two mugs, when I heard it. Freya was up at the treeline checking the raft patch — we'd holed her in the fog chase two nights back, and she'd been sewing her like a woman suturing a wound, muttering apologies to the boat the whole time. My grandmother — Maren, though my mouth still tripped on the word *grandmother* like a stone in a shoe — was inside the dam house, copying names.

Then the crack came, and every bird in the valley went up at once, and I knew before I turned around that our eleven days were over.

"Freya!" I shouted, already running. "Freya, it's going!"

She came down the slope at a dead sprint, patch kit still in one fist, and we met at the bank where the river had begun to do something rivers aren't supposed to do: run backward. The surge wave from the calving was coming down the gorge toward us, a brown-green wall maybe eight feet high and moving faster than anything with that much water has a right to move, and behind it the whole channel would rise, and everything the syndicate had moored downstream of us would be kindling.

Everything except the dam house. The dam house sat on bedrock above the outflow, and Harkness's men were in it, and so was Maren.

* * *

Let me back up, because I've been telling this story to myself all night and even I need the order straight.

Two days ago we'd come down off the ridge above the reservoir and seen the town for the first time — rooftops under green water, a church steeple poking up like a drowned man's finger. Freya had gone quiet in a way I'd never heard from her. She talks constantly, Freya. She narrates landings, argues with engines, gives running commentary on other people's flying like the whole sky is her personal theater. Silence from Freya Fairweather is a five-alarm fire.

Maren had met us at the outflow with fish and questions, and then there'd been the reunion — which I'm not going to describe, because some things belong to the people who lived them, and because I spent most of it looking at the water and pretending I wasn't crying too. And then Maren had taken us into the dam house cellar, past a false wall of cordwood, to the ledger.

A ledger. That's what twenty years of hiding came down to. A oilcloth-wrapped book, fat as a Bible, full of names in three different hands. The names of everyone the syndicate erased when they bought the town, burned it, and flooded it. Miners who'd gone on strike. Miners who'd asked for safety equipment after the third collapse. A schoolteacher who'd written to the territorial government. Families. Children. Elias Vane's confession was bound into the back of it, in his handwriting, the same cramped letters I'd seen on the backs of envelopes he'd never sent us.

I read it by lantern light while Freya stood watch at the door, and I did not forgive him yet, but something in me unclenched. Not forgiveness. More like — recognition. He'd been a coward for thirty years and brave for one, and the brave part was this book.

And then, yesterday morning, Harkness came for it.

We heard the boats first — two of them, outboard motors carrying across flat water the way sound does over a lake. Freya got Maren moving through the cordwood wall and out the back while I stayed long enough to see them come ashore: six men, rifles, and Harkness himself stepping onto the gravel like he was arriving at a hotel that owed him an apology. Courteous. That's the worst thing about him. He'd said *please* when he offered to capture us at the crash site. He probably said *please* now, through the door of the dam house, to men who were going to open it or die behind it.

We got Maren up the goat trail to the ridge, and we'd been up there since — two days of cold camp, watching the dam house through Freya's binoculars, watching Harkness's men tear the cellar apart and find the false wall empty, watching Harkness stand on the dock doing arithmetic with his jaw. They hadn't left. They knew Maren hadn't gone far — an old woman, bad knee, nowhere to run but up a mountain. They were waiting us out.

And then the glacier decided it was done waiting too.

* * *

The surge wave hit the lower gorge and the sound came back up the valley like applause from God. From the ridge we watched the river swallow the syndicate's moored boats — both of them, snapped from their lines and rolled under in seconds, spinning like leaves. One of Harkness's men went in after a fuel drum and didn't come up where he went down; another dragged him out forty yards along, coughing, alive, furious.

"They're cut off," Freya said beside me. Her voice had that pilot-flat calm she gets when the world is trying to kill her, which I've learned is actually her most honest voice. "No boats. Radio mast took damage in the surge — look, it's leaning. Even if it works, Harkness can't call for extraction across flooded country. He's stuck here with us until the water drops."

"How long?"

"Gorge floods like this hold for a day, maybe two. Then it'll drop fast." She lowered the binoculars. "Which means we have maybe a day to get Maren out of this valley before he stops being desperate and starts being methodical."

Below us, the dam house sat on its bedrock knob, surrounded now by churning brown water on three sides. Six men in it. One old woman supposedly hidden somewhere on the mountain. And a ledger wrapped in oilcloth against my spine, because I hadn't let it out of arm's reach since the cellar.

Here's the thing I figured out during those two cold days on the ridge, and I'm not proud of how long it took me: Harkness didn't need to find us. He needed to find *her*. And the only way to make an old woman with a bad knee come down off a mountain was to stop searching and start burning.

He hadn't done it yet. But he would. Men like Harkness always arrive at arson eventually; it's the last tool in the box and the one they're best with.

So we couldn't wait him out. We had to go get her. Today, in the flood, while his boats were driftwood and his men were bailing water and his whole careful plan was drowning.

"He'll have posted sentries," Freya said, reading my face the way she read water. "Two on the dam crest, probably one roving."

"Probably," I agreed. "But nobody posts a good sentry in a flood. Everybody's watching the water, because the water is trying to kill them. Nobody watches the mountain."

Freya looked at me for a long moment. "You know," she said, "your grandfather ran wildcat surveys through country like this for thirty years and never lost a man. It's in the blood, apparently. The reading-water part."

"Don't," I said.

"I'm not softening him. I'm crediting genetics." She shouldered the pack. "There's a difference. Ask me again in a year."

* * *

We came down the north side of the ridge through the spruce, single file, Maren between us with her hand on Freya's shoulder and mine steadying her elbow, and she climbed down wet rock better than either of us expected, swearing steadily under her breath in a language I didn't recognize and Freya clearly did, because she kept almost laughing and swallowing it.

The flood had changed the shape of everything. The trail we'd used two days ago was underwater. The outflow was a roaring brown torrent twice its width, dragging whole trees. The dam house knob rose out of it like an island, and the cordwood false wall — the back way in — faced the water side, which meant getting to it meant crossing thirty feet of current moving hard enough to peel skin.

"We're not swimming that," I said.

"No," Freya agreed. "But the flood is doing our work for us. Look at the dam crest."

I looked. Two sentries, sure enough, rifles slung, standing at the parapet. But they weren't watching the mountain. They were watching the reservoir, where the calving had set the whole surface heaving, icebergs the size of houses grinding against each other with sounds like train wrecks. Every man in that valley was watching the glacier, because the glacier was the biggest thing that had ever happened to them.

"The cordwood wall," Maren said suddenly. She'd stopped on the rocks above the knob, breathing hard, and her eyes had gone sharp in a way that made me understand exactly where Freya got it. "It opens inward. There's a bar lift on the inside. But the cellar floods in high water — knee-deep, sometimes more. Elias and I kept the records on pallets for exactly this reason." She looked at the churning water between us and the wall, and then at Freya, and something passed between them that had twenty years of silence in it. "You used to be able to hold your breath for two minutes. You'd practice in the bathtub and I'd time you with the oven timer."

"I remember," Freya said quietly.

"Well," said Maren. "The oven timer's broken. You're on your own count."

* * *

Here is what we did, and I want it on the record that it was mostly my idea, because Freya tells it differently and she's a liar with excellent delivery.

The raft. Our patched, holed, half-drowned raft that Freya had been mothering for two days. We carried it down to the edge of the flood on the upstream side of the knob, where an eddy curled behind a dead spruce and the current slackened just enough. Freya rigged the bow line to the tree with a slip knot on a trigger stick — her knots are genuinely beautiful; it's the one aesthetic opinion she holds. The plan was this: we'd paddle the eddy around to the water side of the knob, Freya and I would go through the cordwood wall into the flooded cellar, Maren would stay with the raft as the pickup, and if anything went wrong, the trigger stick released the line and the flood took the raft — and us — downstream, where the gorge was suicide anyway, but suicide with options.

"You could stay with her," Freya said to me at the edge. "Someone should be on the raft."

"And leave you to go through a dark flooded hole alone? No." I checked the rope at her waist, then mine, tied to hers with six feet of separation. "Besides, you can't read water. You fly over it. I grew up on cannery docks. If the cellar entrance shifts, you'll drown politely and I'll know before you do."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me all week."

"It wasn't nice. Get in the boat."

We went around the eddy with paddles, low and quick, and the roar of the outflow covered us better than any stealth could have — the whole valley was white noise, the glacier still booming every few minutes like artillery. The sentries never turned around. Why would they? Nothing alive crosses that water.

The cordwood wall was exactly where Maren said, a section of stacked logs set into the rock face at the waterline, and the gap at its base was underwater by eighteen inches. Freya went first. She filled her lungs, folded herself through the gap like she was slipping into a sleeping bag, and was gone. I counted. At forty-five my rope went taut and hauled twice — her signal — and I followed.

The cellar was black and knee-deep and freezing, water moving through it in slow pulses, and I came up gasping next to Freya, who put her hand over my mouth before I'd finished inhaling, which is a good way to learn who your partner is. Above us, faint through the floorboards, boots. Voices. Somebody laughing about the fuel drum. The bar lift was where Maren promised, a wooden lever stiff with twenty years of disuse, and Freya and I lifted it together, slow, a quarter inch at a time, while the boards overhead creaked with walking men.

The cordwood swung inward. Gray light. A ladder.

We came up into the woodshed behind the dam house, dripping, and found ourselves ten feet from a man.

He was young. That's what I remember most — young, wet to the thighs, holding a rifle wrong, staring at us with the expression of someone whose brain was still processing the flood and had no room left for two women rising out of the floor. He opened his mouth.

Freya hit him with the paddle. Not hard — she caught him across the forearms as he raised the rifle, knocked it spinning into the woodpile, and then had him by the collar with her forearm across his throat, all in about a second and a half, moving with a speed I'd only ever seen her use on a jammed cargo door.

"Options," she said conversationally, while he went purple-ish. "One: you scream, my friend here puts you out, and you wake up in an hour with a headache while we're gone. Two: you sit in that corner and count to five hundred, and you tell Harkness whatever keeps you employed. Three—" she leaned closer "—you ask yourself why you're guarding a ledger for men who flooded a town full of people like your mother."

He chose option two. I want to believe it was option three working on him underneath, but I was seventeen once too, and mostly it was the paddle.

We went through the shed, along the dam house's blind side, and down to the water where Maren held the raft against the eddy with the bow line wrapped twice around her fist, small and soaked and absolutely steady.

"Records?" she asked.

"Got them," I said, patting the oilcloth bundle at my back. "Ledger's safe."

And that's when the glacier calved again.

* * *

The second collapse was bigger than the first. Later, hydrologists — actual hydrologists, this story got around — would say the first calving had undercut the toe and the whole face let go in sequence, a chain failure, eleven million tons of ice entering the reservoir in under four minutes. All I know is that the sound arrived through the ground before it arrived through the air, and then the air got it too, and the surface of the reservoir stood up.

The displacement wave came across the water at us like a moving hill.

"Maren — boat — NOW—"

We shoved off into the eddy as the wave hit the dam. I will spend the rest of my life hearing that sound: the dam taking a hit it was never built for, concrete groaning somewhere below its bones, spray going up two hundred feet in a white curtain that fell on us like rain. The raft surfed the reflected surge, spun, nearly broached — Freya and I on the paddles, Maren bailing with a mug, the three of us screaming things at each other that I won't repeat because none of them were strategic.

Behind us, the dam crest simply stopped existing. The wave took the parapet, took the sentry post, took the two sentries — I saw one of them swimming, actually swimming, clawing for the high ground of the abutment, and I remember thinking with a clarity that still shames me: *good, live, you're just a boy.* The dam house lost its roof. Men scattered along the abutment like ants on a log that's rolling. Harkness stood at the highest point with his coat streaming, and even at that distance I could see him doing it — the arithmetic, the jaw. Counting what he'd lost. Deciding what it cost.

The flood took the rest. The gorge below the dam, which had been running at flood stage, now received the reservoir's entire temper on top of it, and every syndicate boat that had survived the first surge — the skiffs, the tender, the aluminum workboat hauled up on the gravel — went into the torrent and came apart. Their radio mast went down. Their fuel cache went down. In ninety seconds, Mr. Harkness went from a man conducting an operation to a man stranded on a rock in a drowned valley with six wet employees and no way out except through us.

"Freya," Maren said. She was gripping the gunwale, hair plastered to her face, and she was smiling — a fierce, terrible smile I suspected had waited twenty years for employment. "Elias always said the glacier would settle it. He said the syndicate built their dam on borrowed time and the ice kept the real books."

"Yeah, well," Freya panted, hauling us into the relative calm behind the point, "the ice just called in the loan."

That's when I felt it. The lightness. The wrongness.

My hand went to my back, to the oilcloth bundle, and came away holding oilcloth and about four pages of sodden paper.

The rest was gone. Somewhere in the surf, in the spin, in the moment the wave hit — the ledger had torn free of the lashings and gone into the flood, into the brown water, into the gorge, into the sea eventually, into the whole patient circulatory system of the world. Twenty years of Maren's record-keeping. Elias's confession. Every name.

I must have made a sound, because Freya turned, and Maren turned, and I held up the ruined pages like a boy presenting a dead bird.

"It's gone," I said. "It's — the ledger's gone. I lost it. I'm sorry, I—"

Maren looked at the pages. Then at me. Then she started to laugh — not cruel, not kind, something older than both.

"Child," she said, "what do you think I've been doing for two days on that ridge while you two sulked and Freya sewed her boat? I copied it. Every name, every date, every page, twice, in different hands, in case one copy was taken." She tapped her own temple, then pointed at me. "And I made you read it aloud to me by lantern light, twice, because I wanted to hear whether you stumbled. You didn't stumble once."

I stared at her. The sodden pages dripped into the bilge.

"It's in your head," she said. "All of it. I watched you take it in. Your grandfather had the same trick — read a survey page once and carry it through a whiteout. I told you it was in the blood." She picked up a bail mug and handed it to me like a medal. "Paper drowns. Names don't. Not anymore."

Freya was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read — awe, maybe, or the particular horror of a pilot realizing her passenger has been carrying the entire mission in her skull without a parachute.

"So we didn't lose it," I said slowly.

"We never had it," Maren said. "Not where they could take it. Harkness just watched his men tear apart a cellar for a book that was sitting in a seventeen-year-old's memory eating smoked fish." She settled back against the thwart, exhausted and radiant. "Now. Someone tell me which way is downstream, because I have twenty years of names to deliver, and I'd like to start while my knees still work."

Downstream, the gorge roared, flooded, impassable — for boats. For planes, the ceiling was lifting; I could see blue tearing through the cloud over the divide, and Freya saw it too, her head tipping back the way it always did, reading sky the way I read water.

"There's a gravel bar," she said slowly, "two miles down, above the gorge narrows. High enough to stay dry. Long enough, maybe, if the wind cooperates, for a very stupid takeoff." She grinned at us both, wild and soaked and alive. "Ladies, we're going to need a longer bar and a shorter prayer. Everyone start praying."

We paddled into the flood, three of us, one raft, and a valley full of names that no one — not Harkness, not the water, not twenty years of silence — was ever going to drown again.
