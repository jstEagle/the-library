## 19. What Power Offers

The dam was singing.

That's the only word I have for it. Not groaning — singing, a long low note through the concrete like a wet finger on a wineglass rim, and every pilot knows that sound because every engine makes it just before it quits. I'd spent twenty years learning to hear the note before the failure. Now I stood on top of the instrument itself, and the whole structure was holding its breath.

Harkness held a pistol the way he did everything else: courteously, correctly, and with an absolute certainty that the world would arrange itself around him.

"Ms. Fairweather." He had to raise his voice over the water thundering through the spillgates below us. "I want to be clear about our position."

"Our position," I said, "is four hundred feet of cracked concrete above a drowned town, in a gorge that's about to be full of iceberg, with no boat, no radio, and your helicopter pilot smart enough to have left already. I'm clear on our position."

"The ledger." His eyes went past me to the satchel at my hip, then back. "And the woman who wrote it. That's all I need. You can walk away from everything else. I'm told you're good at walking away."

There it was. Twenty years of my life summarized by a man in a syndicate windbreaker as though it were a professional qualification. And the worst part — the part I'd examine later, if there was a later — was that for one half-second, standing on that crumbling crest with a gun pointed at me, I felt *flattered*.

"I don't have the ledger," I said. "It's gone into the flood. Ask the river."

"Then we'll wait for the river to give it back." He gestured with the pistol toward the intake tower, where Maren had been taken, where Noor had been taken, where the water was rising by the minute as the calving glacier choked the outflow. "Rivers are patient. So am I."

The compass sat in my breast pocket, against my heartbeat, and it had been getting warmer since sundown.

* * *

You should understand something about the compass before I tell you what happened next, because I've had time to think about it and I still don't fully believe myself.

My mother left it behind when she vanished. A brass thing, old, the kind sailors used to hang over charts, and for twenty years it had done the one thing a compass must never do: lie. It pointed south when north was due. It pointed at empty sky. Once, on approach to this very valley, it had swung around and pointed directly at Noor Vane, seventeen years old and asleep in the right seat, and I had decided — rationally, sanely — that the instrument was broken and I was tired and altitude does strange things to the mind.

Except it wasn't broken. It never had been. It points at what you've lost. My mother made it here, or her people did, in the town under the reservoir, and it is not a navigation tool at all. It's a question. It asks: what will you pay to get it back?

On the dam crest, with Harkness's gun steady and the concrete shivering under my boots, the compass stopped asking and started answering.

* * *

It began as light.

Not sunlight — the sun was down behind the ridge and the gorge was going blue-dark — but a light from inside the brass, leaking through my shirt like a coal through cloth. Harkness didn't see it. I know because he kept talking, kept making his courteous demands, and men don't keep talking when the world starts glowing unless they can't see it.

The first vision was small, almost kind.

I was in the cockpit of the de Havilland, but not my battered one — a new one, or new to me, paint unchipped, engine humming the way engines hum in dreams. Through the windshield: the coast, my coast, the gray-green water and the black spruce, and a strip of gravel below with a house beside it. My house. There was a garden. Someone had planted a garden, rows of something green, and there was a dog, and there was —

Noor, younger, maybe twelve, running out to meet the plane, waving both arms, and the feeling that came off that image was so dense it had weight. Belonging. The specific gravity of being expected somewhere. I hadn't felt it since I was six years old, and I hadn't known until that moment how much of my flying — all of it, every cargo run, every rumor hauled across the interior, every night sleeping in the plane because motels asked questions — had been one long search for exactly that.

"You could have this," said a voice, and it wasn't Harkness's voice, and it wasn't my mother's either. It was mine, from some version of me that had made different choices. "You could have all of it. You only have to stop running."

The compass grew warmer. The vision widened.

* * *

Here's the thing about temptation: nobody ever tempts you with something you don't want. The devil doesn't offer you a timeshare. He offers you the exact shape of the hole in you, filled.

The second vision was bigger. In it, I had stayed. Not just landed — *stayed*. I saw a life where Freya Fairweather had put down roots at nineteen instead of pulling them up every eleven months, and the roots had grown into something enormous. I owned the airstrip. Then three airstrips. Then the fuel contract for half the interior, and the mail run, and when Calloway came to collect his imaginary debt I looked at him across a desk that was taller than his and forgave it like a queen tossing a coin to a beggar, and he smiled and bowed and never bothered me again because power is the only language men like Calloway respect.

In the vision, no one had ever left me. My mother hadn't walked into this valley; she'd stayed at the kitchen table and taught me to read charts. Elias Vane hadn't died on a ridge trail; he'd come to Sunday dinner. Nobody I loved was anywhere I couldn't reach. Everything lost had been found, and everything found had been kept, and the compass hung on a nail by the door pointing steadily, faithfully, at nothing — because there was nothing left to point at.

I wanted it so badly my knees nearly went.

That's the honest part. I've been wry about a lot of things in my life — dead engines, dead friends, my own spectacular talent for leaving — but I won't be wry about this. Standing on that dam with a gun on me and the world coming apart underneath us, I would have traded anything, anyone, for that warm lit room where everyone I'd ever lost was safe at home.

"Freya." Harkness again, sharper now, because I'd stopped listening to him and men like him can't tolerate silence. "The woman. Maren Fairweather. She's your mother, isn't she? Our records go back further than you'd think." He smiled his courteous smile. "Family reunions are so much simpler when everyone cooperates."

And the vision shifted, because the compass heard him too.

* * *

The third vision showed me how to win.

Not survive — *win*. It laid it out like a flight plan, clean and legible. Take the deal. Give Harkness whatever fiction of a ledger he'd accept, walk off this dam alive, take Calloway's forgiveness, and then — here was the elegant part — use what I knew. The drowned town. The massacre. The names. I could own the syndicate with that information, or sell it, or bury them with it, and either way I'd never again be a woman in a battered bush plane owing money to fixers and favors to strangers. I'd be the one they came to. I'd hold the compass and the record and the valley, and no one would ever leave me again, because no one leaves power.

The compass pulsed against my chest like a second heart, and I understood, finally, what my mother had understood twenty years ago, standing somewhere near this same water with this same brass weight in her hand.

It wasn't offering me a vision. It was offering me a *trade*. Everything I'd lost, returned — priced at everything I might yet lose. Because the ledger in my memory, the names Noor had memorized, the truth we were carrying out of this valley in our mouths — all of that was future loss, and the compass only knew how to point backward. To keep the vision, I'd have to let the present die. Let the names drown twice. Let Noor's grandfather stay a stranger. Let my mother stay a ghost I'd gotten back only as a comfortable fiction at a kitchen table that never existed.

Power, it turns out, is just loss with better lighting.

"No," I said.

Harkness blinked. "I haven't offered you anything yet."

"Not you." The compass was burning hot now, and I took it out — brass glowing faintly, needle spinning like a trapped moth — and held it up between us, and even Harkness stopped talking then, because whatever he saw, he saw enough. "I'm declining the other offer. The real one."

The needle slowed. Stopped. Pointed — not south, not at Noor, not at any lost thing.

It pointed straight down, through the concrete, through the green drowned water, at the rooftops of the town, and I swear on every hour I've logged that the needle trembled there like a hand letting go.

Then it went dark. Cold. Just a compass again, pointing wherever broken things point.

* * *

"What," Harkness said carefully, "was that."

"A negotiation," I said. "I won."

He raised the pistol, and I'll give him this much: he did it without drama, the way he did everything, a man executing a clause rather than committing a crime. "The ledger exists. The girl memorized it. I watched her do it. Memory is recoverable, Ms. Fairweather. People are remarkably cooperative when properly motivated, and I have two proper motivations in the intake tower."

Below us, the river changed its voice.

I'd been hearing it all evening — that deep spillgate roar — but this was different. This was the sound of a lot of water arriving at once, the way a river sounds different an hour before a flood, and I turned toward the upper gorge and saw the moonlight catch on something white moving down the channel.

Ice. The calving front had let go early. A whole season of glacier, arriving all at once, a white tide filling the gorge wall to wall, and the dam — the singing, shivering, sixty-year-old dam that the syndicate had built on lies and rebar and hope — was about to receive it.

Harkness heard it too. For the first time since I'd met him, Mr. Courteous looked uncertain, and I watched the calculation move behind his eyes: the ledger, the hostages, the helicopter that had already gone, the water coming down the gorge like the end of the world with a deadline.

"The tower," he said. "Both of you. Now."

"Or what?" I said. "You'll shoot the dam?"

But I moved, because Noor was in that tower, and because the concrete under my boots had begun, very softly, to sing a higher note.

We were halfway across the crest when the first crack opened — a seam down the middle of the roadway, yawning, breathing cold air up out of the dam's guts — and Harkness grabbed my arm, not to help me, just to keep his balance, and for one absurd moment the syndicate man and I clung to each other on the spine of a dying monster while the white tide came on.

The compass sat cold and silent in my pocket. I'd chosen. I'd refused the warm lit room, the garden, the desk taller than Calloway, the whole gilded architecture of never-losing-anything. What I'd bought instead was this: a cracking crest, a rising flood, a mother and a girl locked in a tower, and no plan whatsoever except forward.

It was, I can report, the best trade I've ever made.

Behind us, the crack reached the edge of the crest and the singing stopped — replaced by a sound I'd only ever heard once before, the day a mountain let go of itself above Kettle Lake, a sound like the earth clearing its throat before it speaks.

The crest was going to break. And everything downstream of it — Harkness's empire, the drowned town's long prison, the syndicate's whole flooded secret — was about to surface into daylight whether it liked it or not.

I ran for the tower.
