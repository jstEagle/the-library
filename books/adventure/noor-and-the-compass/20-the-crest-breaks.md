## 20. The Crest Breaks

**FREYA**

The dam had been singing all morning.

Not metaphorically — I want to be clear about that, because I've spent enough years around engines to know the difference between a thing that hums and a thing that prays. The dam was praying. A low, wet groan came up through the concrete every time the water behind it shifted, which by noon was constant, like a choir of drowned men clearing their throats. Harkness stood on the crest in his immaculate survey jacket, pointing a pistol at my mother's shoulder and explaining, with the patience of a man who has never once been interrupted, that salvage rights were a sacred instrument of commerce.

Calloway stood ten feet behind him, hands in his pockets, wearing the expression of a man watching his own trap close on his own hand.

"Freya," Calloway said. "We should talk."

"We're talking," I said. My mother's arm was under Harkness's grip and the ledger — the real one, the paper one, the one we'd already decided didn't matter anymore — was somewhere at the bottom of Maren's pack, and none of it mattered, because the sound the dam made when it groaned had changed key. It had gone from minor to something worse. Something final.

"You brought me here," I said to Calloway. "The debt. The informant. Twenty years of keeping me on a hook so short I could feel it when I slept. You wanted Vane's map to lead you to whatever the syndicate buried, and instead it led us to people. To names. To my mother, alive, holding twenty years of receipts."

Calloway's smile did something complicated. It started as a smile and ended as an accounting error.

"Freya," he said again, softer, the way men say your name when they're calculating whether you're worth more alive or quiet. "Whatever she has—"

The dam answered him.

I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe that sound and I will fail every time. It wasn't an explosion. Explosions are rude and quick. This was a long exhale, a structural confession, the concrete admitting out loud what the water had known for years: that it was tired, that it had been asked to hold back a town's worth of grief plus forty feet of spring melt, and that it was done. A crack opened along the crest like a zipper being pulled by God. Water came through it not as a wall but as a decision.

Harkness looked at the crack. Then at my mother. Then at the crack again, and for the first time since the muskeg, since the fog chase, since the courteous little speech about how everything could be arranged — for the first time, Mr. Harkness looked small.

"Get her inside," he said, meaning the dam house, meaning safety, meaning himself, though he dressed it up as concern for Maren, because even drowning men file paperwork.

Nobody moved toward the dam house. Everybody moved away from the dam.

* * *

**NOOR**

I was two hundred yards downstream on the scree slope with the raft half-dragged to higher ground when the crest went.

You hear things in this country before you see them — avalanches, calving ice, a moose deciding you're its problem now. But nothing had prepared me for the voice of a dam failing. It was a roar with a floor under it, a sound so heavy it arrived through the ground first, up through my boots, into my teeth, before the air caught up. Gravel jumped off the slope around me like startled birds.

I turned and the valley had changed.

Where there had been green water and rooftops — the drowned town, silent under its lid of reservoir for twenty years — there was now brown chaos, and the rooftops were coming up. That's the part I can't stop seeing. The water dropped and the town surfaced, whole streets of it, gable ends and chimney stacks rising out of the draining flood like a congregation standing back up after a century underwater. Chimneys first. Then roof ridges. Then, on the far shore, the blackened frame of the company hall where they'd locked the strikers in before the fire, before the guns, before the flooding, before the erasure.

It was surfacing into daylight. It was *testifying*.

And in the middle of the outflow channel, riding a surge that would have folded our raft like paper, was a white boat with the syndicate's markings, and a man standing in it, and the man was not steering. He was holding something above his head with both hands — papers, a leather folio, salvage rights, actual physical documents — while the boat pirouetted under him like a leaf in a gutter.

That man was Mr. Harkness.

I watched him for eleven seconds, which is longer than you'd think. Long enough to count. He wasn't trying to live. He was trying to keep the papers dry. He had them lifted over his head like a trophy, like a deed, like a man at a land auction winning something, while the river he'd spent three days hunting us down took his boat apart around him. The motor tore free first. Then the transom. He stepped — actually stepped, I saw it — from the sinking hull onto the roof of a submerged house that the receding water had just exposed, and he stood there on somebody's shingles, in the ruins of a town his syndicate had bought and burned and drowned, clutching his documents, and the next wave took the shingles too.

He went under still holding them up. I swear it. The last I saw of Mr. Harkness was his arms above the water, folio high, like a man hailing a taxi that had already left.

The river closed over him without comment.

* * *

**FREYA**

Harkness's grip on my mother died with his footing. One minute he had her wrist, the next the crest under him was moving sideways, and Maren Fairweather — seventy-one years old, twenty years of fish and rain and stubbornness in her shoulders — pulled free and ran.

I ran after her. Not toward the high ground. Toward her.

The crest was breaking in sections, west to east, each collapse sending a tongue of water across the walkway ahead of us. We were running on a structure that was becoming a waterfall in installments. Behind us Calloway was doing arithmetic at full sprint — I could hear him muttering figures, actual numbers, depreciation schedules, the book value of a dam versus the liquidation value of a man — and then I heard him stop muttering, which meant he'd reached a number he didn't like.

"Freya!" he shouted. "The plane! There's a strip on the ridge — Harkness's crew ferried fuel up yesterday—"

Of course there was. Of course Calloway knew about the fuel. Calloway always knew where the exits were; it was the only honest thing about him.

"Maren!" I grabbed her arm at the top of the fish ladder stairs. "Ridge. Now. We're leaving."

She looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors — the look of a woman being told to abandon something. Because below us, in the draining basin, the town was coming up. Her town. Her twenty years. The records were safe — Noor had the names in her head, and the ledgers themselves were lashed in oilcloth to the highest shelf of the dam house, above any line the flood would reach. But the town itself, the physical fact of it, the streets she'd walked alone for two decades keeping faith with the dead — she was being asked to watch it drown twice.

"No," she said. Then she looked at the water coming down the stairwell, and she said, "Yes," and we ran.

* * *

**NOOR**

Calloway almost made it.

I want that on the record, because everyone assumes men like him don't run well, and the truth is worse: they run beautifully. He came down the ridge trail ahead of us, fifty-three years old and moving like a man who'd done this before in other countries for other employers, and he reached the grass strip where the syndicate's Beaver sat fueled and pointed downhill, and he got the door open, and he was halfway into the pilot's seat when he looked back.

He looked back at Freya.

I was thirty feet behind her, hauling my grandfather's granddaughter weight up a slope I'd have sworn an hour ago I couldn't climb, and Calloway looked at the two of us — at the debt he'd manufactured, the bait he'd set, the whole twenty-year architecture of hooks and favors designed to walk Freya Montrose into this valley and flush out a ledger — and I watched him do the math one final time. Two seats. Three people. A valley filling with water behind them and a syndicate's lawyers in front of them and a piece of paper in his breast pocket that was, as of four minutes ago, signed by a dead man.

He slid across to the passenger side. He left the controls open.

"Calloway," Freya said, breathing hard, throwing the preflight like she was dealing cards. "You're going to sit there and tell me you planned all of this to end with you flying us out."

"I planned it to end with me flying *out*," he said. "You were ballast. You've promoted yourself. Congratulations."

"That's not an apology."

"It's a ride," he said. "Take the apology up with the river."

Maren climbed in last, soaked to the collar, and looked at Calloway for a long moment with the flat gray patience of a woman who had spent twenty years writing down the names of men exactly like him. "You'll testify," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a sentence being served in advance.

Calloway, to his eternal credit and absolutely no redemption, said, "If the alternative is staying here," which is the most honest yes anyone ever gave my mother's mother-in-spirit, and we were rolling before the wheels finished spinning up, downhill, into the wind, off a strip that was already losing its far end to the flood, and the last thing I saw of the valley through the window was the water going over the place where the dam had been, and the town standing up out of the ruin, chimney by chimney, into the light.

* * *

**FREYA**

There is a version of this story where I describe the flight out with poetry. Where the sunset does something meaningful and the mountains forgive us and I understand, finally, at altitude, what the compass was trying to tell me all along.

Here's what actually happened. Noor flew. I sat in the right seat with my mother pressed against my side, both of us soaked, neither of us speaking, while the girl I'd met six days ago on a cannery dock put a damaged Beaver through a go-around in a gorge that was actively refilling with the Pacific Ocean's opinion of our plans. She flew it the way she reads water — early, unbothered, already living ten seconds in the future. At some point she said, "Fuel's ugly, we're stopping at the lake," and at some point after that I realized I hadn't touched the controls and hadn't needed to, and that this was the single most humiliating and wonderful hour of my aviation career.

Maren slept against my shoulder for nine minutes. I counted. In those nine minutes she was twenty-six years old again, the way sleeping people are, and I got to look at her face without owing it anything. Just a face. Tired. Alive. Mine, in the way that matters more than blood — chosen, returned, unpaid-for.

Then she woke and said, "The compass," and I told her it was at the cairn, buried, done, and she nodded like a woman closing a ledger, because of course she understood. It had never pointed north. It had pointed at her. And I had carried it for twenty years thinking it was broken, when really it was just faithful, waiting for me to fly somewhere honest enough to follow it.

Below us the valley settled. By dark the flood would find the sea and the drowned town would stand in open air for the first time in twenty years, and tomorrow people would come — descendants, journalists, a prosecutor if the world had any spine left — and Noor would stand among the chimneys and read names until her voice gave out, because the paper ledger was safe in oilcloth but the real ledger was in her head, and heads don't drown.

Calloway broke the silence once, over the lake, watching us refuel from the passenger seat with his hands still shaking.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the debt's dead. Drowned with everything else. You're free, Freya. Clean slate. Never owed me a thing."

I looked at him. Wry, I thought. Even now. Even ruined, he couldn't resist the transactional flourish — forgiving a debt that had stopped existing, taking credit for the tide.

"Thanks," I said. "I'll send a card."

* * *

**NOOR**

We landed at the coast strip at dusk with the Beaver coughing and the light going amber and my grandfather's confession folded in my jacket pocket, softened by river water into something closer to a letter than a document.

Maren stood on the gravel and looked inland, at the direction we'd come from, and I watched her do it — the way you check on a grave. Except the grave was empty now. The town was up. The names were out. Somewhere behind those mountains, water was running through streets that hadn't seen sky since before I was born, and the man who drowned clutching his salvage rights had become, in the space of one afternoon, the least important thing in the valley.

"He'd have liked today," I said, about Elias. About the old man who shot a map into my life and a hole into my chest and turned out, in the end, to be a witness who'd built the thing he testified against and couldn't live with either fact.

Maren squeezed my hand. "Tomorrow," she said, "we go back. All of us. And you read them the names."

I nodded. My throat was already tight and the reading hadn't started.

Behind us, Freya was walking the length of her airplane in the last light, running her hand along the cowling, checking the leading edges, doing the small devotions pilots do — and I noticed she kept glancing at the horizon the way she used to glance at doors. Except now, for the first time since I'd known her, she wasn't looking for the exit.

She was looking at the morning.
