## 21. Names Read Aloud

The valley after the flood smelled like a drowned thing turned inside out.

I'd smelled it before — the cannery in August when the herring boats came in heavy and the offal pits went sour in the sun — but this was worse, because underneath the rot there was something older, something that had been held underwater for sixty years and finally let go. Mud everywhere. Mud on my boots, mud in my hair, mud dried in the creases of my knuckles so that when I flexed my hands they cracked like river ice. Somewhere downstream a machine was still groaning, one of the syndicate's pumps or generators, drowning itself in silt, and nobody had gone to shut it off because nobody cared anymore.

The dam was gone. Not all of it — a jagged V of concrete still stood at the north end like a broken tooth — but the middle had let go the way my grandfather must have known it would someday, the way every lie lets go eventually, all at once and worse than anyone predicted.

And the town was out.

You could see it from the ridge where we'd slept, or what was left of it: street grids gone soft, foundations like gravestones laid flat, the steeple of the old church tipped against its own foundation like a drunk leaning on a fence. The reservoir had dropped forty feet in a day, and the water that remained was brown and shallow, and things were surfacing all along the margins. A wagon wheel. A stove. A child's boot, small as my fist, standing upright in the mud like it was waiting for its owner to come back.

Freya stood beside me on the ridge and didn't say anything for a long time. That was the thing I'd learned about her in the last two weeks, the thing nobody would guess from the way she talked — nonstop, wry, running commentary like a bush pilot narrating her own life — which was that Freya Montrose could be silent when silence was the right tool. She used it the way she used the throttle. Deliberately. With respect for what it could do.

"Your grandfather walked through there," she said finally. "When it was still standing."

"I know."

"He was young. Younger than you."

"I know, Freya."

She shut up. Good.

Because here's what I couldn't stop seeing: Elias Vane, nineteen years old, in a photograph I'd never see, standing on a street that had a name — the ledger said the town was called Aldermill, which nobody alive seemed to remember — walking past houses where people were making dinner, and he didn't know yet. He didn't know what the company was going to do. He didn't know he'd help them do it. He didn't know that sixty years later his granddaughter would be standing on a ridge looking at the bones of the place he'd helped drown, holding the names of everyone in it in her head like water cupped in her hands.

I had memorized the ledger. All of it. Four nights in Maren's cabin at the source, reading by lamplight while the outflow chuckled over the rocks, I'd read the names until they stopped being ink and started being people. Two hundred and eleven names. Men, women, children. The strikers who'd been shot in the mill yard in the winter of '61, and the families the company "resettled" — that was the word in the corporate minutes, *resettled*, like they were birds — and the ones who just disappeared between one payroll and the next. Elias Vane had copied the payroll records before the company burned the town office. He'd written the confession in the back, in handwriting that shook so badly by the end it looked like the pen was cold.

*They were not resettled. I helped load the trucks. Some of the trucks did not go where we were told they went. I have carried this sixty years. I am sorry. I am sorry. I am sorry.*

Three times. He'd written it three times.

I'd hated him for two weeks — hated him in the raft, hated him in the bear den, hated him halfway up the reservoir — and then I'd read that, and the hate didn't go away, it just changed shape, the way ice changes shape when the sun hits it. It didn't vanish. It turned into something that moved.

Now people were coming.

We'd sent word out — Maren knew the old signal trails, mirror flashes from ridge to ridge, a language the drowned town's scattered descendants had kept alive the way you keep a pilot light alive, small and blue and always burning. *The water is down. The valley is open. Come and see.* And they came. Not many, not at first. An old man and his grandson poling a dugout up the brown shallows. Two women on foot from the east fork, one of them carrying a tin box she wouldn't let go of. A family in a freight canoe with a tarp for a sail. By the second day there were thirty of them camped on the gravel flat above the church, and by the third day there were sixty, and they kept arriving the way water finds a low place.

They were all old, mostly. That was the first thing I noticed. The second thing was that the ones who weren't old had been brought by the old ones, grandchildren and great-nephews, kids my age who kept looking at the valley like it was a story they'd been told at bedtime and had stopped believing.

An old woman named Ingrid Solberg — the ledger said her father was the first name on it — took my hand on the second evening without asking, turned it over, and looked at my palm like she was reading it.

"You have his hands," she said.

"Whose?"

"Elias Vane's. He had hands like a girl's, all his life. Delicate. Terrible at rope work." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile I've ever seen on a living face. "He was at my father's funeral. Before. Before any of it. He stood at the back and he cried, and I never understood why until now."

I didn't tell her I'd read the confession. I just held her hand back, and we stood there looking at the water, and after a while she let go and went to help with the fire, and I understood that this was how it was going to be. Not one big accounting. A hundred small ones. Hand to hand.

* * *

Freya found me at dawn on the third day, sitting on the church foundation with the tin box Ingrid had finally opened — photographs, dozens of them, water-stained but alive, faces looking up out of the past like fish rising to a lure.

"You slept out here?"

"Didn't sleep."

"Yeah." She sat down next to me, knees cracking, and looked out at the valley. "I buried the compass at the cairn yesterday," she said, apropos of nothing, or of everything. "Maren came with me. We didn't talk much. It was — " She stopped. Started again. "You know what the weird thing is? I spent my whole life thinking that thing was pointing me somewhere. North, home, away, whatever. Turns out it was just pointing at her the entire time. All those years I thought I was lost, I was actually — " She made a gesture that took in the valley, the mud, the surfacing town, me. "I was actually just early."

"That's a terrible metaphor."

"I'm a pilot. All my metaphors are terrible. That's why I fly." She bumped my shoulder with hers. "Maren wants to know if you're ready. The old ones are asking. Ingrid says it has to be done while the light's on the water."

"Has to be what done?"

"Noor." Freya turned and looked at me straight on, and her face had none of the joke in it. "You memorized two hundred and eleven names. You're the only one who did. The paper's gone — it went into the flood with everything else, and honestly, paper was always the wrong place for it. Paper burns. Paper floods." She tapped my sternum with one finger, once, gently. "It lives here now or it doesn't live anywhere. Ingrid says that's how it was always done, before. Names read aloud, every spring, on the ice. The town had a tradition. The company knew about it. That's partly why they flooded the place — a record that lives in mouths can't be confiscated."

My chest felt tight. "I'll get them wrong. Some of them. The handwriting — "

"You'll get some wrong. And the old ones will correct you, and that's the point. That's the whole point, Noor. A record that lives in mouths is a record that gets argued with. It stays alive because it stays contested. The moment it's perfect and fixed and written down, it's dead, and dead things can be buried." She stood up and offered me her hand. "Come on. The light's on the water."

* * *

They gathered on the gravel flat where the mill yard had been.

I know that because Ingrid told me, standing at my shoulder as the sixty-odd of them arranged themselves in a half circle facing the brown shallows. *This is where they stood when the company men came out of the office with rifles. This is where your grandfather stood too, at the back, with a truck key in his pocket.* The light came down the valley the way it does in the north in the long evenings, low and gold and lying flat on the water, so the whole drowned town was lit up like something on display. Rooftop ribs. The church steeple. The stub of the mill chimney, standing in two feet of water like a man wading out to his waist, refusing to turn around.

Maren stood at the edge of the group. Freya stood beside her. They weren't touching, but they were standing close enough that their shadows overlapped, and I thought: that's a start. That's more than twenty years got them. Freya had told me once, on the raft, that her mother's absence was the only country she'd ever been able to find on a map. Now the country had a person in it, and the person was old, and the reunion was going to take years, and that was okay. Some things you can't fly. Some things you walk.

Ingrid raised her hand and the crowd went quiet. Even the river seemed to quiet, the outflow over the broken dam dropping to a mutter.

"We read the names on the ice, every spring," she said, in a voice like dry birch bark. "My mother read them. Her mother read them. When the town drowned, we read them in kitchens, in church basements, in the backs of boats, wherever two of us were gathered. The company took the town. They took the mill. They took the mountain's insides." She looked at me. "They did not take the names."

Sixty faces turned to me.

I had a speech. I'd written one at 3 a.m. in my head, something about justice and memory and the arc of history, and standing there with the light on the water I understood it was garbage, every word of it, and I threw it away. I took a breath. The air tasted like mud and pine and the cold coming off the shallows.

"Alderman, Mikkel," I said. "Mill foreman. Shot in the mill yard, January the fourteenth, 1961."

My voice cracked on the first name and I let it crack. Ingrid had told me: *let it crack, girl, a steady voice reads a list, a breaking voice tells the truth.*

"Alderman, Signe. His wife. Resettled. Died on the road east, February 1961."

"Her sister took the children in," an old man called out from the second row, his voice wet. "My grandmother. The children lived. One of them is standing right here."

"The record is corrected," Ingrid said, formal as a judge. "Continue."

"Bakke, Oskar. Striker. Shot in the mill yard."

"Bakke, Anna. Striker. Shot in the mill yard."

I went down the list. The A's, the B's, the long middle of the alphabet where the Scandinavian names piled up like driftwood — Halvorsen and Henriksen and Holm, the mill families, three generations of them in some cases, and for each name somebody in the crowd had something. A correction. A survival. A place where the bones were, or where the children went, or a detail so small and so human it stopped me cold: *He played the fiddle. She was the one who could whistle through her hands. He was afraid of the water, and they drowned him anyway.* The record wasn't a list. It was a town. It had been a town all along, and the flood had only drowned the streets, and the streets were the least of it.

Two hundred and eleven names took three hours. The light went from gold to copper to the long blue dusk, and nobody moved, and when my voice gave out around name one-forty — Halvorsen, Petra, aged nine — Ingrid put a cup of something hot in my hand and I drank it without looking at it and kept going. Freya stood at the edge the whole time with her arms crossed, and I caught her once, around name one-ninety, mouthing the names along with me. She'd been reading over my shoulder at the source. She'd never said so. She'd memorized them too, or most of them, in secret, the way she did everything — competently, quietly, and then pretending it was nothing.

When I said the last name — "Vollan, Tomas. Company clerk. Turned witness. Died in exile, age eighty-one. Also known as Elias Vane." — the crowd didn't cheer. There was no cheer in it. What happened instead was that Ingrid Solberg walked to the water's edge, knelt down in the mud, and put her hand flat on the surface of the brown shallows, and one by one the others did the same, sixty hands on the water, and the ripples from each hand crossed the ripples from all the others until the whole surface of the drowned town was moving.

"The record is kept," Ingrid said. "The record is kept in mouths. Let them try to drown *that*."

* * *

Afterward, around the fire, the old ones told stories until midnight, and the young ones listened, and I sat between them with mud in my hair and the names still ringing in my skull like struck bells.

Maren came and sat next to me at some point. Up close she smelled like woodsmoke and fish and the inside of a cabin that has been lived in alone for a long time. She didn't say anything for a while. Then:

"Your grandfather came to me. Twenty years ago. Before Freya. He'd heard there was a hermit at the source keeping records, and he walked in out of the bush, old and sick and carrying his guilt like a pack he couldn't set down. He said, *I can't go back. But I can add to what you have.* And he gave me the pages he'd copied, and the confession, and he asked me one thing." She looked into the fire. "He asked me to make sure it reached his family. Not the world. His family. He said the world could wait; his family couldn't."

"He waited too long."

"He was a coward," Maren said, simply, without cruelty. "Cowardice and conscience are often roommates. They share a kitchen and never speak." She turned to me. "You're not a coward, Noor. I watched you read those names with a breaking voice. That took more spine than anything the syndicate did, in either direction."

I wanted to say something wry. It was the family disease, apparently — Freya had it, and now I could feel it in myself, the joke rising to meet the grief, the reflex of deflecting. But I was too tired, and the fire was too warm, and Maren's shoulder was right there, so I just leaned into it, an inch, barely, and she let me, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

Across the fire, Freya was showing a couple of the kids how to fold a paper airplane out of a page of Harkness's salvaged survey notes — *aim into the wind, not with it, that's the whole secret of everything* — and she caught me watching and grinned, and mouthed something at me across the flames.

I read her lips. *Not bad, kid.*

* * *

I woke before dawn on the fourth day to the sound of an engine.

Not a boat engine. I knew boat engines now the way I knew river water — by the pitch, by the load, by what they said about the hull beneath them. This was different. This was an aircraft engine, high and thin, and it was coming up the valley from the south, and my whole body went tight before my brain caught up, because the last time an engine found us in this valley it belonged to men who wanted things.

I got out of the tent. The gravel flat was gray and still. And coming down the valley line, low over the broken dam, flying the way Freya flew — which is to say like the landscape was a conversation she was winning — came a floatplane I didn't recognize. Not the Cessna. The Cessna was dead on a gravel bar two hundred miles south, may she rest.

It circled the drowned town once. Then it circled again, lower, and I saw the door open in passing, and something small and dark tumbled out and fluttered down into the shallows where the mill chimney stood.

A paper airplane.

Freya was suddenly beside me, still half-asleep, hair wild, and she watched the plane climb, turn south, and drone away down the valley, and she started laughing — a real laugh, deep and unguarded, the kind I'd only heard from her twice.

"That's Calloway's plane," she said. "Or it was. I'd know that engine anywhere. Which means either he's come to gloat, or — " she squinted at the southern horizon, where the plane had gone, and her grin faded into something sharper, something with an edge of the old dread in it, "— or somebody's flying Calloway's plane who isn't Calloway. And there's exactly one person I can think of who'd want this valley's attention one more time."

She looked at me, and the firelight was gone and the dawn was gray, and she said:

"Get your boots, kid. I want to know what that plane dropped before the water does."
