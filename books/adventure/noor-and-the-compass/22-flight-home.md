## 22. Flight Home

The compass went into the ground on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the day my mother used to bake bread and then forget about it, and the whole house would fill with the smell of something abandoned, and I'd spend the rest of the week hungry for a thing that was already gone. Burying the compass felt like that in reverse — like finally eating the bread instead of mourning it.

I dug the hole at the cairn myself, four feet down through muskeg and gravel until I hit permafrost and had to stop pretending I was going deeper. The brass case went in first, then the journal page Maren had written for me — the one explaining what the needle actually wanted, which was never north, which was always whatever you'd lost and couldn't stop looking for. I'd carried it for twenty years without knowing what it was carrying of me.

Noor stood at the edge of the pit with her hands in her pockets and watched me work, and when I asked if she wanted to say anything over it, she said, "It's not a funeral, Freya. It's a receipt." Seventeen years old and already better at grief than I'll ever be. She threw in a handful of river stones from the source, green ones, the color the drowned town glowed through forty feet of water before the dam came down and let it breathe again.

Then we filled it in, and I stamped the earth flat with my boot heel, and that was that. No ceremony. No last look. The needle could point at whatever it wanted now; nobody down there was listening.

* * *

Three weeks after the dam broke, I flew them out.

Maren sat up front beside me, which took some negotiating, because for twenty years the right seat had belonged to cargo — medicine crates, mail sacks, one memorable consignment of live bees that I still think about when I smell smoke. She folded herself into it like she'd been flying with me her whole life, knees drawn up, hands braced on the dash the way you do when you've spent two decades walking everywhere and your body has opinions about engines. She kept her window cracked the entire flight. "So I can hear the country," she said. "You forget, up here, how loud the quiet is."

Noor rode in the back with the ledger's ghost, which is to say with nothing at all except the names in her head, three hundred and eleven of them, memorized in order, recited nightly like a rosary she refused to call a rosary. She'd written them out twice since the flood — once in pencil, once in ink, because she said pencil could be erased and she wasn't taking any chances with erasure anymore, not from anyone, not even time. The ink copy went into a tin that had once held Vane's pipe tobacco, which Noor claimed as inheritance by right of being the only person alive who gave a damn about him.

The valley fell away beneath us the way valleys do when you leave them — slowly, then all at once. The reservoir was gone. Where the syndicate's green water had lain flat and lying for sixty years there was now a river running brown and honest through the bones of the town, and the rooftops stood in open air like teeth after braces come off, strange and pale and finally visible. People were down there. Real people, descendants of the scattered, arrived from every direction with shovels and cameras and lawyers, turning over the mud their grandparents had been promised was empty. I saw tents on the old main street. I saw someone had strung laundry between two chimneys, which struck me as either the most hopeful or the most defiant thing I'd witnessed all year, and I'd witnessed a dam explode.

Maren looked down at it for a long time and didn't cry, which surprised me more than crying would have. She just said, quietly, "They're home," and turned her face to the window so the wind could take whatever she was feeling out of the plane before it could settle anywhere permanent.

We climbed out of the valley on a heading I knew by heart and a destination I didn't know at all, and that difference, that specific difference, was the whole point of everything.

* * *

The coast town where they wanted to start over was four hours south and one fuel stop east, a fishing place with a cannery that still ran, which mattered to Noor, who had strong feelings about canneries and stronger feelings about working in one only if she chose to. We landed on a strip of crushed shell that the tide owned half the year, and the whole town turned out to watch, because that's what coast towns do when a bush plane arrives — they gather like gulls, assessing whether you're bringing anything worth stealing.

What I was delivering was a grandmother and a granddaughter and a tobacco tin full of names, and I'll be honest, handing them over at the edge of that runway was harder than any rapid, any crash, any night in a bear den listening to searchlights. Because for eleven days and then some, those two had been my crew, my cargo, my reason, and a pilot without a reason is just a woman with an airplane and a debt, and I'd been that woman long enough.

Calloway's debt was settled. I want to be precise about this, because precision was the one thing he respected. When he'd shown up at the dam house playing buyer for Harkness — smiling his collateral smile, telling me the debt was bait, that he'd been fishing for the ledger through me for years — I'd told him the truth: the ledger was in the river, and the river doesn't negotiate. He'd looked at me for a long moment with that expression he does, the one like a man doing arithmetic on your soul, and then the glacier calved early and the gorge ate his boats and his leverage and most of his composure, and by the time the water dropped he'd decided I was worth more to him free than broken. "Consider us square," he'd said, which from Calloway is practically a love letter. "And Fairweather — next time someone offers you a map, check who printed it."

There won't be a next time. But I appreciated the sentiment the way you appreciate a warning printed on a ride you're never getting on again.

So: square. Free. Standing on a shell runway watching the two people I'd flown into hell walk away from me toward a house with a blue door that Maren had already, in the space of one afternoon, filled with bread smell. Some things are genetic.

"You could stay," Noor said, at the fence line, not quite looking at me, the way you don't look at someone when you're asking them something you're afraid they'll refuse. "There's room. There's a spare room. Maren bakes too much anyway, someone has to eat it."

"I have a plane," I said, which is not an answer, it's a reflex.

"That's not what I asked."

I know. I knew then. And I stood there at the fence with the tide coming in under my wheels and let the question sit in my chest like a stone I hadn't decided whether to keep or throw, and eventually I said the truest thing I had, which was: "Not yet. But I'll be back Thursday. And Thursdays after that. I'm keeping the route."

Noor smiled the way she smiles, which is sideways, like she's letting you in on something you haven't earned yet. "Thursdays," she said. "Put it in the logbook."

I did. First entry under the new heading. Not cargo. Visits.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rootlessness: it isn't a condition, it's a habit, and habits don't break just because the reason for them does. For twenty years I'd flown away from every place that started to feel like it might hold me, because holding meant losing, and losing was the family business. My mother held nothing. My father — whoever he was, the question mark on my birth certificate — held less. I learned young that the only safe way to love a place is from a thousand feet, moving, committed to nothing but the next horizon.

The compass had fed that. That was the thing I understood standing over its grave: it hadn't just pointed at what I'd lost. It had made losing into a navigation problem, something you could fly toward, chase, circle, never arrive at. A needle that points at absence turns absence into a destination, and destinations are how pilots justify leaving. I'd been navigating by my own abandonment for two decades and calling it a career.

Burying it didn't fix that. Nothing fixes that. But flying the route back on Thursday fixed a piece of it, and the Thursday after that fixed another piece, and I am a slow learner but I am, God help me, a persistent one.

The coast town got used to me the way towns get used to weather. By the third week, the harbormaster saved me a mooring spot near the fuel dock without being asked. By the fifth, the bakery — Maren's, obviously, because of course she opened a bakery, the woman spent twenty years keeping records for dead men and her reward was finally getting to feed living ones — had my order waiting on Thursdays, which was two loaves and whatever experimental thing she'd made that morning, usually involving salmon berries and usually involving regret, though Noor ate it all and pronounced it genius, because that's what granddaughters are for.

Maren herself settled into the town like sediment into still water. She walked the beach every morning at low tide, collecting things — sea glass, mostly, and once a ship's fitting that turned out to be from a cannery tender built in 1911, which she researched obsessively and then wrote a paper about that got read aloud at the historical society meeting while I sat in the back row realizing, with a jolt, that my mother had been interesting this whole time and I'd spent my entire childhood thinking of her as a closed door. Twenty years of silence, and it turned out the silence was mine as much as hers. She'd stayed away to keep a record. I'd stayed away because staying away was easier than asking why.

We talk now. Not easily — there are still stretches of road between us where neither of us says anything and both of us mean too much — but we talk. On Thursdays, mostly, in the bakery, over bread, which is the traditional Fairweather medium for difficult truths. She told me about the day she found the drowned town, and why she let the compass choose me instead of coming home, and I listened without interrupting, which for me constitutes a personal best.

"Why give it to me?" I asked her once. "The compass. Why not throw it in the river yourself?"

She thought about it longer than the question deserved, kneading dough the whole time, which is how she thinks — hands first, words trailing after. "Because you were the one who left," she said finally. "And I thought maybe what you needed wasn't to find me. It was to find out that finding things doesn't fix you. Only staying does. And I couldn't teach you that from here. You had to fly the whole circle yourself."

I didn't argue. Arguing with Maren Fairweather about loss is like arguing with a river about which way is downhill.

* * *

Noor finished school in the spring. I know because I was there, in the second row, wearing the only clean shirt I own, clapping while a girl who eight months ago was gutting fish on a dock and refusing to mourn anybody accepted a diploma with the names of three hundred and eleven people folded into her pocket. She read six of them aloud in her valedictory speech — the youngest victims, children, the ones whose names would otherwise have gone with the water — and the gymnasium went quiet in that way gyms go quiet when everyone suddenly understands they're standing inside something larger than a graduation.

Afterward she found me by the plane. "I applied for the maritime academy," she said. "Navigation program."

"Of course you did."

"They teach charting. Currents. Reading water." She shrugged, that sideways shrug. "Turns out I already know half of it. Somebody taught me on a raft in a gorge while we were being chased by murderers."

"Somebody did," I agreed. "That somebody sounds extremely qualified."

"She said she learned it from her mother, who learned it from nobody, who just always knew which way the water was going." Noor paused there, and I watched her decide to say the thing she'd been circling. "Freya. When I get my papers. Are you going to disappear?"

I heard the question land. I heard it the way you hear a question that has your whole history inside it — not as words but as weight. And I stood there on the shell strip with the tide doing its patient honest work under my wheels, and I answered the way I should have answered at the blue door months ago, the answer I'd been flying toward without knowing it:

"No. I'm done disappearing. It's the one maneuver I've retired."

She made me prove it, of course, because Noor doesn't accept verbal contracts any more than she accepts pencil records. She took the logbook out of the plane and wrote her name and Maren's name and the address of the blue-door house on the inside cover, under the emergency contacts section, in ink, and she said, "These are your landing coordinates now. Anywhere, anytime, no notice required. If you go more than two months without touching down here, I'm filing you as missing and mounting a search, and I run searches the way I run rapids, which you know."

"Which is terrifyingly well."

"Exactly."

* * *

So now I fly. Same routes, same medicine crates, same gossip hauled from strip to strip like ballast that talks. The interior hasn't changed — Calloway still runs his little empire of favors out of the airstrip hotel, Harkness is still legally a rumor, the syndicate is regrouping under new letterhead and new lawyers, and the valley is full of people digging up history faster than anyone can decide what to do with it. There's a hearing scheduled in the fall about the massacre, about reparations, about whether the land goes back to the descendants or gets parceled off to the highest bidder, and Noor has been subpoenaed as a witness, and she has informed me — in the tone of a person announcing weather — that she intends to recite all three hundred and eleven names into the official record, slowly, and that the proceedings will simply have to accommodate her.

I'll fly her in for it. Of course I will. It's on the calendar, in ink, under VISITS.

But the difference, the whole difference, the difference I dug four feet down to buy, is this: every leg now ends somewhere. Every flight out has a return filed. When I land at the dying airstrips, the ones like the town where this all started, I still bring medicine and gossip, but I also bring news of the blue door — who baked what, who got into the academy, who testified — and people ask after them by name, and the asking feels like a kind of anchor chain, invisible, stretching from my wings down to a house on the coast that expects me.

Last Thursday, Maren handed me a loaf still warm and said, without fanfare, "Your grandmother's recipe. I found it in her letters. I thought you should have it back." I didn't know my grandmother baked. I didn't know there were letters. I stood in that kitchen holding bread made from a recipe older than my abandonment and realized the Fairweather women had been leaving each other things in the ground and in the drawers and in the dough for generations, waiting for someone to come back and dig.

I came back. I keep coming back. It turns out that was the trick the whole time — not the compass, not the map, not the source. Just the returning.

The plane's on the shell strip now, tied down against tomorrow's blow, and I'm writing this in the bakery after close, in the yellow light, with Noor quizzing herself on navigation formulas across the table and Maren proofing tomorrow's dough and the tide coming in under the town like a slow promise. In the morning I'll fly north, because there's freight waiting and a hearing to prep for and a valley full of rooftops that deserve witnesses.

But I'll file the return trip first. That's the new rule. Outbound legs get logged with their homebound twins, no exceptions, no one-way tickets, no horizons I commit to more than I commit to the blue door.

Somewhere out there, under four feet of muskeg and permafrost, a brass needle is pointing at whatever it points at now, and I hope it's pointing at nothing. I hope it's resting. Some instruments are only good for one journey, and theirs is over.

Mine, apparently, is just getting properly started — the kind where you always know, before you ever leave the ground, exactly where you'll set down.

The engine's cold. The bread's wrapped. Thursday's coming.

I'll be here for it.
