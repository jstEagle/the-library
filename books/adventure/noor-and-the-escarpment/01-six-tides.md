## 1. Six Tides

The river gave Marguerite Villalobos three things every morning: a smell of diesel and drowned leaves, a line of barges crawling north like patient beetles, and a job she was too good for.

She took all three without complaint. Mostly.

"Villalobos! The Marrow's winch is crying again," called Dockmaster Ferro, a man shaped like a bollard and about as sentimental. "And don't tell me it's fine. I heard it from the chandlery."

"It's not crying, it's singing." She wiped her hands on a rag that had once been a shirt and had ambitions of becoming a rag again. "There's a difference. Crying means it wants to stop. Singing means it wants to be listened to."

"And what does it want you to listen to?"

"That someone's been overloading it and lying to me about it."

Ferro had the decency to look sheepish, which for him meant he examined his boots with great scholarly interest. Marguerite climbed into the winch housing, greased what needed greasing, cursed what needed cursing, and by noon the *Marrow* was lifting salvage crates off a half-sunk grain barge like a mother picking up children who'd fallen in a puddle.

This was her life now, four years after the dig at Cañada Roja. Four years since the shaft collapsed and took her mother and, in a quieter way, took her father too — or rather, revealed what he'd always been underneath: a man who could stand at a funeral and talk about stratigraphy. She had walked out of the cemetery and kept walking until she reached water, because water didn't pretend to hold anything it couldn't hold.

She stayed near it. That was the whole arrangement. Docks, wrecks, salvage crews with their superstitions and their sunburned humor. She told herself it was practical — a woman who knew engines and channels would always eat. But Ferro, watching her read the river's moods the way other people read weather, knew better. You don't learn to love a thing's voice unless you grew up listening for it.

Her father had taught her the channels above the Escarpment when she was nine, drawing them in the dirt with a tent peg while her mother laughed and said he was raising a river spirit, not a daughter. He'd been right about the first part and wrong about the second, and Marguerite had spent four years making sure he never got to find out which parts were wrong.

Then, on a Tuesday smelling of rain that hadn't arrived yet, everything changed.

* * *

The man came down the dock at an hour when honest people were still asleep and dock rats like Marguerite were just getting philosophical. He wore a coat too fine for this stretch of waterfront and carried himself like someone who had been apologized to his whole life and found it insufficient.

She knew him before she knew his name. Everyone in her father's world knew that silhouette.

"Brandt," she said, not looking up from the carburetor she was rebuilding on an upturned crate. "You're a long way from your lecture circuit."

"Marguerite." Her name in his mouth sounded like a formality he was performing correctly. "You look like your mother."

That landed where he probably intended it to land. She set down her wrench with exaggerated care. "Say what you came to say."

"Elias has gone silent."

The word *silent* did something to the air between them. The river kept moving behind her, indifferent as arithmetic.

"He went up past the Escarpment six weeks ago," Brandt continued. "Survey work. Old wreck, pre-colonial, the kind of site he's chased his whole career. His last transmission was eleven days ago. Routine. Then nothing. No storm season yet, no reason for radio failure he hadn't already planned around. Your father plans around everything. It's his most infuriating quality."

"Was," Marguerite said. "You said *has*. Present tense. Interesting choice for a man reading a eulogy."

"I'm not reading one." For the first time, something cracked in the polish. "I'm asking for help, and I hate asking, so appreciate the theater while it lasts."

"Ask the fleet. They've got gunboats and sonar and a payroll."

"The fleet," Brandt said carefully, "is why I'm here and not them. A salvage consortium filed survey claims on that stretch last month. If they reach him first, they won't rescue him. They'll strip whatever he's sitting on and call him collateral. I need to get there before they do, and I need someone who knows the channels above the Escarpment better than the charts do. There is exactly one person alive who does, and she is currently insulting a carburetor."

Marguerite stood. Slowly. Wiped her hands with great ceremony, buying time, because her heart was doing something treacherous — hammering not with fear but with hope, and hope was a debt she'd sworn off four years ago.

"No," she said.

"Marguerite—"

"You think I don't know what you're doing? You come down here with his silence in your pocket and expect me to run upriver for a man who—" Her voice caught on the rust of old anger. "He didn't come to my mother's grave for two years. Two years, Brandt. He sent a letter about the excavation permits."

"I know."

"You know nothing."

"I know," Brandt said quietly, "that he keeps a photograph of you in every field kit he owns. I know because I've seen him repack those kits a hundred times over thirty years of rivalry, and the photograph goes back in first, every time, before the instruments."

The carburetor suddenly required her full attention again. She stared at it until her eyes stopped stinging.

"I'll think about it," she said, which in Villalobos meant *yes but I will make you suffer first*, and by the way Brandt's shoulders dropped, he spoke fluent Villalobos.

* * *

The boat was called the *Perseverance*, which Tam the deckhand insisted was either very hopeful or very ironic depending on the day. She found it moored at the derelict slip downstream, and it looked like a vessel that had survived several careers and remembered none of them fondly.

Odile met her at the gangplank. The pilot was sixty if she was a day, built like driftwood, with hands that had clearly argued with ropes and won most rounds. She looked Marguerite up and down the way one inspects a tool of uncertain provenance.

"So you're the channels."

"So you're the boat that's going to die on them."

A pause. Then Odile grinned, all gray teeth and no mercy. "Good. The last navigator we interviewed cried when I showed him the rapids chart. Get aboard, girl. Touch nothing. Ask questions. Eat what Tam gives you even if it moves."

Tam, appearing from below decks with hair like a startled hedge and a charm bag swinging at his belt, gave her a solemn nod. "I put salt on the threshold," he confided. "For the river spirits. Also there was ants. Both problems addressed."

"Efficient," Marguerite said.

"We aim to survive," Tam said. "Everything else is decoration."

They cast off at dawn on the third morning, when the tide turned and the fog lifted off the water like a held breath finally released. Marguerite stood at the bow with the channel maps she'd drawn from memory — her father's lessons made ink — and felt the strangest sensation: that she was going home and away from home at the same time, up a river she loved toward a man she couldn't forgive.

Odile worked the wheel beside her, reading her silences with the fluency of a woman who'd buried her own share of unfinished sentences.

"For what it's worth," the pilot said, eyes on the current, "you don't have to forgive him to save him. River doesn't ask you to love what you pull out of it. Just asks you to pull."

"That's the wisest thing anyone's said to me in four years."

"Don't spread it around. I have a reputation."

By afternoon the banks narrowed, the hills shouldered in close, and the water began to speak in the low, muscular voice of the upper reaches. Tam appeared at her elbow, uncharacteristically pale, holding his radio handset like it had bitten him.

"There was a broadcast," he said. "Faint. Fleet band, encrypted short-burst. Coming from somewhere... close."

Marguerite looked at the crew — Odile grim at the wheel, Tam clutching his charms, Brandt studying the shoreline a shade too intently — and felt the first cold thread of doubt slide under her ribs.

Behind them, far downriver, a black hull had just cleared the headland, running their wake.

Six tides, she thought. We have six tides.

She hoped to God they'd be enough.
