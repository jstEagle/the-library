## 2. The River Keeps Its Dead

The river woke us before dawn, slapping the hull like a patient creditor. Odile was already at the wheel, coffee thermos wedged between her knees, muttering at the fog the way she muttered at everything — as if the world were a slow apprentice who ought to know better.

"Channels today," she said, not looking at me. "Your channels. The ones only you know."

I did know them. My father had taught me them when I was eleven, calling out depths from the bow while my mother sketched the banks in her field journal, and I had hated every minute of it because it meant I couldn't swim. Now I could swim, and I hated it for a different reason: every bend of this water was a memory with teeth.

Tam crouched at the bow with a sounding pole, cheerful as a man going to church. He wore his grandmother's red scarf knotted at his throat and claimed the river respected the color. "The dead don't take what's marked," he told me solemnly, patting it. I didn't argue. On this river, I'd learned to let people keep their armor.

Brandt sat amidships with the radio log open on his lap, transcribing my father's final transmissions in a small, careful hand. That was how we'd found the trail at all — Odile had pulled the old survey frequencies out of storage, and there he'd been, week after week, faint as breath on glass.

* * *

**RADIO LOG — SURVEY VESSEL *ARAGUAY* — DAY 41**

*"…position holding above the second narrows. Water's down half a meter from the charts. Brandt, if you're hearing this — and I know you are, you old vulture — the escarpment site is real. The strata match. I should have listened to you twenty years ago. Marguerite, if you're listening: the river keeps its dead, but it also keeps its promises. Come at the sixth tide. Not before. Not after."*

His voice had sounded tired that day. Tired and lit up from inside, the way he used to sound when he forgot to be disappointed in me.

* * *

The first rapids came an hour past sunrise, white water combing over black stone like sugar frosting on slate. Odile took us through with the calm of a woman threading a needle in an earthquake, jaw tight, hands loose. Tam whooped. I gripped the rail and called the channel markers from memory — *left at the split, hug the drowned cedar, do not trust the eddy that looks friendly* — and each call felt like speaking to my father across twenty years of silence.

We made the second narrows by noon. That was where Tam found the fuel problem.

"Odile." His voice went flat, which for Tam was an alarm bell. "Odile, come look."

She looked. Then she swore in three languages, one of which I suspect she invented. The auxiliary tank's line had been cut — not torn, not chafed, *cut*, clean as surgery — and worse, the reserve drums stowed aft had been opened and bled into bilge rags. Someone had been tidy about it. Someone had wanted us stranded quietly, far from anywhere, at exactly the wrong hour.

Nobody spoke. The river spoke instead, sliding past, indifferent.

"Who had access?" Odile finally said.

"We all did," I said. "That's the point."

Brandt stood very still, which was unlike him; usually he moved like a man perpetually late for an argument. His eyes went to the radio shack, then away. I noticed. I filed it. Filing things is the one inheritance from my father I've never managed to lose.

We limped on, rationing fuel, running the engine just enough to hold current. It was during the third hour of that tense, whispering progress that the river decided to test us properly.

* * *

**RADIO LOG — DAY 43**

*"…salvage fleet sighted below us. Three hulls, running lights dark. They're not surveying anything. They're following someone's coordinates. I've killed our beacon. Whoever is feeding them — I hope the money warms you. Marguerite, the tide tables say five days now. Five days, and then the chamber floods for good. Everything your mother catalogued goes under. I can't move it alone. I'm not even sure I can move myself. The collapse took my left leg. Don't tell her that part. Tell her the rest."*

* * *

The fifth rapid nearly kept its promise about the dead.

It came at us around a blind bend, a standing wave taller than Tam, and Odile hit it wrong — or right, depending on whether you ask her or physics. The boat bucked. Tam, forward with his pole, went over the side like a coin off a table.

I heard the scarf go silent. That's what I remember most — not the shout, but the sudden absence of him, the cheerful noise of the man simply gone.

He surfaced once in the boil, arms windmilling, and the river began doing what it does: pulling him toward the undercut where the water goes under the rock and doesn't come back. I'd seen that undercut work before. I knew its schedule better than I knew my own heartbeat, because my father had made me memorize it while other children learned multiplication tables.

"The eddy!" I shouted. "Behind the splitter rock — the backwash recirculates! Tam — SWIM FOR THE ROCK, NOT THE SHORE!"

He either heard me or the river grew briefly merciful. He clawed sideways, against every instinct screaming at him to fight downstream, and caught the seam of slack water behind the splitter. The backwash held him, turning him slowly like laundry, and I was already over the rail with a coiled line, Odile swearing behind me, holding the boat in the eddy's edge with engines that had no business still running.

I threw twice. The first coil fell short. The second landed across his chest, and he grabbed it with both hands and the desperation of a man remembering his grandmother's scarf was not, in fact, waterproof protection.

We hauled him aboard. He lay on the deck plates coughing river, shivering, alive, and when he could speak he said, "You called the eddy. Nobody calls the eddy. Only family knows the eddy."

"I grew up here," I said.

"No," Tam said, gripping my wrist with a wet hand. "Only *family* knows the eddy."

Odile handed him a blanket and pretended not to have feelings, which is how she has feelings. Brandt poured whiskey into Tam's coffee without asking, which is how *he* has feelings. And something settled over the boat that hadn't been there that morning — some quiet agreement, unsigned, that we were crew now in the way that matters, the way that gets tested by undercut rocks and cold water.

Which made what I found that evening so much worse.

* * *

I wasn't snooping. I want that on the record. I was looking for the spare antenna wire to patch the mast, and the radio shack was the logical place, and Brandt's logbook was sitting open on the desk beside the set.

Beside the set that was warm. Tuned to a frequency nobody had authorized. A frequency I recognized, because I'd spent two years working salvage docks and you learn the fleet's working bands the way you learn a scar.

There was a notebook next to it. Coordinates. Our coordinates, dated daily, in handwriting I almost didn't recognize because it was neater than Brandt's usual scrawl — careful, deliberate, the handwriting of a man composing messages rather than recording them.

I read three entries. Then I read the last page, and my hands stopped shaking and started burning.

Because the last page wasn't coordinates. It was arithmetic. Fuel consumption, distance to the escarpment, tonnage of the fleet's nearest hull — and a figure circled twice, underlined, annotated in the margin: *"enough to cover their toll if they turn us through the third narrows. Enough to buy passage. Enough to buy all of us passage."*

I stood in that little shack a long time, the river sliding dark beneath the hull, holding a man's secret in my hands. The traitor who'd cut the fuel line — or the man bargaining with pirates to save us? Both? Had the sabotage been his, to make the bargain urgent? Or someone else's entirely, someone using his transmissions as cover?

Above me, Tam laughed at something Odile said, warm and alive and trusting, and I understood suddenly why my father had written in his log: *the river keeps its dead, but it also keeps its promises.* Everything on this water comes back around. Every debt. Every betrayal. Every kindness.

I closed the notebook and put it back exactly as I'd found it, down to the angle of the pen across the page. Then I climbed to the deck and watched Brandt at the stern, smoking in the dark, and made myself a promise older than any of his bargains:

Before the sixth tide, I would know which man he was. And whatever the answer, the river would collect.
