## 3. What the Water Remembers

The radio crackled awake at dawn, and Marguerite heard her father's voice in it the way you hear a dead man's voice in a seashell — faint, warped, unmistakably his.

*"...holding position. Chamber's flooding faster than expected. If anyone receives this — I'm sorry about your mother, Margie. I never told you what really happened down there."*

She stood on deck with the handset pressed to her ear long after the transmission dissolved into static, as if she could squeeze one more sentence out of it. Odile leaned against the wheelhouse door, coffee mug steaming, watching her with the patient skepticism of a woman who had ferried enough dreamers to know when one was about to do something foolish.

"He's alive," Marguerite said.

"Maybe." Odile shrugged. "Radios lie. Rivers lie worse."

"Then we go anyway."

Tam appeared with a coil of rope over one shoulder and a look of profound religious dread on his face. "The sixth tide," he said, as though announcing a plague. "Today is the sixth tide. The water remembers everything, Miss Villalobos. Everything."

"Then let's give it something worth remembering."

* * *

They found Brandt on the foredeck at first light, hunched over the radio like a man confessing to a priest who couldn't absolve him. Marguerite had spent two sleepless nights turning the transmissions over in her mind — the coded bursts, the coordinates, the way he always keyed the mic when he thought no one was watching. She had rehearsed this confrontation a dozen times: the fury, the accusations, the satisfying moment where she'd throw him off the boat at the next lock.

Instead she found him crying.

"I'm not selling us out," he said, before she could say anything at all. His hands shook around the handset. "I want you to understand that. I've been talking to the *Corvane*'s fleet since the second day. Trading them our position — a little at a time, nothing they could act on fast enough to matter — because it's the only currency they accept."

Marguerite's jaw tightened. "You led them to us."

"I bought us passage." He looked up at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and honest in a way that made anger feel suddenly beside the point. "Your father and I hated each other for thirty years, girl. Do you know why? Because we were the same kind of fool. We both believed the work mattered more than the people doing it. I watched him bury your mother's memory under survey grids and grant applications, and I swore I wouldn't die the same way. But here I am, on a boat, going upriver after him, because some part of me still thinks he can be saved from himself." He exhaled, ragged. "So yes. I bargained. With pirates. Because the alternative was arriving at that escarpment with a salvage crane already parked over the site, and no one left alive to argue with them."

Odile, who had materialized behind Marguerite with the silent inevitability of weather, said only: "Did it work?"

"It bought us until the sixth tide," Brandt said. "No longer. After that, they take what they came for, and they don't care whose blood gets in the water."

Tam crossed himself with rope-burned fingers. "The water remembers," he murmured. "But men forget. That's the trouble."

Marguerite looked at Brandt a long moment. Then she took the handset out of his shaking hands and set it down gently, the way you set down something that might still go off.

"When we get there," she said, "you're diving with me."

* * *

The Escarpment rose out of the river like the spine of some drowned animal — black basalt veined with white mineral run-off, the flood-stage water churning brown against its base. The dig entrance gaped halfway up the rock face, half-swallowed by the rising tide, a dark mouth filling drop by drop.

And there, anchored in the channel below like a vulture that had learned patience, sat the *Corvane*'s salvage barge. Cranes folded. Lights off. Waiting.

"They'll move at high water," Odile said, cutting the engine and letting the current carry them into the shadow of an overhang. "Not before. Even pirates respect the river's schedule."

"Then we have until the tide turns." Marguerite was already pulling on the dive suit, her hands moving through the familiar ritual — straps, buckles, the weight belt her mother had once worn, its lead stamped with initials no one else would recognize. "Brandt, you're on the surface line. Tam, keep the tender ready. Odile—"

"I know my job," Odile said, and put the boat where she wanted it, which was exactly nowhere, invisible against the rock.

The water swallowed Marguerite whole. Below the brown churn, the river went green-black and cold, and the flooded dig opened beneath her like a cathedral — corridors her father had cut through silt and stone twenty years ago, now drowned, now collapsing, the ceiling shedding grit in slow gray snowfalls. Her light found the survey markers still nailed to the walls, her mother's handwriting on the old tags: *Grid 4. Grid 5. Here.*

Here. Where it happened. Where the river had taken her mother, and where her father had gone back every year since, as if grief were a dig site you could excavate if you just kept digging.

She heard him before she saw him — a tapping, rhythmic, deliberate. Three taps, pause, three taps. The distress signal he'd taught her when she was nine years old, sitting on the dock with her legs in the water, back when the lessons felt like love instead of inheritance.

Her light swung into the last chamber and found him.

Elias Villalobos sat wedged beneath a fallen lintel of stone, one leg pinned, his face gray with exhaustion and something older than exhaustion. In his arms, wrapped in a tarp and clutched against his chest like a newborn, was the artifact crate.

"Daddy," she said into the regulator, and the word came out as bubbles, shapeless, but he heard it anyway. His eyes found her light and filled.

"You came," he whispered. "I knew you'd come. I told them — I told everyone — my daughter knows these channels better than God does."

"The ceiling's coming down," she managed. "We have minutes. Maybe less. The tide's rising — the chamber will be fully flooded before we're clear of the corridor."

He looked down at the crate. Then at her. And Marguerite watched, in real time, thirty years of a man's life arrange themselves into a single terrible arithmetic.

"I can't lift the beam alone," he said. "If you help me lift it, the crate goes in the mud. It stays. The tide takes it. Twenty years of work — your mother's work — gone."

"If we try to save both, the ceiling finishes falling, and we both stay too."

His breath caught. He held the crate tighter, and for one awful moment Marguerite saw the man she had resented her whole adult life: the collector, the keeper, the fool who believed things could hold people together. Then something shifted in his face — something she recognized, suddenly, horribly, as her own expression in mirrors. Pride. Grief. The same stubbornness wearing different clothes.

"Take it," he said hoarsely. "I'll hold the crate. You get free. Tell your mother—"

"Shut up." Marguerite swam to the beam, set her shoulder against the wet stone, and pushed. It groaned, lifted an inch, no more. "You're coming out. Now. Leave the crate."

"I can't—"

"*Daddy.*" Her voice cracked through the water like a whip. "Mom is not in that box. Mom is in *me*, and I am leaving, and so are you, or I swear to you I will come back down here every year of my life and dig you out of the silt the way you dug for her, and neither of us will ever rest. Drop it."

The ceiling shed another load of grit. The water around them shivered.

And Elias Villalobos — who had never abandoned a trench, a theory, or an argument in his life — opened his arms and let the crate sink into the mud.

* * *

They broke the surface together as the sixth tide reached its throat, gasping, hauling each other into the tender while Tam screamed encouragement in three languages and none of them words. Behind them, deep under the escarpment, the chamber finished its collapse with a sound like the river clearing its own throat — swallowing the corridor, the grid markers, the crate, twenty years of a family's history in one indifferent gulp.

On the surface, the *Corvane*'s barge lit up like a carnival. Cranes unfolding. Floodlights swinging across the water.

Brandt stood at the rail, radio in hand, and spoke into it one last time — not coordinates this time, but a warning, loud and clear and in the open: *"Site's collapsed. Nothing left but mud and ghosts. Come spend your money dredging silt."*

The cranes paused. Considered. Somewhere aboard the barge, a man did arithmetic of his own.

Marguerite pulled her father's arm over her shoulders and felt how light he was, how broken, how *alive*. He was crying without any attempt to hide it, and between sobs he kept saying her name — not *Margie*, the child, but *Marguerite*, the woman, the equal, the diver who had ordered him to let go and been obeyed.

"There was a journal," he kept saying. "Your mother's field journal. I lost it years ago, I thought the river took it, I thought—"

"She didn't lose it," Marguerite said quietly, watching the salvage lights sweep toward them across the darkening water. "It was never lost."

Above them, the escarpment groaned again, and the tide, still rising, began to close over the place where the water remembered everything — except, perhaps, the one thing that mattered.
