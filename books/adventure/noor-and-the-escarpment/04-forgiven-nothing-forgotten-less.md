## 4. Forgiven Nothing, Forgotten Less

The salvage fleet came up the channel at dawn, all chrome and diesel arrogance, three hulls riding low with the confidence of men who had never once been told no by a river. We watched them from the bend above the Escarpment, Odile's boat tucked into the willow shade like a heron pretending to be a stump.

"Look at them," Odile said, spitting into the current. "Like vultures in dinner jackets."

"They earned it," my father said quietly. "I led them here. Every transmission I sent, every bearing I logged — I handed them the map."

He sat wrapped in Tam's spare blanket, gray as river stone, three days out of the flooded chamber and still moving like a man who expected the ground to betray him again. His hands, those famous hands that had coaxed a lost dynasty out of mud, were empty. The fleet's divers had spent yesterday hauling the crates up from the dig — the seals, the tablets, the burial goods of a people who had trusted the water to keep them. Everything he had given his life to. Everything he had, in the end, given his life *for*.

"Say something, Marguerite," he said. "Tell me I was a fool. You've had twenty years of practice."

I should have been angry. I had rehearsed anger for two decades the way other people rehearse speeches. But watching him watch the salvage cranes work, I found the anger had gone soft at the edges, the way driftwood goes soft, and what was left underneath was just a tired man and a tired woman and a river that didn't care about either of them.

"You were a fool," I said. "But so was I. I spent twenty years being angry instead of being your daughter. We're even."

"We are not even," he said. "You pulled me out of a collapsing chamber. I pulled nothing out of anything but my own pride, and the river kept that too."

Tam, who had been pretending to mend a net for the last hour so he could eavesdrop, looked up. "In my village we say: the man who loses his goat should not also lose the story of the goat."

"That's very wise, Tam," Odile said.

"My grandmother said it. She was very wise and also very rich in goats."

Brandt laughed from the wheelhouse — a real laugh, rusty from disuse. He had spent the salvage operation standing at the rail with his hat off, like a man at a funeral, which I suppose it was.

* * *

The fleet finished at midday. The lead captain, a broad man with a handshake like a trap closing, came alongside and offered my father a settlement — a finder's percentage, "given the circumstances," delivered with the delicacy of a man tossing coins to a beggar. My father took the envelope without opening it.

"Buy something with it," the captain said.

"I intend to," my father said. "Fuel. We're going downriver, and I'm told the channels are treacherous."

The captain glanced at me, at Odile, at the scarred old boat, and decided — correctly — that we were not worth a longer conversation.

After they'd gone, my father sat on the crate the envelope had come from and turned it over and over in his hands. "Twenty years," he said. "I told myself I was doing it for your mother. That if I found enough, proved enough, it would mean her death had bought something. And now it's all in the hold of that ship, and do you know what I feel?"

"Tell me."

"Relief." He said it like a confession. "The river has been carrying it for me and now it's someone else's to carry, and all I can think is that I never once, in twenty years, asked whether *you* wanted me to carry it at all."

"You never asked me anything," I said. "That was the whole problem."

"Yes." He looked at his hands. "Well. I'm asking now. What do we do, Marguerite? I'm an old man with an empty crate and a daughter I don't deserve, and the tide's going out."

I reached into my jacket.

* * *

I had carried it against my ribs since the second night on the river, through the rapids and the sabotage and Tam going over the side, through the dive and the collapsing chamber and the sixth tide coming in like a debt collector. A oilcloth-wrapped rectangle, soft-cornered, smelling of cedar and old rain.

My mother's field journal.

I had found it when I was nineteen, the summer after the dig, the summer I stopped speaking to him. I'd gone back to her study to hate her things in peace, and there it was — not hidden, not buried, not sealed behind any clever lock. Sitting on her desk, under the lamp she always used, spine cracked from use. She had never hidden it. She had simply known that neither of us would ever look at the ordinary places again, because we'd both be too busy digging for treasure.

The journal held three seasons of her own survey work. Not his. *Hers.* Her own notes on the Escarpment channels, her own sketches of the current patterns, her own theory — written in her quick slanted hand — that the wreck was not a trade vessel but a family boat, that the people aboard had been nobody famous, nobody royal, just a family going home, and that this was precisely what made them worth finding.

"She knew," my father whispered, turning the pages with the care he'd never shown the artifacts. "She knew and she never told me."

"She told you," I said. "It was on her desk. You just never came home long enough to sit down."

He made a sound I had never heard him make — half laugh, half something else — and pressed the journal to his chest the way Tam pressed his charms in a storm.

"The fleet took the tablets," he said. "The seals. The dynasty. And I find I don't — Marguerite, I don't mind. Is that terrible?"

"It's the least terrible thing I've heard all week."

"Because this—" he tapped the journal "—this is *her*. Not her bones, not her grave goods. Her. Her handwriting, her arguments with herself, her terrible sketches — look, she's drawn me here, she's given me an enormous nose—"

"She was generous that way."

"—and no salvage captain in the world can put a price on it, because no one else knows what it is." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and clear at the same time. "You let them take everything and kept the only thing that mattered. When did you get wise?"

"I had a good teacher. She just wasn't you."

He laughed — actually laughed — and then he did something he had not done since I was small enough to be carried: he leaned his shoulder against mine and left it there, and we sat like that while the river ran gold with evening.

"Forgiven nothing," he murmured. "Forgotten less."

"Don't get poetic on me. I'll throw you back in the river."

"Promise?"

* * *

We went downriver the next morning, Odile at the wheel, the channels easy now because I carried my mother's notes in my breast pocket and read the water through her eyes. Tam had strung charms along the rail — "for the goat," he explained, unhelpfully. Brandt stood at the bow with my father, two old rivals arguing cheerfully about a dig neither of them would ever run, and every few minutes one of them would laugh and the other would pretend he hadn't.

Odile leaned over to me at the wheel. "So. Family."

"Something like that."

"Good. Because I've decided you're all terrible passengers and I'm keeping you anyway." She nodded downriver, where the channel widened toward home. "There's a wreck two days south. Small one. Nobody famous. Probably just a family going somewhere."

I looked at my father, laughing in the spray. I looked at my mother's journal, warm against my ribs.

"Odile," I said, "plot us a course."
