## 1. Twelve Miles Down

The gauge read one hundred and forty meters, and Ulyana Nakamura had counted every meter of it.

Not because she had to. The dive computer did that work for her, ticking off depth in green digits against the black of her wrist display. She counted anyway. It was a habit older than her memory — twelve years old now, though she'd stopped being able to say when it started, because the years before the accident were a room with the furniture removed. She knew the shape of the room. She knew where the windows had been. But when she reached for what stood there, her hands closed on air.

So she measured instead. Miles on the surface, meters below it, minutes between breaths. A life in units. It was not the same as a life, but it was close enough to navigate by, and navigation was the only thing she'd ever trusted completely.

The wreck came up out of the gloom like a decision someone else had made long ago.

She'd seen the sonar images for three days before the dive: a hull profile lying on its port side across a basalt shelf at the edge of a deep-water trench, broken amidships, its superstructure collapsed into a tangle that looked almost organic on the imaging screen, like something grown rather than built. The client's file called it *decommissioned naval vessel, cause of loss unknown*. Four words that managed to be both precise and useless. Ulyana had read enough salvage manifests to know that "unknown" usually meant "someone paid to make it so."

Devi went down first. He always did. Not because he was braver — he wasn't, and he'd be the first to tell you, at length, over dinner, with diagrams if you let him — but because his buoyancy was better and he liked to be the one who said hello to things. Ulyana followed thirty seconds behind on the descent line, watching his lights shrink from two white stars to one smeared glow, and let the water take her weight the way it always did, completely, without negotiation.

At eighty meters the light died. At a hundred and ten, the thermocline hit her like a door — six degrees gone in the space of a body length, the water turning from bathwater to something with intent. Her suit's heating elements kicked in and she felt them as a faint warmth along her spine, a reminder that somewhere above, on the support boat *Kestrel*, there were people who wanted her back.

Below a hundred and twenty, the world simplified. No current worth naming. No fish. Just her lights, her breathing — slow, four counts in, six out, the rhythm she'd drilled until it lived below thought — and the numbers. One hundred and thirty. One hundred and thirty-five. She said them aloud into her mask, quietly, the way some people pray.

"One-forty," she murmured, and the wreck rose to meet her.

* * *

It was big. Bigger than the file suggested, which was itself information. Naval vessels got mislabeled all the time — tonnage fudged, classes renamed, whole careers of paperwork bent to keep budgets tidy — but they didn't usually get *smaller* on paper than they were in the water. Someone had described this ship as a coastal patrol craft. What lay under Ulyana's lights had the beam and the keel line of something that had crossed oceans.

She found Devi hovering at the break, his lights playing across torn plating, and fell in beside him without a word. They didn't need words down here anymore. Eleven years of working together had compressed their vocabulary to hand signals and a shared silence that was, in its way, more communicative than most conversations Ulyana had ever had on land. Devi tapped his temple twice: *look at this.* She looked.

The fracture point was wrong.

Ulyana had seen ships die in every way the sea offered — grounding, fire, collision, the slow structural suicide of metal left too long in salt water. Wrecks told you how they died if you knew how to listen. The edges of torn plate curled outward or inward depending on whether the force came from inside or out. Explosions left their signature in spall patterns and burned paint. Collisions left paint transfers, sometimes even the other ship's ghost in a smear of contrasting color.

This break had none of it. The steel was parted clean, almost machined, the edges cold and bright where they weren't furred with rust. And running parallel to the fracture, etched into the hull plating in a line she could follow with her glove, was a seam of discoloration — a band maybe half a meter wide where the metal had been changed somehow, hardened or heated or *something*, in a pattern too regular to be damage and too strange to be manufacture.

Devi looked at her. She looked at Devi. Above their masks, invisible to each other, they raised the same eyebrow.

"Log it," she signed, and he did, camera flash strobing the dark in silent white pulses while she swam the length of the seam with her torch held close, counting rivets, counting frames, counting because counting was the rope she climbed down into herself by.

* * *

They worked the forward section first, per the task list: engine instrumentation, nav suite, anything from the bridge that might have survived. Standard recovery. The client — a maritime law firm acting for an insurance syndicate, according to the contract Devi had negotiated — wanted documentation of the vessel's condition prior to any liability proceedings. Boring money. Reliable money. The kind of job Ulyana took deliberately, the way you eat plain rice before a feast: not because you love it, but because it keeps you honest.

The bridge was a cathedral of silt. They moved through it slowly, finning with their hands more than their legs to avoid stirring the fine grey sediment that hung in the water like weather. Devi cleared the helm station; Ulyana took the chart table, peeling back a curtain of marine growth with the flat of her knife.

Underneath, fused to the tabletop by eleven years of corrosion, sat a document case.

She saw it before she understood it — a rectangle of darker shadow beneath the growth, too regular, too rectangular, holding its corners when everything around it had surrendered its shape. She brushed the silt away in slow strokes. Aluminum case, hard-sided, sealed with a compression latch designed to keep water out at depths considerably greater than this one. Whoever had put it here had expected it to be recovered. Or had expected it never to be.

Stenciled along the top edge, in paint that had faded from whatever it once was to a ghost-grey suggestion of letters, was a marking. Ulyana wiped it with her thumb, twice, three times, patient as erosion.

It wasn't a navy stamp.

Naval markings have a grammar. Hull numbers, pennant codes, the eagle-and-anchor crests of specific fleets, inventory tags in formats that follow regulations written by committees who genuinely believe committees matter. Ulyana had recovered enough military material to read that grammar fluently. This marking spoke a different language entirely: a corporate logo, geometric, a stylized hexagon enclosing something that might have been a waveform or a mountain range or a keyhole depending on how your eye landed on it, with a company name beneath in a sans-serif font chosen by a design firm, not a quartermaster.

She photographed it. Then she photographed it again from four angles, with a scale bar in frame, because that was procedure, and procedure was the skeleton she hung her selfhood on. On days when she couldn't remember who she'd been, she could at least be someone who did things correctly.

Then she hesitated.

There was no reason to hesitate. The case was on the manifest — well, the *category* was on the manifest: "secure storage containers, contents unknown." The client owned the wreck's salvage rights. Opening it was, if not exactly authorized, certainly not forbidden. Devi would have opened it already; Devi opened everything, on the theory that knowledge was heavier than consequences and floated better.

But Ulyana's hands stopped above the latch, and stayed stopped, and she examined the hesitation the way she examined everything — carefully, from multiple angles, looking for the fault line in herself.

It wasn't fear. Fear, she knew intimately; fear had texture, temperature, a taste like copper at the back of the throat. This was quieter. It was the feeling of standing in a room where the floor plan didn't match the building — the sense, wordless and total, that the story she'd been told about this wreck had a wall missing somewhere, and opening the case might show her what was on the other side of it.

Twelve years ago — or so she'd been told, by people whose accounts she'd had no choice but to accept — she'd been a different diver, on a different boat, with a past that included parents and a hometown and, allegedly, a sister. A flooding accident. A coma. Nine days her medical file called a "complete retrograde gap," as if memory were a ledger and someone had simply torn out the pages. She'd woken up in a hospital in a city she didn't recognize, with a face in the mirror she had to learn like a route map, and everyone around her had been very kind and very certain, and none of their certainty had ever quite fit together. There were gaps in the gaps. Stories that contradicted other stories. A sister who visited twice and then never again, and whom Ulyana could not, to this day, have picked out of a crowd.

So she'd done what she knew how to do. She'd built a life out of measurable things. Distances dived. Kilometers run on deck at dawn. Hours of bottom time logged in handwriting so consistent it looked printed. She kept the logs in waterproof notebooks, forty-one of them now, stacked in a locker on the *Kestrel* like the annals of a small, meticulous nation. If anyone ever asked her who she was — and people mostly didn't; divers understand — she would hand them the notebooks and say: this. This is who I am. Everything in ink. Nothing in ink is false.

The case sat under her hands, holding its secret the way the sea held the wreck: patiently, indefinitely, without malice.

She closed her eyes for one breath cycle. Four in. Six out. In the dark behind her mask, she watched the numbers.

Then she stowed the case unopened in her equipment bag, clipped it down, and signaled Devi: *found item, moving on.*

He gave her the signal that meant *you're not going to open it?* — a shrug translated into thumbs — and she gave him back the one that meant *not yet,* and he accepted it, because he always did, because underneath his chatter Devi understood her better than almost anyone alive, and possibly better than anyone dead, given that she couldn't vouch for the dead.

But she saw him look at the bag. Twice. And she filed that away too, in the part of her mind where she kept the things she wasn't ready to think about yet — a crowded district these days, though less crowded than it used to be, because every year she got slightly better at going in.

* * *

The rest of the dive was routine, and routine was a gift. They pulled the nav suite's core module, photographed the engine telegraph frozen at ALL STOP, documented the galley with its pots still racked — evidence, Devi said later on the boat, of a crew that had abandoned ship in order rather than panic, which fit poorly with a hull parted like sheet metal. Everything went into the log. Everything went into the notebooks. Depth, time, bearing, condition, coordinates corrected to three decimal places.

Ulyana surfaced at dusk, into a sky the color of hammered tin.

The *Kestrel* rode a low swell forty kilometers off a coast whose name changed depending on which government's chart you bought. Devi came up the ladder behind her, shedding gear, talking — he talked the way other men breathed, continuously, as a metabolic function — about the hull seam, about the cleanliness of the cut, about how in nineteen years of diving he'd never seen a failure mode like it and had he mentioned he once dove a wreck in the Sulu Sea that turned out to have been scuttled by its own navy, and did she think—

"—and the case," he finished, finally, pulling off his hood. "You're really sitting on it."

"I'm really sitting on it."

"For how long?"

"Until I know what we're sitting on."

Devi considered this. Devi considered most things briefly and then said them anyway. "You know the client's going to ask."

"The client can ask."

"You know what I mean. The insurance people. They'll want an inventory. Sealed cases are either assets or liabilities and nobody pays triple rate—" he checked himself, wincing at his own slip, "—nobody pays *good* rate for liabilities."

Ulyana dried her hair with a threadbare towel that had survived more wrecks than most crews did, and watched the light go out of the west. The monsoon season was building somewhere beyond the horizon; the barometer on the bulkhead had been falling all day, slowly, the way pressure falls before it commits. By the end of the week, she figured, this stretch of water would be unfishable, undivable, and largely uninteresting to anyone with sense.

"We open it tomorrow," she said. "On deck. Daylight, dry deck, cameras rolling, both of us present. Procedure."

"That's not procedure, that's theater."

"Theater is procedure with witnesses."

Devi laughed, and went below to argue with the cook about chili quantities, and Ulyana stayed on deck alone with the notebooks and the falling glass and the case locked in the dive locker below her bunk, directly beneath where she slept, which she told herself was coincidence and knew was not.

That night she wrote up the day's log in her careful hand. *Hull anomaly: linear fracture, non-ballistic signature, metallurgical discoloration band approx. 0.5m width, frames 12–31. Recovered: nav module, documentation case (sealed, unopened), misc. photographic record.* She paused over the last entry, pen hovering.

*Marking on case: corporate logo, non-naval. Identity unresolved.*

She wrote it, capped the pen, and sat for a while in the lamplight listening to the sea work against the hull. Somewhere under forty kilometers of darkening water, a dead ship lay on its side with its secrets arranged around it like furniture in a room someone had left in a hurry. Somewhere in the morning, a case waited to be opened.

And somewhere in the back of her mind — the furnished part, the part she'd built plank by plank out of distances and depths and correct procedure — a small voice noted, without drama, that she had felt that hesitation at the latch before. Once. Twelve years ago. On the last dive she could not remember.

She fell asleep counting. Not miles tonight. Heartbeats. It made a change, and changes were data, and data was the closest thing she had to a self that couldn't be erased.

In the morning, the case would give up its logo's name. And the name, when she ran it through the satellite terminal with the *Kestrel*'s aging uplink, would lead somewhere no insurance syndicate had any business being connected to a wrecked naval hull — somewhere inland, somewhere mountainous, somewhere the charts marked with contour lines and nothing else.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was only the count, and the swell, and the rain still holding its breath beyond the horizon.
