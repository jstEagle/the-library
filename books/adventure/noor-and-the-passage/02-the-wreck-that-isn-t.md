## 2. The Wreck That Isn't

The case came up on the second lift bag, and it came up wrong.

Ulyana knew the weight of things. Twelve years of salvage had taught her to read a load by its drag through the water before she ever saw it break the surface — the lazy pendulum of an empty ammunition crate, the stubborn verticality of a flooded engine block. This rose like neither. It came up balanced, almost buoyant, rotating slowly in the green murk as if it were being careful about how it arrived. When it cleared the water and Devi's winch took the strain, it swung aboard the *Marigold's* work deck trailing a curtain of seawater that smelled of rust and something else, something faintly sweet, like the inside of a new refrigerator.

It was a transit case, matte grey, roughly a meter long. Military pattern but not quite — the latches were civilian, aerospace-grade, the kind you'd find on flight-case equipment for a touring band or a satellite uplink crew. The gasket seals were intact. The pressure equalization valve on the end cap was still factory-stiff, which meant the case had never been opened underwater, never been breached, never flooded. Whatever sat inside had spent its time at forty-one fathoms in a dry pocket of nitrogen.

And there was no stencil on it. No NSN number. No navy property stamp, no serial prefix from any inventory system Ulyana had ever stripped parts from. Just a single mark, laser-etched into the composite skin near the handle: a hexagon enclosing a stylized wave, and beneath it, small enough that you'd miss it if you weren't looking for it, the word **MERIDIAN**.

She'd seen the logo once before, three years ago, on a pallet of sonar arrays being offloaded at a commercial pier in Colombo. She hadn't thought about it since. Now she found herself thinking about it very carefully indeed.

"Devi," she said. "Gloves."

Devi Osei came across the deck wiping diesel off his hands, his braids still dripping from the morning's first dive. He was a big man who moved like he was trying to take up less space than he did, and he had the particular patience of someone who had spent a decade working with Ulyana Nakamura, which was to say: he didn't ask questions until she was ready for him to ask questions. He pulled nitrile gloves from the box bolted to the deck locker and snapped them on.

"You want to open it here?"

"I want to look at it here." She crouched beside the case, running her palm along the seams. "Then I want to decide."

The *Marigold* rode at anchor two hundred meters off the wreck site, her hull rolling gently in a long swell out of the southwest. Below them, sixty-eight meters down on a shelf of grey sand, lay what the charts called *hulk, unidentified, decommissioned*, and what the salvage permit in Ulyana's cabin called, in bureaucratic shorthand, **SITE K-9 / NON-ASSET**. A decommissioned vessel. Struck from the rolls. Sold as scrap on paper eleven years ago and then, apparently, never actually sold, because here it still was, sitting in sovereign waters where nobody salvaged without permission, and nobody had given them permission except a broker in Singapore who paid triple rate and asked no questions.

Ulyana had dived the site twice now. The first descent had been reconnaissance: a hull lying on its starboard side, half-buried, its superstructure collapsed inward like a crushed tin can. No name on the bow — the plates had been ground off, deliberately, with a tool that left broad swirl-marks rather than the clean gouges of a cutting torch. The second dive, yesterday, was when she'd gone inside.

Wrecks tell you what they are if you know how to listen. This one had been talking all along, and Ulyana had been translating wrong.

Decommissioned ships get gutted. That was the whole point of decommissioning — the valuable systems come out, the sensitive ones get destroyed, and the hulk goes to the breaker's yard or the artificial reef program. What you do *not* find in a gutted ship is a combat information center with its console racks still bolted to the deck. You do not find cable runs, freshly terminated, their ends capped with waterproof connectors that showed almost no marine growth. You do not find a galley stocked with ration cases whose expiration dates postdated the ship's official death by four years.

And you do not find, welded to the inside of a compartment that should have been a laundry, a rack mount holding six empty slots and one occupied one — the occupied slot containing a black box the size of a shoebox, unmarked, warm to the touch even at depth, which was impossible, which she had told herself was her drysuit playing tricks, which she had cut free anyway because a diver who leaves a warm box sitting alone in a cold wreck is not a diver who works for long.

That box was in the hold of the *Marigold* now, wrapped in a wet cloth in a bucket of seawater, because Ulyana had decided that whatever it was, it shouldn't be allowed to dry out or power down or do anything at all except sit there and be mysterious. The MERIDIAN case was different. The MERIDIAN case had been sitting in an open passageway, strapped to a cargo cradle, positioned — and this was the detail that kept snagging in her mind like a hook — positioned as if someone had been carrying it toward the exit when the ship died.

* * *

They opened it on the aft deck, in full daylight, with the camera running.

This was procedure. Ulyana documented every recovery, every artifact, every hour of bottom time, not because anyone audited her logs but because documentation was the skeleton of a life. She measured dives in miles — distance covered, distance descended, distance back to the line — and she measured everything else in records. Bottom times. Air consumption. Manifests. If a thing wasn't written down, then in some sense it hadn't happened, and Ulyana Nakamura had already lost twelve years of happening; she guarded the remainder jealously.

"Manifest check first," Devi said. He had the folder open on his knee, the shipping documents their Singapore broker had provided, photocopied twice removed and stamped with an export authority's seal that looked right and felt wrong, the way counterfeit money looks right and feels wrong. "Item fourteen. 'Salvage of derelict materials, miscellaneous ferrous and non-ferrous scrap, est. 40 tons aggregate.' That's it. That's the whole manifest. Forty tons of nothing in particular."

"No line items."

"Not one. Which is how you move cargo you don't want described." Devi tapped the page. "You put 'miscellaneous scrap' on a manifest, customs reads the first three words and waves you through. Nobody inventories scrap."

Ulyana nodded slowly. She'd run cargoes like that herself, early days, before the accident — before the accident, she corrected herself, because there was no *after* in her memory to measure against, only a hard wall at age twenty-six and then white, and then waking up in a naval hospital in Kochi with a head wound, a missing year, and a diving certification that had somehow survived the gap intact. She built herself out of procedures after that. Procedures held.

"Cutting the latches," she said, and took the angle grinder to the hasps rather than picking them, because picked locks left evidence of entry and she wanted, obscurely, to be able to say the case had surrendered itself.

Inside, packed in foam cut with surgical precision, lay three objects.

The first was a hard drive array, eight bays, seven filled, each drive sealed in an anti-static sleeve. The second was a document wallet, waterproof, containing perhaps forty pages of printed material that Ulyana did not read yet, only riffled, the way you riffle a deck of cards to confirm they're all there — charts, tables, dense paragraphs of technical prose with headers she clocked and moved past: *RESONANCE SURVEY — PHASE II*, *Kharang Corridor*, *SUBJECT RESPONSE MATRIX*. The third object was a laminated card, the size of a credit card, holding what looked like a grid of numbers, and clipped to it a note in ballpoint, in handwriting too neat to be hurried:

**If recovered, contact M. Voss. Do not transmit. Do not copy.**

"Voss," Devi said. "You know a Voss?"

"I know a logo." Ulyana held the card up to the light without touching the surface. "Meridian Applied Systems. They do defense integration work. Sensor packages, signal processing. The kind of company that shows up in the middle pages of procurement journals and never in the news." She set the card down on the deck, on top of a clean rag, aligned square with the case. "They don't lose things, Devi. Companies like that don't lose things. Things like this get *placed*."

"Placed where? On a wreck at sixty-eight meters?"

"On a wreck that isn't supposed to exist."

She stood and looked west, toward the coast, invisible beyond the afternoon haze. Somewhere under that haze was a harbor where a naval patrol boat was based, the same patrol boat whose launch had passed them at dawn, slow and close, close enough that Ulyana had seen the crew on deck watching them with binoculars and not waving. She'd logged the encounter in her dive book the way she logged everything: *0640, gray hull, pennant number obscured by spray, course 090, made no sound signal.* At the time she'd filed it under routine harassment — navy launches liked to remind salvage crews who owned the water. But now, standing over an open case full of memory drives and survey documents pulled from a ship that officially did not exist, the dawn encounter rearranged itself in her mind into something with intent behind it.

* * *

The launch came back at dusk.

Ulyana heard it before she saw it — the flat, purposeful slap of a rigid-hulled inflatable running at speed, a sound distinct from fishing boats, distinct from the coastal freighters, a sound that said *government* the way a uniform says government. She was in the wheelhouse going over the day's video log when Devi called up from the deck: "Company."

The RHIB swung alongside the *Marigold* in a smooth arc, killing way at the last moment so that it kissed the hull rather than hit it. Two sailors in the bow, a coxswain at the stern, and standing amidships a lieutenant with a clipboard and an expression Ulyana would later describe, in her log, as *professionally unfriendly*. Not hostile. Hostility was honest. This was something more controlled, more rehearsed, a performance of authority by someone reading from a script he hadn't written.

"Lady and gentleman." The lieutenant raised a hand in something adjacent to a salute. "This is a restricted operations area. You'll need to depart within the hour."

Ulyana leaned on the rail. "We're operating under salvage permit SP-2219, issued by the maritime authority. Site K-9 is listed as open to licensed recovery."

The lieutenant looked at the permit when Devi held it up, and Ulyana watched his eyes move across it without reading it, the way a man reads a menu in a language he's pretending to know. "Permit's been suspended as of sixteen hundred today," he said. "You'll receive written notice through your agent. Effective immediately, no further dives on the site. No recovery of materials. Anything you've taken from the site is subject to forfeiture under the antiquities and state property statutes."

"State property," Ulyana repeated. "The site's registered as a non-asset. Decommissioned hulk. There's no state property on a non-asset."

"There is now." He said it flatly, without inflection, and the flatness was the loudest thing he'd said. Behind him, one of the sailors shifted his weight, and Ulyana noticed — because noticing was her profession — that the sailor's rifle sling was adjusted for quick release, and that the coxswain held the RHIB in position against the swell with an engine touch so precise it suggested he'd done this particular maneuver, at this particular kind of boat, more times than a routine warning required.

"We've recovered scrap," Devi said mildly. "Rusted plate, some fittings. Nothing—"

"You have a sealed transit case aboard." The lieutenant's eyes flicked to the aft deck, where the case sat under a tarp, and Ulyana felt the hair rise along her forearms, because he shouldn't have known that. There was no way to see it from the RHIB's deck. Someone had told him what to look for. "Meridian-marked material is classified under a technology control agreement. Possession is a criminal matter. Hand it over and this stays administrative."

The wheelhouse was quiet. The swell rocked both vessels in counterpoint, hulls groaning softly. Out at the horizon the last light was going copper, and somewhere beyond it, Ulyana knew, weather was organizing — the long-range forecast had shown a depression building over the coast all week, an early monsoon pulse, weeks ahead of schedule, the kind of anomaly that made fishermen nervous and made everyone else shrug.

"Administrative," Ulyana said. "Meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning a fine, a hearing, and your license suspended pending review." He smiled, and the smile was the worst part, because it was friendly, and friendliness from a man delivering threats is a kind of signature. "Or meaning a magistrate, if we have to come back. I'd rather not come back. Weather's turning. Even you can't argue with the barometer."

Ulyana looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the two sailors, at the rifle sling, at the coxswain's hands. And then she did the arithmetic she always did, the mileage arithmetic, the distance between where she stood and where trouble began, and she concluded that the distance tonight was short and closing.

"The case stays aboard," she said. "It's logged. It's documented. It's evidence of salvage conducted under a valid permit, and if your authority wants it, they can issue a seizure order with a case number I can verify. I don't surrender property to a verbal request from a launch that won't give me its pennant number."

Something moved behind the lieutenant's eyes — not anger; recalibration. He looked at her the way a chess player looks at an opponent who has just declined a trade. "Captain Nakamura," he said, and her breath caught, because he had her name, her *full* name, and nowhere on the permit was her name written, only the vessel's registration and the broker's agency. "Everyone on this water knows who you are. That's not surveillance. That's just reputation." He stepped back to the gunwale. "One hour. Then we consider you warned."

The RHIB peeled away into the darkening water, its navigation lights shrinking to red and green embers, and the wake it left rolled under the *Marigold*'s keel and out the other side, rocking her once, like a sentence being served.

* * *

Below, in the cabin that served as office, galley, and shrine to procedure, Ulyana sat with her dive book open and did the thing she had decided, thirty seconds after the launch disappeared, that she was going to do.

She wrote down coordinates that were not on any chart she was authorized to annotate.

Not the wreck's position — that was already logged, permitted, public to anyone who read the notices to mariners. These were other numbers. The bearing and estimated range of the launch's origin point, triangulated from its approach vector across two encounters. The exact phrasing of the lieutenant's warnings, transcribed verbatim while memory was fresh, including the pause before *there is now* and the smile. The fact that he knew her name. The fact that he knew about the case. And beneath all of it, in the cramped notation she used for things she didn't yet understand but refused to forget:

*K-9 not derelict. K-9 active or recently active. Meridian cargo staged, not abandoned. Navy aware, navy complicit or navy deceived — determine which. Broker's permit pulled within hours of recovery = someone watching in real time. Question: who told them what we lifted, and how did they know before we surfaced?*

She closed the book and pressed her palm flat against the cover, the way she did every night, a small ritual, a diver's superstition dressed up as discipline: *this happened, I was here, I am the one who remembers it.*

Because that was the thing about Ulyana Nakamura that no one who worked with her ever said to her face. She counted miles, she logged hours, she documented everything, because somewhere behind the wall in her mind was a woman who had lost the ability to trust her own past, and everything Ulyana had built since — the meticulousness, the caution, the habit of writing down coordinates she shouldn't keep — was scaffolding erected around an absence. She did not remember the accident. She did not remember the year before it. She remembered diving the way other people remember breathing, continuously and without origin, as if she'd been born at depth.

So she kept the coordinates. She photographed the documents in the wallet before resealing it — RESONANCE SURVEY, PHASE II, KHARANG CORRIDOR — and noted, with a professional's detached unease, that the survey grids referenced a mountain pass four hundred kilometers inland, in country she had never worked, in terrain that had nothing whatsoever to do with a sunken hulk off a tropical shelf.

A defense contractor's resonance survey. A dead ship full of live equipment. A navy that lied casually and threatened precisely. And a handwritten note naming a man called Voss.

Devi came down the companionway carrying two mugs of tea and the satellite phone, and set both on the chart table. "Singapore's calling back at 0600," he said. "Broker wants to talk about 'logistics adjustments.'" He paused. "That's what he said. Logistics adjustments. Like we're moving furniture."

Ulyana wrapped her hands around the mug and looked at the phone the way she'd look at a piece of ordnance.

"Before he calls," she said, "I want you to hear something." She opened the document wallet, took out the top sheet, and laid it flat under the lamp. A survey map, contour lines climbing into high country, a valley marked with a symbol she didn't recognize — a hexagon, the same hexagon as the logo, drawn in red ink over the pass itself. Beside it, typed in small letters: *Site designation: THE PASSAGE.*

"Passage to where?" Devi asked.

"That's the question," Ulyana said. "But it's not the first one." She tapped the map, on a cluster of buildings marked at the valley mouth, basecamp symbols, tents, a helipad. "The first one is this. The survey team listed here — nine names, nine signatures on the phase authorization — went in five months ago."

She turned the page. An interim report, dated eleven weeks ago, the last transmission received. After that, nothing. No extraction record. No casualty report. No search annex. Nine people, swallowed by a mountain pass, and not one line of paper saying anyone had gone in after them.

"And tomorrow morning," Ulyana said quietly, "a man in Singapore is going to offer us money to go get their equipment."

Devi was silent for a moment. Outside, the wind had begun to shift, coming around to the southwest, carrying the first smell of rain from a storm still two days over the horizon.

"Triple rate," he said finally. "He'll offer triple rate."

"He'll offer triple rate," Ulyana agreed. "And we're going to take it. Because whoever Voss is, and whatever the Passage is, that survey team has been missing for eleven weeks, and the only people on earth planning to go look for them are us — and we're only going because we're paid." She closed the wallet and squared it with the edge of the table, precise as everything she did. "Log the coordinates, Devi. All of them. If this goes wrong, I want the record to show exactly where we started."

He took out his pen. And far to the southwest, unseen, the monsoon took another breath and began, patiently, to gather.
