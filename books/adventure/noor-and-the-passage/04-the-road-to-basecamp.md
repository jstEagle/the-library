## 4. The Road to Basecamp

The trailhead sat at the end of a road that had given up pretending. Forty kilometers of switchbacks above the river valley, and then the asphalt simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when the speaker loses interest, and a dirt track took over — rutted, braided, occasionally swallowed whole by landslips that previous travelers had marked with cairns of stacked stone. Ulyana stood at the edge of it with her pack on her hip and her boots laced double, watching the mountains assemble themselves against the sky in ranks of blue and gray, each range behind the last a shade colder, a shade less certain.

She counted the distance before she did anything else. It was what she did. Twelve years since the accident, since the coma, since the blank years that followed, and she had learned to measure her life in miles the way other people measured theirs in birthdays or anniversaries or scars. Miles were honest. A mile was a mile whether you remembered earning it or not. She kept a small notebook in a ziplock bag in her chest pocket, and on its first page, in pencil, she wrote: *Trailhead to basecamp, 31 km by Tsering's estimate. Verify.*

Everything about this job would be verified. That was the clause she'd written for herself after the wreck, after the sealed case with the contractor's logo, after the navy launch that had come at them out of a fog bank with its guns manned and its intentions unreadable. She didn't trust the contract. She didn't trust the fixer who'd brokered it. She trusted Devi, mostly, because twelve years of shared rope and shared air and shared cold made a kind of ledger that no lawyer could forge. And she trusted the numbers. Distances, depths, pressures, barometric readings, the count of days until weather. Numbers didn't lie to you. People did.

Devi was already three steps up the track, testing it with his weight the way he tested everything — like a man who had once watched a deck plate fail under a colleague and never stopped expecting the world to do it again. He was broader than her, slower on the climb, better on the descent, and they had long ago stopped discussing which of them was the stronger diver because the answer changed with the water. He turned back and looked at her with an expression she knew well: the one that said *I have a bad feeling and I'm going to say it out loud anyway.*

"Triple rate," he said. "You know what triple rate means."

"It means someone wants this done badly enough to overpay."

"It means someone thinks we won't walk away halfway." He adjusted his pack straps and squinted up-valley, where the track bent around a spur of black rock and vanished. "The cognitive hazards clause. You read it twice."

"I read it four times." She patted her chest pocket, where the contract's copy rode folded next to the notebook. "It doesn't define the hazard. That's not sloppy drafting. Sloppy drafting happens when people don't know what they're doing. This is careful drafting. Someone knows exactly what they're doing and doesn't want it written down."

"So why are we here?"

Ulyana looked up the valley. The air came down off the high country cool and resinous, smelling of pine and cold stone and, beneath it, something faintly mineral that she couldn't name — a smell like struck flint, like the inside of a cave, like deep water where no light had ever reached. She noticed it the way she noticed everything now: catalogued, filed, unexplained. There were more unexplained things in her head than she liked to admit. Most days she managed not to think about that.

"Because the money is real," she said, "and because if there's survey equipment sitting in a government camp that officially doesn't exist, I want to see it myself before anyone else decides what it means." She started walking. "And because whoever erased those people might have left fingerprints. I've spent twelve years looking for mine."

Devi fell in beside her without another word. That was their rhythm. Everything important got said in under twenty words; everything else was carried in silence, and the silence held.

* * *

They met Tsering at the tea house at the top of the first ridge, a low stone building with a tin roof weighted down by rocks and a view south all the way to the haze where the plains began. He was already there when they arrived, which told her something; guides who arrived early were guides who wanted to be gone early. He was maybe fifty, built like the terrain itself — compact, dense, unhurried — with a face weathered into deep creases around eyes that did not miss much and forgave less. He poured them tea without asking, salted butter tea in chipped bowls, and watched them drink it the way a man watches animals he is deciding whether to trust.

"You're the divers," he said. Not a question. "The ones going to Kharang Pass."

"We're the salvage team," Ulyana said carefully. "The pass is where the work is."

"The pass is where people stop coming back from." He set the kettle down. "My grandmother had rules about that valley. You don't whistle near it. You don't camp in it. You don't say names out loud under the cliffs, because the cliffs keep what you give them." He looked at her, and something in his gaze snagged — briefly, almost imperceptibly — on her face, as though he were comparing her to someone. "Your fixer paid me through a bank in a country I had to look up. That tells me what your fixer is worth. What I want to know is what you are worth."

"Ask Devi," Ulyana said. "He's known me longer than anyone alive."

"That's either a good answer or a sad one," Tsering said, and drank his own tea.

Okafor arrived forty minutes later, out of breath, apologizing for being out of breath, carrying a hard case of communications gear that weighed nearly as much as he did. He was young — late twenties, maybe — precise in his movements the way anxious people are precise, everything squared away, cables coiled to identical diameters, a laminated checklist in a plastic sleeve clipped to his chest strap. He introduced himself twice because the first time he wasn't sure he'd done it right. When Ulyana asked him how long he'd been doing expedition comms, he answered honestly: two years, mostly on geophysical surveys, nothing above four thousand meters. Then he corrected himself, unprompted, because he'd realized the number sounded too small and he didn't want them to think less of him. Ulyana liked him immediately. People who lied upward were dangerous. People who lied and then couldn't stand having lied were just frightened, and frightened people could be steadied.

"Satellite window looks good," Okafor reported, spreading his printouts on the tea house table and weighting the corners with stones. "There's a system building over the coast. Early monsoon signature — the models are running it eight to ten days ahead of climatology." He tapped a swirl of pressure contours. "If this holds, we have a hard deadline nobody put in the contract."

"How hard?" Devi asked.

"Hard as in aircraft don't fly, passes close, and everything above the snowline belongs to the storm until October." Okafor's voice thinned slightly on *October*, and Ulyana noted it without comment. "Eight days. Maybe nine."

Tsering grunted. "The mountain will decide. It always does. Your satellites can argue with it if they like."

Ulyana wrote in the notebook: *Weather window: 8–9 days. Monsoon early. Confirm daily.* Underneath, after a moment: *Tsering distrusts valley. Grandmother's rules. Ask him later, alone, away from others.* Some questions you didn't ask in front of an audience. An audience changed answers.

* * *

The escort met them at the second bridge.

They heard him before they saw him — or rather, they heard the absence of him, the way the birds went quiet along the gorge and stayed quiet, and then a figure stepped out from behind a boulder with the unhurried inevitability of weather. He was tall, broad through the shoulders, dressed in expensive synthetic trekking kit that had clearly never been worn anywhere difficult, and he carried himself with the stillness of a man whose profession was being unnoticed until it was useful. No introduction. No name offered. The fixer's message had described him only as *escort, ex-military, do not engage in conversation*. He walked twenty meters behind them for the rest of the day, exactly twenty meters, and when they stopped he stopped, and when they ate he withdrew to a rock and ate facing outward, toward the approaches, like a sentry.

"What's his name?" Okafor whispered, unnecessarily, because whispering to a man twenty meters behind you was pointless.

"He doesn't have one," Devi said. "Not for us."

"That's not— people have names."

"Not everyone," Ulyana said quietly, and thought of the blank years, and said nothing else.

The escort bothered her in ways she couldn't fully articulate, so she articulated them in the notebook instead, that night, in the blue hour before sleep. *Escort: disciplined, watchful, armed (concealed). Trained. Not a guide. Security for whom? Us, or the equipment? Or us FROM the equipment?* She underlined the last question twice. It was the one that wouldn't sit still. If the contractor believed there was a cognitive hazard in that valley — believed it strongly enough to write a clause about it, to hire security, to pay triple rate — then the escort wasn't there to protect the team. He was there to make sure the team came back *correct*. Whatever correct meant to them.

She slept badly, in the way she always slept badly on the first night of an approach march, her body still calibrated to the sea, to the slow roll of swell, to the pressure of depth on the ears. The mountain silence was wrong to her — too total, no rhythm to it, no pulse. She missed the pulse. At night on a dive boat she could feel the ocean breathing against the hull, a long slow beat like something enormous asleep below, and she had always found it comforting, the way you find a heartbeat comforting even when you can't hear your own. Here there was nothing. Just cold air and stone and the stars turning overhead with mechanical indifference.

Except that wasn't quite true, and it was the thing that would stay with her when morning came. Around two in the morning she woke — not from cold, not from the altitude, but because of a sound. Or not a sound. A pressure. A faint rhythmic pulse in the air itself, felt more than heard, slow as a sleeping heart, rising from somewhere up-valley. She lay in her bag and listened to it for a long time, telling herself it was blood in her own ears, altitude, wind moving through a distant gorge. But wind didn't keep time. Blood didn't keep time at two beats per minute. She timed it against her watch, thirty seconds between pulses, steady as a metronome, and wrote the interval in the notebook by headlamp before she let herself doubt it. *0230. Pulse from up-valley. ~30 sec interval. Felt, not heard. Cause unknown.*

Then she lay back down and stared at the underside of the tarp and understood, with the particular clarity of the sleepless, that she had felt something like this once before. Deep. Deeper than any recreational table allowed. On the wreck, in the dark, in the corridor where the sealed case had been. A hum in the steel. She had logged it as hull vibration and moved on. Hulls vibrated. But hulls vibrated from engines, from current, from stress — and that wreck had no engines, and the current was slack, and the vibration hadn't been random. It had been patient.

She didn't tell Devi. Not yet. Some things you verified first.

* * *

The third day the track climbed out of the pine into birch and juniper, and the air grew thin enough that Okafor began counting his own breaths on the steep sections, visibly embarrassed by the habit and unable to stop. Devi ranged ahead with Tsering, learning the route, arguing amiably about water sources. Ulyana walked in the middle, which was where she always walked, close enough to both ends to reach them, far enough from both to think.

The escort kept his twenty meters. All day. Through two stream crossings, past a shrine of stacked mani stones where Tsering paused and touched each stone in turn and the escort waited outside the cairn's shadow as though it were a border post, up a moraine shoulder where the trail became a suggestion and the mountain became an argument. Twenty meters. Ulyana tested it once, deliberately, stopping without warning to re-lace a boot. He stopped. Exactly. She caught herself almost admiring it.

At the high point of the day's march they got their first look at the pass itself, and Ulyana stopped pretending she was thinking about anything else.

Kharang Pass sat between two walls of black rock like a door left ajar, a notch of pale scree and old snow perhaps six hundred meters across, and beyond it the range continued in ramparts of white and gray that no map in her possession rendered honestly. But it wasn't the geometry that held her. It was the light. The afternoon sun lay full on the pass mouth, and instead of glare the rock seemed to drink it, throwing back nothing, a rectangle of perfect matte darkness in a landscape of glittering ice — and as she watched, a cloud shadow crossed the upper slopes, and for half a second she would have sworn the darkness in the notch pulsed. Once. Slowly. Like something breathing behind a closed door.

"Optical effect," she said aloud, to no one, to test how it sounded.

"Probably," Devi said, beside her, also looking. Neither of them believed it.

Tsering had stopped ten paces back and was not looking at the pass at all. He was looking at the ground, at the trail, at anything else, with the studied concentration of a man performing ignorance. Ulyana filed it. Later, alone, she would ask him about his grandmother's rules, and she suspected that when he finally spoke about the singing stone his grandmother had forbidden, he would speak the way people speak about things they were told as children before they were old enough to disbelieve them — completely.

That evening they made camp in the last sheltered hollow below the treeline, a bowl of dwarf juniper with a spring and a wind-shadow and a view down the long valley they had climbed. Okafor established the radio schedule with the fixer's relay station and got a weather update that soured the evening: the coastal system had organized faster than modeled. Landfall estimate revised. Seven days. Possibly six.

"Six days to get in, find the camp, recover the equipment, and get back below the snowline," Okafor said, running the arithmetic aloud because the arithmetic was how he calmed himself. "That's tight. That's really tight."

"Tight is fine," Devi said. "Impossible is the problem. Which is this?"

Neither of them answered, which was an answer.

Ulyana checked the pulse before she slept. Same interval. Thirty seconds. Steady. Closer now, or louder, or perhaps she was simply more attuned to it, the way a diver becomes attuned to a regulator's rhythm until the rhythm disappears into breathing. She wrote the time and the interval and then, underneath, the question she had been carrying for three days and had not yet spoken:

*What keeps time?*

Because that was the thing. Machines kept time. Hearts kept time. Rivers kept a kind of time. But whatever was up that valley kept time the way a memory keeps time — patiently, perfectly, without effort, without decay — and Ulyana Nakamura, who had lost twelve years of herself to a blankness no doctor could explain, lay in her bag under the turning stars and felt that patience reaching down the valley toward her like a hand extended across a table, and could not have said, even under oath, whether she wanted it to arrive or feared it.

In the morning, Tsering was up first, as always, and when she crawled out of her bag he was standing at the edge of camp facing up-valley, tea untouched in his hands, steam rising past his face.

"It sang last night," he said, without turning around. "Didn't it."

It wasn't a question either.
