## 5. Gear Without Tracks

The rain stopped an hour before they reached the survey camp, and Okafor wished it hadn't. Rain was honest. Rain made noise, made the world small and legible, gave you something to blame when your hands shook. The silence that followed it — the foothills dripping, the clouds lifting off the valley like a lid coming off a pot, and beneath all of it nothing, no birdsong, no insects, no wind in the cedars — that silence was a different animal entirely.

Chidi Okafor had spent eleven years calibrating instruments for people who worked in places where the earth misbehaved: seismic stations on volcanic ridges, magnetometer surveys over old minefields, once a full month mapping electromagnetic anomalies around a decommissioned reactor site in a country whose name he still couldn't say out loud without his mouth going dry. He knew what wrongness sounded like. Wrongness had a texture. It was the absence of signal where signal should be, or worse — signal where there should be none.

He was hearing both at once.

"Radio check," Devi said behind him, for the fourth time in twenty minutes. The big salvager walked with his head slightly down, shoulders rolled forward, as if perpetually bracing against a blow that hadn't landed yet. "Okafor. You copy?"

"Copy." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "You're breaking up a little past the ridge line. Might be the terrain."

"Might be," Devi said, and didn't believe it any more than Okafor did. Neither of them said what they were both thinking, which was that this valley shouldn't have been degrading their signal. It was open. Wide as a stadium floor, floored with wet grass and glacial till, walled by slopes so steep the trees grew sideways out of them. A valley like this should carry a whisper half a mile.

Ulyana passed him without comment, her stride long and metronomic, counting something under her breath — he'd noticed it the first day out of basecamp, a low murmured arithmetic, *four hundred and twelve, four hundred and thirteen* — and Tsering brought up the rear with his rifle slung across his back and his face doing something complicated every time they crested a rise. The guide kept looking up-valley. Not scanning. Looking. The way you look at a door you've decided not to open.

They came around the last spur at mid-morning, and there it was.

The camp sat on a shelf of flat ground above the river confluence, six dome tents in two neat rows, a mess fly strung between two cedars, solar panels angled toward a sky that had finally agreed to give them light. It looked like a photograph from an equipment catalog. Every guy-line taut. Every stake driven true. The kind of camp that took forty minutes to strike and an hour to pitch properly, maintained by people who expected to come back to it.

Nobody was there.

"Hello the camp!" Tsering called, and the words fell into the silence and did not echo. He tried again, louder. Same result. Okafor felt his scalp tighten. Sound didn't behave like that. Not here.

Ulyana was already moving through the tents, and Okafor followed her because following Ulyana Nakamura was the closest thing to a protocol this expedition had. She opened each tent flap with the same deliberate care she'd shown handling the sealed case off the wreck — no hurry, no hesitation, gloved hands, eyes doing a systematic sweep before she touched anything. In the first tent: four sleeping bags, unzipped, folded back. In the second: clothing racks, boots paired at the tent walls, socks still draped over a line. Third tent was the comms shelter, and that was where Okafor's professional composure began to come apart at the seams.

Three field radios sat on the charging rack. All three showed full charge. The base station's log was still cycling. He stood in front of it for a long moment before he trusted himself to touch it, and when he did, the screen woke instantly, brightly, eagerly, like a dog that had been waiting by a door.

Last transmission logged: fourteen days ago. 06:12 local. A routine position report, voice, thirty-one seconds long. Signal strength excellent. After that — nothing. No distress call. No degraded battery warnings. No scheduled check-in missed and retried. The log just stopped, clean as a guillotine, and the equipment had sat there ever since, fully powered, fully functional, waiting.

"Fourteen days," Okafor said. He heard his own voice go thin. "Full charge. Full function. They had power, they had comms, they had—" He turned. "Devi. There is no scenario where a government survey team loses contact for fourteen days with charged radios and doesn't transmit. Not one. Even if they walked out, even if they were ordered silent, the log would show *something*. Traffic. Tests. Anything."

Devi had gone very still near the mess fly. "Food's warm."

"What?"

"Come feel this." Devi lifted the lid off a pressure cooker set into a rock ring at the edge of the fire pit. Steam curled up into the cold air, real steam, fresh. Inside: rice, still soft, still holding its heat. A pot of tea beside it, dark and strong, skin just beginning to form on the surface. Devi held his palm above the cooker the way you'd test a stove. "Warm. Not warm-from-the-sun warm. *Cooked* warm. This was hot inside the last hour."

Tsering crouched at the fire pit and did something Okafor had never seen a guide do: he picked apart the ashes with a stick, gently, like a man examining a wound. Then he looked up, and his face had gone the color of the fog on the ridgeline.

"The ash is cold," he said quietly. "Cold all the way through. Days cold. But the coals under the ash—" He reached in, fast, and pulled his hand back. "Hot. There are live coals under cold ash, and rice cooked on top of them, and nobody has been here."

"That's not possible," Okafor said.

"No," Tsering agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

They spent two hours documenting the camp, because that was the job, because contracts were contracts even when the world stopped making sense, and because keeping their hands busy was the only thing standing between the four of them and the shape of the thought they were all circling. Ulyana photographed everything with the same unhurried precision she applied to wreck interiors. Devi inventoried supplies — food stocks barely drawn down, medical kit sealed, fuel cans full. Okafor pulled data cards from the survey instruments and found the geophones had been recording continuously, right up until fourteen days ago, and then—

Then the data itself went strange.

He sat in the comms tent with his laptop and his headphones and listened to fourteen days of seismic channel, and for the first thirteen days it was exactly what it should be: microtremors, rockfall ticks from the high walls, the distant grind of the glacier up-valley, wind signatures, the faint rhythmic thump of someone walking near a sensor. Human footsteps, recorded at 2 a.m., someone pacing. Insomnia made audible. And then, on day thirteen, at 03:47, the footsteps stopped mid-stride — he could hear it, the left-right-left rhythm cut off between one footfall and the next, no stumble, no scramble, just *gone* — and underneath everything, rising slowly like something surfacing from deep water, came a tone.

Not an earthquake. Earthquakes had broadband chaos, a whole spectrum of ugly. This was narrow. Pure. A single frequency, low, below the range where it felt like sound and down where it felt like pressure, like a hand pressing rhythmically on the inside of your eardrums. It rose for ninety seconds, held, and then the recording ended. Not faded. Ended. The geophone's internal clock kept running afterward — timestamps continued, battery telemetry continued — but the data channel recorded nothing at all. Flatline. As if the ground had stopped existing.

Okafor took the headphones off and sat in the canvas dimness with his heart going hard and shallow, and became aware, gradually, that he could feel it. Not hear it. Feel it. Through the ground, through the soles of his boots, through the folding chair's aluminum legs humming faintly against his spine. A pulse. Slow. Patient. Roughly one swell every eight seconds, which meant nothing to him then except that it was too slow to be machinery and too regular to be geology.

He stepped outside. Devi was standing in the middle of the camp looking at the treeline. Ulyana was already walking toward it.

* * *

The footprints started at the edge of the camp and ran northwest, toward the pass, in a loose cluster — nine sets, maybe ten, boot treads of at least four different patterns, government-issue soles among them. They were crisp. The ground was damp clay loam, perfect for holding prints, and these had been made recently enough that water hadn't yet pooled in the heel wells. Hours old. Maybe less.

"They left this morning," Devi said. "On foot. Together. That's— okay. That's fine. Teams move camps."

"In slippers?" Ulyana said.

She was crouched over the prints, and Okafor came close enough to see what she meant. Half the tracks weren't boots. Some were sock feet. One sequence — a long-strided set, size eleven or so — was barefoot, toes splayed in the cold clay, and the gait analysis part of Okafor's brain, the part that had once helped police reconstruct a fugitive's movements from riverbank mud, flagged it immediately: even pace, no hesitation marks, no weight shifts. Whoever had walked out barefoot over freezing ground in the pre-dawn had done so calmly. Comfortably. Like a stroll after dinner.

The trail ran straight for the treeline two hundred meters away. Straight as a surveyor's baseline. Nobody walks that straight — not soldiers, not shepherds, not search parties. People wander. People avoid mud, rocks, low branches. These prints ignored all of it, climbed over a fallen log in perfect formation, one after another, each foot landing where the previous person's had landed, as if they were following a line painted on the world that only they could see.

At the treeline, the prints stopped.

Not faded. Not scattered into the brush, not turning aside along a game trail, not climbing. Stopped. Mid-stride. Okafor knelt at the last clear print — a government-issue boot, right foot, toe dug in with the full forward drive of a walking stride, heel lifted, weight committed — and there was simply nothing beyond it. No scuff. No broken fern. No second print. The forest floor beyond was undisturbed moss and needle duff, soft enough that a falling leaf left a mark, and it held nothing.

Ten people, walking in step, had ceased to touch the ground.

"We should go back down," Tsering said. He said it evenly, without drama, the way a man says a thing he has known since childhood and has been hoping not to say professionally. "This is the singing stone's valley. My grandmother drew a line on the map when I was seven years old. Everything above the confluence. She said the mountain keeps what walks into it. I used to think it was a story about avalanches." He looked at the print ending mid-stride, and his jaw worked once. "It isn't a story about avalanches."

Devi laughed. It came out wrong, too loud, a single bark. "Keeps them how? Ten trained surveyors don't just levitate into the trees."

"I don't know how," Tsering said. "I know my grandmother lost a brother up there. The family went looking. The searchers came back and could not say what they'd seen. Not wouldn't. *Could not.* They'd sit at the fire and open their mouths and nothing came." He turned and looked up-valley, at the white wall of the pass where it cut the sky, and Okafor watched the guide's face do the thing it had been doing all morning, the complicated thing, the thing that wasn't fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. Grief arriving early. "We are standing in the place my family has avoided for three generations, and we followed a contract up here."

Okafor's instruments chose that moment to speak.

He'd clipped the handheld spectrometer to his pack strap an hour ago out of habit, running a background scan the way other men chew gum. Now it began to chirp — not an alarm, alarms were sharp, and this was soft, almost apologetic — and he pulled it around and watched the readout climb. Magnetic anomaly, rising. Slow, steady, directional. He turned a quarter-circle and watched the number fall. Turned back. Watched it rise again. The needle of the digital display didn't oscillate; it *leaned*, the way grass leans toward light.

"It's coming from up-valley," he said. "Consistent bearing. The pass mouth." He checked the flux reading twice, recalibrated against the local baseline he'd established at basecamp, and got the same answer. "And it's pulsing. Same period as what's on the geophone data. Eight seconds." He looked up. "Something up there is broadcasting. Low band. Structured. It's not a deposit, it's not a ore body, magnetic ore bodies don't keep time."

"Broadcasting what?" Devi asked.

Okafor opened his mouth to say *I don't know*, and stopped, because that wasn't quite true, and the not-quite-true version was so much worse. What he wanted to say was: it's broadcasting *at us*. Because in the last twenty minutes — he checked his watch, yes, roughly since they'd reached the treeline — he'd caught himself three times thinking in a rhythm. An eight-second rhythm. His thoughts swelling and easing, swelling and easing, like breath, like tide, like something breathing *with* him, patient as geology, and each time he'd surfaced from it feeling like a man waking in a chair he didn't remember sitting down in.

He said none of that. He said, "Unknown. Structured, though. Repeating pattern. I can record it and run analysis tonight."

"Do that," Ulyana said. Her voice was level, and Okafor had learned enough of her in five days to know that the levelness cost her something. She was looking at the last footprint, the one with the toe dug in, the stride unfinished. "Record everything. We document, we pull the survey data, and then we make a decision about whether we're equipped to go further. Nobody wanders. Nobody goes anywhere alone. And nobody—" she paused, and Okafor saw her glance at the treeline, at the soft green nothing beyond the final print, "—nobody finishes a stride they didn't start."

They made camp inside the survey team's abandoned tents, because the alternative was pitching their own in the open and because, though none of them said it, the tents felt safer than the empty ground. Warmth without a source. Rice that cooked itself. Okafor told himself it was residual heat from some geological feature, some thermal vent the survey team had tapped, and the explanation held together until he actually lay down in his bag and put his palm flat against the ground beneath the tent floor.

The ground was cold. The air was warm. Heat without origin, hanging at chest height, gentle as a held breath.

He lay awake listening to the pulse through his earphones, amplified, isolated, and somewhere past midnight he made the mistake of letting the analysis software run a pattern match while he drifted. When he surfaced — eight seconds later, or eighty, he genuinely could not have said — the screen had produced its result. Confidence interval 94 percent. The closest structural match in the entire acoustic database wasn't seismic. It wasn't industrial. It wasn't animal.

It was human speech. Compressed, slowed roughly four hundred-fold, stripped of everything but cadence — but speech. Someone talking. Or something wearing the shape of talking, the way a lure wears the shape of a fish.

Okafor sat bolt upright in the dark tent, fumbled for the lamp, and found Devi already awake, propped on one elbow, watching him with wide eyes.

"You felt it too," Devi whispered. "Just now. You went somewhere. Your face went somewhere."

"Devi." Okafor's mouth was dry. "The pulse is talking."

Down the row of tents, a zipper moved. Then Ulyana's voice, quiet and absolutely awake, the voice of a woman who had not slept at all:

"I know. It's been saying my name for the last hour."
