## 6. What the Pulse Does

Tsering Norbu had been guiding outsiders into the Kharang range for nineteen years, and in all that time he had never once taken them past the bend where the valley narrowed and the river went quiet. Not because the trail ended there. The trail continued, good and wide, all the way to the pass. He avoided it because his grandmother had told him to, when he was seven years old and still small enough to sit on her lap while she carded wool by the fire.

*The stone sings up there,* she had said, in the old dialect that only elders spoke anymore. *And whatever hears it singing forgets whose voice is its own.*

He had thought it a story, the kind grandmothers keep in reserve for children who wander too far from the village dogs. He had grown up, married, buried a wife, raised a daughter who now lived in Leh and sent him photographs of buildings taller than any he would ever enter. And through all of it he had kept the rule without ever testing it. Some rules you obey not because you believe the reason but because the cost of being wrong is paid by someone who trusted you.

Now four strangers were camped at the treeline, forty minutes below the forbidden bend, and one of them — the nervous one with the instruments, Okafor — kept talking about a pulse.

* * *

Tsering heard it first as a sound in his own chest, which was how he knew to be afraid of it. A sound you hear with your ears can be measured, avoided, argued with. A sound you feel in your sternum, low and patient, like something breathing inside the mountain itself — that sound does not ask permission. It had started sometime after they made camp, faint enough that he mistook it for his own heart working harder than usual at this altitude. By dusk it had a rhythm. Not a heartbeat's rhythm. Something slower. Something that waited between beats longer than any living thing would wait.

Okafor sat apart from the fire with a pair of headphones clamped over his ears and a handheld recorder cradled in his lap like a sick bird. Every few minutes he'd turn a dial, listen, write something in a logbook with letters and numbers Tsering couldn't read. When Tsering refilled the man's tea, Okafor looked up and said, without being asked, "It's coming from up-valley. Low frequency. Under twenty hertz. It pulses every eleven seconds." Then he smiled, the way frightened men smile, to prove they are not frightened. "Probably geological."

"Probably," Tsering said, and let the word stand there alone, because he had learned long ago that *probably* was what outsiders said instead of *I don't know*, and *I don't know* was what they said instead of *I'm afraid*.

The tall diver, Nakamura, asked him directly: "What's up-valley, Tsering? You've been steering us away from that bend since noon. I've watched you do it three times. What's there?"

He could have lied. He had lied to clients before — about distances, about weather windows, about the true difficulty of a crossing — always in the direction of safety, never away from it. But Nakamura had a way of looking at a man that made lying feel like throwing a stone into still water and pretending it wouldn't ripple. She had eyes the color of deep water herself, and she used them the way he imagined she used her diving lights: steadily, without blinking, until whatever was hiding came out or was confirmed absent.

So he told them. All of it, sitting close to the fire because the telling required warmth, in his careful English, the words arriving one at a time like porters crossing a narrow bridge.

His grandmother's name had been Dolkar. She had been born in the year the glacier advanced, or so she claimed, though Tsering suspected she chose the date the way people choose impressive ancestors. She had lived eighty-one years in the same stone house above the river, and in all those years she had gone up-valley exactly once, as a girl, and had come back down having lost something she never described and never stopped guarding against losing again.

"She would not say what happened," Tsering told them. "Only that she went to look at the singing stone because her brother dared her, and she stood near it while it sang, and when she came home her mother asked her a question — I don't know what question — and she could not answer it. Not would not. Could not. The answer was simply not in her anymore. Like water poured into sand." He paused. The fire popped. "She remembered everything else. Her goats, her wedding, the names of her children. But that one thing, that one afternoon, that one question — gone. And around the hole where it had been, the edges were smooth. As if it had never been there at all."

Nakamura leaned forward. "A memory. Just one?"

"One," Tsering said. "But my grandmother said the stone does not take only one. She said it takes one first, to make room. Then it gives you something back. Something that fits the space. Something that feels like yours." He looked at each of them in turn, the way a man checks the knots on a rope before trusting it with weight. "She said: *the stone sings, and whatever hears it singing forgets whose voice is its own.*"

The fixer's escort — the silent ones, the two men Voss's money had bought, who never spoke and never removed their sunglasses even after dark — sat at the edge of the firelight and did not react at all. Tsering found their stillness more unsettling than anything he had said. A man who hears a ghost story and feels nothing is either very brave or already dead in some way that hasn't finished announcing itself.

Devi, the partner, laughed. Not cruelly — Devi didn't have a cruel bone, which was part of why Tsering liked him and part of why he worried about him. "Every mountain range on earth has a singing stone," Devi said. "Resonant rock, wind channels, thermal expansion at dusk. There's one in Ladakh the army uses for artillery calibration." He waved his hand at the darkening valley. "This is just geology with better folklore."

"Folklore," Tsering said quietly, "is what we call the memories of people who stayed alive by listening." He did not argue further. Arguing with a client about folklore was bad business. But he noted, privately, that Devi had said *just geology* with the same confident tone Okafor had said *probably*, and that both men were speaking about a thing neither had seen, while he himself was speaking about a thing his family had carried for three generations like a wound that never closed.

He told them one more thing before the fire burned down. His grandmother had died when he was twenty-two, and near the end, when her mind had begun to shed itself the way old minds do, shedding decades out of order, she had remained lucid about exactly one subject. She had called him to her bed and gripped his wrist with a strength that surprised him and said: *Whatever you find up there, Tsering-la, however beautiful it seems, however much it offers you — do not touch it. It is generous. That is what makes it dangerous. It gives. Nothing that gives like that gives freely.*

Then she had died, peacefully, remembering everything except the afternoon at the stone.

* * *

They slept in shifts, the way climbers sleep on exposed ground, and Tsering took the middle watch because he no longer slept well anyway and because the hours between two and four were when the valley did its strangest work. At half past two the pulse changed. He felt it before he heard it — a thickening in the air, a pressure behind the eyes like the moment before a storm front breaks over a ridge. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. Then, for the first time since they'd arrived, a variation: nine seconds, then thirteen, then back to eleven, as if something up-valley had noticed them noticing and was adjusting.

He woke Okafor. The comms tech sat up in his bag so fast he nearly knocked over the recorder, and his hands were shaking, but his face was lit with the particular joy of a technician who has finally gotten a clean signal. "It responded," he whispered. "I pinged it with an ultrasonic burst at midnight — standard survey procedure, harmless — and it answered. Changed its interval. Twice. That's not geology. Geology doesn't answer." He said it twice more during the conversation, *that's not geology*, each time with more conviction, as if repeating it could make the implication smaller.

Tsering listened to him and thought of his grandmother's smooth-edged hole and felt the first cold drop of real fear slide down his spine. Not fear of the mountain. Fear of what was happening to the man in front of him, right now, under his protection, on his mountain, on his contract.

Because Okafor began to talk. Nervous men talk when they're afraid; Tsering had guided enough of them to know the pattern, the rambling, the self-soothing narration. But this was different. This was a monologue delivered in a calm, warm voice, eyes half-closed, reclining against his pack as if settling into a familiar chair.

"You know what's funny," Okafor said. "When I was a boy, in Enugu, we had this compound with a mango tree, and my grandfather — Baba — he used to sit under it in the dry season and tell us stories about the Biafran war. Not the fighting. He never talked about the fighting. He talked about the songs. How everyone sang in the shelters, and how the songs were how you knew who was still alive, because every family had their own verses, and if you heard your family's verse sung from another shelter you knew—" he stopped, and smiled, and his eyes were wet and bright, "—you knew they'd made it out."

Tsering stood very still in the cold.

He had guided Nigerians before. He had guided Germans, Israelis, Japanese, Brazilians, Americans from six different states. He knew, vaguely, where Enugu was; he knew there had been a war there; he knew none of the details, because why would he. But something about the way Okafor told it snagged on Tsering's ear the way a fishhook snags on silk. The detail was too rich. Too complete. Too *owned*. Men recounting their childhoods fumble. They hesitate at dates, they contradict themselves, they say *I think it was* and *maybe it was my uncle, maybe my father*. Okafor wasn't fumbling. He was reciting. Every beat landed. Every name arrived on schedule. It was less like memory than like a well-rehearsed performance of memory — and Tsering had spent nineteen years watching exhausted clients tell stories around fires, and he knew the difference the way a horse trader knows the difference between a horse that knows its gait and a horse that has been drilled into it.

But it was the next part that turned his fear into something with claws.

"And the mangoes," Okafor said, still in that warm, settled voice, "we'd cut them green and eat them with salt and dried pepper, sitting on the wall by the gate, and Baba would say the same thing every time, he'd say, *Chidi, taste it slowly, sweetness you rush past is sweetness you never had.*" He laughed softly. "I must have heard that a hundred times. I can still see him doing it, the way he held the slice, thumb and forefinger, the salt on his knuckles."

Tsering walked around the dying fire to where Nakamura was sleeping and woke her without ceremony, pressing his boot gently against her bag, because some things cannot wait for morning and this was one of them.

"Tsering?" She surfaced fast, the way divers surface, already oriented.

"The Nigerian is dreaming out loud," he said. "Listen to me carefully. He is describing his childhood. He is describing it perfectly. Too perfectly. And the name he keeps using — Chidi — is not his name. His name is Okafor. He has said Okafor perhaps five times since we left basecamp, always when signing something, always formally, always reluctantly, like a man signing a document written in a language he doesn't read. But the name inside the memory, the name his grandfather calls him, the name he answers to when he isn't paying attention — is Chidi."

Nakamura sat up fully. In the red light of Tsering's headlamp her face looked carved from something older than her years. "You're saying the memory isn't his."

"I am saying worse than that." Tsering's breath was fogging hard now, and each word came out visible, hanging in the air like a small accusation. "I am saying the memory is *better* than his. Have you heard him speak of his actual life? His flat in the city, his sister who doesn't call, the job he complains about, the money he needs? Yes — many times, bitterly, quickly, like a man reading charges against himself. But tonight, under the pulse, he speaks of mango trees and war-shelter songs and a grandfather's salted knuckles, and he speaks like a man in love. Like a man describing the life he wishes he had lived." He crouched beside her, keeping his voice below the wind. "My grandmother said the stone takes one thing to make room, and then it gives you something back. Something that fits. Something that feels like yours." He pointed, deliberately, at Okafor across the dead fire. "That is what giving looks like. On day one."

Nakamura said nothing for a long moment. Then she did something Tsering hadn't expected: she walked over to Okafor, alone, in the dark, and sat down next to him, and asked him — Tsering watched from ten meters, unable to hear the words — asked him something gentle. And Okafor talked for another twenty minutes, happily, warmly, about the mango tree, about Baba, about the songs, and then, at the end, he said one sentence that Tsering saw land on Nakamura like a physical blow, because her whole body changed posture afterward, her shoulders dropping, her chin tucking, the way a person holds themselves when they've just been shown the edge of a cliff.

Tsering couldn't hear the sentence. But he could see, in the headlamp light, Okafor's face as he said it — serene, certain, lit from within — and he could see Nakamura's face as she received it, and the distance between those two expressions was the widest gap Tsering had ever seen between two people standing side by side.

Afterward Nakamura came back and stood beside him in the wind and said, in her flat, deep-water voice: "He told me about a day at the shore. A specific day. Age six. His mother holding his hand as the waves came in. He described the color of his mother's wrapper. The exact sound of the surf on that stretch of coast. The smell of the fish smoke from the shacks behind the beach." She paused. "Tsering, Okafor grew up in Lagos. Lagos is a lagoon city. There are beaches, yes. But he told that memory like it was scripture. And here's the thing — the details weren't wrong. They were *too right*. Fish smoke, wrappers, the particular sound of a West African shore — he narrated it like a documentary, like footage. Like something he'd been given rather than something he'd lived."

"Given," Tsering repeated. The word hung between them. His grandmother's word, translated at last into someone else's language, spoken by someone else's hands. *It is generous. That is what makes it dangerous.*

He looked up-valley, toward the bend he had never rounded, toward the place where the river went quiet and the trail climbed into cloud. The pulse was stronger now. He could feel it in his boots. Eleven seconds. Nine. Thirteen. Eleven. Patient as tide.

"We leave tomorrow," he told the group at dawn, bluntly, in front of all of them including the silent escorts, because a guide who hides his judgments gets his clients killed. "Not up. Across. There is a high route west of the valley floor, old herding trail, above the resonance line — I estimate, I *hope*, I pray — that puts distance between us and the stone. We go around, not through. Anyone who wants to push to the pass mouth can explain to me first why their curiosity outweighs my grandmother's eighty-one years of caution."

Devi pushed back immediately, of course. The contract said pass mouth. The contract said survey equipment. The contract, Devi reminded them, had a clause about cognitive hazards that nobody had read closely, and triple rate, and a window closing with the monsoon. "We skirt it," Devi proposed. "Close approach, quick retrieval, out. We don't linger. We don't touch anything."

Tsering shook his head slowly. "You don't understand what I'm telling you. Lingering is not how it works. My grandmother stood near it for the length of one song. One. And she lost a piece of herself so cleanly that she only discovered the loss because someone asked her a question." He fixed Devi with a look. "You are thinking of danger like a pit. Something you fall into if you step wrong. This is not a pit. This is weather. This is air. It reaches for you whether you step toward it or not. It reached Okafor last night while he slept fifty minutes below the treeline, and it gave him a childhood, and he thanked it by smiling."

Okafor, present for all of this, reacted strangely — not with denial, not with fear, but with a mild, reasonable puzzlement, as if someone had accused him of borrowing a book. "I don't understand what the problem is," he said. "The memories are good ones. They're better than mine. Is that... is that supposed to be a tragedy?"

And there it was. Tsering felt the question go through the little camp like a blade through cloth — not cutting anyone yet, just hanging there, sharp on both edges. Because Nakamura flinched when Okafor said *better than mine*. Tsering saw it. A small flinch, quickly controlled, the flinch of a woman who had once lost her own past and might be wondering, silently, dangerously, whether what the stone offered was really so much worse than what she'd already lost.

He did not ask her about it. A guide learns when not to ask.

But he wrote it in his head, in the ledger he kept behind his eyes, where he recorded everything a client revealed by accident: *Nakamura listens to stolen memory and hears possibility.* And beneath it, darker, in the column he hoped never to show anyone: *The stone does not need to hunt her. She is already walking toward it.*

By midmorning they were moving west along the herding trail, single file, the pulse fading behind them second by second — or seeming to fade; Tsering no longer trusted his own ears to report honestly. Okafor walked in the middle of the line humming. Not a local tune. A melody Tsering didn't know, in a minor key, with a rhythm that repeated every eleven seconds.

Tsering counted. He couldn't help it. Eleven seconds, and the phrase turned over. Eleven seconds, and it turned again.

At noon they stopped for water below a ridge of black schist, and Okafor, cheerful, rested, entirely himself — whoever that was now — opened his pack and offered around a bag of dried mango he'd apparently been carrying since basecamp. Everyone took a piece. It was good. Sweet, tart, dusted with chili salt.

"Baba's way," Okafor said, smiling, handing the bag to Nakamura last. "Taste it slowly. Sweetness you rush past is sweetness you never had."

Nobody laughed. Nobody moved. For three full seconds the whole team stood frozen in the thin sunlight, chewing, each of them privately understanding — at different depths, at different speeds — that the man feeding them was quoting a grandfather who had never existed, a childhood that had been installed in him like software, and that the installation was taking hold so smoothly, so *kindly*, that he had already begun distributing it to others.

Tsering swallowed his piece of mango and tasted nothing at all.

That night, checking his satellite phone for weather, Tsering found a message waiting from a number he didn't recognize, routed through some relay he didn't trust, text only, no signature:

*Your guide is correct. Do not let them touch the stone. Do not let HER touch the stone. Whatever she tells you she wants — check it twice. — a friend of Dolkar's granddaughter*

He read it three times. Then he looked across the campfire at Ulyana Nakamura, who was cleaning her dive knife with slow, meticulous strokes, counting something under her breath — miles, maybe, or seconds, or years — and for the first time in nineteen years of guiding, Tsering Norbu understood that the most dangerous thing in the valley might not be the stone that sang.

It might be the one person in camp who wanted, more than anything, to hear it sing.
