## 7. First Contact

The pass mouth announced itself the way a throat announces a scream — not with sound but with pressure, a change in the air that made my ears ache and my fillings hum. Tsering stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into his pack.

"There," he said, and pointed.

The valley narrowed ahead of us into a corridor of grey granite, and at its mouth the trees simply gave up. Not thinned — *gave up*, as if they'd reached an invisible line drawn by something with more authority than altitude or soil. Beyond the treeline the rock was bare and pale, scoured clean of lichen for perhaps two hundred meters in every direction, and in the center of that bleached ground stood the survey team's forward cache: two tents, a solar array, a stack of supply crates, all of it neat as a display in a shop window.

"Nobody camps past the trees," Tsering said quietly. "Not even in summer."

"The survey team did," Okafor said. His voice had been getting thinner every day since the camp, since the footprints that ended mid-stride like a sentence cut off by an editor. He'd stopped sleeping well. I'd watched him lie awake inside his bag, watching the tent ceiling, listening to whatever his instruments were whispering to him through their headphones.

"They didn't have a grandmother from Kharang," Tsering said, and started walking again, which was his way of saying he'd do it anyway, God help him.

We crossed the bare ground just after noon. My boots crunched on something that wasn't quite rock and wasn't quite salt — a fine pale rime over everything, like frost that refused to melt even though the sun was warm on my neck. Devi crouched and touched it, rubbed it between his fingers, sniffed it.

"Mineral," he said. "Calcium carbonate, maybe. Like scale out of a boiler." He wiped his fingers on his trousers and didn't touch anything else for the rest of the day.

I counted as we walked. It was what I did; it was who I was, as far as I could tell. Four hundred and twelve meters from the treeline to the first tent. Sixty-one paces around the perimeter of the camp. The solar array angled correctly for this latitude and season, which meant whoever set it up knew their work. The tents were expedition-grade, government-issue olive drab, stenciled with a survey designation Okafor photographed without being asked. Everything was tidy. Everything was *finished*. There were no half-eaten meals, no scattered gear, no signs of panic or haste or struggle.

There were no people either.

"Camps don't fold themselves," Devi said. "Somebody struck these tents this morning. Or last night." He looked at me. "They're close, Ulyana."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

Because the pulse was louder here. We'd all stopped pretending we couldn't feel it somewhere above basecamp — a low rhythmic pressure, slower than a heartbeat, patient as tide. Down in the valley it had been a rumor. Here at the pass mouth it was a presence, something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the mountain. And threaded through it now, faint but unmistakable, was something new.

Voices.

* * *

We found them singing.

That's the only word for it. Not chanting, not humming — singing, wordless, six voices braided into a single line that rose and fell with the pulse from the mountain, so perfectly matched to it that I couldn't tell whether they were following it or feeding it. They stood in a loose semicircle on a shelf of granite above the camp, facing the pass, and the sound came out of them the way breath comes out of you: effortless, continuous, necessary.

Six figures in faded survey-team fleece and trekking pants gone grey with weeks of weather. Alive. That was the first thing my mind insisted on cataloguing, because dead was what we'd expected, dead was what the contract assumed, dead was what triple rate was supposed to buy back in boxes. Alive, and standing upright, and — this was the part that made the hair rise on my arms — *clean*. Their clothes were worn but their faces were washed. Someone had braided one woman's hair with a precision that would have taken twenty minutes and steady hands.

Tsering said a word in his own language, low and horrified, and I understood enough of it to know it meant *the taken*.

Devi moved first, because Devi always moved first when moving was dangerous and hesitated when it was safe. "Hello!" he called, hands up, open-palmed. "Namaste! We're looking for the survey team — are you—"

The singing stopped.

Six heads turned toward us in unison, smooth as a single mechanism, and I felt Okafor make a small sound behind me. It wasn't the synchronization that got me. It was their expressions. They weren't startled. They weren't afraid. They looked at us the way you look at rain, or at a bird landing nearby — interested, mildly pleased, entirely untroubled.

The woman with the braided hair smiled. "You're late," she said pleasantly. "But that's all right. You're exactly on time too. Both things are true here."

Her voice was clear, educated, unhurried. Her eyes were the problem. They were warm and empty at once, like windows of a house where every lamp is lit and nobody is home — no, worse, like lamps lit for someone expected, someone who hadn't arrived yet.

"What's your name?" Devi asked. Standard protocol, keep them oriented, give them a hook to hang onto.

She considered the question with genuine curiosity, the way you might consider a riddle. Then she laughed softly, without embarrassment. "I don't have one anymore. Isn't that wonderful? I keep reaching for it and there's just... space. Room." She pressed a hand flat against her sternum. "It doesn't hurt. You'd think it would hurt, but it's like setting down luggage you didn't know was heavy."

Okafor stepped up beside me. "Dr. Ansel? Dr. Priya Ansel? You led the Kharang geological survey. Your team went missing eleven weeks ago. There were search parties. Your families—" His voice cracked on the word and he steadied himself. "Your families think you're dead."

Something flickered across her face then — not recognition, not grief. Something more like politeness toward a stranger's strange custom. "Ansel," she repeated, tasting it. "That sounds like it belonged to someone. A person who worried a lot." She tilted her head. "She had a bad shoulder. Left side. I can almost remember how it ached before storms." She flexed her left arm experimentally, found nothing, and smiled again. "Gone. All of it. Do you want to see?"

"See what?" I asked, and immediately wished I hadn't, because she turned those lit-empty windows on me and everything about her attention changed. The others had been scenery. I was suddenly the entire landscape.

"You," she said. "You're so *loud*. All the rest of you are quiet — little murmurs, little streams. But you're a river under ice." She took a step toward me. Tsering shifted, and one of the men in the semicircle raised a hand — not threateningly, just a gentle *please don't*, the gesture of a librarian. "May I? I won't take anything. Taking isn't how it works. It only gives."

"Ulyana," Devi said sharply. "Don't."

I should have listened. Twelve years of diving had taught me never to put my hand into water I hadn't tested. But there was something in her face — that terrible serene kindness — and underneath it, faint as a signal through fathoms, something that sounded like my own name spoken by a voice I didn't recognize.

"It's all right," I heard myself say.

She crossed the distance between us and laid two fingers against my wrist, light as a nurse taking a pulse. Her skin was cool. And then—

*Cold water. Green-black cold, the kind that lives below ten meters in northern seas, and a small hand in mine, a child's hand, mittened, trusting. A harbor wall going down into dark. Gulls screaming over fish guts and diesel. My mother's voice calling from the top of the steps — not words, just the shape of it, the particular music of a mother calling a child home before dark. The taste of salt and iron. The weight of a body in water, how it changes, how a living body fights and a still one doesn't. The boat. The engine coughing. The moment the world tilts.*

I tore free and staggered back three steps, heart slamming, and the mountains tilted around me because I knew that water. I knew that harbor. I had never seen it in my life — I had no life before the accident, no childhood, nothing but white space and hospital corridors and Devi's voice saying *you're safe, you're safe* — and yet I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, from the inside, from having lived it.

"That's yours," the woman said gently. "Isn't it? From when you were small. The harbor. The winter your father's boat went down." She frowned slightly, the first imperfection in her serenity. "No — that's not right. That's not how it happened. The stone shows me both versions and I can't always tell which one is the true one. It says the true one is the one that hurts more." She looked at me with something that was almost pity. "Yours hurts more."

"Ulyana." Devi's hand on my shoulder, solid, real, smelling of sweat and woodsmoke. "Hey. Look at me. You're here. You're with me."

I looked at him. I held onto his sleeve like a rail in heavy swell.

"What did she do?" I managed. "What did she *do*?"

"She read you," Okafor whispered. He'd gone the color of old paper. "Or the stone read you through her. Ulyana — she described your childhood. A childhood you don't have. In *detail*. How could she possibly—"

"The Passage keeps everything," the woman said behind us, conversational, helpful. "Everything anyone ever brought near it. Every mind that passed under the mountain leaves an impression, the way feet leave prints in snow. Yours is very deep. You've been here before." She paused. "Or you will be. Time is soft here. I'm sorry — I know that's frightening. It stopped being frightening to us. That's what we lost, I think. Fear. Along with our names." She spread her hands, encompassing her five companions, the bare rock, the humming mountain. "And honestly? Looking at your faces right now? I'm not sure we lost the bad parts."

* * *

We made camp that night four hundred meters downslope, past the treeline, because Tsering refused to sleep on the bare stone and none of us argued. We built a fire even though the smoke would be visible for kilometers, because some needs outrank tactics, and I sat closest to it.

The survey team didn't leave. They drifted down as the light failed and settled at the edge of our firelight like cats deciding whether a household was worth adopting, and eventually they simply joined it, sitting among us with the unselfconscious ease of family. They accepted tea. They complimented the food. One of the men — young, freckled, formerly someone — asked Okafor intelligent questions about his radio equipment and listened to the answers with real delight.

"They're not damaged," Okafor said to me in a low voice, watching him. "That's what's horrifying. Medically they should be wrecks. Amnesics, dissociatives — they should be terrified, confused, violent. They're *happy*, Ulyana. Look at them. When did you last see six people that happy?"

I was watching the woman with the braids. She had positioned herself near me again, not intruding, just near, the way you sit near a fire.

"Ask her," I said.

He did. "What did it feel like? Losing yourselves?"

She thought about it properly, giving the question its due. "Like a tide going out," she said finally. "First the small things go — the name, the address, the phone numbers. Then the middle things — the job, the grievances, the appointments. And you'd think the big things would fight. Love, fear, the people who mattered. But they don't fight. They just... pour away, smooth and quiet, and what's left is—" she gestured at herself, at all of them, at the star-crowded sky above the black teeth of the pass, "—this. Space. The stone calls it the clear room. It says most people live their whole lives in a room so full of furniture they can't lie down. It offers to clear the room. And you say yes. Everyone says yes. That's the part your faces keep asking and I keep answering: *everyone says yes.*"

"Not everyone," Tsering said from the darkness beyond the fire. His grandmother's stories were sitting on him; I could see their weight. "My grandmother said the singing stone gives you a better life than the one you had. That's how it catches you. Nobody walks into a trap that looks worse than the road."

The woman smiled at him, unoffended. "Your grandmother was wise. Yes. It gives you a better life. Or it gives you *a* life, which feels the same, once the old one stops arguing."

I fed the fire another branch and watched the sparks climb. My hands were steady. Diver's hands. But inside me the harbor kept rising — green-black water, a mitten in mine, my mother's call hanging in cold air — memories I had mourned for twelve years without knowing their shape, delivered to me by a stranger's fingertips, accurate down to the smell of diesel and fish blood.

Either the stone had given them to her from some record of a girl who once lived. Or it had invented them, whole, perfect, plausible. And I understood, sitting in the warmth with the pulse beating slow beneath the mountain, that I had no way to tell the difference — and that Voss's contract, the equipment, the triple rate, the clause about cognitive hazards, all of it had been aimed at this exact moment, this exact hunger, from the very beginning.

The woman leaned toward me across the fire, and her lit-empty eyes caught the flames.

"You want to know the rest," she said. It wasn't a question. "About the boat. About why the engine failed. About the man who was watching your father's harbor that winter, and why." She held out her hand, palm up, an offer, an invitation, a door. "It's all still in there. I can show you. Or the mountain can. Either way, Ulyana Nakamura —"

She smiled.

"— you didn't survive that accident by chance. Nothing about you was chance. Shall I tell you who arranged it?"

Behind me, in the dark below the pass, I heard — impossibly, absurdly, unmistakably — the thrash of helicopter blades coming up the valley.
