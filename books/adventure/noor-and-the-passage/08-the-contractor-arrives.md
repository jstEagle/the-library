## 8. The Contractor Arrives

Devi heard the helicopter before he saw it — a thrum rising through the valley like a wasp the size of a house, and then, over the shoulder of the ridge to the south, a black shape resolving out of the haze. It came on low and fast, skimming the treeline of the lower slopes, rotors chopping the thin air into something that sounded almost like anger. By the time it banked over their camp at the mouth of the pass, Devi had already counted the hardpoints under its stub wings: two. This was not a rescue aircraft. Rescue aircraft did not carry ordnance.

It flared, hovered a moment in a cloud of dust and grit that scoured every surface in camp, and set down on the flat shelf of gravel fifty meters below their tents. The dust took minutes to settle. When it finally did, Devi was standing at the edge of their little plateau with his binoculars raised, watching six men disembark in matching grey tactical gear, none of them wearing anything that identified an employer, a nation, or a name. They moved like soldiers who had done this a hundred times: perimeter first, weapons up, covering arcs that had nothing to do with rescuing anyone.

The seventh man off the ramp was different. He wore civilian clothes pressed sharp enough to survive a trans-Himalayan flight — charcoal trousers, a jacket the color of wet slate — and he carried himself with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once been told no by terrain, weather, or government. He crossed the gravel toward their camp in long, even strides, and Devi found himself doing the thing he always did when a new variable entered one of his operations: running numbers.

Triple rate. That was what the fixer had promised them in Kathmandu, back when the contract still looked like paper instead of prophecy. Triple rate for survey equipment retrieval, no questions asked, cognitive hazards clause buried in annex four like a landmine in a legal document. Devi had signed it because Ulyana's salvage business was three bad seasons from dead and because he'd told himself that men who wrote clauses about cognition were simply lawyers being thorough. He had watched Okafor's instruments register a rhythmic pulse from up-valley. He had watched Tsering go quiet in a way guides never went quiet. He had watched a woman describe Ulyana's drowned childhood with the fluency of lived memory. And through all of it, he had kept telling himself the same sentence, over and over, until it wore a groove in him: *we are contractors, we are professionals, we finish jobs.*

Now a man was walking toward him across the gravel whose entire existence was proof that the job had never been theirs to finish.

"Good morning." The stranger stopped at the edge of their camp without entering it, which was itself a message — he could have walked straight in, and chose not to, because permission was cheaper than force and just as effective. "I'm looking for the Nakamura-Okafor salvage party. I understand you've located the government survey team."

"We located where they *were*," Devi said carefully.

"And where they are now?"

"Up-valley," Devi said. "Alive."

The stranger smiled, and the smile was the worst thing Devi had seen in three weeks in these mountains, worse than the footprints ending mid-stride at the treeline, worse than the pulse on Okafor's spectrograph. Because it was a genuine smile. Warm, even. The smile of a man receiving good news.

"That's excellent," he said. "That's exactly what my client hoped to hear. My name is Voss. Marcus Voss. Site chief, Meridian Applied Systems. You may have encountered our logo — stamped on a sealed case recovered from a wreck your partner dove last month?"

Devi felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world.

He knew that case. He had helped Ulyana haul it aboard, had watched her crack the seal, had stood beside her while she read the manifest inside and understood, all at once, that the navy vessel they'd been told was decommissioned and stripped had gone down carrying active sonar arrays and hydrophone clusters manufactured by a defense contractor. Ulyana had logged coordinates she shouldn't have kept. Devi had begged her to wipe them. She hadn't. He'd thought, at the time, that she was being sentimental — divers kept things, it was how they were built, they hoarded locations the way other people hoarded photographs. Now he understood she had been right and he had been wrong, and the distance between those two positions was standing in front of him in pressed trousers, smiling.

"You've been busy," Voss said pleasantly. "Wreck salvage, then a mountain contract. My client appreciates thoroughness. Tell me — has anyone briefed you on what you're actually retrieving from this pass?"

"Survey equipment."

"Mm." Voss turned and looked up-valley, toward the notch in the ridgeline where the pass began, where the pulse lived. He looked at it the way Devi imagined a man looks at an oil field he's just confirmed. "Let me save you some time. What you're retrieving is not equipment. It's data. Human beings are the equipment. The government team that walked into this valley eight weeks ago walked into the most significant cognitive phenomenon ever documented on this continent, and my client — whom I am not authorized to name, but you can call them the client — intends to have it mapped, characterized, and contained before the monsoon makes this range inaccessible for five months."

He said it all casually, the way a man describes a routine acquisition. Devi listened and felt each word settle into him like ballast.

"Contained," Devi repeated.

"Contained." Voss nodded. "There's a geological anomaly in that pass. A formation — the locals call it the singing stone; our seismographs call it a resonance chamber — that stores human memory the way limestone stores water. It takes it in. It holds it. Under certain conditions, it gives it back altered. Eight weeks ago it took twelve government surveyors whole. Their bodies are healthy. Their minds are blank slates, and into those blanks, something has been written — fragments of other lives, other valleys, other drownings. One of them described your partner's childhood to her yesterday. Did she tell you that? In perfect detail. Details she herself cannot access."

"She told me."

"And how did she take it?"

Devi thought about Ulyana's face that evening. The way she'd held the woman's hands and listened, and the way she'd gone afterward to sit alone at the edge of camp for two hours, facing up-valley, saying nothing to anyone. Devi had assumed grief. He was beginning to suspect it was something more dangerous than grief.

"She took it quietly," Devi said.

"They usually do." Voss checked his watch — an expensive one, matte-cased, the kind that didn't advertise. "Here's the situation as my client sees it. Monsoon builds over the coast in nineteen days, give or take. Once it makes landfall, this valley closes. Helicopters can't fly in that. Resupply becomes impossible. Extraction becomes impossible. Which means everything my client wants done here must be done inside a nineteen-day window, and the clock started the day you signed your contract."

"You knew about us before we signed."

Voss didn't deny it. He didn't confirm it either. He gave Devi the answer that was somehow worse than either: "Your contract was drafted by people who read our feasibility study."

* * *

The rest of the morning passed in a series of conversations that Devi would spend the rest of his life trying to forget.

Voss established himself in camp the way water establishes itself in a crack — slowly, politely, everywhere at once. His escorts set up a perimeter that happened to encircle the salvage party's four tents. His comms specialist requested — requested, always requested, never demanded — access to their satellite uplink to "deconflict frequencies," and Okafor, who noticed everything, noticed within twenty minutes that the deconfliction involved routing their traffic through a relay that hadn't existed yesterday. His logistics officer inventoried their supplies with a clipboard and a smile and reported, gravely, that they were under-provisioned for a full ascent, and that Meridian would be happy to supplement.

Everything was offered. Nothing was forced. That was the genius of it, and Devi recognized genius when it was applied against him, because he'd spent fifteen years in this trade watching men try to buy, bully, and bluff their way through mountains, and Voss did none of those things. Voss made himself useful. He made refusal expensive. He made cooperation feel like the only rational act available, and he did it all with such impeccable manners that objecting felt rude.

By noon, Devi had learned three facts that reorganized his understanding of the entire operation.

First: the demolition gear. He'd seen the crates coming off the helicopter — heavy, stenciled, handled with the care men reserve for things that go bang. He'd assumed blasting material for landing-zone improvement, maybe trail work. Then he'd watched Voss's chief engineer open one crate for inventory, and inside, nested in foam cut to millimeter tolerances, lay shaped charges. Not quarry charges. Not avalanche control. Shaped charges with directional liners, the kind used for precision breaching of hardened structures. Nobody brings shaped charges to rescue a survey team.

Second: the jurisdiction claim. Voss carried documents — thick ones, bound, embossed with a government seal Devi half-recognized and couldn't place. Emergency recovery authority, joint tasking order, letters of delegation. Devi read them twice and understood less with each reading, except for one clause that leapt off the page: authority extended to "the securement and disposition of personnel affected by environmental cognitive events." Disposition. The word sat in Devi's chest like a swallowed stone. You rescued people. You repatriated people. *Disposition* was a word for cargo.

Third: the blanks themselves. Voss's medical officer — a compact, cheerful man named Reyes who smiled constantly and slept four hours a night — hiked up-valley that afternoon with two escorts and returned at dusk with a report that Devi heard secondhand and would remember verbatim anyway. The survey team was alive. Twelve of them, though two had wandered deeper into the pass and hadn't come back down. The remaining ten were healthy, serene, cooperative, and entirely without names. They had built themselves shelters. They had organized food stores. They had, Reyes said, developed routines, jokes, preferences — a culture, nascent and gentle, growing in soil where identity had been scraped clean. And they remembered things that had never happened to them. One of them, a former glaciologist named Anselm, now described in loving detail a fishing village on a coast he'd never visited. Another hummed a lullaby in a language nobody in the team spoke.

"They're not suffering," Reyes said, and his cheerfulness made the statement obscene. "That's the remarkable thing. Whatever took their memories gave them contentment. We could learn something from them, frankly."

Devi wanted to throw the man off the mountain.

* * *

Evening came down the valley like ink poured into water, and with it came the meeting Devi had been dreading since the helicopter landed.

Voss requested it formally — a coordination briefing, he called it, in his tent, which had been erected that afternoon and was larger than any structure Devi had seen above basecamp altitude. Inside: a folding table, a satellite terminal, a wall map of the pass printed at a resolution that made their own maps look like children's drawings. The map showed contour lines around a chamber marked in red. Someone had surveyed this valley recently. Someone had surveyed it very well.

Ulyana attended. So did Tsering, arms crossed, face closed, radiating the silent hostility of a man watching strangers handle his grandmother's mountains like inventory. Okafor sat at the table's edge with a notebook and the particular alertness of a man who has decided to survive by knowing more than everyone else in the room.

Voss opened with weather. Nineteen days. Maybe seventeen. The monsoon was building faster than the models predicted; his meteorologist had revised the window twice since Kathmandu. Then objectives: map the resonance chamber, characterize its storage behavior, establish containment protocols, extract the survey team — phrase chosen carefully, Devi noted, because extraction implied persons and disposition implied property and Voss used both words interchangeably depending on audience. Then timeline: ascent teams in the field within seventy-two hours, chamber work complete inside the window, everything packed out before the first wall of rain hit the range.

Then Voss paused, and looked directly at Ulyana, and Devi watched the temperature of the room change.

"I want to address something openly," Voss said, "because I believe in direct dealing. Ms. Nakamura. Twelve years ago, you were in an accident. A diving accident. Saturation dive, North Sea sector, catastrophic bell failure. You lost — the term the physicians used was *retrograde erasure* — approximately eleven years of autobiographical memory. Everything before the accident is, as far as your neurologists could determine, unrecoverable through ordinary means."

Ulyana said nothing. Her face did the thing it always did, the thing Devi had catalogued over years of partnership: it went flat, level, professional, a diver's face, a face built for pressure. But underneath it Devi could see the keel shift.

"How do you know that?" Ulyana said.

"Because my client has files on you, Ms. Nakamura. Extensive ones. Your medical history, your dive logs, your psychological evaluations. You were flagged years ago as a candidate of interest." He spread his hands, palms up, the gesture of a man offering transparency. "I'll be honest with you, because honesty costs me nothing here and buys a great deal. The phenomenon in that pass doesn't just erase memory. It stores it. Everything those surveyors lost didn't vanish — it went into the stone. And there is reason to believe that material stored in the chamber can be accessed, extracted, reconstructed. Including yours."

The silence in the tent lasted four seconds. Devi counted them. He counted everything under stress; it was how he kept from screaming.

"Eleven years," Voss said gently, "of who you were. Sitting in a rock two kilometers up that valley. And my client has the technology, potentially, to bring it back."

Tsering spoke first, and his voice was low and rough as river stones. "You're offering to sell a woman her own mind."

"I'm offering her a choice," Voss said. "Which is more than the accident offered her."

And Devi watched Ulyana look at Voss — really look, the way she looked at a wreck before descending into it, reading hull plates the way other people read faces — and he watched her say, calmly, evenly, in a voice that gave away nothing:

"I'll think about it."

Four words. Devi had heard her say ten thousand sentences in fifteen years of working together, and he had never once been afraid of her the way he was afraid in that moment, hearing those four words. Because Ulyana Nakamura did not think about offers. She evaluated them, priced them, accepted or refused them inside a minute, the way she decided whether a wreck was divable. She did not *think*. Thinking was what she did when something had gotten past the hull plating and into the machinery, and now something had.

Voss smiled again — that warm, genuine, terrible smile — and closed the meeting with logistics. Departure at dawn for the advance team. Coordination protocols. Shared comms. And one final item, delivered with the lightness of an afterthought:

"One administrative note. Your contract specifies delivery of survey equipment to my client's designated representative. I am that representative. I'd ask that you continue fulfilling your obligations — escort duties, route knowledge, high-altitude support. Your rates remain triple. Completion bonus on delivery." He glanced around the tent, meeting each of their eyes in turn, and finished: "We're all after the same thing here, I think. Let's not make it complicated."

After the others left, Devi stayed behind. He told himself it was to negotiate supply rates. He stayed forty minutes. He came out of the tent after dark, and Ulyana was waiting by the fire, and she looked at him the way she looked at a gauge reading wrong.

"What did you talk about for forty minutes?" she asked.

"Supplies," Devi said.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she turned back to the fire, and Devi stood in the cold between the firelight and the dark, holding a lie he hadn't yet decided was a lie.

Up-valley, faint beneath the wind, the stone was singing.
