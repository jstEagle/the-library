## 9. Miles as Memory

The night before the ascent, Ulyana sat outside her tent and counted.

It was what she did when the silence got too large. She counted the miles she had logged in twelve years of salvage work — two hundred and eleven thousand, give or take, most of them underwater where miles meant nothing to anyone but her. She counted the dives: four thousand and change. She counted the wrecks: sixty-three. She counted the bodies she had recovered for families who needed something to bury: nine.

Nine was the number that always stopped her.

She had a system for it, the counting. A small waterproof notebook, grease pencil, columns ruled by hand. Distance on the left, depth on the right, a third column for what she'd found. The notebook went back to the day she'd woken up in the naval hospital at Kochi with no memory of how she'd gotten there, and everything before that page was blank because there was nothing before that page. Twelve years ago, a saturation dive gone wrong off the Andaman shelf. A blowout, a chamber fire, forty-one minutes without air. She'd surfaced into a life with the first three decades amputated clean at the wrist.

The doctors called it retrograde amnesia, dense and permanent. Devi, who had been assigned as her recovery diver and never quite managed to leave, called it something else. He called it the gift nobody would have asked for.

She didn't call it anything. She called it the water before the water.

* * *

The accident came back to her sometimes, not as memory but as furniture — the shape of it, the weight of it in the room of her mind, even though the room itself was empty.

A saturation deck, 180 meters down, helium hissing through the umbilical. She knew these facts the way you know facts about a country you've read about but never visited. The bell had flooded during transfer. Something in the scrubber stack had caught — electrical, they told her later, though "later" was a word that belonged to other people's continuity. Forty-one minutes. The number was hers now; she'd claimed it the way you claim a scar. Forty-one minutes of hypoxia, and whatever the brain does when it starves like that, it had done thoroughly. Not just the incident. Everything behind the incident. Her mother's face. Her father's fishing boat out of Visakhapatnam. A marriage, the hospital records said — there had been a husband, a man named Anand, who had visited her every day for six weeks and then signed the papers and stopped coming. She knew this from a social worker's file summary, four paragraphs long, written in the flat bureaucratic voice of someone describing weather.

Four paragraphs. That was the total mass of twenty-nine years.

She remembered waking up. That much was real, or felt real, which after enough time is the same currency. White ceiling. The sound of a ventilator breathing for her. A nurse with kind hands saying *you're safe, you're safe*, and Ulyana — twenty-nine years old inside a body that had forgotten its own name — trying to answer and finding that the name wouldn't come. It took three days for *Ulyana Nakamura* to surface, and when it did, it arrived like a piece of salvage: useful, intact, belonging to someone she couldn't find.

The blank years afterward were worse than the blank ones before. Eighteen months of rehabilitation, of learning to walk a gait she'd already owned, of speech therapy re-teaching her English she'd spoken since childhood. People kept telling her things about herself. *You were so funny. You loved mangoes. You hated being cold.* Each fact landed like a coin dropped down a well — she heard it strike, she never heard it land.

So she started diving again. Not because anyone recommended it. Because down there, at depth, the arithmetic was simple. Depth was depth. Bottom time was bottom time. Gas was gas. The ocean asked nothing of her past and paid her strictly in present tense. Every dive was a life measured in miles, and miles could be counted, and counted things couldn't be taken away, because they existed only in the column where she'd written them.

Devi understood this before she did. He'd watched her build the system from the hospital bed outward, watched her turn recovery into architecture. He never once said *don't you want to know?* He was the only person in her life who never asked that question, and she had married him for it in all but paperwork, and she suspected he knew that too.

* * *

Now she sat in the blue cold of nine thousand feet with a notebook open on her knee, and the numbers weren't holding.

That was the trouble. The system worked because the past stayed past. But for two days, walking among the survey team — the five of them moving through their camp like sleepwalkers in sunlight, serene, nameless, untroubled — she had been watching her own condition worn by other people, and it fit them differently than it fit her.

She'd spent twelve years assuming her blankness was damage. Wound, loss, theft. The vocabulary everyone used around amnesia was the vocabulary of crime: *she lost her memories, we tried to recover them, so much was taken.* And she had organized her entire existence around that premise. Counting forward because backward was a wall.

But the survey team hadn't lost anything. That was what she couldn't stop turning over, there in the dark with the pulse humming faintly up-valley, a sound below hearing, more felt than heard, like a note held under the floor of the world.

They'd been given something.

She'd seen it in the woman with the gray-streaked braid — Dr. Meenakshi Rao, according to the manifest Okafor had pulled from the camp radio's cached files. Rao had stood at the treeline that morning watching the sunrise with an expression Ulyana recognized from mirrors: complete, unguarded attention. No dread. No backlog. When Ulyana had asked her — carefully, the way you approach a bird — whether she wanted her name back, Rao had looked at her with something close to pity.

"Why?" Rao had said. "Was she unhappy?"

Ulyana hadn't known how to answer. She still didn't. Because the file summaries, the fragments Okafor had recovered from the camp's laptops before the batteries died, painted a picture of Dr. Meenakshi Rao that was not serene. A funding war with her institute. A divorce filed eight months before the expedition. A disciplinary letter for falsifying sample dates — one letter, maybe a mistake, but the kind of thing that metastasizes in a career. A woman, in short, arriving at Kharang Pass with her life already in ruins, mapping a geological anomaly she did not understand, carrying thirty years of accumulated grievance like ballast.

And then the Passage had opened beneath her, whatever it was — stone, resonance, some property of the rock that held human memory the way limestone holds water — and thirty years of grievance had simply drained away. Not suppressed. *Drained.* The way silt clears after a flood.

Rao wasn't broken. Rao was light.

Ulyana looked at her own notebook, at the columns, at two hundred and eleven thousand miles of forward motion, and admitted to herself, alone, in the dark, what she had not said aloud: the system was a load-bearing wall built over a hole. She counted because counting was the only form of remembering available to her. Take away the need to count and what remained? A woman of forty-one with no childhood, no first love, no reason for any of her own preferences, running on discipline and salt water.

The Passage had offered the survey team a better life than the one they'd had. That was the sentence she kept circling, and each pass around it tightened like a noose.

Because if it was true for them —

* * *

The flashback came that night, uninvited, the way it always did: not as memory but as a kind of vertigo with scenery attached.

She was in the hyperbaric unit, six weeks post-accident, and a man was sitting beside her bed. She knew this scene the way she knew the wreck manifests — secondhand, reconstructed, told to her by Devi in careful pieces over the years because he'd been there. The man was Anand. Her husband. He brought jasmine in a paper cone because the florist near the hospital sold it that way, and he put it in the plastic cup on her tray table, and he talked to her about ordinary things because the neurologist had said familiar voices helped even when she couldn't answer.

He talked about the house in Panjim. About the monsoon shutters they needed to oil. About the argument they'd had the week before her dive — some small married thing, dishes or money or his mother, Devi had never learned which — and how he'd lain awake afterward listening to rain and thinking that no fight was worth the sound of her breathing next to him, and how he'd decided to tell her that when she woke up.

She woke up. He never got to say it. Or he did, and she couldn't hold it, and either way the jasmine wilted in the cup and he signed the papers and the file closed at four paragraphs.

Ulyana had heard this story perhaps ten times. She had never once cried at it. She noticed that about herself the way you notice a numb tooth — clinically, with a small internal flag. Grief requires a container, and she had none for Anand. He was a fact about a stranger who shared her medical history.

But tonight, sitting outside the tent with the pulse in the rock below her like a second heartbeat, the story rearranged itself.

If she walked into the Passage — if she pressed her hands to the singing stone the way Tsering's grandmother had forbidden it a lifetime ago — what would it take? Not add. *Take.* Thirty years of grief she couldn't access anyway. The phantom weight of a marriage she experienced only as paperwork. The humiliation of learning herself from files. The counting itself, maybe — the whole scaffolding of miles built to brace against an absence that the Passage could simply... fill.

What frightened her was not that she was tempted. She was a professional; she catalogued the temptation, wrote it in the margin of the notebook in grease pencil like a hazard notation, *unstable formation, do not enter.*

What frightened her was that she could not articulate why it would be wrong.

The team's contract had a clause — *cognitive hazards* — that neither she nor Devi had understood when they signed. She understood it now. The hazard wasn't death. The hazard was that the Passage made erasure feel like mercy, and mercy was the one thing a person could not refuse on behalf of themselves.

* * *

Devi found her there past midnight, notebook closed, staring up-valley toward the faint mineral smell the wind carried down from the pass mouth. He crouched beside her with two cups of tea gone lukewarm and handed her one, and for a while neither spoke, which was their oldest language.

"You're doing the math," he said finally. He knew her tells. Twelve years of partnership; he could read her columns from across a room.

"What math?"

"The one where you go in." He said it gently, without accusation, which was somehow worse. "I've been watching you watch Rao. You look at her the way you look at a wreck you haven't decided whether to cut."

Ulyana turned the cup in her hands. "She's at peace."

"She's a hole in the shape of a person." Devi's voice stayed level, but she heard the fear underneath it, the specific fear of a man who had spent twelve years anchored to a woman whose past was a locked room, and who had made his peace with the lock, and who now watched the lock standing open at the end of a valley. "You think she got a better life. Maybe. From in here." He tapped his own temple. "From out here, she's five people who can't testify, can't consent, can't remember why they came. Voss is coming for them tomorrow with a helicopter and a smile. You want to trade your worst decade for that?"

"I'm not trading anything." But her voice came out a half-tone flat, and he heard it, because he heard everything.

They sat with the tea cooling. Below them, the valley breathed. Somewhere in the rock, the pulse continued its patient rhythm, and Ulyana thought: *it isn't calling me. It doesn't have to call. It just has to wait.* Things that waited in deep water had always been patient with her.

"Twelve years," she said at last. "You never once asked me if I wanted to know. Why?"

Devi looked into his cup. "Because I was afraid of the answer," he said. "Either one."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then the wind shifted, and the pulse strengthened for a moment — a swell, like a distant ship's engine clearing a headland — and in that swell she felt something she had no column for. Not a memory. A *shape* where a memory should be. Warmth, salt, a hand on her shoulder from behind, the exact pressure of being known by someone whose face she could not see.

It lasted two seconds. It was the first thing in twelve years that had ever come from behind the wall instead of in front of it.

She wrote nothing down. Some measurements you don't log, because logging them makes them yours, and she was suddenly, terribly unsure she wanted this one to belong to her.

At dawn, Tsering shook her shoulder and pointed up-valley, where the first light had found the pass mouth: a seam of pale stone in the black rock, wide as a road, glowing faintly like something lit from within.

"We leave in an hour," Tsering said. "Before the weather turns."

Ulyana packed the notebook last, on top, where she could reach it. And in the margin of today's empty page, beneath the grease-pencil hazard notation, she added a single line she immediately regretted and could not bring herself to erase:

*Forty-one minutes took everything. What else is waiting that takes less?*

By the time the column moved out, the sky over the coast had gone the color of a bruise, and Okafor came running back from the radio tent with the handset in his fist and his face stripped of every ounce of the nervous humor that usually armored him.

"Satellite window," he said. "We need everyone. Now."
