## 10. Voss's Ledger

Okafor had always believed that fear was a sound. Not a loud one — not the scream or the gunshot or the avalanche roar that people imagined when they thought of terror in the mountains. It was quieter than that. A tick in an engine. A hiss in a regulator. The particular silence of a radio channel that should be carrying traffic and wasn't.

He heard it now, sitting cross-legged inside the survey team's command tent with the flap zipped against the wind, listening to a frequency he was almost certain he wasn't supposed to hear.

The pulse from up-valley hadn't stopped. That was the first thing he'd tell himself later, when he tried to reconstruct the night: the whole time he worked, the mountain kept its slow rhythm beneath everything, four seconds apart, like something breathing under a blanket. He'd learned to work around it the way you learn to sleep next to a snoring partner — by pretending it was furniture. But it wasn't furniture. And neither, it turned out, was the transmission.

It started as bleed. He'd been running a diagnostic sweep on the survey team's spectrum analyzer — better equipment than anything Devi owned, which told its own story about what this "survey" had really been — and at 0317 local time, buried under the noise floor, he caught a carrier wave. Encrypted. Burst transmissions, short and disciplined, coming from the direction of basecamp. From the tents where Voss's people slept.

Okafor should have logged it and gone back to sleep. That was the professional move. You didn't go digging into a client's comms; you especially didn't dig into the comms of men who carried rifles they never explained and looked at you the way Voss's escorts looked at everyone, like inventory.

But Okafor had spent six years as a signals tech for a disaster-relief NGO before the money ran out and salvage became the only trade that would have him, and there was one thing those years had burned into him deeper than any training manual: *when people encrypt, they are hiding something from someone.* And he needed to know if that someone was him.

He set up the intercept properly. Passive receive only, no handshake, nothing that would light up their threat board. The survey team's analyzer could strip the first layer of encryption in about forty minutes per burst, working cold. He recorded three bursts, then fed them through one at a time while the wind worried the tent fabric and the pulse went on underneath, patient as tide.

The first burst decrypted to logistics. Fuel quantities, rotor hours, a request for two more pallets of demolition charges air-dropped to the col before the 20th. Demolition charges. For a rescue operation. Okafor read the line twice, then a third time, feeling the words rearrange themselves in his chest like stones shifting in a scree slope.

* * *

He thought about waking Ulyana.

He actually got as far as unzipping his sleeping bag, one boot on, before he stopped. Because here was the problem, laid out plain: he didn't know yet what he knew. Fragments of a decrypted burst were not evidence. They were a story he might be telling himself because he already distrusted Voss, and distrust made pattern-machines of all of them. Tsering would say the mountain was doing it. Ulyana would say check your instruments twice. Devi —

Devi had signed the contract. Devi had taken the fixer's triple rate with the cognitive-hazard clause folded into page nine like a landmine folded into a road. If Okafor came out of this tent at four in the morning shouting about demolition charges, and it turned out to be nothing, he'd have spent trust they couldn't afford to lose. And if it turned out to be something — well. Then he wanted to know the shape of the whole thing first. All of it. Before anyone else's face changed.

So he stayed in the tent and pulled the second burst.

This one took longer. The encryption nested — a commercial cipher wrapped around something harder, military-grade, and the analyzer chewed on it for nearly two hours while Okafor drank tea gone bitter from oversteeping and watched the signal strength meter breathe with the pulse from up-valley. That detail he noticed without understanding it: every time the pulse cycled, the encrypted carrier strengthened by a fraction of a decibel. As if the transmission and the mountain were somehow the same conversation.

At 0540 the second burst opened like a lock giving way.

*ASSET STATUS REQUEST — SUBJECT COHORT K-7. CONFIRM VIABILITY FOR PHASE TWO EXPOSURE. NOTE: CONTROL GROUP (EXTERNAL CONTRACT PERSONNEL) PERFORMING WITHIN PARAMETERS. CONTINUE OBSERVATION. DO NOT INTERVENE.*

Okafor read it standing up, though there was nowhere to go in a tent four paces long.

Subject cohort. K-7. Seven letters, seven members of the survey team — no. Six. Six missing surveyors plus their team leader made seven, except the leader was somewhere ahead of them with the access codes, so maybe the cohort was the six blanks they'd found serene and nameless at the pass mouth, plus one more designation he couldn't see. It didn't matter. What mattered was the grammar of it. *Subjects.* Not survivors. Not patients. Subjects.

And then the second sentence, the one that turned his hands cold despite the sleeping bag draped over his shoulders like a failed apology: *control group (external contract personnel).*

External contract personnel. Four divers and a guide. Him.

* * *

The third burst was the worst, because by then he understood enough to read it the way it deserved to be read — slowly, line by line, letting each clause detonate on its own schedule.

*VOSS LEDGER — INTERNAL. RESONANCE FIELD DEMONSTRATES RELIABLE MEMORY-STATE MODIFICATION AT RANGE OF 2.3 KM AND CLOSING. APPLICATION VECTOR CONFIRMED: TARGETED OVERWRITE VIA CALIBRATED EXPOSURE. DEFENSE APPLICATIONS DIVISION ASSESSES MARKET VALUE AT [REDACTED]. REQUIREMENTS FOR WEAPONIZATION: (1) COMPLETE GEOMETRIC MAPPING OF SOURCE CHAMBER, (2) EXTRACTION OF CALIBRATION DATA FROM PRIMED HUMAN SUBJECTS, (3) SECURE SITE BEFORE MONSOON ONSET. NOTE RE: CONTRACT STRUCTURE — SALVAGE RETAINER SERVES DUAL PURPOSE. SUPPLIES SITE ACCESS AND UNDECLARED TEST POPULATION. COST EFFICIENCY RATED EXCELLENT.*

Cost efficiency rated excellent.

Okafor sat down on the equipment case because his legs had opinions of their own. Outside, dawn was doing its gray work on the valley, turning the glacier above them the color of old bone. Somewhere out there Ulyana was probably already awake, counting her morning miles, and Devi was making tea the way he always did, too much sugar, humming some Lagos pop song off-key, and none of them knew. None of them knew that the contract that had brought them up this mountain had been written not to rescue anyone but to *deliver* them — that they were the control group, the undeclared test population, the cost-efficient part of a weapons program.

He thought about the clause on page nine. *Cognitive hazards.* He'd laughed about it at the time, in the office in Kochi, tapping the paper with his finger. "Cognitive hazards," he'd said. "Like the mountain's going to make us stupid." And the fixer had smiled his small dry smile and said nothing, because the fixer had known exactly what the hazard was, and the hazard was the point.

His own memory chose that moment to betray him — or to serve him, he couldn't decide. His mother's voice, from years ago, from the kitchen in Enugu, telling him: *Chidi, when someone shows you who they are, believe them.* Except nobody had shown anything. That was the elegance of it. Voss hadn't shown them who he was; he'd let them assume. A rescue contract, signed in good faith by good people, and every assumption they'd made had been load-bearing for the lie.

Okafor wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand and got angry instead, which was better, anger being a fuel that worked at altitude where grief sputtered.

He copied everything. Three bursts, raw captures, decrypted transcripts, timestamps, bearing data — onto the survey team's ruggedized drive, and then again onto the little personal drive he kept in a waterproof pouch against his sternum, the one with his sister's photos on it, because nobody ever searched a man's family pictures. Then he did the thing that separated him from the panic he could feel circling the tent like a bird: he verified. Ran the decryption twice more from the raw capture. Checked the bearings. Confirmed the transmission origin sat squarely inside Voss's camp perimeter. Confirmed the pulse-correlation he'd noticed earlier — measured it properly this time, signal strength against the seismometer trace the survey team had left running, and found the correlation coefficient sitting at 0.91, which was not coincidence, which was architecture. Whatever Voss was transmitting, the mountain was carrying it.

Then, and only then, he unzipped the tent flap and stepped out into the blue morning cold to find Ulyana Nakamura.

* * *

She was at the camp's edge, facing up-valley, doing her stretches in the methodical sequence he'd watched her perform every morning since Kochi — the ritual of a woman who measured her body in distances covered. He stood beside her for a moment without speaking, because you didn't hand a person a live grenade; you let them see your hands first.

"Ulyana."

"Mm." She didn't stop stretching.

"I intercepted traffic from Voss's camp last night. Encrypted bursts. I broke them." He kept his voice level, the way you kept a gauge needle level — no sudden movements. "It's not a rescue. It was never a rescue. We're the test subjects, Ulyana. The survey team is the primary cohort and we're the control group. Voss is here to harvest the resonance for memory-editing technology. Defense applications. There's a weapons division sign-off in the ledger."

She stopped stretching.

For a long moment she said nothing, and Okafor watched her face do something he'd never seen it do: flicker. Not fear exactly. Something older and more private, surfacing and going under again, like a diver checking depth. When she finally spoke, her voice was quiet and very steady, which he knew by now was how she sounded when she was least steady of all.

"The ledger. Did it say why they picked us?"

"It said we were cost-efficient." He hated the words as they left his mouth. "Undeclared test population. The retainer was structured to supply site access and — I'm sorry, Ulyana, but it named us. Contract personnel. Control group."

She nodded slowly, once, the way she nodded when a wreck's hull finally gave up a number she'd been chasing. Then she turned and looked up-valley, toward the pass, toward the source of the pulse, and Okafor saw her jaw set in the particular way it set before a deep dive — the look of a woman deciding how much water was between her and the surface, and whether she could swim it.

"Get Devi," she said. "And Tsering. Don't say anything in front of the escort. And Chidi—" She almost never used his first name. "—if there's anything else in that ledger about me, about my file, about why they chose *me*, I need to know. Today."

"There might be," he said carefully. "There's a fourth burst I haven't cracked yet. It's flagged priority. Addressed to Voss personally."

Her eyes came around to him, and in them he saw the thing he'd been afraid of seeing since the first night at the dead camp: not the question *what does the mountain do to memory*, but the question *what did the mountain take from me, and who wants to sell it back.*

"Crack it," Ulyana said. "Before Voss decides we've outlived our cost efficiency."

Behind them, faint under the wind, the pulse went on — four seconds, four seconds, four seconds — and high on the glacier, catching the first sun, Okafor saw the glint of glass and metal that meant Voss's forward team had broken camp and was moving up-valley after them.
