## 11. The Ascent Begins

Tsering Dorje had climbed the Kharang Pass eleven times in his life, and every one of those times he had been alone.

Not alone in the sense of solitude — he'd guided trekkers, surveyors, a pair of German geologists who complained about the altitude as if the mountain owed them an apology. Alone in the sense that no one he loved had ever stood on the ice with him. His grandmother's rule was absolute: no family above the treeline. The singing stone took what it wanted, she said, and it started with the ones you carried in your heart. He had honored her rule for forty years, and now, standing at the mouth of the pass with his breath steaming in the gray dawn light, he broke it by bringing strangers — which was worse, because strangers were only hearts he hadn't learned to carry yet.

He looked down the line of them. Ulyana Nakamura adjusting the straps of her pack with the economy of someone who had dressed underwater more often than on land. Devi beside her, chewing the inside of his cheek, guilt already visible in the set of his shoulders like weather coming over a ridge. Okafor checking his instruments for the fourth time in ten minutes, the pulse meter cradled against his chest like something injured. And behind them, walking where they were told to walk, hands folded, eyes soft and empty as river stones: two of the blank ones. Mira. Anselm. Names Tsering had given them himself three days ago, because they could not say their own, and a person without a name is a person the wind can take.

They were going up. All of them. Into the throat of the thing that had eaten eight minds whole.

Tsering spat to the side, touched the cairn at the pass mouth — old habit, older than any religion anyone had written down — and turned uphill.

* * *

The first hour was kind, the way mountains are kind before they decide not to be. A goat track widened into a moraine shelf, the morning sun climbing the eastern wall in a slow gold tide. Ulyana set a pace that surprised him — steady, metronomic, neither fast nor slow, the pace of a woman who measured distance the way other people measured time. She had told him once, around a fire at basecamp, that she counted miles the way monks counted breaths. That a mile was the smallest unit of trust between herself and the world. He hadn't understood then. Watching her move now, each step placed exactly where the last one had promised solid ground, he began to.

Mira walked behind her like a shadow that had learned to imitate its owner. The blank woman did not stumble. That was the thing about them that kept Tsering awake at night — they moved well, ate when fed, slept when told, laughed at nothing in particular with a serenity that was worse than screaming. Their bodies remembered how to be human even after whatever lived in the rock had lifted the rest out of them like marrow from a bone.

"Guide." Anselm had drifted up alongside him, which the blanks never did; they held formation like sheep. "There's water under the ice here. I can hear it."

Tsering stopped. Listened. Under the crust of last winter's snowfield, yes — meltwater moving through a channel he knew from summer crossings, invisible now beneath two meters of ice. A man would have to know this mountain twenty years to hear that.

"How do you know that?" he asked.

Anselm smiled. It was a beautiful smile, untroubled, and it made Tsering's skin crawl. "I don't know," he said pleasantly. "I just know."

* * *

By midday they had gained nine hundred meters and the kindness was over. The track narrowed onto a traverse above a gully that dropped away in loose grey scree, hundreds of meters of it, funneling down into shadow. Tsering had crossed this section in every season. In autumn it was honest — you could see the rock, pick your line, move fast. But the night's cold had glazed the upper slope, and now the sun was working at the frozen face, thawing what should have stayed frozen, and the whole wall above them ticked and settled like a house in a fire.

"Rope up," Ulyana ordered, and there was no argument in her voice, none of the negotiation Devi might have attempted. "Okafor, you're on my rope. Blanks in the middle. Tsering leads."

He led. Of course he led. It was his mountain, his grandmother's mountain, and if the singing stone meant to take anyone today it would have to go through him first.

Halfway across the traverse he heard it — a crack, high and sharp, like ice on a lake, but wrong, too deep, too mineral. Rock, not ice. He froze, hand flat against the wall, and looked up into the sun-glare where the sound had come from and saw nothing, which was how rockfall always announced itself: with absence.

"Move. Now. Don't run — move smooth." He heard Ulyana translating his urgency into motion behind him, heard Okafor's breathing go ragged, heard the blanks obey instantly, terribly obedient, stepping where Ulyana stepped because Ulyana had become, in three days, the shape their emptiness organized itself around.

The first stone came down the gully to their left with a sound like a door slamming in the earth. Then five more. Then a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and a sheet of face-rock the size of a truck hood peeled off the wall thirty meters above Devi and went over.

Tsering saw it happen the way you see things in dreams — clearly, slowly, uselessly. Devi looked up. Devi pressed himself flat. The slab hit the traverse where he had been standing, burst, and the shrapnel of it went past Tsering's shoulder close enough to feel the wind of it, hot stone smelling of iron and ancient sea.

Silence. Dust drifting gold in the sunlight. The gully swallowing its debris in small hungry clicks, stone chasing stone down into the dark.

"Devi." Ulyana's voice, stripped to wire. "Devi, report."

"I'm here." A cough. "I'm — my pack's not. My pack's in the gully."

His pack, with most of the food. With the satellite phone. With the medical kit. Tsering felt the arithmetic of the climb rearrange itself behind his eyes, cold and exact: four days of food became two and a half. Two ropes became one and a half. The margin between them and the snowline, which had been thin, was now a knife's edge.

"We don't go down after it," Ulyana said. Not asking. Telling. "We don't lose hours we don't have."

"The sat phone—" Okafor began.

"Is gone. We have mine, and Voss has the other frequencies anyway." She said it flatly, and Tsering caught what lived underneath the flatness: she had known about Voss's interception for a day now, since Okafor's discovery, and she had already rebuilt her plans around being listened to. A woman who planned around her enemies' ears. He was beginning to understand why Devi followed her, and why it frightened him.

They roped tighter, redistributed weight, and moved. Behind them, far below on the valley floor, glinting like a bead of mercury among the pines, Tsering saw the flash of glass. Optics. Someone watching them with magnification, patient as a vulture, keeping pace without ever seeming to move.

Voss's men. They had been shadowing them since basecamp — never closing, never speaking, a presence at the edge of vision like a headache you keep forgetting you have. Tsering marked the position, memorized it, and said nothing. There was nothing yet worth saying. But he noted, with the particular bitterness of a man watching his own country used against him, that the watchers moved well on rock. Soldiers, or men paid enough to pretend.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow below the second icefall, sheltered from the wind that came down the pass in long cold breaths, and Tsering lay awake listening to the mountain breathe.

It wasn't silence up here. People thought mountains were silent, people who had never slept on one. The glacier groaned and ticked. Wind combed the ridgelines. Water ran somewhere under everything, all the time, the mountain's slow blood. And underneath all of it — faint, so faint he could convince himself he imagined it, except that Okafor's meter read it too and the blanks leaned toward it in their sleep like plants toward a window — the hum. Low. Rhythmic. Patient. Rising and falling in a pattern that was almost, but not quite, a melody.

His grandmother had called it the stone's dreaming. She said the mountain sang the lives it had taken, over and over, the way a woman sings to a child that won't stop crying. He had thought it a story when he was young. He had spent three days among the blanks and he no longer knew what a story was.

Near midnight he got up to check the rope anchors and found Ulyana awake, sitting against a boulder with her boots on, watching the stars wheel over the col they would cross tomorrow.

"You sleep?" he asked.

"No." She didn't apologize for it. "You?"

"Some." A lie between professionals. He sat down next to her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to make clear it was strategy, not intimacy. Above them the icefall gleamed blue-black, a frozen staircase of broken towers, and beyond it the col, and beyond the col — though nothing showed at night — the high valley where the Passage waited.

"Your grandmother," Ulyana said. "The rule about the treeline. You broke it for us."

"For them," Tsering corrected. "For the blanks. They can't choose. Somebody has to choose for them until they can again." He paused. "And for you, maybe. I haven't decided."

She almost smiled. "Honest."

"My grandmother was honest. It's inherited." He pulled his jacket tighter. "You know what's above us. You've seen what it did to eight trained people. Why are you still climbing?"

Ulyana was quiet for a long time. Long enough that he watched a satellite crawl across the sky and vanish behind the western ridge.

"Because the thing that took them," she said finally, "took me too. Twelve years ago. Different mechanism, same result. I woke up in a hospital with no past and everyone telling me to be grateful I was alive." Her voice didn't waver; it never seemed to waver; it just went quieter, denser, like water going deep. "I built myself out of miles after that. One at a time. Every dive, every job, every distance swum or climbed or walked — proof that the person doing it existed, even if I couldn't prove who she'd been before." She looked at the icefall. "Up there is the only place on earth that might give her back. And up there is also the thing that will eat these two if we turn around and leave them. So I'm climbing for both reasons, and I can't tell you which one is heavier, and I've stopped pretending I need to."

Tsering nodded slowly. In the hollow behind them, Mira stirred in her sleep and murmured something — a word, half a word — and both of them turned to listen, and the word dissolved before it could be caught, and Mira sighed and went quiet, and the hum pulsed once, gently, like something turning over.

"It talks to them in their sleep," Tsering said.

"It talks to all of us in our sleep," Ulyana said. "That's what Okafor's data says. Some of us just don't answer."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old bone, and with it came the wind, and with the wind came the knowledge Tsering had been carrying since basecamp and had not yet spoken aloud, because speaking it made it true: the snowline was coming down to meet them.

The monsoon was early. Everyone had said so — the fixer's weather feed, Okafor's satellite pulls, the wet smell riding up-valley on the southern winds for two days now. What that meant at altitude was simple and merciless: within seventy-two hours, maybe less, the pass would fill with cloud, and the cloud would fill with snow, and the snow would not stop until spring. Whatever happened on this mountain would happen in the next three days or not at all.

He called the halt at the base of the icefall and laid it out the way his father had taught him to lay out bad news: plainly, completely, once.

"One route up. Through the icefall, across the glacier, over the col. Two days hard travel if the weather holds, three if it doesn't, impossible if it breaks early. Down-climbing the icefall in storm is death. Once we commit to the icefall, there is no back. Anyone who wants to turn around turns around now, and I'll take the blanks down myself and meet you on the far side in spring, if there is a far side."

No one spoke for a moment. Then Devi said, "We go up," too quickly, the voice of a man outrunning his own doubt. Okafor said, "We go up," with the grim calm of a man who had read the contractor's transmissions and knew what waited behind them was worse than anything ahead. Ulyana said, "We go up," and it wasn't a decision, it was a fact, announced.

The blanks said nothing. Mira looked at the icefall with an expression Tsering couldn't read — wonder, maybe, or recognition, some ghost of feeling moving behind the empty glass of her — and then she looked at Ulyana and stepped forward to stand where Ulyana stood, and that was her answer, and it was the most frightening answer Tsering had ever received from another human being.

They fixed the rope. Tsering led into the icefall, into the blue shadows between the towers, axe biting, crampons squeaking, the hum growing beneath the ice with every meter of altitude like a heartbeat under a floorboard. Behind him the rope came taut, six lives strung along sixty meters of line over crevasses that went down further than light.

At the top of the first pitch he looked back. The valley floor was already distant, the basecamp a smudge, the treeline a dark thread — his grandmother's border, crossed, irrecoverable. Far below and behind, at the edge of resolution, the watchers had stopped. Three figures on the moraine, standing still, looking up. Not following. Waiting. As if they knew something Tsering didn't about what the mountain would do to the people climbing it.

As if they were letting the mountain do their work for them.

He turned back to the ice, drove his axe in, and climbed. There was no way back down. There was only the col, and the storm gathering somewhere south of the world like a debt come due, and the humming heart of the mountain waiting above them with everything it had ever swallowed — including, if the blanks' borrowed memories were true, a drowned girl who had grown into a woman who counted miles, standing on his rope, climbing toward her own name.
