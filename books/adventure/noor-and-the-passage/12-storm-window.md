## 12. Storm Window

The satellite phone weighed more than it should have. Everything did, at four thousand meters — the water bottles, the rope coils, the silence between breaths. Ulyana sat on a flat shelf of granite above their camp with the handset cradled against her ear like something that might bite, listening to a ring tone that took eleven seconds to travel from this wind-scoured ledge to a satellite and back down to some operations room in a city she'd never see again.

Eleven seconds. She counted them the way she counted everything now. Miles, meters, minutes, seconds. The habit had kept her alive underwater for twelve years; it was keeping her sane up here, where distance had stopped being something you measured and started being something that measured *you*.

The line connected.

"Nakamura." The voice was Voss's, dry as paper, familiar from the helicopter pad two days ago. "Good signal. I'll be brief."

"You're never brief."

"I'm brief when there's weather." A pause, the faint hiss of encryption doing its work. "I have a fresh feed off INSAT-3DR. The depression in the Bay has organized faster than the models predicted. Landfall on the coast in roughly sixty hours, but the leading edge hits your range in forty. Maybe less if the jet stream dips. After that, the pass closes. Snow, then rain, then snow again. Nothing moves through Kharang until April."

Ulyana looked down-valley. Below her, the camp was a scatter of tarps and figures: Devi coiling rope with the mechanical patience of a man avoiding conversation, Okafor hunched over his radio rig, Tsering standing at the treeline facing up-valley the way he'd been facing it for three days, as if the mountain might blink first. Two of the blank ones — Mara and the tall man they'd taken to calling Aldous because he answered to nothing else — sat by the fire with their hands folded, serene as churchgoers.

Forty hours. She'd been doing the arithmetic all morning without letting herself finish it. Twenty-two kilometers to the col. Fourteen hundred vertical meters. Two of their party moving at the pace of people who didn't fully believe their own legs existed. And behind them, somewhere below the cloud deck, Voss's team with their better boots and their better gear and their helicopters that would all be useless in forty hours too.

"Say the rest of it," she said.

Voss's voice didn't change. That was the thing about him she'd learned fastest — no inflection wasted, no warmth manufactured, no threat delivered with theater. Just terms.

"The rest of it is an offer."

* * *

She made him repeat it twice, not because she hadn't heard it but because hearing it twice let her pretend the second time was for Devi's benefit, who wasn't listening, who was thirty meters below her pretending to work.

A drive. Solid-state, encrypted, palm-sized. On it, Voss said, were her files. Her accident reports — the real ones, not the sanitized salvage-board summary she'd read a hundred times looking for a sentence that would explain why a routine dive off a decommissioned trawler had ended with her waking up in a Kochi hospital with twelve years missing and a scar she couldn't account for. Dive logs. Medical imaging. Witness statements. "And the original survey data," Voss said, "from the vessel you salvaged. The one that isn't on any manifest."

Her past, compressed into eighty grams of plastic and silicon.

The price: deliver the team. Not fight them. Not stall them. Deliver them — Mara, Aldous, the others still up-valley near the chamber mouth, and the survey leader whose access codes Voss needed to safely instrument the Passage before the monsoon sealed it. Walk them down to the extraction point at the lower moraine where his men would be waiting, and hand them over, and take the drive, and walk away rich and whole.

"Whole," Ulyana repeated. The word came out flat.

"You've spent twelve years measuring distances," Voss said, and she hated how precisely he'd read her, how much someone had told him. "You count miles because miles are the only thing that stayed consistent after the accident. I'm offering you the years themselves. Not a reconstruction. The actual record. Who you were. What happened. Why."

"And what happens to the blanks?"

"They get their memories restored under controlled conditions. Medical supervision. Better outcomes than your alternative, which is that they wander the high country during a whiteout because you decided sentiment was a survival strategy."

It was a good lie because it was mostly true. That was the craft of it. Every offer Voss made came wrapped in a skin of plausibility, and you had to cut through the skin to find out what it was wrapped around, and sometimes — this was the trap — the thing inside was also true.

She said she'd think about it. He said the window was closing on both of them, and hung up, and the eleven seconds back felt longer than the eleven seconds out.

* * *

Devi found her an hour later at the edge of the glacier's lateral moraine, where the rock gave way to ice the color of old bone. He carried two cups of the tea Tsering made from something that grew under stones and tasted like being scolded. He handed her one and stood beside her and didn't talk, which was unlike him, and the silence had a shape she recognized, and she didn't like the shape.

"Forty hours," he said finally.

"Thirty-eight now."

"Are we going to make the col?"

"Tsering says yes if we move tonight. Night movement on the ice is stupid, so yes means stupid, so probably yes."

He laughed, briefly, without much air for it. Then: "What did he offer you?"

She held the tea and watched steam come off it in ribbons that the wind tore apart immediately. There was no version of this conversation that went well. There was only the version where she chose what to tell him and lived inside the choice.

"A drive," she said. "My file. The accident. All of it."

Devi went very still. Twelve years of partnership had taught her every version of his stillness — the stillness before a deep dive, the stillness when a client lied about cargo weight, the stillness now, which was new, which had guilt in it somewhere, though she couldn't have said how she knew. Instinct. The same instinct that had told her to keep coordinates she shouldn't keep, back on the wreck that wasn't.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's everything you've wanted."

"It's everything I lost. Those aren't the same thing anymore." She turned to look at him. His face was doing something complicated around the eyes. "You knew he had it. Didn't you."

"Ulyana—"

"Don't answer yet. Answer me later, when I ask you a different question, and answer it honestly, and we'll both know if you can." She drank the tea. It was terrible. It was exactly what she needed. "Right now I need to know about the route. Tsering wants to cross the icefall at first light instead of tonight. Says the seracs shed in the afternoon heat and we should sleep while we can. Okafor thinks we should move now and sleep never. Someone has to decide, and it's going to be me, and I need my partner's read, not my partner's face."

His face eased, gratefully, into the professional question. That was the tragedy of them, she thought, watching him lay out the icefall options with his hands: they were excellent partners and had never quite figured out how to be anything else, and now the mountain was going to force the issue at altitude with a storm coming.

"We move at first light," Devi said. "Okafor's right that the window's shrinking, but a broken ankle shrinks it to zero. We sleep four hours, cross cold, gain the shoulder by noon, and we're at the col by tomorrow night with the storm on our heels instead of on our heads."

"Then that's what we do."

* * *

Sleep didn't come for her. It rarely did above three thousand meters, and it came even less often with a palm-sized drive sitting in her imagination like a stone she couldn't put down.

She lay in her bag and ran the offer through the same machinery she used on salvage contracts. Asset value: incalculable, personally. Cost: five human beings who had already been robbed once by the thing in the mountain, handed over to be robbed again by a man with a ledger. Risk profile: Voss had no reason to honor the deal once he had the team — the drive cost him nothing to promise and everything to deliver, and men who weaponized memory did not build their businesses on honoring bargains. Probability of clean outcome: low. Probability she could live with herself afterward: she turned that number over and found she couldn't compute it at all, which was its own kind of answer.

Because here was the thing she circled all night and never landed on: she didn't actually know what was on the drive. Voss said it was her past. But Voss's entire operation was built on the fact that a past could be written. What if the file was accurate? What if it was worse than accurate — curated, shaped, a memory engineered as surely as the Passage engineered the survey team? She had spent twelve years building a self out of mileage and competence and Devi's loyalty and the specific weight of a dive helmet, and every year the structure bore more load, and she trusted it the way you trust a tested mooring. The drive was a stranger offering to swap her mooring for one she couldn't inspect.

And underneath that, quieter, the current she wouldn't look at directly: part of her wanted it anyway. Part of her lay in the dark and imagined being twenty-six again for one hour, knowing her mother's voice, knowing whatever small harbor she'd come from, and the wanting was enormous and humiliating and it did not go away when she named it.

Tsering found her awake near midnight. He didn't ask why. He sat down on the rock beside her bag with the unhurried ease of a man who had slept at altitude since before she was born, and for a while they just listened to the glacier settle, those deep internal groans like something turning over in its sleep.

"My grandmother," he said, "told me about the singing stone when I was seven. She said: it will give you back whatever you miss most. And I asked her, what if what you miss most is a lie? And she said that was the right question, and that the stone never answers it, and that was how you know to walk away." He fed a stick to the fire. "You are asking the right question, Nakamura."

"I haven't decided anything."

"No. But your face has been deciding things all evening, and your face is wrong." He rose, joints cracking like the ice. "Whatever Voss offered you — he offered it because he needs you more than you need him. Remember that when you do the arithmetic. Men who need something always price it generously."

* * *

First light came gray and windless, which Tsering called the mountain holding its breath, and which everyone understood to mean hurry.

They crossed the icefall in a roped line with the sun barely clearing the eastern ridge, the light blue and metallic, their crampons biting ice that groaned under them in long slow complaints. Okafor led the middle of the rope with Mara clipped behind him, talking to her constantly — not instructions, just words, his voice steady, because he'd discovered on day two that the blanks moved better when someone narrated the world to them, anchored them in a present tense they could trust. *Left foot on the blue ice. Good. There's a crevasse breathing on your right, don't look at it, look at my shoulders.* Mara followed his voice like a handrail. Sometimes she hummed along with it, tunelessly, and the humming echoed off the seracs and sounded almost like the pulse from up-valley, and nobody said so.

By noon they'd gained the shoulder and the pass opened ahead of them — a vast stony corridor climbing toward the col, flanked by walls that threw the wind into confusion, and beyond the col, visible for the first time, the notch of the upper valley where the Passage lived, where the remaining blanks waited, where the hum lived. Ulyana stood with her binoculars and picked out movement: tiny figures on the upper slopes, six or seven of them, moving with purpose. Voss's advance team, dropped yesterday before the weather window shut, digging in at the chamber approach. They'd beaten the storm to the high ground. Of course they had.

Okafor's satellite unit crackled at 1400. He waved her over, and she crouched under the tarp he'd rigged against the wind and listened to the same dry voice from the ledge.

"Thirty-four hours," Voss said. "My team is in position. Yours is half a day behind and slowing. Nakamura, I'm going to say this once without encryption polish. You're a salvage operator. You know how to read a wreck. So read this one: the monsoon takes the range, my aircraft are gone for eight months, my board writes off the asset, and the only person who loses anything is you — because the drive gets destroyed with everything else, per protocol, and you spend the rest of your life counting miles. Or you bring me the team tomorrow, and you leave this valley with your history in your pocket. I don't care which you choose. But choose tonight."

The line went dead. Ulyana crouched under the tarp a moment longer, listening to the fabric snap in the wind, feeling the offer sit in her chest like a swallowed hook.

Devi was watching her from ten meters away. He'd heard his end of nothing, but he'd heard enough of everything else, and his face was doing the complicated thing again, and this time she could read it, and what it said frightened her more than the storm did.

Because Devi knew something. Had known it, maybe, since the fixer's office, since the clause about cognitive hazards, since some conversation she hadn't been part of. And tomorrow — at the col, where Voss would be waiting with guns and a deadline and the last helicopter fuel in the world — whatever Devi knew was going to come out at the worst possible moment, at the worst possible altitude, unless she made him tell her first.

Tonight, she thought. Before the ice. Before the col. Before the mountain got a vote.

She caught Devi's eye across the wind and held it, and jerked her chin toward the shelter of the boulders, and watched him understand, and watched him be afraid of understanding.

"Walk with me," she said. "We need to talk. Now — while we still know who we are."
