## 13. Devi's Bargain

The satellite phone weighed more than it should have. Devi knew it was four hundred grams of plastic and lithium, the same as any other satphone he'd carried across three continents of salvage jobs, but in his palm, crouched behind the survey tent with the wind moaning through the guy-lines, it felt like a stone pulled from a riverbed — dense with everything that had already happened to it.

Forty hours. That was what Okafor's intercept had given them. Forty hours before the monsoon made landfall and closed the Kharang Pass until spring, and somewhere inside those forty hours, Voss was coming for them.

Devi had watched Ulyana sleep for an hour before he made the call. She lay on her side in the small dome tent they shared, her breathing slow and even, one hand curled near her face the way she slept on boats too, always ready, some part of her never fully off duty even now, even here at five thousand meters with her lungs working overtime just to keep her body fed. Twelve years since the accident. Twelve years since the water took her memory and gave back a woman who measured her life in miles because miles were the only thing that stayed put.

He had loved her for nine of those years. Not said it — neither of them dealt well with words that couldn't be audited against a manifest — but loved her the way you love a bearing you steer by. And that was exactly why his thumb hovered over the call button like it was a detonator.

Because he'd read the file. That was the thing Okafor didn't know, the thing Tsering didn't know, the thing that had been sitting in Devi's chest for two days now like swallowed ballast. When Voss's advance team had dropped their supply cache at the col — ostensibly a goodwill resupply, actually a leash — there had been a document case among the crates, sealed, mislabeled as medical records. Devi had opened it because opening things was half his job and because trust was the other half and he'd wanted to know which half was heavier.

Inside: the contract. The real one. Not the triple-rate salvage agreement he and Ulyana had signed in a hotel bar that smelled of fried onions and diesel, but the layer beneath it. Test subjects, the document called them. Acquired via standard recruitment channels. Exposure parameters to be documented across a minimum of six individuals with pre-existing memory deficits preferred.

*Pre-existing memory deficits preferred.*

Ulyana's name was on page three.

They hadn't recruited a salvage diver. They'd recruited a woman whose past had already been hollowed out, on the theory — Voss's theory, initialed in blue ink — that a subject with nothing to lose would be less resistant to resonance exposure, and more pliable afterward. Her accident twelve years ago wasn't even in the file, but the shape of its absence was. *No next of kin. No durable social ties. Ideal profile.* Someone had gone looking for divers with holes in them, and found her, and reeled her in with money she needed and work she was good at, and none of it — not the wreck job, not the navy launch that warned them off, not this whole doomed expedition — had ever been about equipment recovery.

Devi had sat with that file open on his knees for forty minutes while the wind tried to take the tent apart, and by the end of it he had learned something about himself that he would have paid good money never to know: that he could hold a betrayal in his hands, understand it completely, and still think first about how to keep the woman he loved from being hurt by it twice.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd done, alone, in the dark, while Ulyana slept eight feet away: if she learned the full truth — that her accident might not have been an accident, that the blank years weren't random cruelty but possibly design, that the man who'd hired them had built the entire expedition around using her as a laboratory animal — she would break. Not dramatically. Ulyana didn't do dramatic. She'd go quiet, the way she went quiet after bad dives, and then she'd do something irreversible. She'd take the drive Voss was offering. She'd load every byte of her stolen past into her own head to find out what had been done to her, and whatever was on that drive would finish what the water started. He'd seen what the Passage did to people who went looking for themselves. The survey team had walked into that valley whole and come out serene and nameless, and two of them were following his partner up a mountain right now like children who'd misplaced their own faces.

So when the satphone connected and Voss's voice came through — dry, unhurried, the voice of a man reading terms aloud in a room with no windows — Devi was ready to trade.

"Nakamura's partner." Voss didn't use names. Names were data points and data points got attached to things. "Calling to surrender the survey leader's access codes?"

"Calling to make a different deal."

A pause. In the background, wind, and the faint percussion of a helicopter rotor being tested — Voss keeping his aircraft warm, counting the same forty hours Devi was counting.

"I'm listening."

"You leave Ulyana out of it. Whatever comes next — the harvest, the mapping, the containment, whatever you're planning to do with that valley before the weather seals it — she walks off this mountain clean. No exposure. No follow-up. Her name comes out of your files the way it went in: quietly."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange I get the codes from the survey leader myself, and I hand them over, and I don't tell her what's in your paperwork." Devi heard his own voice go flat, professional, the voice he used with clients who tried to renegotiate mid-job. "You get your data. She gets her life. The one she has now. Not the one you people took from her."

Another pause, longer. When Voss spoke again there was something almost warm in it, which was worse than menace. Warmth from men like Voss was just lubricant.

"You've read the file, then."

"I've read enough."

"Then you know I'm not the villain of your story, Mr.—" a beat, deliberate, letting Devi hear that he still wasn't going to be granted a name either, "—partner. I didn't arrange her accident. I inherited her profile. There are people above me who've been curating subjects like her for a decade. What I can offer is containment. If she goes up that pass and touches that chamber again, unmanaged, the resonance will take the rest of her. You've seen what it does. Your comms man is already carrying someone else's childhood around in his head like contraband." A breath. "I'm offering to save her from herself. You're offering to help me do it. We're not so far apart."

"We're a mountain apart," Devi said. "She doesn't touch your tech. She doesn't take your drive. She goes down the south side with Tsering and the blanks and she never hears the word Kharang again."

"The drive stays on the table. It's hers by right, if she wants it."

"It stays lost."

Voss laughed softly, without humor. "Guilt is an expensive fuel, Mr. Partner. Burns fast. I'll give you until the col. Bring me the codes and your terms stand. Fail, and I proceed under my original authority, which includes your partner, whose consent — I'll remind you — was signed for, by you, on her behalf, in a binding instrument." Another soft laugh. "You did sign for her. Page eleven. Check your copy."

Devi checked nothing. He hung up.

* * *

He told himself, walking back toward the campfire, that what he'd done was protection. That was the word he'd chosen for it and he intended to live inside that word like a hermit crab lives inside a borrowed shell, never looking too closely at whether it fit.

Protection. Yes. He had negotiated a cordon around Ulyana. He had bought her exclusion from the experiment with the only currency he possessed that Voss valued: the access codes locked in the mind of a woman who no longer knew her own name. Mira-something, once. Now she answered to nothing, smiled at everyone, described other people's drowned childhoods in perfect detail while her own sat sealed in the rock up-valley like cargo in a flooded hold.

Devi had spent two evenings with her already, sitting beside her at the fire, asking gentle questions, watching for the flicker. The others thought he was being kind. He was casing a vault. Every fragment he coaxed loose — a phrase about tide tables, a reflexive hand-sign that meant *all clear* in some survey-team shorthand, a half-hummed interval that made the tall one, Anselm, stop chewing and stare — was a piece of the code sequence, and Devi was assembling it the way he assembled anything: patiently, without letting the pieces see him wanting them.

That was the part that shamed him most. Not the lying. Lying, he could carry; he'd lied to customs officers and insurance adjusters and once, memorably, to a monsoon. It was the tenderness. He had to be tender to extract from her, and the tenderness was real, and the extraction was real, and the two of them lived together in his chest like tenants who hadn't been introduced.

At the fire, Ulyana looked up when he sat down. Wind-flushed, eyes bright with altitude, a tin cup of tea cradled in both hands. Behind her the peaks went black against a sky where the stars were starting to be eaten by cloud — the monsoon's outriders, arriving early, exactly as the forecast had promised and nobody had hoped.

"You were gone a while," she said.

"Checked the cache lines. Storm's going to hit the col before it hits us. We should move tomorrow, first light, no later."

"Mm." She drank. Then, without looking at him, in the tone she used for hull inspections — flat, factual, devastating: "Your hands were shaking when you came back into camp. Both of them. You don't shake after gear checks."

"Cold," Devi said.

"At four thousand meters, sure." She set down the cup. "Devi. Nine years. I've seen you lie to port authorities with better craft than that."

And there it was — the moment, the exact seam in the dive where the pressure differential flips and the water decides for you. He could tell her. Right now, firelight, two cups of tea, the truth like a weight-belt finally unbuckled: *they recruited you because you were broken, they may have broken you on purpose, I read the file, I called Voss, I traded a stranger's memories for your safety, and I decided — alone, in the dark, eight feet from where you were sleeping — what you could bear to know.*

Or he could say *cold*, and smile, and let the shell hold.

He said, "Cold."

Ulyana held his eyes for three seconds longer than comfort allowed. Then she nodded slowly, the way she nodded at gauges that read wrong but not dangerously wrong, and picked up her tea, and said, "First light, then. Wake me for watch. I'll take second."

She trusted him. That was the unbearable part. She was handing him the night watch, the route, the blanks, her life at altitude — handing all of it to a man who had spent the evening selling pieces of her future to the enemy, and doing it with total, unguarded confidence, because in nine years he had never once given her reason not to.

Love weaponized. He'd heard the phrase somewhere, maybe from Okafor, maybe in a book, and hadn't understood it until now. It wasn't that love became a weapon. It was that love made such a precise, silent weapon of the lover — calibrated to the millimeter, because who else knew exactly where all the soft places were?

* * *

He didn't sleep. First light came gray and mean, the clouds lower, the air tasting of iron and distance-water. They broke camp in the methodical silence of people who have stopped talking because talking costs breath they might need later. Tsering moved ahead to scout the moraine; Okafor packed the instruments with the careful motions of a man guarding something fragile in his skull; the two blank survey members — Mira and Anselm — helped without being asked, serene and useful, which somehow remained the most frightening thing about them.

Ulyana rigged the rope teams. Of course she did. She moved through the camp like the answer to a question nobody had finished asking, knots going tight under her fingers, checking everyone's harness twice, and Devi watched her and felt the shell he was living in start to crack along its spiral seams.

Because here was what he hadn't let himself calculate, back at the fire, back on the phone: Voss's terms required the codes *at the col*. The col was six hours up. And between here and the col, the mountain would do what mountains do — and the mountain did not care about anyone's bargains.

It happened at the second gully. A serac calved somewhere above them, unseen, and the slope below the traverse let go — not big, not dramatic, just a shrug of scree the size of a truck, and Anselm, walking last, went down with it. Rope snapped taut. Ulyana was on the anchor before Devi had finished shouting, boots braced, taking the load through her harness with a grunt, hauling, hand over hand, while Tsering shouted angles and Okafor clung to the rockface white-faced. Ninety seconds. Anselm dangled over the drop, swinging gently, smiling up at them — *smiling* — and Ulyana hauled him out with arms that had pulled engines out of sixty meters of black water, and when it was done she knelt in the scree breathing hard, and Anselm patted her shoulder and said, kindly, "You're very strong. Have you always been strong?" and she laughed, short and startled, and something in Devi's chest tore along a line he hadn't known was there.

She saved people. Reflexively, totally, without ledger or bargain. And he had spent last night in the dark converting her safety into currency, spending a stranger's selfhood to buy hers, and telling himself it was protection.

At the rest halt below the col, she sat beside him on a boulder, sharing a chocolate bar broken unevenly, giving him the bigger piece without comment. Below them the foothills smoked with the first rain-shafts of the approaching front, gray curtains marching up the valleys like slow troops. Above them, the col, and beyond the col, faintly visible through torn cloud, the notch of the pass mouth, and beyond that — though nothing showed — the hum. Okafor said the pulse had strengthened overnight. Said it sounded almost glad they were coming.

"Devi." Ulyana's voice, quiet, around the chocolate. "Whatever's eating you — and something is, you've been carrying it since the cache site, you hold your left arm differently when you're hiding something, you've done it since we met —" she looked straight ahead at the rain curtains, not at him, giving him the mercy of no eye contact, "— you should know that whatever it is, I'd rather have it ugly and true than neat and kind. That's not a threat. It's just... the only diving rule I've ever kept. Never surface on a lie. Bad things grow in the deco stops."

His throat worked. The codes were in his breast pocket — transcribed last night from fragments Mira had surrendered to his gentleness, a sequence of alphanumerics he'd cross-checked against Anselm's humming until he was certain. Six hours' walk from Voss's forward position. All he had to do was deliver them at the col, and the bargain held, and Ulyana walked out of these mountains owned by no one.

All he had to do was betray her one more time, cleanly, and forever.

"I hear you," he said.

She bumped his shoulder with hers — the old gesture, boat-language, *steady as she goes* — and stood, and called the rope order, and started upward.

Devi followed. The col waited above them, and Voss waited at the col, and the rain was climbing the valleys toward all of them, and Devi understood, with the clarity that only arrives after the point where it can do any good, that he had not protected Ulyana Nakamura at all.

He had simply decided, in the dark, eight feet from her sleeping breath, that he knew better than she did what she could survive.

The mountain, he suspected, was about to correct him.
