## 14. The Singing Stone

The last pitch to the chamber was a crack in the mountain's spine, no wider than Ulyana's shoulders, and she went into it the way she went into everything: measured, breath by breath, counting the meters as they passed.

Four hundred and twelve. Four hundred and thirteen. The numbers had been her religion for twelve years, ever since the sea took her memory and left her nothing but distance. Miles logged, meters dived, kilometers run before dawn on whatever deck happened to be under her feet. If life could be reduced to arithmetic, then the blank inside her head was just another number — one she hadn't solved yet, but might.

Tsering went first, his lamp throwing her shadow huge against the wet stone. Behind her came Okafor, breathing hard at altitude, his instruments wrapped in foam and strapped to his chest like an infant. The two blank members of the survey team — Mara and Ilsa, though "blank" wasn't quite right anymore; they had names now, new ones they'd chosen themselves on the glacier, names that belonged to nobody's records — climbed without complaint, without fear, without any of the weight that pressed down on everyone else. That was the strange mercy of them. They carried nothing. They moved through the mountain like water moves through water.

Devi came last. He hadn't met her eyes since the col. She'd stopped asking why. Some questions you save for when there's enough air to survive the answer.

"Close," Tsering said, his voice flattened by the stone into something almost liturgical. "You feel it?"

She did. She'd been feeling it for hours, actually — a pressure below hearing, a fullness in the ears that diving had taught her to read as depth. But here it wasn't depth. It was *presence*. The rock around them was not silent. It hummed, low and continuous, a sound with no source she could locate because it came from everywhere at once, from the grain of the stone itself, as if the entire massif were a struck bell that never finished ringing.

Okafor's instruments confirmed what her body already knew. He held up the recorder without being asked: a waveform, steady, rhythmic, pulsing at just under one cycle per second. The same pulse he'd registered at the abandoned survey camp, ten days and a lifetime ago. But here it was a hundred times stronger, and it wasn't uniform. When he swept the sensor across the wall, the amplitude rose and fell in patterns — clusters, repetitions, structures. Not noise. Something organized.

"It's layered," Okafor said quietly. "Like sediment. Like — "

He stopped himself. He didn't want to say it out loud. Ulyana said it for him, because someone had to.

"Like memory."

* * *

The crack opened.

They stepped out of the throat of the mountain into a space that should not have existed this deep in folded granite — a chamber, high-vaulted, its ceiling lost in shadow above the reach of their lamps. The floor was smooth, polished in a way that water alone couldn't explain, sweeping down in gentle terraces toward a central pool. The pool was the source. Or rather, the pool was where the sound became visible: the water stood in it unmoving, glass-flat, and across its surface played slow rings of light, silver-white, expanding outward in perfect circles, one after another, timed exactly to the pulse. One per second. Like ripples from a heartbeat buried in the rock beneath.

The walls were veined with quartz, and the quartz caught their lamplight and threw it back fractured, so that the whole chamber seemed faintly, impossibly alive. Warm. The air was warm — twenty degrees, maybe more, when everything above them was ice. There was a smell too, faint, mineral, and underneath it something else. Something organic. Old rain. Wet earth. A smell that belonged to forests, to summers, to places no forest or summer had ever been.

Tsering put his hand flat against the wall and left it there for a long moment. His face changed. Ulyana watched it happen — watched the lines around his eyes soften, watched something move behind them that might have been grief or might have been relief.

"My grandmother's stories," he said. "She told me the singing stone holds voices. People who died in the pass, people who walked into it on purpose. She said if you listen long enough, you can hear your mother calling you home." He took his hand back slowly, like lifting it cost him something. "I thought they were stories. Warnings dressed up as wonder so children would stay away." He looked at the pool. "It's not a warning. It's an invitation."

"That's worse," Okafor said.

"Yes," Tsering agreed. "It is."

Ulyana circled the pool's rim, keeping to the outer terrace, professional discipline warring with the pull she felt in her sternum — a tug, gentle and constant, toward the center, toward the light-rings, toward whatever was making the mountain sing. She'd felt currents like this underwater. Thermoclines. Wrecks that breathed. Places where the sea wanted something from you. You learned to respect the pull without obeying it, because divers who obeyed pulls didn't come back.

But this wasn't water. And she wasn't diving. And the pull wasn't going away.

Mara — the woman who had been Dr. Mara Voss-adjacent once, who was now simply Mara, who had chosen herself a new name on the glacier and defended it with her fists — stood at the far edge of the chamber, deliberately apart, arms crossed, facing away from the pool. She refused to look at it. When Ulyana asked if she was all right, she said, "I know what's in there. Part of me is in there. I'm not giving it a second chance to take the rest."

Ilsa, by contrast, stood close to the water, crouched, watching the rings of light with something close to tenderness. "It's beautiful," she said. "It doesn't feel like theft. It feels like — keeping. Like the mountain keeps us. Somewhere in there is the person I was, and she isn't suffering. She's just... archived. Safe. Warm." She smiled, and the smile was serene in a way that frightened Ulyana more than Mara's defiance. "Is that so wrong? To be kept?"

Ulyana didn't answer. She didn't trust herself to.

Because here was the thing she had not said aloud, not to Devi, not to Okafor, not to the dark hours of her own mind: the Passage terrified her less than it tempted her. Everyone else feared losing what they had. Ulyana had already lost it. Twelve years of nothing — a coma, a hospital, a rehabilitation ward where nurses spoke her name like strangers reading it off a chart, a life rebuilt from salvage plans and tide tables because there was nothing else to build from. The survey team's condition wasn't a horror story to her. It was a diagnosis she recognized. She had been blank once. She knew the terrain.

And the mountain, humming its one-per-second heartbeat, was offering her the only thing anyone had ever offered her: the missing years. Not restored — *available*. Sitting in the stone like cargo in a hold, waiting for a diver.

She was a salvage diver. Recovery was the only language she had ever truly spoken.

* * *

It was Okafor who broke the equilibrium, because Okafor always broke things with data before anyone could break them with desire.

"The pulse is structured," he said, hunched over his recorder, headphones on, voice tight with the particular excitement that Ulyana had learned to distrust in him — excitement was how his fear came out. "I've got discrete bands. Distinct packets. It's not one signal, it's thousands. Hundreds of thousands. Each packet has a signature — a decay envelope, a harmonic set — and the signatures cluster." He pulled the headphones off. "These aren't geological formations. Geology doesn't file things. This is storage. This is a library."

"A library of what?" Tsering asked, though his tone said he knew.

"Lives," Okafor said. "Whole lives, compressed. Every packet is a person. Or pieces of persons. Memories, indexed, cross-referenced, preserved at — " he checked his readout, and his voice went strange — "at what looks like perfect fidelity. No degradation. Nothing decays in here. The stone doesn't forget."

The chamber hummed on, indifferent to the enormity of what he was saying. One ring of light after another spread across the dead-calm pool, patient as tide.

"And access?" Ulyana asked. "How do you read it?"

"Contact," Okafor said. "Skin contact. The resonance couples through touch. That's how it takes people — that's how it took the survey team. Prolonged exposure at close range, skin to stone or skin to water, and it starts... writing. Reading and writing both. It reads you while it writes over you." He turned a dial, frowning. "Short contact should be safe. Seconds. Maybe a minute. Beyond that—" he shrugged, unhappy— "beyond that we're guessing. The survey team's logs suggest they mapped this chamber for six days before the blanks started. Six days of touching the walls, sampling the water, running cables through the pool. Six days of coupling."

Six days. Ulyana thought about that number the way she thought about bottom times. Decompression limits. Saturation ceilings. Everything had a dose, and every dose had a price. The survey team had paid theirs without knowing the currency.

"So we don't touch anything," Tsering said flatly. "We look, we record, we go."

"We came to destroy it," Ulyana reminded him. Voss's demolition charges sat in Devi's pack and hers, packed in wax and shock cord, primed but unplaced. The plan — such as it was, forged in the whiteout and ratified at gunpoint by circumstance — was to breach the resonance chamber, collapse the pool, bury the library. Forty hours until the monsoon sealed the range. Less now. Maybe thirty. Maybe twenty-five.

But standing in the chamber, hearing it hum, watching the light move across water that had never known wind — the plan felt different here. Smaller. Cruder. Dropping charges into a library. Burning the Library of Alexandria because some of the books were cursed.

Ulyana knew which side of that argument history had landed on. She also knew that some books are cursed for a reason.

* * *

She told herself she was going to check the placement points for the charges. That was the professional fiction, and she wore it well: hard hat, gloves, the calm narration of a woman doing a job. She walked the lower terrace with a slate and a grease pencil, marking fracture lines, measuring stand-off distances, doing genuine work. The chamber cooperated. The stone was sound. Two charges at the pool's rim, one deeper in the fissure behind it, and the resonance would choke on its own rubble.

Then she reached the near edge of the pool, where the terraced stone dipped lowest and the light-rings passed closest to the shore, and she stopped.

Not because the work stopped. Because she stopped.

The rings came toward her feet and passed through the spot where she stood, one per second, silver, silent, passing through solid rock as if rock were no obstacle at all, as if the light were not light but something else wearing light's clothing. And each time a ring crossed her, she felt it. Faintly. A tick of warmth along her spine. A flicker behind her eyes. Not pain. Recognition. As if each ring were a page turning somewhere, and every turn was a word she almost remembered.

She knelt. She told herself it was to inspect the placement point. Her knee told a different story. Her gloved hand came to rest on the stone at the water's edge, palm down, fingers spread, and through the glove she felt it immediately — the coupling, the connection, a circuit closing between her body and the body of the mountain. The hum entered her. Not through her ears. Through her bones.

What happened next, she would spend the descent trying to describe and fail.

It began as warmth. Then the warmth acquired texture, and the texture acquired direction, and the direction was backward. Time did not reverse — nothing so cinematic — but *she* reversed, or a part of her did, peeling back through years like layers off a cable spool. Twelve years of blankness, and the stone filled them. Not gradually. All at once, in fragments, in flashes, each one complete and vivid and wrong in ways she couldn't yet name.

A hospital room. White curtains, a window overlooking a parking structure, the smell of antiseptic and cut flowers. Someone — a woman, middle-aged, tired-eyed, holding a phone to her ear with her shoulder — saying, "No, they still haven't told us anything. They say it's touch and go. They say if she wakes." Ulyana heard her own name spoken by a stranger's voice, and the name was right, and the voice was wrong, or the voice was right and everything else was wrong, because she had no memory of this woman, and yet the woman grieved for her like family.

A dive. Cold water, green-black, kelp swaying. A wreck — not the naval wreck, an older one, a freighter broken on a shelf, her job to cut loose a fouled propeller shaft. Her partner on that dive had a name. *Ravi.* Ravi, who checked her gauges twice and laughed at his own jokes and kept a photograph of a girl in Colombo taped inside his mask box. Ravi, whose existence she had never once suspected, whose loss she had never mourned because she had never known there was anything to lose. The stone gave him back to her whole: his voice, his laugh, the exact weight of his hand on her shoulder when he told her, surface interval, day three, "You dive like you're trying to leave something behind you."

She had laughed at that. In the memory, she had laughed.

More fragments. An apartment with plants on every sill. A dispute with an insurance company, her own voice sharp with fury on a phone call: *"That's not what the policy says and you know it."* A scar on her left forearm she did not have now — she looked down at her arm, mid-flash, and the arm was bare and unscarred, and the dissonance rang through her like a cracked bell. A birthday. Hers. Thirty-one candles. People singing. Faces she almost knew, faces the stone showed her in perfect detail and her ruined hippocampus refused to file.

And then the last fragment. The deepest layer. The one the stone saved for last, the way the sea saves its coldest water for the bottom.

A meeting. A room she didn't recognize — carpeted, windowless, a conference table, four people, coffee cups, a projector showing bathymetric charts of a coastline she knew intimately and had never seen. Her name was in the room. Not spoken — written. On a folder. A personnel file. *Nakamura, U. — candidate. Post-incident recruitment profile.*

She heard a man's voice — dry, unhurried, amused in the way of men who have never once faced a consequence — and the voice said:

*"The incident itself will handle the erasure. What matters is the selection beforehand. We need divers with no ties. No family pressing for answers. No partners asking questions. The amnesia does the rest. Give me six months and a suitable accident, and I'll give you a salvage operator who remembers nothing and questions nothing."*

The voice paused, and someone else in the room laughed softly, and the dry voice continued:

*"Voss found her, by the way. Flagged her off the Colombo registry. Good instincts, no attachments. The accident is scheduled for March."*

*Scheduled.*

The word detonated in her skull and kept detonating, echo after echo, long after the fragment ended and the stone released her.

Her hand came off the rock. The circuit broke. The hum receded from her bones like a tide going out, and she was kneeling at the edge of black water in a chamber under a glacier, and the light-rings were still crossing her feet, one per second, patient, indifferent, offering.

She had touched the stone for eleven seconds. She'd counted. Counting was the one thing the blank years had left her, and it had probably saved her life, because eleven seconds was a fragment and forty seconds, she understood now with absolute clarity, would have been a rewrite.

* * *

She surfaced from it gasping, on her knees, and the others were around her — how long had she been down, how long had they let her stay — Tsering gripping her shoulder, Okafor holding his recorder toward her like a defibrillator.

"Eleven seconds," she managed. "I counted."

"You're bleeding," Tsering said, and pointed, and there was blood on her lip where she'd bitten through it, and her pupils, Okafor reported grimly, were uneven.

"I saw—" She stopped. Started again. Her voice came out level, which was its own kind of horror, levelness being the last thing she felt. "I saw my accident. Before my accident. I saw the room where it was decided."

"Saw it how?" Okafor asked. "The stone stores memories. It can't store events it didn't witness. Whose memory was that?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. The meeting had happened in a windowless room in an office park, nowhere near the pass, nowhere near the stone. Unless the stone reached further than anyone believed. Unless the resonance wasn't contained in the chamber at all — unless it ran through the range like groundwater, touching everything, recording everything, and the chamber was only its heart, its loudest room. Unless the man in the meeting had spent time in these mountains, or carried something out of them, or —

Or unless the stone didn't store memories at all. Unless it stored *everything*, indiscriminately, the way the sea stores everything it's given, and what Okafor had called a library was closer to an ocean floor: silt, wrecks, bodies, secrets, all of it settled and preserved and available to whoever dredged.

"Whose memory was that?" Okafor asked again.

"Mine," Ulyana said. "From before. Someone told me about it — no. No, that's not right either." She pressed her palms flat against her thighs, steadying herself against vertigo that had nothing to do with altitude. "I don't know whose memory it was. That's the worst part. I don't know if I recovered something or if the stone just handed me a piece of someone else's life and let me wear it."

Mara, from across the chamber, arms still crossed, watching Ulyana with the hard clarity of a woman who had already made her decision: "Now you understand. Now you know what it does. It doesn't return things. It *mixes* them. Whatever you pull out of that pool, you can't verify. You can't source it. You'll build a self out of it and never know how much of the self is yours."

"I know," Ulyana said.

"Do you?" Mara's voice cracked like a whip. "Because in about ten minutes you're going to be standing next to those charges deciding whether to place them, and everything you just felt is going to argue with the mission. The stone will make sure of it. It's patient. It has all of us in there. It can wait."

Ulyana got to her feet. The chamber swayed and steadied. The blood on her lip had dried to a dark crescent. She wiped it with the back of her glove and looked at the pool — at the rings still spreading, silver, silent, one per second, the heartbeat of a mountain that had swallowed lives the way the sea swallows ships.

Somewhere in that water was the rest of it. The full record. The accident itself, perhaps, from the inside. The hospital. The blank years from the other side. The name of the man with the dry voice — and whether that name was Voss, or Voss's superior, or someone Voss served. Enough to hang them, if hanging meant anything out here. Enough, maybe, to save the survey team, to prove what had been done to them and by whom.

Enough to destroy her, if she stayed too long.

Thirty seconds of contact would give her everything. Thirty seconds would also, by every law the survey team's fate had written, begin taking her. Trade the blank she had survived for a self she couldn't verify, assembled by a machine that mixed lives like current mixes wreck debris. Mara was right. The stone didn't return things. It traded them.

And yet.

She had spent twelve years measuring her life in miles because miles were honest. Miles didn't lie about where you'd been. And here was the truth the stone had shown her, the one fact beneath all the fragments, the one thing she now knew with the same certainty she knew her own breathing: her past had not been taken from her by the sea. It had been taken from her by men. Deliberately. For profit. With a schedule and a signature and a laugh.

Which meant the blank wasn't damage. The blank was a *product*. She was not a broken woman missing pieces. She was a manufactured one, built to specification, delivered on schedule — and everything she had built since, every mile, every dive, every careful count, had been constructed on top of a crime.

The rage arrived late, the way deep-water rage does — no surface splash, just pressure, total and silent, filling her from the boots up.

Tsering saw it land. He'd guided enough people through enough mountains to read a face, and what he read on hers made him step between her and the pool, gently, one hand raised.

"Ulyana." Quiet. "The charges."

She looked at him. Then at Devi, who had come forward at some point without her noticing, who stood at the chamber's mouth with the packs, whose face was doing something complicated and guilty and afraid — and who was looking at her the way a man looks at a door he knows he locked, or failed to lock, or opened for someone he shouldn't have.

There was something Devi needed to tell her. She could see it in him the way you see weather coming over water.

But the monsoon was coming too, and the charges were still in their packs, and the stone hummed on beneath them, one ring of light after another, patient as tide, holding everything — her drowned years, her scheduled accident, the dry voice laughing in a windowless room — and Ulyana Nakamura turned from the pool with blood on her lip and rage in her bones and said, in a voice that carried in the warm stone like a bell of her own:

"Place the charges."

And Devi, at the mouth of the chamber, finally opened his mouth to speak.
