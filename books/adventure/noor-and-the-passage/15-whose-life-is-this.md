## 15. Whose Life Is This

Okafor had named the pulse in his head *the metronome*. It had been with him since the second night in the valley — a low rhythmic pressure behind his eyes, like a bass note played through wet stone, and he'd told himself it was altitude, dehydration, the thin lie of thin air. But lying awake in the survey team's old mess tent, wrapped in a sleeping bag that smelled of someone else's four-year-old coffee, he knew better now. The pulse wasn't in his head. It was in everything. In the granite. In the glacier melt ticking against the rocks. In the six serene people who sat by the fire each evening with their hands folded, watching the flames as if fire were a language they were relearning.

He checked the recorder again. Battery at sixty-one percent. Signal gain maxed. The waveform on the little screen was a flat line interrupted every eleven seconds by a soft swell, like a heartbeat slowed to the speed of geology.

*Eleven seconds,* he thought. *Everything here comes in elevens.*

"Okafor."

Tsering's voice from outside the tent flap. Not loud. Tsering was never loud anymore; the mountain had taught him that volume was a debt you paid for later. Okafor crawled out into the blue hour before dawn, breath smoking, and found the guide standing over one of the blank ones — the woman they called Asha because she'd answered to nothing else and Devi had picked the name out of the air like a man naming a stray dog.

She was kneeling at the edge of camp, facing up-valley, toward the pass mouth where the rock wall rose black against the stars.

"She's been like this for an hour," Tsering said. "I didn't want to touch her."

"Asha." Okafor crouched beside her. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

She turned her head with a slowness that made his skin crawl — not drugged-slowness but *tuned*-slowness, the way a compass needle settles. Her eyes were pale gray in the starlight, calm as deep water.

"It's louder," she said. "In the night. When everything else is quiet, it's clearer. Like a choir warming up very far away." She smiled. It was a beautiful smile. That was the worst part. "You can hear it too. I've watched you rubbing your ear when you think no one's looking."

"I have tinnitus," Okafor said automatically, and then heard himself say it, and understood that he had just lied to a woman without a past in order to protect himself from a future he didn't want to name.

Asha looked at him with terrible gentleness. "You don't have to be afraid of it. It doesn't take anything. It only gives."

*It only gives.* He thought about the footprints ending mid-stride at the treeline. He thought about the warm food, the charged radios, the camp that looked like a photograph of itself with the people cut out. *It only gives,* she said, and somewhere below them Voss was landing helicopters full of demolition charges and armed men, and somewhere above them the pass waited with its humming stone, and Okafor — who had signed on to carry radios and fix antennas and ask no questions — felt the contract's strange clause surface in his memory like a drowned body: *cognitive hazards. Contractor not liable for alterations to personnel memory, identity, or continuity of self.*

They had all signed it. He had initialed that page without reading it, because triple rate was triple rate, and because Ulyana had read it twice and shrugged, and if Ulyana shrugged then it couldn't be that bad.

Ulyana shrugged at things the way other people prayed.

* * *

The argument started at breakfast, which meant it had actually started the night before, around the fire, when Devi had opened the case he'd been guarding since basecamp like it held his mother's ashes.

Inside, nested in foam: a portable resonance unit, contractor-gray, with a hand crank and a single dial. Voss had left it with them at the col briefing two days ago — "field therapy," he'd called it, smiling with all his teeth — along with instructions delivered in the tone of a man discussing fertilizer application. *Low amplitude. Short exposure. See if the subjects respond.*

Devi had carried it up-valley in his pack, and Okafor had watched him do it, and neither of them had spoken about what they both understood: that Voss wasn't testing the blanks. He was testing the *process*. The blanks were data. Everyone here was data. Okafor's own sleepless nights were probably being logged somewhere on a drive in Voss's helicopter, filed under *subject response, prolonged exposure, non-indigenous personnel.*

But this morning Devi set the unit on a flat rock and said, "We should try it on one of them. Properly. With their consent, such as it is."

"Consent," Tsering repeated. The word sat in his mouth like a stone he was deciding whether to swallow or spit.

"They can't consent," Ulyana said. She was sitting slightly apart, as she always did, mending a strap on her dive harness with waxed thread, her hands moving with the economy of someone who had rebuilt herself from spare parts. "That's the whole problem. You can't get consent from someone whose self was taken. Whatever they agree to, you're asking the wrong person."

"So we ask no one?" Devi's jaw was tight. "We just walk them out of these mountains as blanks? Hand them back to a world that will treat them like ghosts? Their families are down there, Ulyana. Mothers. Kids. Somebody's been lighting candles for four years."

"Somebody's been grieving for four years," Ulyana said, not looking up from her stitching. "Grief is finished business. You're talking about digging up the grave and putting the body back in the kitchen."

"That's obscene."

"That's honest."

The wind came down the valley in a long exhale, and the tents snapped, and the pulse swelled in Okafor's skull — eleven seconds, eleven seconds — and he realized he was holding his breath without deciding to.

Asha was watching them from across the fire ring. All six of them were watching. They always watched arguments the way other people watched weather: attentively, without alarm, as though conflict were a phenomenon that happened to other species.

"We could ask them," Okafor said quietly. "Not whether they want their old lives back. They can't answer that. But we could show them. A taste. Thirty seconds at low amplitude. And then ask how they feel about what they felt." He paused. "That's not consent. But it's more than nothing."

Ulyana's needle stopped. She looked at him for a long moment, and there was something in her face he recognized from mirrors — the look of a person measuring a distance they weren't sure they wanted to close.

"Thirty seconds," she said finally. "Low amplitude. And if anyone screams, we stop. Immediately. No heroics."

Devi exhaled like a man surfacing.

* * *

They chose Asha because she had come closest, the night before, to saying something that sounded like a question. Because she had knelt facing the mountain. Because — though Okafor would not admit this even to himself until much later — because she was the smallest, and some animal part of him still ranked people by how easily they could be managed, and he hated himself for it in the quiet way he hated most of his own decisions.

They sat her on the flat rock in the weak noon light. Tsering stood apart with his arms folded, refusing to participate but refusing to leave, which was its own kind of testimony. Two of the other blanks drifted near and stood watching, hands folded, serene as churchgoers.

"You'll feel something," Okafor explained, turning the crank slowly, watching the needle climb. "Like remembering. It might feel like it belongs to someone else. If it does — if it feels wrong — tell us and we stop. Say *stop*, any word, anything."

"What if I like it?" Asha asked.

The crank stuttered under his hand.

"Then we talk about that too," Ulyana said, from where she stood at Asha's shoulder, one hand resting lightly there — the first time Okafor had seen her voluntarily touch another person in three weeks of travel.

Devi raised the emitter pad toward Asha's temple. "Ready?"

"I'm always ready," Asha said pleasantly. "That's the thing you keep not understanding. I'm ready for everything. Nothing surprises me. Nothing *grieves* me." She tilted her head. "Do you know what that's worth? Do you know what people pay to feel like this?"

Okafor turned the dial. The hum climbed through the soles of his boots, up his shins, settled into his teeth.

For nine seconds, nothing.

On the tenth second, Asha screamed.

It was not fear. Okafor had heard fear scream before, in Lagos, in the flood year, in the dark of a stairwell with water rising. This was different. This was the sound of a person being *invaded* — a raw, tearing, full-body rejection, the sound a soul makes when it recognizes a trespasser wearing its own name. She went rigid on the rock, spine bowed, fingers clawing at the granite, and the emitter pad chattered against her temple as her whole body shook, and through the noise she was shouting words that took Okafor several seconds to parse because they made no sense:

"—not mine — take it BACK — that's not my MOTHER — I never — I DON'T HAVE A SISTER — take it back take it back TAKE IT—"

Ulyana hit the cutoff. The hum died. The silence afterward was enormous, ringing, worse than the sound.

Asha slid off the rock onto her knees in the dirt. She stayed there, breathing in great ragged gulps, hair hanging over her face. Nobody moved. Tsering took one step forward and stopped, as if crossing some line in the scree.

And then Asha lifted her head, and her eyes — those calm gray eyes — were blazing.

"Don't ever do that again," she said, and her voice had changed, dropped into a lower register, acquired an accent Okafor couldn't place, something coastal, something educated, something that belonged to a woman who had presumably once had a job and a flat and a sister she apparently did not love enough to miss. "Do you understand me? What you just did — that wasn't a gift. That was a burial. You dug her up and you put her on top of me and you expected me to lie down underneath."

"Asha—" Devi began.

"My NAME isn't Asha!" she shouted, and then, horribly, she laughed — a cracked, wild laugh with tears running through it. "No. No, wait. Yes it is. That's the point, you idiots. That's the whole point." She pressed her palms against her eyes. "Four years ago there was a woman with my face who had a mother and a sister and a mortgage and a grudge against her father, and she walked up this valley and the mountain ate her, and what was left — what grew in the space where she was — is ME. I'm not her ghost. I'm not her recovery project. I'm what's HERE now. And you want to trade me for a stranger just because the stranger got here first."

The other five blanks had gathered behind her, standing shoulder to shoulder, and for the first time since Okafor had met them they did not look serene. They looked like a congregation whose faith had just been tested by fire. One of the men — the tall one Devi called Dorje — spoke for the first time in days, his voice low and certain:

"She's right. We're not missing persons. We're new persons."

*New persons.* Okafor turned the phrase over and felt it cut on both edges.

* * *

The fracture ran through camp all afternoon like a crack through ice.

Devi argued for restoration with the desperation of a man arguing for his own salvation. "You saw her face," he kept saying. "Underneath the rage, you saw it. She remembered her mother's kitchen. That's not invasion, that's *homecoming*, and she's too frightened to know it—"

"She said stop," Ulyana said. "Twice. You don't get to decide she's frightened instead of certain. That's exactly what Voss does. He decides what people really want and then he administers it."

"And what do YOU want, Ulyana?" Devi's voice cracked upward. "Because from where I'm standing, you've got a drive in your pack with your whole life on it, and every day you don't load it is a day you're agreeing with her."

The silence that followed had weight. Okafor watched Ulyana's hands go still on the harness strap, watched her look at Devi with an expression that contained, in equal measure, love and the beginning of something colder.

"I'm not her," Ulyana said finally. "And she's not me. And nobody in this camp gets to use either of us as an argument for the other."

Tsering said nothing all afternoon. He sat on his heels by the cold fire ring, and when Okafor finally sat beside him and offered tea, the guide accepted it and spoke to the middle distance.

"My grandmother forbade us the singing stone," he said. "You know why? Not because it takes. Anyone can forbid a thing that takes. She forbade it because it gives. Because a man came down from the high pasture one autumn, forty years ago, walking wrong, smiling wrong, calling my grandmother's mother by a name that wasn't hers — a name of a woman who had died in that valley as a girl. And the family took him in. What else could they do? He remembered her. He loved her. He grieved her death with her brother's grief." Tsering sipped his tea. "He lived with them eleven years. Good years, everyone said. Kind man. Useful man. And my grandmother told me: every festival, every wedding, every birth in those eleven years, her mother's brother would look at that man across the fire and see his sister's killer sitting there wearing a borrowed heart. Not because the man did anything. Because the mountain did. But the family had to live with it anyway. The mercy and the erasure were the same act, boy. They always are."

Okafor stared into his cup. "So which side are you on?"

"I'm on the side of the living," Tsering said. "Whoever they turn out to be."

* * *

Dusk. Okafor sat inside the comms tent running diagnostics he no longer believed in, because the work steadied his hands, and his hands needed steadying. The recorder's waveform scrolled: flat, swell, flat, swell. Eleven seconds. He had begun counting without meaning to. Eleven seconds between pulses, and he'd been in the valley nine days, and he caught himself doing the arithmetic — seventy-eight thousand pulses, give or take — and made himself stop, because counting was Ulyana's disease, not his, and he could feel it spreading anyway. That was the thing about proximity to her: her coping mechanisms were contagious. Miles, seconds, pulses. Numbers were how people like them built walls against the water.

The satellite phone chimed. Encrypted burst. He shouldn't have listened. He listened.

Voss's voice, compressed and clipped, addressed to his site chief: *"Subject Seven showing spontaneous recall episodes — confirm? If the blanks are fragmenting on their own, the window is closing. Natural decay of the imprint. We need baseline captures within thirty-six hours or the entire cohort degrades past harvest value. And check on the divers' team — if Nakamura has touched the stone, I want to know what she saw. Everything hinges on what she saw."*

Okafor sat very still in the dark tent, the handset warm in his hand, and understood three things at once, the way you understand a rockfall — all together, too fast to dodge.

First: the blanks were decaying. Asha's rage wasn't just trauma. It was the imprint breaking down, the old life bleeding through the seams of the new one whether anyone cranked a dial or not. The serene calm was a temporary sealant, and time was dissolving it, and when it failed completely those six people would be torn in half — two selves fighting over one body, with no winner possible.

Second: Voss knew. Voss had always known. The rescue contract, the field-therapy unit, the gentle suggestions — it was all a harvesting operation with a humanitarian paint job, and the monsoon wasn't a deadline for saving anyone. It was a deadline for *collection*.

Third: *everything hinges on what she saw.* Ulyana had touched the stone. Okafor hadn't known that. Hadn't been there. Which meant it had happened in the hours he'd spent asleep, or on watch, or simply not looking — and Voss had eyes here that Okafor hadn't found yet, and one of them might be sleeping ten feet from him in the next tent.

He set the handset down carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening.

Outside, the sky over the western peaks had gone the color of a bruise, banded with cloud that hadn't been there that morning. The wind had turned. It was coming from the south now, off the plains, and it smelled wrong — thick, wet, green, the smell of a sea that had decided to move inland.

Tsering stepped out of his shelter, looked at the horizon for a long moment, and said quietly, to no one and therefore to everyone:

"The snows are early. We move tomorrow. First light. Everyone — the blanks included. Especially the blanks."

Asha stood at the edge of the firelight, looking up at the black mass of the pass, and Okafor saw her lips moving — not praying, he didn't think. Counting. Eleven seconds, eleven seconds, keeping time with the mountain, keeping time with him.

Behind her eyes, he would swear, two women were both awake.
