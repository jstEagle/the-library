## 16. Snowline Crossing

Tsering had grown up reading weather the way other children learned to read books. His grandmother taught him on the roof of their stone house in Kharang, holding his hand flat against the sky. *Warm wind from the south with dust in its teeth — snow by morning. Clouds stacking like broken pots over the pass — turn back now.* He had ignored her lessons for thirty years, and she had died before she could see him prove them right one last time.

Now he stood at the edge of the glacier tongue below the second col and watched the clouds do exactly what she would have warned him about. They were wrong. All of them — the shape of them, the speed of them, the way they boiled up from the valley floor instead of sliding in off the peaks like monsoon weather should. This was not a storm arriving. It was a storm being born, here, above them, out of air that had no business carrying this much water at this altitude.

"The front's early," Okafor said, checking her handheld against the satellite feed. "Landfall was supposed to be tomorrow night. This is—" She turned the screen toward Ulyana and Devi, toward the two blank members of the survey team who walked when told to walk and stopped when told to stop, docile as yaks. "This is twelve hours ahead of the model."

"Models are written by people who've never been here," Tsering said.

Ulyana looked up from the screen. Her face did something it rarely did — it went uncertain. Not frightened. Uncertain, which for a woman who navigated by numbers and distances was worse. "How much time do we have?"

"Before what?"

"Before the pass closes. Properly closes. Not inconvenient. Closed."

Tsering spread his map on a flat rock, weighted the corners with stones. The route ran up the glacier tongue, across two kilometers of icefall where the surface broke into seracs like the teeth of a saw, then over the col itself — a saddle of black rock between two walls of ice — and down the far side into the rain shadow where the going turned steep but honest. In good weather, with a strong party, it was eight hours. He said so plainly, because lying about distances was how people died in his mountains, and he had watched enough of that for one lifetime.

"In good weather," Devi repeated. "And this weather?"

"This weather, I don't cross it at all." He met their eyes one at a time. "I want to say this clearly, because you're paying me and because I like some of you. Below us is shelter — a cave behind the moraine, dry, defensible, room for all seven of us plus the two—"

"Six," Ulyana said. "The blanks don't count as separate decisions. They come or stay with us."

"—plus four," Tsering amended without breaking stride. "The cave holds through anything short of an avalanche. We wait out the front. Two days, maybe three. Then we cross on hard frozen snow at dawn, fast and clean."

"And Voss?" Okafor asked quietly.

Tsering had been waiting for someone to ask it. "Voss has a helicopter, satellite uplink, and forty men at basecamp with equipment we can't spell. Voss doesn't need to beat the storm. Voss needs only to be patient while we freeze in a hole in the rock, and then walk up and take what he wants."

Ulyana folded the map along its old creases. "Then we cross now."

"Tsering," Devi started.

"We cross now," she said again, and there was nothing uncertain left in her voice. "He offered me my memories on a drive, Devi. You know what's on that drive better than anyone. If we stop, if we hide, he comes anyway — and next time there won't be a negotiation, there'll just be men with guns and a bag over my head and the Passage intact for another season of whatever he intends to do with it. I didn't climb this far to sit in a cave and wait for him politely."

Tsering looked at the sky again. His grandmother's voice in his head, flat and certain: *turn back now.* And beneath it, older and harder, the voice of every guide who had ever lost someone: *the mountain does not care what you are running from.*

"If we cross," he said, "we cross my way. Roped. Spaced. No one moves until I say. The blanks go in the middle, tied to the rope whether they understand it or not. And when the whiteout comes — and it will come — nobody makes a decision alone. Not even you, Ulyana-ji. Especially not you."

"Agreed."

Devi rubbed the back of his neck. "What about supplies? We're carrying what, three days of food?"

"Two and a half," Okafor said. "After the rockfall took the cache."

"After the rockfall took the cache," Tsering confirmed. "Which means the crossing costs us a day of food minimum, and if the whiteout pins us anywhere on the ice, we start eating the margin. So we eat now. Everything warm. Both stoves. If it goes in our bellies it can't be dropped in a crevasse."

They cooked and ate quickly, hunched against the wind that had begun to slide down the glacier in long cold breaths. The blanks ate what they were given, calmly, gratefully, watching the mountains with expressions of mild pleasant interest, as though the peaks were paintings in a doctor's waiting room. One of them — the woman whose name had been Anjali Rao, though none of them could have said so anymore — hummed softly while she ate. A tuneless little melody, three notes rising, three notes falling. Tsering had heard it before, on the night Okafor described a childhood that wasn't hers. The same three notes. The Passage's signature, leaking out of them like radiation.

He did not say so. Some things were better carried silently.

* * *

The first hour on the glacier was almost kind.

Hard nevé underfoot, crampons biting cleanly, the rope stretched between them humming faintly with each step. Tsering led, probing with his axe, reading the ice the way his grandmother read clouds. Behind him came Ulyana, then the two blanks roped together, then Devi, then Okafor at the rear with the satellite phone in a padded case against her chest like something newborn.

The icefall was worse than the map promised. Seracs leaned overhead at angles that made the eye flinch; crevasses opened under the snow bridges like mouths deciding whether to speak. Twice Tsering probed a bridge that held, and twice more he rerouted twenty minutes around a gap that the summer heat had widened since any map was drawn. He kept count aloud — steps, minutes, meters — because counting was a rope too, a way of keeping everyone's mind on the near world instead of the vast indifferent one overhead.

At the heart of the icefall the light changed.

It happened between one step and the next. The grey glare dimmed as though a hand had passed over the sun, and the shadows of the seracs swung like compass needles. Tsering stopped, planted his axe, and turned to look south.

The wall of cloud had arrived. There was no other word for it. It stood over the foothills like a cliff face, vertical, boiling, dark as wet slate, and it was moving faster than anything that size had a right to move. He had seen monsoon fronts hit the range eleven times in his life. He had never seen one arrive early, hungry, ahead of itself, as though the mountain had pulled it up by the collar.

"How long?" Ulyana asked. She had seen his face.

"An hour. Perhaps less." He raised his voice over the wind, which had begun to sing in the seracs — a high thin note, rising. "We make the col or we make neither. From here to the saddle there is no shelter. Nothing. If it catches us on the ice we dig in and survive it or we don't."

"Then we run it," Ulyana said. "Rope tight. Everyone moves."

They moved.

For forty minutes they made better time than Tsering had thought possible, the whole party swinging up the glacier in a rhythm that felt almost like grace. The blanks surprised him — their bodies remembered mountaineering even if their minds remembered nothing, feet finding the rhythm of crampon work, breath settling into the pace he called. Whatever else the Passage had taken from them, it had left the animal competence underneath, the body's own deep memory, and Tsering found himself thinking that there were worse things than being reduced to what your muscles knew.

Then the wind came down.

It came off the col like water over a weir, a single continuous avalanche of air, and it hit them with a temperature drop you could feel tooth-deep. Snow followed — not falling but flying, horizontal, a white roar that erased the seracs, the sky, the rope's far end. Tsering got the word out — *"Belay! Down! Closer!"* — and hauled the rope hand over hand until the party bunched against him, six bodies leaning into the wind like men leaning into a door.

"Col's two hundred meters!" he shouted. "Maybe less! Stay on the rope, follow my axe strikes, and whatever happens — *whatever happens* — no one lets go!"

He led into the white.

There is a particular loneliness in leading a rope team through a whiteout that those who haven't done it will never understand. The world shrinks to the length of your arms. Every footfall is a question asked of the mountain, and the mountain answers in the language of crunch and give, and somewhere behind you the people you're responsible for are trusting that your questions are good ones. Tsering had done it perhaps fifty times in his life. He had never once done it without fear, and he had never once let the fear choose the route.

A hundred meters. The wind tore at his hood. He counted steps aloud, sixty to the minute, a metronome against the chaos. Somewhere behind him the rope paid out and took up and paid out, alive in his hands, each tug a heartbeat from the people he couldn't see.

At a hundred and forty meters the ground fell away under his left foot.

Not a crevasse. A schrund at the bergschrund's ugly cousin, hidden under fresh windslab, and it took him to the waist before his axe arrested and his shoulders screamed and the rope snapped taut with five people's weight behind it. For a moment he hung in the white silence inside the crack, snow dust sifting past his face, looking down into blue nothing, and he thought with strange calm: *so this is where it ends, and it ends mid-stride, like the footprints at the treeline.*

Then Ulyana's voice came down the rope, flat and clear as a radio transmission: "Tsering. On three. One. Two."

They pulled him out the way salvage divers pull a wreck off a seabed — slowly, with mechanical patience, no wasted motion. He crawled onto the nevé, spat snow, checked his fingers, checked the rope, checked the party. Five faces in the white. All present.

"Okafor," he rasped. "Count off."

Her voice came back thin and shaking. "Here. I'm here. But Tsering — the case —"

"What case?"

"The satphone case. When you went in, the rope jerked, I slid, I caught myself, but the strap—" A pause, filled with wind. "It's gone. Into the crack. The phone, the spare batteries, the charger. Everything."

Silence, except the wind. Tsering closed his eyes and did the arithmetic he didn't want to do. No phone meant no weather updates, no call to basecamp, no SOS, no forty-hour window countdown. They were now a party of six moving blind through a closing pass with two and a half days of food and no line to the outside world.

"We keep moving," Ulyana said, and her voice had the finality of a hatch closing. "Grieving later. Col first."

* * *

They reached the saddle ninety minutes later, in the last grey light, half-frozen and silent.

The col itself was narrow as a knife blade, black rock scoured clean by wind, and on its far side the descent gully dropped away into cloud. Tsering got the tent up in the lee of a boulder with hands he could barely feel, and they crowded in — six bodies in a space built for four — and lit the stove and did not speak for a long time.

It was Okafor who broke the quiet, in a small voice aimed at her empty hands.

"I killed our weather window. I killed our call for help. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

"You slipped because the rope caught a falling man," Ulyana said. "That's not negligence, that's physics."

"It doesn't feel like physics."

"No. It never does." Ulyana reached across the crowded tent and put her hand flat on Okafor's pack, the way you'd steady something. "Listen to me. I've spent twelve years without a past. Do you know what I've learned about loss? The thing itself is survivable. It's the story you build afterward — *this happened because I'm careless, because I'm cursed, because I deserve it* — that kills people. Don't build the story. Count what's actually gone: one phone. That's the number. Not yourself."

Okafor laughed, wetly, unexpectedly. "You sound like a dive instructor."

"I was a dive instructor. Briefly. Before." She paused, and Tsering watched her decide not to finish the sentence. Before. The word she used for the country she couldn't enter anymore.

Devi handed out food — half rations, decided without discussion, the kind of decision that gets made in tents at altitude — and Tsering chewed mechanically and listened to the wind hammer the fly and thought about his grandmother's roof and the flat of her hand against the sky. She would have taken one look at today and turned them around at dawn. He had chosen otherwise, because the alternative was Voss, and Voss was a human storm, and human storms could be outrun in ways that natural ones could not.

Or so he had believed.

Because at first light, when he crawled out to read the sky and found it scoured improbably clean — a hard blue window between systems, maybe six hours, maybe eight — he also found tracks.

Boot prints. Fresh, crisp-edged, coming up the descent gully from the far side. Modern boots, gaitered, spaced with military precision. Six sets. And beside them, pressed into the snow at intervals, the round shallow scars of a sled runner — the kind used to haul demolition charges and drilling gear over ice.

Tsering crouched over the prints for a long moment, feeling the cold come up through his knees, and understood that everything he had calculated about storms and patience and the mercy of the weather had just been rendered obsolete. Voss hadn't waited out the front. Voss had *used* it — moved during the whiteout with thermal gear and GPS and men who followed arrows on a screen, while Tsering's party groped blindly through the same blow counting steps. Nature had been indifferent to both of them. But equipment wasn't indifferent. Equipment chose sides.

He stuck his head back into the tent.

"Up," he said. "All of you. Now. We have company on the mountain."

Ulyana was already reaching for her axe. "How far?"

"Down-gully, maybe two hours behind us. Moving fast." He looked at each of them in turn, and then at the two blanks, who sat placidly in the corner of the tent humming their three-note tune while the world came apart around them. "And Ulyana-ji — they're not following us. They're herding us. Look at the route. Every track points up-valley. Toward the Passage."

The wind had died. The blue window was already narrowing at the edges, new cloud stacking over the western peaks like broken pots. Tsering read it with his grandmother's flat certainty and gave them the only forecast that mattered:

"They'll catch us before the chamber. Decide fast."
