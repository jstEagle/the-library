## 17. Ambush at the Col

Devi felt the col before he saw it — a change in the wind, a thinning of the air that made each breath feel borrowed. They had been climbing since first light, six hours of switchbacks over scree the color of old bone, and now the ridge line rose ahead of them like a wall someone had started building and abandoned. Beyond it, if Tsering's maps were honest, lay the descent toward the southern glacier and, three days out, the valley where the helicopters could reach them.

Three days. He kept doing the arithmetic and hating every answer.

Ulyana climbed twenty meters ahead, rope slack between them, her movements economical in the way he'd admired for twelve years — no wasted motion, no wasted breath, nothing given away for free. That was the thing about Ulyana. She had built herself out of nothing, out of a coma and a blank file and whatever scraps the sea left her, and everything she did was load-bearing. Devi had spent four days on this mountain carrying the knowledge that he was the crack in her foundation.

He'd made the call to Voss on night two, while she slept. *Spare her. Keep her name off the ledger. I'll get you your access codes.* He'd told himself it was protection. He'd told himself a lot of things.

"Storm's moving faster than they said," Okafor called from the rear, voice flattened by altitude. The comms tech had a satellite handset pressed to his ear, hood up against a wind that was starting to taste of ice. "Front's accelerated. We've got maybe — Tsering, what did you say?"

"Less than you want," Tsering answered. The guide stood at the lip of the final pitch, scanning the col with the particular stillness he reserved for bad news. He'd been still like that twice already today: once at a cairn that had been knocked over, once when he found boot prints in the snowfield that weren't theirs. Military tread. Fresh enough that the edges hadn't crumbled.

Devi watched him read the ridgeline and saw the stillness deepen into something older. Fear with a lineage.

"Tsering?" Ulyana said.

"We are not alone up here." Tsering didn't turn around. "And we are exactly where they would want us."

* * *

The col itself was a saddle of wind-scoured rock perhaps sixty meters across, bracketed by two granite towers, the only practical crossing for a day's march in either direction. Tsering had warned them about places like this back in the foothills, back when the danger was theoretical: *In the mountains, the map chooses your fights.* You could detour around almost anything except geography.

They came up onto the saddle single file, roped in pairs, and Devi understood within ten seconds that they had walked into a room with one door.

Voss stood at the center of the col as though he'd been installed there, a fixture rather than a man — gray parka, gray beard trimmed close, hands folded in front of him with the patience of someone billing by the hour. Four of his men flanked him in a shallow arc, rifles slung but hands near, positioned with the casual precision of people who had done this in worse places than a Himalayan pass. Behind them, two more figures knelt by a stack of hard cases — the demolition gear, Devi realized, hauled up the same route they'd just climbed, carried by men who hadn't stopped to sleep.

"Ms. Nakamura." Voss's voice carried easily in the thin air; he'd learned to project without shouting, another efficiency. "You're ahead of schedule. I appreciate that. It means we can conclude our business before the weather does."

Ulyana unclipped from the rope and stepped forward, putting herself between Voss and the others with a smoothness that looked instinctive and wasn't. Devi knew better. Everything about her was chosen.

"You don't have jurisdiction on this mountain," she said. "You're not military, you're not government, and the survey team you claim to be rescuing is standing right behind me."

"They are indeed." Voss's gaze moved past her to the two blank survey members they'd brought this far — Marek and Ilse, walking docile between Okafor and Tsering, their faces wearing that terrible serenity that Devi had come to dread more than any expression of pain. "Which is why I'm here. Their team leader, Dr. Anand, remains at the Passage with the rest. And Dr. Anand holds something I need."

"The access codes," Ulyana said flatly.

"You've been reading my mail." Voss smiled without warmth. "Yes. The resonance chamber responds to a keyed sequence — frequencies, timings, a protocol Anand's team spent eleven months deriving. Without it, my engineers can stabilize the site for extraction, but slowly, crudely, and the monsoon will arrive before they finish. With it—" he spread his hands, "—everyone goes home. Including your people. Including Ms. Ilse and Mr. Marek, who are currently experiencing what our medical staff would describe as profound dissociative amnesia, and who will remain that way unless the chamber is properly shut down. Or properly used. That part depends on decisions above my pay grade."

"And if we say no?"

"Then I take the codes anyway, because Ms. Ilse has them too." He said it gently, almost apologetically. "Resonance imprinting isn't selective, Ms. Nakamura. Everyone who entered the chamber carries the key. Your friend there—" a nod toward Marek, "—simply can't find it. Yet. My people have equipment that can help him look. I'd prefer not to use it at this altitude. Hypoxia makes the process untidy."

One of the kneeling men opened a case. Inside, nested in foam, sat a headset that looked like it had been designed by someone who hated heads.

Devi's stomach dropped through the rock beneath him. He'd seen the manifest for Voss's gear weeks ago, in the contract annexes he'd signed without reading past the indemnity clauses. He'd told himself the cognitive hazard language was boilerplate. He'd told himself a lot of things.

Okafor made a small sound beside him. "That's an induction rig. That's — those were in the contractor's air freight, I flagged them, nobody—"

"Yes," Voss said pleasantly. "You flagged them. You're thorough, Mr. Okafor. It's one of the reasons you were hired."

The sentence landed on all of them at once. Devi watched it land on Ulyana last, watched her go very still in the way she went still underwater, conserving, calculating.

"Hired," she repeated.

* * *

The wind picked up while Voss talked, streaming snow off the western tower in long white banners. Somewhere below the southern edge, thunder muttered — the monsoon announcing itself, days early, indifferent to schedules both mercenary and human.

"You should understand your position," Voss continued, in the tone of a man reviewing terms he'd already decided on. "The storm front will close this col within the hour. After that, descent is suicide and ascent returns you to the Passage, which my teams now control. I have tents, fuel, food for twelve, and aircraft on standby at the first break in weather. You have what's in your packs. This is not a threat. It's a forecast."

Tsering spat, deliberately, into the snow between them. "My grandmother told me about men like you. She said the singing stone eats memories, but men like you eat everything."

"A colorful culture," Voss said, without malice, which was somehow worse. "Ms. Nakamura. I'm going to make this simple, because the barometer is falling and I respect your time. Give me your cooperation — walk Dr. Anand's people to my technicians, help me effect a controlled shutdown — and everyone leaves this range alive and employed. Resist, and I'll still get what I came for, but the margin for error disappears, and mountains punish error." His eyes, pale and patient, settled on her. "I know you understand margins. Twelve years diving other people's wrecks. A woman with no past has to be careful with her future. Wouldn't you agree?"

Devi heard the trap close and knew — knew with the sick certainty of a man watching his own mistake come home — that Voss was about to spend the information Devi had sold him.

Because that was the transaction. That had always been the transaction. *Spare her* meant *tell me how to move her*, and Devi had told him: she'll trade anything for her past, offer her the drive, offer her the accident, offer her the missing years, and she'll hand you the keys to the chamber herself. He'd thought he was arming her. He'd actually been loading the gun.

"No deal," Ulyana said.

Voss sighed, genuinely regretful, and nodded to one of his men. "Then let's remove the intermediaries. Bind them. Not the guide — he knows the descent routes, and I may need him alive and willing. The others."

Two men started forward. Okafor stumbled back, pulling Marek with him. Tsering moved with startling speed to put himself between the nearest rifleman and Ilse, arms wide, saying something rapid in his own language that Devi didn't need translated.

And Devi stepped forward.

"Wait." His voice cracked on it. He cleared his throat. "Wait. Ulyana. Don't — don't do anything. Don't give them anything. Because it won't matter, because he already—"

"Mr. Rao." Voss's voice was soft. Almost kind. "Don't."

But Devi was already talking, because the words had been building pressure behind his ribs for four days and the col was cold enough, high enough, empty enough that there was nowhere left for them to go but out.

"He called me. Before basecamp, after the contract — no. During. There was a clause, a rider, I signed it, triple rate plus a performance bonus contingent on 'subject cooperation,' and I told myself it meant the survey team, I told myself—" He was shaking. He could hear himself shaking. "He asked me questions about you. What you'd want. What you couldn't resist. And I told him about the drive. About your memories. I told him you'd trade anything for them, and he promised — he gave me his word — that you'd be kept out of it. That you'd be safe. That when this was over you'd never know."

The silence that followed was total. Even the wind seemed to hold itself.

Ulyana turned and looked at him.

It was not the look he'd feared — not rage, not betrayal's hot theatrical wound. It was the dive look. The one she wore at forty meters when something on the wreck moved wrong, when the plan met the water and the water won. Assessment. Recalculation. A woman measuring the distance to a new reality and finding the bottom deeper than her line.

"Never know," she said quietly.

"I was trying to protect you."

"You sold me." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. It stayed level, professional, the voice she used to log damage. "You sold the only thing I have. Not my memories — I don't have those. You sold the fact that I'd want them. You took the one hole in me and you handed it to a man with a shovel."

"Ulyana—"

"How much?"

"What?"

"The bonus. How much was I worth?"

Devi opened his mouth and nothing came out, because there was no number that survived being spoken aloud at five thousand meters in front of the woman it purchased.

"She doesn't need to answer that," Voss interjected smoothly, "because the bonus is moot. Mr. Rao's cooperation concluded at basecamp. Yours, however, is ongoing." He gestured, and two of his men shifted, rifles coming off their shoulders with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. "Here is what happens now. My technicians take Ms. Ilse and Mr. Marek down to the forward camp. Mr. Rao and Mr. Okafor accompany them. Ms. Nakamura and the guide will come with me to the Passage — because Dr. Anand, it turns out, has been asking for her. By name. Which is interesting, since she's never met him, and suggests the chamber has been doing some reading of its own."

"That's not happening," Ulyana said.

"It's already happened," Voss said. "You're standing in it."

* * *

They bound Devi's wrists with a ratchet strap, not cruelly, which somehow made it crueler — the professionalism of it, the care taken to leave his fingers warm enough to grip a rope later. They sat him against the eastern tower with Okafor, whose quiet had curdled into something Devi recognized: the sound of a man replaying every flag he'd raised and counting the ones that had been ignored on purpose.

Through the gap between the towers, Devi watched them prepare Ulyana. She wasn't struggling. That frightened him more than screaming would have. She stood with her hands loose at her sides while a technician checked her harness, her eyes moving constantly — the towers, the sky, the snow banners streaming west, the faces of Marek and Ilse, the rifle positions, the cloud bank boiling up from the south like smoke from a fire the size of a country.

She was counting exits. He knew her well enough to know that. He'd known her twelve years and apparently not at all, and both things were true, and both things were his fault.

Tsering they left unbound but flanked, the guide staring south at the storm with an expression Devi finally placed: recognition. The old stories again. The singing stone, and the men who came to mine it, and what his grandmother said always followed them down the mountain.

The first rain hit the col at 3:40 in the afternoon, fat drops that froze on contact, ticking against the granite like slow code. Within minutes it thickened to sleet, and the world shrank to thirty meters of gray. Voss's radio crackled with reports from the lower camp — the helicopter grounded, the forward team requesting shelter, the front arriving a full day ahead of every model.

Voss listened to all of it with his hands clasped behind his back, and then he laughed. Short, genuine, almost delighted.

"Well," he said, to no one and everyone. "Nature's voted. Nobody's flying out of here. Which means, Ms. Nakamura, that you and I are going to the Passage together whether you cooperate or not — and that my employers' timeline is now officially fiction." He turned, sleet beading on his gray beard, and smiled at her with something that in a better man might have been respect. "So let's stop pretending this is a negotiation. You want your past. I want my key. And the mountain wants us all dead. Only one of us has ever beaten the other two."

He started toward the northern descent, into the teeth of the storm, and his men pulled Ulyana after him.

Devi strained against the strap until his wrists bled, shouting her name into the wind, and the last thing he saw before the whiteout swallowed the column whole was Ulyana turning her head — not to look at him, but to look at the storm, at the wall of the monsoon descending on the range, with the expression of a diver checking her gauges before going over the side.

She wasn't being taken anywhere, he realized.

She was descending on purpose. Into the dark. Toward the chamber. And she had just been given exactly what she needed: Voss, his charges, and his men, all of them sealed inside the mountain with her when the rain came down for good.
