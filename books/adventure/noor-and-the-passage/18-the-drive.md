## 18. The Drive

The drive was the size of a matchbox and weighed almost nothing.

That was the first thing that struck her, crouched in the lee of a granite slab while the col's wind tried to peel her off the mountain: twelve years of her life, or the shape of it, fit into forty grams of plastic and silicon. Voss had pressed it into her palm an hour ago with the ceremony of a man handing over a set of keys, and she had carried it since the way you carry something hot — not in a pocket, not against skin, but cupped in her hand inside her glove, as if distance from her body might keep it honest.

Below them, the glacier fell away in blue-white terraces toward the pass mouth, where Voss's camp glowed like a wound of orange light against the coming dark. Above, the Passage waited behind its throat of rock, humming its low note through the soles of her boots. Tsering had taken Okafor and two of the blanks up-scree to scout the breach point. Devi sat ten meters off with his back to her, which was its own kind of statement. The confession at gunpoint had done what confessions do. It had made a map of everything between them and labeled all of it.

So she was alone, which was how she did her best work anyway.

She took out the drive.

* * *

There was no screen up here, no reader, nothing to load it into even if she wanted to. But Voss's people were careless in one specific way: they encrypted their secrets and then printed their paperwork. Folded into the drive's case, where a shipping label would go, was a single sheet — a chain-of-custody form, the kind that follows evidence through a bureaucracy. She'd kept it because she kept everything. Paper didn't lie about its own history the way people did.

She smoothed it against her knee in the last grey light.

*NAKAMURA, U.* — subject designation. *Incident 04-11-KHARANG.* Her accident had a file number. That alone should have told her everything; accidents don't get file numbers, incidents do. The custody chain listed transfers, handlers, redacted names rendered as black bars so dense the page looked bruised. And at the bottom, in the summary field, a sentence in the flat language of people who catalog other people's lives:

*Subject viable post-intervention. No surviving next of kin. No institutional ties. Recommend retention for maritime recovery programs.*

No surviving next of kin.

Her mother had been alive when Ulyana went into the water. She knew this the way you know your own hands — not as information but as structure. Her mother had called her every Sunday from Nagasaki, had sent her a charm for her dive bag that Ulyana had never once believed in and never once removed. After the coma, after the blank years, there had been no mother to find. She had assumed — she had needed to assume — that grief had simply misplaced her, that some aunt or cousin had absorbed her the way families absorb the broken. She had never gone looking because looking required a past to look from, and hers was a room with the furniture gone.

The form said otherwise. The form said *no surviving next of kin* dated eleven days after her incident, when her mother had been four thousand miles away and breathing.

Voss hadn't recruited a diver with no ties. Voss had made one.

* * *

She read it again. Of course she read it again; she was a salvage diver, and salvage meant going back down until the thing is found or you are. The wind moved. Somewhere above, the Passage hummed on, patient as tide.

The details assembled themselves with the terrible efficiency of a wreck settling on the seabed. A survey vessel, a contractor's subsidiary, a decompression accident that shouldn't have been possible at those depths with that equipment — she had spent years telling herself it was bad luck, a valve, a moment. Now there was a file number, and files are written by someone. *Intervention.* They had chosen the word themselves. You intervene in a life the way you intervene in a hemorrhage: to stop it being what it was.

And then the resonance. She understood it now, standing here with the source of it humming overhead. The Kharang anomaly didn't just store memory — it could be tuned. Voss's people had found her afterward, a woman with a hole where her history used to be, and they had seen not a tragedy but a template. A diver who couldn't remember why she might say no. Who measured her life in miles because miles were the only unit left that belonged to her. Twelve years of contracts, of wrecks, of work nobody else wanted — she had thought she was choosing it. Some of it she had been. But the first door had been opened by someone else's hand, and she had walked through it grateful for the open air.

The cruelest part, the part that made her laugh — a short, ugly sound the wind took — was that the drive probably held the rest. Her mother's voice, maybe. The Sundays. The charm on the dive bag. Everything Voss had taken and archived like core samples. He had offered it to her at the col with the magnanimity of a man returning stolen goods he intended to keep stealing. *Your memories, Ms. Nakamura. All of them. Just bring me my team.*

Her team. As if the blanks were his. As if any of them were anyone's.

* * *

Devi came over without being asked. He always knew when to break a silence she wouldn't.

"You've been reading that paper for twenty minutes," he said. "In this light, that's either very slow or very bad."

"Both." She didn't look up. "It's a custody form. For me."

He lowered himself onto the rock beside her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to be refused. Below them the camp lights burned. Above them the hum. Between those two poles, the whole rest of her life waiting to be decided.

"Voss," she said. "My accident. It wasn't the sea. It wasn't bad luck. It was procurement." She turned the page toward him, though the light was nearly gone. "They erased me on purpose, Devi. Made me the perfect hire. No ties, no history, nothing to walk away for. I've been working for them for twelve years and calling it freedom."

Devi was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had the careful weight he used on bad dives. "Then the drive—"

"Has the rest of me on it. Probably. My mother. My childhood. Whatever else they cut out and kept." She closed her fist around it. "He said if I bring him the team, I get to be myself again."

"And?"

"And I've been sitting here trying to remember what I'd even be getting back." She looked at him then. His face was wind-burned and guilty and more familiar than any face in the missing years could ever be. "I know what you're going to say. You negotiated with him to save me from exactly this. You decided for me, same as Voss did. Same as whoever wrote this form." She let that land. She watched it land. "We'll deal with that later. On the boat, maybe. Or never. But right now I need you to hear the actual problem."

"Tell me."

"If I load this drive, I'm doing what Voss wants. Not because he gets the team — because he gets the argument. He gets to prove the whole thesis. That a person is data, that memory is inventory, that you can take someone apart and sell them back to themselves piece by piece and call it rescue." She stood, knees cracking in the cold, and held the drive up between two fingers against the dying sky. "Every year I counted miles because miles were mine. Nobody gave them to me. Nobody could take them back. That was the only true thing I owned, and I built a whole self on top of it, and it held. It held for twelve years."

"The woman who climbed this mountain," Devi said slowly, "isn't in that drive."

"No," Ulyana said. "She isn't. She's the one they couldn't put in a file."

* * *

What surprised her was how little the choice cost once she actually made it. She had expected agony — had braced for it the way you brace before a blowout at depth. Instead there was only clarity, cold and clean as the water below sixty meters, where the light stops pretending and things are only what they are.

She walked to the edge of the shelf, where the glacier dropped away in a long blue curve, and weighed the drive in her palm one final time. Somewhere inside it, maybe, her mother's Sunday voice. Maybe the charm. Maybe the name of the street she grew up on, the smell of her first boat, the face of whoever she'd been before Voss's people decided she was raw material. All of it real. All of it hers, in the way that stolen property is yours.

But she had learned something on this mountain, from the blanks and from the stone that sang: a memory isn't a life. It's the sediment a life leaves. The survey team down in the valley had proven you could be given yourself back entire and still refuse the delivery. The woman on the scree had screamed at her own past like it was a stranger's hand on her throat. Ulyana understood her now completely.

"I'm sorry," she said — to the drive, to the mother in it, to the girl she had been before the water closed over her. "You deserved better than a filing cabinet. So did I."

She threw it.

It caught the last light as it went, a small silver arc over the icefall, turning end over end, and then the crevasse field took it without a sound. No splash. No echo. Twelve years of questions, answered and buried in the same motion, falling toward a depth no one would ever dive.

She stood there longer than she meant to. Not grieving. Taking inventory. Miles to the pass mouth: nine. Miles to the chamber: three. Wind speed rising. Hours of usable light: fewer than she'd like. The numbers came the way they always came, steady as breath, and for the first time in her life she heard them for what they were — not a substitute for a self, but the sound one makes.

When she turned back, Devi was watching her with an expression she'd never seen on him before. Not guilt. Something closer to awe, and underneath it, fear.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. It's just—" He gestured at the glacier, at the place where the drive had vanished. "Most people would have loaded it. Even knowing. Even hating him. Most people need to know."

"I know enough," Ulyana said. "I know who did this to me. I know why. And I know that whatever he's holding over everyone in that camp, it only works if we want our old lives back more than we want each other." She pulled her hood up against the wind, which had begun to carry the first wet mineral smell of the approaching monsoon, impossibly far away and already on its way. "He doesn't have anything to sell me anymore."

Above them, faint through the rock, the Passage changed its note.

She felt it before she heard it — a shift in the hum, the way a ship's hull telegraphs a change in the sea. The tone deepened, widened, spread through the scree under her boots like a struck bell deciding to ring again. Down-valley, the camp lights flickered. One of the blanks, the tall man they'd named Ansel because he couldn't name himself, lifted his head toward the summit ridge with an expression of pure, unguarded terror — the first real expression any of them had seen on his face.

"It knows," he said. His voice cracked on the second word, rusty, human. "It knows what you did."

Ulyana looked up at the black throat of the pass, where the storm light was beginning to move behind the peaks like something waking.

"Tsering!" she shouted into the wind. "Get everyone moving. We breach tonight — before it decides we're worth keeping."
