## 20. Descent in the Rain

The rain came down the pass like something with intent.

Not falling — arriving. A gray wall that swallowed the glacier, then the col, then the switchbacks below it, moving at the speed of a train and sounding like one. Devi stood under an overhang of gneiss with Okafor strapped to his back, watching the front come for them, and for the first time since he'd signed Voss's contract he felt something close to relief.

The weather had grounded the helicopter.

He'd heard it on the radio — Tsering's handheld, patched through to the contractor's frequency because Devi still carried the codes, because some part of him hadn't been able to let go of them even after everything. Voss's pilot, a South African named Kruger who chewed a toothpick through every transmission, calling in from the col: *Negative on extraction, sir. Visibility zero. Airframe icing. We're not flying into that.*

And then Voss's voice, and Devi had heard a man bargain with the sky.

*There has to be a window.* Not a question. An instruction. As if the monsoon were a vendor who could be talked down on price. *I have personnel on that mountain.*

*You have personnel,* Kruger said, and there was something almost gentle in how he said it, *who are walking down. Sir.*

Devi turned his face away from the radio so no one would see what crossed it. Because he knew — he was the only one who knew the whole shape of it — that Voss did not consider them personnel. He considered them assets. Test subjects who had inconveniently developed opinions. The rescue contract had been bait; the survey team had been inventory; Ulyana had been the prize specimen, a diver with no past to anchor her, delivered to the Passage like a seed to soil. And Devi had signed his name to the arrangement. Had sat across a table from a fixer in a Kochi hotel bar and read the clause about cognitive hazards and told himself it was boilerplate.

*We're not flying into that,* Kruger said again, and this time it sounded like a verdict.

Okafor shifted against his back, her weight settling lower, her arms slack around his chest. She'd stopped shivering twenty minutes ago, which should have frightened him more than it did. Hypothermia's quiet phase. But she was breathing, slow and even, and when he pressed his cheek back against hers to check — a habit now, three days old, learned on the glacier when she'd gone gray and silent and he'd carried her through whiteout by feel alone — her skin was warm against the rain-cold of his own.

She was asleep. Actually asleep, in the middle of the end of the world, on the back of a man who had sold her.

He adjusted the strap across his chest and started down.

* * *

The pass in flood was a different country than the pass they'd climbed.

On the way up, it had been stone and scree and sky, a staircase built by glaciers and argument. Now the same route ran with water. Every gully had become a chute. Every chute a torrent. The meltwater that had trickled under their boots two days ago came down now in brown ropes thick as a man's leg, and where the trail crossed the base of the hanging valleys, they waded knee-deep through current that wanted their feet out from under them.

Tsering went first. He always went first now; somewhere above the snowline, in the whiteout that had cost them half their supplies and nearly cost them Okafor, the guide had stopped being a hire and become something else. He moved with a pole in each hand, reading the water the way other men read faces, choosing their line across each crossing with a patience that seemed almost geological. When he found a footing he liked, he planted both poles and stood there, immovable, while the rest of them used him as a rail.

Ulyana came behind him with the last of the blank team members — Iyer, the tall one, who had wept for six hours straight when the hum died and everything came back, and who now walked in a silence worse than the weeping, because Devi understood what the silence was. It was a woman doing arithmetic. Adding up the months she'd lost. Subtracting the person she'd been at the Passage from the person standing in the rain, and trying to find what remained.

Behind Devi came the second blank — Rao, the survey leader, who had given up the access codes at the col with a gun at his throat and who had not spoken to anyone since except to say, once, quietly, looking at nothing: *I remember my daughter's birthday now. I'd forgotten it for four months. Four months. Do you understand what that is?*

No one had answered him. There was no answer.

Devi carried the rear, which was where he'd asked to be. Rear guard was penance position. It was the spot where you ate everyone else's dust, took the first fall on bad ground, and had time alone with your thoughts, which was the punishment and the point. His thoughts were bad company. They kept replaying the night at Camp Two, when he'd sat outside Voss's tent with a cup of tea going cold in his hands and traded his partner's future for her safety, and told himself, in the voice men use for such bargains, that he was protecting her.

*The memories will destroy her,* he'd said.

*Then she can refuse them,* Voss had said, and smiled, and Devi had known even then that the smile was the trap, because Voss never offered anything without already knowing which way a man would jump.

He jumped the way Voss knew he would. That was the ledger entry he couldn't stop reading. Not the betrayal itself — betrayals were simple, a signature, a handshake, done. It was the *knowing*. Voss had looked at him and seen exactly what he was: a man whose loyalty had a price tag and whose love had a seam in it, and Voss had simply found the seam and pulled.

The trail dropped through a stand of birch, silver trunks streaming, leaves hammered flat by rain. Below them the valley opened its throat — the river they'd followed up as a clear mountain stream now a churning tan beast filling its banks, carrying whole trees. Somewhere downstream, invisible beneath the roar, was the suspension bridge.

Devi had been thinking about the bridge for an hour.

* * *

They reached it at midafternoon, if midafternoon could be said to exist inside the rain, which was doubtful. The light was the color of dishwater. The bridge hung over the river forty feet up, swaying, its planks running with water, its cables beaded and dripping, and on the far side the trail continued down toward the treeline camp where they'd found the warm food and the charged radios and the footprints ending mid-stride, a lifetime ago, in another weather.

Tsering tested the anchors and pronounced it sound, which from Tsering meant he'd hang his own grandchildren off it. They crossed in pairs. Ulyana took Iyer across first, hand on the cable, the bridge pitching under them like a deck in a swell, and Devi watched from the near bank and thought about how Ulyana measured her life in miles — twelve miles down, she'd say, describing a dive; nine hundred miles, describing a passage; and you got the sense that the numbers weren't about distance at all but about proof. Miles accumulated. Miles couldn't be taken away. A woman whose past had been erased had rebuilt herself out of distance, out of the measurable, out of things that stayed counted once counted.

And he had helped a man try to sell her back the debt instead.

When his turn came, with Okafor on his back, the bridge moved differently. Her weight high between his shoulders changed the sway, delayed it, and halfway across, when a gust came down-valley and the whole structure leaned, he froze with his hands locked on wet cable and stood there swaying over brown water going by at the speed of freight.

*Keep moving,* Tsering called from the far side. *Swaying is fine. Stopping is not.*

Devi kept moving. Each step landed on plank that tilted and gave, and Okafor's breath stirred against his neck, and somewhere in the middle third of the crossing she woke — he felt it, the small change in her weight, the hands tightening on his shoulders — and she didn't say anything. Just held on. Which was somehow worse than if she'd screamed at him, and better too, and he crossed the rest of the span with his eyes burning and the rain hiding it, which was convenient, because the rain hid everything now, and he was beginning to be grateful for that.

On the far bank, she slid down off his back and stood on her own legs, unsteady, testing them, one hand on his shoulder for balance. She looked at him. Rain ran off her hood in a steady curtain between them.

*I heard the radio,* she said. *At the col. Before I went under.*

Devi waited.

*You asked him to leave the drive behind.* Her voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone handling broken glass. *When Voss offered extraction for himself and his core team. You asked him to leave it on the mountain.*

*I did.*

*Why?*

Because the drive was Ulyana's past in a contractor's file format, and Voss had engineered the accident that made the past worth having, and every hour that drive existed was an hour someone might load it. Because he'd watched Ulyana hold it at eighteen thousand feet and choose not to read it, and he'd understood in that moment that she'd chosen herself — the self made of miles, the self he'd dived beside for six years — and that his own bargain had been a vote against her choice, cast in secret, before she'd ever made it.

But he didn't say any of that. What he said was:

*It wasn't mine to carry down.*

Okafor studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way you nod at a statement you've decided to enter into evidence rather than accept.

*Okay,* she said. *Okay.* And she took his arm anyway, because the trail down was slick and her legs were unreliable, and forgiveness, Devi was learning, was not a feeling. It was a set of logistics. You forgave with your feet before you forgave with your heart, or maybe you never forgave with your heart at all and just kept walking, and maybe that was enough, and maybe it wasn't, and the rain fell on all of it equally either way.

* * *

By dusk they were below the treeline, in forest so dense the rain arrived secondhand, shaken loose from the canopy in fat intermittent drops after the real rain had already passed through. The river noise faded behind them. In its place: mud, roots, the smell of wet pine and wet earth and wet wool, and the small animal sounds of a valley coming alive with water.

They made camp in the ruins of the survey team's lower cache — a corrugated shelter half-collapsed under a fallen limb, dry enough underneath if you were selective about the dry. Tsering got a stove lit under the lee wall, a minor miracle, and they drank tea scalding and sweet because sugar was morale and morale was calories and calories were the difference between walking out and lying down.

Rao spoke again around the stove. He was doing it more now, the restored ones, talking themselves back into existence the way you'd test ice — one sentence, listen for the crack, another sentence. He told them about the survey. About the resonance readings that had made no sense, the instruments logging a pulse with no source, the day the whole team walked up-valley and the footprints ended mid-stride and none of them remembered deciding to walk. He described the Passage chamber from the inside, the rock humming with stored lives, voices layered in the stone like sediment, and he said something that lodged in Devi and wouldn't come out:

*It doesn't take your memories. It trades them. It gives you someone else's grief to carry so you'll put yours down.*

That was what Voss had wanted to bottle. That was what the demolition charges had killed, yesterday, above the snowline, when Ulyana and Tsering had breached the chamber with the contractor's own explosives as the monsoon hit the range — and Devi had stood at the mouth of the tunnel and felt the hum die through the soles of his boots, a vibration leaving the world, and heard the blanks collapse weeping as four months of borrowed serenity drained out of them and their own lives came flooding back with all their debts attached.

Voss's operation was unraveling behind them. Devi could hear it in the radio traffic, fragments caught on the handheld as they descended — Kruger reporting the airframe iced and grounded at the col, Voss demanding satellite uplink, the uplink degraded by the storm cell sitting on the range like a lid. A man who had bought weather forecasts and government frequencies and silence itself discovering that the one counterparty who didn't take payment was the monsoon. There was talk on the band, garbled, of Voss's local fixers going quiet, of the navy launch that had once warned them off the wreck now asking questions through channels, of a name — Voss's name — appearing in transmissions it shouldn't appear in. Someone upstream was cutting losses. Someone always was.

Voss himself had come down the pass on foot, ahead of them or behind them, Devi wasn't sure which, and that uncertainty was its own low fever. Armed men in the flooded forest. Men with demolition experience and nothing left to demolish.

*Tsering,* Devi said quietly, when the others slept. *If Voss's people are on this trail—*

*They're walking down like us,* Tsering said. He was cleaning his poles with a rag, methodical, unhurried. *In this rain, guns are heavy and wet and useless. And my grandmother's valley does not care whose helicopter they rented.* He set the poles down. *Sleep, Devi. Guilt keeps better than fear. Fear spoils fast.*

Devi lay awake anyway, Okafor's breathing audible somewhere near him in the dark, and did the thing he'd been doing for three days: counting. Not miles — Ulyana's currency, not his. Debts. The contract fee he'd never collect. The clause he'd never read properly. The night of cold tea. The word *okay*, entered into evidence, not yet accepted.

Somewhere below them, the foothills ended and the road began, and the road led to the coast, and the coast was where the monsoon made landfall, and somewhere in all of it was a navy inquiry that would want statements, and Devi had a statement in him the length of a confession. He'd give it. All of it. Starting with the sealed case stamped with a contractor's logo, twelve miles down, in the dark, on a wreck the navy officially denied — because that was where it started, really, that was the first mile of this particular descent, and he'd been walking it ever since without knowing.

He slept finally, near dawn, and dreamed of water, and in the dream the water was clear.

* * *

They broke camp late, letting the light strengthen, and made the last miles down through forest thinning into terraced slopes, the rain easing at last from wall to mere persistence. The village appeared around a bend in the trail the way villages do in mountains — suddenly, all at once, smoke and slate roofs and a white stupa bright against the wet green — and Tsering stopped at the edge of it and put his poles together and stood for a moment facing home, and Devi saw him do it and understood that whatever else this descent had been, for Tsering it was also an arrival.

There was a jeep on the track below the village. Government markings. Two officers under a tarp, drinking tea, waiting — for whom, they wouldn't say, though they looked at the descending party with the focused interest of men who had been told to look.

And on the far ridge, visible for perhaps ten seconds before the cloud closed over it, a figure in a red shell jacket, alone, descending a parallel trail toward the valley road. Moving fast. Unburdened. Devi got his binoculars up in time for three seconds of glass on the man's face before the mist took him.

Red shell. Close-cropped hair. The set of the jaw.

Voss.

Walking out. On foot, stripped of everything — no helicopter, no escorts in evidence, no satellite uplink answering his calls — carrying nothing Devi could see but a pack and, Devi would have bet the contract fee he was never going to collect, a drive of his own. Copies. Contractors always kept copies. Somewhere in that red jacket was the Passage's harvest, the resonance data, the memory-editing files, the whole stolen archive of other people's lives, walking down a mountain in the rain toward a road and a vehicle and a border and a buyer.

Ulyana stood beside him at the village edge, soaked through, looking at the same closing cloud. She didn't say anything for a moment. Then she said, quietly, in the voice she used for depth readings, precise and calm:

*He's heading for the road. The jeep's not his — those two won't move for money today, not with the inquiry already sniffing. So he walks to the valley junction. There's a logging track. There will be a truck.*

She turned to Devi, and what was in her face was not hatred. It was something more dangerous. It was decision.

*The drive I left on the mountain was the master,* she said. *His copy is worthless without the chamber — but the files, the research, the names. The subjects. Us.* She looked at the cloud where the red jacket had been. *I know these trails better than he does now. And I know which way the water runs.*

Devi looked at her, and at Okafor leaning on his arm, and at the officers under the tarp who were beginning, slowly, to stand up.

*Ulyana,* he said. *What are we doing?*

She was already pulling her hood tight, already turning toward the valley junction, already walking.

*Catching a man before he reaches the sea,* she said.
