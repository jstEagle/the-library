## 1. Weight and Balance

The rule was simple, and Jonah Zhou had built her whole life on the back of it: never carry what you can't deny.

She repeated it to herself at four in the morning, kneeling on the concrete apron of a rented hangar in Tanjung Selat, loading a de Havilland that was older than she was and twice as stubborn. The vaccine crates went in first — forty kilos of cold-boxed diphtheria and measles doses, the ice still sweating through the burlap wrapping, destined for a mission clinic on the far side of the strait that would never know her name and would be better off for it. She strapped each crate down with the care of a woman handling her own future, because in a way she was: the vaccines were the part of her ledger she could look at straight.

The other crate sat by the hangar doors under a tarpaulin, and she made herself not look at it for as long as she could.

The monsoon was three weeks out but already announcing itself. The wind had turned in the night, come around from the southwest with a wet, mineral smell that meant the whole coast was holding its breath. Overhead, the last of the night's stars were being erased one by one behind a scud of cloud, and the hangar's single bulb swung on its wire, throwing Jonah's shadow in long, uncertain arcs across the fuselage.

She was thirty-one years old and she had been flying the Sunda interior for five years, and in that time she had learned that the coast kept two sets of books. There was the one the harbormasters kept — manifests, duties, landing fees paid in crumpled rupiah and dried fish — and there was the other one, the one that lived in the memory of fixers and debt-collectors and women like Auntie Ru, who never raised her voice and never needed to. Jonah appeared in both books. In the first, she was a charter pilot, competent and forgettable, ferrying medicine and mail and the occasional passenger to strips so short they were really just opinions about where the trees had been cut back. In the second, she owed a number. The number grew every week. She did not think about the number. Thinking about the number was like looking directly at the sun over open water — you could do it exactly once, and it cost you something you didn't get back.

She finished the tie-downs on the vaccine crates, checked the cold-boxes' seals with her thumb, and then walked to the hangar doors and pulled the tarpaulin off the other crate.

It was a meter long, half a meter wide, made of plywood that had been painted flat black to kill reflections, with no markings at all except a stenciled weight — 62 KG — and a serial number that matched nothing in any registry on earth. It had been delivered to the hangar two nights ago by a boy on a motorcycle who hadn't looked older than fifteen and hadn't said a word. The payment had been left under the crate: an envelope, correct amount, no note. The Marrow Syndicate did not deal in notes. Notes were evidence, and evidence was a thing you could deny or a thing that could deny you.

Jonah put her shoulder against the crate and walked it up the cargo ramp into the fuselage, and she did the arithmetic she always did, the comforting arithmetic, the lie she told herself in the same voice every time: *I don't know what's in it. I've never opened one. For all I know it's machine parts, radio spares, tinned milk for somebody's plantation. A courier doesn't need to know. A courier only needs to fly.*

The lie fit as well as it ever had, which was to say it fit the way a wet shirt fits — technically, and with a coldness you carried all day.

* * *

She was wheels-up at 5:12, twenty-two minutes behind the schedule she'd set herself, and the lateness was its own small stone in her shoe. The Otter — a DHC-3 with a tail number that was real this month and might not be next month — lifted off the strip into air that felt like the inside of a mouth, warm and damp and waiting. To the east the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing, a thin band of iron-red under the cloud deck, and the strait below her was black glass except where the fishing boats were already out, their lamps doubled in the water, a scatter of small fires that weren't fires.

Jonah flew the way she did everything else: with a checklist in her head and a second, longer checklist behind it. Fuel: two hours forty. Weather: marginal and getting honest about it. Cargo: forty kilos of vaccines for the clinic at Pulau Anak, and sixty-two kilos of nothing in particular for a beach landing at Tanjung Rhu, where a man named Pak Usman would meet her with two boys and a boat and would not meet her eyes, and she would not meet his, and the whole transaction would take eleven minutes from touchdown to takeoff, and then Tanjung Rhu would be another strip she would never land at twice.

That was the other rule. *Never land twice in the same place.* Not because the Syndicate had told her to — the Syndicate never told her anything, that was the whole architecture of the thing, requests arrived as weather, as coincidence, as a crate appearing under a tarpaulin — but because she'd figured it out herself in her first year, watching a pilot named Desmondo work the same three strips until he knew the dogs by name, and then watching him get stopped at the second strip by men who knew his schedule better than he did. Desmondo had spent nine days in a room with a drain in the floor and had come out older and quieter and eventually not at all. Jonah had driven — flown — the lesson home that same week: familiarity is a debt too. Every place you return to, you owe it your pattern, and patterns get collected.

So she varied. She landed at Karang Laut in the wet season and never in the dry. She came into Pulau Anak from the north when the clinic expected her from the south. She paid her landing fees in small bills and remembered no one's name, and if a village headman pressed a bag of dried fish on her she took it, because refusing a gift on the coast was its own kind of signature.

The strait passed beneath her, twenty minutes of open water, and she let the plane fly itself with her knees and thought, the way she only ever thought at altitude, where the thoughts couldn't follow her down.

The vaccines mattered. That was the sentence she kept in the front of the drawer, the one she opened when the work got dark. The vaccines mattered. Children at Pulau Anak would get their doses this morning because she had gotten up at three-thirty and pre-flighted an airplane in the dark and done her control checks with cold hands. Whatever was in the black crate — and she did not know, she had never looked, she was *sure* she had never looked — whatever it was would be unloaded on a beach by men who already knew what it was, and it would pass out of her life the way a wave passes out of a cove, and the cove stays the cove.

But the drawer had a back to it, and behind the back of the drawer was a night eighteen months ago when she had carried a similar crate to a village up the Kapuas with no name on her manifest, and six weeks later she had heard — rumor, only rumor, the interior ran on rumor the way engines ran on fuel — that the village had burned. All of it. The longhouses, the drying racks, the church with the blue door she'd seen from the air on approach. And the thought she had never let finish itself, the thought she banked hard away from every time it came up on final, was: *what was in the crates, Jonah? What burns like that? What do you carry that makes men come back and burn a village to the waterline?*

She had never gone to look. That was the truth at the bottom of everything: she could have flown up the Kapuas, could have stood in the ash and asked her questions, and she had not, because she had understood — not with her mind but with the animal part of her that read weather — that the answer would be a debt too, and it would be hers.

The vaccines mattered. She said it again, out loud, over the engine noise, and the plane shook its head the way planes do, and she let it.

* * *

Pulau Anak came up out of the sea at first light, a green comma of an island with its clinic strip cut into a coconut grove, 640 meters of laterite that turned to porridge in the rain. Jonah made her approach into the wind off the water, touched down at the thousand-foot marks with the mains kissing first the way the book said, and taxied up to the shed where a nun named Sister Bernadette was already waiting with a wheelbarrow, because on Pulau Anak the wheelbarrow was the ground-crew.

"You're late," Sister Bernadette said, in the tone of a woman reporting the tide.

"Wind," Jonah said, which was true, and "customs," which was not, and the nun took both explanations with the same flat mercy she took everything.

They unloaded the vaccines together, and Sister Bernadette counted the cold-boxes twice, and then, as Jonah was signing the delivery chit against the wheelbarrow's fender, the nun looked at her with those pale, unhurried eyes and said: "There's a rumor on the water. About the reef at Karang Laut."

Jonah kept her face doing nothing at all. Five years of this work had taught her that her face was cargo too, and it was never, ever labeled.

"The tide," the nun went on, tying off the empty burlap, "went out further than anyone living has seen. Last week, at the dark of the moon. And the reef — the reef that's on no chart, the one the old people charge pilgrims to walk to — it opened. There's a cave under it. A mouth in the rock." She folded the burlap into a neat square. "The fishermen are saying the sea gave something back."

"Sea does that," Jonah said. "Takes it back too."

"Mm," said Sister Bernadette, who had heard every deflection the coast could manufacture. "Fly carefully, Miss Zhou. The weather's turning."

It was the closest thing to a blessing the coast offered, and Jonah took it, and was rolling before the nun had reached the shed.

* * *

The crossing to Tanjung Rhu was forty minutes of low flying under a deck that kept lowering, and Jonah spent them doing what she always did over open water: reading the coast the way other people read a face. There was the fishing fleet, working the channel markers. There was the long grey smear of a patrol boat that might have been customs and might have been nothing. There was, far out near the horizon where the reef country began, a cluster of boats that were not fishing — too still, too evenly spaced, holding a line the way men hold a line when they're looking for something or guarding it. She marked them without deciding to. Her mind kept a second manifest, always had: things that were wrong, arranged by distance.

Tanjung Rhu was a beach, technically, and an airstrip only by the optimism of the man who'd cleared the palms off it. She came in over the treeline at 6:40, put the Otter down on the wet sand at the waterline where the surface was firmest, and there was Pak Usman with his two boys and his boat, exactly on time, exactly as silent.

The black crate came out through the cargo door and down the ramp and into the boat, and the boys took the weight without a word, and Pak Usman counted nothing because there was nothing to count — the payment had already been made, months ago, in a currency Jonah was not shown. Eleven minutes. She was back in the pilot's seat with her hands on the yoke and her heart doing something she chose not to examine, and she was thirty meters into her takeoff run when she saw the survey stakes.

They were new — the wood still pale, the paint still bright — driven into the sand above the high-tide line in a neat row, each one flagged with a scrap of orange surveyor's tape. She'd landed here four months ago. There had been no stakes then. Somebody was measuring the coast. Somebody with orange tape and patience and, if the boats out by the reef were any indication, a fleet.

She lifted off over the treeline and did not look back, because looking back was a habit and habits were debts, and she had enough of those.

* * *

The number was eleven thousand four hundred.

She didn't let herself think it during the day — daytime was for fuel sums and weight-and-balance and the small honest arithmetic of a working pilot — but it came for her at night, the way it always did, in the hour after she'd tied down the plane and before sleep, when the hangar was dark except for the bulb and the geckos were ticking in the rafters like slow clocks. Eleven thousand four hundred rupiah-times-a-thousand, owed to a woman called Auntie Ru who had never once mentioned the number aloud, because the number was not the point. The number was just the interest that the *other* thing accrued — the thing Jonah had signed for five years ago with a thumbprint and a cup of tea, when she'd been twenty-six and freshly licensed and one bad season from losing everything, and Auntie Ru had sat across from her in a floating restaurant with her hands folded and said, in the voice of a favorite aunt, *the coast is full of pilots who can fly, Miss Zhou, and full of pilots who can be trusted, and so rarely full of both.*

Jonah had understood even then that she was signing something, and she had signed it anyway, because the alternative had been a specific and vivid kind of drowning, and because — this was the part she kept in the deepest drawer, under the vaccines, under the burned village, under everything — because some traitorous part of her had been *flattered.* Auntie Ru had chosen her. The Marrow Syndicate, which owned the coastal trade the way the tide owned the beach, had looked at Jonah Zhou and seen a woman who could be both.

The flattery had lasted a year. The debt had compounded weekly.

She sat in the hangar that night with the logbook open on her knees and did not write in it, and the geckos ticked, and out over the water the first true rain of the season came down the strait like a curtain being drawn across the world, and she thought: *I'm late. Not on the delivery — the delivery was made. Late on the other thing. Three weeks past the date the envelope was supposed to come, and no envelope, and no message, which from Auntie Ru was worse than a message.*

Silence, from a fixer, was an invoice.

She was still sitting there when she heard the motorcycle — a small engine far off on the coast road, coming closer, its sound rising and falling with the wind. It stopped outside the hangar. Through the gap under the doors she watched a headlamp swing, and then a boy's silhouette, and then a folded paper slid through the gap onto the concrete, and then the engine pulled away and was gone.

Jonah sat for a full minute, letting the dread arrive properly, the way you let a storm front arrive — no point pretending you could outrun it, better to stand still and feel the first drops and know what you were in for.

Then she picked up the paper. It was a telegram form, the kind the coastal office used, and the message on it was seven words long, and it was signed with a small, neat, unmistakable character that everyone on the coast knew and no one said aloud.

*Come to the floating restaurant. We will have tea.*

* * *

She read it four times. The rain came harder on the hangar roof, a sound like the whole coast applauding something she hadn't done yet.

Tea. Not a summons — a *tea.* That was the frightening part, or one of the frightening parts, because Auntie Ru did not summon over tea; she *negotiated* over tea, and negotiation meant the ledger had moved, and a ledger that moved meant a number that had changed, and Jonah's number had been changing in only one direction for five years.

She looked at the plane, tied down in the dark, its tail number a month from expiring into whatever false registration she'd forge next. She looked at the telegram. She thought of the survey stakes at Tanjung Rhu, orange flags snapping in the wind, and the boats holding their line out by the reef, and a nun's flat voice saying *the sea gave something back,* and she felt — beneath the dread, beneath the weariness worn smooth as a throttle knob — the first thin current of something she hadn't felt in years and could not afford.

Curiosity.

She folded the telegram into her breast pocket, over the heart, where the coast kept everything it couldn't deny, and went to find a lamp, and a chart, and the answer to a question she was already afraid of: *how far is it, by air, to the floating restaurant?*
