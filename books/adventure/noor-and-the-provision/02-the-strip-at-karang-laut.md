## 2. The Strip at Karang Laut

The tide was Jonah's co-pilot, and like all her co-pilots it never spoke.

She came in low over the strait with the first grey light still stuck to the water, the Cessna's floats kissing a skin of mist that hadn't decided yet whether to be sea or cloud. Below her, Karang Laut revealed itself the way it always did at this hour: a crescent of black volcanic sand, a scatter of stilt houses on the landward side, and then the strip—a long tongue of packed coral and shell that existed only between tides, appearing and disappearing twice a day like something breathing. On her chart it was marked with a dotted line and a single word in faded type: *unsurveyed*. Most pilots took that as a warning. Jonah had learned to take it as an invitation, because unsurveyed meant unwatched, and unwatched meant she could land without anyone upstream noting her arrival in a ledger she'd never see.

She checked the tide table taped to the yoke—her own handwriting, ink gone brown with salt—and judged she had perhaps ninety minutes of strip before the sea came back to reclaim its property. Ninety minutes was enough. It was always enough. That was the whole architecture of her life these days: enough time, barely; enough money, almost; enough distance from the things chasing her, if she never stopped moving long enough for them to measure the gap.

The floats touched down with a sound like tearing silk, and the plane settled into its long hissing run, spray fanning out behind her in two white wings. She taxied up onto the coral shelf, killed the engine, and sat for a moment in the sudden silence, listening to the hull tick as it cooled and the distant argument of fishing boats heading out past the reef.

*Never land twice in the same place.* Rule one. She had already broken it three times with Karang Laut, which either meant the rule was softening or the village was becoming something other than a place on a manifest. She preferred not to examine which.

*Never carry what you can't deny.* Rule two. In the cargo bay behind her seats sat two crates of influenza vaccine, cold-chain intact, destined for the clinic Ibu Salmah ran out of her front room. And beneath them, lashed down with the same care and the same rope, a third crate with no label at all, no manifest entry, no existence whatsoever—which was precisely what made it deniable, and precisely what made it heavy. It weighed nothing, that crate. It weighed four hundred kilos. Both statements were true, depending on which part of the night you asked her about.

She climbed out onto the shelf, boots crunching on broken coral, and began unloading the medicine first. Always the medicine first. It was a small vanity, a piece of theater she performed for herself: *this is who I am, this is the job that matters*. The lie worked better before dawn, when no one was awake to see her hands hesitate over the second crate.

"You're early," said a voice, and there was Ibu Salmah coming down the beach with a lantern she didn't need anymore, because Salmah carried her lantern the way other women carried handbags—as an accessory to authority rather than a source of light. She was seventy if she was fifty, small and square as a mooring post, wrapped in a batik sarong the color of dried chilies, and she walked the tidal shelf barefoot with the certainty of someone who had been reading this reef since before Jonah's parents were born.

"Early is on time," Jonah said. "Late is a rumor."

"And you are never late." Salmah reached the plane and put one weathered hand flat against the fuselage, the way you'd greet a horse. "Only the tide is late, and only when it has business elsewhere." She peered into the cargo bay, saw the vaccine crates, and nodded once—the nod of a woman doing arithmetic she would not share. "Cold?"

"Chain's intact. Ice packs are good for another six hours. Tell your nurse to get them into the icebox before the sun finds them."

"They'll be in the icebox before your wheels leave the sand." Salmah took one end of the first crate without being asked, and together they hauled it up above the high-tide line, where the village's boys would come for it. Jonah noticed, as they worked, that Salmah kept glancing—not at the plane, but past it, out toward the reef, toward a stretch of water half a mile east where the morning light lay flat and strange.

"You keep looking at my horizon," Jonah said, setting down the second crate. "Should I be worried?"

Salmah laughed, a short dry bark like a gull being robbed. "Worried. Hah. You fly through storms for a living; what could my horizon do to you?" But she didn't stop looking, and Jonah had survived five years in this trade by noticing exactly that kind of thing—the glance that lingered, the question folded inside a joke, the way a woman who owned every secret on her coast would give one away for free and pretend it cost nothing.

They got the second crate up the beach together, and then Salmah did what she always did: she paid. Not in currency—currency was for people who trusted banks—but in fish, whole and gleaming and packed in a woven basket lined with banana leaf: two yellowfin tuna, a bundle of dried squid, and a clay pot of sambal that smelled like it could strip paint. It was generous beyond any fair exchange rate, and both of them knew it, and neither of them named it, because naming it would have made it charity, and Salmah did not deal in charity. She dealt in accounts.

"The fish is too much," Jonah said anyway, because the ritual required it.

"The fish is correct," Salmah said, closing the ritual. Then she stood there a moment longer than the ritual allowed, lantern swinging gently from her fist, looking at Jonah with those eyes that had watched forty monsoons drown this beach and rebuild it. "You have time? Before the tide comes for your runway?"

Jonah checked the light on the water. Eighty minutes, maybe less. "A little."

"Then walk with me. There is something you should see. And afterward—" Salmah lifted the basket of fish, "—you will want to be paid in warnings instead."

* * *

They walked east along the sand, below the tideline where the going was firm, Salmah setting a pace that looked leisurely and covered ground like a patrol boat. The village woke around them in stages: smoke rising from cooking fires, a dog conducting its morning grievances, children hauling nets that were bigger than they were. Everyone greeted Salmah. Several people greeted Jonah by name, which she noted with the small cold pleasure and smaller cold alarm that recognition always brought her. A pilot who was known was a pilot who could be found. But Karang Laut had a way of making her forget to be careful, and she resented the village a little for it, the way you resent a warm bed for making you sleep.

The reef curved away ahead of them, a dark serrated line where the swells came apart in long white seams. And then the beach bent inward, following a shallow bay Jonah had flown over a hundred times, and Salmah stopped walking, and pointed with her chin.

"There," she said. "Look at what the sea has returned."

At first Jonah saw nothing but water—the same green-brown shallows, the same lazy swell. Then the light shifted, or her eye finally found the right angle, and she saw it: a gap in the reef. Not a channel—a *mouth*. A ragged oval of black opening in the coral maybe thirty meters across, ringed with wet stone that glistened even at this distance, and beyond it, receding into darkness, the suggestion of a passage leading down and inward, under the island itself, or under whatever older thing the island was built on top of.

The tide was low—lower than low, she realized, lower than any tide she'd seen on this coast in five years of flying it. The water had pulled back so far that flats she'd never known existed lay exposed and steaming, and the cave mouth stood clear of the retreating sea like a door left open by someone in a hurry.

"That wasn't on any chart," Jonah said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended.

"No," Salmah agreed. "Charts are young."

Jonah turned to look at her. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Salmah said, "that the men who draw charts measure the water. They do not measure what the water remembers." She began walking again, along a path of flat coral that led toward the mouth, and after a moment Jonah followed, because of course she did, because curiosity was the one debt she'd never managed to stop servicing.

Up close, the cave mouth was larger than it had seemed from the beach—high enough to drive a truck through, though no truck could ever reach it—and the stone around it was not natural stone. Jonah saw that within ten steps. The edges were too regular. There were courses of fitted blocks beneath the wilder coral growth, and carved into the lintel above the opening, worn nearly smooth but still legible to a patient eye, was a band of script. Not Latin characters. Not Arabic. Something older, angular, dense, running in horizontal lines like text on a page—or like entries in a ledger.

She stopped in front of it and felt the hair rise on her forearms, which was absurd, because she did not believe in the hair-rising kind of history. She believed in fuel gauges and tide tables and the weight of cash in a sealed envelope. But standing before that carved doorway, with the smell of wet stone and salt and something faintly mineral underneath, like iron, like old blood, she understood that she was looking at writing, and that writing meant records, and records meant somebody had once considered this place important enough to keep accounts in.

"What is it?" she asked.

"A toll house," Salmah said simply. "Or a treasury. Or a court. My grandmother called it the counting house. Her grandmother called it the mouth of the ancestors. The names change; the door does not." She crouched, touched the lowest course of blocks with two fingers, the way you'd touch a grave marker. "For as long as anyone can remember, this reef has been closed. Sealed under the water except at the very lowest tides of the year, and even then, only a crack. Pilgrims used to come from villages three days' walk away, and we charged them for the crossing, and we told them what our grandmothers told us: the sea opens the door when it wants the debts read, and closes it when the reading is done."

"And now?"

"Now the sea has opened it wider than my grandmother ever saw, and a week earlier than the season, and nobody knows why." Salmah straightened, and her voice lost its storytelling warmth and became, abruptly, the voice of a woman managing a crisis. "And the fishermen who found it—Pak Daud and his sons, out before dawn last Tuesday—came back to the village white as sailcloth and would not go near it again. They say there are chambers inside. Chambers, plural. Walls of writing. And something at the far end, deep in, that they would not describe except to say it was *kept*, the way you keep a thing that belongs to someone."

Jonah looked into the mouth. The darkness inside was total, absorbing the morning light a few meters in and giving nothing back. From somewhere deep within came a sound—rhythmic, hollow, patient. Water moving in a chamber, filling and draining, filling and draining. The cave was breathing with the tide.

"How long has it been open?" she asked.

"Since last week. And the barometer is falling, girl. When the monsoon comes—and it is coming early, my knees have already filed their report—the storm surge will close this door until next year. Perhaps forever, if the reef shifts." Salmah turned and fixed Jonah with a look that had weight and edges. "Which means there is a window. And windows, on this coast, attract moths."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning three days ago a boat came. Fast boat, new engine, men in clothes too clean for fishing. They walked the flats. They measured the mouth with instruments on tripods. They drove steel stakes into the coral and tied orange tape to them, here and here and there." Salmah pointed, and Jonah followed her finger to a line of survey stakes marching across the exposed reef like a row of planted knives. "They did not ask permission. They did not pay the crossing fee, which even pilgrims pay. And when Pak Daud asked them what they were doing, the one who spoke said—" she paused, and delivered the words with careful contempt, "—he said, 'This reef is now under survey for resource development.' Resource development. On a door my family has guarded for nine generations."

Jonah said nothing. Her stomach had begun performing a slow, familiar contraction, the one it made whenever two separate pieces of information moved toward each other in her mind and she saw, with terrible clarity, how neatly they were going to fit. An unlabeled crate in her hold. A syndicate whose fixer called her *little bird* in a voice like syrup. Survey stakes on a reef that had just opened a door full of records.

It fit. Of course it fit. Everything in her life fit together eventually, like ballast settling in a hold.

"Ibu," she said carefully, "these men with the clean clothes. Did they leave a name? A company?"

"They left stakes and tape and a smell of diesel." Salmah's eyes narrowed, reading Jonah's face with the fluency of a woman who had spent a lifetime reading weather. "You know them."

"I know the *type*," Jonah said, which was true, and which was also the most honest lie she'd told in weeks.

Salmah held her gaze for a long moment. Then she sighed, and the anger went out of her posture and left something wearier behind, something that looked almost like pity. "Come," she said. "The tide is turning. Walk me back, and I will finish paying you."

* * *

They walked back along the sand in silence for a while, the basket of fish swinging between them, the cave mouth shrinking behind them into the landscape as though embarrassed to have been seen. The first wavelets of the returning tide were already licking at the outermost survey stake, tugging at its orange ribbon.

"You will want to go inside," Salmah said at last. It was not a question.

"Everyone wants to know what's in a locked room," Jonah said. "That's not the same as wanting to open it."

"No. But you are a woman who delivers things," Salmah said. "And a delivery woman looks at a room full of records and thinks about what they weigh, and where they could be flown to, and who would pay for the flight. Am I wrong?"

Jonah kept her eyes on the sand. "You're spending my fish money pretty freely."

Salmah barked her laugh again, but it died quickly. "Listen to me, Jonah Zhou. I am going to tell you something my grandmother told me, and you may file it with the rest of your cargo." She stopped walking and faced the sea, and the wind off the water pushed her sarong against her like a hand. "This coast runs on debts. Every family, every boat, every field—we are all of us in each other's books, and we have been for longer than any government has had a name for us. The cave is not magic. The cave is a *ledger*. The oldest one we have. And ledgers are dangerous things, because they do not care who is powerful now. They only care who owed whom, and how much, and whether it was ever paid." She turned back to Jonah. "The men with the clean clothes understand this. That is why they came with measuring instruments and not with offerings. They are not pilgrims. They are collectors."

"And what would you have me do about it?" Jonah asked. "I deliver vaccines and fish gets sold and—" she gestured vaguely at the reef, "—old doors stay shut. I'm not a historian. I'm not a policeman. I'm barely a pilot, some months."

"You are the only person on this coast with wings," Salmah said, "and the only person who has come to my door in five years who asks nothing and owes nothing and can leave whenever she wishes. Do you know how rare that makes you?" She picked up the basket again and resumed walking, and her voice went lighter, deliberately, the way a helmsman eases the wheel after a hard turn. "I am not asking you to do anything. I am an old woman on a beach. I am telling you what I see, and what I see is this: the sea opened a door, and the first ones through it were men with stakes and tape, and the second one will be you, because you cannot help yourself—I watched your face at that lintel, girl, I watched you *read* it. So I am paying you in advance. Fish today. Warnings today. And one more warning, free of charge, for the road."

They had reached the plane. The tide was creeping up the coral shelf now in visible increments, silvering the surface where Jonah had landed an hour ago. She checked her watch, then the water, doing the arithmetic of departure.

"Which is?"

Salmah set the basket down at the edge of the strip and looked at her, and for a moment all the authority fell away and she was simply an old woman with salt-stiffened hands and a lantern she didn't need.

"When the sea gives something back after this long," she said, "it is not giving. It is *presenting*. Like evidence." She pressed the basket into Jonah's arms. "Fly carefully, little bird. And whatever you carry under the vaccine crates—carry it lightly. This coast weighs everything."

Jonah stood there with the fish in her arms and the word *little bird* sitting in her ear like a splinter, and watched Salmah walk away up the beach, barefoot, unhurried, a woman collecting her morning from a village that had been keeping its own books since before anyone thought to burn them.

By the time she had the engine running, the water had reclaimed the first twenty meters of strip. She lifted off into air that smelled of rain to come, banked east out of habit, and then—before she could argue with herself—banked west instead, and made one slow pass over the bay.

From the air, at low tide, with the sun fully up, the cave mouth was unmistakable: a black eye in the face of the reef, lidless, open, watching the sky the way she was watching it. Around it, the Syndicate's orange tape fluttered on its stakes like small flags marking a claim. And beyond the reef, far out in the strait, she saw a boat—fast, new, riding low with equipment—turning in a wide, patient arc, the way a man turns when he is checking his traps.

Jonah leveled out, set a heading for home, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the altitude.

She had ninety minutes of daylight and a crate she couldn't deny, and somewhere behind her a door was standing open that no chart had ever recorded, full of accounts older than any ledger she owed on.

She flew north, and the reef closed slowly behind her like a book being marked, not finished.
