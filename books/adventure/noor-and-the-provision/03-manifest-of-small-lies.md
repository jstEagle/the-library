## 3. Manifest of Small Lies

The hangar at Tanjung Serai smelled of avgas and old rain, of canvas that had been wet too many times and never quite dried, of the particular sourness of a man's sweat soaked into a pilot's seat over five years of flying. Jonah had rented it under a name that wasn't hers—*L. Marasigan*, which was close enough to her mother's maiden name to feel like a joke and far enough to pass a casual check—and she paid the rent in cash, in person, on the first Tuesday of every month, because cash left no trail and Tuesdays were when the harbor master drank.

She climbed out of the plane's cabin into the blue hour before dawn, stiff in every joint that mattered, and stood for a moment in the wide mouth of the hangar watching the strait go from pewter to silver. The vaccines were gone—delivered to the clinic at Muara Dua, signed for by a nurse with beautiful handwriting who would never know what else had ridden in the same hold. The other crate was gone too. That one she hadn't signed for anyone. She'd set it down on a pallet behind the fish sheds at Karang Laut, exactly where the manifest said, and walked away without looking back, because looking back was how you started asking questions, and asking questions was how you ended up in the water.

Five years of this. Five years of holding two cargoes in her head at once—the true one and the deniable one—and keeping them separate the way you kept oil and water separate, with a steady hand and constant attention and the knowledge that one bad day would mix them forever.

She pulled the hangar doors shut against the light. Not all the way. A crack, so the morning could watch her work if it wanted to. There was comfort in being watched by something that couldn't testify.

* * *

The ledger lived in a tin box under the floorboards beneath the tool chest, and the tin box was itself a lie—a biscuit tin, Royal Baking brand, painted with a smiling woman in a kitchen she'd probably never entered. Inside: three notebooks, a roll of banknotes wrapped in oilcloth, a spare ignition key taped to a card reading *SPARE — DO NOT LOSE*, and a photograph of a woman Jonah had stopped being able to look at directly sometime around the second year. Her mother, standing in front of a house that no longer existed, squinting into a sun that had since set on everything they owned.

Jonah sat cross-legged on the concrete with the third notebook open across her knees and did what she did every time she came home, which was audit herself. Not her accounts. Herself. The accounts were simple; it was the lying that required maintenance.

*Registration number,* she wrote, or rather had written, months ago, in pencil so it could be amended: PK-4471. False. The true registry was buried in a shipping manifest from 1936, in an archive she'd never seen, attached to a plane whose original owner had died owing money to men who now, through some chain of acquisition she preferred not to trace, had a claim on the airframe itself. PK-4471 belonged to a decommissioned crop-duster that had crashed into a rubber plantation in '49 and been quietly absorbed into the paperwork of the living. When she flew, she flew as a dead plane wearing a dead plane's clothes. Customs officers along six hundred miles of coast knew her by the false number. Some of them knew it was false. Two of them had helped her choose it. None of them would ever say so.

*Base of operations:* Tanjung Serai, rented, month to month, landlord bribed quarterly. *Fuel supplier:* two, alternating, so neither could describe her habits completely. *Insurance:* lapsed, deliberately; a policy was a document, and documents were confessions waiting to be subpoenaed. *Next of kin:* blank. She had filled that line in once, early, with her mother's name, and then spent a week unable to sleep until she'd burned the form and filed another. A next of kin was a door someone could knock on. She had spent five years making sure there were no doors.

And then, at the bottom of the page, in ink this time, because pencil felt like cowardice even to a woman fluent in it:

*Owed to Auntie Ru: 4,800. Compounding weekly.*

She looked at the figure the way you look at a diagnosis. It had started at 1,200—engine overhaul, three years ago, the kind of bill that arrives when you're already drowning and offers itself as a rope. Auntie Ru hadn't lent her money. Auntie Ru had bought her, at a fair price, in installments, and called each installment a courtesy. The interest was not usurious. That was the genius of it. Twelve percent, compounding weekly, calculated by hand in a green ledger book with a cracked spine, explained aloud in a voice like warm syrup, itemized down to the individual rupiah so that there was nothing to dispute, nothing to argue, nothing to grab onto. Just arithmetic. Just the tide coming in, a centimeter at a time, over ground you'd thought was yours.

Four thousand eight hundred. She earned perhaps six hundred on a good run, four on a typical one, and half of everything went to fuel and fees and the quiet envelopes passed to men whose titles she didn't ask about. At this rate she would die solvent around the age of ninety-one, assuming the engine held, assuming the monsoons stayed polite, assuming Auntie Ru's patience was infinite.

It was not infinite. She knew that the way you know a storm is coming—not from any single sign but from the way the air begins to taste of iron.

* * *

The morning passed in maintenance, which was its own kind of meditation. She drained the water from the fuel sump and held the sample jar up to the light: clear, a faint amber, no particulates. Good. She checked the tension on the landing wires by plucking them like instrument strings—an E flat meant serviceable, anything sharper meant corrosion working its way in—and got, from the third wire on the port side, a note slightly too bright. She marked it with chalk. Next week. Everything was next week; her whole life was a schedule of deferrals balanced on top of a schedule of debts.

Teodoro Reyes did not exist for her yet. The man who would matter was still a rumor she hadn't heard, a tail number she hadn't matched, a grief she hadn't witnessed. Today the only other soul in the hangar was Pak Usman, the mechanic, who came twice a week and asked no questions because not asking questions was the most valuable service he provided and he priced it accordingly.

"Engine sounds rich on the left mag," he said, head buried in the cowling, hands moving with the blind certainty of forty years.

"Rich is better than lean," Jonah said.

"Lean is honest," Pak Usman said, and said nothing further, and Jonah understood that he had just told her something true in the only language they shared, which was the language of things almost said.

She thought about honesty while she cleaned the spark plugs. Not the abstract kind—the kind you could practice. What would it cost her, actually, to be honest? With whom? About what? The questions multiplied like the debt. Honest with customs about the crate behind the fish sheds: prison, or worse, depending on which office got to her first. Honest with Auntie Ru about the vaccines' true value: renegotiation, and Ru always won renegotiations. Honest with herself about why she was still flying: that was the expensive one. That one cost sleep.

Because here was the thing she circled every time, the drain at the bottom of every audit: she told herself she was flying to pay off the debt. And the debt never went down. So either she was bad at arithmetic—which she was not, arithmetic was the one clean thing in her life—or the flying was not for the debt at all. The flying was for the flying. For the hour after takeoff when the coastline unrolled beneath her like a scroll being read aloud, and no one could reach her, and the radio hissed with static instead of demands, and for eleven minutes or forty she was not a debtor, not a smuggler, not a woman with two manifests in her head, but simply a body in motion above the water, answerable to gravity and weather and nothing else.

That was the escape route. That was always the escape route. And it had taken her five years to notice that the escape route ran in a circle.

* * *

The heat came up off the tarmac by noon like a second sun rising from below. Jonah retreated into the hangar's shade with cold tea and the notebook and turned to a page she visited less often than the accounts: the map.

Not a real map. A diagram, drawn freehand, of her own life rendered as geography. In the center, a small square labeled HANGAR. Radiating outward, lines: to Muara Dua (clinic, safe, 40 min), to Karang Laut (pallet behind fish sheds, semi-safe, 55 min), to the fuel dock at Selat Panjang (neutral ground, 25 min), to the floating restaurant at Batu Ampar (Auntie Ru's territory, unsafe, 90 min), to the airstrip at Pulau Naga (hostile since the incident, do not use), to the strip at Teluk Sendok (abandoned, runway crumbled at both ends, usable by her alone because she alone knew where the crumbles were).

She had drawn this map two years ago, in a burst of what she'd then called strategy. Look at it now and it resolved into something else entirely. Every line terminated. Every line terminated at a place where someone knew her face, or her false registration, or the sound of her engine at idle, which was distinctive—she'd been told so by a fisherman at Karang Laut who identified her from two miles off by ear alone, *the one that coughs on the left*, and had been proud of it, and she had smiled at him, and had then spent the whole flight home thinking about what it meant that strangers could catalog her.

Every line led somewhere someone was waiting. There was no line leading out. No line that simply ended in open water, in altitude, in elsewhere. She had built a life whose entire architecture was approach routes. She had mistaken corridors for exits because corridors felt like movement, and movement felt like freedom, and freedom was the word she used instead of the truth, which was that she had never once, in five years, chosen a destination. Every destination had chosen her—by debt, by cargo, by rumor, by the path of least surveillance. She went where she was known, because where she was unknown was where she was vulnerable, and where she was known was where she was owned, and she had somehow arranged things so that those were the same places.

She put the map away and sat very still in the tin-box smell of the hangar while the afternoon thickened outside, and let herself feel it fully for the first time: not fear exactly. Claustrophobia. The specific claustrophobia of a bird that has been flying in a cage so long it has mistaken the cage's dimensions for the sky's.

There was a version of her, she knew—the version from before, the one in the photograph in the biscuit tin, the twenty-three-year-old with her mother's chin and her father's stubbornness—who would have looked at this map and burned it and flown anyway, anywhere, consequences be damned. But that version had believed in doors. This version knew better. Knew that the world past the last friendly airstrip was not freedom but a larger corridor with fewer lamps. Knew that running was a skill and she had mastered it, and mastery was its own trap, because the better you got at fleeing the more your life became a function of pursuit, defined at every moment by what you were escaping rather than what you wanted.

What did she want?

She wrote the question in the notebook, in pencil, small, in the margin beside the debt figure. Then she sat there while the tea went cold and the geckos resumed their argument in the rafters, and she did not write an answer, because she didn't have one, and writing a fake one would have violated the only rule of the audit, which was: record what is, not what should be.

* * *

The telegram came at dusk, which was when telegrams always came, because important people conducted their business at the hours when their counterparts were tired.

The boy who delivered it—fourteen maybe, bicycle rattling, shirt sticking to his ribs—came to the hangar office window and handed over the envelope with both hands, formally, the way you hand over something that might be a summons. Jonah signed the book. *L. Marasigan.* The boy pedaled off toward whatever his evening held, and she stood in the doorway holding the envelope unopened for a full minute, because opening it immediately had become, over the years, a discipline: the sixty seconds between receipt and reading were the last seconds in which the message did not exist, and hoarding those seconds was a small luxury she allowed herself the way a miser allows himself one gold coin kept apart from the rest, touched but not spent.

Then she opened it.

The telegram slip was the standard coastal stock, thin as onion skin, printed in faded violet. The text was in Auntie Ru's characteristic style, which was to say: courteous, precise, and carrying underneath its politeness the pressure of a thumb on a scale.

> TO L. MARASIGAN TANJUNG SERAI STOP TRUST YOU ARE WELL AND THE ENGINE BEHAVING STOP BUSINESS MATTERS REQUIRE OUR ATTENTION JOINTLY STOP REQUEST YOUR PRESENCE BATU AMPAR THURSDAY EVENING MEAL PROVIDED STOP NO REPLY NECESSARY AS ATTENDANCE IS UNDERSTOOD STOP REGARDS TO THE LEFT MAGNETO STOP RU

Jonah read it three times.

*Business matters require our attention jointly.* Which meant: I have decided something and you will hear it in person, because what I have to say does not survive being written down. *Meal provided.* Which meant: come hungry, leave fed, be softened. Ru fed people before she measured them; there was a whole choreography to her table, where you sat, which cup was yours, whether the fish arrived head-on or filleted, and every detail was data and every courtesy was a claim. *No reply necessary as attendance is understood.* Which meant: I am not inviting you. I am informing you. The difference between an invitation and a notification is the difference between a guest and an asset, and Jonah had crossed that line so long ago she'd forgotten there was a line.

But it was the last line that made her go back and read the whole thing again. *Regards to the left magneto.*

Pak Usman had said, that very morning, standing right there in this hangar with his head in the cowling: the engine runs rich on the left mag. Jonah had mentioned it to no one. Had written it nowhere except the chalk mark on the cowl itself, inside the hangar, in a building she believed was private.

Ru knew about the left magneto.

Which meant Ru's ears reached into the hangar at Tanjung Serai. Which meant Pak Usman reported, or the landlord reported, or the boy who delivered parts reported, or all of them, a lattice of small informants none of whom would call themselves informants, each passing along a harmless detail here, a harmless detail there, until the details arrived at Batu Ampar and were assembled into a picture of her life more complete than the one she kept in her own notebook. They knew when she flew. They knew what she carried—at least the shape of it. They knew the state of her engine. They probably knew how much cash was in the biscuit tin, or close enough.

And the phrase itself—*regards to the left magneto*—was not information. It was a demonstration. Ru was showing her the depth of the net, casually, in a closing pleasantry, the way you show a dog its leash by giving it a gentle tug. Not hard enough to hurt. Just enough that the dog remembers the leash exists.

Jonah sat down on the oil-stained stool by the workbench and let the envelope lie flat on the bench in front of her and stared at it while the light failed. Thursday. Three days. Ninety minutes' flight to Batu Ampar, over open water, in a plane whose engine condition was known to the person she was flying to meet. If the left magneto failed over the strait—if it *was made* to fail—there would be no mystery, no investigation, no wreckage recovered worth recovering. The sea would file the report. The sea lost things all the time.

She caught herself planning the flight already, involuntarily, the way her mind planned everything: departure at first light Thursday to arrive well before the appointed evening, approach from the north so the sun wouldn't be in her eyes on descent, land at the restaurant's mooring pontoon with the wind abeam, keep the engine hot in case—

In case. In case of what? In case she needed to take off from a dinner party hosted by the woman who owned her? She had done it before, once, eighteen months ago, when a meeting at Batu Ampar had curdled mid-dessert and she'd excused herself to "check on the plane" and taken off from the pontoon in the dark with Ru's people thirty seconds from deciding to detain her. She'd told herself afterward that it proved she could always get away. It had actually proven something worse: that Ru let her go. That the escape had been permitted, part of the lesson, a demonstration that flight was possible and therefore that staying was a choice, and choices could be billed.

Every escape route is a corridor. She had drawn the map herself. She had just never read it properly until tonight.

* * *

She did not sleep so much as negotiate with the dark. Around two in the morning she gave up, lit the workbench lamp, and took out the notebook again, and this time she turned past the accounts, past the map, to the last pages—the ones she rarely wrote in, the ones labeled in her own shorthand *IF*, as in *if it goes wrong*.

The IF pages contained plans. Real ones, detailed ones: bail amounts and which magistrate could be bribed and which couldn't; the location of a boat at Muara Dua that could be borrowed in extremis; the fact that the airstrip at Teluk Sendok, the crumbled one, had a stand of casuarina trees at its northern end tall enough to hide a parked plane from the air; the name of a lawyer in the capital who owed her nothing but hated the Syndicate, which amounted to the same currency. Escape plans, contingency plans, the furniture of a fugitive's inner life. She reviewed them the way other people reviewed prayers.

And tonight, going through them line by line by lamplight, she saw what she had never let herself see: that every plan assumed the same shape. Get out. Get clear. Get gone. Start again somewhere the name Zhou meant nothing and the face meant less. Every plan ended in disappearance, and disappearance, examined honestly, was just exile with better branding. She would land somewhere new, invent a new name, rent a new hangar under it, build a new lattice of acquaintances who didn't ask questions, acquire—inevitably, because a pilot needs an engine overhaul eventually, needs a favor eventually, needs credit eventually—acquire a new Auntie Ru. Maybe not named Ru. Maybe a man, maybe a bank, maybe a customs colonel with a gambling habit. But the same creature. The same green ledger. The same twelve percent compounding weekly against a life measured in flight hours.

You could outrun a debt. You could not outrun the shape of yourself that kept signing for debts.

Unless. Unless there was something a person could do that wasn't running and wasn't staying and wasn't any position on the axis she'd been sliding along for five years. She didn't know what it was. She only knew that the IF pages had never once contained a plan that began with *tell the truth*, and the absence was starting to feel less like prudence and more like a hole in the hull, below the waterline, in the dark, where you couldn't see it until the boat was already taking on weight.

She closed the notebook. Put it in the tin. Put the tin under the floorboards. Stood in the hangar mouth at three in the morning with the strait black and breathing below her, the reef at Karang Laut somewhere out there under the water, keeping whatever it kept, and the monsoon still weeks off but already assembling itself beyond the horizon like a debt of weather.

Three days to Batu Ampar. A meal provided. Attendance understood.

She went back inside and, for the first time in five years, wrote a letter she did not send—to no one, to the woman in the photograph, to whoever might someday read the biscuit tin—and the letter said only this: *I think I am going to be asked for something on Thursday that cannot be flown away from.*

She sealed it in an envelope, addressed it to herself, General Delivery, Tanjung Serai post office, and put it in her flight jacket's inner pocket, where documents waited. Not evidence. A message in a bottle, launched inland, addressed to the person she might need to become.

Then she checked the left magneto herself, by lamplight, plug by plug, wire by wire, because if the engine was going to fail she wanted it to fail honestly—and found, tucked behind the magneto housing, wedged where only a hand doing exactly what she had just done would ever find it, a scrap of paper folded to the size of a stamp.

Someone had left her a note inside her own engine.
