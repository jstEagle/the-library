## 4. What the Tide Owes

The tide had been going out for three hours when Teodoro Reyes noticed the plane.

He was on his knees in the mud at the edge of the mangrove flats, which was where he spent most mornings now, though he would have said—had anyone asked, and no one ever did—that he was *working*. The distinction mattered to him. A man who knelt in the mud was a vagrant. A man who worked the mud with a trowel, a sieve, and a notebook bound in oilcloth was an archivist whose archive happened to be the intertidal zone of a coast that had forgotten him. He had been sorting through the silt where the old customs jetty had stood before the war, before the fire, before everything, looking for whatever the sea had decided to give back: a brass fitting, a shard of export porcelain, once—a morning he still thought about—the corner of a ledger page, waterlogged and ghostly, its ink gone to brown shadow but its ruling lines still true.

That was the thing about paper. It wanted to come back.

The plane came out of the east, low over the strait, flying the way no scheduled flight flew—skimming, banking around weather rather than through it, dropping toward the coast as if it had business with the water itself. Teodoro knew aircraft the way other men knew horses. He had catalogued enough manifests in his years at the colonial archive to know what moved across this strait and why: the mail planes kept their altitudes, the survey flights kept their grids, the government transports kept their schedules and their dignity. This one kept none of those things. It flew like something hunted or hunting, and it landed—or so he judged from the sound, a fading drone swallowed by the heat haze somewhere past Karang Laut—on the tidal strip the charts marked as unusable.

He sat back on his heels and watched the sky where it had been until his neck ached.

Then he took out his notebook, because that was what he did with everything now, and wrote: *Twin-engine, high-wing, patched cowling port side. Tail number partial—Z-something, last character possibly 7. Heading west-northwest along the reef line.*

And then, because the habit of forty years is not a habit but a spine, he added the date, the hour, the state of the tide, and the wind. An observation without provenance was gossip. An observation with provenance was evidence. His father had taught him that before Teodoro could read, back when the family archive still existed in a building instead of in a boy's memory, and his father had been dead thirty-one years, and Teodoro still could not write down a fact without dressing it properly for burial.

* * *

He lived in a hut at the top of the beach above the fishing village of Batu Tiga, three houses down from the salt shed, in a structure that had been a boat shed until the boat rotted and the owner died and nobody with a claim wanted it. The roof was corrugated iron over palm thatch, and it sang in the wind like a struck pan, and he had stopped hearing it years ago. Inside there was a cot, a kerosene lamp, a crate that served as desk and table both, and the crates behind it—eleven of them, each stenciled in faded paint with the crest of a shipping company that had ceased to exist before Teodoro was born—which held, as far as the village understood, papers. Old papers. Useless papers. Papers the man was welcome to, since they were worth nothing to anyone with sense.

The villagers were kind to him in the way coastal people are kind to harmless madmen: they left fish on his step when the catch was good, they sent children to fetch his water when the rains made the well path slick, and they called him *Tuan Arsip*—the Archive Gentleman—half in affection and half in the tone you'd use for a man who talked to ghosts. Which, in fairness, he did. Every night by lamplight he transcribed from memory another fragment of the collection that had burned, and every night the fragments came harder, and every night he wrote them anyway, because a memory unrecorded is a memory the next fever can take.

His family had kept records for four generations. Not government records—worse than that, better than that. Private records. The Reyes family had been keepers of accounts for the harbor lords going back to the days when the harbor lords were pirates with letterheads, and somewhere in the middle of the last century they had become keepers of something else entirely: the toll-books of the coast. Records of what was paid, to whom, for passage past certain waters, for anchorage in certain coves, for the right to fish certain reefs—and, folded into the dry columns of cargo and coin, references to a place that appeared in no chart Teodoro had ever seen, in no survey, in no pilot's guide. A cave. A cave in the reef that opened at certain tides and had been, according to entries his grandfather had copied out in a hand like copperplate embroidery, a countinghouse before there were banks. A place where debts were carved in stone because stone does not burn.

Stone does not burn. Paper burns. Teodoro's family had learned that in a single afternoon forty-one years ago, when men came to the archive house with kerosene and official-looking patience, and by evening four generations of the coast's memory was ash and the Reyes family was scattered like chaff—one brother dead, one brother fled to another island and never written again, his mother broken in a way that never mended, and Teodoro, nineteen years old, a junior clerk with ink-stained fingers, standing in the street watching the smoke go up and understanding, with the terrible clarity of the very young, that the fire had not been random.

You do not burn an archive to destroy paper. You burn an archive to destroy *accounts*. Someone had owed something, or claimed something, or feared something written down, and the fastest way to rewrite history is to burn the only honest copy of it.

It had taken him twenty years to learn who. Another ten to confirm it. The answer had a name everyone on the coast knew, a name printed on rice sacks and fuel drums and the sides of boats: the Marrow Syndicate. Founded in the famine year by a man who had arrived with nothing and left his heirs owning half the coastline, the Syndicate's origin story was taught to schoolchildren as enterprise—how one clever, hungry man had built an empire from a single salvaged boat. What the schoolchildren were not taught was that the founder's first fortune had been financed, if the fragments Teodoro remembered were true, by money borrowed against collateral he had never owned. That the founder had come, in the year of his arrival, to a family of record-keepers with a proposition. That some version of the toll-books—the ones describing the cave, the ones recording who owed what to the coast itself—had passed through his hands. And that afterward, the keepers of those books had burned.

Teodoro had no proof. Proof was the one commodity the sea had never given him. What he had was memory, and fragments, and a conviction worn smooth as sea glass from forty years of handling, and a life spent in huts at the edges of villages, asking careful questions of old men who mostly answered with shrugs, and digging in mudflats at dawn, and writing everything down with dates and hours and states of the tide.

He was sixty-one years old. He told himself, most nights, that this was fine—that archives are not built in a season, that patience is the archivist's whole armory. And then some mornings he woke with his heart hammering and the arithmetic of his remaining years laid out beside him like a debtor's schedule, and he understood that patience was also just a slower way of losing.

* * *

The tail number came back to him two days later, in the way these things always came—not summoned but surfaced, rising through the sediment of his mind like the ledger page had risen through the mudflat.

He had been re-transcribing, from memory, a manifest he had handled exactly once, in his last month at the colonial archive before they closed it and dispersed the staff and shredded what they called redundant holdings. A shipping manifest, 1930s vintage, freight forwarded through the old harbor, consigned to a trading concern whose name had later been absorbed into the Syndicate's early holdings. Routine. He had catalogued thousands like it. But this one he remembered because of an anomaly, the kind of small wrongness a clerk learns to feel in his teeth: the vessel listed was real, the cargo listed was plausible, but the air-freight supplement attached to it referenced an aircraft registry that did not match any operator the harbor office had licensed. A private registration. A *Z*-prefix registration, the old colonial civil series, long since superseded.

*Z-...7.* He had written the partial number in his notebook two days ago without knowing why his pulse picked up. Now he knew. The aircraft on that manifest—an aircraft carrying freight for the concern that became the Syndicate, eighty-some years ago—had carried a tail number ending in seven, beginning in Z, and the series had been retired, and registrations get reused the way names get reused, and the odds were a thousand to one against.

But Teodoro Reyes had spent forty years learning that the coast ran on odds a thousand to one against. The reef opening after generations. The ledgers surviving in stone. His own survival, some weeks, felt like a clerical error in death's favor.

He began to watch the sky.

This was not difficult work, as surveillance goes; the difficulty was doing it while appearing to do nothing, which was, after all, his lifelong specialty. He arranged his mornings on the flats so that his face was toward the eastern approach. He learned the local traffic the way he had once learned filing systems: the mail plane Tuesdays and Fridays, high and indifferent; the government health flight monthly, white and purposeful; the timber spotter that wandered the coast like a drunk. Everything else that crossed the strait low and unscheduled went into the notebook, dated, timed, described. Three weeks produced eleven entries. None matched.

On the twenty-fourth day, the plane came back.

It came out of the southeast this time, riding ahead of a squall line the way gulls ride ahead of rain, and it passed directly over the flats at perhaps six hundred feet, close enough that Teodoro could see the patch on the port cowling, the sun-bleached fabric of the wing walkway, and—when it banked slightly, correcting for the wind off the reef—the tail, clean and legible against the gray cloud wall.

*Z-217.*

His hands were shaking. He noted this with detached professional interest, the way he might note a discrepancy in a column, and then he made himself sit down on an overturned hull and wait for the shaking to pass, because a man does not write evidence with trembling hands. When his fingers were steady he wrote the number, the time, the heading, the weather. Then he sat a while longer, looking at what he had written, feeling the strange vertigo of a hypothesis becoming a fact.

Because here was the thing he had not let himself think, all these weeks, all these years: registrations in that retired colonial series were not randomly reassigned. They were *reissued*—sold, transferred, inherited through the paperwork of defunct companies, passing from balance sheet to balance sheet like souls through purgatory. An aircraft wearing Z-217 today was either a coincidence beyond reason, or it was the same registration, resurrected through some chain of sale and salvage that a good archivist could trace—and the original Z-217 had flown freight for the ancestors of the Marrow Syndicate before the fire, before the famine deals, before everything.

Which meant the woman flying it—if it was a woman; he had seen a silhouette at the controls, slight, quick-moving—was flying a plane with a pedigree. She might not know it. Most likely she didn't. Planes change hands the way guns do, and histories stick to them the way rust does, invisible until you scrape. But she was flying, week after week, low along the exact reef line where the charts showed nothing and the old toll-books showed a cave, landing on strips no licensed operator used, carrying—who knew what—for whoever paid.

She was either the Syndicate's courier, in which case she was dangerous and useless to him.

Or she was something else. Something unattached, indebted, deniable, desperate—the kind of pilot who flies low and lands twice nowhere, the kind who carries what she can't afford to ask about. And a person like that, Teodoro thought, standing slowly in the ankle-deep water with the tide sucking at his legs, a person like that has one quality no Syndicate man will ever have, one quality the coast has not seen in forty years:

She can be reached. Not bought—reached. There is a difference. Bought men belong to whoever holds their price. Reached men belong, briefly and dangerously, to an idea.

He looked east along the reef line, where the plane had vanished into the haze past Karang Laut, and he became aware that his heart was doing something it had not done in decades. Not hope exactly. Hope was a word for younger men with more to lose. It was closer to *plan*. The cold, bright, waking-up feeling of a plan assembling itself out of parts he had carried for forty years: her access, his records. Her wings, his memory. The cave that stone had kept when men could not.

The tide was turning. He could feel it first in the water around his calves, the subtle reversal, the slack, the pull. Forty years he had waited for a turning like this one, and he had learned from the sea that turnings announce themselves quietly, that the moment of slack looks exactly like the moment of stagnation, and that only a fool—or a man with a notebook—fails to mark the hour.

He waded ashore, dried his feet on the rag he kept for the purpose, climbed to his hut, and pulled from the eleventh crate—the bottom one, the one wrapped in oilcloth inside a rice sack inside a bait box—a folder he had shown to no living soul. Inside were his transcriptions. Fragments of the toll-books. References to the cave. And one page, copied out in his own careful hand from a memory he trusted above all his other memories, because he had held the original in his nineteen-year-old fingers on the last morning it existed:

A manifest. 1930s. Air-freight supplement. Registry *Z-217*, consigned freight, forwarding concern ancestral to the Marrow Syndicate.

He set it on the crate that served as his desk, weighted its corners with shells, and looked at it for a long time by the light coming through the gap in the iron roof.

Then he took out a fresh sheet and began, in the copperplate hand his father had drilled into him, to draft a letter he did not yet know how to send—to a pilot whose name he did not know, at a strip that existed only at low tide, in a village that paid its debts in fish and warnings.

Somewhere out past the reef, the tide was going out, and going out, and going out. And somewhere under all that retreating water, patient as stone, the coast was keeping its oldest account open—waiting, as it had waited for four hundred years, for someone to come and settle it.

Teodoro dipped his pen.
