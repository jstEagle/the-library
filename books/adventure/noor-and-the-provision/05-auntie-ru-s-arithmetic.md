## 5. Auntie Ru's Arithmetic

The floating restaurant sat at the mouth of the Selat Merah like a patient animal, its pilings grown over with barnacles and its tin roof patched in three colors, none of them matching. Jonah had seen it from the air a hundred times—a smudge of corrugated light on the brown water where the river gave up and the strait began—and she had always assumed she would die somewhere else.

She came in by water taxi because Auntie Ru had specified it. *Come by boat. Come alone. Come before the rain.* Three instructions, each one a small demonstration that Ru knew exactly how Jonah traveled, exactly who she trusted, and exactly what the sky was doing this week. The monsoon's edge was visible from the taxi already, a gray wall standing on the southern horizon like something built rather than weathered, and the water beneath the hull had gone the color of old pewter, choppy with a wind that hadn't yet decided to be serious.

Jonah counted her exits out of habit. The restaurant had two: the front steps down to the mooring platform, and a gap in the railing at the rear where the kitchen staff evidently hauled their fish aboard. She noted the gap, priced it—fifteen feet of open water, a swim through current toward mangroves, a wet run along a mudflat with no cover—and then dismissed it, because running from Auntie Ru was not an escape. It was an invoice. Everything was an invoice with Ru. That was the whole architecture of the woman.

The maitre d' was a boy of perhaps nineteen with a shaved head and the flat, unblinking calm of someone raised inside an organization that did not raise children so much as acquire them. He led her across a dining room full of empty tables set for a hundred guests who would never come, past a tank of groupers turning in slow circles like thoughts that wouldn't resolve, and out onto the rear veranda, where one table stood apart under a canvas awning and a woman in a pale green cheongsam sat pouring tea.

Auntie Ru did not look up when Jonah arrived. That was the first lesson of every meeting: you existed when she permitted it. The tea went into two cups, lid lifted, steam rising, lid replaced. Only then did Ru extend one hand toward the opposite chair, a gesture so economical it might have been an accident, and said, without any particular warmth or coldness:

"Xiao Zhou. Sit. You look thin."

"I eat," Jonah said.

"You fly." Ru poured nothing for herself yet; her own cup waited, untouched, while she studied Jonah with the mild attention of a woman appraising a horse at auction. "Flying is not eating. Flying is spending. Sit down before the wind takes the table."

Jonah sat. The chair was teak, worn smooth, and the veranda floor shifted gently beneath it with the swell, so that even sitting still felt like being underway. She hated that. She hated that Ru had chosen a room that moved, that never held still long enough to be mapped, that kept its occupants subtly off-balance without ever once rocking hard enough to complain about. It was, she thought, the most honest room the woman could have picked.

The tea was Tieguanyin, the good kind, the kind that cost more per ounce than Jonah's monthly fuel bill, and it arrived already at temperature, which meant it had been timed. Which meant Ru had known, to within minutes, when Jonah would walk through the door. Which meant Ru had known before Jonah herself had decided.

"Drink," Ru said. "You'll want your hands busy."

"I'd rather talk numbers."

"We will talk numbers." Ru finally lifted her own cup, inhaled the steam with her eyes closed like a woman smelling rain coming off rice fields, and drank. When she set it down, the porcelain clicked against saucer with a precision that made Jonah's back teeth ache. "But first I want to tell you about my grandmother."

This was new. In eleven years of knowing Auntie Ru—first as a name whispered by other pilots, then as a voice on shortwave that always knew too much, then as the fixed point around which Jonah's debts orbited like moons—Ru had never once offered biography. Biography was collateral. Biography was something you surrendered, not something you spent.

"She sold salt," Ru said. "On this river, before the war, before the second war, before any of the wars anyone bothers counting. Salt and kerosene, upriver to the Dayak longhouses, downriver with rubber and bird nests. Small trade. Honest trade, mostly." A pause, delicately weighted. "Mostly."

The wind found the awning and worried it, a soft snapping like distant small-arms fire. Out past the mooring platform, a fishing prau ran for shelter with its sail half-furled, and Jonah tracked it automatically, the way she tracked everything—by heading, by speed, by whether it might become a problem.

"My grandmother kept her accounts in a notebook," Ru continued. "Every debt written down. Every favor owed, every favor given. And do you know what she taught me, Xiao Zhou? Not arithmetic. Anyone can learn arithmetic. She taught me that a ledger is a promise you make to the future. You write a number down, and you are telling tomorrow: *this is true, and I will make it true.*" Her eyes opened, and they were dark and unhurried and absolutely without mercy. "The Marrow Syndicate is a very large notebook. And you are in it."

"I know what I owe."

"Do you?" Ru tilted her head, and the lamp above the table caught the fine silver at her temples, hair she wore pulled back so tightly it seemed less styled than disciplined. "Recite it for me. Principal, interest, weeks outstanding. Take your time. I enjoy watching people count."

Jonah recited it. She had no choice, really—not if she wanted to keep the fiction that she and Ru were having a conversation between equals, which was itself a fiction she maintained the way other people maintained religion, less from belief than from terror of what its absence would require. Principal: four thousand seven hundred Straits dollars, borrowed in a bad year against a good engine. Interest: compounding weekly at a rate that was not written anywhere but was understood by everyone. Weeks outstanding: nine.

"Nine weeks," Ru repeated softly. "And in those nine weeks, how many times have you landed twice in the same place?"

"Once."

"Lie."

Jonah said nothing. The grouper tank gurgled behind them. Somewhere below the floorboards, water slapped against hull in a rhythm like a slow heartbeat.

"Twice," Ru said. "Sungai Papan, and again at the mission strip outside Tanjung Api. Both times for medicine deliveries, both times logged under the same registration number, which is not yours, and both times witnessed." She refilled Jonah's cup without asking, the gesture almost maternal, and that was the worst part—the way cruelty and care shared the same hands here, indistinguishable, interchangeable. "I am not angry, Xiao Zhou. Anger is for people who are surprised. I am disappointed, which is more expensive. Sloppiness compounds faster than money does. You know this."

"The medicine was real," Jonah said. "The people who got it were real."

"Yes. You are a good person who does bad bookkeeping. The world is full of you. The world runs on you." Ru folded her hands on the table, and Jonah saw for the first time the ring she wore—a heavy band of dark gold, old workmanship, some crest worn nearly smooth. "So let us discuss your account. As of this morning, your balance stands at fourteen thousand one hundred dollars."

The number went through Jonah like turbulence. Fourteen thousand. Tripled, and then some, and dressed up as arithmetic. "That's not—"

"It is exactly what it is. Nine weeks of compounding, plus penalties for the duplicate landings, plus a service charge for the telegram I had to send, plus—" Ru smiled, briefly, a smile that involved only her mouth and none of the rest of her face, "—plus interest on the penalties, because the penalties were themselves extended credit. You may verify the figures with my clerk if you doubt me. He is very thorough. He has been thorough with better pilots than you."

*Better pilots than you.* The sentence hung there, and Jonah understood it completely without needing it explained: there had been others, and they were gone, and their accounts had been closed in ways that did not appear in any ledger.

"And how do I clear it?" she asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later, in the way you are proud of small things done inside large disasters.

"Now," Ru said, "we arrive at the interesting part."

She reached into the sleeve of her cheongsam—Jonah had forgotten people actually did that, kept things in sleeves, kept things close—and produced a folded square of paper, which she opened on the tablecloth and turned to face Jonah with two fingers.

It was a chart. Hand-drawn, recent, the ink still sharp. The coastline from Karang Laut to the river mouths, rendered in confident strokes, and there, off the reef line, a single mark: a small circle, and beside it, in Ru's own handwriting, characters Jonah couldn't read and beneath them, in English, one word: *OPENING.*

"You've heard the fishermen," Ru said. It was not a question. "The reef showed a cave at the last spring tide. No chart records it. No government survey records it. My grandmother's notebook mentions it—once, in a marginal hand, in a code we never fully broke. Something about the coast keeping accounts older than any of ours." She tapped the circle once, lightly, the way you'd tap a sleeping animal. "The monsoon seals it within the month. Perhaps within the fortnight. After that, the sea closes its mouth and keeps whatever it keeps until the next freak tide, which may be ten years, may be fifty, may be never in my lifetime."

"So you want me to—"

"Survey it. Fly the coast, land at Karang Laut, hire a boat, go in at slack water, and report to me what is inside. Chambers, passages, anything man-made, anything written, anything valuable. You are the only pilot I have whose manifest gives her reason to be on that coast without my fingerprints anywhere near her." Ru leaned back, and the canvas awning snapped again overhead, and her voice remained gentle as bathwater. "Complete the job, and the entire balance—principal, penalties, interest on the penalties—is struck. Zeroed. Your account closed. You walk out of my notebook, Xiao Zhou, and you never have to see my face or hear my voice again."

The offer was everything Jonah had wanted for five years. That was how she knew it was a trap. Traps were always baited with the thing you actually wanted; that was what separated a trap from merely a bad deal.

"And if I touch something?"

Ru's expression didn't change, but the quality of her stillness changed, the way the sea changes before it changes visibly. "Touch nothing. Photograph nothing for yourself. Remove nothing. Report everything. Those are the terms." She paused. "You are wondering why such simple terms need stating so firmly. I will tell you why. Because the last person I sent into a place with terms like these touched something. And now that place is a hole in the ground, and he is a hole in the ground, and his family received his final wages with my condolences." She lifted her cup again. "I am telling you this as courtesy. Courtesy is expensive. I hope you appreciate it."

The rain arrived—not the monsoon proper, just its advance scouts, fat drops hitting the tin roof in scattered reports like a drummer tuning up. The groupers circled. The tea cooled by degrees in its cup, and Jonah watched the surface tremble with the swell and thought about the word *opening*, and about Ibu Salmah's fish-and-warnings payment, and about the fact that Ru had known about the cave before the telegram, before the meeting, possibly before the reef itself had opened—which meant someone had told her, which meant the coast had a leak, which meant everyone Jonah had spoken to in the last week was already compromised or already watched.

"One question," Jonah said.

"You have many questions. Ask the one that matters."

"Why me? You have boats. You have divers. You have men who'd go into that cave for a fraction of what I owe."

Ru looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but a kind of recognition, the look of one professional acknowledging another's competence even while holding the knife.

"Because boats can be followed," she said, "and divers can be bought, and men can be tortured, and all of those things leave witnesses. But a pilot who owes me fourteen thousand one hundred dollars, who wants her freedom more than she wants air—that pilot has the best incentive structure ever devised. You will be careful, because your life depends on careful. You will be honest, because your freedom depends on honest. And you will be discreet, because discretion is the only currency you have left to spend." She rose, unhurried, smoothing her cheongsam, and the meeting was clearly over though the rain had barely begun. "Finish your tea. It was expensive, and unlike you, it is paid for."

She stopped at the edge of the veranda, one hand on the wet railing, and turned back with the expression of a woman remembering a small kindness.

"Oh—one more thing. A courtesy, since we are being honest with each other tonight." The rain thickened behind her, veiling the far shore. "There is a man on the coast asking questions about aircraft registrations. An archivist, or something like it. Disgraced, threadbare, obsessive. If he approaches you—and he will, he has approached two of my people already—you will report the conversation to me. In full. Within a day." Her smile returned, brief and bloodless. "Because a ledger, Xiao Zhou, is a promise you make to the future. And I keep very, very careful books."

She went inside. The boy with the shaved head materialized to escort Jonah out, and the water taxi was waiting as if it had never doubted she would need it, and the rain came down harder over the strait, erasing the horizon, erasing the far shore, erasing everything except the small circle on the chart that Jonah could still see perfectly behind her closed eyes.

*Opening,* the word said.

She rode back through the gray water with the chart folded against her ribs like a second heartbeat, doing the arithmetic she could not stop doing: fourteen thousand dollars against one cave against one fortnight against one disgraced archivist asking questions about tail numbers—and against the small, quiet, unforgivable fact that she had already seen the black mouth in the reef herself, days ago, from eight hundred feet, and had told no one.

Which meant, by Auntie Ru's own definition, her account was already overdrawn.

* * *

The hangar smelled of avgas and rust, and the rain on the roof was loud enough to make thinking feel private, which was probably why Jonah did her best lying to herself there. She pinned Ru's chart to the corkboard above the workbench, next to the tide tables and the photograph of a coastline she'd taken from eight hundred feet and never developed properly, and she stood looking at the little inked circle until the bulb overhead buzzed and flickered.

One job. Touch nothing. Report everything. Freedom, at the end of it—actual freedom, the kind she'd stopped letting herself imagine sometime around the third year, when she'd realized that every escape route she'd ever built had turned out, on inspection, to be a corridor with a door at the far end that opened onto another corridor.

She should feel trapped. Instead, checking her fuel figures by lamplight, planning a flight plan to Karang Laut that would look routine on any manifest, she felt something worse than trapped.

She felt scheduled.
