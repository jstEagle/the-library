## 7. The Man Who Knew the Tail Number

The fuel dock at Tanjung Pasir smelled of diesel and drying fish, and Jonah had chosen it for exactly that reason: nobody lingered where the smell was bad and the shade was worse. She'd been pumping her own avgas since dawn, drum by drum, because the dock boy who usually did it had a cousin in the Syndicate's coastal office, and she'd stopped letting cousins of anyone touch her plane two years ago.

The morning was already thick. The monsoon hadn't arrived yet, but you could feel it assembling itself out past the strait—a bruised weight on the horizon, a pressure behind the ears like a held breath. The fishermen were pulling their boats higher up the sand than usual, stacking them hull to hull, and every one of them moved with the particular hurry of people who knew something the sky hadn't said out loud yet.

Jonah wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last season and checked the tie-downs on the Cub. *Pipistrelle*, the old girl was called, though the registration painted on her tail belonged to a man who had died in Surabaya in 1949, according to the paperwork Jonah had bought from a clerk who owed Auntie Ru a different kind of debt. The paint was fresh enough to pass a squinting inspection and wrong enough to survive one.

She was halfway through the third drum when she heard footsteps on the planking behind her—unhurried, deliberate, the walk of someone who wanted to be heard coming.

She didn't turn around. "Fuel's self-serve today," she said. "And I'm not carrying passengers."

"I'm not asking for a ride." The voice was dry, educated, with an accent that had been polished somewhere expensive and then left out in the weather for years until it rusted. "I'm asking for five minutes, Ms. Zhou. Or should I say—" a pause, and she could hear him consulting some interior ledger, "—or should I not? That's rather the point, isn't it."

Jonah set down the pump handle slowly. There were rules about this. Rule three: never let a stranger finish a sentence that starts with your name. But rule three assumed strangers, and there was something in the man's voice—not confidence exactly, but certainty—that made her suspect he'd already finished all his sentences somewhere else, days ago, and was only now delivering them.

She turned.

He was tall and stooped at once, the way men get when they've spent decades bent over desks and then lost the desks. Fifties, maybe older, hard to tell under a sunburn that looked less like health than like neglect. Wire-rimmed glasses mended at one hinge with copper wire. He wore a linen shirt that had once been white and now held the color of old paper, and he carried a leather satchel against his hip the way other men carried weapons—close, protective, ready.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Jonah said.

"Yes." He seemed pleased by this, in a scholarly sort of way. "That's intentional. My name is Teodoro Reyes."

The name meant nothing. The satchel, she noticed, meant something—the way he shifted it when a gust came off the water, keeping it out of the spray. People guarded what they couldn't replace. She knew the type. She was the type.

"Mr. Reyes. You have four minutes."

"Three and a half, then." He didn't smile, but something around his eyes suggested he appreciated the arithmetic. He opened the satchel with the care of a man handling a reliquary and drew out a folder—oilcloth-wrapped, tied with string, the string knotted in a way that looked ceremonial rather than practical. "I'm going to show you a document. I want you to understand what it costs me to show it to you, so that you understand I'm not here to threaten you or to sell you anything. I'm here because we appear to be reading the same book."

"I don't read books. I read manifests and weather."

"Then this will be familiar ground." He untied the string, unwrapped the oilcloth, and held out a single sheet.

Jonah took it the way she took everything from strangers: by the edge, ready to drop it.

It was a manifest. A shipping manifest, typed on paper gone the color of tea, with a letterhead she half recognized—the old colonial coastal survey office, the one that had burned its files in '46 and blamed the Japanese. Cargo listed: medical stores, general provisions, one aircraft crated for delivery. And beside the aircraft entry, a registry number.

Jonah's breath did something she didn't authorize. It stopped.

Not her fake number. Not the dead man's number from Surabaya. The number underneath—the original one, the one painted on the airframe when it came out of the factory, the one she'd found stamped into the engine mount when she rebuilt the plane in a borrowed hangar in Kuching and decided, in the way of practical women, to paint over history rather than argue with it.

"That's not possible," she said. "Those records burned."

"Some burned." Teodoro's voice went quiet, almost gentle, which was somehow worse than if he'd gloated. "My family kept copies. My family kept copies of everything. That was the problem, as it turned out."

Below the registry number, stamped in faded violet ink, was a mark: an open book over crossed keys, and beneath it, letters worn nearly smooth—*Archivo Reyes*.

Jonah looked up. "Your family ran an archive."

"My family *was* an archive. Three generations of us, clerks and keepers, cataloguing everything the coast didn't want remembered. Tolls. Treaties. Land grants. Debts." He said the last word lightly, but it landed heavy, and Jonah felt it land in a place she kept locked. "In 1937, my grandfather certified this manifest. In 1951, someone burned our archive to its foundations, and my father spent the rest of his life trying to reconstruct what he could from memory and smuggled fragments. I've spent mine doing the same. Less successfully. More obsessively."

"And what does any of this have to do with me?"

"Nothing. Everything." He took off his glasses, cleaned them on his shirttail with the fussy precision of a man whose rituals were load-bearing, and put them back on. "Three weeks ago, a fishing boat out of Karang Laut saw a plane flying the reef line at low tide, low enough to count the barnacles. They described it. Yellow wings, dented cowling, a scar on the port side where something hit it—ice, a bird, a fence post, no one agrees. And a tail number that doesn't exist." He tapped the manifest. "Except it does exist. It existed first. Your plane has been on this coast longer than you have, Ms. Zhou, and it has a history, and histories are the one cargo I know how to track."

Jonah handed the sheet back with fingers that were almost steady. Almost. Inside, something was rearranging itself—the cold, professional alarm of a woman realizing her cover story had a hole in it she'd never known about, because she'd never known the plane had a true name.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"To hire you. Except I can't pay you, which is why I'm showing you documents instead." He wrapped the manifest back in its oilcloth with those same careful hands. "I want access to the cave at Karang Laut. The one the reef opened. The one your Syndicate friends are already staking out—I've seen their survey flags; they use a distinctive red, very considerate of them. And before you tell me you don't know what I'm talking about, consider that you flew the reef line at low tide, which is what a surveyor does, and that you refueled here instead of Karang Laut, which is what a woman does when she doesn't want to be seen asking questions at the scene."

* * *

The tide was going out. Jonah could see it in the way the fishing boats settled onto their bellies in the mud, listing gently, abandoned-looking, though nothing on this coast was ever really abandoned—it just waited for the water to come back. She thought about that more than she wanted to.

"Suppose," she said carefully, "that I knew nothing about any cave. What would you do?"

"Find another pilot. Badly. Probably die." He said it without self-pity, which made it worse. "Ms. Zhou, I'm going to be honest with you, because I suspect honesty is a currency you rarely see and might find novel. I have spent eleven years chasing fragments of my family's records across three countries and two revolutions. What's left of them describes a cave on this coast—a cave that appears in shipping ledgers from the 1880s as a toll station, a place where cargo was weighed and duties recorded and treaties signed. Not a temple. Not a treasure vault. An *office*. The oldest office on this coast, run by people who understood that whoever keeps the accounts owns everyone in them."

"And the Syndicate wants it because—"

"Because their founder's name is in it." He said it flatly, watching her face. "I don't know how, or in what capacity. But a man named Marrow—who built an empire on land titles and shipping concessions and the gentle application of fire—does not send survey teams to a cave full of old toll receipts unless one of those receipts has his family name written in it in a hand he doesn't control."

The wind shifted, bringing the diesel smell back around, and Jonah used the moment to look away, out at the horizon where the monsoon was still deciding. She ran the numbers the way she always ran them, fast and cold: a stranger who knew her plane's true registration was a stranger who could sell her to Auntie Ru for the price of a decent dinner. A stranger who knew the Syndicate's founder was nervous was either a fool or the most dangerous man on the coast. And a stranger who stood in front of her offering partnership instead of blackmail was either the best thing that had happened to her in five years or the most elaborate trap she'd ever been invited to walk into.

"Why show me the manifest?" she asked. "You could have sold it. To Ru. To Marrow's people. A pilot's real registration, plus proof she's sniffing around their cave—that's worth money to somebody."

"It is." He agreed easily, terribly. "But selling it buys me a year, maybe two, and then I'm back where I started, older, with fewer documents. Whereas—" he patted the satchel, "—whereas if the cave holds what I believe it holds, then eleven years ends, my father's life means something, and a great many forged land titles on this coast become what they always were: paper. Paper burns, Ms. Zhou. Records in stone are harder to argue with."

"You sound very sure it's stone."

"I'm sure of nothing. That's why I need you." For the first time, something cracked in his composure—not weakness, exactly, but the glimpse of the machinery underneath, turning and turning. "I need someone who can fly me to a reef that's underwater twice a day, who knows how to move quietly along a coast owned by bad men, and who—if I'm honest, and I promised myself I'd be honest—is probably better at staying alive than I am. In exchange: whatever the cave holds, you see it first. Before the Syndicate. Before the magistrate, if it comes to that. Before anyone. You'd know everything I know, and I'd know everything you're willing to tell me, which I suspect is considerably less, and I've made my peace with that."

Jonah laughed. It surprised her coming out—short, rusty, genuine. "You've made your peace with partnering with a liar."

"I've made my peace with partnering with a survivor. The lying seems to come with the model." He almost smiled. "I lie too, for what it's worth. Mostly to myself, about how close I am to finishing. Eleven years of 'nearly.' It's a kind of debt, isn't it? Compounding."

The word went through her like a splinter finding its depth. She thought of Auntie Ru's floating restaurant, of tea poured in perfect silence while numbers tripled themselves politely on a napkin. She thought of the telegram folded in her breast pocket, and the meeting she was already late preparing for.

"Here's my problem, Mr. Reyes," she said. "Everything you've told me checks out, which frightens me more than lies would. Lies I know how to price. But I don't know you. And the last person who claimed to want to show me the truth cost me four hundred dollars and a week in a jail cell in Pontianak."

"Then don't trust me." He shrugged, and the gesture had forty years of practice in it. "Verify me. Ask anyone on this coast about the Reyes archive. Ask the old priests, the headmen, the ones who remember when records mattered. They'll tell you two things: that the Reyes family kept the truest books in the strait, and that they died for it. Both facts will be accurate. I offer you the same package."

Out on the flats, a fisherman began dragging his boat further up the sand, alone, cursing at it, and the ordinary stubbornness of it steadied Jonah somehow. People kept doing the next necessary thing. That was the whole coast, really—everyone bailing, everyone hauling, everyone waiting for the tide to make up its mind.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "you follow my rules. We never land twice in the same place. You carry nothing I can't deny. And if I tell you to run, you run, and you don't ask what I know that you don't."

"Agreed."

"And if this goes wrong—if you're working for someone, if this is a long game—if I even *suspect*—"

"Then you'll leave me wherever we are, and I'll deserve it." He held out his hand. "Do we have an alliance, Ms. Zhou? Her access, my records, and the truth about what's in that cave—split between two people who've both survived by knowing things?"

She looked at his hand. Dry, ink-stained at the cuticles, steady in a way hers never quite managed. She thought: *this is either the smartest thing I've ever done or the last.*

She shook it.

"One condition of mine," Teodoro added, retrieving his hand and settling the satchel across his body. "When this is over—whatever's over—you let me write down your plane's true history. The whole of it. Every owner, every repaint, every name it's carried. Someone should keep the account."

"The plane's history is the least damning thing about me."

"All the more reason someone should record it accurately." He glanced at the sky, at the bruise gathering on the horizon, and his voice dropped. "One more thing, and then I'll leave you to your fuel. The red-flagged men at the reef—they're not surveyors. I watched them yesterday through a fisherman's glass. They were measuring the cave mouth. Width, height, clearance at high water." He met her eyes. "People measure a doorway like that when they're planning to bring something large through it. Or keep something from ever coming out."

He turned and walked away down the planking, unhurried, the oilcloth folder safe against his hip, and Jonah stood alone with her drums of fuel and the telegram in her pocket and the sudden, certain knowledge that the monsoon wasn't the only storm on its way to this coast.

She looked at the Cub—at the scarred cowling, the painted-over name, the number that wasn't hers and never had been—and for the first time in five years, she wondered what else she'd been flying without knowing.

The barometer on the dock wall had begun, very quietly, to fall.
