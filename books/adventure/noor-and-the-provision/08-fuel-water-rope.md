## 8. Fuel, Water, Rope

The hangar at Tanjung Sepat smelled of diesel and old canvas and, faintly, of the sea itself—salt worked so deep into the timber that no amount of sweeping would ever get it out. Jonah had rented the place three years ago from a man who asked no questions, which was the most expensive thing about it. Questions were cheap everywhere else on the coast.

She worked by lamplight with the doors half open, letting the pre-monsoon wind move through in long, damp gusts that made the hanging tarpaulins breathe like sleeping animals. The plane sat under the one good bulb: a 1930s bush transport she'd bought wrecked and rebuilt twice, her registration numbers painted over so many times the metal beneath had gone thin as paper. *Permiten*, the locals called her kind of aircraft—the patient ones. She talked to it sometimes, low, the way other pilots talked to saints.

Fuel first. That was the discipline: fuel before fear, water before want, rope before regret. Her father—or the man who'd claimed to be her father, back when she'd still believed in claims—had taught her that much before he taught her anything else. You provisioned for the trip you planned and then you provisioned again for the trip that actually happened, and the difference between those two lists was where most pilots died.

So she counted drums. Four for the survey run to Karang Laut and back with margin. Two more than margin required, because Auntie Ru's arithmetic had a way of becoming everyone's arithmetic, and if the job went sideways she wanted enough fuel to be somewhere Ru's people weren't. She siphoned, measured, logged everything in her own hand in a ledger she kept inside a waterproof oilskin bag—a ledger of her own, though hers recorded nothing but numbers, and numbers, unlike words, could not testify against you.

Water next. Eight tins, filled from the rain barrel, boiled because the coastal wells went brackish this time of year. Rice in oilcloth. Salt fish wrapped in banana leaf, the smell of it already arguing with the diesel. A medical kit she built herself—sutures, sulfa powder, quinine, a bone saw she'd never used and prayed never to use—and beneath the medical kit, flat as a sin, a pistol with two spare magazines. She didn't count the pistol in the manifest. Some things you carried the way you carried scars: officially unlisted, always present.

Then the charts.

This was the part she did slowly, and she did it slowly on purpose, because haste left fingerprints. On the workbench she spread the coastal survey sheets—the colonial ones, forty years out of date but drawn by men who'd cared about coastlines the way accountants care about margins—and beside them her own working charts, annotated in pencil over five years of flying: every sandbar that moved, every strip that flooded, every reef that showed its teeth only at certain tides. Karang Laut was marked there in her own hand, the tidal airstrip, the village, the channel through the mangroves where a plane could hide if a plane had to.

She fed blank sheets into the copying frame she'd rigged from glass, lamp-black, and patience. Press, trace, lift. Each chart took twenty minutes and cost her something she couldn't name—every line she duplicated was a line she might someday have to burn, or flee, or hand to someone with better intentions and worse outcomes. But she traced them all. The approach to the cave mouth. The tide tables for the reef passage. The sketch Ibu Salmah had drawn for her on the back of a fish-wrapping sheet, the black mouth of the cave rendered in charcoal with a circle around it and three words in the coastal script: *the sea remembers*.

By the second hour her wrist ached and the copies lay drying in a stack, face down, like cards being dealt against a future she couldn't see yet. She'd tell herself later that she'd copied them for safety—for redundancy, for professionalism—but the truth was simpler and she knew it even then, standing there with lamp-black on her fingers: she was building an escape route out of information, and escape routes were the only inheritance she trusted.

The radio crackled at dusk.

She let it crackle. Three breaths, four—long enough that answering wouldn't look eager. Then she keyed the set and put the straight voice on, the one she kept folded flat inside her chest like a chart she never flew with unless someone official was listening.

"Zhou."

"Miss Zhou." Auntie Ru's voice came through the static the way silk comes through a keyhole—smooth, and wrong-sized for the opening. "You are well?"

"Well enough."

"And the preparations? Fuel, water—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid on porcelain, "—rope?"

Jonah looked down at her own hands, at the coiled manila rope she'd been splicing an hour ago, and felt the laugh she didn't let out sit somewhere behind her sternum like swallowed glass. Of course Ru knew the litany. Ru knew everything that could be known about provisioning, because Ru had spent thirty years provisioning other people's ruin.

"On schedule," Jonah said. "Weather window looks like three days, maybe four, before the front closes the coast. I'll fly the survey at slack tide, photograph what's visible from the air and the shallows, and report."

"You will go inside." Not a question. Ru's voice never asked questions; it issued findings. "My client is specific on this point. What is inside must be seen by eyes he trusts. Yours qualify. Barely."

"I'll go as far as the water allows."

"The water allows everything, Miss Zhou, for those who ask correctly." A pause, and in the pause Jonah could hear the floating restaurant behind that voice—the clink of glasses, the lap of harbor water, the small orchestra of a life built on other people's debts. "You sound tired. Are you eating?"

"I'm eating."

"Good. A pilot who faints over open water inconveniences everyone." Another pause, longer, and when Ru spoke again the silk had thinned enough to show the wire beneath. "One more matter. There is a man on the coast. Reyes. An archivist, or something pretending to be one. He has been asking questions about the reef. About the cave. If he approaches you—if he has already approached you—you will report it to me. Immediately. This is not a request dressed as courtesy; this is courtesy dressed as a condition."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a night bird called across the water, three notes falling like stones.

"I don't know any Reyes," Jonah said, and heard how easily it came, and hated how easily it came.

"Mm," said Auntie Ru, in the tone of a woman writing something down. "Then we understand each other. Fly carefully, Miss Zhou. The monsoon is coming, and monsoons reveal what fair weather conceals. So do I."

The set clicked dead. Jonah sat in the dark of the hangar for a long moment, hands still resting on the radio's cold housing, doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the fuel dock. He had approached her. He was probably approaching her now. And she had lied to Auntie Ru—not the practiced, weightless lies of false registrations and unlabeled crates, but a real lie, a lie with mass and consequence, about a living man with a living plan.

She tested the feeling the way she'd test new rigging: pulled it once, gently, waiting for it to give. It held. That frightened her more than anything Ru had said.

* * *

Teodoro Reyes arrived the next morning without knocking, which was possible only because the hangar's side door had never once been locked—Jonah had decided years ago that locks told visitors you had something worth taking, and she preferred to keep her valuables illegible rather than defended.

He stood in the doorway holding a wooden document case against his chest the way a man holds a child in a crowd, and he was already talking.

"—the third chamber, the causeway runs north-northeast, and the script changes halfway along the west wall. Pre-colonial toll records on the seaward side; treaty-era entries after that, mixed scripts, some of it phonetic transcription of an oral ledger tradition. I can read perhaps sixty percent. Seventy on a good day. Yesterday was not a good day." He stopped, registered her expression, and had the grace to look briefly embarrassed. "I've been standing here for some time. You were tracing charts."

"You were watching me trace charts."

"I was confirming you were tracing charts." He set the case down on her workbench with the reverence other men reserved for altars. "There's a difference between surveillance and scholarship, Miss Zhou, though I grant the distinction narrows under monsoon conditions."

"My radio conversation last night—"

"Was audible from the ridge above the fuel dock, if one knows which frequency to listen for, and I know which frequencies everyone listens for. Old archive habit. You file things; I listen to them being filed." He opened the case. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay papers—old ones, foxed and soft-edged, their ink gone the brown of dried blood. "Auntie Ru believes you don't know me. I believe Auntie Ru believes many convenient things. The question is what *you* believe, and I've come to complicate it."

"Get out of my hangar, Teodoro."

"No." He said it mildly, almost apologetically, the way a man declines a second helping. "Because here is what will happen if I leave. You'll fly the survey alone. You'll enter the cave alone, or nearly alone. And you will stand in front of walls covered in a script you cannot read, holding a camera, photographing stone you cannot interpret, while the meaning of every mark walks past you like a stranger speaking your mother's tongue. Then you'll report to Auntie Ru exactly what your instructions require—carvings yes, contents no, touch nothing—and the Syndicate will send its own readers, and those readers will read selectively, and whatever the ledgers say about the Syndicate's founder will quietly fail to survive the translation." He tapped the oilcloth bundle once, softly. "I am the translation, Miss Zhou. I'm the only one left."

She turned away from him, back to the copying frame, and began cleaning lamp-black off the glass with a rag that had seen better decades. It was easier to argue with her own hands than with his voice.

"I don't need a translator. I need altitude and a tide table."

"You need both, and you have neither without me, because the tide table for the inner chambers isn't in any colonial survey—it's in the oral record my family kept, and the oral record says the cave floods from below on the ebb, not the flood, which contradicts every chart ever printed, and if you trust the printed charts you will drown in a cathedral with your boots full of the past." He said it fast, without drama, and that was the worst part—the absolute administrative calm of it, a man reading out a defect notice on her survival. "Also, the chest. You saw the scouts' stakes. They're coming back for the chest with proper equipment, and they will take everything, and you will have helped them do it, and one morning—maybe a year from now, maybe ten—you'll wake up in some rented room and realize you flew the map for the burning of another village. You've done it before. I can see it in how you hold your shoulders."

That landed. She hated that it landed. He had known her for six days and he'd found the load-bearing wall of her guilt on the first try, the way water finds a crack.

"I refuse," she said finally, turning back to face him. "Officially. For the record. You are refused."

"Noted." He smiled, and it transformed his face from a clerk's face into something older and more stubborn. "And yet."

"And yet what?"

"And yet you haven't thrown me out. Your door has a bolt—I checked. You have a pistol within reach of where you're standing—I watched you check its position twice while I talked. You are a woman whose entire methodology is denial, and you are not denying me. Draw your own conclusions; I've drawn mine." He repacked the case, slowly, each paper sleeved and squared. "I'll be at the fuel dock tomorrow at dawn. If your plane lifts off without me, I'll make my own way to the reef, and I'll be inside the cave before you are, because I know the entrance the fishermen use, the one that doesn't show on any chart—including yours."

"There is no other entrance."

"There are three," Teodoro said. "Your charts show one. The legend shows none. My family's records show three, and one of them floods from below." He lifted the case and settled it against his chest again, that same careful carry. "Good evening, Miss Zhou. Sleep well. Provision well. Rope before regret—I heard your father's rule in how you work, though I suspect the man who taught you stole it from someone else, possibly from an archivist."

He left. The wind took his footsteps and scattered them. Jonah stood in the doorway a long time, watching the water go silver and then pewter and then dark, and she did not throw his logic out after him, though she considered it seriously, weighing it in her hands like ballast she wasn't sure she could afford to keep.

* * *

She spent the evening losing arguments with herself, which was her oldest form of exercise.

Provisioning finished, she ran the final checks by lamplight: fuel drums lashed and logged, water tins stowed forward where she could reach them, rice and salt fish sealed, the medical kit strapped under the copilot's seat, the pistol in its unlisted place. The duplicated charts went into a second oilskin bag, which went into the emergency kit behind the pilot's seat, which was itself strapped to a floatation cushion that could be cut free in eight seconds. Everything doubled. Everything deniable. She was, she realized, packing for two futures simultaneously—one where she came back with a clean report for Auntie Ru, and one where she didn't come back at all except as a rumor, and both futures fit in the same aircraft, which told her something about her life she preferred not to examine.

Dawn came up the color of weak tea.

At the fuel dock the mist was doing its slow work on the world, erasing the far shore, softening the masts, turning the moored boats into suggestions of themselves. She walked the dock with her kit bag over her shoulder and her mind made up in the way that minds are never actually made up—firmly, and with a trapdoor installed.

The plane waited at the end of the dock, tied down, dew silvering the wings. And leaning against the strut with his document case between his feet and a canvas rucksack at his side, wearing an expression of profound administrative patience, stood Teodoro Reyes.

"I refused you," Jonah said.

"You did. Formally and memorably. I've entered it in my records." He patted the case. "But consider the logistics. You cannot read the ledgers. You cannot find the second and third entrances. You do not know the true flood behavior of the inner chambers. Every hour you spend in that cave without me is an hour in which you are not surveying—you are sightseeing in a language you don't speak, in a building designed to drown the illiterate." He straightened, and the mist moved around him, and for a moment he looked less like a disgraced archivist than like a man reporting for a duty he'd been assigned at birth. "You can refuse me again. I'll wait. I'm very good at waiting—it's most of what archives teach. But the tide won't wait, the scouts won't wait, and Auntie Ru certainly won't wait, and somewhere behind all of us the monsoon is assembling itself like a debt coming due."

Jonah looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the plane—at the fuel, the water, the rope, all of it provisioned for one passenger too few—and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only after all the alternatives have drowned, that she had packed for him days ago. That some part of her had started making room the moment he'd shown her the manifest with her tail number on it, and had simply declined to inform the rest of her.

"If you slow me down," she said, swinging the hangar door wide, "I leave you on the reef."

"Acceptable terms." He picked up his case. "If you lie to me about what you report to Auntie Ru, I walk into the sea."

"Those aren't acceptable terms."

"They're honest ones." He paused at the cabin door, one hand on the frame, and glanced back at her through the mist. "We're going to spend several days in very close quarters, Miss Zhou, in a cathedral full of other people's debts. Honesty is the only rope long enough for both of us. Best we start coiling it now."

She climbed in after him, pulled the canopy, and ran the engine up until the whole dock shuddered and the mist tore loose from the water in ragged sheets. Ahead, out past the headland, the sky over the open strait had begun to organize itself into something with intent—the monsoon's advance guard, stacked and bruise-colored, waiting with the patience of everything on this coast that was owed.

Jonah set her heading for Karang Laut and pushed the throttle forward, and did not say, and would not say for a long time yet, that the plane had never felt so properly loaded.

Behind them, at the fuel dock, a boy ran down the planks waving a telegram flimsy over his head, shouting a name into the fog that neither of them could hear.
