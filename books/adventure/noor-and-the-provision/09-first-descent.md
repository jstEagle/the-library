## 9. First Descent

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and Jonah felt it before she saw it—the longboat going slack beneath her, the current that had been dragging them toward the reef simply letting go, like a hand opening.

"Slack water," Teodoro said. He'd been sitting with his knees drawn up against the bow thwart for an hour, watching the black water with the expression of a man reading a letter he already knew by heart. "Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty. After that the sea starts taking it back."

Jonah shipped the oars and let the boat drift. Ahead of them, where the charts showed nothing but coral and drowned rock, the reef had opened its mouth.

She'd seen it from the air twice now, and both times her mind had tried to file it as something else—a bomb crater, a blue hole, a trick of light on wet limestone. From above it was a wound in the world's skin, a circle of impossible black ringed by foam-white coral. But at water level, in the grey hour before dawn, it was something older than any word she had for it. The cave mouth rose out of the tide like the doorway of a drowned cathedral, its limestone arch furred with barnacles and hung with the roots of some salt-stunted tree that had no business growing there. Water moved inside it—not waves, but a slow breathing, the whole sea inhaling and exhaling through stone.

"You're sure about the depth at the entrance," she said. It wasn't really a question. She'd asked it three times since they'd left the mangrove anchorage, and each time Teodoro had answered the same way, patiently, as if she were a student who kept forgetting her own lesson.

"Four feet at slack tide, per my grandfather's notes. Six inches more if the monsoon swell is running, which it is." He patted the oilcloth bundle between his feet—his family's papers, copied out in his own careful hand over years of nights he didn't talk about. "He measured it himself, in 1931. The Reyes men have been measuring this entrance since before your plane's grandfather was riveted."

"My plane is not old."

"Your plane is a 1936 airframe wearing someone else's registration like a stolen coat." He said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "Everything on this coast is older than it pretends to be. That's rather the point of what's down there."

Jonah took the lantern from under the thwart and lit it, cupping the flame against the wind that wasn't blowing yet but would be. The light made a small gold room around them, the two of them and the boat and the bundle of paper, and beyond its edge the dark went on being enormous.

She thought about turning back. She thought about it the way you think about anything at four in the morning—clearly, coldly, with all the arithmetic laid out. Turn back: Auntie Ru's survey report never gets filed, the debt keeps compounding, the Syndicate sends someone less careful than her to do the same job. Go in: she becomes the woman who mapped the thing, and whatever happens after that happens because of her.

There was no column in either ledger marked *leave it alone*. There never was. That was the joke of her whole life, told very quietly.

"Row," she said.

* * *

The entrance swallowed them gradually, then all at once. First the arch passed overhead close enough that Teodoro could have touched it, dripping, streaked with mineral shadow—and then the walls fell away and the lantern light stopped reaching anything and they were inside the earth, floating on a lake that shouldn't exist, in a silence so complete Jonah could hear her own pulse in her ears.

Then Teodoro raised the second lantern, the big one, and the chamber revealed itself.

"Oh," he said. Just that. A single syllable, punched out of him.

The space was vast beyond the lantern's power to insult it. Their light showed them the first hundred feet and suggested the rest: a vaulted ceiling lost in darkness overhead, its limestone hanging in great folded curtains, white and ochre and a deep blood-red where iron had bled through the stone for ten thousand years. Stalactites came down like the pipes of an organ built for a god with too many lungs. And the water—they were floating on still, glass-black water that held their reflection perfectly, so that it seemed the boat floated on nothing at all, suspended in the middle of a darkness that extended equally up and down, and the two of them were the only lit things in either direction.

Jonah had flown through weather that could kill her in nine different ways. She had stood in rooms where men decided whether villages burned. None of it had prepared her for the simple, animal awe of this—the feeling of being small in a way that was not humiliating but almost restful. Here was something that did not care about debts. Something that had been keeping its own accounts since before anyone alive had been born, and would go on keeping them after every ledger on the coast had burned.

"The causeway," Teodoro whispered, and even his whisper echoed, coming back to them twice from opposite directions like a lie corroborating itself.

Ahead, rising out of the black water, ran a line of fitted stones—each one the size of a coffin lid, dressed flat on top, laid in a curve that marched off into the dark toward the far wall of the chamber. Not a natural formation. Not recent. The tops of the stones were dry, polished smooth in the centers by centuries of feet.

"People walked here," Jonah said. "Regularly. For how long?"

Teodoro didn't answer right away. He was leaning out over the gunwale, lantern held low, looking at the underside of the nearest causeway stone. When he spoke, his voice had changed—flattened, professional, the voice of the archive clerk he had once been before the archive was taken from him.

"There are marks cut into the facing. Tally marks, I think. Or numbers." He looked up at her, and in the lantern light his face was wet-eyed and fierce. "Jonah. My grandfather's notes said there were records here. I thought—he described them as documents. Papers. Things that could burn." His hand closed on the gunwale hard enough to whiten the knuckles. "They carved them into the stone. You can't burn stone."

They tied the longboat to a rusted iron ring set into the rock—an anchor point old enough that the ring had worn a groove in the limestone beneath it—and stepped onto the causeway. The stones held their weight without complaint. Jonah carried the big lantern; Teodoro carried the oilcloth bundle and, she noticed, touched the wall as he walked, trailing his fingers along the carved faces of the stones the way a man touches a banister in his childhood home.

The carvings began within twenty paces.

At first, in the lantern's small circle, they looked like decoration—rows of geometric signs, chevrons and circles and hooked characters, chiseled into panels maybe a foot square, set into the causeway walls at reading height. Then the panels multiplied. They were everywhere. Every flat surface of the passage bore them, in ranks and columns, some crisp as yesterday's masonry and others worn to ghosts, and where one panel ended another began, and above them, and below, following the walls back into the dark farther than the light would go.

"It's a script," Teodoro said. He had stopped walking. He was standing in front of a panel with his lips moving, the way illiterate men look at writing—or the way literate men look at writing in a language they almost know. "It's related to the old trade script. The one the harbor clerks used before the Company standardized everything. My family's archive had fragments—fragments, a few dozen characters, mostly from toll receipts." His finger hovered over a hooked sign without touching it. "This one is 'measure.' This one—" he moved to the next, "—'grain.' These two together, 'measured grain,' that's a tax entry. That's a *tax entry*, Jonah. This is a receipt. Someone paid their grain tax here, and someone recorded it, and the record has outlived the kingdom that collected it."

"How many of these can you read?"

"A tenth. Maybe less. Enough to know what the rest are." He turned slowly, taking in the walls, and when he faced her again his voice cracked down the middle. "Enough to know that this is a customs house. A port authority. A *treasury*. Centuries of it. Tolls, treaties, harvest levies, loans—" he stopped on that word, visibly steadied himself, "—loans, repayments, defaults, all of it, written where everyone who entered could see it. Public accounts. Do you understand? They wrote the debts in public. In stone. Where no clerk could lose them and no lord could burn them."

Jonah looked at the little panel in front of her—at the neat rows of hooked characters recording, presumably, some dead person's honest payment of some dead kingdom's levy—and felt the hair rise on her arms.

Because she understood. She understood exactly. She had spent five years keeping her own accounts in the only medium the modern coast allowed: memory, misdirection, false registrations, manifests that lied. Everything written down could be seized. Everything spoken could be denied. She had built a life on the principle that the safest ledger was the one that didn't exist.

And here, three hundred years ago or a thousand, people had concluded precisely the opposite. The safest ledger was the one no one could destroy. Carve the debt in stone in a cathedral of the tide, and let every debtor walk past it forever.

"They brought people here," she said slowly. "Debtors. To see it."

"To see it, or to swear on it. My grandfather's notes mention oaths." Teodoro had started walking again, faster now, his lantern swinging. "Come on. The chest should be in the last chamber. If the notes are right about the layout—if anything is right—"

* * *

They passed through two more chambers, each vaster than the last, each with its own population of stone—the second chamber's floor studded with low tables of living rock, flat-topped, clearly meant for sitting and writing; the third dominated by a pillar of flowstone thick as a lighthouse, its base ringed with carved bands like the rings of a tree. In the third chamber the air changed. Cooler. Moving, faintly, from somewhere deep in the rock, carrying a smell of salt and old iron.

And in the fourth chamber, at its far end, on a natural shelf of stone raised like an altar above the high-tide line, sat the chest.

It was smaller than Jonah had imagined. Perhaps the length of a coffin, but shallower—more like a sea chest, the kind a ship's purser would keep under his bunk. Iron, banded with iron, corroded to a single unbroken color, brown-black, the brown-black of things that have given up any argument with time. It had no lock that she could see. Instead the lid was closed by a mechanism she couldn't parse at first—then understood, and felt her stomach drop: a seal. Molten lead, poured into a channel around the lid's seam, stamped while soft with a device she recognized from Teodoro's papers.

"That's the Reyes stamp," she said.

"My family sealed it." Teodoro's voice had gone very quiet. "Or helped seal it. Or—" He climbed onto the shelf beside it and knelt, not touching it, hovering his hands over the lead seal the way a doctor hovers over a wound. "The date stamp is worn. But the device is ours. Jonah, my family didn't just keep records *about* this place. We were its custodians. This is a formal archival seal. Whatever's in here was deposited under our authority."

"And the Syndicate wants it."

"The Syndicate wants everything. That's what Syndicates are." But he said it absently, all his attention on the seal, tracing the stamped device with one fingertip. "If we open it—"

"We're not opening it." Jonah heard her own voice come out flat and hard, the pilot's voice, the voice she used when the weather was turning. "Ru said touch nothing. I know what you're thinking, and we're not opening it. Not because of Ru. Because the moment that seal breaks, we've changed what this place is. Right now it's evidence. The magistrate—Salmah's niece, whoever ends up on the right side of this—can break that seal themselves, with witnesses, with the lawyers watching. We break it, and everything inside becomes *our story*. Two strangers' word against a Syndicate's lawyers." She shook her head. "We photograph it. We document the seal. We leave it shut. That's worth more than whatever's inside."

Teodoro looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, and something in his face eased—as if she'd told him a thing he'd wanted someone else to say for forty years.

"You'd have made a good archivist," he said.

"No, I wouldn't. I'm too good at lying." She got the camera out of her pack—the cheap one, the one she used for survey work, its flashbulbs wrapped in cloth so they wouldn't rattle. "Hold the lantern steady."

She photographed the chest from four angles. She photographed the seal close-up. Teodoro copied the stamped device into his notebook, and the position of the chest relative to the chamber walls, and the height of the shelf above the tide-scour line, in a small precise hand that made Jonah think, absurdly, of her own preflight checklists.

It was while she was changing the film, kneeling on the causeway with her back to the chamber, that she heard it.

At first she took it for the cave breathing—that slow tidal respiration they'd heard at the entrance, the sea moving in its sleep. But it was wrong. Rhythmic in the wrong way. Regular. Mechanical.

An engine.

Above them. Faint, throttled down, but unmistakable to anyone who had spent five years identifying engines by ear the way other people identified voices. A marine diesel, laboring, coming down from high revs toward idle.

Jonah froze. Teodoro, copying the last of the seal markings, looked up at her face and went still.

"Slack tide ended eight minutes ago," he breathed. "Nobody comes at the end of slack. Nobody sane."

"Ru's scouts aren't sane. They're early." She was already moving, killing the big lantern, plunging them into a darkness so total it pressed on the eyes. In the black she found his sleeve, his wrist, guided his hand to the camera strap. "Pack it. Quietly. We're going to the boat."

The engine sound grew as they felt their way back along the causeway, and with it came a new sound that turned Jonah's blood to ice water: voices. Male voices, echoing distorted through the chambers behind them, and the hollow knock of something metal against stone. And threaded through it, thin and wrong in the cave's clean air—

A flashlight beam swept the mouth of the third chamber behind them, catching the edge of the flowstone pillar, and Jonah pulled Teodoro off the causeway into a cleft in the rock that smelled of bat guano and cold stone, and they crouched there in the absolute dark, shoulder to shoulder, breathing through their mouths.

The lights came on in the third chamber. Then the second. Men's silhouettes moved against them, carrying survey equipment—tripods, a theodolite case, coils of electric lamp wire. Methodical. Professional. Already setting up to work.

And over the splash and echo of their movements, one voice said, clear enough to reach the cleft where Jonah and Teodoro hid:

"Start with the chest. The old man says the seal doesn't matter anymore."

In the dark, she felt Teodoro stop breathing.
