## 10. Witnesses

The engine sound came down through the throat of the cave like a swallowed thing, muffled and wrong, and Teodoro felt it in his sternum before he heard it with his ears.

They had been in the third chamber for the better part of an hour—Jonah sketching the causeway by lantern light, him working his way along the carved wall with a pencil stub and a roll of waxed paper he'd carried for eleven years against exactly this day. The script ran in horizontal registers, older than anything in the confiscated archive, older than the colonial cataloguing system that had eaten his family's work and called it administration. He could read perhaps one character in five. But five was enough to know what he was looking at. Tolls. Grain debts. A treaty between two river lords whose names had otherwise survived only as place-names on charts nobody consulted. Centuries of it, cut into limestone by hands that had understood something the paper world had never learned: that stone does not burn.

He had written that phrase in his notebook so many times over the years that it had worn smooth with repetition. *Stone does not burn.* It was the sentence he said to himself on bad nights, in the lean-to at Pasir Panjang, when the rain came through the palm-thatch and the rats moved through the rafters and he wondered what exactly he was doing with the remainder of a life that had been subtracted from him one drawer at a time. Stone does not burn. His family's records had burned. His father's careful hand, his grandfather's marginalia, forty years of a coastal civilization's memory reduced to ash in an official incinerator behind the district office while a clerk with a rubber stamp watched and made notes. But the original—the source those papers had only ever described—had waited here, under the reef, under the tide, keeping its own accounts in a language of chisel-marks. Waiting for someone to come read it.

And now someone had come, and they were not readers.

"Down," Jonah said. Not loudly. She never said anything loudly; it was one of the first things he'd noticed about her, back at the fuel dock, this woman who lived her whole life at a volume just below alarm. "Lights out. Now."

She killed her lantern and killed his with a flat of her hand before he'd finished reaching for it, and the dark that followed was total in a way Teodoro had not experienced since childhood—not the dark of a room at night but the dark of underground, of stone pressing in from every side, of air that tasted of salt and cold calcium. He heard her moving, quick and certain, and then her hand found his sleeve and drew him sideways, off the causeway, into a cleft between two ledger-walls where the rock folded back on itself and left a pocket deep enough for two people who were trying very hard not to exist.

"Boat," she breathed, close to his ear. "Get low. Whatever you hear, whatever you see—we don't move until I move."

He wanted to ask how she knew. He didn't need to. Five years of flying contraband rumors across the Sunda interior had given her instincts he could only borrow, the way a man borrows a coat from a corpse. She had heard the engine change pitch. She had known, before any sane person would have known, that the Syndicate's scouts were not coming tomorrow or next week or after the monsoon, as Auntie Ru's arithmetic had implied. They were coming now. Early. Hungry. And somewhere above them, at the mouth of the cave, a boat was already grinding its way up the flooded entrance channel on a tide that would not hold much longer for anyone.

* * *

They came in with lights, four of them, maybe five—it was impossible to count precisely from the pocket, from below, with only the wash of their torches to go by—and their voices carried down the chambers with the flat confidence of men who believed no one else was in the world. One of them spoke with the clipped cadence of a man reading instructions: photograph everything, in order, left wall first, don't waste film on the waterline sections. Another answered in the lazy drawl of a man who had done this kind of work before and found it beneath him.

Teodoro pressed his spine against the cold stone and did the arithmetic of breathing. In through the nose, slow. Out slower. His heart was a fist against his ribs, and every pulse seemed loud enough to carry, though of course it didn't—of course it couldn't—over the slap of water against the causeway and the clank of equipment being unloaded onto the flat stones where he and Jonah had stood not twenty minutes earlier.

The torch beams swept the walls. He watched them play across the registers he had been transcribing, watched the light stutter over characters he had spent half the night learning, and something in his chest turned over slowly, the way a grave turns over when the ground above it is disturbed. They were photographing his inheritance. Not to understand it. To own it. Every frame was a theft conducted politely, methodically, with tripods and exposure meters and the patient competence of people who had stripped other rooms in other places and been paid well for it.

He should have felt nothing but fear. Fear was the correct emotion; fear was what kept animals alive in holes. But underneath the fear, in the silt at the bottom of him, something else was moving, and it took him several minutes of enforced stillness to identify it because it had been so long since he'd felt it that he'd almost forgotten its shape.

It was grief. Grief with a witness. For eleven years he had grieved alone, privately, in the way of a man mourning something the world insisted had never mattered—a provincial archive, a family of clerks, papers about fish tolls and rice loans, the sort of thing that appears in obituaries as a single line: *his interests included local history.* Nobody had burned anything important. That was the finding. That was always the finding. The fire behind the district office had consumed documents of no historical value, the report said, duplicates and working copies, nothing irreplaceable. His father had signed the inventory himself, under duress, and died within the year with the signature on his conscience like a swallowed hook.

Now, crouched in a crack in the earth while strangers photographed the unburnable originals, Teodoro Reyes finally had company in his mourning. The grief had witnesses. It was simply that the witnesses were the arsonists.

* * *

The chest came next.

He heard it before he saw it—the scrape of iron on stone, the grunt of men levering something heavy across the chamber floor toward the light. Then the torches converged, and from his angle in the pocket, wedged low with Jonah's shoulder hard against his, he could see a slice of the far chamber reflected in the black sheet of standing water between them: a rectangle of rusted metal, banded, cornered with rivets, sitting on the causeway like a coffin that had decided against burial.

The scouts worked at it with tools. There was discussion—one voice arguing for care, another for speed, a third apparently overruled by whoever carried the crowbar. The lock gave with a sound Teodoro felt through the stone itself, a groan and then a snap, and the lid went back, and the torchlight fell inside, and even from thirty meters away, even reflected, even upside down in water, he understood what they were seeing.

Paper. Bundles of it. Oilcloth-wrapped, corded, stacked with the loving density of things that had been sealed away by someone who intended them to last. Deeds, probably. Charters. The founding documents of whatever arrangement had turned this coast into a ledger with people written in the margins. The contents of the chest were the reason for the cave's value—the reason Auntie Ru's courtesy had wrapped itself around Jonah like a debt, the reason survey stakes had appeared on the reef within days of the tide's first withdrawal. Everything else—the carvings, the causeway, the cathedral space—was architecture. This was the treasury.

The scouts passed bundles out hand over hand, repacking them into canvas cases with the brisk efficiency of men on a schedule. Teodoro watched a wrapped packet cross the torchlight and thought, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in: *that is the famine year.* He couldn't have said how he knew. Instinct, maybe. The same instinct that had told him, standing in the archive ruins at nineteen, sorting wet ash with his bare hands, that the fire had not been an accident of wiring. Some knowledge doesn't arrive through evidence. It arrives through the body, the way weather arrives, and all the documentation in the world is only a formal acknowledgment afterward.

His hands, pressed flat against the stone floor to keep them quiet, began to shake. Not from cold. From the effort of not doing anything.

Because that was the choice, laid out in the dark with terrible simplicity. He could move. He could stand up out of the pocket, walk into the torchlight, announce himself—*Teodoro Reyes, formerly of the Provincial Archive, son of Emilio Reyes who signed the burning order's inventory, keeper of the true record*—and they would kill him, or worse, they would take him seriously, which amounted to the same thing with different paperwork. Or he could stay still. Stay small. Let them carry the past out of the cave in canvas cases while he hid in the planet's oldest filing cabinet and told himself that survival was strategy.

Jonah's hand closed over his wrist. Light. Deliberate. Not restraining—he hadn't been moving—but anchoring, the way you anchor a boat that wants to drift. He realized she could feel the shaking through his bones, and that she had chosen to answer it not with a whisper but with a grip.

*Later,* the grip said. *Not now. Later.*

* * *

It was during the loading, in the long minutes when the cases were being ferried back up the channel toward the boats, that the torchlight swung wide and gave him the face.

He had been watching the reflected chamber more than the direct light—reflection was safer, reflection let him look without his eyes catching a beam—and so he saw the lead scout first as an inversion, a pale smear of jaw and forehead floating in black water. Then the man stepped closer to the causeway's edge, and the reflection resolved, and Teodoro stopped being able to think.

He knew the face.

Not the way you know a face from a crowd, not the way you know a neighbor, not even the way you know a man you've met once and remember. He knew it the way you know a face from a photograph you have studied until the studying became a wound. There was a file. There had always been a file. After the fire, after the inquiry that found nothing, after his mother's death and his own slow exile down the coast, Teodoro had spent three years doing the one thing a disgraced archivist knows how to do: he had built an index. Personnel files, shipping manifests, licensing registries, police gazettes—he had cross-referenced every name that touched the destruction of his family's work, and he had kept photographs, poor ones, photostats of photostats, faces blurred by generations of copying but legible to someone who had looked at them nightly the way other men look at saints or enemies.

The face in the water was younger than the photostat. Of course it was. Forty years. But bone doesn't age the way skin does, and the set of the jaw, the heavy brow, the scar that split the left eyebrow into a fork—the fork. God. The fork. He had drawn that scar in the margin of his index card a hundred times, inked it, re-inked it, until he could have identified the man from the scar alone, from across a harbor, in bad light, upside down in standing water.

Armando Vásquez. Assistant district officer, seconded to land registration, 1931. The youngest man in the room the night the records burned. The one whose signature appeared on the transfer manifest that moved the surviving fragments—fragments of fragments, the leavings of leavings—into private hands. Private hands. The phrase had a specific meaning on this coast, and the specific meaning was the Marrow Syndicate, and the Marrow Syndicate had employed Armando Vásquez continuously for forty years, promoting him from assistant district officer to fixer to scout-master to whatever title they used now for men who went into caves and took things.

Still employed. Still burning. The realization landed in Teodoro's chest not as a thought but as a physical event, a pressure, a heat. Because he could see it happening again, right now, in front of him, in miniature. The same competence. The same methodical appetite. The same cheerful willingness to treat a civilization's memory as inventory. Forty years ago Vásquez had helped carry his family's papers to a fire. Tonight he was carrying the originals out of the earth, and the expression on his face—Teodoro could see it even inverted, even reflected, even at this distance—was the expression of a man doing a job he was good at and mildly enjoying.

Grief, Teodoro had learned, is a solvent. It dissolves the self you were and leaves you soft, formless, available to whatever fills the space. For eleven years the space had filled with patience, with indexing, with the long slow discipline of waiting. But there is another thing that fills that space, and it moves faster, and it smells like smoke, and it tells you that patience was only ever the polite name for cowardice. Crouched in the stone with the fork-scarred jaw floating in the water in front of him, Teodoro felt the second thing arrive, and it arrived with a weapon in each conceptual hand.

His mission had been personal from the beginning—that was what he'd told himself, that was the story, the archive was his family, the truth was his inheritance. But there is personal and there is personal, the way there is rain and there is the sea falling on you. Until tonight, his mission had been about documents. About proving the fire happened, about recovering what could be recovered, about writing the coast's history back into existence. Clean purposes. Purposes you could explain to a magistrate, to a magistrate's clerk, to God.

This was not clean. This was a man's face, and a scar, and forty years of a debt that had compounded in the dark the way Auntie Ru's arithmetic compounded Jonah's, weekly, mercilessly, with interest paid in ash. Teodoro understood, in the sudden brutal way understanding comes underground, that he had not spent eleven years preparing to recover records. He had spent eleven years preparing to be in this room, at this moment, when the man who burned them walked past close enough to touch.

He did not touch him. He wants that recorded somewhere, anywhere, even if the only place is the inside of his own skull: he did not touch him. Jonah's grip held. The pocket held. The scouts loaded their cases and fired up their engines and withdrew up the channel with the tide, leaving behind the empty chest, the tripod legs' puncture marks in the moss, and two people in the dark who now knew far too much.

* * *

The silence afterward was worse than the noise. Noise has edges; silence doesn't. They stayed in the pocket a full twenty minutes after the last engine faded, because Jonah was Jonah, because she trusted nothing, because the men who leave caves sometimes leave men in caves. And in those twenty minutes, in the absolute black, with the water rising millimeter by millimeter around their shins as the tide began its turn, Teodoro sat with what he had seen and let it change him.

He tried to say it aloud, once, to the darkness, testing whether his voice still worked. "Vásquez," he said, very quietly, and the name came out sounding like something exhumed.

Jonah turned toward him. Her eyes caught nothing—no light left to catch—but he felt the turn, the attention, the way her whole body oriented toward sound the way a compass needle orients toward north. "You know him," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know his file." His voice was steadier than he expected. "I've known it for eleven years. I just never thought—" He stopped. Starting sentences he couldn't finish was becoming a habit down here, and the cave was teaching him that unfinished sentences are a kind of drowning. "Forty years ago he carried my family's records to the fire. Tonight he carried the originals out of this cave. Same hands. Same employer. Same job."

Jonah absorbed that in silence, the way she absorbed most things, filing it somewhere behind her eyes with the efficiency of a woman who had learned that information is cargo and cargo must be secured. When she finally spoke again it was practical, immediate, stripped of everything except the next hour: the tide was turning, the boat was where they'd left it, the exit channel would close within ninety minutes, and whatever they were going to do about any of this would have to be done from above, from daylight, from distance.

But as they waded back along the causeway, lantern relit, the carved walls sliding past them register by register, Teodoro caught her looking at him sidelong—caught the look, held it, understood it—and he understood that something between them had shifted weight. She had watched a man shake in the dark and had chosen, with a pilot's economy, not to ask why. And he had watched her choose not to ask, and had understood that this too was a kind of loyalty, the kind that keeps you alive precisely by refusing, for now, to know you completely.

At the boat, hauling the painter off the causeway ring, his hands steady now in a way that frightened him more than the shaking had, Teodoro said the thing he had been composing in the dark:

"They'll read what they took. Or their lawyers will. And they'll find out what my family knew forty years ago—that the chest isn't the treasure. The chest is the receipt." He pushed off into the channel, the black mouth of the cave receding behind them, the tide drawing them out toward a sky just beginning to consider gray. "The carvings stay. The ledgers stay. Stone does not burn."

Jonah said nothing. But her hands on the tiller were white-knuckled, and he had flown enough beside her now—or fled enough beside her—to know what her silences cost, and this one was expensive.

Above the reef, dawn was assembling itself out of cloud. Below, the tide was already sealing the mouth, black water folding over black water, closing the book for another twelve hours. And somewhere out in the strait, running fast and low before the wind, a boat full of canvas cases was carrying the coast's founding lies toward a floating restaurant where a woman drank tea and multiplied debts.

Eleven years, Teodoro thought, watching the cave disappear. Eleven years of indexing, and it had taken one night to learn the difference between recovering the past and avenging it—and only one of those could be done without becoming Armando Vásquez.

He gripped the gunwale and made himself say the harder sentence, the one he'd been avoiding since the torchlight found the fork-scarred jaw:

"Now I have to decide which one I am."
