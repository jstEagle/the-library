## 11. The Report She Didn't Give

The radio room at the Karang Laut mission station smelled of kerosene and salt-rotted wood, and it was the only place on the coast where a wire could be sent without three people listening to it being born. Jonah had learned that on her second run through these islands, back when she still believed rules were things you made instead of things that made you. The station keeper, a half-blind Chinese Methodist named Elder Goh who took payment in quinine and conversation, had gone to his evening prayers, and she had the room to herself for exactly as long as it took God to be patient with an old man's knees.

She sat down at the key with the lamp turned low and her hands flat on either side of it, the way you'd rest them on a table before signing something.

The report had been composing itself in her head for two days, ever since the engine noise above the cave had sent her and Teodoro scrambling up the causeway with wet rope burning grooves into their shoulders. She knew what Auntie Ru expected: coordinates, dimensions, an inventory. She knew what Auntie Ru would do with it. And she knew—had known since the moment she saw the first survey stake driven into the reef like a nail through the coast's own coffin—that she did not intend to give any of it.

What she intended to give was a shape of a report. A report with the meat carved out of it and the skin left on, so that from a distance, at a glance, held up to the light, it would look whole.

*Survey conducted per instructions,* she began drafting on the pad of telegram blanks, in pencil first, because pencil could be erased and ink could only be burned. *Site accessible approximately ninety minutes either side of dead low tide. Entrance via boat only; approach channel narrow, coral heads unmarked. Interior consists of one principal chamber, approximate depth two hundred meters, with secondary passages not yet surveyed.*

That much was true. Every word of it true. That was the craft of it—the thing she'd learned five years ago from a smuggler in Borneo whose name she'd never learned but whose method she'd never forgotten: the best lie is a true report with a hole in it, because a lie can be caught, but a hole just looks like work not yet done.

*Walls bear extensive carvings in an archaic script. Preliminary assessment: commercial records, toll schedules, possibly treaty texts. Significant historical value. Recommend scholarly translation prior to any removal or disturbance.*

There it was. *Recommend scholarly translation.* A sentence that sounded like diligence and functioned like a lock. If Ru swallowed it, the Syndicate's men would need experts, permits, time—and time was the one commodity the monsoon was about to confiscate from everyone equally. And scholars, real ones, wrote things down. Published things. Scholars were witnesses, and witnesses were the one thing the Marrow Syndicate had spent forty years structuring itself to avoid.

Her pencil hovered over the next line for a long moment.

*No sealed containers or chests observed.*

Three words short of a lie. The chest existed. She had stood in front of it, had put her palm flat against iron gone cold and furred with corrosion, had felt through the metal the particular density of paper stored against damp by someone who had cared whether it survived. It sat in the last chamber behind a fall of stalactite that had grown over its hasp like a candle waxed shut. It was not hidden. It was simply not mentioned.

Jonah read the draft four times. Then she did what she always did with documents that could hang her: she read it as her enemy. She imagined Auntie Ru's eyes moving across it, those eyes that missed nothing because they had never needed to miss anything, and asked herself the question Ru would ask: *what is this woman not telling me?*

And the answer came back ugly and immediate: everything. The report was thin. Thin in the specific way reports are thin when the writer is frightened. There were no measurements of the carvings' height off the floor, no count of chambers, no sketch, no sample rubbing. Ru had run couriers and pilots and worse for thirty years. She had read a thousand reports from a thousand frightened people, and she knew the texture of omission the way a fishmonger knows the texture of a fish that's been packed on ice to hide the smell.

But there was nothing to be done about that. Perfection was impossible; the art was in making imperfection look like incompetence rather than intent. So Jonah added a line about the tide tables being uncertain this season, another about her instruments—a barometer with a hairline crack, a compass that wandered near the reef's iron-bearing basalt—and let the report sag under the weight of plausible inadequacy. Let Ru think her pilot was sloppy. Sloppy pilots lived longer than careful ones, in Jonah's experience, because nobody bothered to check their sums.

She copied the final version onto a proper blank in her careful radio hand—not her natural writing, which leaned and hurried, but the round, patient script she'd been taught by a mission schoolteacher in another life, the handwriting of a woman who had nothing to hide.

Then she sat with her hands on the key and did not send it.

Outside, the dusk was doing what dusk did here, which was less a fading than a drowning—the light going under all at once, green water closing over green sky. A fishing boat was coming home along the channel, its masthead lamp swinging, and the sound of its engine carried across the flat water with terrible clarity, the way sounds carry when the air itself seems to be holding its breath before the monsoon.

She thought about Ibu Salmah paying her in fish and warnings. She thought about the offerings on the reef—frangipani going brown in salt water, a child's sandal set upright on a rock like a small monument to whoever had lost it. She thought about Teodoro Reyes standing in the last chamber with his lantern raised, reading stone the way other men read letters from home, his face lit with a hunger so old it had stopped looking like hunger and started looking like geology.

And she thought about the arithmetic, because she was a pilot and arithmetic was her native language. Five years of debt. Interest compounding weekly against a principal she'd stopped being able to see the bottom of sometime in the second year. One job—one survey, touch nothing—to make it die. All she had to do was send the true report, the full one, chest and coordinates and all, and walk away clean into whatever the Syndicate decided the coast should become afterward.

Clean. As if the word meant anything. As if she hadn't already flown crates whose contents she'd trained herself not to imagine, and woken at night in rented rooms hearing a village name she refused to say even inside her own skull.

Elder Goh's knees must have been slower than usual tonight, because the door was still closed and the lamp oil was still burning and no one had come to ask her if she wanted tea, which everyone in the mission station eventually asked her, because asking was free and kindness cost nothing here except the asking.

She keyed the transmitter and sent the report.

It went out in a stutter of dots and dashes across the darkening strait, past the fishing boat, past the reef with its black mouth now swallowing the tide again, out toward the floating restaurant anchored somewhere beyond the horizon where a woman named Ru drank oolong and kept accounts the way other women kept cats—affectionately, and with total control.

Then Jonah did the second thing, the thing that mattered more, the thing she'd actually come here to do. She waited until Elder Goh's transmission log would show nothing unusual, and then, using the station's secondary set—the ancient backup wired to a different aerial, the one Goh believed only he remembered how to operate—she sent a second message. Short. Addressed to a number in the capital that Teodoro had given her, scrawled on the back of a fuel receipt, belonging to a retired magistrate's clerk who owed Teodoro a favor older than either of them could explain sober.

*Documents exist. Iron chest intact. Location known to two persons. Request contact re: preservation. — R.*

R for Reyes. Not her initial. If anyone ever traced it, it would land on a disgraced archivist with nothing left to lose, not on a pilot with a plane and a debt and a tail number the Syndicate had owned since before she flew it. She told herself this was prudence. She knew, in the way you know a storm is coming before the barometer moves, that it was also cowardice wearing prudence's coat.

* * *

The reply came the following evening, and it came the way weather comes to this coast: not gradually, but as a single change in the quality of the air.

Jonah had been refueling at the strip, checking the tie-downs twice because the wind had begun to back around out of the southwest, when the strip boy came running down the shingle path holding a flimsy pink telegram form like it was something hot out of a fire. He didn't say who it was from. He didn't have to. Pink flimsies from the coastal relay office, hand-delivered instead of left in the rack, meant one sender on this entire stretch of water.

She opened it standing beside the plane's port wheel, with her back to the sea, because she'd learned long ago that bad news reads better with your face pointed away from open space.

It was one line. Of course it was one line. Auntie Ru had never in her life used ten words where six would hold the meaning, and she had never once sent a message whose surface matched its depth, the way a calm lagoon never once matched the current underneath it.

*THE TIDE SHOWS EVERYTHING EVENTUALLY.*

Jonah read it three times. The strip boy hovered at a respectful distance, pretending to inspect a mooring cleat, and she folded the flimsy into quarters and put it in her shirt pocket, over her heart, which was either sentiment or strategy depending on which version of herself you asked.

The tide shows everything eventually.

It was not an accusation. That was the horror of it. An accusation she could answer, deny, outrun. This was something worse: a statement of physics, delivered with the serenity of a woman who had never once needed to hurry because the sea did her hurrying for her. Ru was telling her, in the gentlest possible voice, that she already knew. Or suspected. Or—most likely, most Ru of all—had arranged matters so that she would know soon regardless of what Jonah did, and was merely informing her pilot that the clock existed.

Had the scouts reported the chest? Had they photographed the survey stakes and found them moved? Had someone on Karang Laut sold her a description of a woman and an archivist going into the reef together at slack water? Or was it simpler than all that—was it just that Ru had flown with enough pilots to know that a report with a hole in it was a confession with the dates removed?

It didn't matter which. What mattered was the sensation, which Jonah stood with for a long moment while the wind freshened and the strip grass bent all in one direction like a congregation: the sensation of having crossed a line and discovered that the line had been behind her for days.

She had thought the betrayal would feel like something. A cold shock, a ringing in the ears, the moral equivalent of a stall warning. Instead it felt like this: ordinary. She had filed a false report, sent a secret message, lied to the most dangerous woman on the coast, and the world had declined to notice. The fish sheds still stood. The gulls still argued over the gutting tables. Somewhere inland a dog was barking at nothing with the tireless conviction of dogs everywhere.

The point of no return, it turned out, was not a wall you crashed through. It was a threshold you stepped over in the dark while carrying something heavy, and only later, measuring your stride, did you realize the floor had changed.

* * *

Teodoro was waiting in the shade of the fuel drum shelter, and he had the particular stillness of a man who has been waiting long enough to start rehearsing what he'll say and then decided, correctly, to say none of it.

"You got an answer," he said. Not a question. He'd watched her face when she read the flimsy; whatever had crossed it had been legible from twenty feet.

"I got an answer."

"And?"

"She knows." Jonah fed the pink form into the drum stove, where it curled and blackened and gave up its one line to the smoke. "Or she will. It doesn't matter which. With Ru it's never mattered which."

Teodoro watched the paper go. He had a way of watching destruction that she'd noticed before—no flinch, no satisfaction, just the attentive neutrality of a man who had spent his working life watching records burn and had long ago stopped expecting the fire to mean anything personal. Except that it was personal. Everything about him was personal. That was the whole trouble with him.

"Ru's line," he said slowly, "is famous in certain archives. My father quoted it once. He said the Syndicate founder used to say it too—before it was Ru's. It's older than her. It might be older than him." He looked at her. "It's in the cave, Jonah. Carved above the outer chamber. Toll-keeper's maxim. *The water keeps honest books.* Same sentence, three hundred years of hands."

Jonah felt the ground tilt slightly, though the ground was not moving. Three hundred years of debt collectors, all quoting the same tide. She had thought she was betraying a woman. She was beginning to understand she had betrayed an institution, and institutions did not bleed—they compounded.

"So we assume," she said, "that the scouts go back in at the next low tide. With better equipment. Possibly with orders regarding the chest."

"We assume they go back with orders regarding more than the chest." Teodoro's voice dropped into the register he used when he was reciting rather than speaking, the archive clerk's cadence, facts laid out like specimens. "A chest can be cracked and carried. But a carving can be chiseled. And a witness—" he paused, and finished the sentence with his eyes instead of his mouth, which was somehow worse.

Jonah looked down the strip, at the village below it, at the fish sheds with their corrugated roofs going copper-colored in the last light, at the small figures of people hauling nets, at the children racing the tide line the way children everywhere race the edge of things that will eventually take something from them. Ibu Salmah was down there somewhere. Salmah's grandson was down there, the one who had run alongside the plane on her first landing, shouting questions about the engine until she'd let him sit in the cockpit and touch nothing, absolutely nothing, and he had touched nothing and glowed for a week.

"They'll burn it," she said. Not a question either. Just the arithmetic, spoken aloud at last, in the accent of a woman who had done this sum many times before with other villages and always, always arrived at the same total.

"They've burned for less," Teodoro said. "My family's records were worth less than this."

The wind was rising properly now, the first real breath of the season, smelling of rain that hadn't started yet but had already been dispatched. The barometer in the cockpit—her cracked, wandering, inadequate barometer—had fallen three points since noon, and she had watched it fall with the professional detachment of a pilot and the growing dread of a person who understood what falling glass meant on this coast: the airstrip at Karang Laut was a tidal strip, built on the reef's own spine, and within days the sea would take it back. The cave would seal. Everything inside it—chest, ledgers, truth, evidence—would close like a fist for an entire season.

And the scouts would not wait for the season. The scouts would work the tides they had.

"There's a window," Teodoro said, and his hand came up, unconsciously, the way it did when he talked about the cave, palm open as if feeling for a surface. "Two, maybe three days of workable tides before the swell makes the entrance channel suicide. After that, nothing until April."

Jonah looked at the plane. Her plane, in the sense that she had signed for it, bled for it, and owed more on it than it would ever be worth—which was to say, the Syndicate's plane wearing her decisions like paint. It sat on the strip with its tanks three-quarters full and its fabric skin patched in six places, and beyond it the sky was assembling something vast and gray and patient.

Every instinct she had, every rule written into her bones across five years of staying alive, said the same thing: *go now. Fly east ahead of the front. Land somewhere Ru's money doesn't reach, which is nowhere, but try anyway.* Every instinct said run.

And for the first time in five years, running felt like the wrong heading. Not dangerous. Wrong. The distinction was new to her, and she examined it the way she examined a strange vibration in the airframe—with suspicion, with attention, with the unwilling admission that some noises mean the machine is trying to tell you something.

"The tide shows everything eventually," she said aloud, testing the words in her own mouth, hearing them the way Ru must have heard them a thousand times, hearing the toll-keeper's ghost underneath. "You know what she forgot to mention? It works both ways."

Teodoro almost smiled. It was the closest thing to hope she'd seen on his face since the fuel dock.

"The tide," he said, "shows everything. Eventually. To whoever's standing there when it goes out."

The first drops hit the strip then—fat, scattered, experimental, the monsoon sending down its scouts the way the Syndicate sent down theirs. Jonah pulled the plane's cover from the locker and got it half-fastened before the squall arrived in earnest, and in the white noise of it, huddled under the wing with a man she'd known eleven days and trusted further than she'd trusted anyone in five years, she understood that the report she hadn't given had already been answered, and the answer was weather.

Three days of tide. One storm coming. Two people with a hole in their boat and a copy of mislabeled documents drying badly in an oilskin packet.

And somewhere out past the rain, a woman drinking oolong who had just written *eventually* into the record, and meant it as a deadline.
