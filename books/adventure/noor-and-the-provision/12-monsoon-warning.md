## 12. Monsoon Warning

The barometer on Jonah's instrument panel had been falling for two days, and by the third morning it had dropped low enough that the needle seemed to be apologizing. She tapped the glass with one fingernail, the way her first instructor had taught her—*if you have to ask, the answer's already no*—and watched the needle shiver in its housing like something trying to get out.

Twenty-nine point four inches and sliding. Over open water in the Sunda Strait, that wasn't weather. That was a countdown.

She stood in the doorway of the rented hangar at Karang Laut and looked up at a sky she didn't trust. The clouds had come in overnight from the southwest, stacked in gray shelves, their undersides torn into ragged streamers that never quite touched the sea. The air had gone thick and sweet, the particular heaviness that meant the monsoon was done announcing itself and was simply arriving, the way an aunt arrives—uninvited, inevitable, planning to stay. The palms along the tideline leaned all one direction now, combed flat by a wind that hadn't yet decided to become serious but was rehearsing.

The tidal strip below the hangar was still dry. Barely. The last spring tide had left its signature in a line of black weed and broken shell halfway up the coral ramp, and Jonah could read the arithmetic written there: three more tides of this height, maybe four, and the strip would go under and stay under until the season turned. She'd landed here eleven times in five years. She knew this strip the way you know a staircase in the dark—the soft patch at the eastern end where the coral crumbled, the crosswind that came off the headland in the late afternoon and shoved the tail sideways if you weren't expecting it, the exact spot where Ibu Salmah's grandson liked to sit and watch her wheels touch, close enough that she could see him counting them out loud.

Eleven landings. Never twice in the same place—that was the rule—but Karang Laut had worn a groove in her anyway, the way any place does when you keep coming back to it and telling yourself you haven't.

Behind her, inside the hangar, Teodoro Reyes sat on an ammunition crate with a kerosene lamp balanced on a second crate beside him, reading. Not reading—*deciphering*. He had the photographs spread out around his feet in overlapping fans, the ones they'd taken in the cave before the scouts arrived, and he was working through them with a magnifying glass and a pencil stub, transcribing the script from the carved walls onto the backs of old fuel chits. He worked the way water works, without apparent effort and without stopping, and when Jonah came in he didn't look up so much as tilt toward her, the way plants do.

"The barometer," she said.

"I saw." He'd rigged a fisherman's barometer to the hangar wall with wire and a bent nail, and he checked it more often than she checked hers, which she found both irritating and reassuring. "It fell another tenth since midnight."

"Then you know what that means."

"It means we have days." He set down the magnifying glass and finally looked at her properly, and she saw that he'd already built the argument while she slept, laid it out brick by brick, and was only waiting for her to walk into it. "Three days. Maybe four if the front stalls over Java, which it won't. After that the reef closes and the cave goes under and nothing comes out of it until March."

Jonah poured herself coffee from the dented pot on the shelf above the workbench. It was terrible coffee, burnt twice, made with brackish water, and she drank it anyway because it was hot and because holding the cup gave her hands something to do besides shake. "And?"

"And the chest is still half-full." Teodoro said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "We saw what the scouts took. We saw what they left. There were bundles in that chest, Jonah—sealed bundles, wax on the seams, the founder's own marks on the wrappers. They got two of them out before the tide turned. There were at least five."

"You counted."

"I count everything. It's the only thing I'm good for." He gathered the photographs back into their tin, squaring the edges with the fussiness of a man who had spent thirty years making other people's chaos legible. "Two bundles bought. Five bundles still sitting in an iron box on a stone causeway that will be twenty feet underwater by Friday. And the men who took the first two are coming back for the rest as soon as the weather gives them a window—which will be *before* our window, because their boats don't need a tidal airstrip. They just need calm-ish water and a willingness to drown, and the Marrow Syndicate has never once shown a shortage of either."

Jonah drank her coffee and said nothing, because saying nothing was a skill she'd honed to a professional edge, and because every word she could think of was the wrong one.

What she thought was: *run*. The word rose in her the way it always rose, smooth and automatic, a reflex older than memory. Run north, run east, run anywhere the debt couldn't follow. She had a plane. She had fuel enough for six hundred miles if she leaned the mixture and lied to herself about reserves. In forty-eight hours she could be a thousand miles from Auntie Ru's arithmetic, from the scouts, from the cave and its iron chest and the whole drowning coast, drinking worse coffee in a better hangar under a name she hadn't used yet.

That was the instinct. That was always the instinct. Five years of staying alive on it.

But standing there in the hangar with the wind worrying at the door seam and Teodoro waiting with his terrible patient certainty, Jonah noticed—for the first time in five years—that the instinct had a counterweight. Something small and dense on the other side of the scale. Not courage; she distrusted the word. Something more like arithmetic of her own: the knowledge that if she ran now, the chest would be emptied by men who burned fish sheds, and the ledgers would surface someday anyway, stripped of context, in some syndicate lawyer's filing cabinet, doing exactly the work they'd been moved to do. And she would have her thousand miles and her new name and the same question she'd been flying away from since the village whose name she wouldn't say, except this time she would know, precisely, what she'd chosen instead.

She set the cup down. "Say the rest of it."

Teodoro blinked. He'd expected resistance; the directness unsettled him more than argument would have. "The rest of it is that we go back down." He stood, and the lamplight caught the gray in his stubble and the fever-brightness that came into his eyes whenever the cave was mentioned, the brightness that Jonah had learned to read as both his engine and his illness. "Slack tide, day after tomorrow. The front won't be fully ashore yet—we'll have a window of maybe ninety minutes of workable sea between the last ebb and the first real blow. We take the longboat in, we take what's in the chest, we're out before the water turns."

"Day after tomorrow the swell will already be building. You've seen what the passage does in a moderate sea. In a building one it's a meat grinder with coral teeth."

"Yes."

"You could die. I could die. The boat could die, which granted is the least of it."

"Yes," Teodoro said again, and then, because he was incapable of leaving anything unindexed: "Probability of fatality given current forecasts, I'd estimate—"

"Don't estimate. Don't ever estimate at me again."

He closed his mouth. For a moment the only sound was the wind testing the hangar door, pushing, retreating, pushing—a big animal leaning its shoulder against something to see if it would hold.

Jonah walked to the hangar mouth and looked out at the strip, at the gray water beyond it, at the reef line where—somewhere under all that moving gray—a black mouth waited with its iron chest and its centuries of carved debts. She tried to summon the old clean fear, the fear that had kept her alive, and found it diluted. Cut with something else. Responsibility, maybe, or its cheaper cousin, guilt. She'd filed a partial report to Auntie Ru. Carvings yes, chest no. She'd told herself it was buying time, and it was, but time bought is time owed, and Ru's reply still sat folded in Jonah's breast pocket like a splinter she kept forgetting until she moved wrong:

*The tide shows everything eventually.*

"She knows," Jonah said quietly. "About the chest. Or she'll know within a day. My report buys us nothing anymore—it was a down payment on a lie and she's called the note. When the scouts come back and find us ahead of them, or find the chest empty, she'll know exactly who emptied it."

"So we go before they do."

"So we go before they do," Jonah agreed, and heard how strange it sounded, her voice agreeing to stay, to go *toward*, to spend the margin she'd spent five years hoarding. "And I want it understood that this is the stupidest flight plan I've ever drawn, and I once flew a load of contraband penicillin through a thunderstorm to win a bet."

Teodoro almost smiled. It transformed his face briefly into someone younger, someone who might once have been easy to know. "I'll bring the transcription kit."

"You'll bring rope, bailer, and a life vest, and you'll wear the vest, and if you argue I'll leave you on the causeway for the crabs." She held his eye until he nodded. "We go at slack tide day after tomorrow. Which gives us tomorrow to prepare, and tomorrow night to not sleep, and—" She stopped. Through the hangar door, out past the strip, she'd seen the boat.

It was a Syndicate launch, running dark-gray through the channel with the particular arrogant speed of a vessel that owns the water it's displacing, and it was heading not for the village jetty but for the far side of the reef, where the survey stakes stood. Where the cave was.

Jonah felt the bottom drop out of the morning.

"They're early," she said. "They're not waiting for the window. They're going now, in this, because they think nobody sane would be out there to see them—and they're right, nobody sane would be." Her mind was already moving, cold and fast, laying the map over itself: the launch's speed, the tide tables pinned above the workbench, the state of the sea in the passage, the hours of daylight left. "Which means our window isn't day after tomorrow. Our window is *now*, or it's never, because by tonight that chest will be a rumor and by Friday the reef will be a secret again for another season."

Teodoro was already reaching for the tin of photographs, already becoming motion. "The longboat's at Salmah's jetty."

"Then we've got maybe twenty minutes to provision it and ten to change my mind, and I'm telling you now, Teo, I am *very good* at changing my mind." She grabbed the fuel chit list off the bench, her hand steady in the way it only went when things were worst. "Get the rope. Get the lantern. And say a prayer to whatever archive god your family kept, because we are about to file a request under the worst possible conditions."

Outside, the first rain came in off the strait—not the monsoon proper, just its advance pickets, hard and horizontal and gone again in ninety seconds, leaving the coral strip dark and steaming. The barometer needle slid another notch.

Somewhere past the reef, the black mouth of the cave was filling with gray water and gray light, and the tide, indifferent as a clerk, had begun to turn.

* * *

They ran. There was no other honest word for it. Twenty minutes became twelve because Ibu Salmah, without being asked and without asking why, put four of her grandsons on the longboat lines and had them carry it down the shingle to the water like it weighed nothing, which, for them, it nearly did. She pressed a bundle into Jonah's arms at the jetty—dried fish, a coil of fresh line, a storm lamp already filled—and said only, in the tone of a woman settling an account she'd been keeping longer than Jonah had been alive:

"Come back before the water does."

Jonah promised. It was, she reflected as she took the tiller, the first promise she'd made in five years that she had every intention of keeping, and therefore the first one that genuinely frightened her.

The passage was already ugly. The swell had come up out of the southwest in long gray ridges, not breaking yet but organizing, and the longboat climbed each one and slewed down the far side with that hollow slamming shock that Jonah felt in her spine and her fillings. Teodoro bailed without being told, scooping with a cut-down jerry can, and shouted readings to her over the noise—wind, sea state, the position of the stakes on the far reef, visible now and then between swell crests like the masts of drowned ships. Twice she lost sight of the reef entirely and navigated by the shape of the rain, which had begun again in earnest, stitching the sea white in patches.

The cave mouth appeared on the third crest and vanished on the fourth, and when they finally slid through it, into the sudden cathedral hush behind the reef, the silence hit them like a change of pressure. Their breathing echoed. Water dripped somewhere deep in the dark with the patience of a metronome counting down something neither of them wanted to name.

The causeway stones were already slick and dark, awash to the ankle in places. The iron chest sat where the scouts had left it, lid thrown back, and even from thirty feet Jonah could see it was lighter than it should have been. Emptier. They waded to it together, and Teodoro reached in with both hands and brought out—

Nothing. A wax seal, cracked, lying in the bottom like a shed skin. A scrap of wrapper paper, stamped with the founder's mark, sodden. And wedged in the corner, overlooked or discarded, a single document case, thin, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with the kind of string that archives use and no one else.

Teodoro went very still. Then he lifted the case out with the care of a man handling a relic or a wound, and turned it over, and read the label penciled on the oilcloth in a clerk's hand forty years dead.

His face did something complicated. "This is it," he whispered. "This is the index. Whatever else they took—this tells us what it was. What it all was." He looked up at her, and the lamplight and the fever in his eyes made him look briefly like a man standing in front of a fire. "They missed it. Jonah. They *missed it*."

Above them, faint through the cave mouth, carried flat across the water by the building wind, came the sound of an engine. Not theirs. Getting closer.

The tide had an hour left to turn, the swell was rising, and the Syndicate's launch was coming around the reef with its lights off.

Jonah put her hand flat against the cold iron of the chest, felt the whole weight of the season pressing down on the reef above her, and started calculating how fast a longboat could load a ghost.
