## 13. Ibu Salmah's Price

The rain had settled into its long argument with the coast by the time Jonah walked up from the waterline, and Ibu Salmah's house stood at the top of the village like a verdict—stilted above the mud, roofed in corrugated iron gone the color of old pewter, its veranda hung with drying nets that smelled of salt and patience. A kerosene lamp burned behind the mosquito mesh. Inside, someone was grinding coffee by hand, a slow circular sound like the tide itself thinking.

Jonah climbed the ladder because there was no other way up, and because she had learned years ago that you never refuse an invitation on this coast. You climbed, you sat, you drank what was poured, and you understood that every cup was an entry in somebody's ledger.

Salmah received her without surprise, which unsettled Jonah more than any ambush would have. The old woman sat cross-legged on a woven mat, her back straight as a mast step, silver hair pinned so tightly it seemed structural. She poured two glasses of the thick, bitter coffee without asking whether Jonah wanted any, and pushed one across the low table between them.

"You came back," Salmah said. In Malay first, then again in the trade pidgin they both leaned on when precision mattered. "I said you would. The others bet against me."

"Others?"

"The women of the cooperative. We gamble on everything. Weather, husbands, the price of dried squid." She sipped. "I won eleven ringgit."

Jonah wrapped her hands around the glass. The heat of it worked into her knuckles, into the ache she carried there from gripping a yoke through weather no sane pilot entered. Outside, the rain drummed the iron roof like fingers testing a lock.

"I came to ask about the cave," Jonah said. "Properly, this time."

"I know why you came." Salmah set down her glass. "You came because the men with the survey stakes paid you to come, or because the woman with the soft voice on the radio paid you to come, or because some part of you that hasn't been sold yet wanted to see it for yourself. It doesn't matter which. All three roads end at my veranda eventually."

The frankness of it landed like a slap delivered gently. Jonah had spent five years perfecting the art of being underestimated—the tired eyes, the shrug, the manifest full of small lies—and here was a grandmother dismantling her in four sentences over bad coffee.

"And which road brought you?" Salmah asked.

* * *

Jonah looked out through the mesh at the village below: forty houses on stilts, the fish sheds already patched once since the scouts started coming around, children running the plank walkways with the fearlessness of people who had never owned anything worth losing. She thought about lying. The reflex rose in her throat like bile, smooth and practiced, and she swallowed it—not out of virtue, but because she suspected, suddenly and completely, that lying to this woman would be like lying to the tide.

"Money," Jonah said. "I owe money to dangerous people. They tripled the debt and made the cave the payment. Survey it, report what's inside, touch nothing. That was the job."

"And did you touch something?"

"I went inside. With a man who can read the carvings. And then the Syndicate's scouts went inside after us and took what was in the iron chest, and I reported only part of what we found, and now I'm—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't know what I am yet. That's the honest answer."

Salmah nodded slowly, as if Jonah had recited a familiar prayer in an unfamiliar accent. "My husband used to say a pilot is just a ferryman who forgot the river. You carry things across. The question is always what you're carrying, and for whom." She refilled both glasses. "Now I will tell you what my grandmother told me, and her grandmother told her, back before anyone wrote our words down in your alphabet. You have seen the walls in the cave. The ledgers carved in stone."

"Tolls," Jonah said. "Treaties. Debts. Teodoro—he's the man who reads them—he says some go back six hundred years. Maybe further."

"Six hundred years is a number for clerks." Salmah's voice didn't rise, but something in the room tightened around it. "Here is what the number means. Every family on this coast, from Karang Laut to the river mouths, has its name in that stone. What they borrowed. What they repaid. What they still owe. When a man died, his debts were read aloud at the entrance so the whole coast would know where he stood. When a marriage joined two families, the ledgers were consulted like a priest consults scripture. The cave was not a temple, child. It was a bank. The oldest one on the water, older than any sultan's mint, older than the companies that came after with their paper and their guns."

"A bank," Jonah repeated. The word felt wrong and right at once, like a key that fit a lock it was never cut for.

"A bank that kept honest books." Salmah leaned forward. "And here is the part your Syndicate does not want written anywhere but stone. Their founder—the grandfather, the one whose portrait hangs in every office from Singapore to Penang—his grandfather's name is in those ledgers. Not as an owner. As a debtor. He borrowed from the cave, in the famine year, against land he did not yet own, and the terms are carved there in full. And the debt was never discharged. There is no release carved anywhere, because no release was ever given."

The rain filled the silence. Jonah sat very still, the way she did when flying through turbulence—loose hands, quiet breathing, waiting for the air to tell her what it wanted.

"If that's true," she said carefully, "then the land he put up as collateral—"

"Was taken when he defaulted. Or was never lawfully his to begin with. Either way, everything built on top of it inherits the flaw. Every title. Every plantation grant. Every lease the Syndicate holds, and everything the Syndicate sold or mortgaged since." Salmah spread her hands, palms up, an accountant's gesture. "You understand? If those records surface—if they are read aloud the way they were meant to be read aloud—then every land title on this coast is void. Not weakened. Void. The whole company is a house built on a loan it never repaid, and the stone remembers."

* * *

Jonah thought of Auntie Ru's floating restaurant, the lacquered tables, the tea poured with such exquisite courtesy, the arithmetic performed in a voice soft as monsoon drizzle. *The tide shows everything eventually.* She had taken it for a threat aimed at her. Now she understood it might have been a confession—that Ru knew exactly what the tide could show, and had built her whole life on keeping it buried.

"Why haven't the villages done something before now?" Jonah asked. "If everyone knows the legend—"

"Because knowing and proving are different animals, and the second one eats the first." Salmah's mouth twisted. "Who would we prove it to? The courts were built by the same companies. The magistrates took their salaries from the same purse. For three generations, the answer to the cave was to forget it, and forgetting was safer than remembering. My grandmother charged pilgrims at the entrance and told them stories about spirits, and the spirits kept the ledgers safe better than any police could. Fear is a good fence."

"But the reef opened."

"The reef opened." Salmah said it the way other people say *the war came*, or *the sickness came*. "And the fence is broken, and the men with the stakes came within a week, and the chest is gone, and now it is only a matter of time before the stones themselves are chiseled flat or drowned under concrete and called a marina." Her eyes, hooded and dark, fixed on Jonah with a weight that had physical mass. "Unless someone carries the truth out first. Properly. To people who can make it stick."

"Teodoro photographed what we could. His records, his family's knowledge—it matches the walls. He's building the case."

"His family." Something moved across Salmah's face—pity, or its harder cousin, respect. "I know that name. The Reyeses kept copies once, generations back. Copies the Syndicate burned. He told you this?"

"He told me enough."

"Then you know he is not neutral. Good. Neutral men are useless; I need a man with a wound." She waved this aside as though setting down a heavy basket. "But photographs can be called forgeries. Memories can be called madness. Stone cannot. Stone, witnessed, in place, dated by the tide itself—that is proof no lawyer can carve away. But the monsoon seals the cave within days, and after that, months. And the Syndicate will not wait politely. They have already shown you what they do to villages that stand near their questions."

The fish sheds. The wordless arithmetic of smoke and char that Jonah had flown over twice now, carrying bandages, saying nothing, hating herself in the efficient way she'd perfected—hating as a task, completed and filed.

"What are you asking me?" Jonah said, though she already knew, and the knowing sat in her chest like ballast.

Salmah rose, slow, joints cracking like rigging in a squall, and went to a lacquered chest in the corner. From beneath folded sarongs she drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the table: a strip of bark-paper, brittle as autumn, covered in dense script that was neither quite Malay nor quite anything else—a coastal hand, a merchant's shorthand, the ancestor of whatever Teodoro deciphered on the cave walls.

"My grandmother's copy of the entrance readings. Transcribed before the last time the cave sealed for a generation. It names the founder's debt. It gives the chamber, the line, the season." She pressed it flat with both palms. "It is not proof. It is a map to proof. And maps need pilots."

"I'm not a good candidate for trust, Ibu."

"No," Salmah agreed pleasantly. "You are a terrible one. You fly for smugglers and debt-holders. You lie the way other women breathe. You have carried cargo to burning villages and asked no questions, because questions are heavy and your plane has limits." Each sentence landed without cruelty, which made it worse. "And that is exactly why I ask you. The Syndicate watches honest people. They watch their own people. But you—they own your debt, so they think they own your direction. Nobody guards the dog they believe is leashed."

The word *dog* stung precisely because it was accurate. Jonah stared into her coffee.

"And if I fly out with the documents and disappear?" she asked quietly. "Sell them. Vanish. Pay my debt and start over somewhere the monsoon doesn't reach."

"Then my grandchildren will grow up on land the Syndicate stole fairly, according to paper, and I will have lost eleven ringgit betting you'd come back twice." Salmah shrugged. "I am an old woman. I have survived Dutchmen, Japanese, a revolution, three typhoons, and one husband. Do you know what all of them had in common?"

Jonah shook her head.

"They all believed everyone could be bought, and so they never saw the ones who couldn't." She slid the oilcloth bundle across the table until it touched Jonah's hands. "I am not buying you, pilot. I am trusting you. It is a different transaction. The ledger works differently. You will feel the difference in your chest, and you will not enjoy it."

Jonah looked down at the bundle. Bark-paper, oilcloth, string tied in a knot she didn't recognize. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than anything she had ever carried, including the crates she didn't open and the ones she should have.

Trust. She had dealt in every currency the coast offered—cash, contraband, silence, blood-oaths of convenience—but trust she had only ever counterfeited. Smiles that promised safety she couldn't deliver. Radio voices that swore the weather was clearing. And here was a woman who ran gambling books on her neighbors' marriages handing her the real thing, unsecured, uncollateralized, because of a bet and a feeling and perhaps because the desperate times had stripped away every other option.

"I'll need to talk to Teodoro," Jonah said. It wasn't agreement. It was the closest thing to it she could say aloud without hearing how much it cost.

"Talk to him. Tell him the Reyes name is known here, and honored, whatever his family sold in the famine year. Debts of the fathers are not debts of the sons—that is also in the stone, if he reads far enough." Salmah gathered the glasses. "One more thing. The scouts removed papers from the chest, yes? Papers can be copied, mislabeled, abandoned. Men in a hurry make poor archivists. Whatever they left behind in that cave may matter more than what they took. Remember that when the tide turns."

Outside, the rain slackened for a moment, and through the gap Jonah could see the black line of the reef, and beyond it, invisible under all that water, the mouth of the cave holding its centuries of accounts in the dark.

She climbed down the ladder with the bundle inside her jacket, against her ribs, where she kept the things she couldn't afford to lose and couldn't afford to be found with. The plank walkways gleamed under the lamplight spilling from doorways. Somewhere a child laughed, sharp and bright, and the sound followed her all the way down to the boat.

She was halfway across the anchorage before she let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the veranda:

Every escape route she had ever built ran through other people's ruin. This one, for the first time, might run through their rescue—and hers, if she could bear to let it.

The barometer at the fuel dock had fallen another two points when she checked it by flashlight. Three days, maybe four, before the airstrip at Karang Laut went under and the cave sealed for the season. And when she reached the hangar, wet to the skin, Teodoro was waiting inside with a lantern held high and an expression she'd learned to read as trouble wearing a scholar's face.

"We have a problem," he said. "The scouts are going back tomorrow. And they're taking dynamite."
