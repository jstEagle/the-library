## 15. What Teodoro Buried

The engines did not come.

That was the first mercy of the night, and like all mercies that arrive without explanation, it frightened Teodoro more than the alternative would have. He and Jonah stood at the top of the drowned causeway with the lantern held between them, listening as the sound swelled through the stone—the deep, patient thrum of longboats running heavy—and then, instead of arriving, it simply turned. Faded. Slid away along the outer reef like a hand passing over a page without reading it.

"They can't find the mouth," Jonah said at last. "Not in this. Not at night."

"Or they already know what's inside," Teodoro said, "and they're waiting for us to come out."

Neither of them said the third possibility, which was that the scouts had finished their work days ago and there was nothing left down here worth an engine's fuel. The lantern light trembled on the water below them, and the water answered with a slow, muscular swell that climbed another step of the causeway and lay there, gleaming, like something taking its ease.

They retreated. There was nowhere else to go. Back up the stair-cut passage, past the chamber where the carved ledgers ran along the walls in their ancient columns—tolls and treaties and debts, centuries of them, a civilization's whole arithmetic cut into limestone—into the last chamber, the small one, where the iron chest had been cracked open like a walnut and its contents carried off into the dark by men with cameras and crowbars. The scouts had left their camp here: a folding table, a paraffin stove still ticking with residual heat when Teodoro touched it, canvas satchels, cigarette ends ground into the sand in a neat little cemetery. And one document, mislabeled, misfiled, abandoned—a single sheet from the chest's contents that some tired man had set down under the wrong weight and never missed.

Jonah had taken it up with both hands, the way one handles a bird with a broken wing.

Now she sat on the folding table with her boots off, wringing seawater from her socks into a puddle that immediately joined the general dampness of the world, and Teodoro sat on an ammunition crate across from her, and the storm outside made itself heard through forty feet of rock as a pressure more than a sound—a rhythmic leaning against the cave, as if the monsoon were a large animal resting its shoulder against the reef to see if it would hold.

The tide rose. They could track it by the sound: the breathing of the cave, the wash in the lower passages, climbing note by note until the entrance they'd come through was sealed behind a wall of black water and the air in the chamber went close and mineral-sweet, tasting of wet stone and old salt and the paraffin stove. Trapped. Properly trapped, for six hours or eight, until the ebb.

"We should inventory what we have," Jonah said, because she was a woman who dealt with fear by making lists. "Lantern oil. The document. Your head, my hands. That's roughly it."

"And the truth," Teodoro said.

He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out the way water comes through a hull—not through a hole you can point to but through the whole fabric of the thing, everywhere at once, because the pressure outside had finally exceeded the strength of whatever had been holding it. Jonah looked up from her socks. In the lantern light her face was all planes and shadows, watchful, the face of a woman who had heard a great many confessions in her time and trusted none of them.

"Yours or mine?" she said.

"Mine." His mouth was dry. He reached for the water tin, found his hands unsteady, and set it down again without drinking. "You've earned mine first. You gave me yours on the boat coming in—or near enough. You told me about the crates."

"I told you about one crate."

"You told me enough." He looked at the lantern instead of at her. It was easier. It had always been easier; that was the whole disease of him, the family inheritance he'd sworn he'd refuse and here he was, forty years old and still reaching for the easier thing. "And I sat there and let you think you were the only one on this boat carrying something rotten. Which was its own kind of lie. So."

The storm leaned against the reef. Somewhere below, the water took another step.

"My family kept records," he began, and then stopped, because that wasn't true, not really, not the way it needed telling. He tried again. "People say *kept*, and they picture a room, shelves, dust. Respectable. Custodianship. But keeping is a verb with two hands, Jonah. One hand holds the thing safe. The other hand is free."

"The Reyeses were toll-keepers," Jonah said. "You said. Before the colonial archive absorbed the collection."

"Toll-keepers, ledger-keepers, tide-readers. We served whoever needed the coast counted. Four hundred years of it. My great-grandfather recorded debts for sultans. My grandfather recorded them for the Dutch. My father—" A breath. "My father recorded them for anyone who paid, and in the famine year, someone paid very well indeed."

The stove ticked. The lantern hissed its small yellow hymn.

"You have to understand what the famine year was," Teodoro said. "You're young enough that it's history to you. To me it's my mother's hands. Rice at four times its weight in silver, then eight, then a number nobody wrote down because writing it down would have been an admission. The coast ate its seed grain. People sold children—I knew their names, we were neighbors. And into that comes a young man from the interior with money that has no smell yet, no history, buying shipping rights, buying warehouses, buying *silence*. Everyone called him a benefactor. He bought grain barges that arrived half-empty and everyone agreed not to notice, because noticing would mean admitting the bargain, and the bargain was the only food moving."

"Harto Wibisono," Jonah said quietly.

Teodoro flinched. Even now, even here, the name landed like a hook. "You know it."

"I know the name on the Syndicate charter. I didn't know it was a man before it was a company."

"He was a man first. That's the whole horror of it—he was a man, hungry and clever, and the coast made him a company so it wouldn't have to remember his face." Teodoro's voice had gone low and flat, the voice he used for cataloguing, for entries that must be exact because feeling would ruin them. "In the third year of the famine, my father came to him with an offer. Not the other way around—that's the part the stories get wrong, if there are any stories left. My father went to *him*. Because my father had worked out something that the rest of us took years to understand: that the cave ledgers weren't just old. They were *load-bearing*. Every land title on this coast, every shipping right, every claim of ownership going back four centuries—all of it rested, somewhere in its chain of custody, on an entry in those stones. Whoever controlled the record controlled the reality. My father understood that the way a carpenter understands a joist."

"And he sold it."

"He sold a copy." The words came out of Teodoro like teeth. "A partial transcription. The founder's own debt—the original loan, the collateral terms, the whole architecture of what Harto Wibisono owed before he ever owned anything. My father copied it onto paper, sealed it, and delivered it to the young man's warehouse, and in exchange the Reyes family ate that winter. All of us. Rice in the jar, oil in the lamp, my little sister's fever bought down with proper medicine. We ate like kings for a season on the price of a secret."

Jonah had gone very still. It was a stillness Teodoro recognized from the cockpit photographs, from the way she held herself on approach in crosswinds—the stillness of a woman computing something terrible and wanting the computation done before anyone noticed she was doing it.

"Why would Wibisono want proof of his own debt?" she asked. "Why pay for the thing that could ruin him?"

"Because a debt you hold the only copy of isn't a debt," Teodoro said. "It's an asset. Think like a bookkeeper. If the cave says Harto Wibisono owes everything, and Harto Wibisono possesses the only transcription, then he owns the proof of his own ruin—and he can bury it, alter it, or produce it whenever it's useful. My father thought he was selling insurance. What he actually sold was the founding document of the Marrow Syndicate. The charter everyone treats as sacred—it's built on that transcription. Forged forward from it, entry by entry, in our own ledger style, our own notation. The Syndicate didn't steal the coast's history, Jonah. It *bought the pen*."

The lantern flame bent, though there was no wind. Or perhaps there was—the cave breathed, the sea moved somewhere beneath them, and every flame in the world bends when the world inhales.

"What happened to your family?" Jonah asked. She asked it gently, but he heard the question underneath it, the one she couldn't help: *what happened to everyone who touches this?*

"You know what happened. You've seen the pattern your whole flying life—you just didn't know it had a first instance." Teodoro folded his hands so they'd stop shaking. "For three years we were comfortable, and for three years my father told himself the transaction was complete, clean, deniable. Then Wibisono's operation grew past needing intermediaries. A man who owns the record doesn't need the family that used to keep it. There was a fire in the archive house in 'thirty-nine—officially a lamp, officially an accident. My grandfather's collection, the originals, the master indexes: gone. My uncle, who'd begun asking questions about the famine-year transcription, went into the harbor the same month. Officially a drunk. My father never asked questions again. He swept floors for the colonial archive until he died, a man who had once been the most dangerous bookkeeper on the coast, sweeping floors, keeping his eyes down, teaching me the notation in whispers because he couldn't bear for me not to know it and couldn't bear for me to know it."

"And you," Jonah said.

"I became a clerk." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked thing. "I became exactly what he was. I filed papers for the same archive that had eaten our collection. I spent nineteen years within arm's reach of the truth and I touched none of it, because touching it meant becoming my uncle, and my uncle was in the harbor. Loyalty, Jonah. That's what I called it. Loyalty to the living. My mother died believing we'd survived because we'd been sensible. Sensible. That was the word on her deathbed, like a prayer. *We were sensible.*"

The storm pressed. Below them the water climbed, patient as interest accruing.

"But you're here," Jonah said. "Sensible men don't chase tail numbers across a coastline."

"No." He looked at her then, finally, because this part belonged to her—she was the reason he was saying any of it, the woman who had flown him into the dark on a stranger's promise and a stolen chart. "Three years ago I found a manifest. Shipping records, pre-war, misfiled in a box of rubber quotas. A plane's original registry, transferred through three owners, and the transfer stamped by a surveyor who'd once flown coastal cargo for a company that didn't exist on paper. Your plane, Jonah. Tail number and all. And beside it, in the margin, in my family's own notation—a reference mark. A cave. Coordinates disguised as a tide-table erratum. Someone in the chain had known, and written it down the way we always wrote things down: sideways, deniable, waiting."

"So you hunted me."

"I hunted a ghost with a propeller." He almost smiled. "I expected a smuggler. I expected to buy access, or steal it. I did not expect—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "I did not expect to find someone else flying away from a question. But I recognized it. That's why I cornered you at the fuel dock instead of reporting you to Ru's people, though God knows they'd have paid me. I saw a woman doing arithmetic she hated, and I thought: *there. That's the family business. That's the inheritance.* We keep accounts we can't bear, and we call the bearing loyalty."

The word hung between them. *Loyalty.* Outside, the monsoon threw its whole weight against the reef, and the cave groaned like a ship, and neither of them moved.

"You want to unwrite it," Jonah said. "Your part in it."

"I want to *write it*," Teodoro said. "There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. Unwriting is what Wibisono did—what the Syndicate does, what fire does. Burning a record is still a relationship with the record. It's letting the lie win by destroying the evidence of the lie. No. I want it entered. I want the famine-year sale written into the public account alongside everything else—my father's crime, my uncle's death, my nineteen years of sensible silence. The cave doesn't need me to defend it. Stone keeps better books than men. What it needs is a witness willing to sign his own name to the ugliest entry." His voice caught, and he let it. "I've been seeking the cave for years, but I think—I think I've been seeking the cave since I was nine years old, watching my father sweep floors, understanding even then that our survival was purchased with something, and never once being allowed to see the bill."

Silence. The stove had gone cold; the lantern burned lower; the tide spoke in the deep places with a voice like a ledger being thumbed.

Jonah got up. He thought she was going to check the water line, and she did glance at it—but then she crossed the chamber and sat down beside him on the ammunition crate, close enough that their shoulders touched, and said nothing at all for a long moment. It was, he understood, the largest gift she knew how to give. A woman who navigated by denial, sitting in the full weather of his confession without turning away from it.

"My father flew mail," she said at last, quietly. "Before the war. Before everything. He used to say a pilot's real cargo is always invisible—weight and balance, you count what you can't see or you die." Her jaw tightened. "I've been counting wrong for five years."

"We both have."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Maybe that's the thing. Maybe there's no *wrong*. Maybe there's just the debt, and the debt compounds either way—yours in silence, mine in altitude. Same ledger. Different columns." She looked at him, and in the guttering light her eyes were fierce and exhausted and, unbelievably, amused. "Loyalty and truth. My aunt used to say they were the same animal seen from different sides of a river."

"Then we've been swimming," Teodoro said, "in opposite directions, toward the same bank."

Something passed between them then—not forgiveness, nothing so settled, but recognition, the grim comfort of two debtors comparing notes in the night. He felt lighter. Not absolved; nothing would ever make him absolved, and he no longer wanted to be. Lighter the way a boat is lighter after you've thrown the ballast you were pretending was treasure.

He reached into his shirt and drew out the mislabeled document, the orphaned sheet from the iron chest, and unfolded it on his knee between them. The old notation marched down its margins in columns fine as stitching. His finger found a line, then another, tracing the chain backward through forged entries to the root.

"It's here," he murmured. "The founder's original debt. Collateral terms. This sheet alone, properly entered, starts the unraveling. And somewhere above us, in the rain, are men with the rest of it in waterproof cases, and engines in the channel, and orders." He looked up at her. "Six hours until the ebb. When it turns, we go out through a rising sea in a holed boat, and everything we do after that is war."

Jonah took the document's edge in her fingers, careful as with the broken-winged thing it was.

"Then we'd better decide," she said, "what we're willing to burn to carry it out."

And far below them, through the living stone, the water took another step—and this time, faintly, unmistakably, from the direction of the sealed entrance, came the knock of oars against a hull.
