## 16. What Jonah Buries

The water in the bilge had stopped rising. That was the only good news Jonah had, and she held onto it the way a drowning man holds a plank—gratefully, and without any illusions about what it meant. The longboat sat wedged against the causeway where the reef had holed it, tilted at an angle that made everything in the cave feel slightly wrong, like furniture in a house where someone had died. Their lantern threw a circle of amber light no wider than a grave.

Outside, the monsoon worked the reef like a debt collector working a debtor. Rain came down in sheets that sounded less like weather and more like arithmetic—thousands of small calculations striking the stone, each one asking *how much do you owe, how much do you owe, how much do you owe.*

Jonah had been counting her own debts for so long she'd stopped hearing the question. Now, trapped in a cathedral of other people's accounts—centuries of tolls and treaties carved into walls that predated every ledger she'd ever falsified—the question was all she could hear.

Teodoro Reyes sat across from her on the stone causeway, his back against a column of carved script, his spectacles fogged from the lantern's heat. He'd been quiet for a long time. Not the comfortable quiet of two people who've run out of things to say, but the loaded quiet of two people who each have one thing left to say and are both waiting to see who flinches first.

He'd gone first. Last night, or what passed for night down here—a tide-trapped interval measured in lantern oil rather than hours—he had told her about the famine year. About his grandfather selling a partial copy of the family's records to a man named Hartono, whose name would later sit at the top of the Marrow Syndicate's charter. About the betrayal that followed, the fire, the confiscated archive, the forty years Teodoro had spent chasing the smoke of his own inheritance.

*Your turn,* his silence said now. He wasn't rude enough to say it aloud. He just sat there being a man who had handed over his worst thing, and waited.

Jonah found a loose chip of stone near her boot and turned it over in her fingers. It was smooth on one side, rough on the other, like everything else in her life.

"You already know most of it," she said finally. "You saw the manifest. You knew my tail number before you ever knew my name."

"I knew the plane," Teodoro said. "Planes are honest. They carry what they carry." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his shirt hem, a gesture she'd come to recognize as his version of clearing his throat. "The pilot is another matter."

"The crates," Jonah said. "The ones I told you were vaccines."

Teodoro didn't nod. He just went very still, the way men do when they've been waiting for a sound and finally hear it.

"For five years I've flown for Auntie Ru. For the Syndicate, before I understood that's what it was—at least, before I let myself understand it. And for five years there have been two kinds of cargo on my manifests. The kind with labels, and the kind without." She set the stone chip down on the causeway with exaggerated care, as if it might detonate. "The labeled ones were mostly honest work. Vaccines upriver. Rice seed. A midwife once, and her entire goat, which is a story I'll tell you someday when we're not sitting in a flooded temple of debts. I told myself the unlabeled ones didn't matter. Small weight. Sealed crates. Paperwork that said *agricultural equipment* or *textile samples* or nothing at all. I never asked. That was the rule. Never carry what you can't deny."

"And you carried it anyway."

"I carried it because denying it paid better than knowing it." She heard how it sounded the moment it left her mouth, and hated it, and kept going, because stopping now would be worse than any of it. "There's a village up the Kapuas tributary. Kampung Teluk. You won't find it on most charts anymore. Two years ago I flew a crate in there—unlabeled, heavier than it should have been, packed with something that sloshed. Fuel of some kind, I told myself. Generator fuel. Villages need generators." Her voice did something strange on the word *generators*, thinned out like fabric worn through. "Three weeks later the strip I'd landed on was ash. The whole settlement. Fish sheds, longhouses, the school. The radio traffic called it an accident. Cooking fire, dry season, wind from the east."

The rain filled the pause. Down the tunnel toward the lower chambers, water moved with a patience that felt deliberate.

"Did you go back?" Teodoro asked quietly.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because going back means finding out." She looked at him then, straight on, and let him see all of it—the thing she'd been flying away from for two years, doing a hundred knots, staying one airstrip ahead. "And I already know the answer, Teo. That's the part I've never said out loud to anyone. I don't need to confirm anything. I know what an unlabeled crate full of accelerant does when it arrives three weeks before a village burns. I know what it means that the Syndicate's scouts had surveyed that strip a month earlier. I flew the matchbook into the room and then I flew away before anyone struck it, and I've spent two years telling myself I don't know if I'm an arsonist."

Teodoro's face didn't change. That was the thing about him—she'd learned it on the boat, learned it in the cave. He didn't flinch at confessions. He received them the way the stone walls received the tide: completely, without judgment, and with a memory that outlasted everything.

"My grandfather sold paper," he said at last. "You delivered it. We're the same crime at different altitudes."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you accurate." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lantern caught the gray in his beard. "I spent forty years telling myself my family were martyrs. Keepers of truth, burned by villains. And some of that was true. But underneath it, we were brokers. We sold access to the past, and we chose our buyers by their purse, not their conscience. My grandfather knew what Hartono wanted those records for. Any man with eyes knew. He sold anyway, because the famine year makes monsters reasonable, and then he spent the rest of his life calling himself a victim." He tapped the wall behind him, and the carved script clicked under his knuckle like an abacus. "These ledgers don't distinguish between the man who writes the debt and the man who carries it. Look at them. Tolls, treaties, loans, betrayals—all of it recorded in the same hand, generation after generation. The stone doesn't care who's guilty. It only cares what happened."

"What happened," Jonah repeated. It came out flat.

"What happened is that you flew a crate to Kampung Teluk, and you don't know what was in it, and you could find out." He said it gently. That was the cruelty of Teodoro Reyes—he wielded gentleness like a pry bar. "Radio logs. Shipping manifests. The colonial records still hold copies of freight declarations from that corridor—I've seen the filing system. If your crate was agricultural equipment, there'll be a customs stamp. If it was fuel, someone signed for it under a false category, and false categories leave fingerprints. I could find them. It's what I do."

"And if the fingerprint is mine?"

"Then it's yours," he said simply. "And you'll have done what my grandfather never did. You'll have read your own entry in the ledger instead of burning the page."

Jonah got up. There wasn't far to go—the causeway ran maybe thirty meters between the boat and the black water of the inner pool—but walking helped, the way it always helped, motion as anesthesia. She stood at the edge of the pool and watched the tide breathe in the entrance tunnel, slow and enormous, the way a sleeping animal breathes.

"I used to think the rules kept me alive," she said, to the water more than to him. "Never land twice in the same place. Never carry what you can't deny. Never owe anyone more than you can outrun. Five years of rules, and you know what I figured out, sitting in that floating restaurant while Auntie Ru poured tea? The rules weren't keeping me alive. They were keeping me moving. There's a difference. Alive is a state. Moving is just a direction, and I picked the direction that pointed away from Kampung Teluk."

"Away from the question."

"Away from every question." She laughed, and it came out wrong, too short. "Do you know what I did when I saw the reef open? When Ibu Salmah told me about the cave? For about four seconds I thought, *there it is, the biggest secret on this coast, and I'm the only outsider who knows.* Four seconds of feeling like I'd won something. And then I realized what I actually felt, underneath. Relief. Because a bigger secret meant my secrets got smaller. Everyone was looking at the cave, and nobody was looking at me." She shook her head. "That's the arithmetic I've been running my whole life. Keep the ledger crowded enough and no auditor ever finds your line item."

Teodoro rose too, slowly, joints complaining the way joints do after a night on stone. He came and stood beside her at the pool's edge—not close, but close enough that the lantern light held them both.

"You know what I keep thinking about," he said. "Down here. In all this." He gestured at the walls, the tiers of carved script rising into the dark above the lantern's reach. "My family kept copies of these records for two hundred years. Paper copies. And every copy we made was a little wrong. A smudged character. A skipped line. A scribe's tired eye. By the time my grandfather sold his partial to Hartono, it was wrong in ways nobody could measure, because the original was locked in a chest at the bottom of a cave only the sea could open." His voice dropped. "And then the sea opened it. One freak tide, and suddenly there's an original again. Suddenly every forgery built on top of it—the Syndicate's charter, the land titles, forty years of *we own this coast*—is standing on sand. Do you understand what that means for people like us?"

"People like us," Jonah said. "Liars."

"Witnesses." He almost smiled. "A liar lies to change what happened. A witness just says it. The Syndicate has spent decades making sure no witnesses survived long enough to compare notes. And now there are two of us, sitting in the original, and neither of us can leave until the tide turns, and we've both just handed the other our worst page." He turned to look at her fully. "So here's my proposal, and it's not a kind one. When we get out—and we will, the ebb comes in six hours, I've been watching the tunnel—we find out what was in your crate. All of it. Customs stamps, freight logs, whatever survives. And whatever we find, it goes in the report. Not Auntie Ru's report. The real one. The one that eventually exists somewhere Ru can't buy the ink."

"And if it convicts me?"

"Then you'll be the first honest criminal on this coast," Teodoro said, "and probably the last. And you'll sleep, Jonah. God help me, but I think you'll actually sleep."

She wanted to tell him he was naive. She wanted to tell him that confession wasn't currency out there, that the world outside this cave ran on Auntie Ru's arithmetic, where truth was just a commodity with bad liquidity. She opened her mouth to say all of it—

And stopped. Because she realized, standing in a ten-thousand-year-old ledger with seawater climbing the walls and a holed boat and a man who had given her his family's shame like a key, that she couldn't remember the last time she'd slept more than three hours. Couldn't remember the last time she'd flown anywhere without checking her mirrors twice. Couldn't remember the last time the rules had felt like anything but a corridor with the doors bricked up behind her.

"I flew medicine too," she said quietly. It wasn't a defense. It was just the rest of the inventory. "Same plane. Same hands. Sometimes the same day. I want that in the record too, if there's ever a record. Whatever I am, I'm both cargoes."

"I know," Teodoro said. "I've known since the fuel dock. Nobody carries vaccines and contraband in the same aircraft unless they're trying to balance a scale." He paused. "The question is what side the scale tips."

The lantern guttered, and Jonah knelt to feed it oil, and for a moment her hands were busy and her mouth was free to say nothing. The tide breathed in the tunnel. Somewhere above them, muffled by thirty meters of stone and a wall of rain, an engine droned—distant, uncertain, maybe a fishing boat, maybe not. Neither of them looked up. They were done performing vigilance for each other. That was the strangest gift of the whole drowned night: two professionals in the art of concealment, mutually disarmed, sitting in the one place on earth where concealment had never once worked. The stone remembered everything. You could carve a lie into it, but you'd have to chisel it yourself, and even then the chisel marks would tell.

"Teo," she said, when the flame steadied. "If we get out of here, and we start pulling threads—your archive, my manifests, all of it—it ends with testimony. You know that. Somebody stands in a room and says the true thing out loud, and the Syndicate stops pretending to be a business and starts acting like what it is. And the person saying the true thing will be me. My name, my license, my crates. Ru won't survive that, but neither will my flying days. Maybe not my freedom."

"I know."

"And you're asking me to do it anyway."

"I'm asking you to stop running from the answer you already have," Teodoro said. "What you do with it afterward—that's yours. But you can't spend your whole life paying interest on a question you refuse to read." He nodded at the walls around them, at the centuries of entries, the debts of the living recorded in stone by hands that had never heard of the Marrow Syndicate and would never know what grew from their careful bookkeeping. "Everyone in here is just a name and a number, Jonah. But somebody wrote them. Somebody chose to write them down instead of letting the tide take it. That choice is the whole coast's inheritance. Good and bad. It's the only reason we know anything."

The engine sound faded. Or they stopped listening. Hard to say which.

Jonah looked down into the black water of the inner pool, at her own reflection broken by ripples—half a face, half a shadow, the way she'd always seen herself. Two cargoes. One pilot.

"When the ebb comes," she said, "we go back for the chest documents the scouts missed. And then—" She exhaled, long and slow, the way she did before committing to a landing in weather no sane pilot would accept. "Then I file a report. The real one. Everything. Even the parts that hang me."

Teodoro didn't celebrate. He just reached over and squeezed her shoulder once, hard, the way you grip a strut to test whether it will bear weight. It would. It would have to.

Above them, faint through the stone, the rain changed its rhythm—easing, gathering, turning. The tide was thinking about turning too. And far off across the drowned channel, where Karang Laut's fish sheds stood dark against the squall, the first grey suggestion of dawn began the long work of deciding what kind of day this coast was going to have.

* * *

They spent the waiting hours reading.

It was Teodoro's idea, and it was either wisdom or madness—sitting in a flooding vault, transcribing by lantern light the passages his family had spent two centuries copying and forty years losing. He worked slowly, lips moving, translating the old script into whispers Jonah half-caught: grain debts remitted in a good year; a treaty between two river clans sealed with a marriage; a toll dispute settled by a wrestling match, of all things. Centuries of ordinary life, bookkept in stone.

"Here," he said suddenly, and went still.

Jonah leaned over his shoulder. The passage was newer than the others—two hundred years old instead of a thousand, the carving shallower, the hand less sure. A date. A name. *Hartono*, in the old orthography, and beside it a sum, and beside the sum a word Teodoro translated twice to be certain of it.

"*Collateral*," he whispered. "It says collateral. Jonah—this is a loan record. Someone lent Hartono something, and this is the security." His finger traced below the entry, where the script continued for another dozen lines, dense and urgent, the carver pressing hard. "This is why the Syndicate's charter matters. This is why they'll burn villages for a cave full of rocks. If this entry says what I think it says—"

The lantern flickered. From the direction of the entrance tunnel came a sound that was not the tide and not the rain: the wet slap of oars, and voices, low and coordinated, coming closer through the dark.

Jonah killed the lantern with her palm.

In the sudden blackness, she felt Teodoro's hand find her sleeve, and she felt the stone around them holding its breath, ten thousand years of everyone's debts keeping theirs.
