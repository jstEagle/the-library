## 17. Out on the Ebb

**Teodoro**

The water began to leave them before dawn, the way it always did—without apology, without ceremony, simply withdrawing its claim inch by inch until the world they had been drowning in became a world they could walk through again.

Teodoro had not slept. Neither of them had. The lantern between them had burned down to a thumb's width of oil, and in its last amber light he had watched Jonah bail with a rhythm that never faltered and never quickened, a metronome set to some tempo only she could hear. Her hands were cracked from salt. She had not once complained.

Now the tide was on the ebb, and the longboat sat tilted on the causeway like a beached animal, holed below the waterline where the coral had taken its toll. Teodoro crouched beside the gash with his sleeves rolled and his fingers inside the wound of it, feeling the shape of their problem. Two fingers wide. A splintered lip of planking driven inward. Not fatal. Not nothing.

"We patch it or we swim," he said.

Jonah looked up from the documents she had been wrapping in oilcloth—the mislabeled copies the scouts had abandoned, the ones that might be worthless and might be everything—and her expression was the one he was beginning to know best: the face of a woman doing arithmetic she refused to show anyone else.

"We patch it," she said. "I don't swim in water with things that have opinions."

He almost laughed. He hadn't laughed in years, properly, and the sound that came out of him surprised them both—a short, rusty bark, half cough, as if his body had forgotten the mechanism and was improvising.

They worked by lantern light while the sea drained away around them, exposing the causeway stone in long black shelves slick with weed. The sailcloth came from the boat's own kit, stiff with mildew, smelling of a decade of folded storage. Jonah cut a patch with a knife she kept sharper than any tool had a right to be, and Teodoro held the planking steady while she drove the tacks home with the flat of a wrench, each blow ringing off the hull and out across the exposed flats like a bell rung in an empty church.

"Prayer," she said, when the last tack went in. "You said sailcloth and prayer. Where's your prayer?"

Teodoro pressed his palm flat against the patched wood. It was ugly work. It would hold or it wouldn't, and no amount of theology would change which. But he thought of his grandmother, who had kept the family archive through two wars and one confiscation, and who used to say that a record survived not because it was protected but because someone, somewhere, at some cost, had decided it should.

"I prayed forty years ago," he said. "For this. I'm only now collecting."

Jonah studied him for a moment. In the grey pre-light her face lost its competence and showed what lived underneath it, which was exhaustion, and under that, something rawer that she kept banked like a fire in wet fuel.

"That's not prayer," she said. "That's stubbornness with a liturgy."

"It's the same thing," he said. "Ask anyone who's ever kept books against an empire."

* * *

**Jonah**

The tide turned them loose at first light, and they went with it.

Jonah took the tiller because taking the tiller was the one thing in the world she still trusted herself to do. The patched hull groaned beneath them like an old man climbing stairs, but it groaned evenly, honestly, without the intermittent suck of water finding new ways in, and she decided—provisionally, the way she decided everything—that they would live.

The channel opened ahead of them in the monsoon's grey half-dark, the water the color of hammered tin, the mangroves on either hand standing knee-deep in their own reflections. Behind them the reef was already drinking the tide back in, the cave mouth swallowing itself inch by inch, keeping its ledgers dry for another season. She did not look at it. Looking felt like asking permission.

What she felt instead was something she had no ledger entry for. Lightness. Actual, physical lightness, as if five years of cargo had been stacked in her chest and someone had finally begun unloading it. They were fugitives now. That was the truth of the morning, and she found—to her genuine surprise, sitting there with salt in her hair and a boat held together by sailcloth and stubbornness—that the truth fit better than the lies had. Lies chafed. Truth, even dangerous truth, even ruinous truth, sat close against the skin like something tailored.

"You're smiling," Teodoro said. He said it carefully, the way a man touches a wire to see if it's live.

"I'm noting an anomaly," she said. "Don't make anything of it."

"The documents are real. We have them. The Syndicate doesn't know yet." He counted them off on his fingers like a man reading a manifest. "By every measure I know, this is the worst morning of your life."

"And yet."

"And yet."

She let the boat run. The ebb carried them seaward, past fishing stakes leaning at drunken angles, past a floating shrine nobody maintained anymore, its little roof green with moss, its offering bowl filled to the brim with rainwater and one white hibiscus going soft at the edges. Someone had left it recently. Someone on this coast still believed the old accounts mattered. Jonah looked at the flower and thought about Ibu Salmah's voice, low and certain: *the cave keeps the debts of the living.* She had been carrying that sentence for days now like a stone in her shoe, and she had stopped trying to shake it out.

Then the rain wall hit.

There was no warning worth the name. One moment the horizon was a bruised smear; the next it was gone entirely, replaced by a vertical grey country moving toward them at the speed of weather, and Jonah had flown into enough storm fronts to read one coming over open water the way other people read a face. This one was not coming. It was arriving. It had the particular confidence of a monsoon's first true assault, the season announcing itself, planting its flag.

"Hold everything," she said. "Documents under the thwart. Now."

The wall took them the way a wave takes a sandcastle—not violently at first, then all at once. The wind arrived before the rain, flattening the channel surface to a shudder, and then the rain came down in ropes, in sheets, in columns, a density of water that turned the world to white noise and white light. Visibility collapsed to twenty feet. Thirty, if you knew where to look. The tiller went heavy in her hands. The patched hull, God help them, held—she could hear it holding, feel it holding, the groan shifting pitch but not splitting—and for one full minute she did nothing but steer and exist, steering and existing, the two activities suddenly identical.

And inside that minute, inside the hammering white dark, she felt it again. Exhilaration. Real exhilaration, the kind she hadn't felt since her first solo flight at nineteen, since the last time she'd done something purely because it was hers to do. They were running. They were running *from* something, yes—but for the first time in five years she was also running *toward* something, and the difference was a physical sensation, a loosening in the shoulders, a steadying of the hands. Fugitives, and lighter for it. She'd have laughed again if laughing wouldn't have meant swallowing half the South China Sea.

"Teodoro," she shouted over the din, "tell me you're bailing!"

"I'm bailing!"

"Tell me you're bailing *enthusiastically!*"

"I'm bailing with religious fervor!"

She steered by dead reckoning and memory, which was to say she steered by faith, holding the channel's general heading and trusting the mangrove walls to announce themselves before they ate the boat. And for perhaps ten minutes it worked. Ten minutes of pure motion, pure flight, the boat planing on the ebb current with the rain curtain streaming off the bow like torn silk—

—and then the rain thinned, just slightly, just enough, and the channel widened, and she saw them.

Three boats. Grey-hulled, low-slung, engines idling, sweeping the channel in a line abreast like men beating a field for game. Syndicate boats. She knew the silhouette the way she knew her own registration numbers—knew it from years of pretending not to notice, from years of flying cargo for the people who owned those hulls. They were quartering the water methodically. Sweeping. The way you sweep when you've lost something small and valuable and you have all day and all the money in the world to find it.

The exhilaration died so fast it left a taste behind, metallic, like biting foil.

"They're not looking for us," Teodoro said beside her, and she couldn't tell if it was hope or arithmetic. "They can't know yet. The scouts are still inside the cave, or—they were—"

"They're looking for a boat leaving the reef," Jonah said quietly. "Doesn't matter whose. Doesn't matter why. Ru sweeps channels the way other people sweep floors." She eased the tiller over, feathering them toward the mangrove fringe, cutting their wake, killing their noise. "Get down. Both of us get down."

The boats were a mile off. Maybe less. In this visibility a mile was a country and thirty feet was a room, and the question was which side of that arithmetic the rain would keep them on. Jonah brought the longboat into the mangroves the way she landed planes on tidal strips—slow, committed, reading the water under the hull by feel alone—and killed the engine, and let the current carry them the last few yards into the hanging green silence.

The roots closed around them like ribs. Above, the canopy took the rain and broke it into a thousand smaller rains, drumming on leaves, dripping from twigs, a soft constant percussion that would cover the sound of breathing if they breathed quietly enough.

Through a gap in the roots, she watched the sweep pass.

The middle boat came closest. Close enough that she could see the men aboard—four of them, ponchos dark with rain, one at the bow with a pair of binoculars he wasn't really using because nobody uses binoculars in a rain wall, one aft at the engine, two amidships with the loose, bored readiness of hired muscle waiting for something to happen. The boat's wake rolled into the mangroves and slapped against the roots a few feet from the hull, and Jonah put her hand flat on the gunwale and felt her own pulse knocking back at her, and did not move, and did not blink, and watched.

Beside her, Teodoro had gone very still. Stillness was his native language; she was learning that. He held the wrapped documents against his chest with both arms, the way you hold a child in a crowd, and his eyes were fixed on the middle boat with an intensity that had nothing to do with fear of capture. Something else was working behind his face. She recognized it, because she'd seen it in mirrors for years: the look of a man cataloguing faces.

"Do you know them?" she whispered.

"No," he whispered back. Then, after a moment, with terrible honesty: "But I know the type. I've spent forty years knowing the type."

The boat passed. The wake subsided. The rain kept falling, softer now, settled in for the long haul, and somewhere beyond the mangroves the other two boats continued their sweep southward, engines droning, patient as bookkeepers.

Jonah exhaled breath she felt she'd been holding since the cave.

"They'll come back," she said. "Sweeps come back. It's the whole point of a sweep." She looked at the documents against Teodoro's chest, at the water seeping slowly, patiently, into the bilge beneath their feet, at the grey day closing around them like a fist deciding how hard to squeeze. "We can't stay in the mangroves. We can't take the channel. And my plane is parked on a strip Ru's people watch the way priests watch relics."

Teodoro shifted the documents, protecting them from a drip finding its way through the canopy, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the arithmetic hadn't already said.

"So," Jonah said, and heard her own voice find the flat, clear register she used for radio calls in bad weather, the voice that didn't shake because shaking it couldn't afford. "We need a strip the Syndicate isn't watching. We need it tonight. And we need it within walking distance of a man who owes me fish."

Teodoro looked at her. "Ibu Salmah."

"Ibu Salmah," Jonah said, and reached for the engine cord, and the mangroves dripped around them like a slow clock counting down to whatever came next.
