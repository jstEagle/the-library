## 18. The Channel Hunt

The rain did not fall so much as arrive. One moment Jonah was steering the patched longboat through a brown corridor of water with the sky still visible above the mangrove crowns; the next, the world contracted to the width of her two hands and the sound of Teodoro bailing behind her.

"Keep it straight," she shouted, though she couldn't hear herself. She felt him shift his weight instead—felt the boat answer—and steered by that, by the small corrections of a man learning to be cargo.

The channel was barely wider than the boat. Mangrove roots rose out of the water on both sides like the ribs of something drowned and enormous, black and knuckled, furred green at the waterline where the mud breathed. In clear weather she would have called it beautiful. Now it was simply a throat, and she was being swallowed down it at speed while somewhere behind them an engine with more horsepower than sense tried to follow.

She had counted three boats at the mouth of the delta. Syndicate hulls, painted the gray of wet concrete, running without lights because they assumed no one sane ran these channels blind. They were right about the sanity. But sanity was a luxury priced for people who hadn't spent five years learning what a river could forgive.

*Never land twice in the same place.* That was rule one, and it had kept her alive since she was twenty-three years old. But rules one through nine had all been written for a woman with a working airplane, and her airplane was parked on a strip forty miles north that Auntie Ru's people were almost certainly watching by now. So this morning's arithmetic had only one column: get off the water, get to cover, and figure out how to steal back her own life from under their eyes.

The first trick was the fork.

Every river on this coast forked eventually, and every fork looked identical in the rain: two brown throats, equally wide, equally dark, diverging at an angle too shallow to judge at speed. The Syndicate pilots knew the main channels—the ones on the charts, the ones the loggers used. Jonah knew the others. Not from charts. From five years of delivering vaccines to villages that didn't exist on any map worth printing, flying low enough to read the water like text, noting which channels silted shut in dry season, which ones held depth under the roots, which ones the crocodiles preferred and therefore which ones no boat with a motor ever entered twice.

She took the fork left. The wrong fork, by every chart. The right fork, by everything she knew.

Teodoro, hunched over the bail bucket, looked up once. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure I'm not sure," she said, "and neither are they."

That was the whole art of it. You didn't outrun a pursuit boat in a holed longboat patched with sailcloth and prayer. You made *them* unsure. Every second they spent slowing at a fork, sounding the depth, arguing about the chart—that was a second you spent putting brown water between your stern and their bow.

The channel narrowed until the roots closed overhead, a tunnel of arched wood dripping rain. Jonah killed the engine.

The silence hit like cold water. Under it: rain on leaves, a thousand small drums. Their own wake slapping the roots. And far behind, faint but unmistakable, the other engine—still coming, still committed, still on the main channel they'd refused.

"Drift," she whispered. "Paddle when I say."

They drifted. The current here was lazy, tidal, undecided—it couldn't make up its mind which way to run, so it ran nowhere, and the boat sat in the middle of that indecision like a leaf. Jonah shipped the paddle and eased them along by feel, fingertips reading the roots the way a blind man reads a wall. Once her hand found a root scarred smooth in a long groove—a marker, some old boatman's knife-work, a signpost from before charts. She didn't know what it said. But someone had wanted it known, and that was enough company for now.

The engine behind grew louder, then hesitated, then died to an idle. They'd reached the fork. She could picture the men in the gray hull: rain hammering the canopy, chart useless, two throats of water exactly alike, and orders from someone in an office who had never been rained on in his life.

"They'll split," Teodoro murmured. He had stopped bailing. He understood now that quiet was survival.

"Then we've already lost half of them," Jonah said. "And the half that guesses right will guess slow."

* * *

They went on by paddle and pole for an hour, maybe two. Time dissolved in the rain; it had no edges. Jonah navigated by accumulation—this bend matched a memory from a medicine run two monsoons ago, that fallen trunk she'd photographed from the air because it looked like a sleeping animal, the village dog that always barked at her plane and whose bark she could not hear now but whose village she steered wide of, because a village meant people, and people meant questions, and questions meant someone would talk.

Not because they wanted to. Because everyone talked eventually. She had learned that from the inside.

Her hands blistered on the paddle. Her shoulders burned in a way that felt honest, at least—honest work, honest pain, nothing hidden in it. She thought about that too long. About how much of her life had been dishonest pain, pain with a lie wrapped around it, the ache of pretending the unlabeled crates weighed nothing on her conscience.

"You're favoring your left," Teodoro said quietly.

"I'm fine."

"You're favoring your left." He took the paddle from her hand without asking, the way he'd begun doing small things without asking, and she let him because arguing cost breath she needed. He paddled badly but steadily, a clerk's stroke, metronomic, and somehow that was worse for the blisters than her own grip and better for the boat.

Somewhere in the next hour the rain thinned—not stopped, never stopped, but thinned to a steady gray weave instead of a wall—and the world gained ten meters of visibility, then twenty. Enough to see the channel opening ahead into a flooded clearing where a dead tree stood up out of the water like a gallows. Enough to see, on its far side, the roofline of a stilt house she recognized.

Old Weng's place. Fisherman, smuggler, occasional patient. A man who owed her for three years of insulin delivered without question or receipt, and who had never once asked what else might have been under the vaccine boxes in her hold. She had told herself that was trust. She knew now it was complicity, and that the difference between those two words was the width of a payment schedule.

But today, complicity was shelter.

She brought the boat in under his house, into the dry lattice of pilings and fish traps, and killed what little noise they made. Old Weng came down the ladder without hurry, looked at the boat, looked at her face, and said only: "How many?"

"Three boats. Maybe four by now."

He nodded as if she'd told him the tide table. "Rice is on. Come up when the light goes."

* * *

They waited out the daylight in the dark under the house, which sounded like a paradox and was simply the coast. Jonah lay on planks slick with salt and listened to the water rise and fall beneath her, and did arithmetic.

It was bad arithmetic. She kept doing it anyway, the way you keep touching a bruise.

Option one: wait out the watch on her airstrip. But the watch wouldn't wait out the monsoon. The Syndicate didn't need to catch her plane—they only needed to sit on it until the storm sealed the coast, and then it wouldn't matter whose name was on the registration. An airplane underwater is nobody's airplane. And she had seen what Ru's people did to grounded assets before. She'd flown the insurance photographs, once, in the early years, before she understood what she was looking at. A Cessna in a hangar, burned so thoroughly the investigators wrote *electrical*. She had carried those photographs to Jakarta and been paid in crisp bills and a compliment on her punctuality.

Option two: go overland, abandon the plane, buy passage south on some tramp freighter, start again under another name in another port. It was the move five-years-ago Jonah would have made without losing sleep. She had done it twice already; there were two other names folded into the lining of her flight bag like pressed flowers. But the documents were in the bag with the names, wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, and she had finally understood—somewhere in the cave, or in the burning fish sheds, or in Salmah's grandson's bandages, she honestly couldn't say which—that running with these papers was just another form of burying them. She had spent five years becoming very good at disappearing. Disappearing was the one skill the Syndicate respected and the one thing she could no longer afford to do.

Option three: take the strip by force of nerve. Land-side approach, night, rain, one guard post, two men if they were cheap, four if Ru was serious, and Ru was always serious. Fly out through weather that had already eaten two charter flights this season. With a tail number the Syndicate had memorized before she had.

Every option ended in a bad word. *Burned.* *Drowned.* *Caught.* *Buried.*

"There's a fourth option," Teodoro said, from across the dark. He said it carefully, the way he said everything now, as though words were specimens he didn't want to damage. "You haven't considered it because it isn't yours."

"Try me."

"We don't take the plane out. We let them keep watching it. We let them watch it until watching it becomes the thing they're doing instead of looking for us." He turned over, slowly, on the planks. "A watched plane is a fixed point. Everything about the Syndicate is built on fixing points—fixed debts, fixed ledgers, fixed people. You taught me that on the water today. We went where the charts said nothing was."

"And the fourth option ends where?"

"At Karang Laut," he said. "At Salmah. At the magistrate's niece. At the courthouse." He paused. "I know what I'm proposing. I'm proposing we stop running and start arriving."

Jonah stared up into the underside of the floorboards above her, at the knots and salt stains, and hated him a little, and admired him a lot, and could not tell either of those things apart anymore, which frightened her more than the boats in the channel had.

Because he was right. And because the plan he was describing—the real one, the one forming underneath his words like a channel under silt—required her to fly toward the thing she'd spent five years flying away from. Required her to be seen. Required her, eventually, to stand still in front of people who knew her name and let them look.

Old Weng's lamp came on above them around dusk, a weak yellow bloom through the gaps in the boards, and rice smell drifted down, and the rain settled into its nighttime rhythm, and none of it changed the arithmetic at all.

But when she climbed the ladder and sat at his table with her hands around a bowl she didn't remember accepting, and Old Weng asked no questions and offered no judgment and simply said, "Storm's turning tomorrow. Two days of hard wind from the northeast, then it backs around"—when he said that, Jonah heard it the way a pilot hears anything about wind: as an offer.

Two days. A window. A heading.

She looked at Teodoro across the bowl of rice and saw that he'd heard it too, and that he was waiting for her to say it aloud, because he had learned something about her in the past weeks that she was only now learning herself: she could do almost anything, but she could not yet ask.

"The strip at Karang Laut," she said. "Tomorrow night. Low tide, no moon, wind out of the northeast at their backs so they won't expect anyone landing into it."

Old Weng refilled her bowl without being asked. "There's a man on that strip," he said mildly, "who owes me for a propeller shaft. I'll send word tonight."

Jonah set down her spoon. Every option left was a bad one. But for the first time in five years, one of them was hers.
