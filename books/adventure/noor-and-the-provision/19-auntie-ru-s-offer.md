## 19. Auntie Ru's Offer

The radio found her before the rain did.

Jonah had put the Cessna down on a logging strip forty minutes inland from the channel—a shelf of red clay cut into the hillside by men who'd stripped the teak and gone, leaving behind stumps like broken teeth and a fuel drum half-full of water. It was not on any chart she trusted. That was the point of it. She had learned, over five years of running, that safety lived in the places nobody bothered to name.

She had tied the plane down under a stand of durian trees whose fruit would dent the wings if the wind decided to shake them loose, and she and Teodoro had walked the two kilometers to a stilt house belonging to a man who owed her a favor from a season Jonah preferred not to remember. The favor bought them a dry floor, a kerosene lamp, and silence. The man—Hafiz, broad as a door, with hands that smelled permanently of latex—asked no questions. He set out rice and dried fish and a pot of bitter tea, then went out into the rain to check his tapping cups, because even in a storm a man keeps the routines that keep him.

The radio crackled at nine minutes past seven.

It was the set in the plane, not the handheld. She heard it through the open door of the stilt house—a thin, wet thread of static threading through the rain—and she knew before she moved what it meant, because nobody called that frequency except people who already knew where she was. Auntie Ru did not search for people. Auntie Ru simply knew where they were, the way water knows where the low ground is.

Teodoro was reading when it started, hunched over the mislabeled documents they'd pulled from the scouts' abandoned camp, copying entries into a notebook by lamplight with the careful, joyless precision of a man who had spent thirty years handling other people's paper. He looked up when she stood.

"Is it—" he began.

"It's her." Jonah took the oilskin off its nail and shrugged into it. "Stay here. Copy everything. If I'm not back in an hour, take the documents and walk south along the ridge until you find the river, and don't stop walking."

"And do what with them?"

"Find someone who isn't me."

He didn't argue, which frightened her more than the radio did. Teodoro Reyes argued the way other men breathed; it was how he stayed alive inside his own head. If he could let her walk out into the rain without a fight, it was because he understood something about this call that she was still pretending not to.

* * *

The cockpit smelled of wet canvas and old coffee. She keyed the set and said nothing, letting the static fill the cabin like water filling a hull, and after eleven seconds—it always took Ru exactly eleven seconds, as though she counted them deliberately, as though patience were a currency she paid in—Auntie Ru's voice arrived.

"Xiao Zhou." The old nickname, the one from the first year, when the debt had been small enough to be almost friendly. "You're on a logging strip above the Selampang. Hafiz's place. Good choice—the durians will keep anyone from landing on you."

"You're watching my fuel stops." Jonah kept her voice flat. Flatness was armor. She had built the armor herself, plate by plate, over five years of calls like this one, and she knew exactly how much weight it could bear.

"I'm watching everything. That's what you pay me for." A pause, and the sound of porcelain against saucer, faint but unmistakable. Ru always had tea. Ru conducted every negotiation as though it were an afternoon that would never end, and that was the horror of her: she genuinely had nowhere else to be. "You have the documents."

Not a question. Jonah said nothing anyway.

"The scouts' camp," Ru continued, conversational now, the way she was conversational right up until the moment she wasn't. "My people are thorough men, but the weather made them careless. A folder, mislabeled, left under a tarpaulin. You understand that leaving it there was not carelessness on my part. I want you to know that. I don't insult the people I intend to keep."

"You intended me to find it."

"I intended to see what you would do with it. And now I know. You've been flying low all day, Xiao Zhou. Low and slow and south, the way a woman flies when she's carrying something she can't land with." Another sip. Somewhere behind Ru's voice, rain drummed on a tin roof, and Jonah understood that Ru was also sitting inside the monsoon, somewhere, in a room with tea and ledgers, conducting the end of the world as calmly as a market negotiation. "So let us be explicit. You've earned explicitness. You've always hated ambiguity—I've watched you hate it for years."

Jonah's hand rested on the throttle, though the engine was cold. Habit. Her hands needed something to hold that wasn't shaking.

"Return the documents," Ru said. "All of them, including whatever your archivist copied tonight—that notebook is in his bag, and yes, I know about the notebook, and yes, I know his name, and yes, I know he is sitting in Hafiz's house reading by lamplight, so please don't waste our time telling me he's safe. Fly one last delivery. Batam, three days from now, a crate I've already prepared. Land it, walk away, and the debt dies. Not shrinks. Dies. Every number in every ledger I keep on you goes to zero, and you walk out of my arithmetic a free woman."

The rain hammered the canopy. In the silence between Ru's sentences, Jonah could hear her own pulse, quick and shallow, and beneath it another sound, farther off, that might have been thunder or might have been engines.

"And if I refuse?"

Ru sighed. It was a real sigh—not theatrical, not cruel. Almost tired. That was somehow worse than cruelty. Cruelty you could brace against; tiredness suggested that what came next was simply work, a task on a list, and tasks get done.

"If you refuse, I burn Karang Laut."

The word landed in Jonah's chest like ballast dropped from height.

"You've heard the stories," Ru went on, and her voice remained gentle, remained reasonable, remained the voice of a woman explaining arithmetic to a slow student. "Pulau Serumpun, six years ago. Tanjung Wanas, four years ago. Villages that sheltered debts instead of paying them. Fish sheds first—they burn fastest, and burning sheds teach the lesson without killing anyone who matters. Then the boats, so no one can leave. Then, if the village still hasn't produced what we asked for, the houses. We rarely need the houses." A pause. "I am telling you this not as a threat, Xiao Zhou. Threats are for people who might still surprise me. This is a schedule. Karang Laut has been on it since the day your archivist stepped off your wing onto their mud. The only variable is whether you move before we do."

"They're fishermen," Jonah said. "Old women. Children. They gave me fish. They hid nothing for me—they didn't even know they were hiding anything."

"They sheltered you. That is the crime. It has always been the crime." Ru's cup clicked down onto its saucer, and the finality of that small sound made Jonah's stomach drop as though the floor of the world had tilted. "You think I don't understand what you feel right now? I do. I felt it myself, once, a long time ago, standing in a doorway very much like yours, being told very much the same thing. And I chose the way you are about to choose, and I have never once regretted it, because the alternative was watching people die for a principle that died with them. Survival is not betrayal, child. Survival is bookkeeping. Someone has to remain to remember the rest."

"I'm not your child."

"No," Ru agreed softly. "You never were. That was always your tragedy, not mine."

* * *

Jonah sat in the dark cockpit for a long time after the frequency went quiet.

The rain had settled into its monsoon rhythm now—not gusting but constant, a wall of water moving across the strait like something with intent, and through the canopy the world was reduced to gray movement and the green-black shapes of the durian trees swaying. The plane rocked on its tiedowns. Water ran off the wings in sheets and pooled around the wheels, turning the red clay to porridge, and she thought, distantly, professionally, that getting airborne tomorrow would be a fight in itself, that the strip was dissolving under her, that every hour she sat here the earth itself was closing options the way the tide closed the cave.

That was how Ru worked. Not with knives. With time. With the patient certainty that everything erodes, everyone lands eventually, and the ledger always balances.

She thought about the villages. Pulau Serumpun she had actually seen, once, from altitude, two years after—black scars in the green where the sheds had been, new tin roofs glinting like coins someone had thrown into a wound. She had told herself then that she didn't know why it happened. She had told herself a great many things, over five years, and the telling had worn grooves in her the way water wears stone: false registration numbers, unlabeled crates, the story that she carried medicine and only medicine, the deeper story that she hadn't known, the deepest story, the one she kept below the waterline where even she couldn't read it—that she had known, or half-known, or chosen not to know, because knowing would have required doing something, and doing something required having something left to lose.

Now Ru had named the price in full. Not her license. Not her freedom. Not her life. Those were prices she had been carrying since the telegram, since the floating restaurant, since the first gentle cup of tea. No—the price was Ibu Salmah's grandson hauling nets. The price was the woman who had paid her in mackerel and warnings. The price was a whole village of people who had done nothing except extend the small, uncalculated kindnesses that coastal people extend to pilots, because pilots bring vaccines and news and the sense that the outside world remembers they exist.

And Ru had timed it perfectly. Of course she had. The monsoon sealed the coast; the airstrip at Karang Laut would be underwater within days; the villagers couldn't run even if they read the schedule, which they couldn't, because Jonah was the only messenger they had and Jonah was sitting in a dark cockpit being offered the cleanest deal she would ever be offered again.

Return the documents. One last delivery. Zero.

She tried to make herself want it. That was the shameful thing, and she knew it was shameful, and she sat with the shame the way you sit with a fever—unable to sweat it out, unable to sleep it off. She tried to build the case, the way she built flight plans: return the documents, and Teodoro's forty-year hunt dies, but Teodoro lives, probably, if Ru considered him worth keeping alive, which was a coin toss weighted by how much he'd already copied. Return the documents, and the Syndicate's forged charter stays buried, and every land title on the coast stays anchored to a counterfeit, and the villages stay tenants on their own mud forever—but alive. Alive and tenant. Wasn't that, in the end, what most of the world was? Wasn't that what she had been?

But the documents weren't hers to return anymore. That was the thing she kept circling and couldn't land on. Somewhere between the causeway and the leaking boat and the night of bailing by lantern light, the papers had stopped being cargo. Cargo was what you carried for other people. These were what she carried for herself—for the burned village she'd never confirmed, for the question she'd been flying away from for five years. Returning them wouldn't zero the debt. It would compound it, into a currency she didn't have a name for yet, the kind you pay in at three in the morning for the rest of your life.

And there was Teodoro, lamplit in Hafiz's house, copying entries in a script only he could half-read, believing—with the specific, ruinous belief of a man who had lost everything else—that the truth was a thing you could carry to safety if you were careful enough. He had handed her his family's stamp. He had confessed his grandfather's betrayal to her in a flooded chamber with the tide coming in. He had given her every piece of himself that could be used against him, and Ru had just listed, item by item, exactly how each piece would be used.

If she returned the documents, she returned him. Not his body—his body was worthless to Ru, worse than worthless, a rounding error. His life's work. The only thing standing between a man and the grave he had been digging toward for forty years.

She keyed the mic once, held it, said nothing, released it. The channel stayed dead. Ru had said her piece; Ru did not repeat herself; Ru was already pouring the next cup.

* * *

She walked back through the rain with the oilskin streaming and her boots sinking to the ankle, and halfway there she saw the light in Hafiz's window and thought, absurdly, of her mother's kitchen, a lifetime ago, a country ago, a person ago. Then she thought of Karang Laut's fish sheds, and the two images lay down together in her mind side by side, and she understood that this was the actual shape of the choice—not documents versus debt, not truth versus survival, but one lit window versus many, and which ones she could live with going dark.

Teodoro looked up when she came in, dripping. He had the notebook open, the lamp turned high, and there was ink on the side of his hand where he'd braced the page. He read her face the way he read the old script—partially, carefully, missing words but catching the shape of the sentence.

"She offered you zero," he said.

"She offered me zero." Jonah hung the oilskin on its nail and stood there dripping, arms crossed, too tired to sit. "Documents, one delivery, and everything I owe disappears. Clean slate. She even said it kindly."

"And the cost?"

"A village. Ours. Starting with the fish sheds, then the boats, then—" She stopped. Saying it aloud made it a plan rather than a threat, and plans get executed. "Then whatever's next. She says it's a schedule, not a threat. She says Karang Laut's been on it since you stepped off my wing."

Teodoro was quiet for a moment. Then he did the thing that Jonah was beginning to recognize as the truest thing about him: he reached for the notebook instead of away from it.

"So we don't have three days," he said. "We have however long it takes a boat to reach Karang Laut from wherever Ru keeps her matches."

"Two days. Less, in this sea state, if they're running the strait at night." She looked at him across the lamplight, and the exhaustion in her own face was mirrored in his, and between the two of them they made perhaps one complete functioning adult. "Teddy. I need to say something and I need you to hear all of it."

He waited.

"If I give her back what we found, she wins forever. Not just the debt—everything. The charter, the titles, the coast, all of it, locked down for another fifty years, and your family's forty years becomes fifty-one, and I go back to flying crates and lying about them until one day the crates catch up to me the way they caught up to everyone else. But if I refuse her—if I fly these pages anywhere near a courthouse—she burns Karang Laut, and she burns it because of me, and I will have traded a village for a verdict." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, and kept going anyway, because stopping would have been worse. "I've spent five years making sure the bill always landed on someone else. I'm not asking you what to do. I'm telling you what it costs, so that whatever happens, you know I chose it with my eyes open."

Teodoro closed the notebook. He pressed his palm flat on the cover, the way a man steadies something living.

"There's a third column," he said quietly. "In every ledger I ever catalogued—in the cave, in the archive, everywhere—there were always three columns. What was owed. What was paid. And what was *carried forward*. Debts don't vanish, Jonah. They transfer. Ru thinks she's offering to erase yours. She's offering to transfer it—to Salmah, to the boy, to every roof in Karang Laut. And you'd carry that forward for the rest of your life, and it would weigh more than anything the Syndicate could ever bill you." He turned the notebook around and pushed it across the table toward her, open to a page she hadn't seen him copy: an entry in the old script, and beneath it, in his cramped clerk's hand, a translation. "Look at this. I found it an hour ago, while you were on the radio. It's from the chest documents—the mislabeled copies. Read what the founder owed."

Jonah leaned over the page. The lamplight caught the faded ink, and the translation beneath it, and the number at the bottom—a number so large it had its own annotation, its own history, its own line of collateral stretching down the margin like a root system.

Her eyes narrowed. Her breath went shallow. Because the entry wasn't just a debt. It was a map of everything—the loan, the collateral, the villages pledged against it, the whole architecture of the Syndicate laid out in a dead clerk's handwriting, signed and witnessed and carved into stone by people who had understood, centuries before lawyers existed, that power is just a debt someone managed to make unfalsifiable.

Until now.

"Ru wants an answer," Jonah said slowly. "Three days."

"Then we'd better spend them well." Teodoro's hand was still flat on the notebook, holding it against the draft from the door, against the storm, against the whole descending weight of the monsoon. "Because I've been reading all evening, and there's something in these pages you need to see—all of it, properly, by daylight. Something about what the founder's debt was secured *against*."

Outside, the rain thickened, and far off across the blacked-out strait, thunder rolled—or engines—and neither of them said which.
