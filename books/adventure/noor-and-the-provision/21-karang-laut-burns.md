## 21. Karang Laut Burns

The radio woke her before the barometer did.

It was the hour Jonah thought of as the grey hour—not yet dawn, no longer night, when the water off Karang Laut went the color of unpolished tin and the mosquitoes gave up their shift to the rainbirds. She had been sleeping in the plane again, wedged into the space behind the pilot's seat with her jacket rolled under her neck, because the stilt house Teodoro had found them leaked in three places and the hammock in it belonged to someone who might still come back for it. The set crackled against her ear where she'd fallen asleep with the headset on, a habit she'd never managed to break and had stopped pretending was anything but fear wearing the costume of professionalism.

*Jonah.* Auntie Ru's voice, soft as tea being poured. *I'm told you haven't answered my last message.*

She sat up too fast and cracked her skull on the canopy frame, which at least had the virtue of making her feel awake. Outside, the monsoon was gathering itself in earnest now—a bruised wall of cloud sitting on the eastern horizon like something waiting to be fed. The strip at her back was already going soft. She could feel it through the tires, the ground turning from earth to porridge one rain at a time.

*You have until sundown,* Ru said, and there was nothing else. No threat attached. No consequence named. Just the deadline, hanging in the wet air like a hook with nothing on it yet.

That was the thing about Auntie Ru that Jonah had never learned to defend against. Other people threatened you with fire, with knives, with the specific and imaginative torments that men who liked their own cruelty rehearsed in front of mirrors. Ru simply told you what time it was, and then she waited, and whatever happened next arrived with the punctuality of tide tables. She didn't need to say *or else*. The *else* was implied in every syllable she declined to use.

Jonah keyed the mic and said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't yes or no, and she hadn't decided which lie she could live inside for another day.

Teodoro was already up, she realized—she could see his lantern moving in the stilt house, a yellow smudge through the rain-hazed mosquito netting, and then his silhouette against the window as he stood over the documents they'd recovered from the scouts' abandoned camp, reading them again. He read them the way some men prayed: compulsively, hungrily, as though the words might rearrange themselves if he looked away long enough. He had transcribed the whole packet twice by lantern light into a notebook he kept wrapped in oilcloth, and he slept with the notebook inside his shirt, against his skin, like a second heartbeat.

She should have answered Ru. She knew that. Every hour of silence was its own entry in a ledger Ru kept somewhere, in a hand so neat it looked printed, and Jonah could feel the interest compounding even now, even here, even in the grey hour with the rain coming down and the whole coast holding its breath.

But answering meant choosing, and choosing meant naming which part of herself she was willing to burn, and she had spent five years becoming very, very good at not choosing. At keeping both cargoes in the air. Vaccines in the port rack, contraband rumors in the starboard one, and her conscience strapped down amidships where it couldn't shift the weight and balance.

Sundown, then. She had until sundown.

She did not get until sundown.

* * *

The smoke came first, and it came wrong.

Jonah was on the strip doing her morning inspection—checking the tie-downs, cursing the drainage, running her thumb along the propeller's leading edge looking for nicks the way other people ran rosary beads through their fingers—when she smelled it. Woodsmoke she knew; every coastal village smelled of woodsmoke by mid-morning, cooking fires and fish-drying racks and the sweet char of coconut husk. This was different. This was diesel and pitch and something chemical underneath, an accelerant smell that had no business in a fishing village at seven in the morning, and it was coming from the north end of Karang Laut, from the line of fish sheds that stood on stilts over the shallows like a row of patient wading birds.

She climbed onto the wing to see better.

Three columns of black smoke, rising dead straight in the windless hour before the storm wind arrived, the way smoke only rises when the fire beneath it is fat and well-fed and has been burning for more than a minute. Even from two miles out she could see the sheds were gone past saving—the nearest one already collapsed into the water in a bloom of steam and embers, the others guttering along their stilts like candles burning down.

And between her and the fire, low over the channel, a gray boat. Syndicate gray, the particular flat ugly gray that Ru's people painted everything, moving away from the village at a cruising speed that said, more clearly than any broadcast could have, *this is finished, we are done here, you may begin.*

Jonah stood on the wing with the rain starting to spot the fabric around her feet and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, almost like a blow, exactly what she was looking at.

Not an attack. A message.

Ru hadn't waited for sundown. Ru hadn't waited for an answer at all, because Ru had understood something about her that Jonah had been careful never to say aloud even to herself: that silence was also an answer, and the answer was *no*, or close enough to no that the distinction no longer mattered. And so the reply had been sent in the only language the Marrow Syndicate respected, addressed not to Jonah but to everyone watching Jonah—to Ibu Salmah, to the fishermen mending nets under the eaves, to every village on this coast that had ever trusted a pilot with a borrowed tail number.

*This,* the burning sheds said. *This is what your trust costs.*

She got the engine started with hands that wouldn't quite obey her, missed the primer twice, caught it on the third, and was airborne before she'd finished the pre-takeoff checks, climbing out over the channel in air so heavy the plane seemed to swim rather than fly. The gray boat was already a smudge to the south. She didn't chase it. Chasing it would have been theater, and worse than theater—it would have been leaving the burning village behind her to go perform rage for an audience of nobody.

She flew instead toward the smoke.

* * *

Karang Laut from the air was worse than Karang Laut from anywhere else, because from the air you saw the shape of it. The village curved along the shore like a cupped hand, and the fire had taken the fingers—the northern sheds, the drying racks, the covered walkway where the catch was weighed—and left the palm, the houses, the mosque with its tin roof catching what little light the storm allowed. Someone had gotten a bucket chain going. She could see it from five hundred feet, the human chain stretched from the well to the last standing shed, passing buckets hand over hand with the grim mechanical rhythm of people who have stopped expecting to win and started merely refusing to lose.

The beach was littered with what the fire had rejected. Half-burned fish, smoking. Nets fused into black lace. And boats—three of the village's longboats were ablaze at their moorings, drifting loose in the shallows like funeral rafts, and Jonah felt her stomach drop when she counted them, because she knew what it meant when you burned a fishing village's boats. It meant you weren't punishing them for something they'd done. You were taking away tomorrow.

She put the plane down on the tidal strip with the crosswind shoving at her like a drunk trying to dance, and the moment the wheels stopped rolling she was out of the cockpit and running, and the first person she met was a boy carrying water, maybe ten years old, his eyebrows singed to stubble, and he looked up at her and said, in the flat voice children use when they have seen something they will spend the rest of their lives not describing: *"Kakak Jonah. Mereka datang waktu subuh."*

They came at dawn.

At dawn. While she had been sleeping in her plane, headset on, listening to static, telling herself that silence was a strategy. They had come at dawn because dawn was when the men were out on the water and the sheds held the whole night's catch and the only witnesses were women and children, and the Syndicate's scouts had done their arithmetic correctly, because the Syndicate always did its arithmetic correctly. That was the entire horror of the Marrow Syndicate, Jonah thought, hauling buckets with hands that blistered and split within the hour. It wasn't sadism. Sadism would at least have been a passion. It was bookkeeping. Somewhere in a floating restaurant in another life, a neat-handed woman had entered *one village, partially, as demonstration* into a column, balanced it against *pilot compliance, probable*, and found the exchange favorable.

The cost of her hesitation, paid by other people. Again. Always again. The burned village from years ago, the one whose name she still could not say, the crates she'd carried and never asked about—that debt had been paid by strangers too. She had told herself the pattern was bad luck. Standing ankle-deep in seawater and ash, passing a bucket to a woman whose face was streaked with soot and tears in clean channels, Jonah admitted what she had known for years: it wasn't luck. It was a method, and she was part of the machinery of it, and every year she'd flown both cargoes she'd been paying interest on a principal she refused to look at.

*"Di mana Ibu Salmah?"* she shouted, and a dozen hands pointed toward the mosque.

* * *

They had turned the mosque into a hospital, because there was nowhere else. The tin-roofed hall smelled of iodine and smoke and wet sarong, and the floor was covered in a mosaic of suffering laid out on mats and folded sails: burns mostly, the angry red of flash-scalded skin, a few broken bones from collapsing shed roofs, and cuts—dozens of small deep cuts from people who had pulled apart burning structures with bare hands because the boats were moored inside them and the boats were the village's future and you do not let the future burn while you stand and watch.

Ibu Salmah was everywhere at once, the way she always was, directing with her chin and her voice and a ferocity that made grown men flinch. But when she saw Jonah come through the door—soaked, filthy, smelling of avgas and smoke—her face did something complicated. Not forgiveness. Not blame. Something older than either, the look of a woman doing sums she hated.

"You slept," Salmah said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't quite an accusation. "You were on the strip. My grandson heard your engine all night. He said, *the pilot is here, the pilot will hear them if they come.*" Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "He believed the sound of your airplane was a promise."

Jonah's mouth opened and produced nothing.

"He is over there," Salmah said, and turned away to shout at someone about boiled water, because grief is a luxury and there was work.

Yusuf was fourteen. Jonah had flown him and three other boys to the district school at the start of term, back in the season when the sky was kind, and he had spent the whole flight asking questions about the instruments until she'd let him hold the yoke for thirty seconds over open water, straight and level, and he had grinned at her like she'd handed him the moon. Now he lay on a sail near the wall with his left leg wrapped in cloth that was already spotting through, and a gash over his eyebrow that someone had butterfly-strapped with tape, and he was awake, and when he saw her his face lit up before it could stop itself, and that—that was the moment Jonah Pemberton's guilt stopped being an abstraction and became a physical weight she would carry in her chest for the rest of her flying life.

*"Kakak,"* he said. Big sister. "Did you see? I helped pull Pak Daud's boat out. Before it—" He gestured vaguely at his leg, at the collapsed roof beam he clearly didn't remember, or was choosing not to. "Is the plane okay? Did the fire come near the plane?"

The plane. He was asking after the plane.

"It's fine," Jonah said, and knelt beside him, and took his hand, and discovered she was crying without having authorized it, silently, in front of a fourteen-year-old boy who patted her wrist awkwardly and said, *"Jangan menangis, Kakak. Kita boleh bina semula."* Don't cry. We can rebuild.

We can rebuild. As if the burning were weather. As if the Syndicate were a monsoon, an act of God, something to be endured and repaired around rather than answered. And perhaps for him it was; he was fourteen, and the coast had always been squeezed by somebody, and the names changed but the arithmetic didn't. But Jonah knew the name. Jonah had drunk tea with the name. Jonah owed money to the name.

She stayed until the district boat came with proper dressings, and then she flew two runs to the mainland clinic and back with supplies she paid for out of the cash belt she wore under her shirt—money that had been earmarked, in a plan she'd been building for months, for her own escape. The debt to Auntie Ru would notice the missing sum. Let it notice. There were payments and payments, and she was beginning to understand, bucket by bucket, which ones actually mattered.

On the second return, in the last usable light, she overflew the village once before landing. The sheds were charred skeletons. The boats were gone entirely—sunk or towed, she couldn't tell. But on the mudflat below the mosque, dozens of villagers had written words in the silt with sticks and fingers, great characters visible from the air, and she banked to read them and wished she hadn't.

They said: *WE ARE STILL HERE.*

She landed into a headwind that was really the monsoon arriving early, its outriders already combing the strait, and taxied to the tie-down with the rain hammering the canopy like applause from a hostile audience.

* * *

Teodoro was waiting under the wing with an umbrella he was failing to keep upright, and his face, when she climbed down, was the face of a man who had spent six hours composing arguments and abandoned all of them.

"I heard the radio traffic," he said. "The clinic run. I—" He stopped. Started again. "I've been thinking about what I'd say to you if you came back, and none of it survives seeing you actually come back."

"They'll burn the rest of it," Jonah said. Her voice sounded foreign to her, scraped hollow. "Next it's the houses. Then the well, maybe. They'll salt it if they have to. Ru doesn't leave messages half-sent."

"She's giving you until—" 

"There is no *until* anymore. Today proved that. The deadline was decoration. She burned the village to teach me that my hesitation has a price, and she priced it in other people's skin because she knows—I taught her, over five years of cargo runs and straight-faced lies—that I can survive anything she does to me. It's the only currency I've ever had. So she's stopped spending on me." Jonah laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Do you understand? I finally found something I can't pay with. It took me thirty years and a burning village."

Teodoro put his hand on the fuselage, next to hers, not touching her, the way he did everything—close enough to feel the heat of, never closing the distance. "Then we stop paying," he said quietly. "We fly tomorrow. Tonight, if the window holds. Through the core, over their airfield, to the courthouse, to the magistrate. We stop negotiating with the fire and we take the ledger out of its hands."

"The storm—"

"The storm is the only door they haven't guarded."

She looked at him. Rain ran off the wingtip between them in a beaded curtain, and beyond it the village smoked, and the boy who had believed her engine was a promise lay on a sail with a splinted leg, and somewhere south a gray boat rode at anchor with its crew sleeping soundly after honest work.

"Sundown," Jonah said. "Ru gave me until sundown. I'm going to spend it answering her."

"How?"

Jonah pulled the cash belt from under her shirt, heavy with the clinic money she hadn't spent, and pressed it into Teodoro's hands. "Buy diesel, rope, sailcloth, food. Everything the village needs to rebuild the boats. Leave it with Ibu Salmah, anonymously, and tell her—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Tell her the pilot says the debt is hers to call in. Whatever she asks. Whenever she asks."

"And us?"

"We fuel the plane in the dark," Jonah said, "and we file no flight plan, and at first light we go up into the middle of the storm, and we don't come down on this coast again until the truth is on a courthouse record."

Above them the rain thickened from hammering to roaring, and the last light died over the black skeleton sheds, and somewhere in the falling dark a radio crackled to life on Auntie Ru's frequency, patient as tide, asking—in that soft, tea-pouring voice—for an answer that was no longer possible to give.
