## 22. The Debt Called In

The summons came the way Auntie Ru's summons always came: not as a threat but as an invitation, printed on heavy cream paper that smelled faintly of sandalwood and machine oil. *Miss Zhou. Tea. Thursday. The usual table.* No address beyond that, because there was only ever one usual table—the corner of the floating restaurant at Tanjung Selat where the water beneath the floorboards was deep enough to drown a man and calm enough to keep him.

Jonah read the card three times in the stilt house while rain hammered the palm thatch like a fist on a door. Teodoro sat across from her with the recovered documents spread between them, his fingers stained with the graphite he'd been using to make rubbing after rubbing, a second copy of everything, a third, as though paper could be multiplied into safety. He looked up when she didn't speak.

"Ru," he said.

"Ru."

"You're not going."

"I'm going."

"Jonah." He set down the pencil with the deliberate care of a man setting down something he'd rather throw. "She burned Karang Laut's fish sheds to get your attention. She is not inviting you to tea. She is inviting you to a ledger closing."

"And if I don't go," Jonah said, "she closes it anyway, and I'm not in the room when she does." She gathered the card, tucked it inside her shirt against her skin, where all her worst decisions seemed to live. "I've survived her arithmetic before by knowing what she'd count. If I run now, I stop knowing. That's how people die out here—not from the storm, from the surprise."

Teodoro looked at her for a long moment, and there was something in his face that wasn't argument at all. It was the look of a man watching someone walk toward a fire because they believed they could carry water back out of it. "Then I'll be at the fuel dock," he said finally. "With the boat running. If you're not back by slack tide, I'm coming in after you, and we'll both be stupid together."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," Jonah said, and hated herself for meaning even half of it.

* * *

The restaurant swayed on its pilings like a sleeping animal. Jonah came in alone, wet to the knees from the transfer skiff, and found the usual table already set: two cups, a pot of oolong, a plate of kuih arranged with geometric precision, and Auntie Ru.

Ru was smaller than Jonah remembered, or perhaps the monsoon had shrunk everything else and left Ru at her original dimensions—immaculate in a charcoal cheongsam, silver hair pinned so tightly it looked structural, hands folded on the table like two closed ledgers. She poured before Jonah sat. The steam rose between them and hung there, a small curtain neither woman chose to part.

"You look tired," Ru said.

"I look wet."

"You look tired *and* wet." She smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, which was the whole horror of it. "Sit properly. You'll catch your death hunching over that cup like a debt collector."

Jonah sat. The tea was excellent. It was always excellent; Ru bought it from the same hillside family she'd ruined in '09 and then quietly re-established under her niece's name, because Ru understood, better than anyone Jonah had ever met, that you could own a thing completely and still want it to flourish. Ownership without flourishing was just arson with paperwork.

"I'll come to the point," Ru said, "because you're a pilot and pilots think in fuel burns, and I respect that. Your debt stands at—" she recited the figure without looking at anything, the number worn smooth as a river stone from handling, "—and the interest this quarter alone would buy a decent used aircraft. I know you know this. I know you lie awake doing the multiplication. So let me tell you what I'm prepared to do, and then you can tell me what you're prepared to do, and we'll see whether our columns balance."

The rain thickened outside. The restaurant rocked gently, and the lanterns swung, and their shadows swung with them, two figures leaning toward each other and away again, like a conversation conducted by pendulum.

"The documents," Ru said. "The ones my scouts were too sentimental—or too careless—to finish removing from the cave. The copies Reyes pulled off the causeway camp. Bring them to me. All of them, including whatever rubbings the archivist has made, and the debt dies. Not reduced. Dies. Every false registration, every unlabeled crate, every week of compounding interest since the year you first flew for me—struck through, initialed, gone. You'd wake up owing nothing to anyone, which in this part of the world is the closest a person comes to being innocent."

Jonah held the cup halfway to her mouth and put it down again, untouched. "And Reyes?"

"Ah." Ru leaned back, and the lantern light caught the lacquer of her hairpin, a black bird with a ruby eye. "Reyes."

She said the name the way a clerk says a figure that doesn't reconcile—a small, almost tender regret, the sound of a column that must, regrettably, be written off.

"He's an old man with an old grievance and a head full of other people's numbers. He has spent forty years trying to unwrite a story, and stories don't unwritten, Miss Zhou—they only get retold, usually worse. But here is my generosity, and I want you to understand that I mean it as generosity: bring me the documents, and I will accept his silence in place of his head. He goes wherever old archivists go when they stop looking—upriver, across the water, into some town where nobody knows his name—and he never files another claim, never testifies, never surfaces. And in exchange, no one goes looking for him. That is not nothing. There are men who work for me who would consider that a pension."

"And if he doesn't stay silent?"

Ru folded her hands again. "Then his silence becomes a rounding error," she said, "and rounding errors get corrected. You know how my books work. Nothing personal in it. A ledger that tolerates discrepancies isn't a ledger—it's a diary, and diaries are how families like his got started."

There it was. Not cruelty—arithmetic. Ru genuinely, sincerely believed she was offering mercy, and the terrible thing, the thing Jonah had to sit with while the tea went cold between them, was that within Ru's system she *was*. Mercy was a discount applied before write-off. Mercy was choosing the cheaper line item. Somewhere behind those folded hands was a mental page with two columns, and on one side: documents returned, archivist silenced, debt erased, coast quiet for another generation. On the other: a village of fishermen who had trusted a stranger's plane, an old man's life's work, and whatever it was that had been growing between Jonah and Teodoro in the dark of a flooded cave, unnamed and therefore, in Ru's accounting, valueless.

Ru's columns always balanced. That was her genius and her damnation. She had never once in her life carried a loss she couldn't assign to somebody else.

"You're hesitating," Ru observed, with the mild tone of a woman noting weather. "That's new for you. Five years ago you'd have taken this deal before the tea cooled. What changed?"

*You did,* Jonah thought. *And then I did.* She said: "Karang Laut burned."

"Karang Laut *smoldered*," Ru corrected gently. "Fish sheds are flammable. Fishermen rebuild. It was a message, and messages are cheaper than funerals—I've always been economical that way. Though I notice you flew supply drops through the storm's edge to them. Three runs. In weather that would ground my own charters." Something moved behind her eyes, brief and unreadable, like a fish under ice. "I told the board about that. They thought it was loyalty. I knew better. Loyalty lands where it's paid to land. What you did was guilt, and guilt is the one cargo I've never been able to book, because it weighs nothing going out and everything coming back."

"Why am I really here?" Jonah asked. "You have boats. You have men. You could take the documents off us any night you chose."

"Could I." Ru poured herself more tea, unhurried, and Jonah watched her hands—small, steady, the hands of a woman who had signed orders for things no court would ever hear described. "The board thinks so. The board is wrong, but boards are wrong the way tides are wrong—predictably, expensively, and always after the money's committed. Here is what the board doesn't understand, and what you, flying your little plane through the eyewall of a monsoon, apparently do: some things lose value when you seize them and gain value when they're given. Those documents in my scouts' hands are evidence. Those same documents surrendered voluntarily, through you, with the archivist withdrawn—those are *closed accounts*. The difference is the difference between a theft and a settlement. My lawyers can bury a settlement. Evidence they have to chase, and chasing leaves footprints, and footprints are how the magistrate's office found its courage."

So that was it. Not generosity. Bookkeeping. Ru needed Jonah's hands because Jonah's hands made the transaction clean, and clean transactions were the only scripture Ru had ever believed in.

"And if I refuse?"

Ru smiled again, and this time the smile reached all the way to something ancient and patient underneath. "Then the debt stands, the interest compounds, and I stop protecting you from the men below me who think you've become a liability. Do you understand what that means? It means I don't have to order anything. I simply stop standing between you and the weather. Out here, Miss Zhou, that is indistinguishable from a death sentence, and everyone involved can pretend it was the monsoon." She sipped. "I've buried better pilots than you that way. Good planes, bad weather, closed file. The insurance pays out. Nobody grieves twice."

The lanterns steadied as the wind shifted. Through the open shutters Jonah could see the channel, black and churned, and somewhere out there a longboat with a running engine and an old man in it, keeping his promise to be stupid on schedule.

"I need until the tide turns," Jonah said. "Two days."

"For what?"

"To close my own books."

Ru studied her for a moment, and Jonah felt the full weight of that attention—the attention of a woman who had watched her for five years the way a lender watches collateral, noting every scratch, every repainting, every quiet re-registration. Then Ru nodded once, briskly, business concluded.

"Two days. Tide turns Thursday midnight. After that, my protection ends and my patience begins, and my patience, unlike my protection, has never once been extended." She rose, smoothed her cheongsam, and paused beside Jonah's chair just long enough to rest one small hand on her shoulder—the closest thing to affection the Syndicate's books allowed. "For what it's worth, and it isn't worth much: I hoped you'd be smarter than this. You were the best pilot I ever ruined."

She left coins on the table for the tea, because Auntie Ru settled every account, even the symbolic ones, and went out into the rain without an umbrella, because women like Ru were never rained on—they were merely, briefly, exposed.

* * *

Jonah stood at the rail of the floating restaurant for a long time after the skiff pulled away, watching the water that could drown a man and mostly didn't. Her shoulder still held the ghost-warmth of Ru's hand, and she wanted to scrub it off and couldn't, because it wasn't on her skin. It was in the ledger. It had always been in the ledger.

*Bring me the documents and his silence.*

Silence, which amounts to the same thing.

She thought about Teodoro's face when he'd shown her the 1930s manifest at the fuel dock, the archive stamp of his family pressed purple into the corner like a heartbeat. She thought about him making rubbings by lamplight, multiplying paper into safety, believing that copies were armor. She thought about the cave, and the carved ledgers older than any of them, stone that kept better books than men, and the founder's name sitting in them as a *debtor*, and how Ru's entire empire was a forged entry wearing a cheongsam.

Two days. Tide turns Thursday midnight.

Jonah pushed off from the rail, dropped into the transfer skiff, and told the boy to take her to the fuel dock. There was a plan forming in her, half-dread and half-course-line, the way all her real plans formed—not chosen so much as *flown into*, the way you fly into weather you swore you'd avoid and find, somewhere in the middle of it, that you've stopped shaking.

Teodoro was waiting under the lean-to with the engine idling and two cups of terrible coffee, and the moment he saw her face he knew the shape of what she'd been offered, because grief and relief look identical from a distance.

"She wants your silence," Jonah said, taking the cup. "Or your head. She's generous enough to let me pick."

"And what did you tell her?"

Jonah drank the coffee. It was awful. It was the best thing she'd tasted all day.

"I told her I needed two days to close my books," she said. "And then I'm going to need your star-charts, your memory of the cave, and every drop of nerve you have left—because I'm not bringing her the documents, Teo. I'm flying them out through the storm itself, and I'm going to need to burn every route behind me except one."

Teodoro stared at her. Somewhere up the channel, thunder rolled along the water like a drum being moved into position.

"The courthouse," he said slowly. "Salmah's niece."

"Salmah's niece," Jonah confirmed. "Direct route goes through the monsoon's core and directly over the Syndicate's airfield." She drained the cup and set it down. "So we'd better start preflighting a miracle."
