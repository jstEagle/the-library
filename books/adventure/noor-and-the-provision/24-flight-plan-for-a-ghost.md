## 24. Flight Plan for a Ghost

The stilt house smelled of kerosene and drying fish and forty years of rain soaked into the planks. Jonah sat at the table by lamplight with a chart spread out before her, weighted at the corners with teacups, a tin of condensed milk, Teodoro's hand, and a stone she'd picked up from the causeway on their second descent without quite knowing why. The stone was black and dense as a debt. She kept touching it.

Outside, the monsoon was not raining so much as standing. It leaned against the house in walls of grey water, and the sea beneath them had stopped being a sea and become a rumor of movement, something breathing under a blanket. Every few minutes the whole structure groaned—stilt, floor, roof—and settled again, like an animal deciding to tolerate them.

She had been staring at the same three inches of chart for twenty minutes.

Between Karang Laut and the capital lay two hundred and ten nautical miles of the worst weather the Sunda coast could produce in a generation. The barometer had fallen through every line on its dial that mattered and was now in territory Jonah thought of privately as *the numbers where planes die*. Her own aircraft—a 1934 tail-dragger held together by safety wire, prayer, and her refusal to do arithmetic about its age—was rated for weather considerably gentler than what was currently trying to peel the roof off the stilt house. And between here and there, planted across the only sensible route like a tollgate, sat the Syndicate's airfield at Tanjung Bara: two runways, a radio mast, four serviceable aircraft, and men whose entire profession was making sure cargo did not pass without paying.

She drew a line anyway. Pencil, light, from the reef to the coast, then east along the shore, then the long open-water leg north to the capital.

"You're doing it," Teodoro said. He was across the table, cataloguing the recovered documents into oilcloth sleeves with the small, precise movements of a man who had spent his life handling things that must not be damaged. He did not look up. "You've already decided. You're just drawing it slowly so you can pretend you haven't."

"I'm weighing options."

"There are no options. There is this, and there is waiting for Ru's people to find this house, which they will, because this coast has maybe nine dry rooms and they know all of them." He slid another page into its sleeve. "Draw faster."

Jonah exhaled through her nose, the way she did when he was right and she resented it. She bent over the chart and began to draw properly.

* * *

The plan assembled itself the way bad plans always do: one piece at a time, each piece individually reasonable, the whole thing insane.

First, the destination. Not the main courthouse—the Syndicate's lawyers would have the steps of that building watched before her wheels left the water. It had to be a magistrate with authority to receive evidence directly, someone who could take custody of documents and enter them on the record before any injunction landed. Ibu Salmah had given her the name two nights ago, spoken quietly while the fish sheds still smoked behind her: her sister's daughter, Magistrate Adila Rahman, third bench of the coastal circuit, posted to the capital's old colonial courthouse—the granite one, the one built by people who had never imagined anyone would need to hide evidence *from* the law rather than within it.

"She owes me nothing," Salmah had said. "But she owes the coast everything, and she knows it. Tell her the ledgers are real. Tell her my grandson's arm is in a splint because of what's written in them. Then get out of her chambers fast, because if you stay, she'll start asking you questions you'll have to answer honestly, and neither of us can afford that yet."

Second, the route. There were exactly three ways south-to-north in this weather: around the storm's western edge, through it, or not at all. Around the western edge meant hugging the Syndicate's patrol corridor, where their charter aircraft flew low sweeps with radios and patience and all the time in the world. Not at all meant the documents died in a stilt house with them. Which left through it—the monsoon's core, the rotating engine of the thing, the place where the rain went vertical and the wind forgot which direction it had agreed to blow.

No one flew through the core. That was not a rule; it was closer to a fact of nature, like tides. But a core moved, and it had edges, and between the spiral bands there were gaps—minutes wide, sometimes less—that opened and shut like the cave itself opened and shut with the tide. Jonah had spent five years learning to read weather the way Teodoro read script: partially, hungrily, with a constant awareness of how much she didn't know. She had punched squall lines before. She had never done this.

Third, the airfield. Tanjung Bara sat directly beneath the northern approach corridor, because of course it did—the Syndicate had bought the geography decades ago, the way they'd bought everything else, cheaply and permanently. Any flight to the capital from the southern coast passed within eleven miles of their field unless it detoured ninety miles west into worse water. Eleven miles. At their cruise speed, four minutes inside radar-and-eyeball range. Four minutes in which a charter could lift off, climb, and be waiting.

Unless the weather ate the four minutes. Unless the ceiling came down to nothing and the rain turned the sky into a room with no walls and the Syndicate's pilots—good men, well-paid, sensible men—decided that no cargo was worth dying over. Sensible men. That was the whole plan, really. Bet everything on the other side being sensible.

Teodoro watched her draw the final leg and said nothing for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was careful, the way it got when he was about to say something he'd already rehearsed against himself.

"The documents can't fly in one bundle."

She looked up. Rain hammered the shutter behind him; the lamp flame stood perfectly still, which somehow made it worse, as if the storm outside and the calm inside had agreed to swap places.

"If we lose the plane," he said, "we lose everything. So we split them. The founding charter and the founder's debt entry go with you—they're the spine, the two pages that unravel everything else. The copies, the photographs, the translations I've started—" he tapped the oilcloth stack, "—those go separately. If I have to, I carry them out by boat after the tide, or bury them, or give them to Salmah. But the spine flies with you, and nothing else does."

"And if the plane goes down in the core?"

"Then the spine goes down with you, and I spend the rest of my life being a man who knew the truth and couldn't get it out." He said it flatly, without drama, the way he seemed to say everything now, as though the last weeks had burned the drama out of him and left only the load-bearing parts. "I've done the arithmetic, Jonah. I'm better at archives than grief."

"That's not true."

"It doesn't have to be true. It has to be usable." He pulled the two key pages from the stack, sleeved them, and laid them beside her pencil hand. "Here. Your cargo manifest for the ghost flight."

* * *

She worked through the night, because the plan had holes and holes were the kind of thing that killed you at altitude, where you couldn't stop to think.

Fuel: the strip at Karang Laut was underwater now, drowned by the surge, its markers gone. Her plane sat at a grass strip inland—Pulau Senang, half a mile of mowed lalang that belonged to a man named Pak Usman who took payment in outboard engines and discretion. Getting to it meant a boat run through channels the Syndicate was sweeping. Getting away from it meant climbing out under a ceiling that might be two hundred feet or might be sixty. She wrote both numbers down and hated both.

Weight and balance: herself, full fuel, the document sleeve in a map case strapped to her inner left thigh—not the seat beside her, not the baggage bay, but against her body, because everything else on the airplane was replaceable except that. Teodoro stayed behind. She had argued this with him for an hour and won it on physics: his weight was fuel she couldn't afford, and the core allowed no passengers to error. He had accepted it with a look she couldn't read and didn't try to.

Radio: she would fly the whole route dark. No transmissions, not even position calls. Silence was its own kind of signature—if the Syndicate heard her voice on any frequency, they'd know instantly it was her, and Auntie Ru had spent enough years listening to Jonah's radio voice to pick it out of a choir. So: receiver on, transmitter cold, navigate by dead reckoning and the cave's star-charts, which Teodoro had copied onto linen paper in ink that wouldn't run. The old Reyes charts showed the monsoon-season winds the way the colonial surveys never had, because the people who drew them had actually flown—or sailed—through, and had come back, and had bothered to write down what it felt like.

"Four hundred years of pilots' notes," Teodoro said, handing her the linen sheets. "My family collected them. Everyone assumed it was sentiment. It was a survival manual."

Timing: the tide governed everything, the way it always had. The cave's own logic, applied to the sky. Low water at Karang Laut gave her the channel window for the boat run to Pulau Senang; dawn gave her the light to find a grass strip in rain; and the storm's rotation—she'd marked the bands off the barometer's fall rate—put a gap in the spiral somewhere over the strait at roughly mid-morning, if the storm held its course, which storms did not promise.

She wrote the plan out twice. Once in full, for Teodoro, in case she didn't come back and someone needed to know what had been attempted and why. Once abbreviated onto a single card, in shorthand only she could read, to strap to her knee board. The second version ended with a line she hadn't planned:

*If intercepted: descend. They will not follow.*

It was the maddest sentence she had ever written, and looking at it, she felt something that wasn't quite fear. It was closer to relief. For five years every plan she'd made had been a plan for getting away—away from debts, away from questions, away from the smoke over a village whose name she still wouldn't say aloud. This was the first plan she'd ever drawn that was a plan for arriving.

* * *

Dawn did not arrive so much as the night gave up arguing. The rain slackened from solid wall to heavy curtain, and through the gaps Jonah could see the sea for the first time in two days: brown-green, confused, wearing the wreckage of the fish sheds like scars. Somewhere down the coast, Karang Laut was rebuilding. Somewhere further down, the Syndicate's boats were sweeping channels, patient as bookkeepers.

Teodoro stood at the rail, watching her watch the water.

"You're going to ask me something," he said. "I can tell. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you've already asked yourself the question and don't like the answer, so you're going to ask me instead, because I'll say it out loud and then it'll be real and you can stop pretending it isn't."

Jonah looked at him. He was right, and she hated that too, and she asked anyway.

"If I don't come back—how long do you wait before you take the copies and run?"

He didn't answer immediately. He turned back to the sea, and the silence stretched until it creaked, and then he said: "Three days. Three days, and then I assume the worst, and I do what the documents require, which is not the same as what I want." A pause. "But you should know what I'll be doing during those three days. I'll be standing on this rail every hour of daylight, looking south. Because I've spent forty years learning how to lose things, Jonah, and I am very, very good at it, and I am telling you plainly: I do not want the practice."

She laughed—one short, involuntary sound, surprised out of her—and then pressed her hand flat against the table to make it stop, because laughing felt like spending something she'd need later.

"Pak Usman's boat leaves at first light tomorrow," she said. "Low water's at oh-five-forty. We run the channels dark, no engine lights, the way Salmah's fishermen do it. I fuel at Senang, I file nothing, I tell no one, and I'm airborne by seven."

"And the gap in the spiral?"

"Mid-morning, over the strait, if the storm holds. If it doesn't hold—" she shrugged, and the shrug was honest, which was rare enough that it felt like an event, "—then I fly into whatever's there and find out what I'm made of."

Teodoro nodded slowly. Then he did something she hadn't expected: he took the card with her shorthand plan, read it, and put it back down without comment. "You've thought of everything I'd have thought of," he said. "That's either very reassuring or very frightening, and I haven't decided which."

"It's both. It's always both."

* * *

Evening came down like a lid. The barometer ticked lower. In the lamplight, Jonah laid out her flying clothes—two shirts, the good boots, the leather cap with the cracked earflaps she'd never replaced because replacing it felt like admitting time passed—and beside them, wrapped in oilcloth, the two pages that were about to become the most dangerous cargo she had ever carried.

She found herself holding the sleeve against the lamp, reading the founder's debt entry for the hundredth time. The old script swam in the flicker: names, sums, collateral. A loan taken in a famine year. Land pledged. A coast signed away by a man who had then built an empire on the fiction that the pledge had never been recorded anywhere but his own strongbox.

And below it, in a later hand, fainter: a notation Teodoro had translated for her three nights ago, sitting in this same chair, his voice gone quiet in the middle. *Debt acknowledged by debtor's mark. Witnessed.* The Syndicate's entire claim to the coast rested on everyone forgetting that the founder himself had once signed, in front of witnesses, as a man who owed.

She thought about Auntie Ru, whom she had not spoken to since the one-line reply that had changed everything—*the tide shows everything eventually*—and wondered, briefly, whether Ru understood what was coming for her. Probably not. Ru believed in ledgers the way Jonah believed in weather: totally, professionally, as systems that could be mastered. But the cave's ledger was not a system. It was older than systems. It didn't compound. It just waited.

She packed the map case. Strapped it high on her thigh, checked the straps twice, then a third time because the first two were habit and the third was fear.

At the rail, Teodoro handed her one more thing without ceremony: a folded page of his own handwriting, a translation of the star-charts' marginal notes, the ones in his grandmother's hand. *When the sky closes, trust the water's smell*, one note read. *Rain over deep water smells of nothing. Rain over land smells of mud and leaves. The old pilots steered by their noses when the stars went out.*

Jonah folded it into the map case with the documents. Two kinds of inheritance, riding together against her skin: the truth, and the instructions for surviving long enough to deliver it.

"One more question," she said, and this time she asked it straight, no deflection, because the hour was too late for anything else. "When this is over—whatever 'over' means—what happens to us? To you and me?"

Teodoro looked at her for a long moment, and the lamplight caught the grey in his beard and the stubbornness underneath it, and he said, "Ask me when you're back. I'll have an answer by then. I'm working on it."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It's a promissory note." He almost smiled. "I'm told those can be called in."

She stepped off the rail-side platform into the dark, down toward the boat that would take them to the edge of everything, and above her the clouds were already turning, slow and vast, the core assembling itself over the strait like a cathedral nobody had asked for. Somewhere north of it, a magistrate she'd never met was sleeping in a city full of lawyers paid to make sure she never arrived.

Two hundred and ten miles. One gap in the storm. Two pages against her thigh.

She set her watch by the tide tables and went to find Pak Usman's boat.
