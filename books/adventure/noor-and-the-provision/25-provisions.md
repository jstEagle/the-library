## 25. Provisions

The rain had settled into its long argument with the coast by the time Jonah woke in the stilt house above Ibu Salmah's compound—a steady, gray percussion on the atap roof that made the whole structure breathe, creaking and sighing like something alive trying to sleep through a storm. She lay under a mosquito net gone soft with damp and listened to it, and for the first time in five years she did not listen to it the way a fugitive listens: cataloguing sounds, sorting engine from surf from footsteps. She just listened.

Below her, the village was already moving. It moved quietly in weather like this—no one hurried, because hurrying was how you slipped on the boardwalks and cracked your head open—but it moved all the same. Fishermen mending nets under the eaves of their sheds. Children running messages between houses with oilcloth-wrapped bundles held against their chests like infants. Somewhere a woman pounding rice in a wooden mortar, each stroke arriving through the rain a half-beat late, patient as a metronome.

Jonah came down the ladder into Salmah's kitchen, where the old woman sat shelling shrimp with a knife worn to a sliver, and found Teodoro already there, cross-legged on the woven mat, ink-stained fingers sorting papers into three piles while a cat of no fixed opinion supervised from the doorway.

"You slept," he said, not looking up. It wasn't quite an accusation. It was close.

"Some."

"Good. You'll need it." He tapped one of the piles. "I've finished the copies. Three sets. One goes with us, one stays buried here—I've marked the spot only Salmah knows—and one—" He hesitated, and the hesitation had a shape to it, a weight he'd been carrying since the courthouse steps. "One needs to reach someone outside the coast entirely. Someone who isn't us. If we don't come back."

"Cheerful," Jonah said, and took the coffee he poured her without comment. It was terrible coffee, burnt twice over on a spirit stove, and she drank it down like medicine, which after everything it nearly was.

Salmah looked up from her shrimp. "You are both still planning to fly tomorrow," she said. Not a question. Her voice had the flatness of a woman who had asked once already and been answered, and was now simply confirming that the answer hadn't changed overnight, the way answers did when people slept on them.

"Tomorrow," Jonah said. "At first light. Before the barometer finishes falling."

"Then today you prepare." Salmah pointed the shrimp knife at the door, at the wall of rain beyond it. "And today you do not leave this house until dark. The Syndicate has eyes on the road to the strip. Pak Usman saw a man asking questions at the fish market yesterday—stranger, city shoes, wrong questions. Asking about a woman pilot and a thin man with glasses." She shrugged, unbothered, the way she was unbothered by most things, as though the world were a tide and she'd been reading it longer than anyone alive. "So today you are invisible. Today you mend things. Tonight my nephews carry your crates to the plane in fishing baskets, among the night's catch, and no one counts what leaves a fishing village at two in the morning."

Jonah set down her cup. "You've done this before," she said.

"Ibu Salmah has done many things before," the old woman said, with enormous dignity, and went back to her shrimp.

* * *

The day arranged itself into work, and work, Jonah discovered, was its own kind of shelter.

They spread out in the covered space beneath the stilt house—the dry gap between floorboards and mud where boats were stored in monsoon season—and laid the plane's troubles out like patients. The Cessna was parked not at Karang Laut's strip but in a cleared paddock two kilometers inland, hidden under palm-frond camouflage that Salmah's nephews had cut and layered with the seriousness of men building a shrine. Getting to it tonight would be the dangerous part. Getting it ready was today's work, and today's work could be done with hands.

There was so much to do. There always was, with a plane that had lived hard for five years and never once spent a night indoors.

The left main tire had a slow leak that Jonah had been nursing since the channel run—topping it with a hand pump every morning, watching the pressure gauge like a pulse. They pulled the wheel, found the puncture with a basin of water and a child's fascination, and patched it properly for the first time in months. Teodoro held the rubber while Jonah worked the cement, and they discovered without discussing it that they worked well together in silence—that silence between them had stopped being awkward somewhere around the second descent into the cave and had become instead a kind of language, a third person present in every room.

The oil needed changing. The air filter was choked with salt crust and cave dust. The fuel strainer had grit in it that made Jonah's stomach clench when she saw it—grit meant water in the tanks, water meant ice at altitude, ice meant the storm flight becoming a shorter story than either of them intended. They drained the sumps until the fuel ran clear and golden, and Jonah said nothing about what she'd been thinking, and Teodoro said nothing about what he could see her thinking, and the cat watched them both with the serene contempt of a creature that had never lied about anything.

Midmorning, Salmah's youngest nephew—a boy of maybe ten named Arif, all elbows and solemnity—appeared with a tray of fried bananas and stood watching them work with his hands behind his back like a foreman.

"Your grandmother sent these," Jonah said.

"She said to tell you"—Arif delivered it with the careful precision of a boy entrusted with state business—"that the plane is like a boat, and boats must be fed before big water."

Teodoro laughed for the first time in days, a real laugh, startled out of him, and Jonah felt something in her chest loosen that had been clenched since Auntie Ru's last radio call. She took a banana and ate it sitting on the wheel chock, and the sweetness of it seemed impossible given everything, given the storm massing offshore and the enforcer who'd stolen the certified copy and the hangar she still hadn't seen since the booby trap, and she decided—consciously, deliberately, the way you decide to keep breathing—to let it be possible anyway.

* * *

By afternoon the rain had thickened from percussion to roar, and the light under the stilt house had gone the color of old pewter. They worked by lantern. Jonah re-cabled the elevator linkage that had developed a worrying play during the interception—her hands remembering the feel of it even as her mind kept snagging on the arithmetic of tomorrow's route, the storm core, the Syndicate field they'd have to overfly, the courthouse window measured now in hours rather than days.

It was Teodoro who broke first. Not with words—with a question he'd clearly been holding for hours, set down between them as carefully as if it were one of his documents.

"The route," he said. "Can I see it?"

Jonah wiped her hands and got out the chart. She'd drawn it the night before in pencil, in the stilt house, by lamplight: a dogleg north along the coast to stay under the Syndicate's patrol altitudes, then a hard turn west across open water straight into the monsoon's advancing face, then—if the plane, the weather, and God permitted—a final approach into the capital from the south, low, under the commercial corridors, coming up on the courthouse district from a direction no one watched because no one was fool enough to come from the sea in this season.

She spread it on an upturned crate and weighted the corners with wrenches.

Teodoro studied it for a long time. His finger traced the line, stopped at the turn over open water, and stayed there.

"This is the part I don't understand," he said. "This leg is four hundred kilometers. In the eye of a monsoon. In a plane we patched with sailcloth and prayer." He looked up. "Why not go around? South, east, any way but through."

"Because every way around is watched." She kept her voice level, pilot-flat, the voice she used for weather briefings. "The Syndicate owns the coastal fields, the charter companies, half the fuel docks. Going around means being seen, being reported, being intercepted over land where they can put men on a road ahead of me. Through the storm, nobody follows. Nobody *can* follow. The weather is the one thing Auntie Ru doesn't own." She paused. "Yet."

"And if the weather kills us instead?"

"Then the copies do their work. That's why there are three sets." She met his eyes. "That's the whole design, Teo. Nothing we carry exists in only one place. Not the documents, not us. We're redundant cargo."

Something moved across his face—not fear exactly, and not comfort, but the particular expression of a man who has spent forty years among records and recognizes good filing when he sees it. "Redundant cargo," he repeated softly. "Yes. All right. Yes."

He bent back over the chart, and after a moment he began, unprompted, to annotate it—wind vectors from memory, from the almanacs he'd memorized in another life, headings corrected for drift, the compass error of her particular compass noted in his small precise hand. Jonah watched him do it and understood that she was looking at the reason she'd survive tomorrow. Not the plane. Not the plan. This: a man who loved accuracy more than safety, kneeling in the mud under a stilt house, correcting her map for free.

* * *

Dusk came early under the storm, the way it does—arriving not as sunset but as a slow dimming, the world's colors drained out one by one until the lanterns were the only truth left. And it was in that gray half-light, while the rain eased for the first time all day into something almost gentle, that Teodoro Reyes did the thing he had sworn for forty years he would never do.

Jonah was stowing tools when he called her name—her actual name, not "pilot," not "Zhou," the way he'd taken to saying it lately, carefully, as though handling something fragile.

She turned. He was standing by the lantern with a paper bag in his hands. A real paper bag, the kind rice came in, soft with age and handling, folded over twice at the top the way his family had apparently folded everything, documents and grief alike.

"I want you to take this," he said. "On the flight. Not in the crate with the copies. On your person."

"What is it?"

"Open it when we're airborne tomorrow. Not before." He held it out. "I mean that. If you open it now, I'll take it back, and I'll mean that too."

She looked at him. He was wet to the elbows, ink-stained, hollow-eyed from weeks of fear and purpose, and he was holding the bag out with both hands the way you hand over something breakable—or something that has already broken you once.

"Teo. What is it?"

"It's the stamp." His voice was steady, which cost him; she could see it costing him. "My family's archive stamp. Bronze. Older than the colony. My grandfather pressed it into every record the family ever certified, and my father pressed it into the ones he sold, and I swore when I was nineteen years old, standing in the ashes of our house, that no one would ever take it from me again. No one. Not the Syndicate. Not the magistrate. Not—" he exhaled— "not anyone."

"But you're giving it to me."

"I'm entrusting it to you. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point." He pushed the bag toward her until she had no choice but to take it or insult him. "If we're stopped tomorrow—if they search us, if they take the documents—they'll take everything I'm carrying, because they'll know what I am. But they won't know to look at you for this. You've spent five years being deniable. Be deniable one more day, carrying the one object on this coast that can make a forged ledger provably forged."

She weighed the bag in her hand. Light. Impossibly light for what he said it was.

"Why now?" she asked. "You've had weeks to decide this."

"Because last night I heard you talking to Salmah's grandson." His mouth did something complicated. "The boy asked you if you were afraid, and you told him the truth—you said yes, but that afraid was better than certain, because certain was what the Syndicate was, and certainty was how they burned villages. And I thought: she understands it. What the stamp is. It isn't authority. It's the opposite of certainty. It's a promise that someone checked." He looked away, out at the rain. "I carried it for forty years like a wound. I'd like, for one day, to carry it like a tool instead. And I can't do that unless it's in the hands of someone who might actually use it."

Jonah folded her fingers closed around the bag. It was warm from his hands, which undid her more than anything he'd said.

"I'll give it back," she said.

"If we live, I know you will." He almost smiled. "That's not why I'm giving it to you. I'm giving it to you because if we don't live, it should be found in the pocket of a woman who flew into a storm to tell the truth—not in the ashes of a man who spent his whole life reading about courage and never once filing any."

* * *

Two in the morning. The rain had thinned to a drifting mist, and the village moved.

It moved the way Salmah had promised: silently, in fishing baskets. Jonah watched from the shadow of the stilt house as six of Salmah's nephews ferried the crates down the black boardwalks on shoulder poles, each basket riding above a layer of ice and herring, the documents sealed in oilcloth beneath, the whole arrangement smelling overwhelmingly of fish—which was, Jonah reflected, the best camouflage currency on the coast. No inspector in the archipelago opens a basket of herring at two in the morning unless he absolutely must.

She and Teodoro walked last, hooded, carrying the light things: the charts, the survival kit, the bag. At the edge of the village Salmah waited with a lantern turned low, and she took Jonah's forearm in a grip that had hauled nets for sixty years and was not going to be argued with.

"Heard anything?" Jonah asked.

"One thing." Salmah's face in the lantern light was carved and calm. "Pak Usman says the Syndicate's watcher left the market at midnight. Packed his questions and went. Either they've stopped looking for you—" her grip tightened— "or they've stopped needing to."

"They've stopped needing to," Jonah said. "Ru knows where we are. She's known for days. She's letting us load."

"Then fly before she stops letting." Salmah released her arm and stepped back, and for a moment the old woman looked at her—not at the pilot, not at the courier, but at her, whatever remained underneath all the aliases—and said, quietly, in the voice she probably used for her own children once: "Bring the ledgers home, girl. The coast has waited long enough to read its own name."

* * *

The paddock was a black square of deeper black, the Cessna under its palm fronds like a sleeping animal. The nephews stripped the camouflage in minutes and melted back into the treeline without a word, professionals, and then it was just the two of them and the airplane and the sound of the sea working the reef in the dark.

Jonah walked the preflight with her flashlight cupped in her palm, checking everything twice, and found the plane exactly as they'd left it—tires firm, tanks full, control surfaces free, the patched cowling lashed down. She put her hand flat on the fuselage for a moment, the way you'd check a horse, and felt the metal cold and honest under her palm. Five years of running, and this battered yellow-and-white machine was the longest relationship of her life.

Teodoro loaded the last crate and climbed in, folding his long frame into the right seat with the document case strapped across his body. Jonah settled into the left seat and ran the checklist by memory, by touch, by the blue glow of the instrument lamps, and her hands were steady, and she noticed they were steady, and marveled at it.

She reached the item at the bottom of the checklist—the one she'd added herself, years ago, as a private joke that had stopped being funny: *cargo manifest — confirm nothing aboard you can't deny.* She thought of it. She almost laughed. Everything aboard this plane tonight was the opposite of deniable. Four hundred pages of other people's debts, a bronze stamp older than the colony, and two passengers whose names were about to be spoken aloud in a courtroom.

She keyed the radio for one transmission, low power, to a frequency Salmah's nephew had given her—a fisherman's channel, monitored by friends.

"Karang Laut, Karang Laut. Ghost departing. Tell the auntie the tide is turning."

Static. Then, faint through the rain-hiss, a voice she knew: *"Ghost, be advised—weather window closing faster than forecast. Barometer at the capital dropped twelve points in the last hour. Whatever you're doing, do it now."*

Jonah looked at the eastern sky, where the clouds had begun to organize themselves into the long terrible shelves she knew too well, and beside her Teodoro opened the document case and took out his star-charts, and in her jacket pocket the paper bag rode warm against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Mag heading zero-nine-five," she said, and pushed the throttle forward, and the propeller bit the wet grass, and the paddock lights of Karang Laut fell away behind them into the dark.

Ahead, the storm was waiting. So, she had no doubt, was everything else.
