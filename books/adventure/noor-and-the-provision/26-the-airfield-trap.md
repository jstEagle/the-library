## 26. The Airfield Trap

The crossing took forty minutes and cost her ten years.

Jonah flew it low, under the cloud deck, skimming a sea gone the color of gunmetal, and the whole way she watched the eastern horizon where the storm was assembling itself with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry. Teodoro sat beside her with the document case strapped across his chest like a child, one hand flat on it, the other braced against the frame of the door, and he did not speak, because he understood—as she did—that the silence was part of the fuel. Every word spent now was a word they might need later, when the radio was screaming static and the needles were lying.

They were not flying to the capital. Not yet. The *Kestrel*—the old de Havilland that had carried her name and her lies for five years—was still parked on the grass at Karang Laut, and the Syndicate would be watching that grass the way a spider watches the center of its web. So Jonah had done what she always did when the web closed: she went sideways. Forty minutes south by west lay Tanjung Setiu, a disused RAF emergency strip from another war, all coral gravel and waist-high lalang, where a man named Pak Din kept three hangars for boats he never finished repairing—and where, two monsoons ago, Jonah had paid him in cash to house a second airframe under a tarp and tell no one, not even the sea, that it existed.

A Piper Cub, older than she was, fabric wings, no radio worth trusting, a compass that wandered like a drunk uncle. She'd bought it with the last clean money she ever earned and told herself it was her escape hatch. Tonight it was going to be a courier.

She brought it in over the tree line with the lights off, landing on instinct and the pale smear of the strip, and the Cub's tires hit coral gravel with a sound like knuckles cracking. Teodoro exhaled beside her—a small, controlled sound, the sound of a man who had been holding his breath for forty minutes and was furious about it.

"You enjoy that," he said.

"No," Jonah said, and found, to her mild surprise, that it was true. "I used to. It's just arithmetic now."

Pak Din came out of his shack with a hurricane lamp held high, squinting into the rain that had begun to fall in fat, indifferent drops. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a mooring post, and he looked at Jonah, then at the stranger climbing out of her borrowed longboat's replacement—a fisherman's skiff they'd hired at the river mouth with money Salmah had pressed on them and refused to discuss—then back at Jonah, and asked exactly nothing. That was why she paid him double.

"The tarp," she said. "Is she dry?"

"Dry as anything is, this season." He lifted the lamp higher. "You're flying tonight?"

"I'm looking tonight."

He grunted, which from Pak Din was a paragraph, and led them down the strip toward the middle hangar, his lamp swinging shadows across the lalang. The rain thickened as they walked, drumming on the tin roof ahead of them, and Jonah felt the familiar tightening in her chest that she had learned, over five years, to trust more than any instrument: the animal sense that the world had rearranged itself slightly while her back was turned.

She stopped walking.

"Pak Din," she said quietly. "Has anyone come here? Anyone at all. Since the monsoon warning."

The old man turned. In the lamplight his face was a map of every coast he'd ever sailed, and for a moment she thought he would lie to her, kindly, the way coasts lie. Then he set the lamp down on an oil drum with great care, as though setting down something fragile, and said:

"A boat came Tuesday. Two men. Said they were surveyors for the fisheries board." His eyes found hers. "I told them I keep boats here, not airplanes. Which is true, because you told me to say it." A pause, the rain filling it. "But they walked the strip anyway. All of it. Measuring with their feet."

Jonah stood very still. Rain ran off the brim of her cap and down the back of her collar, cold as a finger drawn along the spine.

"Which hangar did they look in?"

"They didn't open any," Pak Din said. "That's what I couldn't make sense of. Surveyors open things. These men just—" he made a vague gesture, a man smoothing a bedsheet, "—walked. And left something at your end of the strip, maybe. Or took something. I didn't see. I mind my own business. That's my whole profession."

Teodoro had gone very quiet beside her, and she could feel him arriving at the same arithmetic she was: two men, measuring with their feet, leaving without opening anything. Men who came to a place and touched nothing were either cowards—or they had already finished their work before they arrived.

"Thank you," she said to Pak Din. "Go inside. Lock the shack. If you hear anything from this hangar tonight that isn't us, walk south and don't stop."

He picked up his lamp. For a moment his hand rested on her forearm, brief and hard, an old man's blessing delivered like a handshake. "Fly straight," he said, which was the only prayer he owned, and then he was a bobbing light receding down the strip, and then he was rain.

* * *

The hangar door stuck the way it always stuck, swollen in the wet, and Jonah put her shoulder into it with a grunt and felt it give all at once, exhaling them into darkness that smelled of diesel, canvas, coconut oil, and time. She found the switch by memory. One bulb burned overhead, dim and brown, swinging faintly on its wire.

Under the tarp, the Cub waited. She pulled the canvas off in one motion, the way you pull a bandage, and stood looking at her escape hatch in the brown light.

At first glance, everything was right. Fabric taut, tires up, pitot cover on, the little tailwheel chocked with the same split coconut log she'd chocked it with two years ago. At first glance, everything was right, and that was precisely what was wrong. Jonah had spent five years learning the difference between a thing being untouched and a thing being *arranged*, and the Cub was arranged. The chock log sat too square to the tire. The tarp folds were too even. Someone had folded her airplane back into place after handling it, the way Auntie Ru's clerks folded documents—with respect, and with intent.

"Don't touch it," she said.

"I wasn't going to." Teodoro had stopped three paces inside the door, and he stood with his hands in his pockets and his head slightly tilted, reading the room the way he read everything, patiently, line by line. "The bulb," he said.

"What about it?"

"It's new."

She looked up. He was right. The bulb was new—brighter than the brown dying thing she remembered, its glass clean, its filament burning steady while the wind worried the roof. New bulb meant someone had needed to see what they were doing. Someone had worked here, under good light, for as long as the work required.

Jonah crouched by the portside door of the Cub and looked underneath, along the fuselage line, and there it was.

Wired to the starter solenoid, wrapped in friction tape with the neatness of a surgeon or a bomb-maker—which are often the same trade—a block of grey plastic explosive the size of a cigarette packet, and running from it, taped along the airframe and disappearing up into the cockpit, a wire. A thin, patient wire. Connected, when she followed it with her eyes and then with her hands, hovering, touching nothing, to the ignition switch itself.

Her debt, made literal. Five years of compounding interest, rendered in plastic and copper, waiting in the dark for her thumb.

She sat back on her heels. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking—not the fine tremor of cold but the deep irregular shake of the body trying to do two things at once, flee and fix, and being ordered by the mind to do neither yet.

"Well," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "There it is."

Teodoro crouched beside her. He didn't reach for it. He looked, for a long moment, the way he looked at a damaged page in an archive—assessing first, grieving later. "If you'd turned the key," he said, "at Karang Laut. If we'd flown the Kestrel out of there tonight instead of coming here—"

"Then it wouldn't have mattered. This one's wired to this one's ignition." She heard her own voice go flat and clinical and was grateful for it. Flat was survivable. "They knew I kept a spare. They've known for how long, I don't know. Long enough to be polite about it." She laughed, once, a short ugly sound. "Do you understand what this is? This isn't sabotage. Sabotage is a message you send to an enemy. This is collateral. This is what you do to an *asset*. You don't burn the asset—you encumber it. You wire the debt to the ignition so that starting the engine means paying it." She stared at the grey block. "Auntie Ru isn't trying to kill me. Killing me costs them a pilot. This is a lien."

"Or," Teodoro said mildly, "it is a bomb, and the philosophy can wait until we are not kneeling next to it."

* * *

They worked by the single new bulb, and the rain on the tin roof was so loud they had to lean close to speak, which meant they breathed the same air, which meant that when her hands shook, he saw it, and when his did not, she saw that too.

"I can't cut the wire at the charge," Jonah said. She had her hands in her lap now, deliberately, like a woman sitting on them at a funeral. "I don't know this arrangement. Some of them are anti-lift. Some of them are anti-cut. The only safe end is the far end—the switch. Disconnect there, and the circuit's dead no matter what the charge thinks."

"And if it's not the only safe end?"

"Then we die in a hangar in Tanjung Setiu and Pak Din inherits a mystery." She flexed her fingers. "Give me your knife. The small one."

He passed it handle-first, and then, instead of stepping back, he crouched closer, shoulder to shoulder with her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through her wet sleeve. "Tell me what you see," he said. "All of it. Out loud."

"Why?"

"Because your hands are shaking and mine aren't, and because in the archive we had a rule: never touch a damaged binding alone. Two people. One reads, one works. The reader sees what the worker cannot." He said it simply, without drama, the way he said everything, and she realized with a small internal shock that he had decided—sometime between the reef and the courthouse steps and this wet tin-roofed hour—to spend his steadiness on her, deliberately, the way you spend coin. "Read it to me, Jonah."

So she read it.

"The charge sits on the solenoid housing, taped at four points. Friction tape, black, stretched not wrapped—they stretched it, which means they wanted it thin. The lead wire runs forward along the longeron, taped every eight inches, very tidy, up over the firewall—" her voice caught slightly and she smoothed it flat again, "—into the cabin. Through the grommet where the magneto wires run. It's dressed into the existing loom. Whoever did this knows aircraft wiring. Not a mechanic—a rigger. Someone who's made things look factory-made before."

"Can you follow it by eye inside?"

"Partly. The loom's wrapped in harness tape. I'll have to open it." She swallowed. "Here's the ugly part. The switch end is the safe end only if the circuit is simple. Switch, wire, detonator. But if they've put a relay in—if disconnecting the switch arms a secondary—I won't know until I'm inside the loom, and by then my fingers are already there."

Teodoro was silent for a moment. Rain hammered the roof. Somewhere down the strip, the sea worked at the coral, patient as bookkeeping.

"Then we make the assumption explicit," he said at last. "We assume it is simple, because a relay requires parts, and parts require supply, and these men walked the strip measuring with their feet and carrying nothing visible. A simple circuit fits their logistics. A complex one doesn't." He turned his head and looked at her, and in the brown light his eyes were very clear. "It's an assumption. Name it as one, and work under it, and if it kills us, at least we'll have died correctly."

"That's the most librarian thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I contain libraries."

And absurdly, horribly, she laughed—really laughed, one breath of it, there under the bomb—and her hands, when she looked at them, had almost stopped shaking. Not entirely. Enough.

* * *

She opened the loom with the small knife, an inch at a time, while Teodoro read the wiring diagram aloud from memory of her muttering, tracing the factory circuits on a scrap of paper by lamplight, calling out colors—red, black, yellow-with-tracer—and she confirmed each against the real wires emerging from the opened harness, and slowly, strand by strand, the intruder revealed itself.

One wire. Grey insulation, newer than everything around it, dressed into the loom with a rigger's cunning but ultimately alone. No relay. No secondary. No junction. Just the one patient thread running from the switch to the charge, a single sentence written in copper: *start me and pay.*

"There's no trick," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded strange, far away, the voice of a woman reporting weather. "It's honest. It's the most honest thing the Syndicate has ever given me."

"Then finish it," Teodoro said quietly.

She cut the master battery lead first—dead aircraft, dead circuit, the safest possible state—and then, with the knife's smallest blade, she severed the grey wire at the switch terminal, one clean stroke, and the little copper end sprang away from the contact and lay against the panel like something released from a long-held breath.

Nothing happened. No thunder, no fire, no white light. Just the rain, and the bulb burning, and the two of them kneeling in a hangar full of diesel smell with their hearts going like trapped birds.

Jonah discovered she was gripping the knife so hard her knuckles had gone bloodless. She made herself let go, finger by finger.

"Out," Teodoro said. "Both of us. Now. We don't disarm the charge—we abandon the whole conversation. Outside, downwind, behind the drum store, and we send Pak Din's nephew for the police in the morning, and we let professionals render it inert."

"We don't have a morning. The tide window—"

"Jonah." He said her name the way you set down something heavy. "The charge stays. We fly the Kestrel problem another day—but we fly *this* aircraft only after that thing is rendered safe by someone whose hands don't belong to either of us. I have watched you be brave all week. Let me watch you be sensible for ten minutes."

She wanted to argue. She catalogued the arguments—time, tide, pursuit, the closing weather, the magistrate's deadline shrinking like a barometer falling—and then she looked at the grey block taped to her airplane's heart, and she got up, and she walked out into the rain with him, and did not stop walking until the drum store stood between them and the hangar.

* * *

They sat under the leaking eave of the drum store, sharing a flask of cold tea that tasted of tin, watching the hangar across the strip as though it might confess. The rain had settled into the long steady fall of a monsoon's working hours, neither rage nor rest.

"They knew," Jonah said finally. "About the Cub. Which means they've known since I bought it. Which means every clever thing I've done for two years—the spare registrations, the false strips, this whole sideways life—" she turned the flask in her hands, "—they've been reading it like a ledger. I thought I was writing in invisible ink. I was writing in their notebook."

"Yes," Teodoro said.

She glanced at him. "You could lie to me. Once. As a treat."

"I could," he agreed. "But you didn't marry the truth for its manners." He was quiet a moment, and then he said, in a different voice, lower: "My family kept records for four generations, and we told ourselves the same story. That secrecy was safety. That a thing hidden is a thing kept." He looked out at the rain. "Tonight there is a bomb wired to your escape, and my family's archive is ash, and I find myself thinking the lesson was never hide better. It was that hiding is just a slower way of being found."

Jonah said nothing. Across the strip, the hangar stood dark and dripping, and somewhere inside it her debt hung off her airplane's ignition switch like a pendant, patient, professional, waiting for her thumb.

Then Teodoro stiffened. His hand closed on her arm—not gently this time—and he turned his head toward the tree line at the southern end of the strip, where the lalang grew tall and the darkness pooled deepest between the lightning-less flashes of the overcast.

Down there, where no boat should be, where Pak Din's shack stood locked and dark, a light had blinked. Once. Twice. Deliberate. The rhythm of a signal, not a lantern.

And answering it, far off through the rain, from the direction of the sea, an engine started.
