## 27. Into the Core

The rain began as a rumor and became a wall.

Jonah had flown into weather before—every pilot who worked the Sunda coast had a story about a cell they'd threaded by luck or stupidity, usually both—but this was different. This was the monsoon's spine, the thing the fishermen tied their boats down for and then went indoors to pray about, and she was flying straight at it on purpose.

"Compass is swinging," Teodoro said from the right seat. His voice was steady in the way of a man who had decided that panic was a luxury item he could no longer afford. "Eleven degrees west, now fourteen. It's dancing."

"It'll dance worse before it stops." She fed the throttle forward another inch and felt the old Heron answer with a shudder that ran up through her boots and settled somewhere behind her sternum. The engine had been overhauled twice in five years by mechanics she'd paid partly in cash and partly in silence, and it had never once been asked to do what she was asking it to do now. "Tell me where we are."

Teodoro didn't reach for the chart clipped to his kneeboard. He closed his eyes instead, and Jonah glanced sideways long enough to see his lips moving—not praying, though God knew he had reason to, but reciting. Numbers. Bearings. The cave's star-charts, transcribed onto stone four hundred years ago by people who navigated these waters without a single instrument between them, and which he had spent three sleepless nights committing to memory because paper could be confiscated and memory, so far, could not.

"Wind should back us southeast of the direct line," he said. "The old pilots corrected for that by holding two points north of the passage markers. There's a current seam off Tanjung Bulan—the water runs lighter green against the deep blue. From altitude, in daylight, you can see it."

"It's not daylight."

"No," Teodoro agreed. "But the storm will light itself. When we're inside, you won't need my charts. You'll need to fly."

* * *

They had taken off from the strip at Karang Laut forty minutes earlier, in weather that any sane pilot would have called impossible and any desperate one would have called marginal. Jonah had qualified as neither sane nor merely desperate for days now. The documents were in a waterproof satchel strapped flat under Teodoro's seat—the forged charter, the ledger rubbings, the photographs of carvings that proved the Syndicate's entire claim to the coast was collateral on a debt its founder had once owed and never recorded. Somewhere behind them, Auntie Ru was waking to the news that her plane, her pilot, and her leverage had all gone up into a sky the color of wet slate.

Somewhere ahead was the capital, and a courthouse, and a magistrate named Wulan who was Ibu Salmah's niece and who would hold office hours beginning at nine.

Between here and there: three hundred kilometers of open sea, a monsoon core that meteorologists on the government band had described that morning in tones usually reserved for funerals, and an airfield belonging to the Marrow Syndicate sitting directly across their route like a hand laid flat across a table.

"You could still turn back," Teodoro said. He said it quietly, the way you offer someone a door you've already locked behind yourself. "No one would—"

"Yes, they would." She banked around a column of rain that reached, as far as she could see, from cloud base to sea. Lightning flickered inside it, slow and structural, like something thinking. "Ru would find out by noon. The scouts would burn what's left of Karang Laut, and this time they wouldn't stop at fish sheds. And the magistrate needs these papers before Ru's lawyers file anything, and Ru's lawyers file at dawn, because that's when lawyers are cheapest." She rolled the wings level. "There's no back, Teo. There's only through."

He looked at her for a moment longer than the turbulence strictly allowed. Then he nodded, once, and turned back to the grey.

Jonah realized, with a strange floating clarity that had nothing to do with altitude, that she meant it. For five years every heading she'd flown had been away from something—a debt, a name, a question about a burned village she'd never let herself finish asking. This was the first time in her flying life that the destination mattered more than the distance between her and whatever was chasing her. It didn't feel brave. It felt like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying, and discovering her arms still worked.

Then the rain hit them, and there was no more time for clarity.

* * *

The world went white and then black and then a strobing alternation of both, and the Heron began to talk to her in the only language that mattered: stick forces, slipstream, the pitch of the engines, the weight of water hammering the airframe like fists on a door.

"Instruments," she said, and heard how flat her own voice came out, stripped of everything but function. "Call them to me."

"Airspeed one-forty. Climbing—no, you're level, the needle's lying. Turn needle's stuck at ten degrees left." A bolt of lightning filled the cockpit, blue-white, close enough that she saw the skeleton of the rain around them, individual drops lit like beads on wires. Thunder arrived instantly, not a sound so much as a physical event, and the plane dropped thirty feet in a breath and came back up with a groan from the wing roots. "Attitude gyro's tumbling. Jonah, the gyro's gone."

"Gone is fine. We fly it by the seat." Which was a lie, or half of one; by the seat worked until your inner ear started voting with the storm, and then it killed you. She forced her eyes to the one gauge she trusted—the turn-and-bank, the oldest, dumbest instrument on the panel, all needles and honesty—and held the little airplane of it level while the real one tried to roll her over and introduce her to the sea.

The radio had died somewhere in the first minute. Or rather, it lived, but everything coming through it was static shaped vaguely like voices, the whole sky shouting at once. Below them, if below still meant anything, the sea was running in lines of white that even at two thousand feet looked like teeth.

"Ice," Teodoro said suddenly. "On the struts. On the leading edge—I can see it building."

She could feel it too, a heaviness creeping into the controls, the airfoil forgetting its own shape millimeter by millimeter. At this weight, in this air, ice wasn't a problem. It was a countdown.

"We climb?"

"We climb, we go deeper into the cell. We stay, we get heavy and slow and eventually the sea decides for us." She pulled the mixture rich, watched the fuel pressure quiver, made the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since takeoff. Fuel said ninety minutes of flying left, maybe eighty if the headwind lied to her, which headwinds always did. The direct route through the core was forty minutes if nothing went wrong. Everything had already gone wrong. "There's a gap," she said. "There's always a gap. Every cell has one—the eye, or near it. The fishermen call it the quiet room. We punch through the worst of it and come out the other side, and if we're lucky the eye gives us sixty miles of calm to sort ourselves out in."

"And if we're not lucky?"

"Then we find out what the plane's really made of."

Teodoro laughed—one short, startled bark, entirely involuntary—and she loved him a little for it, there in the dark and the noise, and put the thought away somewhere it couldn't distract her hands.

"Climbing," she said, and hauled the Heron's nose up into the black.

* * *

The next twenty minutes were the closest thing to a fight she'd ever been in, except the opponent was everywhere and invisible and did not tire.

Ice built on the wings until the stall warning horn moaned continuously, a thin dying note underneath the engine roar. Hail came through at eight thousand feet, white lumps the size of grapes cracking off the windscreen hard enough to star the plexiglass, and she flew with her head turned half-sideways, peering through the unbroken wedge of glass at the bottom corner, because looking directly into hail at closing speeds like theirs was how windscreens stopped being windscreens. Turbulence stopped being discrete events and became a condition, the air itself a churning solid, and twice the plane simply fell—fell the way stones fall, control surfaces slack, the world a smear of lightning and noise—and twice she caught it by feel alone, hands remembering things her mind had never learned.

Teodoro never stopped calling numbers. Even when the numbers were nonsense—even when the compass spun like a roulette wheel and the altimeter disagreed with the window by a thousand feet—he called them, steady as a metronome, because that was the job he'd given himself and he understood, the way she understood, that in a cockpit the worst thing anyone can do is go quiet.

"Two minutes," she shouted over the engine. "Maybe less. If the charts are right we break out past the far eyewall and—" The plane slammed sideways, a gust from a direction wind does not come from, and the right wingtip rose and kept rising and for one endless second they were banked past vertical, sea and sky trading places through the starred glass, and Jonah hauled the yoke into her chest and stood on the ball and brought the horizon back from wherever it had gone. Her shoulder screamed. She ignored it. "—and then it's calm," she finished, as if the sentence hadn't been interrupted by the end of the world.

"The charts are right," Teodoro said. Not bravado. Certainty, worn smooth by four centuries of family use. "My grandmother's people crossed this strait in praus with woven sails. They read the storm by the color of the water and the smell of the wind. They'd have called this—" the plane dropped again, fifty feet, a hundred, his voice riding it without a waver, "—they'd have called this a bad afternoon."

"Your grandmother's people were insane."

"They were accurate. It's not the same thing, but it's better."

And then, all at once, the way a fever breaks—

The rain thinned. Thickened. Thinned again, and this time stayed thin, and the hammering on the airframe eased from assault to argument to mutter. Ahead, impossibly, a soft grey luminosity, and then a shape: a vast circular wall of cloud rising on every side, towering, rotating slowly around a center of impossible stillness, and above it—for the first time since takeoff—stars.

The eye.

Jonah leveled the wings and let out a breath she'd apparently been holding since Karang Laut. In the sudden quiet the engines sounded almost embarrassed, settling back to a cruise drone. Ice still clung to the leading edges, shedding in ragged strips that tumbled away into the dark below. The airspeed crept back up. The turn needle pointed where she told it to point, obedient, forgiven.

"Oh," Teodoro said softly.

He had turned in his seat, pressed to the window, and Jonah risked a glance and understood. The eye of a typhoon over open water at night was not weather. It was architecture—a cathedral of cloud a hundred miles wide, walls going up and up into blackness, lightning flickering along their faces like lamps in distant corridors. And overhead, framed in the round throat of the storm, the stars: hard, cold, indifferent, exactly as they must have looked to the people who carved the charts in the cave, exactly as they looked to every navigator who had ever trusted the sky more than the sea.

"That's what they drew," Teodoro whispered. "That's what the ceiling chamber is. The dome with the holes drilled through it—that's this. They were mapping the eye of the storm. Four hundred years ago, standing in the dark with a lamp, they mapped *this*."

"Later," Jonah said, but gently, because some part of her wanted to look too, wanted to tip her head back and memorize it, and the other part of her knew the eye was borrowed time—a loan, like everything else in her life, compounding by the minute. "How far to the far wall?"

Teodoro tore himself from the window, checked the dead compass out of habit, then navigated the old way instead—by the stars, by the drift of the cloud walls, by the faint green-black line of the sea. "Twenty minutes of calm. Maybe twenty-five. Then the south eyewall, and it'll be as bad as what we just came through, possibly worse, and after that—" he exhaled, "—after that it's ordinary horrible weather to the capital."

"Ordinary horrible I can do." She trimmed the plane, flexed her right hand, which had begun to shake now that shaking was affordable, and looked down at the satchel under his knees. The truth of the coast, riding in a waterproof bag between a smuggler and an archivist, through the roof of a typhoon. If she'd put it in one of her old manifests, the cargo description would have read: *miscellaneous, value nil.*

"Jonah." Teodoro's voice had changed. She looked up.

Off their left wing, low, maybe six miles, running the same corridor of calm they were running—a set of navigation lights. Twin whites and a blinking red, climbing steadily, matching their course, matching their speed.

Not fishing boats. Fishing boats didn't fly.

"That's a charter," she said. "Twin-engine. That's a Syndicate charter."

The radio, dead with static all night, crackled once—and a voice came through the noise, calm and courteous and horribly familiar, the voice of a man who had sat across from her at Auntie Ru's floating restaurant and poured the tea.

*"Heron zero-nine, Heron zero-nine, this is Marrow flight service. You're off your filed route and in restricted airspace. Turn back now and we'll consider this a weather diversion."*

Teodoro looked at her. Jonah looked at the towering black wall of the far eyewall ahead, and at the charter sliding closer through the starlight, and felt something settle in her chest that was not fear—or not only fear—but the cold clean clarity of a woman who has finally run out of corridors.

"They want us to turn back," she said. "Into the storm we just survived. Because they think we're sane."

She pushed the nose down toward the sea, and the eyewall, and everything beyond it.

"Hold on to the documents, Teo. We're going through the middle."
