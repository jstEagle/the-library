## 28. Interception

*Teodoro*

The storm had a shape. Teodoro hadn't known that until this morning, until Jonah had spread her charts across the hangar floor and weighted the corners with wrenches and drawn him into it with a pencil stub — the eyewall like a ring drawn around an eye, the feeder bands spiraling out of it like the whorls of a fingerprint. She'd traced their route through it twice, once with the pencil and once with her finger, and both times her finger stopped at the same place: a gap in the western band, a corridor of merely terrible weather between two corridors of lethal weather.

"Here," she'd said. "We punch through here, or we don't punch through at all."

Now they were in it, and the shape was nothing like the drawing. The drawing had been flat. This was a country with walls.

Teodoro sat in the right-hand seat with the cave's star-charts spread across his knees, held down by his elbows because there was nowhere else to hold them. The cabin pitched and rolled and dropped, and each drop lifted the papers off his thighs and he caught them against the instrument panel and lost his place and found it again. His job was to read the sky the way the cave's carvers had read it — the old navigators who'd charted these waters by stars that had drifted since, by winds that had names no one used anymore. He'd memorized three of the charts in the weeks since the second descent. He'd thought that was obsession. It turned out it was preparation.

"Say your heading again," Jonah said. Her voice came through the headset flattened by static, stripped of everything but function.

"Two-seven-one," Teodoro said. "Hold two-seven-one until I say the wind goes left."

"And when the wind goes left?"

"Then we're in the eyewall's throat, and you climb."

"And if I climb too early?"

"Then we come out the top of the cell into the charter's arms."

She grunted — not agreement exactly, more the sound of a woman filing instructions where she could reach them. The plane bucked again, hard enough that the seatbelt bit into Teodoro's shoulder, and rain hit the windscreen sideways, not falling but thrown, as if the whole ocean had been picked up and hurled at them in sheets. Beyond the glass there was no horizon, no up, no down. There was grey, and darker grey, and once — for half a second — a smear of green far below that might have been an island or might have been his own eyes lying to him.

He had never been afraid like this before. He had been afraid in archives, afraid of men with matches, afraid in the cave when the scouts' engine had sounded overhead like the end of the world clearing its throat. But this was different. This was fear without a person in it. There was no one to hide from, no door to bar, no ledger to misfile. The storm did not want anything from him. It simply was, enormous and indifferent, and somewhere inside its indifference was a single small airplane with two people in it who were betting their lives on a dead navigator's arithmetic.

"You're quiet," Jonah said.

"I'm reading."

"Read out loud, then. I want to know you're still back there."

So he read. Wind southwest, veering. Pressure falling slower now — that was good, that meant the core was near. Swell direction from the old chart: *waves run with the monsoon's left hand.* He said it all in a flat voice, and Jonah said "copy" after each item like a priest responding at Mass, and the liturgy of it steadied them both. That was the thing about the cave's records, he was learning. They had been kept by people who expected storms. Every chart had a margin note in that half-legible script: *if the sky turns, do this.* Four hundred years ago someone had sat in a stone chamber and written instructions for a day exactly like this one, for strangers they would never meet, and those strangers were Teodoro Reyes and a smuggler named Jonah Zhou, and the instructions were holding.

The wind went left.

"Climb," Teodoro said. "Climb now."

Jonah hauled back on the yoke and the world tilted and the engine screamed and for eleven seconds — he counted them, he couldn't help counting them — the plane climbed through rain so dense it was less water than substance, a grey wool they were pushing through, and then the wool tore open and there was light.

Not sunlight. Something stranger. The eye of the storm was a cathedral of cloud, walls rising on every side, blue-black and green-tinged, lit from above by a disc of pale washed sky like the oculus of some ruined dome. The sea below was visible for the first time in an hour — ragged, white-toothed, moving in a hundred directions at once, but visible. And the air itself had gone eerily, horribly calm. Birds rode the eye with them: three frigatebirds, tattered and exhausted, keeping pace off the left wingtip as if the plane were a larger wounded bird they'd decided to trust.

Jonah leveled off and exhaled so long it seemed theatrical, except Teodoro could see her hands on the yoke and knew it wasn't theater. It was a body remembering how to breathe.

"The birds," she said. "Look at the birds."

"I see them."

"They ride the eye. Fishermen say it. The eye is a road." She glanced over at him, and even now, even here, there was something wry in it. "Your ancestors probably wrote that down too."

"They wrote down everything," Teodoro said. "That was the problem."

They had perhaps four minutes of the eye. Maybe five. On the far side waited the eastern eyewall, which Jonah had described, in the hangar, with the flatness of a woman reading a menu, as "the bad one." And beyond that — Teodoro checked his watch, then wished he hadn't — the capital's approach corridor, and whatever the Syndicate had waiting there.

What the Syndicate had waiting there turned out to be already in the air.

It came at them across the eye like a rumor given wings: first a glint, then a shape, then a twin-engine charter rising out of the southern wall of cloud, climbing fast, ugly and purposeful, its paint the color of wet slate. It leveled off two hundred meters off their right wing and matched their speed with the insolent ease of something that cost more than everything Teodoro had ever owned.

The radio crackled.

*Unidentified twin-oh-four, this is aircraft calling on behalf of your creditors. We are instructed to escort you. Deviation will be interpreted as admission.*

Jonah didn't answer. She flew with her left hand and reached over and clicked the radio off entirely, and the silence that followed was so complete that Teodoro could hear the frigatebirds crying.

"You're not going to talk to them?"

"What would I say?" Jonah's eyes stayed forward, on the charter, on the wall of cloud beyond it. "'Yes, please escort me to the people who wired my hangar'?" She shook her head once. "They're not here to escort. They're here to herd. Wingtip to wingtip, all the way to the Syndicate field, where there'll be a reception committee and a very secure room where my testimony gets edited before anyone hears it."

"And how do we avoid being herded?"

"We don't avoid it," Jonah said. "We go through it."

She pointed — one finger, steady despite everything — at the southeastern wall of the eye. At the eyewall. At the thing they had just spent an hour and most of their luck surviving.

"That," she said, "is our exit."

* * *

*Jonah*

There is a moment in every pilot's life — Jonah had always assumed it was hypothetical, a story other pilots told, the way sailors told stories of sea serpents — when the only remaining option is the one every instinct screams against. She was in it now. She was living the story she'd heard told in fuel docks and bush strips for five years, always by someone else, always ending badly.

The charter had position on them. It had radios that worked, engines that were newer, a company behind it with lawyers and enforcers and, presumably, orders that ended in the word *whatever necessary*. What Jonah had was a plane full of documents that could unmake the Syndicate, an archivist who navigated by four-hundred-year-old star-charts, three frigatebirds, and roughly ninety seconds of the eye's false peace remaining before the eastern wall closed over them like a book.

And she had one other thing. She had the certain knowledge that the man flying that charter was sane.

Sane men did not fly into eyewalls. Sane men had salaries, and flight plans, and a healthy animal respect for wind shear and hail and the specific way a storm cell could swallow an aircraft and digest it without ever noticing. Whatever Auntie Ru had promised that pilot, it wasn't worth the eastern eyewall. Nothing was worth the eastern eyewall. Jonah knew that better than anyone alive, because she was about to bet her life that it was true.

"He'll follow us," Teodoro said. It wasn't a question. He was watching the charter the way he watched everything — cataloguing, comparing, cross-referencing against something in his head. "If he follows us, we die together. If he doesn't, we live and he has to explain to Ru why he lost a paper airplane to weather."

"He won't follow," Jonah said. "That's the bet. That's the whole bet." She rolled the plane onto its left wing, gently, the way you'd tilt a cup. "Everything I have left, on one man's sanity."

The radio, which she'd switched back on out of some stubborn professionalism, crackled again. The voice was different this time — calmer, almost kind, the voice of a man doing a difficult job.

*Miss Zhou. I've read your file. I grew up two islands from Karang Laut. Nobody pays me enough to follow you into that wall. Go.*

For a moment — half a heartbeat — Jonah almost loved him.

Then she pushed the nose down and flew into the worst weather on earth.

The eastern eyewall took the plane the way the sea takes a dropped coin: instantly, totally, without malice. The light died. The rain returned, but harder now, harder than anything she'd flown through in five years of flying through things — rain like gravel, like shot, hammering the airframe with a sound she felt in her teeth. Hail came with it, white flecks stinging past the windscreen. The updrafts hit like fists under the fuselage; the downdrafts answered like hands pressing her toward a sea she couldn't see. The instruments spun and lied. The artificial horizon tumbled over itself, drunk. Somewhere below was the ocean, and somewhere above was the top of the storm, and Jonah Zhou had no idea which way was which, and for the first time since Karang Laut burned she was not thinking about debts or ledgers or the look on Ibu Salmah's face.

She was thinking about the next ten seconds. Only the next ten seconds. It was, she realized distantly, the cleanest her mind had been in years.

"Heading," she shouted, though the headset made shouting unnecessary — she shouted anyway, because terror wants noise.

"Two-six-five!" Teodoro's voice came back level, infuriatingly level, the voice of a man reading inventory. "Wind's backed around — we're in the inflow — hold two-six-five and descend fifty meters, the shear layer thins—"

"How do you know that?"

"Margin note!" He actually laughed, there in the dark, there in the hammering hail. "Chart seven! *In the left-hand wall, sink to ride beneath the shear.* Someone wrote it down, Jonah! Someone four hundred years ago wrote it down!"

She sank. Fifty meters, blind, into the one altitude band where the turbulence might be survivable, on the authority of a dead archivist's marginal handwriting. The plane dropped into a slightly gentler violence — the difference, she thought wildly, between being beaten with a chair and beaten with a stick — and held there, shuddering, crawling eastward through the black.

Time went strange. She stopped checking the clock. She flew by feel and by Teodoro's voice, and Teodoro's voice became the whole world: headings, pressure readings, fragments of ancient script recited like rope thrown to a drowning woman. *Waves run with the monsoon's left hand. When the rain turns warm, you are through.* The rain turned warm. She almost sobbed when it did — a physical change on her bare knuckles where they gripped the yoke, cold to less cold, and then, abruptly, to warm, and the hail stopped, and the grey ahead of them began, impossibly, to thin.

They broke out of the storm's trailing edge at four hundred meters, into hard driving rain and low scud, but real air, flyable air, air with a horizon in it somewhere if you squinted. The capital lay ahead under the storm's skirt: a smudge of towers and harbor cranes, half-drowned in cloud, glittering where lightning behind them lit it from the wrong side.

The charter was gone. Of course it was gone. No one sane had followed them in.

Jonah laughed. It came out of her sideways, cracked, halfway to a sob, and she let it, because there was no one aboard to hear it who hadn't earned the hearing.

"Teodoro."

"Yes."

"We're alive."

"I noticed." A pause, and then, drier than the air they were finally flying through: "I'm updating the margin notes. *When the rain turns warm, you are through, and the pilot laughs like a madwoman.*"

* * *

*Teodoro*

The capital assembled itself out of the rain in pieces, the way things do when you've nearly died looking at them. First the breakwater, a long pale line combing white. Then the harbor, brown-green and crowded, every boat that could float apparently sheltering in it. Then the rooftops, tin and tile, steaming. Then the courthouse — Salmah's niece's courthouse — a heavy stone building on its hill above the town, its dome dark against the last of the storm cloud, and beside it the clock tower, and beside that, on the wet boulevard below, the first figures Teodoro had seen in hours: umbrellas, bicycles, a man hauling a cart, ordinary life going on as if the sky had not just tried to kill them.

Ordinary life. He'd forgotten what it looked like from above. From above it looked fragile and miraculous, a thousand small fires burning in a gale.

Jonah brought them in on the flooded grass strip behind the magistrate's residence, the only landing within twenty kilometers that wasn't either closed or watched, and the landing was rougher than any of them deserved and smoother than any of them expected. The plane slewed, bogged to its axles in standing water, and stopped. Rain drummed the fabric wings. Neither of them moved for a long moment.

Then Teodoro gathered the documents — all of them, the mislabeled copies, the photographs, the pages they'd risked everything for twice — wrapped them in oilcloth, and zipped them inside his shirt, against his skin, where forty years of searching had taught him important things belonged.

They waded out through knee-deep water toward the lights of the residence. A woman was waiting on the veranda with a lantern — not the magistrate herself, but her clerk, a young man with a serious face and a dry towel, and behind him, through the window, Teodoro could see lamplight and a desk cleared and ready, and a telephone already lifted from its hook.

The clerk looked at the two of them — soaked, salt-burned, half-dead, absurd — and then at the oilcloth bundle, and something in his face changed. Recognition, maybe. Or dread. Probably both.

"You came through the storm," the clerk said.

"There wasn't another way," Jonah said.

"No," the clerk agreed quietly. "There never is, with these people. Come inside. The magistrate is awake. She's been awake all night." He hesitated on the threshold, lantern raised, rain streaming off its edge. "You should know before you come in. A man telephoned an hour ago. He claimed to represent certain commercial interests. He asked whether any aircraft were expected tonight."

Teodoro's hand went instinctively to the bundle against his chest.

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him," the clerk said, "that all flights were cancelled due to weather." He smiled then, small and tired and entirely on their side. "I have wanted to lie to that man's employers my entire career. Come in. You're just in time to make it a true confession — because whatever you're carrying, they already know it exists. And they are already on their way."
