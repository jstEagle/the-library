## 30. Auntie Ru's Last Ledger

The rain had followed them all the way from the courthouse, thinning from a wall to a curtain to a fine grey mist that beaded on the shutters of the lodging house and dripped off the eaves like a slow clock. Jonah came up the stairs two at a time, still wringing seawater out of her sleeves, Teodoro behind her with the magistrate's receipt folded into an oilskin pouch against his chest. They had argued the whole way about whether to hide the documents again or trust the filing—argued in the low, exhausted voices of people who have been arguing for days and no longer expect to win—and by the time they reached the landing outside their rooms, Jonah had forgotten, entirely, to check who else might be waiting.

She smelled her first.

Not perfume, exactly. Something older than perfume—camphor and dried jasmine and the faint mineral tang of tea kept too long over coals. A smell she had breathed in across a hundred floating restaurants and dim back offices, a smell that meant arithmetic was about to happen to her.

Auntie Ru sat on the narrow veranda outside their rooms, in the rattan chair, with her hands folded on a lacquered case the size of a hymnal.

She did not look up when they stopped. She looked, if anything, smaller than Jonah remembered—the same immaculate cheongsam, the same jade at her throat, but the woman inside it seemed to have been let out slightly, the way a dress lets out around someone who has stopped eating. Her hair was pinned with the same silver combs. Her face was the same courteous mask. But the eyes above the mask were doing something Jonah had never seen them do.

They were tired.

"Miss Zhou," Auntie Ru said, without rising. "And Mr. Reyes. How good. I hoped to catch you both."

Teodoro's hand went into his jacket—not for a weapon; he didn't carry one—but for the oilskin pouch, dragging it closer to his body like a man pulling a child behind him. Jonah put herself half a step forward, between the veranda and the door, an old reflex, and then heard how absurd it was. There were no men in the stairwell. No one on the roof. The rain on the tiles was the only sound in the world, and the only person in it was an old woman holding a box.

"You're alone," Jonah said.

"I am alone," Auntie Ru agreed, "in most senses of the word." She turned the lacquered case over in her hands once, twice, a small fussy gesture, and then set it on the table beside her chair and slid it across the boards until it rested at the edge, within reach of anyone who cared to take it. "Sit down, please. Both of you. I won't keep you long, and standing in doorways makes for poor conversations."

Jonah didn't sit. Neither did Teodoro. Auntie Ru regarded them with something that was almost amusement, almost affection, and then went on as though they had.

"The board met yesterday. Eleven hours. I am told there was shouting, which is unusual—they are generally such polite men. At the end of it, they voted to dissolve the coastal holdings. All of them. The airfield, the boats, the warehouses, the debt portfolio." She paused, and her voice did something delicate and terrible: it softened. "Including me."

* * *

Jonah had spent five years learning to read Auntie Ru the way a pilot reads weather—by pressure, by the color of the light under the clouds, by what the silence between sentences weighed. She knew every register of that voice. The warm register that preceded a trap. The flat register that preceded violence. The brisk register of bookkeeping, which was the most common and the least dangerous, because Auntie Ru believed, sincerely, in accounts. Every cruelty she had ever done to Jonah had been entered somewhere, balanced against something, carried forward with interest. It was the closest thing to honesty the Syndicate had ever shown her, and she had hated it precisely because it was honest.

This was none of those registers. This was a voice she had never heard before, and it took her a full minute to name it, and when she named it she wished she hadn't.

It was grief.

"Designated loss," Auntie Ru said, tasting the phrase. "That is what they called it. You understand the term? When a firm decides a position can no longer be saved, it does not close the position—it assigns it. Someone must absorb the loss so the ledger balances. Someone always must. For forty years I assigned losses to other people. Villages. Pilots. Clerks. Families whose names I never learned because the paperwork did not require them." She looked down at her hands, at the rings, at the knuckles gone soft with age. "Now the paperwork requires mine."

Teodoro made a sound low in his throat, not quite a word. Jonah understood it. There was a part of both of them—larger in him, but present in her too—that wanted to say *good*, that wanted to watch this woman be devoured by the machine she had fed other people into, and to feel righteous about it. And the rest of them, the larger part, the part that had sat across tea from her three times and felt the floor tilt each time, simply stood there in the rain-smell and could not make the righteousness stick.

"Why are you here?" Jonah asked. It came out harsher than she intended, and then less harsh, because her voice cracked on the last word. "Why now?"

"Because of what you did today." Auntie Ru lifted her chin, and for a moment the mask was fully on, the old courtesy, the old terrifying gentleness. "You flew through the core of a monsoon to put papers in front of a magistrate. Do you know what I thought, when the report came in? I thought: there is a woman who has finally stopped running. Not toward anything. Just—stopped. Do you know how rare that is? I have employed hundreds of runners. Pilots, couriers, accountants. They run their whole lives. It is the running we buy, Miss Zhou. Not the flying. The flying is incidental." She smiled, and the smile was genuine, and that was the worst thing yet. "You stopped. And when you stop, you become legible. And when you become legible—" she spread her hands, a small shrug of enormous meaning, "—the board reads you. They read the cave. They read Mr. Reyes. They read forty years of my management and found it, in the current climate, expensive."

"And so they cut you loose," Teodoro said. His voice was rough with disbelief. "After everything you've done for them. After—"

"After everything I have done *as* them," Auntie Ru corrected, gently, the way one corrects a child's arithmetic. "There is no 'for,' Mr. Reyes. That is the first lesson of the ledger, and I think your family knew it better than mine. There is no serving a thing like the Syndicate. There is only being it, for a while, in your portion. And then the portion is reassigned."

She rose, slowly, one hand on the arm of the chair, and crossed to the rail of the veranda. Below them the street ran silver with runoff; a boy pushed a cart of durians through the puddles, swearing cheerfully at the mud. Ordinary life, going on, indifferent to ledgers. Auntie Ru watched it the way a drowning woman watches a shore.

"I burned your family's records," she said to Teodoro, without turning around. "Not personally. I signed the order. Nineteen years ago. The clerk who carried it out—you may have seen his photograph in some file or other; he is still employed, or was until Tuesday—is a man named Ong Ka Wai. He enjoyed the work. I noted that in his evaluation. Favorably." She said it flatly, without apology, and Jonah realized with a lurch that this was the confession—not the words themselves but the flatness, the refusal to soften it, the accountant's pride in accurate entry even at the end. "I tell you this because you will learn it anyway, in the hearings, and I would rather you hear it from the hand that signed."

Teodoro's jaw worked. Jonah watched him hold himself very still, the way a man holds still when moving might break something. Forty years of hunting, and the answer walked up the stairs and sat down in a rattan chair and told him the file number.

"Why?" he managed.

"Because your family's records described a debt," Auntie Ru said. "The founder's debt. The original one. And a firm built on collateral cannot survive the discovery that its collateral was itself borrowed." She turned back to face them, and now she came to the table, and now she opened the lacquered case.

Inside, wrapped in yellowed silk, lay papers.

Jonah knew them instantly, the way you know a face from a photograph you've studied too long. The iron chest. The documents the scouts had cracked out of it in the dark while she and Teodoro hid among the causeway stones—she had heard them handled, heard the scrape of tin against stone, had filed her partial report and lied about exactly these. The chest contents. The founder's own hand. The loan instrument, the collateral schedule, the entries in the cave's own script that made the Syndicate's entire claim to the coast a lien on money that had never been repaid.

"They took these from the cave," Jonah said. Her voice was very quiet. "Your scouts. In October."

"My scouts took photographs," Auntie Ru said. "They also took the originals, because the board instructed them to destroy the originals, and my scouts are thorough men who prefer to confirm instructions before following them. The destruction order sat in my pending tray for six weeks. I did not sign it." She closed her eyes briefly, a flicker, a woman allowing herself one moment of something unprofessional. "I told myself I was waiting for a quieter week. There is always a quieter week, if you want one. That is how cowardice compounds, Miss Zhou. Like interest."

She pushed the case across the table until it touched the edge nearest Jonah.

"I am giving them to you. To both of you. Not selling. Not trading. Giving. There is nothing left in my account to buy anything with, and I find, at the end, that I would rather the record show a gift."

* * *

Jonah did not touch the case. Not at first. She stood with her arms hanging at her sides and her hands curled loosely, the way they hung when she was afraid of her own reflexes, and she listened to the rain, and she watched Auntie Ru's face do the thing that faces do when they have run out of performance.

"You want something," she said finally. "You always want something. Even the kindness was invoiced."

"Yes," Auntie Ru said. "I want something. One thing. It is small, and it is large, and I have thought about how to ask it for eleven hours in a boardroom and forty years before that, and I still do not know if I am asking correctly, so forgive me if I ask it badly."

She straightened, and her hands went still in her lap, and she looked at Jonah directly, and the mask came all the way off, and underneath it was just an old woman with wet eyes.

"When it ends—all of it, the hearings, the dissolution, whatever becomes of the coast and its titles and its people—I want you to tell the truth about me. All of it. Not the version the lawyers will build, which will be a monster, because monsters are useful in courtrooms. Not the version my subordinates will tell, which will be a fool, because fools are useful to survivors. The truth. That I tripled your debt because I could, and enjoyed the power of it. That I sent pilots into weather I would not fly myself. That I signed the burning of records and villages and called it risk management. And also—that I taught you the stars over the Sunda Strait when you were twenty-two and knew nothing, that I paid for your first engine overhaul when no one else would touch you, that I warned you away from the cave three separate times in language I chose carefully so that you would understand and live." Her breath caught, and she steadied it, and went on. "Both columns, Miss Zhou. Debit and credit. I have spent my life insisting that books balance. Let mine balance."

The rain thickened against the shutters. Somewhere below, the durian boy laughed at something.

Jonah thought about the floating restaurant, the first meeting, the tea poured in a slow spiral while Auntie Ru explained, with perfect warmth, that Jonah's debt had been restructured and wasn't that fortunate, and all the while the numbers were tripling behind the courtesy like sharks under glass. She thought about the radio call after the partial report—*the tide shows everything eventually*—and how she had read it as a threat and it had been, and it had also been, she understood now, a warning sent by a woman watching her own reflection drown. She thought about Karang Laut burning, the fish sheds, Salmah's grandson with the burns along his arm, and how the order for that had passed through a desk, and the desk had been Auntie Ru's, whatever the board had voted, whatever the enforcers had chosen, because everything passed through that desk, everything always had.

Both columns. That was the horror of it. Not that the credits were false—they weren't, that was the thing, they were real, the stars and the overhaul and the warnings were real—but that they had never once, not for a single day, made the debits acceptable. A balanced book is not a clean hand. Jonah had flown unlabeled crates for years telling herself the vaccines outweighed them, and she knew, with the same certainty she knew the weight-and-balance limits of her own aircraft, that no cargo manifest ever balanced a burned village. Auntie Ru had known it too. Had known it and built the largest book on the coast anyway, and now stood in the rain asking for the only thing her profession had never permitted her: to be read whole.

"I can't promise you sympathy," Jonah said. "In the hearings. If I'm asked about you, I'll say what happened. All of it. What you did and what you gave and what it cost. But I won't shape it for you. I won't make it kind."

"No," Auntie Ru said. "Of course not. Kindness would falsify the entry." And she smiled, and it was the true smile, the terrible one, and Jonah understood that this—this exact exchange, this refusal offered and accepted in advance—was the first honest transaction they had ever completed.

Teodoro stepped forward. He had been silent so long that his voice startled the veranda. "Ong Ka Wai," he said. "The man who burned my family's records. Where is he now?"

"Dismissed Tuesday. Along with me." Auntie Ru's mouth thinned. "He was given severance and a warning that the firm would deny him. He has copies of things, Mr. Reyes. Photographs. Certified duplicates of evidence that was filed this morning—he stole one from the courthouse annex within the hour, or paid someone to. He is a man with nothing left to lose and a professional relationship with fire." She looked at Teodoro, then at Jonah, and delivered the last line of her career with the crispness of a closing entry. "If I were young, I would already be on a plane. As it is, I am an old woman with a taxi waiting, and I am telling you: he will come for the cave, or for the magistrate's copy, or for you. Probably tonight. Probably all three."

She picked up her umbrella. She did not take the lacquered case back. She bowed—slightly, formally, to both of them together, an act Jonah had never once seen her perform—and went down the stairs into the grey light, and the rain took her, and the street swallowed her, and Jonah stood at the rail watching until the umbrella turned the corner and was gone.

* * *

For a long time neither of them moved.

Then Teodoro opened the lacquered case with both hands, the way a man opens a reliquary, and laid the silk aside, and the papers caught what little light the afternoon had left. The founder's signature. The collateral schedule. The marginal annotations in the cave's own script, which Teodoro read aloud in a whisper, translating as he went, his voice shaking on the figures: principal, interest, default, forfeiture. Forty years of hunting ending on a rented veranda in a gift from the woman who had ordered the burning.

"He'll go for the annex copy first," Jonah said. Her mind was already moving, the old pilot's arithmetic, options and fuel and weather. "It's the easiest target and it does the most damage—if the certified duplicate disappears, the filing stalls, and stalls are all the Syndicate's remaining lawyers need."

"The magistrate said days," Teodoro said. "Days just became hours."

Jonah looked at the papers, and at the window going dark, and at the telephone on the hall table with its cord frayed by damp, and she felt the strange lightness she had felt once before, over open water in the eye of a storm—the lightness of having nothing left to protect except the thing worth protecting.

"We need to move tonight," she said. "Before he does."

Down in the harbor, the tide was turning, and the last boats were coming in ahead of the squall line, and somewhere in the wet streets a man named Ong Ka Wai was lighting his first match.
