## 32. Sabotage on the Tarmac

The rain had thinned to a fine grey gauze by the time Teodoro Reyes came back for his spectacles.

It was a small errand—the kind of errand a man learns to distrust after forty years of small errands ending badly—but the light was failing and he could not read a star-chart without them, and tomorrow they would need star-charts, because Jonah's plan required flying through the one place on earth where no instrument made by man could be trusted. The hangar at the edge of the airfield was dark except for the amber glow of the field lamp over the fuel bowser, and the plane sat inside it like a sleeping animal, her skin beaded with damp, her cowling still ticking as the engine cooled from the afternoon's test run.

He should have noticed the second set of footprints in the oil-stained concrete dust. He would tell himself later that he had noticed them—half-noticed, the way a man half-hears a sound in a house he believes is empty—and that some older instinct, some clerk's instinct for an entry that does not balance, had already begun to itch at him before he saw the open panel.

But first there was only the walk across the wet tarmac, the smell of avgas and salt rot, and the particular loneliness of a man returning for glasses he'd left on a workbench, in a country where every institution that might have protected him had been sold off or burned down decades ago.

The hangar door stood a hand's width ajar. He had closed it. He was certain he had closed it; closing things was the one discipline his father had beaten into him that had survived everything else—the archive stamp, the ledgers, the family name ground into mud and ash, but *close the drawer, lock the cabinet, leave no entry unbalanced*. Teodoro stood in the rain a moment longer than was sensible, listening.

Inside, someone was working on the plane with a screwdriver.

Not repairing. He knew within three seconds what he was hearing, because he had spent thirty years among machines of paper and knew the sound of a thing being taken apart versus a thing being put together, and this was the former: patient, unhurried, the small precise grunts of a man who did not consider himself a thief because in his own ledger he was only settling accounts.

Teodoro set down the lantern he'd brought—he would want his hands free—and stepped through the gap in the door.

* * *

The enforcer was crouched inside the radio bay behind the cockpit seats, his back to the door, a coil of severed wire already draped over his shoulder like a dead snake. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with the flat, unhurried competence of someone who had done violence so long it had become maintenance. Teodoro recognized him the way you recognize weather: not this particular storm, but the type. The Syndicate kept men like this the way old families kept dogs—not for affection, but because certain jobs could not be asked of friends.

Beside him on the seat lay a document wallet, oiled canvas, government-stamped. Teodoro's stomach dropped through the concrete floor of the hangar and kept going.

The certified copy. The magistrate's clerk had sealed it that morning—fourteen pages, photographed from the cave ledgers, countersigned, stamped with the court's embossed seal, the only duplicate of the filing that existed outside the courthouse itself. Jonah had argued for leaving it locked in the magistrate's chambers. The clerk had argued that if the Syndicate's lawyers moved against the original—as Auntie Ru's people certainly would—a certified copy in independent hands was their only proof the filing existed at all. And so it had been entrusted to them, to Jonah, to the plane, and now it lay on a greasy seat cushion while a hired man cut the radio out of the aircraft that was supposed to carry it through the eye of a monsoon.

Teodoro did the arithmetic he always did, the archivist's arithmetic: what is lost, what survives, what can be reconstructed. The radio was nothing. A radio was an hour's work and a favor owed. But the copy—if the copy reached Ru's lawyers before dawn, they could move on the courthouse, contest the filing, claim forgery, and by the time anyone verified the cave itself the monsoon would have sealed it for a season, and a season was all the Syndicate needed to make witnesses forget which side of the water they lived on.

The enforcer reached deeper into the bay, and something small and metallic clicked—the last connection parting.

Teodoro should have backed out. He knew that too. He was fifty-eight years old, soft-handed, nearsighted without the spectacles sitting ten feet away on the workbench, and the man in the plane carried a pistol the way other men carried a pen. Every sensible ledger said withdraw, find Jonah, find the field police, find anyone whose trade was force.

Instead he heard himself say, quietly, in the accent the archives had given him, "You've cut the wrong wire."

The enforcer turned. There was a moment—the length of a held breath—in which two men measured each other across the aisle of a cold airplane, and Teodoro understood that the man was deciding whether he was a threat or merely a complication. Complications could be managed. Threats required the pistol.

"The old man from the cave," the enforcer said. His voice was mild, almost courteous, the courtesy of the Syndicate distilled to its essence. "You should have stayed in your archive."

"It wasn't my archive," Teodoro heard himself say. "That was rather the whole problem."

* * *

Later—much later, when his hands had stopped shaking and Jonah had stopped asking him the same question in four different tones of voice—he would try to reconstruct how the next ninety seconds had actually gone, and fail, and finally settle on the truth, which was that he had not planned any of it.

The enforcer came out of the cockpit fast, low, one hand sweeping the seat for the wallet, the other dropping toward his hip, and Teodoro did the single most reckless thing available to him: he threw himself forward onto the document wallet, both arms wrapping it against his chest, and rolled off the seat onto the concrete with the man's weight coming down after him.

The world became impact and noise. His shoulder struck the floor and something in it cracked or tore—he would not know which until morning, and would never have it properly looked at, because there was no time and no money and a long list of worse things. The wallet dug into his ribs. Above him the enforcer swore, grabbed a fistful of his collar, hauled his head up and slammed it down—not hard enough to break him, hard enough to explain the mathematics of the situation—and Teodoro tasted blood and concrete dust and thought, with a clarity that felt almost holy:

*This is the moment.*

Not the moment of danger. The other one. The one he had been walking toward for forty years without admitting it existed.

Because here was what he had never told Jonah, not fully, not in the leaking boat with the lantern guttering: that in the famine year, when his father had sold the partial copy of the records to the Syndicate's founder, it had been Teodoro who carried the documents. Fourteen years old, sent across town with a satchel, told it was a matter of the family's survival and believing it, because children believe. And when the Syndicate's men had come for the rest of the archive two years later—when they had burned forty years of his family's custody in the courtyard while his mother wept into her apron—it had been Teodoro who showed them where the strongroom was. Not under torture. Under a promise. They had promised to spare the house, and they had spared the house, and his father had never once blamed him, which was the cruelest mercy of all, because Teodoro had spent the rest of his life waiting for someone to blame him correctly.

Every choice since had been a variation on that fourteen-year-old boy with the satchel: carry the papers, keep your head down, survive, survive, survive. Even the cave—even the beautiful cathedral of stone ledgers—even Jonah. Some part of him had known that if the moment ever came when he held the truth in his arms and a Syndicate man reached for it, he would let go. That was his inheritance. Not the stamp, not the custody, not the scholarship. The letting go.

The enforcer wrenched at the wallet. Teodoro's grip held—not from strength, he had none to speak of, but from the sudden furious discovery that his hands had decided something his mind had spent four decades refusing to rule on.

"No," Teodoro said. Blood in his teeth. Absurdly formal. "This entry stays balanced."

* * *

What happened next was not brave. He wanted that recorded somewhere, in whatever internal ledger a man keeps when no stone wall will take his writing: it was not brave, it was ugly, graceless, undignified work, two middle-aged men grappling on a filthy hangar floor over a canvas folder while rain ticked on the tin roof.

The enforcer hit him twice more, efficient blows meant to end the conversation. Teodoro took them and did not let go, and discovered in himself a stubbornness he had mistaken all these years for cowardice—the same quality, turned inside out. The clerk's virtue. You do not release the record. You do not release the record even when the building burns, even when your hands blister, even when the men with torches promise you your life. His father had died holding a catalog. His grandmother had drowned crossing a strait with a satchel strapped to her chest. It was in the blood after all, the thing he'd thought had skipped him.

His flailing hand found the fire extinguisher mounted by the cockpit door. He did not swing it—that was for heroes in serials. He simply got his thumb on the pin, pulled, and discharged the entire charge point-blank into the man's face.

The chemical cloud filled the cockpit like a screaming white ghost. The enforcer reeled back, blinded, coughing, and Teodoro—coughing himself, eyes streaming, half-blind without his spectacles anyway—did the last thing, the true thing: he did not pick up the fallen pistol lying gleaming on the floor plates between them.

He looked at it. He looked at it for a long second, longer than safety allowed, and he understood exactly what it offered. The man had burned his family's life's work. The man had been in the cave; the man's colleagues had defaced the carvings; the man was, right now, at this moment, the living continuation of every torch that had ever been put to a Reyes record. No court would ask many questions. No one would even find the body until the tide came in. One blow, and forty years of grief would have its receipt.

*And then you are the archive that burns,* said a voice in him that sounded like his mother. *Then the Syndicate wins the only contest it ever cared about—which is proving that everyone, given the chance, keeps their books the way they do.*

Teodoro set the extinguisher down. He picked up the wallet instead, hugged it to his chest, and knelt on the enforcer's chest with his knee—not to hurt him, though it did, but to hold him, the way you hold a lid on a box, the way you press a flower, the way his father had pressed blotting paper to wet ink—and he bound the man's wrists to the landing-gear strut with the severed radio wire, which was, Teodoro would think afterward, the only fitting use for it.

The enforcer, blinking chemicals out of his eyes, stared up at him with something that was not fear and not respect but recognition—the look of a man seeing a category violated. "You're supposed to run," he rasped. "Old men like you run."

"I know," Teodoro said. His voice shook. His whole body shook. "I've been running for forty years. I'm retiring."

* * *

Jonah found them twenty minutes later, drawn by the field watchman's whistle and the smell of the extinguisher chemical hanging in the hangar like a dead animal's ghost. She came through the door with a wrench in one hand and her lantern high, and took in the scene in a single sweep—the trussed man, the gutted radio bay, the blood on Teodoro's face, the wallet clutched against his chest like an infant.

"Tell me you didn't—" she began, and stopped, because she was looking at the pistol on the floor, and at the distance between it and Teodoro, and at the extinguisher set down neatly beside it, cap replaced, and women who live by reading wreckage read this wreckage perfectly.

"He has a broken nose and a ruined evening," Teodoro said. "That's the full inventory. I counted."

Jonah crossed the hangar floor and did something she had never done in all the weeks he had known her: she touched him first. Just her hand on his shoulder, brief, checking the damage, but her fingers were not steady and she did not pretend they were. "Your radio's dead," she said, when she trusted her voice. "The certified copy—"

"Is here." He passed it to her and watched her count the pages by lantern light, watched her shoulders come down one notch when the embossed seal caught the flame's glow. "He was going to take it to Ru's lawyers tonight. I imagine there's a boat waiting." He nodded at the trussed man. "There may still be. Which means someone is waiting on a beach wondering why their courier hasn't come."

Jonah went very still, and he watched her arrive at it a heartbeat after he had: the Syndicate now knew the copy existed, knew where it had been, knew the flight was real. Whatever element of surprise they had purchased with the magistrate's filing was spent.

"They'll be watching the airfield," she said. "They'll be watching the whole coast."

"Yes," Teodoro agreed. He lowered himself onto the workbench, found his spectacles sitting exactly where he'd left them, absurdly untouched amid the wreckage, and put them on. The world sharpened. The enforcer glared at him blearily from the strut. The plane waited in the lamplight with her throat cut, silent, grounded, useless until someone spliced wire in the dark.

"So we don't fly from here," Jonah said slowly, thinking aloud, pacing the hangar's width in six strides and back. "We move the plane tonight, before whoever's expecting that boat gets impatient. Salmah's people can hide us till the tide window. And the radio—" she glanced at the severed bundle of wires with something close to affection, "—the radio doesn't matter. We weren't going to talk to anyone anyway. Anyone worth talking to would shoot us down."

"There is one more thing," Teodoro said.

She looked at him.

He reached into his shirt, into the inner pocket where he had kept it since the stilt house, since the night he had pressed it into her palm in a paper bag and sworn he would never ask for it back—and found it gone, of course, because it was hers now, and that had been the point. So instead he simply said it: "I had the pistol in my hand. For about four seconds. Long enough."

Jonah studied him for a long moment, rain-wet, bloodied, lit amber, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod once, slowly, the way you acknowledge a figure that finally balances.

"I know," she said. "I saw where you put it down."

Outside, far down the coast road, an engine started—distant, wrong, idling where no honest traffic idled at this hour—and they both turned toward the sound, and the enforcer laughed softly through his broken nose, and said, "That'll be my ride getting tired of waiting," and Jonah was already moving for the hangar door, and Teodoro, archivist, keeper, heir-at-last to nothing but his own choices, followed her out into the rain to steal his family's future back before dawn.
