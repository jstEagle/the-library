## 33. The Last Low Tide

The tide began to leave before dawn, and the sea went with it like a debt being called in.

Jonah stood on the shingle at Karang Laut and watched the water peel back from the reef in long silver sheets, exposing the black spine of rock that ran out toward the cave mouth. The air smelled of iodine and wet stone and the green rot of the mangroves behind them. Overhead the monsoon was still working itself out of the sky—ragged cloud torn into streamers, patches of hard blue showing through like skin under a bandage—but the wind had dropped to a mutter, and for the first time in eleven days the sea was almost quiet.

Almost. She could hear it breathing in the reef's throat, a low suck and gurgle as the channels drained. The charts said they had four hours of slack. Teodoro, crouched at the waterline with his trousers rolled and a brass tide-stick in his hand, said three.

"Charts are written by people who've never been here," he said, without looking up. "The Reyes family has been reading this reef since before there were charts."

"You've never read this reef either," Jonah said. "You've read about it."

"That's how my family did everything." He stood, joints cracking, and wiped sand off the tide-stick with his thumb. "Secondhand. Faithfully. It kept us alive and useless for four generations." He glanced at her, and in the grey light she saw again how much older the last month had made him—the hollows under his eyes, the way he held his left arm slightly away from his body where the enforcer had wrenched it in the hangar. "Today we do something firsthand. Try to contain your optimism."

Behind them, the verification party was assembling itself on the beach with the nervous ceremony of people who had read about danger too. Pak Darma, the magistrate's clerk, a small precise man in a raincoat too good for the occasion, clutching a leather document case to his chest as if the sea might demand it as a fee. Two witnesses from the village—a fisherman named Osman who had helped pull bodies from the burned sheds, and his sister-in-law Wati, who had signed her name with a thumbprint because no one had ever taught her hand otherwise. Ibu Salmah stood apart from all of them, upright on the shingle, watching the reef with an expression Jonah couldn't read and had stopped trying to.

And at the edge of the group, wrists cuffed in front of him, flanked by two constables borrowed from the district post: the enforcer. His name, it turned out, was Aris. He looked smaller out of the Syndicate's shadow, a broad soft man gone deflated, salt-crusted and still coughing seawater from the lower chamber where Jonah had found him drowning. He hadn't spoken more than ten words since. But when the constables had tried to keep him ashore, he'd asked—in a voice like gravel settling—to come. *I can show you what they cut,* he'd said. *I was there.*

Jonah checked her watch against the falling water and made herself stop checking it.

"All right," she said. "Three hours. We go in fast, we photograph what matters first, and nobody touches anything without Pak Darma saying so. If the water starts coming back early, we drop everything and walk. Stone keeps. People don't."

"Amen," said Teodoro.

They crossed the exposed reef in a loose line, boots slipping on weed-slick coral, the party's lanterns swinging even though the light was coming up. The cave mouth grew ahead of them out of the reef's shoulder—that black arch she'd first seen from the air months ago, guarded by nothing but legend, now framed against a sky going the color of old pewter. Offerings still sat in the niches at its threshold: rice wrapped in banana leaf, a child's bracelet, a rusted tin of sardines someone had left with great seriousness. Wati stopped to touch each one as she passed, murmuring, and Jonah found herself waiting for her, matching her pace, though every instinct she owned screamed about the clock.

Inside, the dark took them.

* * *

Teodoro felt the cave take him the way it always did—as a change in pressure, a settling, as if the stone were inhaling and holding him against the inside of its ribs. He'd been down twice before, once in wonder and once in terror, and both times the same thought had arrived unbidden: *this is a library.* Not a temple, whatever the pilgrims believed; not a treasury, whatever the Syndicate had hoped. A library that wrote itself, century after century, in the only ink that doesn't fade.

He lit the big carbide lamp and turned it on the causeway wall.

The sound Wati made behind him was small and terrible.

The carvings in the outer chambers had been smashed. Not chiseled carefully, not erased by scholars—which would have been its own crime but at least a literate one—smashed, with hammers or the backs of axes, in panic. Faces of ledger-keepers gouged blind. Columns of figures hacked into rubble. Someone had taken a blade to the treaty panels and sawed them out entirely, leaving raw scars of lighter stone where the records had lived for six hundred years. Chippings lay ankle-deep along the walls, and among them, glinting, the broken points of steel chisels the scouts had abandoned when the tide schedule stopped making sense.

"They came back," Pak Darma said quietly. He knelt, lifted a fragment the size of a man's palm—a corner of a panel, still bearing three carved numerals—and held it in both hands like a wounded bird. "After the courthouse filing. They came back to destroy evidence."

"They came back to destroy *witnesses*," Teodoro said. His voice came out flat, which was how he knew he was furious. "Stone is slow, gentlemen. You have to kill it in a hurry or it outlives you. They were in a hurry."

Aris spoke from the rear of the column, cuffs clinking. "We used hammers from the survey kit." Everyone turned. He was looking at the floor, at the chippings, at his own boots. "I did the west wall. There was a list. Names of families. Tolls owed by name, going back—" He stopped. Swallowed. "My grandmother's village is on that wall. I didn't know until I read it. Then I hit it anyway, because Ru's orders were Ru's orders, and that's the whole of what I am." He raised his cuffed hands slightly, an odd shrug. "So. Now you know what you brought down here."

No one answered him. Wati spat, deliberately, at the rubble of the west wall—not at him, Teodoro noted, but at the work itself—and walked on.

Because here was the thing, the thing Teodoro had bet forty years of his life on and could finally see with his own lamp: the destruction stopped. Twenty meters in, past the second bend where the passage narrowed and the air went cool and mineral, the hammer-marks ended as abruptly as a sentence cut off mid-word. Beyond that point the walls rose smooth and crowded and *whole*, tier upon tier of carved registers running off into the dark, the deep ledgers, the ones cut earliest and deepest and hardest to reach, and the scouts had run out of time or nerve or light before they reached them.

Or—Teodoro played his lamp along the lowest register, where the oldest script crowded dense as barnacles—or they'd understood, the way his family had understood, that some entries you don't touch. Some debts you leave recorded, because the recording is the only thing holding them shut.

"The deep ledgers survive," he said aloud, and heard his own voice shake, and let it. "Pak Darma. Bring your case. Bring everything. It's all here."

* * *

For the next hour the cave was full of industry, strange and reverent: the clerk photographing panels by lamplight with a folding camera and a notebook of exposure calculations, calling out register numbers in a voice pitched for a chapel; Osman holding reflector boards steady while sweat ran into his eyes; Wati reading aloud, slowly, haltingly, the passages Teodoro translated for her, so that the record would exist in a living mouth and not only on film. Jonah worked the far gallery with the second lamp, marking sections with chalk, and Teodoro kept finding reasons to cross over to her side of the chamber just to be near the place where she was.

It was she who found the founder's entry.

She called him over quietly—not shouting, the way you don't shout in libraries—and led him to a recessed panel near the floor of the third gallery, half-hidden behind a flowstone curtain, which was presumably why the hammers had missed it. The carving there was older than the rest, cruder, cut by a different hand or several, and it recorded in the toll-ledger style a single transaction: a loan. Grain and iron, advanced in a famine year by the keepers of the cave to a merchant house whose name Teodoro had known since childhood, copied out in his father's careful clerks' hand from the confiscated originals he'd spent decades hunting.

Below the loan, in the same register, the collateral clause. And below that, in characters worn nearly smooth by centuries of flood-water, the default.

"It's real," Jonah said. Her lamp-hand was steady but her voice wasn't. "It's not the copy. It's not the chest documents. It's the original entry, cut before any of them were born. The whole Syndicate—it was collateral. On a loan they never repaid."

"And the keepers' descendants foreclosed," Teodoro said. "In law, if not in fact. My family recorded the foreclosure and the Syndicate burned my family for it." He crouched, laid his palm flat against the stone below the entry, the way you'd check a sleeping animal. Warm. Dry. Alive. "Forty years I thought I was looking for proof of what they destroyed. It turns out the destruction was the proof. You don't burn a family's archive over nothing."

Pak Darma photographed the panel nine times. Then he sat back on his heels, closed his notebook, and said, in the voice of a man setting down a weight he'd carried up a mountain: "This will hold. Whatever their lawyers bring, this will hold. Stone doesn't recant."

From somewhere above them, muffled through fathoms of rock, came a sound like the sea clearing its throat.

Everyone stopped. Lanterns swung. The tide-stick in Teodoro's pocket seemed suddenly very heavy.

"How long?" Wati asked.

Teodoro got out the stick, though he already knew, and put it away, because he already knew that too. "Less than we need," he said. "But enough. Enough to finish the third gallery. Move."

* * *

They finished the third gallery with the water already whispering in the entrance channel, a rising hiss that Jonah felt in her back teeth. She worked the last panels with chalk and camera and the particular calm she'd learned in cockpits, where panic is just arithmetic done too loudly, and she was chalking the final section mark when Wati, packing the lamps, said:

"The offerings."

Jonah turned. At the threshold of the innermost chamber, where the villagers had left their rice and bracelets for generations, the niches stood empty. Not robbed—emptied. Swept clean, the dust disturbed in long drag-marks, as if someone had carried everything out in haste. And set in the center of the swept floor, placed deliberately, square to the walls, was a single object: a survey stake, Syndicate-stamped, driven upright into the cave floor like a flag.

Like a claim.

"No," Teodoro said softly, beside her. He'd come up silent, and his face in the lamplight had gone the color of the flowstone. "That's not a claim. That's a message. That's *her* message." He looked at Jonah. "Ru knows exactly what we found. She knows what it means. And she's telling you she was here first, and she'll be here after, and the tide goes out and comes back and goes out and comes back, and she can wait longer than stone."

The water-hiss in the channel deepened. Somewhere overhead, faint as memory, an engine.

Jonah looked at the stake a moment longer. Then she pulled it out of the floor, snapped it across her knee, and laid the two halves on the swept stone where the offerings belonged.

"We'll see," she said, to Ru, to the cave, to whoever was listening in the dark. "Wati—rice. We're leaving an offering. Pak Darma—last exposures, then everyone walks, now, while the channel's still knee-deep."

As they moved toward the growing sound of the sea, Teodoro fell in beside her, and under the noise of the water he said, very quietly, only for her: "You know she won't wait for the courts."

"I know," Jonah said.

Above the reef, through the cave mouth, the sky had gone the color of hammered tin, and the engine sound was getting louder.
