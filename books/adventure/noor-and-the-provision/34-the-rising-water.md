## 34. The Rising Water

The sea did not announce itself. That was the first thing wrong with it.

Jonah had grown up on coasts that shouted their intentions—a day of green water, a smell of iodine and iron, old men spitting and saying *tonight*. The Sunda coast was subtler, but it still spoke. What it did not do was change its mind mid-sentence.

She was forty meters from the causeway mouth when she heard it: not the surf above, which had been a constant drumming all morning, but a sound underneath it, low and sliding, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. She stopped. Teodoro stopped beside her, his satchel of photographs clutched to his chest like a child. Behind them, Pak Darma's flashbulb popped once more in the deep chamber, a white flower opening and closing in the dark.

"Listen," she said.

"To what?"

"That's the problem. I can't."

The channel they had waded in through an hour ago had been knee-deep, clear as gin over pale sand, the tide tables pinned to the magistrate's clerk's clipboard promising them ninety more minutes of ebb. The clerk—a nervous young man named Wati who kept wiping his spectacles—had checked the table three times against his watch and announced it like scripture. But the tables were colonial tables, drawn from harbors sixty miles north, and the reef at Karang Laut kept its own arithmetic. The sound beneath the surf was the sound of water finding a lower place and filling it, fast, the way debt fills a life.

"Go," Jonah said. "Everyone out. Now. Don't gather anything."

They moved. Wati went first, splashing, his clipboard held overhead like a lantern he didn't have. The two witnesses from the village followed, then Pak Darma, cradling his camera bag, muttering about exposures lost. Teodoro hesitated at the junction where the passage forked—the high route back toward the entrance gallery, and the descending stair toward the lower chambers, the ones the Syndicate's scouts had defaced in their panic, the ones nobody had photographed yet.

"The lower gallery," Teodoro said. "We haven't—"

"It'll be here next season."

"It might not be." His voice cracked on it. Forty years of wanting, compressed into four words. "The scouts took hammers to the upper walls, Jonah. If the water gets into the lower stone with that kind of pressure—"

The sound came again, louder now, and this time there was no mistaking it. It was not the sea turning over. It was the sea arriving.

* * *

They made the causeway in water to their thighs. By the time they reached the entrance gallery, where grey daylight fell through the cave mouth in a slanted column, it was to their waists, and cold, and moving—not the idle slop of a filling pool but a current, directional, purposeful, pouring through some throat in the reef below them faster than the mouth above could drain it. Wati stood chest-deep at the threshold, holding his clipboard aloft, shouting numbers at no one.

"The table said—" he began.

"The table's wrong," Jonah said. "Swim when you have to. Go."

One by one they went, out through the mouth, into the channel, into the light and the hammering rain, and Jonah counted them out the way she counted cargo: one, two, three, four, five. Then she turned around, because the count was wrong.

Six had come in. Six should go out.

The enforcer.

His name, if it was his name, was Bram. He had arrived with the verification party wearing the blank competence of a man hired for his hands, and he had spent the entire descent three steps behind everyone else, watching, memorizing, doing whatever it was the Syndicate's remaining loyalists paid him to do. In the deep chamber, while Pak Darma photographed the surviving ledgers, Jonah had caught him running his thumb along a carved line of toll-records with something that was not vandalism and not reverence either—something closer to recognition, a man reading his own name in a book he'd been told didn't exist. And then, when the tide had begun its early turn, when the party had scrambled for the exit, Bram had gone the other way. Down. Toward the lower stair.

She had assumed he was retrieving something. A dropped canister, a final photograph, a last act of sabotage. She had assumed, and she had let him go, because the arithmetic had been simple: five lives climbing toward daylight outweighed one man choosing the dark.

Now the dark was filling up.

"Bram!" Her voice came back off the wet stone, flattened by the roar of water. Nothing. "BRAM!"

Teodoro's hand closed on her arm. He had come back for her—he was soaked to the collar, glasses fogged, hair plastered flat, and behind his eyes was the same look she'd seen in the cave the first time, wonder curdling into fear. "He's a Syndicate man," Teodoro said. "He stole the certified copy off your own tarmac. He would drown you and call it weather."

"I know what he is."

"Then why are we standing here?"

Because stopping to calculate would cost more. She couldn't have said where the thought came from—it wasn't a thought so much as a reflex, older than her rules, older than her debts, buried under five years of never landing twice and never carrying what you can't deny. But it was there, sudden and absolute, the way the tide was there: not negotiable.

"Because in six months," she said, already turning toward the descending stair, "when the hearings start and they put me on a stand and ask me everything I've ever done, I'm going to have to say all of it. Every crate. Every lie. Every village." She looked at him, water streaming down her face, rain from above and spray from below meeting somewhere around her chin. "I am not adding *left a man to drown in the dark because it was convenient* to that list. Not even him. Especially not him."

Teodoro held her gaze for one second, two. Then he unslung his satchel, thrust it at her—the photographs, the only copies, the whole reason any of them had come—and said, "Take these up. I'm a better swimmer than you and worse at forgiving myself. Go."

"No."

"Jonah—"

"*No.* You take the pictures up. That's not a negotiation either." She was already moving, wading toward the stair, the water dragging at her knees like hands. "And tell Wati to keep the boat in the channel as long as it floats. We're coming out through the mouth if we have to."

* * *

The lower gallery took her breath twice: once at the top of the stair, where the air went cold and mineral and thin; and once at the bottom, where her lantern found the water.

It had been a chamber when the scouts worked it—she'd seen it on the first descent, a long low hall where the ceiling dipped close and the carved ledgers ran in bands along the walls like the rings of a tree, centuries of tolls and treaties and debts cut into living rock. Now it was becoming a lake. The water came in through fissures in the floor itself, welling up in silver ropes, and it had already claimed the lowest band of carvings; she could see them through the surface, blurred, the way you see coins at the bottom of a fountain. The ceiling was close enough that the space between stone and water was shrinking from both directions, a book closing.

The lantern showed her nothing else. No movement. No sound but the water.

"Bram!"

Her voice bounced and died. She waded deeper, hip-deep now, feeling with each step for the drop-off the floor must have somewhere—the chamber's original throat, the fissure system that fed the sea. The cold climbed her ribs. The lantern light trembled, whether from her hands or the draft she couldn't tell.

There. A sound that wasn't water: a scrape, a knock of skin on stone, off to her left where the wall bowed inward. She swung the light and caught a gleam—an eye, a forehead, a hand hooked over a ledge of carved ledger-stone barely above the surface.

He had gone down the far side of the chamber, she realized. Looking for the deepest carvings, the oldest records, the founder's own entries—the things the Syndicate most needed destroyed before the magistrate's people read them. And the floor had opened under him. The current in here was pulling toward the fissures now, patient and total, and he was too spent to fight it, his head tipped back against the stone, his mouth working.

She got to him by swimming the last ten meters, lantern held high in one hand, and the cold hit her like a door slamming. The water wanted her feet. It found the gap between her boots and her legs and pulled, gently, insistently, the way Auntie Ru used to pour tea—never forcing, only making the alternative seem unreasonable.

"Give me your hand." He didn't move. His lips were grey. "Bram. Look at me. Your employers left. Nobody is coming. Give me your hand or die in their ledger."

Something moved behind his eyes at that—anger, maybe, or the last ember of professional pride—and his hand came off the ledge and closed around hers, and it was freezing, and heavier than a hand had any right to be.

Getting him off the ledge was the easy part. Getting him across the chamber was not. The current strengthened with every stroke, and the ceiling came down to meet them, and once—once—his weight dragged her under, and the world became green-black roar and the taste of stone, and her shoulder struck something hard enough to whiten her vision, and the thought that rose through it, absurdly, was her mother's voice saying *the sea gives back what it borrows, if you ask politely*. She kicked. She asked impolitely. They surfaced gasping under a ceiling she could have touched with her free hand.

The stair was a black slot ahead. Between them and it, the water had found its rhythm—a channeled flow now, fast and smooth as poured glass, running straight into the fissure field. Crossing it meant letting it take them partway and fighting out at the right moment, the river trick every pilot on the coast knew in its water form: commit, then break, never the other order.

"Now," she told him, and pushed off.

The sea took them. For three seconds she was cargo in someone else's hold, spinning, deaf, her grip the only fact in the universe—and then her boot struck the lip of the stair-cut and she broke, hauling, and the current released them with the offended indifference of a creditor discovering the account was empty after all. They collapsed onto the rising steps, both of them coughing, the lantern miraculously still lit, throwing their shadows huge and shaking up the shaft toward the distant grey coin of the entrance.

Bram tried to stand. Failed. Tried again, got one knee under himself, and stopped, hanging his head, water pouring off him.

"Why," he said. It wasn't gratitude. It was genuinely a question, the question of a man whose entire education had been in the other arithmetic.

Jonah leaned against the wet wall and laughed, once, without much humor in it. "You wouldn't believe me."

* * *

The climb took everything either of them had left. Twice the water chased them up the stairs faster than they could climb, surging into the shaft with a sound like a train entering a tunnel, and twice they flattened themselves into niches in the wall and let it pass around their waists, roaring, and recede. The lantern died on the third landing. They climbed the rest by feel and by the growing grey light above, Bram's hand locked around her forearm—not gripping anymore, just holding on, the way a drowning man holds a spar without believing in it.

They came out of the mouth into a world of noise and silver.

The monsoon had arrived in earnest while they were below. Rain fell in ropes, in curtains, in whole falling lakes, and the channel beyond the reef was a chaos of white water, and the boat—Wati's boat, the magistrate's borrowed launch—was exactly where she'd told him to keep it, riding at the edge of the channel with its engine screaming against the anchor line and Wati himself half out of the cockpit, spectacles long gone, peering into the cave mouth like a man watching a magic trick he was sure would end badly.

It nearly did end badly. The last thirty meters of channel were waist-deep and running, and Bram went down twice, and the second time Jonah went down with him, and it was Teodoro who reached them—Teodoro, who should have been safe in the boat, who had clearly disobeyed her the way she'd known he would—hauling them both by the collar into shallows where a human being could stand. For a moment the three of them knelt together in the boiling shallows, breathing like landed fish, and the rain hammered down, and none of it was dignified at all.

Then hands were on them—Wati, the witnesses, villagers who had come down to the shore despite the storm because word traveled faster than weather on this coast—and they were in the boat, and the boat was fighting its way out of the channel with the sea stacking up gray-green behind them, and Karang Laut's fish sheds, charred skeletons since the burning, slid past on the left like the ribs of something the coast had eaten.

Jonah sat in the bilge with water to her ankles and discovered she was shaking. Not from cold—or not only from cold. Her shoulder was a hot wire where it had struck the stone; her hands were scored with cuts she didn't remember getting; and somewhere below, sealed now behind fifty fathoms of rising tide, the lower gallery was filling with the sea, and the lowest bands of carving—the oldest debts, the founder's own entries, the root of everything—were underwater, and would stay underwater until the monsoon ended and the great tides came back.

Cost, she thought. There it was. Real cost, itemized: the photographs of the lower gallery never taken. The season's access gone. Her shoulder possibly cracked. And a man sitting six feet away from her, wrapped in a tarp, staring at his own hands as though they belonged to the Syndicate and not to him.

It should have felt like a loss. She turned it over in her mind, looking for the grief, and found instead a strange, terrifying lightness—the feeling she'd had once, years ago, jettisoning cargo in a storm to clear a ridge, watching the crates fall away into cloud and realizing the plane flew better without them.

"You're bleeding," Teodoro said. He had wedged himself beside her, one hand steadying her shoulder with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about the moment. His face was wrecked—salt, exhaustion, forty years—but his eyes were bright and fixed on hers. "The lower gallery. Tell me."

"Filling. Fast. The floor fissures went first—the oldest bands are under already." She watched the words land on him, watched the hope and the despair fight for possession of his face. "But Bram was down there reading them before the water came. Whatever he saw, he saw it. And he knows we know he saw it."

Both of them turned, slowly, deliberately, and looked at the enforcer.

Bram met their eyes over the rim of the tarp. The rain drummed on it. Somewhere behind them, thunder rolled along the coast like a clearing throat, and the boat pitched into a trough and climbed out again, and for a long moment nobody spoke, because the next words out of that man's mouth were going to decide what the courts heard, what the Syndicate feared, and what the stone had died to keep.

Bram opened his mouth.

"The founder's entry," he said, and his voice was raw salt and seawater, "is dated forty years before the charter. And it isn't a grant." He swallowed. "It's a confession."

Lightning walked the horizon, and Jonah, holding her ruined shoulder, thought: *the tide shows everything eventually.*

Auntie Ru had been right about that. She just hadn't understood which direction the water would flow.
