## 37. Testimony

The courthouse had been built by men who believed that stone could outlast shame. Its columns were salt-stained, its steps worn into shallow bowls by a century of feet, and its great doors stood open to the monsoon's last wet breath as though the building itself had nothing left to hide. Jonah stood at the bottom of those steps with the recovered documents sealed in oilcloth against her chest and felt every one of her five years of small lies press down on her like ballast.

Teodoro touched her elbow. "You can still turn around."

"No," she said. "That's the whole point."

Inside, the hearing room smelled of mildew and lamp oil and old varnish, the particular smell of institutions that had survived more storms than they deserved. The magistrate—Salmah's niece, a narrow woman named Faridah with ink-stained fingers and eyes that catalogued everything they landed on—sat beneath a ceiling fan that turned with the weary patience of a tide gauge. Along one wall sat the Syndicate's lawyers: three of them, gray-suited, gray-faced, arranged like a filing cabinet given flesh. Their lead counsel was a woman with a voice like a stapler, and she watched Jonah come down the aisle the way a fisherman watches a bird too far from shore.

The clerk called the proceedings to order. The fan turned. Somewhere outside, rain began again on the courtyard tiles, soft as static.

"State your name for the record," Magistrate Faridah said.

"Jonah Zhou," she said. "Pilot. Freight and charter." A pause she hadn't planned. "Smuggler, if we're being thorough."

A murmur went through the gallery. Teodoro, seated behind her with his hands folded like a man at his own funeral, did not move. The Syndicate lawyers exchanged a glance that was almost pleased—a confession offered freely was a gift, in their arithmetic. They thought she was handing them rope.

Faridah leaned forward. "You understand that anything you say here becomes part of the public record."

"I'm counting on it."

* * *

She started with the tail number.

It was, she realized somewhere over the first hour, the only honest way in—the same way Teodoro had found her, through a registry and a manifest and a stamp. She told them about the plane's original registration, the false numbers she'd flown under for years, the hangars rented under names that weren't hers. She told them about Auntie Ru—not as a monster but as an accountant, which was worse—and about the floating restaurant where tea had been poured while her debt tripled. She told them about the unlabeled crates.

"Describe the cargo," Faridah said.

"Sealed. Unmarked. Weighted so I couldn't guess. I learned early not to shake them." Jonah's hands were steady on the witness rail; she made herself look at the gallery while she spoke, at the faces of coastal people who'd come up on the morning boats to watch. "I told myself it was contraband. Cigarettes, spirits, something with a duty stamp missing. Contraband I could live with. It was easier than asking."

"And did you ever ask?"

"Once. Early. The answer was a delivery schedule and a reminder about my balance." She heard her own voice go flat, the flatness she used on radios in bad weather. "After that I stopped asking. That's what they buy when they buy a debt. Not your labor. Your silence about the labor."

The lead counsel rose, smooth as water finding a level. "Madam Zhou, you're testifying that you knowingly transported goods whose contents you chose not to ascertain. Isn't it true that this makes you not a witness to the Syndicate's crimes but a participant in them?"

"Yes," Jonah said.

The word landed in the room like a dropped wrench. The lawyer had clearly prepared for denial, evasion, a long fencing match. She stood there a moment with her next question already obsolete.

"You don't wish to qualify that?"

"No. I flew for them for five years. Whatever came out of those crates went where I put it. If someone died because of something I carried, then I'm not here as a witness with clean hands. I'm here as evidence." She turned slightly, enough to include the gallery. "And I'd rather be evidence than cargo."

* * *

They broke at midday, and the rain came harder, drumming on the courthouse roof like a crowd that wouldn't disperse. In the side corridor, over cups of tea nobody drank, Teodoro watched Jonah sit very still with her palms flat on her knees, the posture of a pilot holding attitude in turbulence.

"The lawyer will come back at you this afternoon," he said. "They'll have spent lunch pulling your flight logs. Every strip you ever used, every village downstream of every crate."

"I know."

"They'll try to make the room hate you before it believes you."

"I know, Teo." And then, quieter, with something that wasn't quite a smile: "Let them. I've been hating me longer. I'm better at it."

He wanted to argue. He understood instead why he shouldn't—that her self-indictment wasn't despair, it was ballast, deliberately taken aboard so the truth would fly level. He had spent forty years doing the opposite: jettisoning his own guilt piece by piece until there was nothing left to hold him down, and look how far that had gotten him off course.

"When I testify tomorrow," he said, "about my family's records. About the copy we sold in the famine year."

"That's yours to tell."

"It's ours now. That's rather the problem." He looked at his tea. "My grandfather kept ledgers for the coast for two hundred years, and the one time it mattered, he sold a piece of the truth to save his own roof. Everything after—the fire, the confiscation, me living rough on beaches reading other people's archives—it all compounded from that entry. Interest on interest." He looked up. "You said once that loyalty and truth were different debts. I've decided they're the same debt. We just paid on different schedules."

Jonah reached over and straightened his collar, a small domestic violence of affection. "Then tomorrow we file jointly," she said, "like bankrupts."

* * *

The afternoon session was the one the lawyers had built their day around.

They produced the flight logs. They projected manifests onto a cloth screen. They walked the room, strip by strip, through five years of Jonah's routes, and stopped—of course—at the one she had been waiting for since dawn.

"This airstrip," the lead counsel said, tapping the map. "Upland interior. One delivery, four years ago, autumn. You logged the cargo as medical supplies. Can you confirm the contents?"

"No," Jonah said. "That's the point of unlabeled crates."

"But you logged them as medicine."

"I logged what I was told to log. That's what the logging was *for*."

The lawyer let the silence stretch, then turned to the gallery with the gentle horror of a prosecutor unveiling tragedy. "Three weeks after that delivery, the village at the end of that strip burned. Forty-one dead. The fire began at the storehouse where such deliveries were received." She faced Jonah. "Did you know?"

Here it was. The question she had been flying away from for four years, finally landed, finally asked, in a room full of people from villages that looked like that one.

"I didn't know," Jonah said. "I suspected. I've suspected for four years, and I never once flew back to ask, because asking meant knowing, and knowing meant I couldn't keep flying for them, and not flying meant the debt, and the debt meant—" She stopped. Breathed. The fan turned overhead, patient as tide. "You want me to say I'm guilty. Fine. Guilty of exactly this: I chose not to know, and not-knowing is a choice, and forty-one people paid for mine. Write it in whatever ledger you like. But write the rest of the entry too, because a half-record is a forgery—I learned that from the best forgers on this coast. Write who filled the crate. Write who ordered the route. Write who owned the storehouse the fire started in, and which company collected the insurance, and which land titles changed hands in the two seasons after the ash cooled. My crime has a chain attached. Follow the chain."

The room had gone very quiet. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

Magistrate Faridah's pen had not stopped moving. "The commission," she said, "will follow the chain."

* * *

What happened over the following three days, no one in that room had quite predicted, though afterward everyone claimed to have seen it coming.

Confession, it turned out, was contagious in the way fear was. Jonah's testimony—unqualified, unarmored, naming herself before she named anyone else—had broken some seal the Syndicate's lawyers had spent decades maintaining. Because here was the thing about an organization built on forged entries and bought silences: every person inside it was also a hostage of it, and hostages only need to see one prisoner walk out the door on her own feet before they start testing the locks.

A fuel clerk from the Syndicate's southern depot came forward on the second day, unprompted, with ledgers showing the insurance fraud behind the burned village. A boat captain testified to running survey stakes and, later, torches. The enforcer Jonah had pulled drowning from the lower chamber appeared in the gallery on the third day with his arm in a sling and his testimony already sworn—he named names, dates, the standing order regarding Karang Laut that had been issued *before* Ru's ultimatum, proving the burning had never been contingent on Jonah's answer at all. It had been scheduled. Arson as bookkeeping.

And through all of it, the documents from the iron chest sat entered into evidence, and beside them the photographs of the cave's carved ledgers, and beside those Teodoro Reyes, upright at the witness rail with his family's archive stamp displayed like a relic, reading aloud in a clear voice the founding entry of the Marrow Syndicate—not a charter but a *debt*, collateral recorded against a loan, the entire empire revealed as a lien that had never been discharged.

The Syndicate's lawyers objected. They moved to seal. They filed motions with the desperation of men bailing with teacups. But the room had changed chemistry. When the lead counsel rose on the third afternoon to characterize Jonah Zhou as "a confessed criminal of demonstrable unreliability," an old fisherman in the second row said, loudly enough for the record, "So were we all, love. Difference is she stopped."

The gallery laughed—not cruelly, but with the relief of people recognizing themselves—and the laughter did more damage than any document.

By the fourth day, warrants had been issued for six members of the board. Two had already surrendered, each claiming to have been the first to cooperate. The Syndicate's accounts were frozen in three ports. Auntie Ru's name appeared nowhere yet, and Jonah understood why with a cold professional respect: Ru had always been the one who kept the books, and the keeper of the books knows precisely which page to be absent from.

"She'll surface," Teodoro said that evening, on the courthouse steps, watching the boats come into the harbor through the clearing rain. "When it's time to negotiate. Or when it's time to disappear. Ru doesn't do anything else."

"I know." Jonah watched the light break gold along the wet stones. "You know what I keep thinking? Five years I stayed loyal to them because I thought loyalty was what kept me alive. Turns out it was just what kept me *theirs*. The truth was the lifeboat. I was swimming past it for years, waving."

Teodoro said nothing for a moment. Then: "Tomorrow they'll turn to sentencing. Suspended license, probably. Fines. Maybe worse—they'll want a price paid publicly, so the coast sees that confessing isn't free."

"I know that too." She flexed her hands, feeling for the controls that wouldn't be there. "Do you regret it? Any of it? You gave them your family's stamp. I gave them—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the whole inventory, "—everything."

"I gave them a stamp," Teodoro said. "They gave me back my grandfather's name. On any honest ledger, that's a profit." He paused. "And you?"

Jonah looked out at the harbor, at the fishing boats riding easy on the settling sea, at the far coast where a village was rebuilding its fish sheds plank by plank because a pilot had finally stopped calculating and gone back for a drowning man.

"I feel lighter," she said, and sounded surprised by it. "Isn't that obscene? I should feel gutted. I confessed to everything. And I feel like I've been flying at half weight for five years and only just noticed."

"That's not obscene," Teodoro said. "That's just what happens when the cargo's finally labeled."

Behind them, through the tall doors, the clerk was calling the order of business for the morrow: sentencing, settlements, and—one item added late that afternoon, in fresh ink, over the objections of three gray suits—a motion to examine certain financial instruments held in trust by a party identified only as *R.M.*, caretaker accounts, port of origin unspecified.

Jonah read the notice twice.

"Auntie Ru's initials," she said quietly. "She's not running. She's *filing*."

Teodoro followed her gaze to the horizon, where the last storm clouds were tearing open over a sea going suddenly, ruthlessly bright. "Then tomorrow," he said, "we find out what the bookkeeper wants when the books are closed."

The fan in the empty hearing room turned on without anyone, patient as tide, over chairs that would not stay empty long.
