## 40. First Flight, New Manifest

The license came on a Tuesday, in an envelope with a government stamp and a coffee ring on the corner where some clerk had set his cup down on her future without noticing.

Jonah held it up to the hangar light the way she'd once held banknotes—checking for forgery, checking for tricks. *Zhou, Jonah. Commercial Pilot, Coastal Class. Restrictions: none.* The photograph was three years old, taken back when she'd still smiled for cameras because she hadn't yet learned what they could be used for. Her hair had been longer. Her eyes had been the same: tired, watchful, already halfway to somewhere else.

"Restrictions: none," she read aloud, to the empty hangar, and laughed—a short, disbelieving sound that bounced off the corrugated walls and came back sounding like someone else's laugh.

The suspension had lasted eleven months. Eleven months of walking everywhere, of taking the mail boat when she needed to reach Karang Laut, of standing at the edge of airstrips watching other people's planes climb out over the strait with her hands in her pockets and her teeth clenched around something that wasn't quite envy and wasn't quite grief. Eleven months of hearings, of testimony given in rooms that smelled like floor wax and old paper, of saying *yes, I carried it* and *no, I didn't know* and then—when the magistrate's niece fixed her with Salmah's same patient stare—*no. I suspected. I chose not to know. There's a difference, and it's mine.*

They had taken the license for that difference. They had given it back for what she'd done after.

She pinned the envelope to the corkboard above her workbench, next to the tide tables and a photograph of the rebuilt fish sheds at Karang Laut, their new iron roofs bright as coins in the sun. Then she went back to loading the plane.

---

The cargo manifest sat on a clipboard hooked to the strut, and Jonah had written it out herself, in her own hand, every line legible, every weight accounted for:

**Item 1:** Vaccines, refrigerated, 40 kg — Karang Laut clinic.
**Item 2:** Archive copies, photographic plates and ledgers, sealed, 22 kg — Coastal Archive, Karang Laut.
**Item 3:** Mail and personal parcels, 9 kg.
**Item 4:** Spare parts, engine, 15 kg.

Four items. Four weights. Nothing unlabeled. Nothing she couldn't hand to a customs officer with a straight spine and a steady voice.

It was the shortest manifest of her career and the heaviest thing she'd ever loaded.

She checked the tie-downs twice anyway. Old habits didn't die; they just changed employers. She'd spent five years learning how to lie with paperwork—how to make a crate weigh less on paper than it did in the world, how to split one shipment into three so no single manifest ever told the whole story. Now she was learning the opposite discipline, which turned out to be harder: making the paper tell exactly the truth, all of it, in order, where anyone could read it.

There was a freedom in it she hadn't expected. Like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was carrying, and then discovering her shoulders had been up around her ears for years.

The dawn was coming up gray-gold over the strait as she ran the preflight. The monsoon had fully broken two weeks ago—the tail end of it, the honest part, when the rain finally admits it's finished and the sky comes out scrubbed and enormous. The air this morning was cool enough to see her breath, and the wind off the sea had that clean edge it only gets after months of being wet. Her breath fogged the cowling as she leaned in to check the oil. Good level. Good color. The engine had been rebuilt during the suspension by a mechanic in the capital who worked slowly and charged fairly and asked no questions about the past, and it turned over now like something with nothing to hide.

She climbed into the cockpit. The seat knew her. That was the strange thing about machines—they kept your shape better than people did.

On the passenger seat, strapped in with its own harness because she'd run out of room in the baggage bay, sat a wooden box the size of a bread loaf. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, was Teodoro Reyes's family archive stamp—brass and horn, older than any government currently claiming the coast—and beneath it, a letter he'd asked her to deliver to Ibu Salmah, which was either about cataloguing schedules or about the thing they had both spent forty chapters not saying, depending on how you read the handwriting.

He'd pressed the stamp into her hands at the archive door last week, awkward as a boy, looking anywhere but at her face.

"You'll be flying past the reef," he'd said. "Someone should carry it over the water once. Just once. For—" He'd stopped, started again. "For the record."

"For the record," she'd agreed, and neither of them had smiled until they were both already turning away, and then they'd both smiled at nothing, like idiots, like people.

---

The tower at the coastal strip was a shed with a radio and a man named Pak Darmawan who had known her since before she'd been worth knowing. He waved her off with two fingers and a grin.

"Zhou! You're heavy today. What are you carrying?"

"The truth," she said into the mic, deadpan, "and forty kilos of vaccines."

"Ha! The vaccines I believe." But his laughter followed her down the strip, warm, unafraid, and she thought: *this is what it sounds like when nobody's listening for the lie.*

The wheels left the ground at 6:14 by her father's old watch, which she still wore even though it lost four minutes a day and had been through salt water twice. Some things you keep not because they work but because they're yours.

She climbed out over the strait, banking south toward Karang Laut, and the coast unrolled beneath her like a chart being redrawn by a kinder cartographer. The mangrove channels where she'd hidden from Syndicate boats were green and glittering, ordinary water doing ordinary things. The fish farms were running again—she could see the stilt houses, the drying racks, the smoke from breakfast fires standing up straight in the still air. Somewhere down there was a village that had burned because she'd hesitated, and been rebuilt because other people refused to let hesitation be the last word. Somewhere down there was Ibu Salmah, who had forgiven her in the only currency that mattered: with work. With a job. With *fly for us now, girl, and fly straight.*

She flew straight.

The reef came up on her left at seven o'clock, right where the charts said it would be, and Jonah tipped the wing to look.

From the air, at this tide, there was nothing to see. Just the reef's dark shoulder breaking the swell, white water stitching along its edge, the sea keeping its own books. No black mouth. No cave. The monsoon had sealed it the way it had sealed it for centuries—sand and coral and rising water closing over the entrance like a ledger being shut, like a hand pressing flat across a page that said: *not yet. Come back when the tide is low and you're ready to read.*

But she knew it was under there. She could feel it the way you feel a scar under clothing—not pain, exactly. Presence. A cathedral of carved stone holding centuries of tolls and treaties and debts, holding the founder's original loan, holding the proof that the Syndicate's whole empire had been collateral on money its own founder never repaid. Holding, too, the places where panicked scouts had chiseled at the carvings in their last desperate weeks, and failing, because stone keeps better books than men. Teodoro had said that once, crouched in the lamplight with his fingers an inch from the wall, and she'd watched him almost weep and almost laugh in the same breath.

The deep ledgers survived. The archive at Karang Laut now held four thousand photographs of them, catalogued in Teodoro's small precise hand, open to any fisherman or landowner or clerk who wanted to come look. Already three villages had filed claims. Already two Syndicate land titles had been voided, and the lawyers in the capital had stopped pretending they could stop it. The truth was loose in the world now, multiplying the way truths do—slowly, then all at once, then irreversibly.

Jonah leveled the wings and flew on.

Below her, the water over the reef shimmered, indifferent and complete. *Keeping its own counsel,* she thought. It would open again next season, and the season after, and pilgrims would pay their tolls and villagers would leave their offerings, and maybe someday a magistrate's commission would come with proper lights and proper witnesses, and the stone would give up everything it had, patiently, the way it always had. The cave owed nobody anything. That was the whole lesson of it, really. It had never been owned. It had only ever been *kept*—by tides, by legends, by a family of archivists who'd paid terribly for the keeping and whose grandson now stood in its doorway with his family's stamp on the lintel and his spine, at last, straight.

She was glad, suddenly and completely, that she couldn't see it. Some things should stay closed until you're ready. She'd spent five years flying away from things. This morning, for the first time, she was flying toward them, and the difference was not in the heading. The difference was in her chest, where the dread used to live and where something quieter had moved in—something that felt suspiciously like anticipation, like a woman going to work.

---

Karang Laut's strip was underwater at high tide and gold sand at low, and she timed her arrival for the ebb, touching down smooth as a gull landing on a rail. The tires hissed on wet sand. Children materialized from between the fish sheds the way children do, running alongside the wing as she taxied to the hard pan, shouting her name—not *Pilot Zhou*, not the wary half-name the Syndicate years had earned her, but just *Jonah, Jonah!*—and she cut the engine and sat for a moment in the sudden silence with her eyes closed, letting the sound settle over her like weather.

Then she climbed out and got to work.

The vaccines went to the clinic, where the new nurse—a round, brisk woman from the capital who'd taken the posting nobody else wanted—signed for them on an actual form, with an actual date, and made Jonah check the cold chain seal in front of her. Jonah did it gladly. Let the paperwork be boring. Boring paperwork was a luxury she intended to enjoy for the rest of her life.

The archive copies went up the hill to the long building with the new door, where Teodoro was waiting on the steps with the stamp already mounted in its case above the lintel, brass catching the midmorning sun. He took the box from her hands carefully, the way he handled everything old, and then stood there holding it and not leaving, which was unlike him.

"They've scheduled the first public reading," he said. "Next month. Salmah wants the founder's debt entry read aloud, on the beach, where everyone can hear. She says—" he paused, and the corner of his mouth moved— "she says the coast has spent eighty years paying interest on a lie, and it's time somebody read the principal out loud."

"She would say it exactly like that."

"I wrote it down verbatim." He looked at her then, properly, and whatever was in his face made her want to stand very still. "You'll come? For the reading?"

"I'll fly you here myself," Jonah said. "Front row. Well—front row floating, on the causeway, if the tide cooperates."

"It never cooperates," Teodoro said. "That's rather the point of it."

They stood a moment longer in the doorway of the archive, under the stamp, in the smell of paper and salt, and the thing they weren't saying sat between them, patient as the cave, keeping its own counsel, waiting for a lower tide. Neither of them rushed it. They had both learned the same lesson from the same stone: some entries you don't force. You wait for the water to go out, and then you walk in, and you write it down true.

---

By late afternoon the plane was fueled, the parcels delivered, the mail collected, and Jonah stood at the edge of the tidal strip with her chart spread on the wing and the wind pulling at the corners, deciding.

This was the part she'd been waiting for all day, though she hadn't admitted it to herself until now. Every flight of her old life had ended with a decision made for her—by debts, by Auntie Ru's arithmetic, by the geography of staying ahead of consequences. Even the great flights, the storm flight, the courthouse run, had been shaped by pursuit. Someone behind her. Something chasing. The heading chosen by fear.

Auntie Ru was gone now—testifying, ironically, from a cell in the capital, having asked with her last free breath that Jonah tell the truth about her, all of it, and Jonah had, and it had been the strangest mercy she'd ever performed. The Syndicate was a name on court documents. The debt was dissolved, entered as settled in a ledger far less honest than the cave's but binding nonetheless. Nobody was behind her. Nothing was chasing.

And so the chart lay open on the wing, and the whole coast lay open beyond it, and for the first time in five years the question was not *where can I go that they won't follow* but simply *where do I want to wake up tomorrow.*

She looked north, toward the capital, where the hearings still droned and her name was now spoken in the same breath as the word *witness*. She looked east, toward the open sea and the islands she'd never mapped because mapping them had never paid. She looked south, down the coast, toward the little strips and fishing towns that had started sending letters to the archive—*we have records too, old ones, family ones, can someone come look*—and toward the growing list in Teodoro's neat handwriting of places that needed a pilot who could land on sand and carry the truth without dropping it.

Salmah found her there, as Salmah always found everyone eventually, moving down the beach with the unhurried authority of a woman who owned the ground by knowing it. She looked at the chart, and at Jonah, and at the empty sky.

"Well?" she said. "Where?"

"That's the thing," Jonah said. "I get to pick."

Salmah snorted. "Pick fast, then. Tide's coming in, and I'm not digging you out." But her hand landed briefly on Jonah's shoulder—heavy, brief, real—and stayed a half-second longer than efficiency required, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

Jonah folded the chart along its worn creases and climbed into the cockpit. She ran the checklist by rote, called the strip clear, and brought the engine up to full song. The children scattered, waving. The fish sheds threw long shadows westward. Behind her the reef slept under the flooding tide, keeping its accounts, needing nothing from her tonight.

She rolled down the golden sand and lifted off into the last of the light, climbing away from Karang Laut with a cargo hold that was empty except for spare parts and her own honest weight, and a fuel load calculated for a specific destination, and a heading she set with her own two hands:

South. Down the coast. Toward the letters, and the unmapped strips, and the towns with old family records in tin boxes under beds—toward whatever needed carrying next, and whoever needed it carried to.

Not running. Going.

The coastline scrolled beneath her, lit gold and rose by the dying day, and Jonah Zhou flew into it steady and level and free, writing her own manifest one mile at a time—and if, far to the south, where the chart showed only blank water and a rumor of islands, something waited that would need finding, well.

She'd always been good at finding things.

Now she was finally allowed to choose what.
