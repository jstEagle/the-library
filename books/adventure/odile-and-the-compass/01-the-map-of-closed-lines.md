## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Petra Galloway would later consider the first wrong thing about it. Tuesdays were for invoices. He kept his life arranged the way he kept his drafting table: everything squared, everything accounted for, nothing left to chance or weather. The letter broke the arrangement before he even opened it, because it had no return address and no postmark—only a smear of wax that had softened in transit and re-hardened crooked, like a bone badly set.

He stood in the doorway of his rented room above the freight office and held it a long moment, letting the rain drum its argument against the shutters. Fifteen years of discipline said: burn it unopened. Fifteen years of living off the books, of mapping closed lines for private clients who wanted their land disputes settled without witnesses, of paying cash and moving every three years and never once giving his true name to a landlord—that discipline had kept him alive. It had also kept him alone, though he'd stopped thinking of it in those terms somewhere around year six. A man can get used to anything. That was the whole trick of it.

He opened the letter anyway. That was the second wrong thing.

*Six men went into the Serrano basin on the fourteenth of March,* it read, in a hand so even it might have been typed. *They did not come out. Their sponsors require certainty. Your fee is enclosed. No reply is expected.*

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were banknotes enough to buy the room outright, and beneath them a single sheet: a government survey warrant, stamped and countersigned, authorizing "the bearer" to enter the flooded districts. The name on the warrant was not his. It wasn't anyone's—it was a name like a placeholder, a coat hung on an empty hook. Whoever wrote the letter knew exactly what kind of man they were hiring. They hadn't needed to ask.

Petra sat down at his table and did what he always did with a problem: he measured it. Six surveyors. Government sponsorship—which meant the client was not the government but someone who could afford to make the government's paperwork move, someone with reach and something to hide. A missing team was a tragedy; a missing team whose disappearance required *certainty* rather than rescue was a liability being priced. And they had chosen him, a man with no records, no history, no name worth tracing, because whatever answer he found, he could be erased as easily as the surveyors themselves.

He should have declined. He drafted the refusal twice, actually—the polite version and the curt one—and then he looked at the warrant again, at the coordinates printed in the corner, and felt something in his chest shift like ballast sliding in a hull.

The Serrano basin. He had mapped a closed line through it once, nine years ago, for a mining concern that had gone under before the rails were ever laid. He remembered the country: drowned villages, a river that spread each monsoon into a brown sea the width of a province, railbeds running into open water like roads into dreams. He remembered standing on an embankment at dusk with his theodolite and feeling—for the first time since he'd begun erasing himself—not fear, but a strange, unwelcome peace. As if the water didn't care who he had been. As if it would take him or leave him on equal terms.

He burned both drafts of the refusal. Then he packed.

* * *

The interior took three days to reach by road and would have taken less by rail, but the rail was closed, and closed lines were his parish. He traveled light: instruments, dry clothes sealed in waxed canvas, a rifle he hoped not to use, and the warrant folded against his ribs where the rain couldn't reach it. The road degraded from gravel to mud to a suggestion of mud, and the world greened around him until it seemed the vegetation itself was breathing. Monsoon country did not have weather; it had moods. The rain came down in sheets, then paused as if listening, then resumed with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

By the third day he was poling through flooded forest in a borrowed skiff, following a railbed that surfaced and submerged like the spine of some enormous sleeping animal. Water stood waist-deep between the trees. Fish moved through the understory, silver flickers among the trunks. Once, passing over what had been a village square—he knew it from his old map, the church bell tower rising black from eight feet of water—a heron lifted off the roofline and regarded him with an eye like a drop of mercury.

He found the camp on the evening of the fourth day, at the place where two old lines crossed: the dead railway and a surveyor's cut through the forest, both of them his own work from another life. The tents were still standing.

That was the first thing wrong. Tents do not stand for weeks in monsoon country unless someone maintains them. These had been guyed and re-guyed, the flysheets taut, the pegs fresh-set in ground that was itself underwater. Someone had been here recently—or the six men had simply walked out one morning and the camp had refused, out of some stubbornness of canvas and cord, to admit they weren't coming back.

But there were no tracks. That was the second thing. The floodplain around the camp was a page of soft silt that recorded everything—heron prints, caiman drag-marks, the stitched trails of wading birds—and it recorded nothing human. Six men had gone in. No feet had carried them out. Not walking, at least.

Petra stood at the edge of the camp for a long time, rain running off his hat brim, and made himself look the way he looked at a collapsed bridge: systematically, without flinching, reading failure in the order it occurred.

The mess kit was stacked and clean. The instrument cases stood open and empty except for padding—someone had removed the theodolites and levels carefully, or they had been removed for them. Boots lined the tent walls in two neat rows. Twelve boots. He counted twice. Twelve, for six men, all present, all laces tucked inside in the manner of men who expected to need them again soon. Nobody walks barefoot out of caiman country. Nobody leaves twelve pairs of boots behind at all, let alone tidily.

And on the folding table in the central tent, weighted down with a stone against the wind, lay a compass.

It was brass, old, heavy for its size, the case worn smooth in the way of things handled daily across generations. The face was porcelain, and the markings were wrong—not degrees but names, dozens of tiny engraved names circling the card like a litany, too small to read without a lens. The needle did not point north. It pointed south-southeast, steady as a plumb line, toward open water and the drowned villages beyond.

Petra picked it up and the needle swung—not toward north, not away from it, but *around*, tracking him as he turned it, settling always on the same bearing relative to his body. He walked ten paces east. The needle followed him. He walked back. It followed again, patient, faithful, pointing at him the way a dog points at game.

His hands, which had not shaken in fifteen years, shook now. He set the compass back under its stone and stepped outside into the rain and made himself breathe.

Certainty, the letter had asked for. Well. Here was a camp maintained by no one, boots for men who never left, instruments vanished without tracks, and a compass that pointed at living things. Six men had not wandered off. Six men had been *taken*—taken cleanly, deliberately, by someone who wanted the camp to look lived-in and the story to end in drowning. And whoever they were, they had left this one instrument behind either carelessly or as bait, and Petra did not believe, anymore, in carelessness.

Far off across the flooded forest, almost lost under the rain, he heard an engine—low, throttled way down, the sound of a boat moving slowly and wanting not to be heard. Then a second engine, farther west. Then silence, which was worse.

Petra looked at the compass through the tent flap, needle holding its patient line toward him, toward any living heart that came close enough. He thought about the empty hook where his name used to hang, and how easily it could be filled with a grave marker instead.

He took the compass. It seemed suddenly very important that whatever it pointed at, it should not be left pointing at nothing.

Then he waded to the skiff and began to pole downstream, fast and quiet, toward the only person the river country had ever named to him without his asking: a pilot who ran the flooded channels for money and asked no questions, and who was, if the freight-office gossip could be trusted, moored tonight at the drowned landing called Vesper Reach.

Behind him, the engines grew no louder. But they did not go away.
