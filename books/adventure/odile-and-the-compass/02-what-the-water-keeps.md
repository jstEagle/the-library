## 2. What the Water Keeps

Odile had counted the money twice before she said yes, and she still didn't like the arithmetic of it. Three hundred and forty in worn notes, enough to patch the *Mireille*'s hull where the rot had gone soft along the starboard strake, enough to buy diesel and rice and a new propeller shaft before the river rose another meter and made liars of all the charts. Enough, too, to pay her crew through the month, which mattered more than any of it, because a pilot who couldn't feed her people was no pilot at all.

The man who'd offered it stood on her dock in the gray hour before dawn, dripping from the walk out, holding a leather case against his chest like something he'd been told never to set down. Petra Galloway — though that wasn't the name on the paper he showed her, and Odile had been running this river long enough to know that a man with two names was either trouble or hiding from it, and usually both.

"You want to go up past the drowned villages," she said. "Past Sengkang bend."

"I want to find six men who walked into the water country and didn't walk out."

"Nobody walks out of the water country in monsoon. The water walks you." She looked him over: the careful boots, the way his hands moved like they were always measuring something, the case he wouldn't let out of arm's reach. "What's in the bag?"

"Equipment from their camp. Instruments. A compass."

"A compass." She almost laughed. "I have three compasses and none of them work past the bend. The iron in the old rail bridge pulls every needle west. You don't navigate up there. You read the water."

"Then I need someone who reads it."

She should have said no. Every instinct she had about strangers with money and missing men said no. But the *Mireille* had a hole in her belly and her crew had eaten through the last of the rice, and Odile had learned long ago that principle was a luxury sold by the kilo to people who owned dry land.

"We leave at first light," she said. "You sleep on the boat. And whatever's in that case stays shut until we're past the bend. I don't need your ghosts spooking my river."

* * *

Petra lay awake most of that first night on a thin mattress in the wheelhouse, listening to the rain come down in sheets and thinking about how much of his life had been spent listening to things he couldn't see. Fifteen years of mapping closed lines had taught him that. A closed line was a promise the railroad made to itself: *this track exists, but nothing runs on it, no one maintains it, no one official remembers it.* He had walked them all. He knew which trestles would hold a man's weight and which would drop him forty feet into black water, and he knew because he'd tested them alone, without report, without witness, building maps that existed only in his own hand and in the waterproof tubes he buried in caches along the routes.

Invisible work for an invisible man. It had suited him once. It had suited him right up until the letter came, unsigned, offering more money than he'd seen in a decade to find six government surveyors who had officially never existed either. Two erasures looking for each other. He'd told himself it was just another job. Lying in the dark while Odile's crew slept in hammocks slung between cargo cradles, hearing the river breathe under the hull, he admitted what he'd known since the dock: he hadn't taken the job for the money. He'd taken it because the surveyors were erased the way he was erased, and some part of him that he kept locked below the waterline wanted to know what happened to men like him when they stopped being careful.

The compass sat in its case at his feet. He hadn't opened it since the camp. At the camp it had already been wrong — needle swinging lazy and purposeless whenever he held it, settling nowhere, pointing at nothing a compass should point at. He'd assumed it was broken. Broken instruments were the one language he spoke fluently.

* * *

By the third day they were past everything Odile recognized as belonging to the ordinary world.

The river had swallowed the lowlands whole. Trees stood to their shoulders in brown water, their crowns making small green islands where macaws screamed at each other across distances that used to be forest floor. The drowned village of Ampasibe surfaced out of the flood like a rumor — rooftops just breaking the surface, a church spire leaning at an angle that suggested the ground itself had given way, and beneath all of it, thirty feet down where the light couldn't reach, the streets where people had lived.

"My grandmother's village," Odile said quietly, steering by a submerged fence line only she could see, reading the current's texture the way other people read print. "The government built the dam upstream and told everyone the flooding would be seasonal. That was nineteen years ago. The water never left."

Petra watched her hands on the wheel. She steered the way he mapped — total attention, nothing wasted, a lifetime of accumulated knowledge held so close it looked like instinct. "You stayed," he said.

"The water is my country now. Somebody has to know its roads." She nodded toward the church spire. "There's a channel behind it. Thirty meters wide, if the silt hasn't shifted since the last big flood. We'll cut through and come out above the bend, save half a day."

"And if the silt has shifted?"

"Then we spend an interesting afternoon digging the boat off a sandbar that used to be somebody's kitchen garden."

It went exactly as badly as that. They grounded hard on a submerged wall around noon, and spent four hours poling and winching the *Mireille* free while the rain fell and the crew cursed in three languages. But it was during those hours, sweat-soaked and shoulder-deep together in warm brown water, hauling on a line while the boat groaned over the obstruction, that Petra noticed something he would think about for the rest of his life.

He was carrying the compass case slung across his back, and as they worked, he became aware of a weight shifting inside it. Not the instruments. The needle.

When they pulled to the north, the needle strained north. When Odile waded east to rig a tackle point, the needle swung east after her. When the crew gathered at the stern, all five hands clustered together, the needle pointed at the cluster and trembled there like a dog scenting home.

"It's not broken," Petra said, standing waist-deep beside her, holding the open case flat on his palm so she could see. "Look."

Odile looked. Her face did something complicated — the wariness draining out of it, replaced by a kind of fierce, unsettled recognition. "That's not a compass," she said. "Compasses point at the pole. This points at *us*."

"Or at living people. It found the crew before I understood what I was seeing. It's been correcting toward whoever's closest and alive." He turned slowly in the water, watching the needle track Odile as she moved. "Six surveyors carried this into the flooded country. Six surveyors vanished."

"They were looking for survivors." Odile's voice had gone flat and hard. "Somebody sent them to find living people in a drowned land. And then somebody made the finders disappear." She hauled herself aboard, wrung out her shirt, and stood at the wheel with her jaw set. "Petra. Who hired you?"

"An unsigned letter. A lawyer's office in the capital. Payment through a bank that handles mining concessions."

"That's not an answer. That's a costume on an answer." She spun the wheel and the *Mireille* came about, heading for the channel behind the church. "We're going to the last place the surveyors were seen, and then we are leaving this river faster than anything has ever left it."

* * *

They found the trail's end at dusk, in the ruins of a smaller village upstream, one so far gone even Odile's charts had let it go. A concrete marker post, government-stamped, driven into what had been a market square and was now a shallow lagoon. Tied to it, a length of orange survey twine, rotted but knotted in the pattern Petra recognized from the camp — the same field-expedient markers, the same methodical hand.

And on the flagstone beneath the waterline, visible only because the afternoon sun broke through for eleven minutes and lit the lagoon like a lamp: boot prints. Six sets, walking in. No sets walking out.

The water around the stones was darker than the water anywhere else. Odile, who could tell you what lay under the surface of any reach of her river by the color of it, went pale and would not say why.

Petra stood in the bow with the compass open in his hands, and the needle did not spin. It settled. It pointed steadily, unwaveringly, at a stretch of flooded shoreline two hundred meters east where the trees grew thick and the water ran still — and beneath the stillness, faintly, unmistakably, the needle trembled the way it had trembled over his own crew. Over living hearts.

"Somebody's out there," he said. "Alive."

"Or the instrument remembers them." Odile's voice was very quiet. "Either way, we're not going in there at night."

They anchored midstream instead, in the current, where nothing could approach without the hull telling them first. Petra took the first watch. At some hour past midnight, rain hammering the canvas, he saw lights.

Not fishing lights. Fishing boats on this river ran single lamps, low, amber, close to the water. These were white, paired, mounted high, moving in formation — three boats abreast, sweeping the channel in a grid pattern, searchlights raking the flooded treeline in slow, deliberate arcs. Government hulls. Gun mounts under tarps. And moving downstream toward them with the patience of men being paid by the hour.

Odile was at the wheel before Petra finished climbing down from the roof. She killed the anchor light, started the engine at minimum turn, and took the *Mireille* into the flooded forest — a maneuver no chart would sanction and no trained pilot would attempt, threading between submerged trunks by memory and moonless instinct, running dark, engine muffled to a mutter.

"How do you know where the trees are?" Petra asked, pressed against the wheelhouse frame, heart going like a trip-hammer.

"I've hit most of them already, over the years. I remember the ones that cost me." She glanced at him, and for the first time since the dock there was no transaction in her face, no employer and no hire. Just two people in the dark, in the same boat, in every sense her language had a word for. "They're not patrolling for smugglers tonight. Smugglers they fine. This is a sweep. Sweeps mean they're erasing something. Or someone."

The searchlights passed astern, close enough that the glare slid across the canopy above them like a hand feeling for a latch. Then the engines faded, and the river closed over the sound, and they were alone again in the drowned world.

Petra realized his hands were shaking, and that Odile's weren't, and that hers had been steady the entire time because this — being hunted on her own river — was a thing she had survived before, in ways she hadn't yet told him.

"They'll be back tomorrow," she said. "With more boats. Whatever those surveyors found, the state wants it dead, and now the state knows someone came asking after them." She looked at the compass lying open on the chart table, its needle trembling faithfully toward the dark eastern shore, toward whatever — whoever — waited there. "So here's my question, engineer, and think carefully, because tomorrow we run it either downriver or in: do we go back for the living people that needle keeps pointing at? Or do we run, and let the water keep them like it kept everything else?"

Petra looked at the needle. He thought about six sets of boot prints walking in and none walking out. He thought about the letter that had hired him precisely because he was a man with no name to lose.

"We go in," he said. "At first light. Before the boats do."

Odile nodded once, and turned the wheel toward the eastern shore, and the *Mireille* slipped deeper into the flooded forest, running dark, carrying them both toward whatever the water had been keeping.
