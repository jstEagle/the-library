## 3. Needle and Noose

The tunnel mouth opened out of the hillside like a wound that had never learned to close. Petra saw it first from the water—a black arch half-drowned, the old rail line's ballast rising in a broken spine above the flood, and beyond it the dark going back and back into the earth. The engineers who built it had cut through two hundred meters of limestone to save themselves four kilometers of river bend, and then the river had changed its mind about everything except the tunnel.

"Kettle Cut," Odile said, easing the *Mireille* alongside the ballast spine. "Freight line, forty years dead. The water comes up to here in a wet year." She held her hand at her collarbone. "This is a wet year."

The compass had led them there. All night it had pulled east-northeast with a patience no magnetic needle possessed, trembling in its brass housing like something eager, and when they rounded the last drowned bend it had swung hard toward the tunnel mouth and stopped—fixed, certain, pointing into the dark where six people might still be alive.

Petra stood in the bow with the boat hook, testing depth. Two feet of clear brown water over the rails. Beyond the arch, the tunnel ceiling came down low enough that they'd have to go in bent double, wading, and the thought of it made his skin crawl—not the dark itself; he'd spent fifteen years in places no map admitted to—but the idea of a space with one entrance. A space you could be sealed into.

"You don't have to come," he said.

Odile was already coiling her mooring line with quick, economical turns. "The needle points at my river," she said. "That makes it mine too."

* * *

Inside, the tunnel was a cathedral of dripping dark. Their headlamps made small trembling circles on the water, on the curved brick of the vault overhead furred white with mineral bloom, on the things the flood had carried in and left: a plastic chair, a child's bicycle grown over with moss, driftwood wedged in the crook of a signal post. The air tasted of stone and iron and something else beneath it, faint and wrong—the sweetish reek of diesel, recent.

Petra counted his own breaths as he walked. It was how he kept fear in its place, filed under measurement. Forty steps in, the water shallowed to ankle depth. Sixty, and Odile's lamp caught the first mark: a chalk arrow on the tunnel wall, professional, survey-grade, pointing deeper.

"They were here," she breathed.

Seventy steps. Another arrow. And then, at ninety, where the tunnel widened into an old passing siding, the lamps found what the water had been keeping.

A camp. Tarpaulins strung between signal posts. Six bedrolls, six packs stacked with military neatness against the wall—and beside them, arranged with a care that turned Petra's stomach, six sets of instruments. Levels, transits, ranging rods, all cleaned and oiled and left. Not abandoned. *Surrendered.* Whoever had taken the survey team had wanted them to understand that nothing they carried would be needed where they were going.

Beside the packs, weighted down with a stone, a radio. Handheld, government issue, its battery light still green.

"It's been used," Odile said. "Look—the casing's warm-worn. Thumb grease on the transmit key."

Petra crouched over it. He should leave it. Every instinct honed across fifteen invisible years said touch nothing, learn nothing, be nothing. But the needle in his breast pocket was straining toward the far end of the tunnel now, quivering, insistent, and somewhere past the limits of their lamplight the water lapped against brick with a rhythm almost like breathing.

He keyed the transmit button and the channel hissed open—and a voice came through, mid-sentence, as if it had been waiting for him.

"—confirming recovery of the instrument. The survey personnel are intact and cooperative. Disposition pending." A pause, static breathing. "Regarding your investigator: identity confirmed. Galloway, P. Formerly Corvus Line engineering staff, dismissed following the Vellacourt inquiry. Fifteen years off-record. Excellent. This is precisely why he was selected. A man with no name cannot testify. A man with no name cannot be missed."

Odile's hand closed on his shoulder. He hadn't heard her cross the water.

"The instrument," the voice continued, unhurried, almost gentle, "is not to be studied. It is to be destroyed, along with all records of the survey. Anyone who has seen it functioning is to be treated as part of the record." Another pause, longer. "You've done well, Galloway. Bring it to the Kessel buoy by tomorrow night and we will discuss restoring what you lost. Your name. Your license. Your line. Everything the river took from you can be given back."

The channel clicked dead.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The water dripped from the vault in slow metronome strokes. Petra became aware that he was shaking—not with fear, or not only with fear, but with a cold fury so precise it felt like clarity. They had chosen him the way a man chooses a match to burn a letter. Cheap. Consumable. And the missing surveyors—*intact and cooperative*—were alive, somewhere upstream, being kept the way evidence is kept.

"They took them alive," Odile said quietly. "Six people. Alive."

"I know."

"And they think you'll trade them for a name."

Petra looked down at the compass in his hand. In the lamplight the needle lay steady, pointing past them both, deeper into the tunnel, toward a second arch half-collapsed at the far end—an old ventilation shaft, maybe, or a service adit, choked with rubble but showing a thread of gray daylight above it. The needle didn't point at the compass's own reflection. It pointed at the shaft. At whatever lay beyond it.

"It's not pointing north," he said. "It never was. It points at—" He stopped, because saying it aloud would make it true, and he wasn't ready for it to be true. But Odile said it for him.

"People," she said. "It points at people."

And then her head came up, sharp as a heron's, and Petra heard it too: engines. Not one. Several. Out on the flooded forest, moving slow, the way boats move when they are searching rather than traveling.

"They know where we are," she whispered. "They've always known. The radio—it wasn't left. It was *baited*."

* * *

Odile

She had run the Kettle Cut rapids twice in her life, both times in daylight, both times with dry holds and a full crew, and both times she had sworn never again. The channel below the eastern weir dropped nine meters in four hundred, threading between the drowned skeletons of the old trestle piers, and the water there did not flow so much as fall in pieces.

Now she stood at the wheel in full dark with a leaking boat, a man she'd known eight days, and a compass that pointed at hearts instead of poles, and she found—to her own astonishment—that she was not afraid. Or she was afraid, entirely afraid, but the fear had changed shape. It had stopped being hers alone.

Petra worked the bilge pump in the stern, methodical even now, counting strokes under his breath. She could hear him through the engine roar. Eleven. Twelve. He counted everything, this man. She understood suddenly that the counting was not caution. It was loneliness, given a profession.

"Hold her true!" he shouted as the first pier loomed out of the rain.

"She has no true!" Odile shouted back, and laughed—actually laughed—as the *Mireille* went up on the first standing wave, the whole world tilting into white noise and spray, and Petra's hand found hers on the wheel and did not let go.

They came through the last gap sideways, hull screaming against stone, and shot out onto the broad black reach below where the rain fell straight and the searchlights behind them shrank to smudges. The boat wallowed, half-full, alive under her hands like a wounded animal that refused to lie down.

Petra came forward through the water on the deck and knelt beside her at the wheel, soaked through, blood on his brow from a gash he clearly hadn't noticed. He was holding the compass. The needle inside it had gone wild—spinning, spinning—because there was no one near, no one ahead, only the two of them and the river.

Except. She watched him watch it. Watched the needle slow, and settle, and swing—not north, not upstream, not toward any shore—but around, trembling, until it pointed at the space between them. At them both.

Neither of them spoke. The rain filled the silence for them.

"We can't outrun them forever," Petra said finally. "Not in this boat. Not anywhere."

"No," Odile agreed. "But we don't have to outrun them. We just have to out-*know* them." She put her hand flat on the deck, on the river running beneath it. "Every pilot, every smuggler, every fisher from here to the delta owes me or I owe them. That's a bigger network than any ministry ever built. If we're going to burn these people down, we burn them with the river's own fire."

Petra looked at her a long moment. Then he did something she would remember for the rest of her life: he smiled. Small, cracked, real—the smile of a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"All right," he said. "Teach me the river."

Behind them the searchlights swept the empty water, finding nothing. Ahead, the rain was beginning to break apart, and through the tearing clouds the first gray suggestion of dawn showed them the way downstream—toward the delta, toward the crews, toward whatever came next.

In Petra's hand, the needle held steady on the only bearing it had ever truly known.
