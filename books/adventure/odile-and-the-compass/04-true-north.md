## 4. True North

Odile had learned to read the river the way other people read faces, and on the morning the agent's boat came up the channel, she read it before she saw it: a wrongness in the water, a hush along the reeds where the herons should have been standing. The government launches ran loud and stupid, churning the current into brown lace. This one came quiet, riding low, its engine muffled like a held breath.

She was mending net on the *Cormorant*'s deck when it slid out of the mist, and she watched Petra stand up from the wheelhouse bench with the particular stillness he had — that engineer's stillness, as if his body were an instrument waiting to be calibrated. He had carried the brass compass against his chest for nine days now, wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, and she could see the shape of it when he breathed.

The boat tied off at the drowned jetty of what had once been Verel, before the water took it. A man stepped ashore in a dry grey suit that had no business surviving this country, and Odile understood immediately that dryness itself was the message: *we are not of this place; we do not belong to its rules.*

"Engineer Galloway," the man said, and the name landed on Petra like a dropped tool.

Odile saw it hit. Fifteen years she'd known him by now — no, nine days, though it felt like fifteen, the way monsoon time swells — and in nine days she had never heard anyone speak his old name aloud. He had given it to her once, drunk on one cup of rice wine, whispered like a confession: *Petra Galloway, chief of alignment, Eastern Division.* Then he'd never said it again, and neither had she.

"I'm not that man," Petra said quietly.

"You could be again." The agent opened a leather folder and let the rain spot it. "Full restoration. Your name cleared, your position reinstated, your pension backdated. There are people who remember your work. They've missed you." He turned a page with two fingers, delicate as a surgeon. "All we require is the instrument you're carrying, and your signature confirming the survey team was lost to natural causes."

"And the team itself?"

"The team is being cared for."

Petra looked at him for a long moment. Odile watched his hands — she always watched his hands, because they were honest even when his face was being careful. His right hand drifted, almost without his knowing it, to the place over his heart where the compass sat.

"They're alive," Petra said. "You took them alive because dead men can't be made to sign things."

The agent's smile thinned. "I'm authorized to be generous."

"You're authorized," Petra said, "to make this river forget. That's all any of you have ever been authorized to do."

* * *

They did not run. That was Odile's decision, and she made it standing at the tiller while the grey suit's boat receded into the rain, because running was what hunted things did, and she refused to teach the river that they were prey.

Instead she did what her mother had done, and her grandmother before the water rose: she sent word down the channels. Not by radio — radio could be listened to. By boat. By boy. By the old network of pilots and smugglers and fuel-runners who moved through the flooded country like blood through a body, invisible and essential and loyal to nothing but each other.

A tin of tea went downstream to Mai-Surin with a strip of paper inside. A fuel drum went upriver to the Khet camps with another. Within two days, copies of the survey records — the ones Petra had photographed in the abandoned camp, coordinates and readings and the compass's impossible behavior logged in his own meticulous hand — were moving through forty boats toward forty mouths, and from there to newspapers in the capital, to foreign correspondents, to a junior minister who owed Odile's cousin a debt older than either of them.

"You understand what you've done," Petra said to her the night the last copy left. He wasn't accusing. He sounded almost frightened for her.

"I understand exactly," Odile said. "I've spent my whole life keeping other people's secrets so this river could stay free. It was time I spent one of mine."

He sat down beside her on the deck, under the tarp, their shoulders touching in the way of people who had stopped pretending touch was accidental. The rain came down in silver rods through the dark.

"They offered me my name back," he said.

"I know. I was there."

"Fifteen years I thought if I could just stay erased, stay unmapped, I'd be safe. Safe from them, safe from—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Safe from wanting anything. Because wanting is how they find you. You want something, you'll fight for it, and then you're predictable."

"And now?"

"Now I think maybe the opposite is true." He turned the compass over in his hands, unwrapped, naked brass gleaming dully in the lamplight. "Maybe hiding is how they keep you. Maybe belonging is the only thing they can't take, because you have to give it away first."

* * *

On the last morning of the flood season, the sky finally cracked open and stayed open, and the river began to fall.

It fell the way it always did — grudgingly, leaving its marks on everything, brown tidelines on the pilings, silt in the rigging, the drowned village of Verel emerging plank by plank like something surfacing from a dream. Odile stood at the rail and watched the water drop a hand's width, then another, and felt the whole country exhale.

Petra came up beside her carrying the compass.

He didn't say anything at first. Neither did she. Some moments you don't narrate; you just stand inside them.

"It was never going to point north," he said finally. "That's why they wanted it destroyed. Not because it works. Because it tells the truth about what matters." He weighed it in his palm. "Every map I ever drew, every line I closed behind me — I thought I was navigating. I was just avoiding."

"What will you do with it?"

"Give it back to the thing that actually knows where home is."

He leaned over the rail, and Odile put her hand on his shoulder — not to stop him, but to go with him, which is a different gesture entirely — and he let the compass drop. It turned once in the air, catching the new sun, and then the river took it without a ripple worth remembering.

Neither of them spoke until the water had smoothed over.

"There will be inquiries," she said. "About the survey. About you. Your name is going to come back whether you want it or not — it's already in the dispatches."

"Let it come back," Petra said. "Names are only anchors if you're ashamed of the ship."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, because he almost never made jokes, and when he did they arrived like rare birds.

* * *

The crew took him in without ceremony, which is the only way that crew did anything. Rassine simply started leaving a second cup at the morning tea. The twins repainted his bunk's name-board without asking what name to paint, and settled on *ENGINEER*, which everyone understood was both true and insufficient. Old Sombath, who spoke perhaps forty words a year, used three of them on the evening of the equinox: "Good. Stay. Family."

Petra burned his map of closed lines that same week, alone at the stern stove, feeding the oilskin pages in one at a time. Odile watched from the wheelhouse, pretending to check charts, watching him watch the fire. Fifteen years of invisibility going up in orange light, every sealed tunnel and decommissioned spur and forgotten siding he'd used to vanish — and his face, as it burned, was not grief. It was relief. It was a man setting down a weight so heavy he'd forgotten it was a weight.

Afterward he came to the wheelhouse and stood beside her at the window, looking out at the falling water, the emerging world, the green shoulders of the hills coming back above the floodline.

"I used to measure everything in miles," he said. "Distance from the nearest record. Distance from the last person who knew my face. I got very good at it. I could tell you to the kilometer how far I was from anyone who might care about me."

"And now?"

"Now I'm measuring in something else." He nodded toward the deck, where Rassine was teaching the younger twin to splice line, their laughter carrying across the water. "In mornings. In cups of tea. In how many people notice if I'm not aboard."

Odile looked at him — this quiet, careful man who had walked into her boat a stranger and a ghost, and who was somehow, without ceremony, without permission asked or granted, simply *staying* — and she felt the compass needle in her own chest swing hard around and settle.

"Then you're home," she said.

The river agreed. It carried the words downstream gently, past the rising village, past the burning-off mist, toward whatever waited next — and for the first time in fifteen years, Petra Galloway was not measuring the distance to anywhere at all.
