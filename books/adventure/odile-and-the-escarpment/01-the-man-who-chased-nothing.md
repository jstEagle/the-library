## 1. The Man Who Chased Nothing

The salt flats began where the world's patience ended.

Ansel Ravensworth had been walking them for three days, and in that time he had counted eleven dead trees, two sun-bleached ribcages of cattle long gone to bone, and one horizon that never moved no matter how many miles he put beneath his boots. The crust of the flats cracked under each step like thin ice over a pond that had forgotten how to be water. Salt dust rose in small white ghosts around his ankles and settled again behind him, erasing his passage as though the land itself were embarrassed by it.

He did not look back. Looking back was a habit for men who expected to be followed, and nobody followed Ansel Ravensworth anywhere. That was rather the point of him.

Ahead, perhaps a mile out — close enough to see the shimmer of heat bend her silhouette, far enough that she might have been a mirage with opinions — walked the thief.

She moved well. He'd give her that much, grudgingly, the way a man gives credit to weather he can't control. She didn't trudge the way most people trudged across the flats; she flowed, low-hipped and easy, her stride eating ground without seeming to spend any. Her dark hair was bound up under a scarf the color of rust, and her pack rode high on her shoulders like it weighed nothing, which Ansel knew for a lie because he'd inventoried what she'd taken from the garrison strongroom at Corren's Ford: four hundred crowns in requisition scrip, a sealed dispatch case, and one silver-handled surveyor's compass that had belonged to Captain Elia Voss.

The compass was why he was here. The scrip was why the commission cared. The compass was why *he* cared, and he had learned over nine years of ranger service that those were not always the same reason, and that the distance between them was where men like him went to die.

He checked the sun, then the ground. The flats would hold their heat another hour past dusk, but the light was already going amber at the edges, thickening, the way honey thickens when it cools. In an hour the shadows would run long and blue and everything on the flats would become a rumor of itself. If she found cover before full dark, he'd lose her until morning. If she didn't find cover, she'd die of exposure like anything else foolish enough to be out here at night, and then he'd have a corpse to carry back to Corren's Ford and a report to write that read like an apology.

Neither outcome suited him. So he walked faster.

* * *

Nine years ago, almost to the season, Captain Elia Voss had pulled him out of a drainage ditch outside the garrison walls and asked him one question: "Can you follow?"

He'd been nineteen, half-starved, wanted in three districts for crimes he had committed and four he hadn't, and the question had struck him as either a trap or a joke. He'd answered honestly, which was rare for him even then. "Anything. Man, beast, rumor."

"Good," she'd said, and hauled him up by the collar with a grip like a dock winch. "Because I need someone who can chase things down and bring them back *whole*. Most rangers only know how to end chases. I need one who knows how to finish them."

She'd meant it as instruction. It had taken him years to understand it was also a warning. A man who ends chases gets to stop thinking about them. A man who finishes them carries every unfinished one home in his chest like river stones, and the weight accumulates, and eventually you either set them down or they set you down into the dirt.

Elia Voss had carried hers for thirty years. Then, last winter, a fever took her in three days, and the stones all came down at once, and Ansel had stood at her bedside holding her hand while she gripped it with that same dock-winch strength, diminished now to the pressure of a child, and said: "The compass. Don't let the compass go to some clerk who'll hang it on a wall. It was my father's. He surveyed the escarpment before anyone thought there was anything worth surveying down there."

"I'll get it back, Captain."

"You don't know who has it yet."

"I'll get it back anyway."

She'd smiled — the first time in weeks — and the smile had cost her something, and she'd spent it anyway, because that was who she'd been. "Stubborn boy," she'd said. "Good. You'll need it." And then she'd slept, and then she hadn't woken, and Ansel Ravensworth had inherited a debt with no ledger entry, no amount owed, no date of collection. Just a silver compass with a hairline crack across its crystal face, walking east out of Corren's Ford on the hip of a thief.

He touched the second compass now — his own, brass-cased, cheap, reliable — clipped inside his coat, and kept walking.

* * *

The thief stopped at midday at a shelf of rock where rainwater had once pooled and left a basin of bitter mineral scum. Ansel watched from six hundred yards, flat against the salt crust, his dust-colored coat doing what it was made to do, which was make him into nothing. She drank sparingly, wet her scarf, wrung it over her neck. Professional habits. She checked her backtrail — swept it left to right, unhurried, thorough — and Ansel felt her gaze pass over him the way a hand passes over a table looking for a coin that isn't there.

Then she shouldered her pack and walked on, and Ansel allowed himself the smallest exhale.

She was good. Better than good. Three days of tracking her had taught him a curriculum: she never doubled back the same way twice; she watered at sources a map wouldn't show; she'd crossed the Sere Wash on a fallen bridge beam that Ansel himself would have hesitated at, and she'd done it at a jog. Once, on the second morning, he'd found where she'd camped and discovered she'd built her fire in the hollow of a dead tree so the smoke would rise through the rotted trunk and disperse before it cleared the branches. He'd stood over that cold fire pit for a long time, feeling something he refused to name, because naming it would have been the first step toward respecting it, and respect was a luxury a lawman couldn't afford a thief.

Not a bandit. Not a cutthroat. She'd taken the strongroom clean, without blood, without waking the duty sergeant — a feat Ansel still couldn't reconstruct, and he'd reconstructed it eleven times. She'd left the pay chest untouched. Only the scrip, the dispatch case, and the compass. Whoever she was, she stole like a surgeon operates: precisely, and only what she'd come for.

It made her harder to hate. He resented that.

* * *

Dusk came down over the flats like a lid closing on a pot.

The light turned the color of old brass, then copper, then a bruised violet that made the salt glow faintly, treacherously, like snow. Ansel closed the distance in the failing light, using the shadows the way she used them, gaining yard by yard while the world lost its edges. She was half a mile out. Then a quarter. Then close enough that he could hear — or imagined he could hear — the soft rhythm of her footfalls on the crust.

He'd planned this part carefully, in the patient, joyless way he planned everything. He would not shout. Shouting gave a runner her decision for free. He would simply close the gap until he was ten paces behind her, and then say, quietly, in the voice he'd learned from Elia Voss — the voice that sounded like paperwork — "Ranger service. Walk with me." And she would weigh her options in the space of three heartbeats, and the flats would take the other options away from her one by one, and she'd walk.

That was the plan. Plans across the flats died like everything else out here — of thirst, mostly.

At two hundred paces, she stopped.

Ansel froze, dropping his weight into his heels, one hand rising slowly to rest on the strap of his pack so nothing creaked. She stood motionless in the violet light, head tilted, listening to something. The wind? He heard nothing but wind. But she stood there a full ten seconds, absolutely still, the way animals stand still when the ground tells them something the air hasn't yet.

Then she turned — not toward him, which would have meant she'd made him, but toward the northeast — and broke into a run.

Ansel swore under his breath and ran too.

The chase lasted four minutes and covered perhaps eight hundred yards, and it was the closest thing to honest conversation he'd had in months. She ran the way she walked, economical, no wasted motion, choosing her footing without looking down. He ran the way he'd been trained, relentless, mechanical, spending endurance like a miser spends gold. The gap held at forty paces, then thirty-five, then thirty, and Ansel felt the old cold clarity settle over him, the tracker's trance where the world narrows to shoulders and footfalls and breath, and everything else — the debt, the captain, the nine years, the nameless thing he refused to name about this woman — falls away like salt dust behind him.

Then the ground opened.

There had been no warning. No dip in the crust, no shadow to betray it. One moment the flats ran flat to the horizon, and the next the earth simply ended, a seam splitting the salt from east to west as far as the dying light could reach — a dry wash, twenty feet deep, its walls sheer and banded with old flood-lines in the clay like the rings of a felled tree. She reached the edge at full speed and did not slow, did not test it, did not hesitate. She simply dropped, boots hitting the wash floor in a puff of pale dust, rolled once with the practiced ease of someone who had done exactly this before, and was up and moving along the channel bottom before Ansel's own skid brought him to the lip.

He stood at the edge, chest heaving, and watched her go.

The wash floor ran northeast, straight as a road, and she ran down its center like a woman following a path she knew. Which was impossible, because this wash had been dry for years — the flood-lines on the walls told him that, decades of them, strata of drought — and no one could know a dry wash the way she seemed to know this one. Unless she'd walked it before. Unless she'd studied it. Unless—

Unless she wasn't running blind. Unless she was running *toward* something.

The thought arrived uninvited and stayed. Ansel crouched at the lip of the wash, watching her figure shrink into the blue dusk, and made himself think it through, because thinking was the only weapon he'd ever trusted more than his legs. Thieves fled. That was the whole architecture of thieving: take, run, vanish. Everything about her for three days had said *fleeing* — the economy, the caution, the fire built inside a dead tree. But this — dropping into a wash without checking it first, running its length in the dark, in country where a broken ankle meant death — this was not flight. This was navigation.

She was going somewhere. She had been going somewhere since Corren's Ford, and the strongroom had merely been a door she'd opened on the way.

Below him, her shape dissolved into the dusk, and the wash swallowed the last sound of her footfalls, and Ansel Ravensworth knelt alone on the edge of the empty flats with his plan in pieces and something new growing in his chest where the cold clarity had been. It wasn't admiration. He refused to let it be admiration. It was the tracker's oldest ailment, the one Elia Voss had warned him about with her dying smile: the moment when the quarry stops being a purse to recover and becomes a question to answer, and questions are the only quarry that runs forever.

He looked down into the wash. Twenty feet below, in the last violet light, something caught his eye — a glint on the channel floor, near where she'd landed. He climbed down, careless of the scrape on his palms, and found it: a button, horn, military pattern, garrison-issue. Torn loose when she'd rolled. Beside it, pressed sharp and complete into the damp clay of the wash floor, her bootprint — small, deep, decisive, pointed northeast.

And beneath the print, faint but unmistakable, a watermark in the clay itself: a thin dark line of older sediment, laid down by water that had run here once, long ago, and gone somewhere.

Northeast. Toward the escarpment. Toward the drowned country that wasn't supposed to exist.

Ansel stood in the empty wash as full dark came down, and the flats wind moved over the rim above him with a sound like a long exhalation, like the land itself sighing at what it was about to give up. He should climb out and circle wide and pick up her trail at first light, by the book, the way Elia Voss would have done it.

Instead he turned northeast, down the channel, into the dark, and started walking.

Behind him, far to the west, over the mountains he could no longer see, the sky flickered once — a dry, heatless flash, there and gone, like a signal from a country that had been underwater for a hundred years.

He told himself it was lightning. He was lying, and he knew it, and he walked on anyway.
