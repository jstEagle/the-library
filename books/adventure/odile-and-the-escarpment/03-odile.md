## 3. Odile

The chest was smaller than the story.

That was the first thing Odile thought, crouched at the rim of the wash with salt dust drying on her lips and a ranger's shadow lengthening behind her — *the chest is too small to hold a nation's grief.* Her grandmother had made the drowned fleet sound like a mountain range, masts like forest pines, holds deep enough to swallow cathedrals. But grief was never in the wood. Grief was in what you wrote on it, and stamped into the lid beneath the crusted silt was a seal she had known since before she could read: a crescent cradling a star, worn half-blind by water.

Vessia.

Her hands were shaking. She hated that. Three weeks of running from this man and her hands had been steady as rigging line the whole time, and now, kneeling over a box of river-spoiled cedar, they trembled like a girl's.

"Step away from it."

Ansel Ravensworth. Of course he'd say it that way — flat, procedural, as if the world were a form to be filled out in triplicate. She didn't turn. She watched his reflection instead, warped in the wet brass of the seal, moving closer with that careful ranger's walk, weight on the outside of the foot, the way men walked when they'd learned to cross ice or suspects' floors.

"It's mine," she said.

"Nothing in this wash is yours."

"You don't know what it is."

"No," he admitted, and that was the thing about Ravensworth that had kept him interesting for three weeks when any other pursuer would have become tedious by the second day — he admitted things. "But I know what it isn't. It isn't salvage with a permit. It isn't property with a chain of custody. It came up out of crown-claimed water not six hours ago, which makes it crown property, which makes you—" he paused, recalculating, "—standing next to it."

"Standing next to it," she repeated. "Generous."

"I'm feeling generous. The flood missed me by a hand's width last night."

She finally turned. He looked terrible, which pleased her more than it should have — salt-crusted, mud to the knees, three weeks of her own evaporation written across his face in sleeplessness. But his eyes were clear, and they were doing something she distrusted profoundly: looking at the chest the way she was looking at the chest.

Not like evidence. Like an answer.

"The bugle," she said. "Your friends."

"Fellow rangers. Patrol rotation." He said it too smoothly. She filed that away — *he hasn't reported this yet* — and let it sit in her pocket like a coin she might spend later.

"They'll want the chest."

"They'll want a report. The chest goes to the garrison, the garrison sends it to the capital assizes—"

"—where it sits in a cellar for eleven years until some clerk sells it for pipe money." She stood, slowly, keeping her hands visible because she knew how he read hands. "I've seen how the crown keeps its promises, ranger. My family has been kept promises by the crown for two generations. We keep them the way the river keeps its dead."

Something moved through his face at that — not guilt, not yet, but the recognition of a word he'd have to look up later. Good. Let him look it up. Let him lie awake doing it.

"Who are you?" he asked. Not *what are you*, not *thief*. Who. And she understood, with a small cold shock, that after three weeks of chasing her across the flats he genuinely did not have her name in a dossier. He'd been chasing a description. A silhouette. A nothing.

Which meant no one had told him who she was. Which meant either her family's enemies were lazy, or her family's name meant nothing out here, past the escarpment, past the old drowned country, where people had forgotten there had ever been anything to drown.

"Odile," she said.

He waited. Rangers always waited; silence was their favorite tool, cheaper than a rope.

"Just Odile?"

"For now." She crouched again, touched the seal. The metal was cold despite the morning heat, river-cold, deep-water cold, and the touch of it went up her arm like a wire. "My grandmother sang me a song about that mark before I could walk. *The crescent holds the star, the star holds the name, the name holds the debt.* Children's nonsense, I thought. A lullaby from a country that never existed." She looked up at him. "Except it existed. Didn't it. Because here it is, ranger, fished out of your crown's precious water with its little blind moon still on the lid."

"Vessia is a legend."

"Vessia is a *line item*," she said, and heard her own voice crack on it, and hated that too. "Somewhere in your capital there is a ledger with this seal in it, and a date, and a sum paid or owed or written off, and my grandmother spent forty years singing about it while we ate boiled salt-grass, and if you tell me one more time it's a legend I will—"

She stopped. Breathed. The wash smelled of wet mineral and old rot and, faintly, impossibly, of tar — ship's tar, centuries old, leaking up from whatever else the river had turned loose in the night.

Ansel hadn't reached for his irons. That was notable. She'd seen him reach for them twice in three weeks, quick as a blink, and both times she'd already been gone. Now his hand hung at his belt doing nothing, and his eyes stayed on the seal, and she watched him do arithmetic she couldn't see.

"What did your grandmother sing about it," he said finally. Not a question. A door, held open.

And because she was tired, and because her hands were still shaking, and because the chest was real under her fingers in a way that made every careful plan she'd built since dawn feel like paper in rain — she told him.

* * *

She told him about the fleet. Not the legend-fleet, the gold-and-glory fleet the balladeers sold, but the other one, the one her grandmother had inherited from her own mother, passed down in verses the way other families passed down land: nine ships that sailed out of Vessia's harbor the night before the water came, loaded with everything the city could carry — records, ledgers, the archive seals, the names. Nine ships that were supposed to come back for the rest.

Nine ships that the crown's charts recorded as lost to weather.

"My grandmother said the weather had help," Odile said. "She said the sluice-gates upstream were opened on purpose. She said it quietly, the way you say things that would get you taken away if you said them loudly. She sang it instead. You can put a lot in a song that you can't put in a petition." She tapped the chest. "And now the river gives up a chest with that seal, in the path of the drowned fleet's route, and inside it — go on, open it, you're required to inventory things, aren't you? Open it and count."

He knelt. She let him. The lock had surrendered to the flood long ago; the lid came up with a suck of trapped water and a smell like a drowned library — ink and rot and something sweeter, lacquer maybe, or embalming spice.

Inside: oilcloth, folded in layers, and within the oilcloth a packet gone soft as bread. Ansel lifted it with the exaggerated care of a man handling a wounded bird. Even waterlogged, even half-dissolved, the format was unmistakable to anyone who'd ever stood in a customs house: columns, stamps, countersigns. A manifest.

Half a manifest. Torn clean down the fold, the left half of the page surviving, the right half gone to pulp or to someone else's pocket years ago.

"Cargo list," Ansel murmured. "Destination codes. This is a salvage intake record, these are hold assignments—" His finger stopped. "These aren't standard marks."

"No," Odile said softly. "They're verse."

He looked at her.

"The column headers. Read them phonetically, not as customs shorthand. *Crescent-holds-star, star-holds-name.*" She watched him mouth it, watched the ranger's trained memory catch and hold the rhythm. "It's the song, ranger. The whole manifest is written in my grandmother's song. Whoever packed this chest wanted it readable only by people who carried the verses. Vessians. Survivors. Us." Her throat tightened. "It's a letter. The drowned fleet mailed a letter forward in time, and it just got delivered."

The wind moved along the wash, carrying the smell of tar and open water. Somewhere downstream, the bugle sounded again — closer, and wrong somehow, too eager, and she saw Ansel hear it the same way she did, his head tilting a degree, his eyes going flat.

"That's not patrol rotation," she said.

"No," he said. "Patrol doesn't sound like that."

*Salvage fleet,* she thought, and the word landed in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Her grandmother had sung about those too — the crown's dredgers, the polite official term for vultures, the ships that came after floods to pick the bones of drowned towns and call it recovery. If the fleet had launched, then someone in the capital already knew what the river had uncovered. Someone had been waiting.

Or someone had opened the gates again.

She didn't say that part aloud. Some coins you keep.

"Here's my offer," she said, standing, brushing off her knees, putting herself back together piece by piece the way she'd done in a hundred rooms with a hundred locks. "Truce. The chest stays with me. You get your thief — me, delivered, unresisting, a feather in your commission cap. Everyone's honest. Everyone's paid."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you carry the chest yourself, alone, through the Cut, where the Marrow Boys collect tolls in fingers, and the fleet is coming downriver, and every bandit between here and the escarpment can smell government property like sharks smell blood. You'll be the most popular man in the badlands, ranger. Every knife in the territory will want to meet you." She smiled, and knew it wasn't a kind smile. "Whereas me — I'm nobody. No flag, no seal, no scent. I can move this chest through country that would eat you alive. That's not a boast. That's a résumé."

"You're asking me to hand crown property to a confessed thief."

"I'm asking you to be arithmetic instead of procedure, just once. One chest. Two sets of hands. Your law gets its prisoner either way — but only one of us gets it there breathing."

He was quiet a long time. Long enough for the light to change, the sun clearing the rim of the wash and laying a bright bar across the open chest, across the torn manifest, across the blind crescent moon. She watched him look at it. She watched him want it — not the arrest, the *answer*. She'd spent her life reading wants; it was the whole trade. And this man wanted the truth of the thing in front of him with a hunger that embarrassed him visibly, and that, more than any argument she could make, told her she'd already won.

"If you run," he said at last, "I will chase you. Truce or no truce. To the escarpment, past it, into the sea if you make for the sea."

"I'd expect nothing less. It's the only thing you're good at."

"And if you betray me—"

"When I betray you," she corrected pleasantly, "you'll know, because I'll have the chest and you'll have a headache. Until then, ranger, we're partners. Shake on it or don't, but decide fast, because that bugle is getting closer and I don't think it's coming to sing."

He looked at her hand. She kept it steady this time. Whatever else the day had done, it had at least stopped her shaking — the chest had given her back her hands, filled them with purpose the way a hilt fills a grip.

Ansel Ravensworth took her hand. His was rope-hard and river-cold, and he shook once, formally, like a man signing something.

"One condition," he said. "You tell me the rest of the song. All of it. As we go."

Odile grinned, shouldered the chest — lighter than grief, heavier than gold — and turned toward the escarpment.

"Oh, ranger," she said. "By the end of it, you're going to wish you'd arrested me."

Behind them, downstream, the bugle sounded a third time — and this time it was answered, far off across the flats, by the low moan of a ship's horn.
