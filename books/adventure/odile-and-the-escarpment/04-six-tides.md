## 4. Six Tides

The trader's cart came out of the west at midmorning, when the salt glare had already begun to peel the horizon into ribbons. Ansel heard it long before he saw it — the creak of axle-grease gone dry, the complaint of a mule that had learned to hate flat country — and he had his hand on his rifle before reason caught up with instinct. Out here, anything that moved was either lost or lying.

The cart was neither. It was a salt-hauler, its bed stacked with pale bricks of evaporite wrapped in oiled canvas, and its driver was a broad woman with a sun-cracked face and the patient eyes of someone who had crossed these flats more times than she could count. She reined in a dozen paces off, unhurried, and looked at Ansel the way traders look at rangers: as a fixed feature of the landscape, like a rock or a dead tree, notable only for what it might cost her.

"Morning, warden," she said. "Water?"

"I have water."

"You have half a skin and two people." She tilted her chin toward Odile, who stood a careful distance behind him with the chest strapped across her back like a coffin she'd decided to carry herself. "That's arithmetic too."

Ansel didn't answer immediately. He'd spent the night learning not to trust his own arithmetic. The flood had given them the chest; the chest had given them the manifest; the manifest had given them a destination — a wreck beneath the escarpment, forty leagues downstream by river, farther by land — and no instructions on how to get there ahead of everyone else who would want it once word spread. Which it would. Word always spread. That was the one law out here that never failed.

The trader watched him think. She had the air of a woman who found thinking mildly entertaining but ultimately beside the point.

"Name's Hesta Vole," she said. "I run salt to Ferrow's Landing and bring back nails, lies, and news, in roughly that order of quality. You look like a man who could use all three."

"We'll take the water," Ansel said.

"Course you will. It's the cheapest thing I've got." She climbed down from the bench, unhitched a jug from under the seat, and poured two measures into their skins without being asked twice. Then she settled against her wheel, produced a pipe, and lit it with the serenity of someone who intended to be paid in conversation whether or not anyone had agreed to the price. "Now. The lies are free, so let's start there. You're chasing her, aren't you? The thief."

"She's not running anymore."

"Mm." Smoke curled out of her nose. "They say that."

Odile shifted her weight but said nothing. Ansel had learned in two days of uneasy company that silence was her native currency and she spent it shrewdly. She stood with her back to the cart, watching the flats to the east — watching, he suspected, for exactly the kind of dust-plume that meant riders, though she'd never once admitted to being watched-for.

"The news, then," Hesta said, tapping ash onto the salt crust. "Since you're paying attention. The crown's salvage fleet launched from Corvane's Anchorage four days ago. Six hulls, steam-assisted, flying the admiralty pennant. They're working downriver toward the escarpment reach."

Ansel felt the words land in his chest like stones dropped into a well. Four days. The fleet had been moving since before the flood had ever uncovered the chest — which meant they hadn't launched because of the manifest. They'd been coming anyway. The crown had known something about this stretch of river for longer than he had, and now he understood why the wreck had been left unclaimed for so long: it hadn't been forgotten. It had been *scheduled*.

"Four days," he repeated.

"Give or take a tide. Steam boats make good time while the river holds." Hesta drew on her pipe, and her eyes, mild as milk, went briefly to Odile and back. "Which brings me to the third item. Nails, I said, but really it's weather. The dry season's early this year. The gauge-station at Redmouth posted low readings a fortnight ago, and every hauler on this road is talking about it. By my count — and I count tides the way other folk count coins — you've got six left before the river drops below diving depth along the escarpment shelf. After that, whatever's down there stays down there until next spring, if the spring floods are kind. And they haven't been kind in nine years."

"Six tides," Ansel said.

"Six. Twelve days, near enough, if you're counting by the sun instead of the moon." She shrugged. "A fleet makes the reach in eight, maybe ten, if the wind serves and the channel hasn't silted. You, walking—" she gestured at the flats, the heat-shimmer, the two of them standing in all that emptiness like punctuation marks, "—you don't make it at all. Not around the river bend. Not unless you cut straight across."

"Across the escarpment," Ansel said slowly.

"Now you're doing sums." Hesta smiled without much humor. "There's an old goat-path over the top. Washed out in three places, according to the last fool who tried it, and the last fool who tried it was me, eleven years ago, and I lost a mule. But it cuts the distance by half, maybe more. Two days' hard walking instead of five. If your legs hold and the cliff doesn't eat you."

Ansel turned the numbers over. He was good at numbers; a ranger had to be, or he'd die of optimism. Forty leagues by river, minus the fleet's head start, plus six tides of falling water. Against that: twenty-odd miles of broken cliff country, no water worth trusting, a path that had eaten a mule, and a companion who was, by every legal definition he possessed, a fugitive he ought to be marching to the nearest garrison instead of planning expeditions with.

The arithmetic was ugly. The alternative was uglier. If the fleet reached the wreck first, the manifest — whatever it truly was, whatever truth sat rotting in that waterlogged paper — would go into an admiral's strongbox and come out as nothing at all. He thought about the seal on the chest, the drowned nation stamped in faded wax, and the strange cold feeling he'd had at dawn when he'd lifted the lid: that he was holding evidence of something the world had agreed to forget on purpose.

He didn't believe in omens. He believed in tides, distances, and the reliability of men's greed. All three were pointing the same direction.

"How much for the water?" he asked.

"For you? Nothing." Hesta knocked out her pipe against the wheel hub. "For the lady, though—" and here her voice did something subtle, a shift in register that Ansel caught but couldn't quite name, "—the price is a question answered honestly. One question. Take it or leave it."

Odile's eyes came around from the eastern flats. For a moment the two women regarded each other across the salt, and Ansel had the distinct sensation of watching a transaction occur in a currency he couldn't read.

"Ask," Odile said.

"Is it true?" Hesta said simply. "What's in the chest. Is it true?"

Odile was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice was level, but Ansel heard the thing underneath it, the same thing he'd heard when she'd first looked at the chest and forgotten, briefly, to be a thief.

"It's true enough that the crown sent six ships to make sure nobody reads it."

Hesta nodded slowly, as if she'd expected nothing less and been paid exactly what she'd asked for. She climbed back onto the bench, gathered her reins, and paused there with the mule stamping impatiently beneath her.

"Ferrow's Landing is a day behind you," she said. "Fleet scouts ride ahead of the hulls. They'll ask questions at every crossing, and they pay better answers than I do. Whatever you two are carrying—" she flicked the reins, and the cart began to roll, "—carry it quietly."

Then she was gone, westward, leaving a wake of dust and pipe-smoke and the distinct impression that Ansel had just been weighed, measured, and found interesting.

* * *

They walked until the light went long and copper, and made camp in the lee of a salt ridge that gave them shelter from the wind and nothing else. No wood for a fire — there was never wood out here — so they ate hard bread and dried fish and drank Hesta's water in careful sips, and the dark came down around them like a lid.

Ansel sat with the manifest across his knees, wrapped in oilcloth, and did not open it. He had opened it eleven times since dawn yesterday. He knew its contents by heart now, or thought he did: columns of cargo, tonnage, salvage rights, a wreck designated by coordinates he could recite in his sleep. What he didn't know was why a list of drowned goods should feel like a loaded weapon. Paper was paper. Unless someone wanted it to stay unread, and then paper became the most dangerous substance in the world.

Odile sat a few feet away, her back against the salt ridge, her knees drawn up. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the stars, or through them, and her silence had a different texture tonight — less like a wall, more like a held breath.

"Six tides," she said finally.

"You heard."

"I hear everything. It's how I've stayed alive." She turned her head, and starlight caught the edge of her jaw. "Two days over the escarpment, if the path exists and our legs hold. That leaves us four to find the wreck and do whatever we're going to do before the fleet arrives. Do you actually believe we can do that?"

"No," Ansel said honestly.

Something that might have been a laugh escaped her, brief and dry. "Good. I distrust plans that require belief."

"And yet you're still here."

She was quiet again. The wind moved across the flats with a sound like sand poured over glass, and somewhere far off a night bird made its one note and stopped.

"Because the chest found *us*," she said at last. "Not me. Us. The river put it between us, ranger. I've stolen a great many things, and I can tell you the difference between something that's mine and something that isn't. That manifest isn't mine. It isn't yours either. It belongs to dead people who trusted the living to finish reading it." She pulled her cloak tighter. "My grandmother used to sing about the drowned fleet. I thought they were songs. Lullabies, nonsense verses, the things old women keep after everything else is taken. Now I'm carrying one in a box on my back, and I find I can't put it down. Can you?"

Ansel looked at his hands. They were ranger's hands — rope-scarred, sunburnt, steady — and they had held a commission for nine years that kept him fed and armored and alive, and that commission said, in language clearer than any manifest, that he should bind this woman's wrists and walk her to the garrison at Ferrow's Landing and hand her over with a receipt.

Instead he was sitting in the dark doing sums about cliff paths.

"There's a version of tomorrow where I do my duty," he said. "I take you in. The chest goes to the magistrate. The fleet reaches the wreck. The manifest goes into a strongbox. In twenty years, some clerk loses it in a records fire, and nobody ever knows what the crown sent six ships to bury."

"That version has excellent paperwork," Odile observed.

"It does. Everything correct. Everyone safe. Nothing true." He turned the oilcloth bundle over once. "And there's a version where we cross the escarpment, and I commit about six crimes before noon on the first day, and I don't know how it ends."

"Do you want to know how it ends?"

"No one knows how it ends. That's what makes it a choice instead of a schedule."

Odile studied him for a long moment, and he had the uncomfortable sense of being read more thoroughly than any document he'd ever filed. Then she nodded, once, the way people nod when a bargain closes — not with satisfaction, but with the grim relief of two parties who have discovered the terms were never negotiable.

"Then we cut the escarpment," she said. "But understand me, ranger. I'm not doing this for you, and I'm not doing it for the crown, and I'm certainly not doing it for your conscience. I'm doing it because my grandmother sang those songs, and someone owes her family an answer, and I intend to collect."

"Noted," Ansel said. "For the record, I'm not doing it for you either. I'm doing it because I took an oath to the law, and somewhere in that oath there was a line about justice, and I've just realized I've never actually read that part carefully."

"Liar," she said, without heat. "You read every line carefully. It's why you're sitting out here miserable instead of asleep."

He almost smiled. It was the closest he'd come in days.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old brass, and with it came the dust-plume.

Ansel saw it first — a smudge on the southern horizon, moving, resolving by degrees into riders. Three of them, maybe four, cutting across the flats on a line that would intersect the salt road well ahead of Hesta's cart. Fleet scouts moved like that: fast, spread out, sweeping ground the way a net sweeps water. His stomach tightened with a dread that had nothing to do with fear of arrest and everything to do with timing. Six tides. The count had already started, and the first tide was being spent right now, standing in the open arguing with himself.

"Riders," Odile said. She'd seen them too; of course she had. She was already moving, low and quick, shrugging the chest higher on her back. "East. There's a wash, half a mile. We can be in it before they crest the ridge."

"They'll pick up our tracks."

"They'll pick up *a* set of tracks. Two people walking south when they expect a thief and a warden walking east is a puzzle, not an answer. Come on."

He hesitated one last second — one second in which the old life called to him, clean and simple, duty like a straight road — and then he turned and followed her, and the straight road bent, and did not straighten again.

They reached the wash as the riders topped the distant rise, and dropped into its sandy throat, and began to move fast and quiet through terrain that hid them and slowed them in equal measure. Above the lip of the wash, the sky was enormous and indifferent. Somewhere beyond it, a fleet was sailing toward a wreck, and a river was falling, and the water was going down, tide by tide, like sand through a narrowed neck.

Five tides left, Ansel thought. Maybe five and a half, if the gauge-readings lied kindly.

He put his head down and walked faster.
