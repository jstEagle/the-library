## 5. The Toll Bridge

The bridge announced itself a full hour before we reached it, the way trouble always does.

First came the crows — a black scatter of them lifting off the flats in ragged spirals, drawn to whatever scraps of grain and spilled cargo the toll-keepers' mules left behind. Then the smell: tar and wet rope and woodsmoke, the particular perfume of a place where people had built something permanent in the middle of nothing. And finally, rising out of the heat-shimmer like the spine of a drowned animal, the bridge itself — old Vessian stonework, three arches wide, straddling a river that had shrunk to half its proper width. The pilings were carved with faces, worn nearly smooth by centuries of current, but I could still make out their mouths, open as if mid-song.

My grandmother would have known every one of those faces by name. She'd have sung them their verses while she braided my hair, and I'd have squirmed and complained and memorized them anyway, the way children memorize things they don't know they'll need.

"Two guards on the near side," Ansel said beside me. He'd gone still in that ranger's way of his, all his weight settled into his heels, his eyes doing the work his body refused to. "Four more at the tollhouse. That's a garrison detail, not local hires. See the cut of their coats?"

I saw it. Slate-blue wool, salt-white trim at the cuffs. The crown's salvage fleet wore its colors even on land, apparently, like a man who can't stop wearing his wedding ring after the marriage has rotted out from under him.

"They're fleet," I said.

"They're fleet." His jaw worked. "A week ago I'd have said that was good news. Fleet means supply lines, means order, means the law is close enough to touch."

"And now?"

"Now it means Corvane is ahead of schedule." He looked at me then, and there was something new in his face — not the flat hunter's stare he'd worn across the flats, chasing me through the washes like a debt come due. Something more tired. More human. "We could go around. Cross upstream where the river narrows. It'll cost us most of a tide."

"It'll cost us more than that." I'd been counting too, ever since the trader with the broken cartwheel told us about the fleet's launch. "The narrows are two days' detour, and the mudflats past them are already going soft. We cross here, or we don't cross at all."

"You want to walk up to a fleet checkpoint carrying stolen property stamped with the seal of a nation the crown spent three generations pretending never existed."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"How else would you like me to say it?"

I smiled at him. I couldn't help it. There was something about Ansel Ravensworth when he was being grimly reasonable that made me want to be unreasonable just to watch the muscle jump in his cheek. "You say it however you like, ranger. But you're not carrying it. I am."

The chest rode against my hip in a sling I'd made from my spare shirt, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as sin. Half a salvage manifest, pointing to a wreck beneath the escarpment, pointing to proof of what the crown had done to Vessia — to what my grandmother had sung about in her last years, when her mind wandered the flooded streets of a city I'd never seen. The other half lay somewhere under the water, waiting for us, if the river let us reach it before the dry season dropped it too low to dive.

Five tides. Maybe less.

"I have an idea," I said.

Ansel closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does before stepping off a cliff. "Of course you do."

* * *

The trick to a forgery is not the hand. Anyone with patience can learn to loop a capital letter or fake a wax seal — I'd learned both by twelve, pressing candle stubs into carved buttons because my father needed shipping papers and we had no money for honest ones. No. The trick to a forgery is the man carrying it. Papers don't convince anyone. People convince people, and people are lazy, and frightened of being wrong, and desperate above all things to believe that the world makes sense.

So I didn't dress like a smuggler. I dressed like a clerk.

I'd bought the gray coat in the last river town with coins lifted from a slaver's purse — a purse I felt no guilt whatsoever about lightening — and I'd spent the walking hours doing what I do best, which is watching. By the time we reached the bridge I knew how fleet clerks stood (spine stiff, chin down, burdened), how they walked (fast, apologetic, important), and how they spoke to soldiers (never meeting their eyes, always invoking a superior). I'd torn a strip from the manifest's oilcloth wrapping and inked it with soot and tallow into something that, at ten feet, in bad light, read as an official writ of survey. I'd signed it with a name I invented on the spot — Magister Ellery Voss, Office of Salvage Records — and given Magister Voss the single most powerful quality any fictional person can possess: a reputation for being unpleasant.

Ansel watched me work with the expression of a man watching a snake charm itself.

"This is a hanging offense," he said quietly.

"So is treason, if the manifest says what I think it says. We're already on the gallows, ranger. We're just arguing about which knot."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

He shouldered our packs and became what he'd been becoming for two days now: not my captor, not quite my partner, but something in between that had no honest name yet. A shadow. A witness. A man who kept choosing, over and over, not to stop me.

We joined the line of traffic shuffling toward the tollhouse — drovers, pilgrims, a family of tanners with their cart stacked high enough to shade a small village. Nobody looked twice at a gray-coated clerk and her tall, silent escort. That was the beauty of bureaucracy: it made everyone invisible, because everyone was bored.

The guard at the rope barrier was young. That was the first thing I noticed, and the second thing was that he was scared, and the third thing — the thing that mattered — was that he was checking cargo. Not randomly. Not thoroughly. He had a list, and he was looking for something specific, and every time he finished with a traveler who didn't have it, relief flickered across his face like sun off water.

They were hunting the seal. They were hunting *us*.

"Next."

I stepped forward, spine stiff, chin down, burdened. Behind me I felt Ansel go very still.

"Purpose of crossing?" the guard asked. He was already reaching for the chest-sling at my hip, and I let him take it, because a clerk with something to hide snatches; a clerk with nothing to hide is annoyed.

"Survey materials," I said, in the flat, put-upon voice of a woman who has answered this question forty times today. "Office of Salvage Records. Magister Voss's party crossed yesterday; I'm the rear courier with the document case. You can check the log if you like — though I'll note for the record that Magister Voss becomes *extremely* difficult when his couriers are delayed."

The guard hesitated. There it was — the flinch at the name, the little recalculation of consequence. He turned the chest over in his hands, and I watched his thumb slide across the oilcloth, and I felt the whole morning balance on that thumb like a coin.

Then he frowned. Through a tear in the wrapping — a tear I hadn't made, a tear the flash flood had made, gods rot the luckless river — the corner of the lid showed through. Dark wood. Old iron banding. And stamped into the iron, worn but unmistakable:

The seal of Vessia. The wave over the tower.

The guard went white. Not confused — *white*, the color of a man seeing a ghost, and I understood in one cold drop of clarity that this boy had grown up on stories too. Everyone had. The drowned nation wasn't legend to the poor; it was memory, passed down in kitchens and work songs, in grandmothers' voices, in names that survived the flooding like seeds survive a fire.

"That's—" he started.

"Vessian salvage," I said smoothly, before the fear could finish forming into words. "Recovered material, catalogued under writ. Which is precisely why it's sealed in oilcloth, and why Magister Voss will want to know exactly whose hands touched it." I held out my palm for the forged writ, and he gave it back to me like it burned. "You're new to this post, aren't you?"

"I— yes, but—"

"Then here's advice worth more than your toll." I leaned in, lowered my voice, and let him see the weary kindness of one small person helping another navigate a machine designed to crush them both. "When the fleet officers ask what came through your checkpoint, you tell them documents and survey gear. Because if you tell them you found a Vessian artifact and lost track of it, the questions they ask *you* will make Magister Voss look like a doting uncle. Do you understand me?"

His throat moved. His eyes darted to the slate-blue coats at the tollhouse, to the officer leaning in the doorway watching the line with the flat patience of a cat at a mousehole.

"Yes," he whispered.

"Wonderful." I tucked the writ away, resettled the chest, and walked across the bridge of my drowned ancestors without breaking stride.

Ansel caught up to me on the far bank, and for the first hundred paces he said nothing at all, which from him was a shout.

Finally: "You lied to a soldier of the crown. In front of witnesses. With paperwork you made out of soot and shirt-lining."

"Yes."

"He believed you."

"They usually do."

"*They?*" He stopped walking. I stopped too, and turned, and found him looking at me the way men look at deep water — wanting to swim, afraid of the bottom. "How many times, Odile? How many checkpoints, how many seals, how many names?"

I shrugged. "Enough to know that fear travels downhill faster than truth."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of a fallen milestone, a day's walk beyond the bridge, far enough that the fleet's lanterns were only a rumor behind us. Ansel built a small fire because the nights were turning cold, and we ate hard bread and dried fish, and neither of us spoke for a long time.

It was he who broke first. Of course it was. Ansel Ravensworth carried silence the way other men carried grudges — carefully, and only until it grew too heavy.

"What else have you stolen?" he asked.

The question came out quieter than I expected. Not an accusation. Almost gentle. That was worse.

"Why do you want to know?"

"Because I've been trying to fit you into a shape since the flats." He poked the fire, and sparks climbed, and his face did that thing it did when he was thinking — the slight furrow, like a man reading a page written too small. "Thief doesn't fit. Thieves run from rangers. You stood in a flooded wash and stared at a dead nation's chest like it was calling your name. Smuggler doesn't fit either. Smugglers wouldn't have talked that boy down at the bridge — they'd have run him through and saved themselves the risk. So what are you? What else is in the ledger?"

I pulled my knees up and watched the fire.

What else. My father's forged shipping papers, pressed from candle stubs. The tax collector's seal I'd copied at fourteen so the landlord wouldn't turn us out in winter. The passage papers — *the passage papers* — the reason for all of it, the debt I'd taken on when I stole from his garrison, the papers that would move my family out of the crown's reach to some coast where our name didn't mark us. The years of small thefts and large ones, each one a brick in a wall between my people and the drowning.

"A lot of things," I said finally. "Names. Writs. Seals. A bishop's ring, once, but I gave it back, because it turned out to be blessed and it blistered my skin for a week, which I choose to interpret as theological commentary."

That earned me the ghost of a smile. It faded fast.

"The passage papers," he said. "For your family. That's why you hit my garrison."

"You already know that."

"I know what you told me. I'm asking what I believe." He set down the bread he wasn't eating. "Here's what I keep coming back to, Odile. Everything you steal, you steal *for*. Never from. For your family, for your people, for a story your grandmother told you. I've spent eleven years learning to see the difference between hunger and greed, and you're the hungriest person I've ever met, and you've never once taken anything for yourself."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightbird called across the flats, three notes falling like water.

"That's not true," I said.

"Name one thing."

"You." 

The word was out before I could weigh it, and I watched it land, and I watched him go still in a way that had nothing to do with ranger training. The firelight moved on his face. He didn't laugh it off. He didn't look away either, and that was the dangerous part — that we sat there in the small warm circle of the fire and let the word stand between us like a third traveler.

"That's not theft," he said at last, roughly.

"Isn't it? I took you off your road. I took you across a bridge on a lie. I'm taking you toward a wreck that might get us both killed, for a cause that was never yours." I hugged my knees tighter. "Where I come from, ranger, that's called taking everything."

He was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gravel in it.

"My grandfather signed the evacuation order for the Vessian coast."

I stared at him. "What?"

"I found the record two years ago, in the garrison archive. Ravensworth, J., adjutant's signature, witnessed and filed. He was a clerk. He didn't open the sluice gates — he just wrote down the orders of the men who did, neat as scripture, and went home to his dinner." Ansel's jaw tightened. "I've been telling myself since the wash that I'm helping you because the manifest is evidence, because duty points where duty points. But I think maybe I've been walking off a debt I didn't know I owed. To you. To all of you."

The nightbird called again. Closer now.

And that was when I heard it — beneath the birdcall, beneath the wind off the flats — the thing that made every hair on my arms stand up. Hoofbeats. More than one. Coming up the trade road behind us at a canter, unhurried, confident, the rhythm of riders who knew exactly where they were going.

Ansel heard it a heartbeat later. He was on his feet with his hand on his knife, and the firelight showed me his face stripped of everything soft the conversation had built.

"Fleet outriders," he breathed. "They're not searching the road. They're riding straight for us."

The hoofbeats grew louder. I pulled the chest closer, and the fire spat, and out of the dark came a lantern swinging — and a voice, hail and clear, calling a name I knew too well.

"Ravensworth! Ansel Ravensworth! In the name of the Ranger Corps, hold where you stand!"
