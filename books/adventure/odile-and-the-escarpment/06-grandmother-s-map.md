## 6. Grandmother's Map

The waystation squatted at the edge of the salt like a thing that had given up. Its walls were mudbrick gone the color of old bone, its roof half-collapsed into a lattice of sun-bleached beams, and its door hung from one hinge, swinging in a wind that had nothing left to carry but grit and the smell of brine. Someone had painted a faded blue stripe around the lintel once — Odile could just make out it under the dust — the mark of a river post that used to collect tolls from barges that no longer came.

She pushed through the door first, because she always went first now. It was one of the small strangenesses of their arrangement: Ansel Ravensworth, ranger, lawman, man who had chased her across forty miles of flat white nothing, let her lead. She'd noticed it on the second day out from the wash where they'd struck their bargain. He watched her hands more than her face. He counted her steps. And when danger showed itself — a rider on the ridge, a smoke plume to the south — he stepped back and waited for her to say what it was.

It should have felt like surveillance. Instead it felt like something closer to respect, though neither of them would have called it that aloud. A truce was a fragile thing between a thief and a ranger, and both of them knew it would hold only as long as it was useful. But usefulness, Odile had learned from her grandmother, was not the same as trust. Trust was what you built when you stopped counting.

Inside, the waystation was one long room with a hearth at the far end and a stair against the east wall leading up to a loft that had mostly fallen into the room below. There were tables still standing, their legs swollen and split from some long-ago flood that had come this far inland. Scratched into one tabletop, someone had carved a game board — a grid of squares, little pits worn smooth by pebbles moved across them by hands that were dust now. Odile ran her thumb along the grooves and thought about how many nights someone must have sat here waiting for the river to rise or fall, playing that game against themselves because there was no one else.

"We'll make camp here," Ansel said. He'd set the chest down inside the door and was circling the room the way he circled everything, checking sightlines, testing floorboards, doing all the quiet arithmetic of a man who expected the world to turn on him. "One entrance, one exit up through the loft if the roof holds. We can see the flats from the window gap."

"You've said 'we can see' three times since we left the bridge," Odile said. "You know I'm not going to run."

"I know you're not going to run *yet*," he said, and there was no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. Or better. She hadn't decided.

She didn't answer. She knelt by the chest instead, because there was a thing she had been avoiding doing since the toll bridge, and the waystation's silence — real silence, the deep kind, the kind the salt made after sundown when even the wind gave up — meant she couldn't avoid it anymore.

The manifest was wrapped in oilcloth inside the chest, and beneath the oilcloth it was wrapped again in waxed linen, and beneath *that* it was simply paper — heavy rag paper, the kind that cost more than most people earned in a season, water-stained along one edge but whole. Half a manifest. Torn down the middle with a jagged hand, the way you tear something when you mean for the two pieces never to meet again unless the right people bring them together.

She had looked at it before. On the first night, by firelight, she and Ansel had pored over it together and found almost nothing: columns of figures, a seal, a date rendered in a calendar neither of them fully understood, and then — this was the part that had made her heart do something strange — a block of text that wasn't figures at all. Lines of short phrases, set in rows, each row broken mid-thought, each phrase seeming to rhyme with the one above it or beside it but never quite. Ansel had called it corrupted notation. Damaged record-keeping, he'd said. Water damage, maybe, or a clerk's shorthand lost to time.

Odile hadn't said anything that night. Because she had known. She had known the moment her eyes touched those lines, the way you know your own name spoken in a dark room.

They weren't corrupted. They were *verses.*

*

Her grandmother's name had been Sefa Vane, though everyone in the camps called her Mama Sefa, including people who weren't family, including, eventually, Odile herself, who had been small enough then that the distinction between grandmother and grandmother-of-everyone seemed unimportant. Sefa Vane had been born on the water — actually on the water, in the cabin of a salvage barge, in the last year before the drowning, though she'd been too young to remember it and too old, later, to forgive herself for not remembering. What she remembered instead was everything that came after: the camps on the high ground, the ration lines, the years of being told by crown clerks that no records survived, that Vessia's ledgers had gone down with the city, that there was therefore nothing owed, no one to owe it to, no way to prove anything at all.

And every night, in the camps, in the tent that smelled of tarpaulin and river mud, Sefa Vane had sung.

They hadn't sounded like records. That was the genius of them, if you wanted to call it genius — Odile had gone back and forth on that word her whole life. They sounded like lullabies. Counting songs. The kind of nonsense-rhyme children clap along to, verses about a miller's daughter and a wheel that turned and turned, about sacks of grain numbered one through nine, about a chapel bell that rang underwater. Odile had clapped along. Every Vessian child had clapped along. The songs were everywhere in the camps, passed mouth to mouth, taught to babies who didn't know what they were carrying.

It was only much later — the night before Sefa died, in fact, when Odile was seventeen and already running cargo for her uncle and already good at it — that her grandmother sat her down and told her what the songs were.

"They're the manifest," Sefa had said. Her voice was thin by then, a thread of itself, but her eyes were still the sharpest thing in the camp. "The clerks burned the paper ledgers. Did you know that? Not lost. Burned. There was an order. My father saw the smoke." She'd gripped Odile's wrist hard enough to leave marks. "So the survivors did the only thing the clerks couldn't burn. They put the lists into verse and taught them to children. Grain counts, cargo counts, names of ships, names of families. Set to melodies simple enough that a child who forgot everything else would still remember the tune, and the tune would drag the words along behind it like a cart-horse drags its load."

"Why not tell people?" Odile had asked, seventeen and arrogant and full of questions. "Why hide it in nursery rhymes? Why not write it down, shout it, make the crown answer?"

"Because paper gets found," her grandmother said. "Shouting gets answered with soldiers. But a lullaby — a lullaby is beneath suspicion. Who arrests a woman for singing her granddaughter to sleep?" She'd smiled then, the first real smile Odile had seen from her in weeks. "We hid the truth where no one powerful would ever think to look. In the mouths of children. For sixty years, Odile. Sixty years of children singing the inventory of a drowned nation, and every clerk and magistrate and admiral in the capital heard it and thought it was nothing. Nothing. The most dangerous thing I know of is being thought nothing."

Sefa Vane had died four days later, and Odile had sung at her burial — one of the counting songs, the one about the miller's daughter, because her grandmother had asked for it and Odile had understood only afterward that she'd been asking to be buried with the evidence.

She had never decoded the songs completely. She'd told herself there was time. There was always time, until there wasn't, and then her uncle had died too, and the family had scattered, and the songs had become just songs again, things she hummed while working rigging, things that hurt to sing and so she mostly didn't.

Until now. Until a flash flood on the salt flats had coughed up a chest stamped with a seal out of legend, and inside the chest, torn in half, was a document whose central block of text was written in exactly the form her grandmother had described: short rhyming phrases, set in rows, nonsense to anyone who didn't know the tunes.

Nonsense to anyone who didn't know the tunes. Which meant it was everything to the one person in a hundred miles who did.

*

Ansel had built the fire small — he always built fires small, a habit of men who'd spent careers being tracked — and the light it threw was narrow and orange and didn't reach the corners of the waystation. Odile sat within its circle anyway, because she needed the light, and spread the manifest flat on a table whose surface had buckled into gentle waves, like water that had dried mid-motion.

He watched her from across the room. She could feel it, the particular weight of his attention, which was different from other men's attention — steadier, less hungry, more like a held breath. He didn't ask what she was doing. He'd learned, over the days of the truce, that asking interrupted her, and interrupting her cost him something he wanted. So he sat and he sharpened his knife and he waited, and the small rhythmic sound of the blade against the stone became, without either of them deciding it, the metronome of the evening.

She began with the song she knew best. The miller's daughter. *One for the wheel that turns, two for the meal it grinds, three for the sack it fills, four for the cart that carries, five for the road, six for the toll, seven for the miller's due, eight for the king's own share, nine for the daughter's keep.* Nine lines. She'd known them before she'd known how to read. She sang them softly, barely above breath, because singing them at full voice in an empty building in bandit country felt like inviting the wrong ears, and also because singing them at full voice hurt, and she wasn't ready to be hurt tonight. Not yet. Maybe not ever fully. Grief didn't finish; it just changed rooms.

The phrases on the manifest matched. Not word for word — the clerk who'd written this version had been a clerk, not a singer, and had rendered the verses in formal register, replacing the camp-slang with proper terms — but the structure was identical. Nine lines. Same cadence. Same breaks. Where the song said *the miller's due*, the manifest said *miller's tithe, assessed*. Where the song said *the king's own share*, the manifest said *crown portion, sealed*. Line for line, the nonsense resolved into sense, and the sense was: this is a cargo list. Grain. Milled and sacked and carted and tolled and taxed. This is a list of what Vessia sent out on the last barges before the gates closed.

She worked until her throat ached, cross-referencing song against page, humming a bar when she lost the thread, letting the melody pull the meaning loose the way her grandmother had promised it would. Ansel didn't move. Hours passed — she knew because the fire needed feeding twice, and he fed it both times without being asked, another small thing that was becoming a pattern of small things — and somewhere in the third hour she caught herself explaining aloud, unprompted, narrating her method to the silent man across the room, and realized she'd been talking to him for some time without noticing.

"—the fourth verse is the one that matters," she was saying, her finger tracing a line of cramped script. "Everyone assumes the manifest is just goods. Grain, timber, salt. But listen." She sang the fourth verse, quietly, the way you sing something sacred: *Four for the strongroom under the waterline, four for the door that takes two keys, four for the ledger chained inside, four for the names written fair, four for the half kept aboard, four for the half carried away, four for the day they are joined, four for the debt that wakes.*

She stopped. Looked at him. He had stopped sharpening the knife.

"It says there's a second half," she said. "Aboard the wreck. Chained in the strongroom. This—" she tapped the torn edge of the document, the ragged border where the paper gave way, "—this isn't damage. This is design. Someone tore this manifest in half on purpose and put half of it on the barge and half of it in the strongroom of the flagship, so that no single survivor, no single thief, no single crown agent could ever assemble the proof alone. You'd need both halves. One buried with the ships, one carried inland and hidden in — in songs, in children, in sixty years of lullabies."

"And the two halves have to be brought together," Ansel said slowly, and Odile watched his face change as the sentence finished forming in his mind, watched the ranger's cool assessment give way to something rawer. "By someone who knows the songs. And someone who can dive."

"Or," Odile said, "by two someones."

The fire popped. Neither of them moved.

He got up eventually, crossed the room, and stood over the table looking down at the manifest, and Odile let him, because there are moments when a person needs to see a thing with their own body bent over it, needs the closeness, needs the shadow of their own shoulders falling across the evidence. He read the deciphered lines in silence, lips moving slightly, a habit she found oddly endearing and would never have told him so. Then he straightened and asked the question she'd been waiting for him to ask, the question any honest man would ask:

"How do I know the songs are true? How do I know your grandmother didn't — I don't know. Embellish. Invent. however beloved she was."

It was a fair question. It deserved a fair answer, and Odile gave it the only way she knew how: "Because the numbers reconcile." She pointed to the columns of figures flanking the verse-block, the parts of the manifest written in plain numerals, the parts any clerk could read. "The verses aren't separate from the ledger. They're the ledger's key. Each verse-line corresponds to a column entry — the ninth verse maps to the ninth column, and the totals match. Sack counts, tonnage, dates. If her songs were invention, they wouldn't reconcile against a crown document to the unit. They reconcile. I checked four times tonight. Whoever wrote this manifest wrote it *knowing* the songs existed. This was the inland half. This was written *for* the carriers. For her. For people like her."

For me, she thought, and didn't say, and felt it land in her chest anyway like a stone dropped down a well.

*

Later — past midnight, past the point where the cold came up through the floorboards and into the bones of their ankles — Ansel slept, or pretended to, stretched by the hearth with his boots off for the first time since she'd known him, and Odile stayed awake at the table because sleep had become impossible in any ordinary way. There was too much singing in her head. Too many dead women. Sefa Vane, and behind Sefa her mother, who'd drowned in the second wave of relocations, and behind her mother a line of women stretching back to the drowning itself, back to whoever had first taken a clerk's dry inventory and folded it into a melody a toddler could hold.

She sang. Not loudly. The waystation walls were thick enough and the night was empty enough that she could have sung loudly, but grief has its own volume and hers was low. She sang the chapel-bell verse, the one about the bell that rang underwater, the one her grandmother said the divers' families had sung at funerals because it was the only funeral rite they had left — no bodies to bury, so they buried the names instead, in sound.

*Ring for the ones the water keeps,* she sang, *ring for the doors that open slow, ring for the ledger and the law, ring for the day the debt comes home.*

*The day the debt comes home.* She'd sung that line ten thousand times in her life and it had always been an abstraction, a far-off country, a thing that happened in stories. Sitting in a ruined waystation with half a manifest spread out before her and a sleeping lawman six feet away who had crossed half the salt at her heels and then, impossibly, chosen to walk beside her — sitting there, the line stopped being abstract. The debt was coming home. It was coming home in her pocket, in waxed linen, in her grandmother's stolen language, and it was coming home whether the crown liked it or not.

She thought about stealing. About what the word had meant to her, all these years — the craft of it, the pride of it, the necessity of it, the shame tangled through all three. She had stolen passage papers and purse-strings and, once, memorably, a customs officer's signet ring off his very hand while he wore it, a trick involving a handshake and a warm coin that she still thought of as her masterpiece. She had been good at taking things that belonged to other people. And now, for the first time, she held a thing that belonged to *her* — not to her alone, not to her family alone, but to every singing child in every camp, to every diver's widow, to the drowned and the scattered both — and the strange, disorienting truth of it was that holding it felt nothing like theft. Theft was quick. This was heavy. Theft was flight. This was a claim.

Her inheritance had arrived in a flooded chest on a salt flat, delivered by a river that everyone said was dead, and it had taken a lawman's stubbornness to haul it out of the water, and she was still working out what to feel about that.

Near dawn, Ansel woke without seeming to wake — one moment asleep, the next sitting up, scanning the doorway out of reflex before allowing himself consciousness — and the first thing he said, before water, before the route, before anything practical, was: "Sing it again. The fourth verse. Slower."

So she did. And this time she sang it at full voice, unhidden, unhurried, letting the ruined room carry it, letting the dead hear it, and when the last line came — *four for the day they are joined, four for the debt that wakes* — Ansel Ravensworth, who did not believe in legends and had said so, who believed in sightlines and supply weights and the reliable dishonesty of rivers, sat still and listened as though the verse were addressed to him personally.

Maybe, Odile thought, it was.

Because the verse named what they had to do next, and it was worse than anything they'd planned. The strongroom was aboard the wreck — the *flagship*, the verses said, the *chained* ledger, the *two-keyed door*. Which meant the second half of the proof wasn't hidden in some camp or vault they could raid. It was at the bottom of the escarpment channel, guarded by water and silt and whatever the crown had left aboard when the ships went down. And the fleet was already sailing. And the tides were falling. And somewhere south of them, right now, Admiral Corvane's scouts were reading charts and finding routes, and none of them knew the songs, and that — that was the only advantage left. That was the whole war, reduced to a single asymmetry: they had the key and the enemy did not.

Ansel stood, stretched, and began repacking the chest with the efficiency of a man who had already decided. When he reached the manifest he paused, held it up to the gray light coming through the window gap, and studied the torn edge one more time.

"The other half," he said. "Chained in a strongroom under the waterline. Behind a door that takes two keys."

"That's what it says."

"Do you believe it?"

Odile looked at him — at the ranger who had spent six days refusing to believe anything he couldn't weigh or count, and who had nonetheless just spent a night listening to a dead woman's lullabies as if they were orders — and she smiled, and it surprised her, the smile, surfacing through the grief like a fish through dark water.

"I believe my grandmother," she said. "Everything else, we go and see."

He slung the chest. She doused the fire. And outside, the salt lay flat and pale to the horizon, and far to the southeast, faint as a scratch on glass, there was smoke — one thin column of it, rising off the river line, exactly where a fleet would anchor.

They had six tides. Now they had five.
