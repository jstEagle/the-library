## 7. Rangers on the Road

They came out of the north at midmorning of the third day, and Ansel saw them before Odile did — two riders where there should have been none, kicking up a haze of salt dust against the pale sky.

He had been teaching himself not to look over his shoulder. Three days of walking the old drover track along the escarpment's spine, three days of Odile humming her grandmother's verses under her breath while she turned the manifest over in her hands like a woman worrying a knot loose, and he had almost convinced himself that the world behind them had gone quiet. The smoke on the river line was a day old now, swallowed by haze. The chest rode his shoulder light as a sin unconfessed.

Then: riders. Two of them. Moving with the economy of men who had nothing to prove to the landscape.

"Company," he said.

Odile didn't startle. She'd learned his voice by then — the flat ranger's tone that meant weather or trouble, delivered the same either way. She shaded her eyes, found them, and swore softly in Vessian, a language that sounded like water running over stones.

"How far?"

"Half a league. Closing." He watched their gait, the way the horses carried them — long, easy strides, no urgency, which was worse than urgency. Urgent riders were chasing something they might lose. These two rode like men who already knew where their quarry would be. "I know the gray one. Halloway."

"The one from your garrison?"

"The one who taught me how to read sign." Ansel felt something turn over in his chest, slow and heavy, like a stone shifting in a riverbed. "Which means he'll read mine."

* * *

They had met the trader on the second morning — a salt-wagon man named Ferro with a face like tanned leather and a tongue that moved faster than his mules. He'd told them about the fleet launching from Corvane's Reach, six ships, dive crews and salvage cranes, and about the dry season coming down the river valley like a door closing. Six tides, he'd said, before the water dropped too low to bring anything up but mud. Then he'd looked at the chest on Ansel's shoulder, and at the seal stamped into its lid — the triple wave of Vessia, worn but unmistakable — and he'd said nothing else at all, which was its own kind of eloquence.

Ansel had done the arithmetic that night by the light of a scrub fire. Sailing from the Reach to the wreck site below the escarpment: four days, if the wind cooperated, five if it didn't. Walking the escarpment path, cutting straight across country no ship could follow: three days, hard walking, maybe less. The fleet had a head start measured in days. They had a route measured in stubbornness.

It should have been an easy choice. It was not, because every step east took him further from his commission, and his commission was the only thing standing between Ansel Ravensworth and the memory of Captain Ellery Voss dying in the flooded wash with Ansel's rope still coiled uselessly at his own feet. The commission kept him fed. Kept him named. Kept him, on bad nights, from being nobody.

Now two riders were coming up the drover track, and one of them was Halloway, and Ansel understood with terrible clarity what the arithmetic had become.

"They'll have orders," Odile said. She crouched at the edge of the track, studying the ground as if she could read it too. "For you, I mean. Not for me. A thief they send men after. A ranger gone quiet for three days — they send friends."

"Halloway isn't my friend."

"No," she agreed. "But he's the closest thing you've got to one, and that's worse."

She was right, and he hated her a little for it, and hated himself more for being grateful she'd said it aloud so he didn't have to.

* * *

There was no cover worth taking. The escarpment spine was bare rock and bunchgrass, the kind of country where hiding meant choosing which direction to be seen from. So Ansel did the only thing left: he slung the chest behind a fallen slab of limestone, wrapped his ranger's coat around it because the gray wool might pass for rock shadow at distance, and told Odile to get below the lip of the track and stay there.

"And you?" she said.

"I'm going to talk to them."

"With what? The truth?"

The question hung between them. He heard everything inside it — the last three days, the manifest, the verses, the smoke on the river, all of it balanced on the point of that word. *Truth.* His whole life he had carried truth like a badge; it was the one thing a ranger owned outright. Lie to your captain and you were done. Lie in a report and you were done. Lie to yourself and you were done slower, but just as certainly.

"Wait here," he said, which was not an answer, and they both knew it.

Halloway reined in forty feet out, the way rangers did when they weren't sure yet whether they were greeting a colleague or making an arrest. Beside him sat a younger man Ansel recognized distantly — Pell, from the eastern garrison, a boy who still polished his boots. Both had crossbows slung but not leveled, which was a message in itself. We hope we don't need these.

"Ravensworth." Halloway's voice hadn't changed in six years. Gravel poured slow. "You're a hard man to find."

"You didn't look very hard."

"We looked exactly as hard as we needed to." Halloway swung down. He was heavier than Ansel remembered, grayer at the temples, but his eyes were the same — patient, unhurried, the eyes of a man who could watch a trailhead for two days without blinking. "Commander Wrenn wants you back at the garrison. Effective immediately. And he wants the thief."

"Thief," Ansel said. "You mean the woman."

"I mean the thief who lifted a garrison strongbox off a commissioned ranger and has been traveling with said ranger ever since, according to every report between here and the ford." Halloway's gaze drifted past Ansel's shoulder, down the empty track, and came back. "Where is she, Theo— Ravensworth. Where is she?"

*Theo.* The old joke, from training days — *theodore, gift of the gods, gift of the garrison* — and Halloway had never once used it kindly. Ansel felt it land the way it always landed, small and needling, and realized with distant surprise that it didn't sting anymore. There wasn't room for it. Something larger had moved into the house of his pride and taken the furniture out.

"She's gone," he said.

"Gone."

"Crossed the flats south. I tracked her to the wash country and lost her in the flash flood." He heard his own voice do it — steady, bored, the tone he used for reports nobody wanted to hear. It frightened him how easily it came. "Flood took half the lowland. Whatever trail existed is under four feet of silt."

Pell shifted in his saddle. "The flood was three days ago, sergeant. You've been out here three days past it."

"Ground needs reading twice in silt country. Once wet, once dry." Ansel shrugged. "Ask Halloway. He taught me."

Halloway didn't answer right away. He walked a slow circle instead, looking at the ground, at the track, at the horizon, and Ansel stood still and let him, because moving was confession. This was the man who had shown him that patience was a weapon. Now Ansel stood inside the reach of it and understood, for the first time, why Halloway had never seemed to fear anything: because he had never had anything to lose.

Until now, watching him, Ansel realized he was afraid. Not of arrest. Of this — of the moment stretching, of Halloway kneeling at the edge of the track and finding a boot print, a thread of gray wool, a woman's smaller stride beside his own.

"You lost her," Halloway said at last. "A thief carrying stolen goods, in open country, and you lost her."

"The flood lost her. I just failed to argue with it."

"Mm." Halloway stopped circling. He looked at Ansel directly, and there was something in his face that hadn't been there before — not suspicion exactly. Recognition. The look of a man seeing another man stand somewhere he himself had once stood. "Wrenn says come home. Says the fleet's put out from the Reach and command wants every available ranger on the river line. Says whatever you've been chasing out here, it's above your rank now."

"It always was."

"Then say that in your report. In person. Wrenn wants you at the garrison within—" Halloway checked the sun, an old habit, though the sun kept no schedule any man trusted. "Four days. You can make it if you leave now."

"And if I don't?"

Silence. Pell's horse stamped. Far below the track, hidden behind the limestone slab, Ansel knew Odile was listening to every word, and he knew she was holding her breath, and he found that he was holding his too.

"If you don't," Halloway said quietly, "then I have to write down that Sergeant Ansel Ravensworth refused a direct recall, and I have to write down why I believe he refused it, and neither of us is going to enjoy what those two sentences do to the rest of your life." He paused. "So I'm asking you plainly, off the record, man to man. Is she worth it?"

And there it was. The whole question, laid out on the bare rock between them like a card turned face-up.

Ansel thought about lying again — a clean lie, *she's nothing, she's a mark, I want my collar back* — and found that his mouth wouldn't shape it. Not because Halloway would see through it. Because Ansel was tired of the taste of it. Three days of half-truths stacked on the chest like stones, and underneath them the true thing, the thing he'd known since the flash flood gave up its dead nation's box: that the manifest mattered more than his commission, that Odile's grandmother's songs mattered more than Commander Wrenn's paperwork, that some debts ran deeper than the one he owed a dead captain's memory.

But he couldn't say that either. Saying it was the same as surrendering her.

"She's a lead," he said finally. "To something bigger than a strongbox. Something the fleet wants buried, and I mean that literally — I've seen what Corvane's people do to evidence. Give me until the dry season closes the river. If I'm wrong, I'll walk into Wrenn's office myself and hand over my commission and my apology in the same envelope."

Halloway studied him for a long time. Wind moved the bunchgrass. Somewhere below, a rock ticked as it cooled, and Ansel's heart slammed against it like a second sound.

"That's not a refusal," Halloway said slowly. "That's a stall."

"It's a report. Verbal. Unofficial." Ansel held his eyes. "Take it or write me up."

Another silence, longer. Then Halloway did something Ansel had never seen him do: he laughed, short and dry, with no humor in it at all.

"God help me, boy, you even lie like a ranger." He swung back into the saddle. "Four days. Dry season. If you're not at the garrison by then, I stop writing reports and start writing warrants." He gathered his reins, then paused, looking down. "One more thing. Off the record."

"Say it."

"The fleet's not just diving. Corvane's got burn crews aboard. Men whose job starts after the divers come up." Halloway's jaw worked. "Whatever you think is down there under the escarpment — whoever drowned to put it there — understand that the crown has spent thirty years making sure nobody reads about it. Don't be holding the proof when they arrive. Be somewhere else entirely."

Then he wheeled the gray horse, and Pell followed, and the two riders shrank down the track into heat-shimmer and were gone.

* * *

Odile rose out of the rocks before the dust settled, which told Ansel she'd been climbing toward the conversation for most of it, rules be damned.

"Burn crews," she said.

"You heard."

"I heard." Her face had gone the color of the salt. "My grandmother said they burned the records first. Before the water even finished rising. She said the fire came before the flood, and everyone thought she was mad."

Ansel walked to the limestone slab and pulled his coat off the chest. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside, he was not steady at all. He was standing on the far side of a line he had crossed without hearing the crack of it — he had lied to Halloway, lied in the shape of a report, bought himself four days with words that were mostly false and partly true, and the strange thing, the thing that made his skin prickle, was that he felt *alive*. Guilt sat in his chest like a swallowed coal. Exhilaration sat beside it, warm as brandy. They fit together the way guilt and exhilaration always fit together, which was badly, and permanently.

There was no uncrossing it. He understood that now. Somewhere on a rider's ledger in the eastern garrison, a sentence was already forming: *Sergeant Ravensworth, delayed, reasons unclear.* And when the four days ran out, the sentence would finish itself, and it would not be kind.

"Four days," Odile said. "He gave you four days."

"He gave me a leash. Four days is just how long the rope is." He slung the chest. "We move tonight. No more fires, no more track — we cut across the scree slopes and drop to the river path below the chapel ruins. It's rougher, but nobody rides scree."

"And after four days?"

"After four days, Halloway stops being the man who trained me and becomes the man who hunts me." Ansel looked southeast, toward the river line, where the haze had thickened since morning. The smoke was gone now, absorbed into the sky, but absence was its own message. The fleet was there. Waiting. Burning things, maybe, already, wherever it found them.

Odile touched his arm — lightly, briefly, the way you touch someone standing near an edge. "You lied for me," she said. "Back there. That was the first time."

"It won't be the last. That's what frightens me."

"Don't let it." Her dark eyes held his, and for a moment he saw past the thief to the granddaughter, the survivor, the woman carrying a drowned nation's songs in her head like coins in a torn pocket. "The law you just broke was built by the same men who built the sluice gates. Maybe breaking it is the first honest thing a ranger's done in thirty years."

He wanted to believe her. He started walking, and the escarpment stretched away ahead of them, mile on mile of broken stone above a drowned country, and behind them, faint but gaining, came the sound he would hear in his sleep for the rest of his life: hoofbeats fading, and beneath them, lower, the river running down toward the sea, dropping inch by inch, taking the six tides with it.

Three remained.
