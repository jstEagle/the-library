## 8. The Escarpment Path

The escarpment did not look like a path. It looked like the edge of the world wearing a disguise.

Odile stood at the lip of it with her toes overhanging nothing and felt the wind come up off the drowned country below — a wet, mineral breath, the smell of a place that had been underwater for sixty years and had never quite learned to be land again. Far beneath them, terraces of old farmland stepped down into gray-green shallows where the roofs of drowned houses showed like knuckles through water. Somewhere down there was Vessia. Somewhere down there was everything.

"Tell me again why we're not taking the river road," she said.

Ansel crouched at the edge, testing a ledge with his boot heel the way he tested everything — patiently, as if the stone might lie to him and he intended to catch it at it. "Because the river road runs past the fleet's anchorage," he said. "And because walking is faster than sailing only if we don't stop to be arrested."

"You say that like it's a small thing. Being arrested."

"For you it would be." He glanced up at her, and there was something in his face that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same neighborhood. "For me it would be an inconvenience."

She snorted and turned back to the cliff. The trail was there — or had been, once. Her grandmother had sung about this path, actually, one of the dozens of half-nonsense songs Odile had memorized without understanding until recently: *the stair of the goat and the grief of the widow, down where the water forgets its name.* A switchbacking track cut into the cliff face by Vessian hands, zigzagging down eight hundred feet of limestone to the old salt road along the waterline. It saved two days around the escarpment's northern spur. Two days they did not have.

The problem was the middle third. A scar of raw rock ran across the face of the cliff where a spring flood had taken the trail with it — forty feet of nothing, or close enough to nothing, a smear of loose scree and tilted slabs with a drop below it that went on long enough for a person to reconsider their entire life on the way down.

"That," Ansel said, "is the goat part."

"And the grief of the widow?"

He looked at her. "What?"

"The song. Never mind." She shrugged off her pack and started digging out the rope. "You have rope, ranger? Or do you people just arrest your way down cliffs?"

"I have rope."

* * *

They roped up at the last solid ground above the washout. Ansel insisted on anchoring it himself, driving iron pitons he carried for reasons Odile suspected were less about climbing than about tying prisoners to things, and she watched him work with the particular attention she gave to anything she might later need to steal or survive. His hands were steady. Ranger's hands, calloused in strange places — from reins, from writing reports, from the oiled wood of a crossbow stock. Not thief's hands. Thief's hands knew locks and ledges; his knew paperwork and pursuit.

It occurred to her, not for the first time, that she had been running from this man for three weeks before the river threw a chest between them, and in all that time she had never once seen him hurry. That frightened her more than speed would have. Hurried men made mistakes. Patient men made plans.

"Ladies first," he said, which she chose to interpret as courtesy rather than distrust.

"Chivalry. From a man who's been trying to shackle me since the salt flats."

"The shackling was professional." He held the rope taut while she swung out over the edge. "This is personal."

She didn't ask what he meant by that, because she was afraid of the answer, and because the cliff required all of her attention and most of her courage.

The first third was easy — good stone, worn footholds, the ghost of the old Vessian masons still visible in the chisel marks, straight and confident even after sixty years of weather. Her grandmother's voice came back to her unbidden, humming the descent verse, and Odile found herself singing it under her breath without deciding to: low, almost inaudible, the melody doing half the work of holding her steady.

"What is that?" Ansel called down from above.

"A ladder song. The Vessians set their work songs to climbing rhythms so masons could keep time on the ropes." She found the next hold. "Half the manifest decoded itself once I understood that. The crown thinks it seized a shipping ledger. It's actually a hymnal."

"That's either brilliant or insane."

"It's both. Most true things are."

Then the good stone ended, and the washout began.

* * *

It was worse up close than it had looked from above, which Odile noted was a general law of the universe and not specific to cliffs. The flood had peeled the trail away like skin off fruit, leaving a diagonal ramp of shattered limestone tilted toward the void, studded with blocks the size of coffins that sat where they had fallen and would move when asked. Forty feet across. Maybe fifty. The rope would reach, barely, if they traversed rather than descended — hand over hand along the anchor line, feet finding whatever purchase offered itself, the whole drop swinging beneath them like a held breath.

Ansel came down beside her, moving with that same unhurried economy, and stopped at the edge of the broken section. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at her.

"We go one at a time," he said. "I'll take the rope weight. You cross, I follow."

"Why you first?"

"Because if the anchor pulls, I'd rather it pull me than you."

She stared at him. "That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard."

"It's arithmetic."

"It's martyrdom dressed up as arithmetic." She took the rope from him before he could argue, wrapping it twice around her forearm the way her mother had taught her on the rigging of a smuggler's skiff, back when her mother still had a name and a boat and a country to be smuggled out of. "I weigh less. The anchor holds longer. *That's* arithmetic. You can be the sentimental one after I'm across."

For a heartbeat he looked like he wanted to fight her on it, and she braced for the argument — for the ranger's certainty, the official voice, the man who believed the world arranged itself into lawful orders. But what crossed his face instead was something quieter and more dangerous: respect. He nodded once, and stepped back, and paid out the line.

She went.

The first ten feet were terrible. The second ten were worse, because they taught her the rhythm of the bad stone — how each block announced its intentions a half-second before acting, a grind, a whisper of grit, a settling — and knowing the rhythm meant knowing exactly how much death was under her at any given moment. She sang. She couldn't help it. The descent verse, low and steady, her breath matching the meter, *down where the water forgets its name,* and the song was a rope too, older than the one in her hands.

Twenty feet. Twenty-five. A block shifted under her left foot and slid six inches with a sound like a coffin lid, and she froze, arms burning, the whole cliff holding its silence around her.

"Odile." Ansel's voice, level, pitched to carry without carrying panic. "Weight to your right foot. Slowly. There's a slab behind you at hip height."

She found it. She breathed. She moved.

Thirty feet. Thirty-five. The far side rose up to meet her, good stone, blessed stone, and she hauled herself onto it and lay flat on her back on the limestone with her heart slamming against her ribs and laughed — one sharp, involuntary bark of laughter at the sky.

"Your turn, ranger," she called. "Try not to die. You're growing on me, and I hate losing things I've grown attached to."

* * *

He was fifteen feet out when the anchor moved.

She heard it before she understood it — a grinding note from above, from the piton, from the crack it was seated in, a sound no piton should ever make. Then the rope jerked sideways in her grip where she'd taken a belay around a boulder, and Ansel swung, and the block he stood on tilted, and the world became very simple and very fast.

Later, she could never reconstruct the order of what she did. She knew she dropped flat and wrapped the belay line around her forearm and her hips and her shoulder, the full smuggler's hitch, the one that ate skin. She knew she screamed something — his name, probably, or a curse, possibly both. She knew the rope burned through her layers and into her arm and she held anyway, held with everything her grandmother's blood had put in her, while Ansel fell six feet and checked hard and swung into the cliff face and caught a ledge with one hand and hung there, boots kicking over eight hundred feet of air, the piton grinding above them both like a slow countdown.

"Rope!" he shouted. "Tie off! Tie it to something!"

"There's nothing to tie it to!"

"Then don't let go!"

"Don't let go," she repeated, half laugh, half sob. "Is that your professional advice? Don't let go? Brilliant, ranger, truly the training shows—"

"*Odile.*"

Something in the way he said her name shut her mouth. He hauled himself up onto the ledge, arm shaking, and got both elbows over it, and for a moment he just hung there breathing, and she saw — clearly, unmistakably, in the space of three seconds — how much he trusted her. Not with words. With his weight. With the whole fact of his continued existence, running through a rope looped around the body of a woman he'd spent three weeks trying to arrest.

The piton held. Barely. They got him across the last twenty feet on slack and prayer, and then they were both lying on the good stone gasping like landed fish, and her forearm was bleeding freely where the rope had flayed it, and neither of them moved for a long time.

* * *

She bandaged her arm with strips of her shirt hem while he re-set the anchor, and he came and sat beside her afterward without being invited, which was new. The afternoon light had gone gold and long. Below them the drowned country lay quiet, roofs and orchard-lines ghosting through the shallows, beautiful in the way that graves are beautiful.

"That rope should have pulled," he said finally. "The piton was in a bad crack. I placed it fast. My fault."

"Yes."

He glanced at her. "Most people would argue."

"Most people weren't watching you dangle. It was a bad placement. You were hurrying because of me, because I went first and you hated it, and haste makes bad cracks." She tied off the bandage with her teeth. "I'm not going to lie to you to spare your pride, Ansel. Lying is expensive. I only spend it when I'm paid well."

"What do you mean, because of you?"

"You didn't want me on the rope. You've been doing the arithmetic of my worth since the chest — what am I worth alive, what am I worth in irons, what does the commission say, what does the debt say. And today the arithmetic almost killed us both." She looked at him straight on. "So here's a payment, free of charge, since we nearly died together. Ask me why I stole from your garrison."

His jaw tightened. "You already told me. Money. Your family's debts—"

"I told you a version. Here's the true one." She flexed her bandaged hand, watching the blood seep pink through the cloth, and found — to her surprise — that the words came easier than she expected. Perhaps near-death loosened locks. Perhaps it was the cliff, the drowned country below, the sense of standing inside her grandmother's song. "Three years ago my family had passage papers within reach. Real ones. Out of the crown territories, up the coast, to the free ports where nobody asks what nation drowned under your name. My mother had scrimped eleven years. We were four hundred crowns short. Four hundred crowns, Ansel. The price of a decent horse."

"The garrison strongbox held closer to two thousand."

"It held two thousand and sixty." She smiled, thin and unapologetic. "I'm a professional. I took the fee and the interest. But here's the part I've never told anyone who wasn't family: I didn't take it for the papers. The papers fell through — the broker was arrested the week after, the route closed, the money useless. By the time I could have returned it, returning it meant confessing, and confessing meant my mother's name on a warrant, and my mother has one lung and no country. So I kept it. And I kept stealing, because once you're a thief on paper you may as well be one in practice, and because—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through. "Because every crown I lifted off the garrison felt like collecting a debt. Your garrison flies the crown's colors. The crown flooded Vessia. Sixty years ago somebody signed an order and a nation went under the water, and my grandmother sang funeral songs until she died, and nobody ever paid. So yes. I stole. Call it theft. I call it the smallest possible installment."

Silence. The wind moved over the escarpment. Below them, far down, the river glinted on its way to the sea, dropping, always dropping, spending the tides they couldn't afford.

Ansel was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way a man's voice is careful when he's walking on the same kind of broken stone she'd just crossed.

"My grandfather," he said, "was a clerk in the Office of Waters. Same office that administered the sluice commissions." He wasn't looking at her; he was looking down at the drowned roofs. "I used to think that was a boring inheritance. Ledgers and gates and rainfall tables." He paused. "Lately I've been wondering what else is in those ledgers."

"Ansel—"

"If the manifest says what you think it says," he said, "then every debt you've been collecting on your own, coin by coin, is a debt the whole crown owes. And I've spent my whole life enforcing collections for the wrong side of the ledger." He turned and looked at her then, fully, and she saw that the patient man was still there — but the patience had changed direction, the way a river changes direction, all at once and forever. "Four hundred crowns. You risked the gallows for four hundred crowns and your mother's name."

"And a nation's memory," she said. "Don't sell me short."

"I wouldn't dream of it." He reached over — slowly, giving her time to move away, and she didn't — and touched the bandage on her arm, light as a question. "This will scar."

"Everything worth having does."

He almost smiled. Then his eyes went past her shoulder, down the escarpment's face to the salt road far below — and stopped.

"Odile." All the warmth drained out of his voice. "Look. On the road. Due south."

She turned. Two miles down, along the pale ribbon of the old salt road, a column was moving. Small at this distance, orderly, unhurried. Wagons, riders, and at the head of them a banner that even from here she could read by its shape alone, because she'd been reading that shape in nightmares since she was a child.

The crown's colors. The salvage fleet's outriders — ahead of schedule, on the wrong side of the escarpment, and marching straight for the drowned chapel country.

"They found another route," Ansel said quietly. "We just lost our two days."

Odile counted the wagons, counted the riders, and did the only arithmetic that mattered now.

"Then we stop losing," she said, "and start taking."
