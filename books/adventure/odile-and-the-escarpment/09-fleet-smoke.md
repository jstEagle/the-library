## 9. Fleet Smoke

The ridge had a name on no map Ansel had ever been given. The locals called it the Watchman's Shoulder, a long spine of rust-colored stone that shouldered up out of the badlands like something buried that had decided, after a thousand years, to sit up and look around. From its crown you could see forty miles of river country: the green vein of the Vessa winding south, the brown smudge of the escarpment forests, and beyond it all, far off where the land gave out into haze, the flat silver gleam of the lower reaches.

Ansel reached the top an hour past dawn, breathing hard, his wounded forearm bound tight beneath his sleeve. Odile crested behind him, moving with that uncanny economy of hers — no wasted motion, no sound, as if the ground itself had agreed not to notice her. She came up beside him and stopped, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

Because there it was.

Smoke.

Not one plume but many, a dozen thin gray threads rising off the river downstream, straightened by the morning wind into a picket fence of masts and funnel-stink. At this distance the ships themselves were invisible, hulls hidden by the river's bend, but Ansel had spent eleven years reading country the way other men read ledgers, and he did not need to see them to count them. Coal smoke meant steam launches. Steam launches meant the crown's salvage fleet.

"They're not ahead of us," Odile said at last. Her voice was flat in the way he'd learned meant she was working hard to keep it that way. "They're *below* us. Downstream."

"I can see that."

"Then explain it to me, ranger, because I've been doing the arithmetic since the toll bridge and none of it comes out right." She turned to face him, and the wind pulled a strand of dark hair across her eyes; she didn't bother with it. "The fleet launched from Corvane's anchorage six days ago. Six days downriver sailing is three days' walk upstream against current, if that. We cut overland through bandit country, we slept in ditches, we lost two tides to your fever—" she caught herself, jaw tightening, "—to the wound. We should be *behind* them. Days behind. So why are they anchored downstream of the wreck site instead of sitting on top of it?"

Ansel looked at the smoke again. Twelve threads. Maybe fourteen. He thought about what he knew of Admiral Corvane — which was mostly reputation and one memorable reprimand filed against Ansel's own captain, years ago, for impounding a salvage barge without warrant. Corvane ran his fleet like a man running a clock: precise, punctual, and utterly indifferent to anything the clock was measuring except time.

A fleet that size didn't anchor short of its objective because it was lost.

"It means they stopped," Ansel said slowly. "They found something between here and the wreck that they wanted more than speed."

"Or someone."

"Or someone." He crouched, pulling his satchel around, and dug out the half-manifest wrapped in oilcloth. He didn't open it — he'd learned that much caution on the escarpment trail — just held it, feeling its weight, the way you'd hold a stone you suspected might be hollow. "The scouts. Corvane would have sent riders ahead of the fleet, same as any commander. Light craft, fast horses, working both banks. If one of them picked up our trail—"

"We covered it," Odile said. "I covered it. You were delirious for most of it, but I covered it."

"You covered our trail. Not everyone's." He looked at her. "The deserter."

Her expression flickered. They hadn't spoken of him since the night they'd taken him — two days ago now, a hollow-eyed young man in a fleet jacket too big for his shoulders, found limping along the towpath with blistered feet and a stolen chicken under his arm. He'd given his name as Pell. He'd given up everything else almost gratefully, the way men do when desertion has already broken something in them and there's nothing left to protect.

He was tied to a stunted juniper at the base of the ridge, under guard of nothing but their own exhaustion and the fact that he had nowhere to run on feet like his.

"Come on," Ansel said.

* * *

Pell sat where they'd left him, slumped against the juniper with his wrists lashed in front of him, watching them come down the slope with the resigned patience of livestock. Up close he couldn't have been more than nineteen. There was a fleet tattoo on his neck — the crowned keel of the Salvage Service — half-finished, the ink gone crooked where the needle had skipped. Apprentices got the tattoo before they got the rank. He'd run before either finished him.

"Water first," Odile said, and handed him the skin before Ansel could object. Pell drank like a man who'd forgotten dignity existed, then wiped his mouth on his shoulder and waited.

"You told us," Ansel said, crouching to his level, "that you jumped ship above the Sorrow Narrows. That you'd been aboard four months. That the fleet was running survey work."

"That's what I told you."

"And now there are twelve ships of smoke anchored thirty miles below us, short of the wreck, going nowhere fast. So let's try again. What is the fleet doing, Pell?"

The boy looked at the ground. Then at Odile. Then, with something like apology, back at Ansel.

"The scouts found a woman," he said. "Upstream of the narrows. A guide. She used to dive the escarpment pools before the crown bought the concessions. She knows a second way down to the wreck — a sinkhole route, from the high plateau, that drops you at the chapel shallows without touching the main channel at all." He swallowed. "The fleet put ashore two days ago. They're portaging the launches overland. Lighters, rollers, a hundred men on ropes. The admiral means to come at the site from above, quiet, while everyone who matters is still watching the river."

Odile went very still beside him. Ansel felt the news land in his chest like a dropped anchor — not surprise exactly, but the sick settling weight of a suspicion confirmed. The sinkhole route. He'd seen its mouth two days ago from the escarpment path and dismissed it as impassable for anything larger than a goat. He had underestimated, again, what a hundred men and a budget could make passable.

"Why quiet?" Odile asked. Her voice had gone soft and dangerous. "Salvage rights are public record. Corvane has writs. Why does an admiral with lawful papers need to sneak up on a hole in the water?"

Pell's laugh came out cracked. "You think the writs say what we're really after?" He shifted against the tree, wincing. "I worked the manifest office. Four months of copying manifests, that's all I was to them — a boy with good handwriting. But you copy enough manifests, you start reading them." His eyes came up, and there was something old and frightened in them that hadn't been there when they'd caught him. "The fleet's official cargo order lists survey instruments, dredging chains, diving bells. Fine. But there was a second order. Sealed. The admiral's own hand on it, countersigned by the Ministry of Interior — not the Admiralty. Interior. And the only thing I ever saw come out of that sealed compartment was fire."

"Fire?"

"Burn barrels. On deck, every evening, in the admiral's quadrant, guarded. Sailors aren't allowed near them." Pell wet his lips. "You don't burn paper every night for a week unless you're destroying a lot of it. And you don't guard burn barrels unless what's in them matters more than the ship does."

The wind moved through the juniper. Below them, miles off, the smoke threads rose and straightened and rose again, patient as sentries.

"What's at the wreck," Ansel said quietly, "that an admiral burns paper over?"

Pell looked at him for a long moment. Whatever he saw in Ansel's face made him decide something.

"My grandfather was Vessian," the boy said. "One of the boat families that scattered north after the flooding. He never talked about it — the ones who lived through it mostly don't — except once, drunk, he told me the water didn't rise. He said the water was *let in*. He said the sluice gates at the upper weir were opened from the crown station, and the garrison log that proved it went down with the flagship of the Vessian merchant marine, the *Corbie*, when they scuttled her in the deep pool under the escarpment to hide the evidence." He shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "I thought it was an old man's story. Then I got assigned to the manifest office, and I copied a supply requisition for the fleet that listed, among other things, sixty fathoms of chain, four diving bells, and enough quicklime to render a whale. Quicklime, ranger. You don't need quicklime to salvage a wreck. You need it to destroy one. To eat the wood, eat the paper, eat anything that was ever written down inside her."

Ansel heard his own pulse. The chest in his satchel seemed suddenly heavier — half a manifest pointing to a wreck that held the other half, and a ledger, and proof, and an admiral sailing toward all of it with barrels of fire and sacks of lime.

"He doesn't want to raise the *Corbie*," Odile said. Each word came out placed, deliberate, like stones laid across a crossing. "He wants to unmake it. Erase it so thoroughly that no one can ever prove what my grandmother sang about. No proof, no reparations, no debt. The survivors stay scattered, the story stays a story, and the crown keeps everything it took." She turned to Ansel. "This isn't a race anymore."

"No," Ansel said. "It isn't."

It should have been a relief, in a way. All this way he had told himself the arithmetic was simple: reach the wreck before the fleet, secure the manifest, bring it back through channels, let the law do what law does. A race had rules. A race had a finish line and a fair course and a loser who could go home. But standing on the Watchman's Shoulder with the fleet's smoke stitching the horizon, Ansel understood that the finish line had been a fiction all along. Corvane wasn't racing them. Corvane was erasing them — or would erase them, the moment the last page turned to ash. You couldn't outrun a man whose plan ended with there being nothing left to find.

And the law — his commission, his captain's memory, the whole architecture of duty he'd built his life inside — the law was on the side of the burning.

"Pell," Ansel said. "The sealed order. Countersigned by Interior. Did you read any of it?"

"Just the heading, before the clerk caught me looking. 'Disposition of Recovered Materials.'" The boy's mouth twisted. "*Disposition.* Like the wreck was a nuisance to be disposed of."

"Do you know what happens to deserters the fleet catches?" Ansel asked.

"I know what happened to the last one. They hung him from the yardarm of the *Vigilant* and left him there through three ports." Pell's voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "That's why I'm telling you all of it. If they catch me, I'm dead anyway. If you get there first—" He stopped. Looked at the ridge, the river, the smoke. "My grandfather's people never got justice. Nobody even got to *ask* for it. If there's proof down there, and I can help it see daylight, then hanging me later is a price I'll have already paid."

Odile knelt and cut his wrists free with one pass of her knife.

"Can you walk?" she asked.

"I've been walking on these feet since the narrows. I can walk."

"Then you're coming with us," she said, "because you're the only one of us who's seen the inside of that fleet, and when we reach the wreck, I want to know exactly how many men Corvane has, how they're armed, and where the admiral sleeps." She stood, slinging her pack. "Consider it restitution. You stole a chicken. We're stealing you back."

Something moved across the boy's face that might, on a healthier face, have been a smile.

Ansel lingered a moment longer, looking south. The smoke stood where it stood, and behind it, somewhere in the drowned dark under the escarpment, the *Corbie* waited with her dead and her ledger, holding her secret the way the river held everything — patiently, and not forever.

He had spent eleven years believing the law was a wall he stood on, keeping the flood back. Now he knew better. The law was a gate. And somewhere upstream, a man with an admiral's flag had his hand on the sluice.

"Six tides," Ansel said aloud, to no one, to all of them. "Maybe five. We move now, we travel light, and we sleep in shifts."

He turned and started down the southern face of the Watchman's Shoulder, and behind him came the thief, the deserter, and the long shadow of everything they were walking toward — no longer chasing or chased, but racing a fire that wanted to eat the truth whole, and knowing, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that from here on, every mile they gained was a mile stolen from the crown itself.
