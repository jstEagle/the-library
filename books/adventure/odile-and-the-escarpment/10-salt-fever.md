## 10. Salt Fever

The rope cut him at the escarpment's third ledge, a thin red line across his forearm that he waved off like a fly. By the time we reached the shelter of the old salt-works — a ruin of stone vats and collapsed drying sheds half-swallowed by the flats — the line had gone angry and dark.

"You should let me look at it," I said.

"It's a scratch."

"Rope fiber in a wound is how men lose arms out here. My grandmother stitched gashes on the river barges for forty years. She'd have boxed your ears for that answer."

He sat down on an overturned vat anyway, which was as close to surrender as Ansel Ravensworth ever came, and let me peel back the sleeve. The flesh around the cut had gone hot and tight, the skin flushed the color of dawn over the salt pans. When I pressed, he didn't flinch, but his jaw did something small and terrible, and I knew it was worse than he'd let me see.

I cleaned it with the last of our clean water, which felt like pouring our future down the stones, and bound it with strips of my shirt hem. He watched me work with that flat ranger's stare, the one that catalogued everything and confessed nothing.

"You've done this before," he said.

"I told you. Grandmother." I tied off the knot. "She said fever tells you the truth about a body. You just have to listen."

"Fever lies," he said. "That's what my captain used to say. Fever lies, and so do wounded men who want to keep walking."

"And where is your captain now?"

The silence answered for him. I hadn't meant it cruelly. It came out of me the way all my sharpest things did — fast, before I could weigh them. His face didn't change, but something behind it went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

* * *

He lasted until midnight.

I woke to a sound I knew from the barges — not groaning, because strong men don't groan, they breathe wrong. Shallow and fast, each breath a little fight. I found him curled against the vat wall where I'd left him, except now he was shivering so hard the stones seemed to rattle with him, and when I put my hand to his forehead it came away scorched.

"Ansel." I shook his shoulder. "Ansel, wake up."

His eyes opened, and they weren't his eyes. They were somewhere else entirely, fixed on some middle distance only he could see, glassy and black in the moonlight coming through the broken roof.

"The manifest," he said. "Where's the manifest?"

"Here. Wrapped in oilcloth, against my chest. It's safe."

"Corvane has ships." He tried to sit up and failed, sliding sideways, and I caught him under the arms and lowered him back down. "Six tides. We had six tides, and I've spent two of them walking too slow, and now—"

"Now you're going to lie still and drink this," I said, and pressed the waterskin to his mouth.

He drank because his body made him, not because he chose to. Then he was under again, muttering — fragments, mostly, names I didn't know, a word that might have been *commission*, a woman's name he said twice with an apology folded into it. I told myself it wasn't jealousy. I told myself a lot of things that night.

I built up the fire from the driftwood we'd hauled in from the flood-line, the salt-crusted branches that burn green and slow and smell like the sea's ghost. I got the last of the willow bark boiling — I'd stripped it from the trees along the wash three days back without knowing why, some instinct older than sense, and I could have kissed every gray branch of it now. I soaked the binding off his arm and looked at the wound properly by firelight.

It was bad. Not dying-bad, not yet, but the redness had spread in streaks toward his elbow, and there was a heat in the flesh you could feel without touching. Rope-gash rot, the barge women called it. Salt would help if I could find brine. Iron would help if I had any. What I had was willow bark, a knife, a fire, and two tides' worth of time I couldn't afford to spend.

Outside, the flats lay silver and silent under a fat moon. Somewhere downstream, the crown's salvage fleet was anchoring, and Admiral Corvane was counting the same tides I was, and every hour I sat here boiling bark was an hour he gained.

I looked at Ansel's face in the firelight — slack now, younger without the vigilance, the lines around his mouth smoothed into something almost boyish — and I understood, with a clarity that frightened me more than the fever did, that I was not going to leave him.

Not for the manifest. Not for the wreck. Not for the six tides or the five or however many we had left.

I sat down beside him and began the long, stupid, stubborn work of keeping a man alive.

* * *

Fever has its own tide. That's what Grandmother taught me. It rises and it falls, and you learn to read it like water — when to force broth between his teeth, when to sponge his skin with cool cloth, when to just sit and let the fire crackle and say nothing at all. Fighting it at the wrong moment exhausts them worse than the sickness does.

So I learned his fever's rhythm. It crested near dawn, and he talked then — not to me, mostly, but to the dead captain whose name I finally caught: *Brannoc*. He apologized to Brannoc for things I couldn't follow. He argued with him, too, in a low cracked voice, defending decisions Brannoc had apparently never questioned aloud. Once he said, "I paid it back, sir. Every coin. Ask the ledger," and then wept without moving his face, tears sliding sideways into his hair, and I had to turn away and press my knuckles to my mouth until I could breathe again.

You learn things about people when they burn. Things they'd never spend awake. I learned that Ansel Ravensworth carried his debt to a dead man the way other men carry gold — close to the chest, counted nightly, never spent. I learned that whatever he'd done to earn that debt, he'd been paying it for years and believed the account still open. And I learned, somewhere in the second night, listening to him murmur my own name — *Odile, Odile, hold the line* — that I was in trouble of a kind no willow bark could touch.

Because I held the line. Of course I held the line. I sponged him down when the fever climbed, mounded blankets when it fell and the chills took him. I walked the quarter-mile to the old evaporation channels and hauled back brine in the dented basin we'd found, and soaked his arm until he whimpered in his sleep, and the streaks in the skin began, grudgingly, to fade. I sang, because Grandmother swore fevered men hear singing even when they can't hear voices, and because the songs were already in my throat, waiting.

They were Vessian songs. They were always Vessian songs.

* * *

On the second evening, the fever broke the way a squall breaks — sudden, total, leaving everything washed and trembling. He slept then, truly slept, the deep dreamless kind, and I sat watching the fire and realized I hadn't slept properly in two days and probably wouldn't until we reached the wreck or died trying.

I unwrapped the manifest from its oilcloth, mostly from habit, checking for damp. Dry. Safe. The strange verse-script crawled across the hide in its looping columns, the cargo-list-that-was-a-song, and I found myself reading it aloud — quietly, because the words worked better aloud, the way Grandmother had taught me, the way they'd been designed to work, syllables locking together like the tumblers of a lock.

*"What the river took in wrath, / the ledger keeps in trust; / count the gates that drowned the path, / and call the debt unjust..."*

Behind me, a voice, rough with sleep: "Keep singing."

I nearly dropped the hide. Ansel had turned his head, eyes open at last and clear, fever-pale but present, watching me across the fire.

"You're supposed to be unconscious."

"I'm told I'm insubordinate even asleep." He pushed himself up an inch against the vat, winced, and looked at his bandaged arm with the expression of a man auditing a report he didn't want to be true. "How long?"

"Two tides. Maybe a bit more."

He closed his eyes. I watched him do the arithmetic — I could practically see the numbers marching behind his lids, tides lost, miles lost, the fleet's lead lengthening — and I waited for the anger, the recrimination, the cold ranger's accounting of what my carelessness had cost us.

Instead he said, "Thank you."

Just that. Two words, spoken to the fire, as if they embarrassed him.

"Don't thank me yet," I said. "We may have traded two tides for nothing, if Corvane reaches the wreck first."

"We were always behind." He opened his eyes again. "Since the day the fleet launched, we were behind. Two tides doesn't change the shape of the race. It changes—" He stopped. Started again, slower, the way he spoke when he was saying something he'd decided not to decide in advance. "It changes what I know. That's all."

"What do you know?"

"That you could have taken the manifest and walked." His voice was steady, but his hands, I noticed, were not quite. "I was unconscious for two days. You had the chest, the verses, the route — everything. One thief, one sleeping lawman, open country in every direction. Nobody would ever have found you."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the flats, wind moved through the salt grass with a sound like whispering.

"I know," I said.

"Why didn't you?"

I looked down at the manifest in my lap, at the looping verse-script, at my grandmother's inheritance written in somebody else's careful hand generations ago. I thought about giving him a practical answer — *you know the escarpment trails, you're useful, two are safer than one* — all true, all beside the point.

"Because you said her name," I said. "In your sleep. Brannoc's wife — no. Your mother? Someone. You said a name like you owed it something." I shook my head. "People who owe debts don't abandon people mid-payment, Ansel. It's a rule where I come from. My whole family lives by it."

He studied me for a long moment, and I let him, and neither of us said anything else, because there was nothing else that needed saying. Some things you admit the way fever admits itself — silently, in the rising, in the care taken, in the fact of staying.

* * *

He slept again before full dark, a real sleep this time, healing sleep, and I kept the fire fed and the manifest dry and my eyes on the southern horizon where the stars came down to meet the salt.

Near midnight, I saw it.

A light, far off, low against the flats — not a star, not a campfire's honest orange, but a cold white glitter, moving. Then another. Then a string of them, strung along the distant dark like lanterns hung on an invisible wire, and I knew what they were before my mind finished the sentence: running lights. Fleet boats, poling through the shallows of the sinking river, heading upstream.

Toward the escarpment. Toward the wreck. Toward the same stretch of falling water we'd planned to reach with a tide to spare.

I counted the lights twice. Nine. Maybe ten.

Then I went and put my hand on Ansel's shoulder and shook him gently awake, and said the words I'd been dreading since the fever started:

"They're here. Ansel — they're already here."
