## 11. The Drowned Chapel

The storm found us an hour before we did.

It came down the escarpment like a gray animal, all shoulders and teeth, and by the time we saw the lightning stitching the horizon to the cliffs, the wind had already stolen the words out of our mouths. Odile pointed — not shouted, there was no shouting over that wind — toward a dark notch in the rock face where the river had once bitten deep into the stone. A cave, maybe. A cut. Anything with a roof.

We ran for it with the chest strapped between us on a litter of rope and driftwood poles, taking turns at the front because neither of us would say aloud that the other was tiring. The rain hit like thrown gravel. The flats behind us turned white with it, and I thought of the flood that had given us the chest in the first place, and I wondered what else the river meant to give up tonight.

The notch was not a cave.

It was a doorway.

I understood that before I understood anything else, standing under the lintel with water sheeting off my hat brim. The opening was too regular, its edges too square, and above it — worn nearly smooth, but legible when the lightning flashed — a carved line of Vessian script arched across the stone like a wing. Below it, smaller, a second inscription in trade-tongue, the kind the old kingdom used for foreigners:

*All waters return home.*

"Chapel," Odile said. She'd gone very still, which for Odile was the same as another person going pale. "Ansel. It's a chapel."

Lightning again, and this time I saw the whole of it: a nave half-drowned, the floor tilted where the escarpment had settled after the flooding, so that one end stood dry above the waterline and the other descended into black, glass-still water that reached perhaps chest-deep toward the far wall. Roof beams had fallen long ago; what remained of the ceiling was stone ribbing open to the storm, so the rain fell into the flooded end in silver threads. Along both walls, columns marched down into the water like drowned men standing at attention.

We dragged the chest up onto the dry stones, got a fire going from Odile's oilcloth-wrapped tinder and the broken pew-wood that littered the near end — pews, I realized, watching her feed the flame. We were sheltering inside a church. The dead of Vessia had prayed here, and now two thieves of a different kind were burning their benches to keep warm.

"I'll see how deep it goes," she said, before I could object. She was already pulling off her boots.

"The water's black as ink. You can't see—"

"I don't need to see." She tied a length of cord around her waist and handed me the end, and something in her face closed the argument better than any words. "Hold on. If I pull twice, come in after me."

She waded in without ceremony, gasping once at the cold, then swimming, the cord paying out through my hands slow and steady. Lightning lit the flooded nave through the broken roof, and for one full second I saw her below the surface, a pale shape gliding between the columns, hair fanned out like ink — and around her, faintly, shapes on the walls. Carvings. Hundreds of them.

The cord went slack. Then it pulled twice.

I went in.

The cold took my breath the way a fist takes a purse. I swam toward the shape of her voice — she was calling, muffled, from somewhere ahead — and surfaced in air. That was the strangest part: past the third column, the tilted roof formed a pocket, a low chamber of trapped air above the water, and Odile was standing chest-deep in it, holding a shuttered lantern she must have stowed in her coat, and she was not looking at me at all.

She was looking at the wall.

"Ansel," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened by the stone around us. "Come here. You have to see this."

I swam to her and stood, and she raised the lantern, and the wall of the drowned chapel gave up its secret.

Names. Carved names, thousands of them, rank upon rank upon rank, covering every inch of stone from the waterline to the roof pocket and no doubt continuing below. Not graffiti. Not decoration. Each name sat inside its own small niche, chiseled with care, some with dates beneath, some with tiny symbols — a fish, a bell, a child's toy. A memorial wall. The chapel hadn't been drowned by accident. It had been built here, at the edge of the escarpment, *after* the flooding, by survivors who came back to carve the names of everyone the water took.

Or — I looked again at the lowest rows, just above the waterline, at the wear-marks of hands touching them — everyone the water *kept*.

Odile's lantern hand was shaking. Just slightly. Just enough that the shadows breathed.

"My grandmother used to sing about this," she said quietly. "A wall of names at the edge of the world. I thought it was a story. The way you think a story is a story." She lifted the light higher. "There are supposed to be whole villages' worth. She said her own mother's name was on a wall somewhere, and that if I ever found it, I should touch it, so that—" Her voice caught. She steadied it the way she steadied everything, by force. "So that the dead would know someone came."

"Find your family's name," I said. "We have time. The storm won't break till morning."

So we searched.

It was slow work, and stranger work than I can easily set down. We moved along the wall in the lantern light, reading names aloud — she read, mostly, her lips moving in the old script faster than mine could follow, though I learned the patterns quickly enough. Rows of names. Whole generations. Some niches held only a date and a word — *lost at the sluice*, *taken in the night*, and once, unbearably, *born in the water*. The carvers had run out of room generations ago and begun stacking names in the margins, sideways, upside down, a civilization's grief compressed into every available inch of stone.

And all the while, some cold clerk in the back of my mind kept doing arithmetic I didn't ask it to do. Dates. The flooding of Vessia was ninety years gone. My grandfather — Captain Edmun Ravensworth, garrison engineer, the man whose commission ring I wore on a cord under my shirt because it was the only inheritance anyone in my family had ever bothered to pass down — my grandfather had been stationed on the escarpment works in exactly those years. I had grown up on his stories. The great sluice-gates. The river management. The proud, dull chronicle of a man who moved water for a living and never once, in all his letters home, mentioned what the water *did*.

"Ravensworth." Odile said it suddenly, sharply, and my heart stopped against my ribs before I understood she was reading, not accusing. "Here. Ansel — *here*."

Her finger rested on a niche three rows above the waterline. The carving was deeper than most, re-cut at least once by loving hands, and beneath the name — *Maren Ravensworth-Odile* — wait. No. I leaned closer, water streaming off my chin, and made myself read it properly.

*Maren Odile-Vessane.* And below it, in smaller characters: *of the family Odile, keeper of songs.*

"That's her." Odile's voice had gone thin. "That's my grandmother's mother. That's the woman who taught her the verses — the ones in the manifest, Ansel, the *same verses*, she was keeper of songs, that's why the cargo list was written that way, it runs in the—" She pressed her palm flat against the niche, against the name, and held it there, and for a long moment she didn't say anything at all, and I stood beside her in the black water and let her have the silence, because some debts are paid in silence.

Then she laughed. It was a wet, cracked sound, half sob. "My grandmother told me to touch it. She said if I ever found the wall, I should touch her mother's name. And I'm — I'm standing in a flooded church in the middle of a storm, and I found it, and she's been dead six years and she'll never—" The laugh broke apart entirely. I put my arm around her shoulders, awkward, waterlogged, and she let me, and we stayed like that while the storm played its drums on the broken roof.

It was later — much later, when we'd climbed back to the fire and wrapped ourselves in every scrap of dry cloth we owned, when the chest sat between us dripping river-water and the lightning had softened to a steady grumble in the west — that I finally said it.

Because I had to. Because she had touched her great-grandmother's name and called it an inheritance, and I had stood there and felt the ring on its cord against my sternum like a brand, and I knew — I had known since the manifest, if I was honest, known in the place where knowledge waits to be spoken — what my grandfather's signature at the bottom of those sluice-gate orders meant. What it would mean. What it already meant, here, in this chapel, with her.

"Odile." The fire popped. She looked up, and her eyes were still red-rimmed, and I hated what I was about to do and did it anyway, because she deserved a truth bought with discomfort rather than comfort. "My grandfather worked the escarpment. During the flooding years."

She went still. Odile could go stiller than anyone I'd ever met — stiller than the water in the far nave.

"He was an engineer. Garrison corps. He signed — there are orders, in the archive at Corvane's fleet headquarters, evacuation authorizations, gate schedules. His name is on them. I've seen the file." I had, once, years ago, pulling records for a routine land dispute, and I had skimmed past the familiar signature without a second thought, the way you skim past your own face in a crowd. "Edmun Ravensworth. He helped manage the gates."

"Managed them," she repeated. Flat.

"The crown's account says the flooding was a natural disaster. River surge. Bad engineering luck." I heard how it sounded even as I said it. "But the manifest doesn't read like bad luck. And men who sign gate schedules during a disaster aren't recording weather, Odile. They're recording *decisions*."

The firelight moved on her face. She didn't look angry. That was the worst part — she looked like a woman doing sums, and arriving at a total she'd suspected all along.

"So your grandfather," she said slowly, "helped drown my great-grandmother's country. And my great-grandmother's granddaughter robbed your garrison. And the river put a chest between us." She looked at the fire. "You believe in signs, ranger?"

"I believe in evidence."

"Same thing, where you're from." But there was no bite in it. She pulled her knees up under the blanket and stared into the flames for a long time, and when she spoke again her voice was quieter. "Does he know? Did he know? Your grandfather. In his letters, in his stories — did he ever say what he did?"

"No. He talked about the gates like they were cathedrals. Beautiful work. Precision." I turned the ring on its cord, feeling its weight. "He died proud. That's the part I can't get around. Whatever happened at those sluice-gates, he died proud of it, and everyone who loved him loved him *for* it, and I was one of them."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in his victims' church, burning their pews."

"Don't." She said it fast, sharp. "Don't do that thing where you make yourself the villain so you don't have to be useful. Your grandfather signed papers. Corvane's fleet is sailing right now to bury the proof. Those aren't the same crime, Ansel, and you know it." She leaned forward, firelight catching the wet ends of her hair. "You want to carry his guilt? Fine. Carry it *forward*. Use it. A man who owes a debt and drowns himself in it pays nothing to anyone."

I looked at her — this thief, this outlaw, this heir of a drowned nation sitting wrapped in a blanket in her ancestors' chapel — and I understood that she had just done for me what I couldn't do for myself, the way I'd held her cord in the black water and hauled when she pulled. That was what we were becoming. Two people holding each other's lines.

"Your grandmother," I said. "The keeper of songs. Why did her family leave? Why weren't they—"

"Drowned?" She said it without flinching. "Because her father read the gates. Read the schedules, somehow — smuggled correspondence, we think, or a bribed clerk — and saw that the spring release was set wrong. Set *deliberately* wrong. He got his family out two days early. Everyone called him a madman until the water came." Her jaw tightened. "He tried to warn the others. Nobody listens to a canal-keeper's son over a captain's seal."

*A captain's seal.*

The ring seemed to burn against my skin. I thought of the manifest verse, the sluice-gate orders Corvane wanted buried, the admiral's fleet anchoring downstream with its holds empty and ready. I thought of a chapel built by survivors at the edge of everything, stacked to the rafters with names, and of a girl who'd crossed a desert to steal a chest she didn't know the contents of, chasing a story her grandmother sang her to sleep with.

Outside, the storm was dying. Through the broken roof, between the last ragged clouds, stars were coming out over the escarpment — and below them, far downriver, a smudge of orange light flickered where no honest traveler would camp.

Fleet lanterns. Dozens of them.

Odile followed my gaze and went still again, that terrible stillness, and then she was moving — snuffing the fire, hauling the chest, whispering the old verses under her breath like a prayer or a countdown.

"They found another route," I said. "They're closer than we thought."

The last of the clouds tore away, and the moonlight showed me the rest: the lanterns weren't camping. They were moving. A long, patient line of them, winding along the river road toward the escarpment. Toward the wreck. Toward us.

Toward the chapel, and the wall of names, and everything the dead had waited ninety years to say.
