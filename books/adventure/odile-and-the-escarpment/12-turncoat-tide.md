## 12. Turncoat Tide

The deserter's name was Pellam Wick, and Odile had never trusted a man who laughed so easily at his own jokes.

He'd come to them three nights past, half-drowned and shivering at the edge of the mudflats below the escarpment, hands raised before Ansel even leveled his blade. A fleet man gone soft, he said — couldn't stomach what the admiral meant to do to the wreck, couldn't stomach the orders whispered below decks. He'd swum the channel at night with nothing but his knife and his regret. He wanted passage inland. He wanted, he said, to be done with the crown forever.

Ansel had kept him at knifepoint for an hour of questioning. Odile had watched the man's eyes while Ansel talked — watched how they flicked toward the chest every time it was mentioned, quick as a fish turning in shallow water. She'd said nothing then. She should have said something.

Now, on this fourth night camped in the lee of a collapsed watchtower, she lay awake listening to the wind move through the salt grass and thinking about those flicking eyes.

The tower had been Vessian once. You could still see it in the stonework — the wave-scroll carvings worn nearly smooth, the keystone above the doorway bearing a seal so eroded it might have been anything. But Odile knew it for what it was. Her grandmother had sung about towers like this one, watch-posts along the old river road where the wardens of Vessia had counted barges and burned beacons when the floods came. *Count the boats, my love, count them slow; the river keeps what kings let go.* She'd thought it nonsense when she was small, a lullaby with no bottom to it. Now she slept beneath one of its verses made stone.

The chest sat between her and Ansel, as it always did, close enough that either could reach it in the dark. The verse-manifest lay inside, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp that crept up from the flats. Pellam Wick slept by the far wall, snoring with the untroubled rhythm of a man with no conscience to keep him awake.

Or so she'd believed.

She heard him first as a change in the snoring — a stop, a silence too deliberate to be sleep. Then the soft scrape of leather on stone. Odile opened her eyes without moving, the way her mother had taught her, back when opening your eyes fast in a strange house could get you killed.

Pellam was sitting up. He was looking south, down the long slope of the flats toward the river, where the fleet's anchor-lanterns had been visible for two days now like a string of low orange stars that refused to set. And he was doing something with his hands. It took her a moment to understand: he was striking flint. Cupped, shielded, the sparks falling into a twist of dry grass and birchbark.

A signal fire. Small enough to hide from her and Ansel. Big enough to read from the water.

Odile's heart went from slow to hammering in the space between two breaths.

She rolled out of her bedroll and put her knife at his throat before the tinder caught properly, and the flame guttered in the sudden motion of his flinch, and for three or four seconds everything hung — the spark dying, the man frozen, her blade trembling not from fear but from the effort of not using it.

"You treacherous little—"

"Careful," Pellam managed, his voice thin under the steel. "Careful, miss. There's men already moving. I lit the first signal an hour past, down the slope. What you've stopped is only the confirmation. They'll come either way."

It was the worst thing he could have said, because it was exactly the kind of thing a cornered liar would say, and because some cold part of her believed it anyway.

Ansel was on his feet within moments, moving through the dark with the economy of a man who had slept in worse places than ruined towers. He didn't ask questions first. He took Pellam by the collar and shook him once, hard, the way you shake a lock that won't open.

"How many?"

"Skiff party. Eight, maybe ten. Scouts, not marines — Corvane doesn't waste marines on mudflats." Pellam's grin came back, that awful easy grin, deployed now like a weapon. "Ranger, listen to me. I did you a kindness. The admiral means to burn whatever you're carrying and everyone carrying it. Amnesty, that's all I asked. My name struck from the deserter rolls. I've got a sister in Harrowgate they'd have pressed into service over it. You'd have done the same."

"I would not," Ansel said, "have done the same."

And Odile, crouched in the dark with her knife still bare, thought: *no, he wouldn't. That's the whole trouble with him.*

There was no time to argue about it. Down the slope, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Odile had grown up knowing better than her own heartbeat — oars in locks, muffled, wrapped in cloth to kill the knock. Someone was crossing the shallows. Someone was coming quiet.

"Back way," she said. "There isn't one."

"There's a drop," Ansel said. "Behind the tower. The escarpment shelf. It's forty feet to the scree."

"It's sixty," Odile said, "and the scree is loose."

"Then we climb carefully."

* * *

They went out through the doorway on the landward side and around the base of the wall, Pellam dragged between them with his wrists bound by his own belt, and Odile hated every second of bringing him — hated that leaving him trussed for the scouts to find felt like murder and taking him along felt like folly, and hated most of all that she couldn't tell which it actually was. The cliff face behind the tower was black limestone, slick with spray off the flats, cut with ledges wide enough for a boot and nothing more. Ansel went first with the rope. Odile followed with the chest strapped across her back, its weight pulling at her shoulders like a second person clinging to her.

That was the thing about the chest, she had learned. You didn't carry it. It carried you — backward, downward, into the past, into her grandmother's kitchen with the salt-stained maps pinned above the stove. Every step down the cliff, the strap dug into her collarbone and she thought: *this is what inheritance weighs.*

Halfway down, the skiff party reached the tower.

They heard them before they saw them — boots on stone above, a lantern hooded and unhooded, a voice calling down into the dark with the flat courtesy of official business: *"Ranger Ravensworth! By order of Admiral Corvane, stand and be recognized!"*

Ansel pressed himself flat against the rock. Odile did the same, cheek to cold stone, the chest grinding painfully between her spine and the cliff. Above them the lantern light swept the ledge they'd just vacated, caught the abandoned bedrolls, the dead fire, the place where Pellam's tinder had scattered.

Then the light dropped lower. Toward them.

There was a moment — Odile would remember it all her life, the way you remember the exact shape of a wave before it breaks — when the beam slid down the cliff face and stopped one arm's length short of Ansel's shoulder, held there, trembling slightly, as the man holding it squinted into the dark trying to decide whether the shadows below were rock or people.

It was Pellam who decided it for them. Whether from spite or panic or some twisted notion of self-preservation, he shouted — a raw, cracking yell of *"Down here! They're on the rock!"* — and the night tore open.

Lanterns everywhere. A crossbow bolt sparked off the stone a hand's width from Odile's ear and went singing away into the dark. Ansel swore and let the rope run free, and they dropped the last twenty feet in a controlled fall that wasn't controlled at all, landing in the scree in a cascade of sliding stone. Odile hit shoulder-first, felt the chest slam against her back, heard — distinctly, horribly — the crack of old wood flexing past its tolerance.

No time. Boots above, voices, more bolts. They ran.

The flats at night were a country of mirrors — thin water over hardpan, reflecting the stars so completely that running through it felt like running across the sky. Behind them the tower blazed suddenly with lantern-light, figures swarming its base. Ahead there was nothing but the silver plain and the distant black line of the channel brush, and Odile ran toward it with her lungs full of fire and her mind full of one thought, repeating like a pulse: *the manifest, the manifest, check the manifest.*

They didn't stop until the brush swallowed them, a quarter mile of willow and salt-cedar dense enough to break up their silhouettes. Ansel called a halt with a hand on her arm, both of them bent double, breathing like bellows. Somewhere behind, the pursuit had stalled at the tree line — skiff parties didn't like country they couldn't see across, and the fleet's discipline ended where the mud began.

It was only then, in the pitch dark under the willows, that Odile opened the chest.

Her fingers found the lid wrong immediately. It sat crooked. One hinge had torn half free, and the seam gaped where the wood had split along a grain-line some flood had already weakened years ago. Inside, the oilcloth wrapping was soaked through.

"No," she said. She said it quietly, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "No, no, no—"

She pulled the manifest out into the meager starlight filtering through the branches. The verse-manifest — her grandmother's song made paper, the cargo list written in verse, the map to the strongroom, the proof, the whole reason any of them had walked a single mile of this cursed country — the page was dark with water. Not ruined, maybe. Not yet. But the ink had begun to bloom at the edges of the letters, each word growing a soft grey halo like a bruise spreading under skin.

She knelt there in the mud holding it the way you hold a wounded bird, and the fury came up through her like floodwater.

"He told them." Her voice came out low and shaking. "He stood in that tower and smiled at us and ate our food and told them exactly where we slept. I *watched* his eyes, Ansel. I watched them flick to the chest and I said nothing, because I thought — I thought a man who'd deserted the fleet must hate them enough—"

"People don't betray you out of hatred," Ansel said. He was beside her, wringing water from his hair, and his voice had the terrible calm of a man conserving everything for later. "They betray you out of a sister in Harrowgate. Out of amnesty. Out of being tired. Hatred's rare. Tiredness is cheap."

"We lost the tides." She was doing the arithmetic even as she said it, and the numbers were ugly. Two days lost nursing his fever, spent like a drunk spends coin. Now this night, this chase, this split and soaking chest. "We had six. We have two. Maybe. If the river falls the way the trader said—"

"Then we stop losing things." Ansel crouched and took the wet page from her hands with a gentleness that undid her more than any comfort could have. He turned it toward the sky, reading what could still be read. "The verses are memorized. Yours are. You sang them to me at the waystation, and again on the trail, and again last night when you thought I was asleep. The paper is evidence. The paper is *not* the truth. The truth is in you, and no one can soak that."

"And if I'm wrong?" she whispered. "If I misremember one line? One line, Ansel, and the strongroom stays shut, and Corvane burns the ledger, and my grandmother's people stay drowned twice — once in the water and once in the record—"

"Then we get the paper dry," he said simply. "Both things. We dry the page and we carry the song. Belt and braces."

From the darkness beyond the willows came a sound that froze them both: a whistle. Three notes, rising. Then, after a pause, three notes again — the same, patient, methodical, moving parallel to the tree line. Search pattern. The scouts hadn't given up. They were herding.

And then a second whistle answered the first, from further north, and Odile understood with a lurch that there were two parties, and that the second had not come from the tower at all.

"They signaled the fleet," she breathed. "Not just the skiffs. Pellam signaled the whole anchorage. That's why the whistles are multiplying. They're putting a net across the flats."

Ansel was silent a moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — quieter, harder, the voice of a man stepping off a ledge he can't step back onto.

"The deserter. Where is he?"

Odile's stomach dropped. In the scramble down the cliff, in the bolt-storm and the running, in the blind flight through the brush — the belt-bound man had been between them, and then he simply hadn't been. Cut loose or slipped loose, she couldn't say. Either way, Pellam Wick was gone into the dark, and every whistle on the flats might be him now, guiding them, earning his amnesty one note at a time.

"He'll tell them about the fever," she said. "About your wound. About how slow we are. He knows everything, Ansel. We gave him everything."

"Then we give him nothing else." Ansel rose, and she saw him wince as his rope-cut leg took his weight, and she saw him refuse to let the wince become a limp. "North through the brush until it thins, then east along the channel — the deep channel, where their skiffs can't follow at low water. We walk all night. We dry this page at dawn against the hot stones past the bend, and we sing the verses until you can recite them backwards, and we do not sleep in the same place twice ever again."

He paused. Looked down at her, at the split chest cradled in her arms like a broken thing that was also, somehow, still alive.

"Odile. Say the first verse. Right now. While they're whistling. Prove to yourself it's still there."

She closed her eyes. The whistles went on in the dark, patient as tide. And inside her head, clear as her grandmother's kitchen, the song rose:

*Count the boats, my love, count them slow,*  
*the river keeps what kings let go;*  
*what sank in silence, sealed in lead,*  
*the water writes, the water said—*

"It's there," she whispered, and opened her eyes, and found him watching her with an expression she was beginning to know better than fear itself.

"Then they haven't taken anything," Ansel said. "Come on. We've got two tides and a head start they think is bigger than it is."

They moved north through the willows, silent as the drowned, while behind them the whistles multiplied across the flats like the first drops of a storm that had finally, irrevocably, broken.
