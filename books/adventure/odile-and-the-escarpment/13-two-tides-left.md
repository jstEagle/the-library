## 13. Two Tides Left

The river was dying by inches.

Ansel saw it first as a change in the light — the water that had run brown and muscular beside them for two days now lay flat and pale in the morning, spread thin over mud that had been riverbed a week ago. Stakes of old pilings stood exposed like the ribs of something long swallowed. A dead barge, hull split and furred with weed, sat high and dry where the current had abandoned it, and gulls were already picking at what the recession had left behind.

"Look at it," Odile said quietly. She crouched at the waterline, touched the wet silt, and rubbed her fingers together. "Two days ago this was under four feet of water. By tomorrow's low tide you'll be able to walk to the middle of the channel without wetting your knees."

"Then we walk faster."

She looked up at him, and he saw she'd done the same arithmetic he had, and reached the uglier answer. "Walking faster won't matter if the wreck sits behind six feet of drying mud when we get there. We needed the water high enough to dive. Every hour the river drops, the dive gets shallower — until there's no dive at all. Just a hull sitting in a field, with the fleet's whole company standing around it."

Ansel said nothing. He took out the map he'd copied from the waystation wall — his own hand, ink smudged, the escarpment country rendered in the careful strokes of a man trained to draw terrain he might have to fight across. The safe route followed the old towpath along the western bank: firm ground, good footing, two days' walk to the wreck site below the cliffs. It was also the route any sensible pursuer would watch, and the fleet had scouts on it already; they'd seen the smoke of their cookfires the night before, three separate fires strung along the valley like beads.

There was another line on the map. The cartographer who'd drawn it had marked it with a broken stroke and a single word: *quicksands*. It cut straight across the widening mouth of the flats where the river braided into a dozen dying channels — a shortcut that would save them the better part of a day and a night, if the mud let them cross.

He'd been looking at that broken line since dawn.

* * *

They argued about it the way they argued about everything now — quickly, quietly, and without either of them pretending they didn't already know how it would end.

"The towpath," Odile said. "We go around. We lose half a day, we still arrive before the fleet can bring up divers."

"We don't know that. Corvane's outriders found another route once already." He tapped the map. "And after last night he knows exactly where we are and exactly where we're going. He doesn't need divers waiting at the wreck, Odile. He needs one boat at the mouth of the channel and patience. The river is doing his work for him."

"So we cross the flats." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "The sinking kind. The kind my grandmother used to scare children with. *The mud that drinks.*"

"I've crossed ground like it before. Up north, on the Merrow flats, when I was a green ranger. You test every step. You keep moving. You don't stop for anything." He met her eyes. "It's not a gamble if you do it right. It's just slow, and ugly, and terrifying."

"That sounds like most of the last month."

"Yes," Ansel said. "It does."

She laughed — short, sharp, real — and shouldered the chest. The verse-manifest inside it was wrapped now in oilcloth, triple-wrapped, dried as best they could manage and guarded like a relic, which was what it was. Her hair was still damp from the night raid, from the river, from all of it, and there was a bruise coming up yellow-green along her jaw where a fleet man's elbow had caught her in the scramble. Ansel had catalogued that bruise with a fury that had surprised him, and he put the fury away now, because fury was heavy and they were traveling light.

"All right," she said. "The mud that drinks. Lead on, ranger."

* * *

The flats began an hour later, and they were worse than the map promised.

What had looked from the ridge like open ground was actually a lattice of channels — some dry-crusted, some holding a hand's depth of brackish water, all of them underlaid with silt that went down farther than any pole could sound. The surface lied constantly. Ground that held Ansel's weight would swallow Odile's next step to the ankle. Crust that looked solid rippled like the skin of a drum when he pressed his boot into it. Twice they backtracked entire channels, and the sun climbed, and the wind came off the escarpment carrying the smell of salt and rot and old wood, and Ansel tested the ground ahead of them with a cut-down tent pole, step by step, the way a blind man reads a street.

"Left here," he said. "Along the dead tree. Not past it — the ground past it gave twice."

"You've said 'gave twice' eleven times," Odile murmured behind him. "I'm keeping count. It helps."

"It shouldn't help."

"It absolutely helps. Fear shared is fear halved. My grandmother again."

"Your grandmother," Ansel said, easing his weight onto a shelf of clay that held, barely, "had a saying for everything."

"She had a saying for everything because everything kept happening to us." There was no bitterness in it — or rather, the bitterness had been worn smooth, like sea glass, into something she could carry. "That's what survival is, eventually. A very long list of sayings."

He glanced back at her. She was moving well, better than he'd expected — light-footed, patient, distributing her weight the way he'd shown her without being shown twice. She'd learned fast since the garrison, since the wash, since the chest. Or maybe, he thought, she'd always known how to move through hostile country, and everything before this had simply been practice.

The trouble came at midday, at the third great channel, where the map's broken line bent north and the ground simply stopped pretending.

It was fifty yards of open ooze, gray-brown and gleaming, pocked with gas vents that sighed small bubbles as they watched. No crust. No islands. On the far side, a bar of firm sand rose maybe two feet above the mire, and beyond it the braided channels continued toward the dark line of the escarpment.

Ansel walked the edge of it twice, probing. The pole went down two feet, three, four, and kept going. In the middle distance something — a log, a spar, possibly a body — protruded at an angle, black and slick, the only fixed point visible anywhere in the waste.

"There," he said. "The spar. If it holds, it's a bridge. If it doesn't—"

"Don't finish that."

"—then we come back and go around, and Corvane wins by a tide." He looked at the crossing, then at her, and the thing he'd been carrying since the night raid rose in him and would not be put away this time. "Odile."

She heard it in his voice. She stopped adjusting the chest strap and looked at him.

"If it goes wrong out there," he said, "it goes wrong fast. Mud doesn't give you time. And I need you to hear me on this, because I've thought about it all morning and I'm going to say it once and then never again." His voice came out lower than he intended. "You could turn back. Right now. Take the towpath alone, move at night, you're good enough to lose anyone they send. The manifest is memorized in your head — every verse, every name. The chest is just lead and wood. Walk away with your life, and your family's claim survives you even if nothing else does. That's the smart play. That's the play I'd order any ranger under my command to make."

The wind moved across the flats and the gas vents sighed, and for a long moment she said nothing at all.

Then: "Is that what you want? For me to go?"

"No," Ansel said. It came out of him plain and unarmored, and he let it. "I have never wanted anything less. But wanting has gotten people killed on ground like this, and I am not going to dress my wanting up as tactics. So I'm asking. Once. Walk away, live, and win anyway. Or come with me across that, knowing what it is."

Odile set the chest down in the crusted mud at the edge — carefully, the way she handled everything that mattered — and stepped close to him, close enough that he could see the salt dried white in the creases of her knuckles.

"My grandmother drowned," she said. "Not in Vessia — she was born after, in the camps at Marrow's Bend. But she drowned anyway, slowly, her whole life, in debt and shame and other people's certainty that her nation had never existed. She taught me the verses so that someone would remember the names. She died before I understood what she'd given me." Her voice didn't shake. That was somehow worse than shaking. "I spent ten years stealing so my family could buy papers saying they were allowed to exist. Papers. From the men whose grandfathers opened the sluice gates. And you're asking me to walk away now — now, when the proof is in a box between us, when the water is finally, finally going low enough to show the world what they did—" She stopped. Breathed. "No, Ansel. I won't take your smart play. I'll take the mud."

"Against all prudence."

"*Especially* against prudence. Prudence is how nations drown people politely." She picked the chest back up. "Besides. You'd follow me anyway. I've seen how you look at my back when I walk ahead of you. It's very unsubtle."

A laugh broke out of him — startled, rough, the first real one in days — and with it something else broke loose too, something he'd been holding behind discipline and duty and the memory of a dead captain's face. He took her free hand, mud and salt and all, and said it plainly, because she had gone first into the truth and he would not be the one to flinch from it.

"I love you," Ansel said. "I've loved you since the toll bridge, probably since the wash, and I told myself it was admiration, and then that it was gratitude, and then that it was the fever talking. It isn't any of those things. I love you the way I used to love the law — completely, and without a backup plan. And I am going to get you across this mud, and to that wreck, and through whatever Corvane has left, because a life where you walk away from me isn't a life I'm interested in saving."

The flats were silent around them except for the sighing of the vents and the far-off cry of gulls working the receding water.

"Well," Odile said at last, and her voice had gone bright and unsteady in a way he'd never heard it, not on the rope, not in the chapel, not once. "That's the second-most reckless thing anyone's said to me this year."

"What's the first?"

"*Marry into my family and meet my mother.* But yours has better timing." She squeezed his hand hard, once, and let go, and turned to face the gray waste. "All right, ranger. Show me the spar."

* * *

The crossing took two hours and cost them nearly everything.

The first twenty yards held — crust over firmer clay, the pole finding purchase, their steps timed to each other's like a slow dance. Then the crust failed without warning, all at once, in a patch the size of a door, and Ansel went in to his thigh before he could throw himself sideways onto his belly, arms spread, weight distributed, heart slamming. The mud pulled at his leg with a slow, patient strength, like a hand that had all the time in the world.

"Don't move," Odile said. She was already flat, crawling forward along the crust, the chest strapped tight to her back. "Don't pull. Give me the pole."

She worked the pole under his chest, levered, and he came free with a sound like a kiss and a smell like the bottom of the world, and they lay side by side on the trembling ground breathing hard while somewhere beneath them the flats settled and sighed.

"That," Ansel managed, "is why we test every step."

"That is why," she agreed, "we never speak of this to my mother."

They reached the spar at last — black wood, iron-hard, some ancient mooring post driven deep in the years before the flood — and crossed the worst of it hand over hand along its length, the ooze reaching hungrily for their boots and missing. On the far bar of sand they collapsed, filthy to the eyebrows, and Odile laughed until she had to hold her ribs, and Ansel found himself laughing too, the unhinged laughter of people who have paid a toll and lived.

From the top of the bar, he looked back the way they'd come.

The flats stretched south, gray and gleaming and indifferent. And far off, at the edge of sight, where the towpath ran along the western valley — movement. Boats being portaged. Men. The glint of brass. Even at this distance he knew what he was seeing, because he'd served under officers who thought exactly that way: Corvane wasn't following their route anymore. He was cutting the corner, spending his crews' lives on the same arithmetic Ansel had spent theirs on.

"He's crossing too," Ansel said. "Somewhere south of us. He knows the river's dropping faster than his ships can sail."

Odile followed his gaze, and her laughter died, but she didn't look afraid. She looked like a woman reading a tide table.

"Then we've got until he lands," she said, "and no longer. Come on, my love. One more day of walking, and the water will tell us everything."

They came down off the sandbar and north toward the escarpment, two small figures on the vast draining face of the world — and behind them, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind off the flats, came the first thin note of a fleet whistle, answering another, much closer than it should have been.
