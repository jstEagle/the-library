## 15. One Tide

The river told us before the sky did.

We came down off the mudflats at first light, and where yesterday the water had lapped gray and sullen against the escarpment's drowned foot, this morning it had pulled back a full arm's length, leaving a rim of dark stone like the mark a cup leaves on a table. Odile saw it first. She stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into her back, and she pointed with one cracked finger and said nothing at all.

She didn't need to. We had sung our way here — or she had sung and I had carried — through two nights of verse and half-remembered tides, and every line of her grandmother's song had been a bearing, every refrain a depth. *Where the chapel bell sleeps under sand, count three hands down to the fleet's cold hand.* The chapel was behind us now, its bell tower a black tooth breaking the shallows a mile upstream. Which meant we were standing above it.

The wreck of the *Vessia's Mercy* lay beneath our feet, under maybe four feet less water than it had held when Corvane's fleet anchored downstream.

"One tide," Odile said. Her voice was hoarse from singing, from the salt air, from everything. "Maybe two if the river argues."

I looked at my side. The rope-cut wound from the escarpment path had closed to a pink seam, but it pulled when I breathed deep, and the fever had only broken two days ago. My body felt like a borrowed coat, too big in some places and too tight in others. Odile followed my gaze and her jaw set in that way I had learned meant an argument was coming.

"Don't," I said.

"You haven't even heard what I was going to say."

"You were going to say you'd dive instead. You can't swim the deep channel and you know it. Your grandmother's people were mountain folk before they were river folk." I said it gently, because it was true, and because I wanted the last kindness between us to be true rather than tender. "It has to be me."

* * *

We spent the morning preparing the way divers prepare for war: slowly, and with more ritual than sense. Odile built a fire from driftwood and fed it the driest scraps of our remaining stores until we had a bed of coals, and over those coals she heated stones wrapped in cloth, the way her grandmother had taught her for the breath-holding rites — though whether the warmth truly kept the blood loose or only kept the diver brave, neither of us could say. She rubbed rendered fat into the seam of my wound until I stopped flinching, and then, without asking, she took my ranger's badge cord from around my neck and tied it around her own wrist.

"For finding your way back," she said. "The river takes what isn't held onto."

"That's not how the badge works."

"It's how it works today."

I laughed, and the laugh hurt, and I decided that was fair payment.

The water was colder than the season should have allowed. That was the first wrongness, standing waist-deep on the wreck's exposed rail — the old galley ship had settled on its beam ends centuries ago, and its hull made a long black spine through the shallows, furred with weed and crusted with salt-bloom. The water running over it came down from the high snows, and it carried the deep cold of places the sun had never argued with. I breathed until my lungs burned, then breathed past it, the way Captain Vey had drilled into me a lifetime ago on a different river. Three breaths to fill. One to empty. Then down.

The world went green and silent.

I had dived wrecks before. Salvage work was honest ranger labor in the wet season — pulling drowned cargo from the crown's rivers, cataloguing it, surrendering it to men who signed papers in warm rooms. I knew the grammar of it: the pressure closing like a fist, the light going from gold to pewter to a bruised nothing, the ears popping their small complaints. What I did not know — what no training prepares a man for — is what it is to descend into a grave that someone dug on purpose.

The *Mercy* lay on her side, and her decks, which should have been chaos — spilled cargo, scattered bones, the honest wreckage of disaster — were orderly. Too orderly. Cargo had been lashed down before she went under. Hatches had been dogged shut from the outside. And along the length of her hull, where the weed had peeled away in the falling water, I saw the marks I would spend the rest of my life trying to unsee: chisel-work, deliberate and recent by the standards of ruin, cutting the Vessian seal out of her stern boards. Someone had come down here, decades after the flooding, and scraped her name off her own body.

My chest was beginning to burn. I kicked along the hull toward the stern, following the song in my head — Odile had made me sing it back to her a dozen times on the mudflats until the verses lived in me like marrow — *count the ports from the broken mast, seven doors to the strongroom fast.* The masts were gone, but their stumps remained, and I counted hatches as I swam: rusted iron mouths, all of them sealed, all of them bolted. Seven.

The seventh hatch was different. It was smaller, set low near the keel line, and it bore no lock at all — only a recessed ring of bronze worked into the shape of a wave cresting over a star. The Vessian seal, whole where every other had been defaced. As if whoever had scraped the ship clean had either missed this one or had not dared to touch it.

I hooked my fingers into the ring and pulled. Nothing. Pulled again, boots braced against the hull, and felt something shift — not the hatch but inside it, a counterweight dropping somewhere in the dark, old grease giving up its grip after a century of patience. The hatch swung inward with a groan I felt in my teeth more than heard, and the water inside the strongroom was black as ink, and my lungs were screaming, and I went in anyway.

* * *

I found it by touch. There was no light down there worth the name — only the pale suggestion of the hatch behind me, a moon seen through dirty glass. My hands walked across shelving, across shapes wrapped in oilcloth gone to leather, across the smooth curve of sealed jars whose contents shifted like sleeping things. And then, lashed to a bracket beneath the lowest shelf, a flat case of lead-lined wood, heavier than it had any right to be, stamped with the same wave-and-star.

I tore it free. The lashings crumbled. And my vision was going gray at the edges, that soft creeping curtain that means the body has begun to spend its final coin, when my fingers found the case's seam and felt, wedged into it, a folded packet of oilskin — and inside the oilskin, paper. Real paper. Dry. A hundred years underwater and dry as a sermon, because someone had planned for this room to be opened by someone who deserved to read what was in it.

I kicked for the light.

* * *

I came up coughing, and Odile's hands were under my arms before I finished coughing, hauling me over the rail onto the black spine of the hull, and she was shouting my name and I was laughing, which frightened her worse than the coughing had.

"Ansel — Ansel, breathe, you idiot, breathe—"

"I'm breathing." I rolled onto my back on the weed-slick planking, the case clutched to my chest like a child, the sky wheeling overhead, blue and enormous and impossibly far away. "Odile. It's there. It's all there."

She knelt over me, dripping, her badge-corded wrist pressed hard against my pulse as if she could hold my heart to its work by main force. "What did you see?"

"Help me up. Not here." I looked downstream, toward the bend where the fleet's masts would appear if the wind turned kind to them. "Not on the water. Up on the bank. Where we can run."

* * *

We climbed to a shelf of rock above the tideline, and there, with the river muttering below us and the escarpment throwing its long morning shadow across the shallows, we opened the case.

The second half of the manifest was exactly what her grandmother's verses had promised — the same verse-form, the same cipher of songs, the tally of the *Mercy*'s true cargo completed at last. But it was the other thing in the case that stopped the world.

A ledger. Bound in government blue, stamped with the crown's quartermaster mark, and — I knew this binding, I had handled a hundred like it in garrison records offices — a sluice authority's log. The official record of the great weir gates on the upper river.

I turned the pages with hands that would not stay steady.

The entries began eleven days before the flooding of Vessia. Routine readings. Gate pressures. And then, dated the fourth day before the drowning, an order in a clerk's neat hand: *By direction of the Crown Salvage Commission, gates three through nine to be held at full closure through the spring melt, contrary to seasonal protocol. Rationale: acceleration of scheduled inundation. Compensation schedules for affected districts deferred to commission discretion.*

Affected districts. I read the word twice, three times, hunting for some other meaning in it, some clerical accident. There was none. They had held the gates shut. They had let the meltwater pile up behind the weirs until it had nowhere to go but over the low country — over the chapels, the orchards, the bell towers, the names — because a drowned valley was cheaper than a bought one, and because the salvage rights to a sunken city could be claimed outright, whereas the salvage rights to a living one had to be negotiated with the living.

And at the bottom of the order, in ink that had faded to the color of old tea, a signature.

I had seen that signature before. I had seen it every day of my childhood, on the flyleaf of the navigation manual my grandfather had left me, the one I had learned my rivers from. *Elias Ravensworth, Commissioner, Crown Salvage.*

"Ansel." Odile's voice came from very far away. "You've gone white. What is it?"

I couldn't answer. My grandfather — the man whose compass I still carried, whose steady hand I had measured my whole life against, the reason I had taken the commission at all, the debt I owed my dead captain and the dead man who had made the captain possible — had signed the order that killed her grandmother's world. Had signed it in a neat clerk's hand, eleven days of entries, initialing each gate reading like a man balancing a household account.

The ledger lay open between us, and the river ran below us, and somewhere in that water were the names carved in the drowned chapel, and among them, I knew now without looking, was hers. And the hand that had put the water there shared my blood.

"Odile," I said, and my voice sounded like a stranger's. "There's something you have to—"

A whistle cut across the shallows. One note, high and thin, from the downstream bend — the fleet's signal for *sighted*, the same note I had blown myself a thousand times on patrol. And around the bend, unhurried, certain, came the leading longboats of Admiral Corvane's net, oars flashing, coming for the wreck while the water still stood shallow enough to reach it.

Odile snatched the ledger to her chest. I got my legs under me, the wound tearing hot along its seam, and the last thing I saw before we ran was the admiral's pennant rising over the bend like a black bird deciding where to land.
