## 16. Corvane's Net

They came out of the morning mist like a judgment.

I saw the masts first — a forest of them, rising where an hour ago there had been only grey water and the ribs of the wreck breaking the surface like the fingers of a drowned hand. Then the hulls beneath, black and barnacled, sliding into the shallows with the patience of things that had never once needed to hurry. Six ships. Maybe seven. The fleet had not needed six tides after all. It had needed only to let us do the diving for it.

"Odile." Ansel's voice came from below me, tight. He was waist-deep in the river, still dripping from the strongroom, one arm clamped around the oilskin bundle pressed to his chest. "Tell me you see them."

"I see them." My mouth had gone dry as the salt flats where all of this began. "How many oars do we have left?"

"One. Broken."

The water around him shivered with the wake of the lead ship's anchor chain paying out. Somewhere above us, a voice called orders in the flat cadence of the fleet — *lines away, lines away* — and I understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like calm, that we had never been racing the fleet at all. We had been carrying its prize straight to its door.

I waded down the bank to him. The ledger was wrapped in sailcloth against his chest, bound twice with cord, and beneath the oilskin I could see the dark stain of old water that no amount of sun would ever fully dry. Half a manifest from a chest the river gave up. Half a manifest sung to us by my grandmother's ghost through every verse I'd ever learned at her knee. And now this — the sluice-gate orders, the crown's own hand, signed and dated and sealed. Proof that Vessia had not drowned. Vessia had been *drowned*.

"They're lowering boats," Ansel said.

"I can count, ranger."

"Ex-ranger." He almost smiled. It didn't reach his eyes. "If they wanted us dead, we'd be dead. This is Corvane. He'll want to talk first."

"Why?"

"Because talking is cheaper than killing, and because he doesn't yet know what we have." Ansel looked down at the bundle. "He thinks he knows. There's a difference, and it might be the only weapon we have left."

The first longboat grounded on the shingle twenty paces from us, and marines poured out of it in their grey-and-white, forming two ranks with the ease of men who had done this a hundred times on a hundred shores. A gangway came down from the lead ship. And down it, unhurried, came Admiral Corvane.

I had imagined him wrong. I had pictured a butcher — thick-necked, loud, some man built for burning villages. Instead he was spare and silver-templed, with the careful hands of a clerk and eyes the colour of shallow water over sand. He wore no sword. He didn't need one. He walked across the shingle as though the whole riverbank were already his, stopped at a courteous distance, and inclined his head.

"Ansel Ravensworth." His voice was mild. That was the worst of it. "And the thief Odile Vessari." My blood went cold at the name — my grandmother's name, the true one, the one I hadn't spoken aloud since I was nine years old. He watched me flinch and noted it, the way a clerk notes a figure in a column. "Yes. We know who you are. We've known for some time. It made finding you considerably easier."

"You've been reading our letters," I said.

"We've been reading everyone's letters. It's a large country." He folded his careful hands behind his back. "Ravensworth. I'm going to make you an offer, and I want you to understand that I make it sincerely, because I remember your captain, and I know what his death cost you, and I know what the commission has meant to you since. A man without family needs something to belong to. You belonged to us."

Ansel said nothing. Water ran off him onto the stones.

"The offer is this." Corvane's tone did not change; it simply became more precise, the way a blade becomes more precise when it is being sharpened. "You surrender the ledger and the manifest. In exchange: full reinstatement, back pay to the date of your disappearance, formal erasure of the debt you carry under Captain Meric's name — all of it, Ravensworth, every coin, struck from the rolls. And the woman walks free. No charges. Her family's passage papers issued under fleet seal, safe conduct to any port she names." He paused. "You would testify that you went rogue alone. That she coerced you. It will read badly for a season, and then it will read like nothing at all, because nothing at all is how the crown prefers its history."

I heard my own heartbeat. I heard the river moving against the wreck's exposed hull. I heard, distantly, the creak of seven ships at anchor, patient as vultures.

"And if he refuses?" I asked.

Corvane turned those shallow-water eyes on me, and for just a moment the clerk fell away and I saw the thing underneath, the thing that had ordered sluice-gates opened on sleeping towns. "Then the ledger goes into the fire, the manifest goes into the fire, and the two of you go into the river, and by autumn no one will remember there was ever anything in these waters but fish." He smiled faintly. "But I don't think it will come to that. Rangers are practical men. It's why we recruit them poor."

* * *

They gave us an hour. They actually gave us an hour — marines at twenty paces, boats holding position in the shallows, Corvane withdrawing to his flagship to await an answer, as though this were a negotiation between equals rather than a net drawn closed. Perhaps he believed it was. Perhaps a man who drowns nations has to believe in the forms even after he's abandoned the substance.

We sat against the wreck's hull, in the shadow of its broken sterncastle, and the wood was warm from the sun and smelled of tar and eighty years of salt.

"He'll keep the papers part," I said. "That part's real. He wants you to believe the whole offer is real so you'll take the poisoned half."

"I know."

"He'll erase your debt and then he'll erase you. Quietly. Next winter. Some garrison posting up-country, some fever, some accident on a night patrol." I pulled my knees up. "You should take it anyway."

Ansel turned his head and looked at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not the lonely resolve from the flats, not the guilt from the rangers on the road, not even the raw devotion of the mudflats two days ago when he told me, plainly, stupidly, wonderfully, that he loved me against all prudence. This was quieter than all of those. This was a man finishing an arithmetic he'd started a long time ago.

"Say that again," he said.

"You should take it. Your debt dies, your name lives, I get my family out—"

"That's not the part I mean." He shook his head slowly. "You said *you should take it*. Not *we*. Two weeks ago you'd have said *we*, because two weeks ago you were still spending trust like counterfeit coin, giving me just enough to keep me walking beside you. Now you're trying to save me alone." He exhaled. "When did that happen?"

"On the rope," I said, before I could stop myself. "On the escarpment, when you held the whole weight of both of us on one arm and told me about your grandfather's signature. Nobody holds a person like that and then sells them." I looked out at the grey water. "I've stolen a lot of things, Ansel. I'm not going to steal your life for you by pretending it's a gift."

He was quiet for a while. Down the beach, the marines stood like fence posts. Gulls argued over something in the wrack line.

"My grandfather signed the evacuation order," Ansel said at last. "I've been carrying that since the chapel. And I kept thinking — the debt I owe isn't really Meric's. Meric's dead; debts die with the dead. The debt I owe is older than that. It's the same debt the crown owes every Vessian name carved in that chapel wall, and I've spent eleven years collecting on the wrong account." He touched the oilskin bundle. "This is the right account. This is the whole ledger — theirs and mine. If I hand it to Corvane, I don't clear my debt. I cosign it."

"So what do we do?" I asked. "There are seven ships, Ansel. Seven. And us."

"There's one thing Corvane can't burn," he said. "No matter what he does to us."

"What?"

"An audience." He stood, and offered me his hand, and pulled me up beside him. "Look at those sailors, Odile. Look at their faces. Half of them have Vessian blood in them — Corvane recruits from the coast towns, the same towns your people scattered to. They've grown up on half-stories about the drowned fleet. Their grandmothers sang the same songs yours did." His grip tightened on my hand. "Corvane thinks paper is power. But paper only rules people who can't hear the truth spoken out loud."

* * *

They brought us before him at midday, on the shingle, with the assembled crews lining the rails of all seven ships — hundreds of faces, young and weathered and watching, because an admiral dispensing justice was better theatre than hauling anchor, and bored sailors will watch anything.

Corvane stood before a brazier one of his officers had lit — for warmth, ostensibly, though the day was mild, and I understood the brazier's true purpose a heartbeat before Ansel did. Fire for the ledger. Fire for the manifest. Fire as the last argument, held in reserve, glowing.

"Ravensworth," Corvane said. "Your answer."

Ansel walked forward until he stood close enough that I could see the pulse in the admiral's throat. Then he reached inside his coat — and the nearest marines tensed, hands going to hilts — and drew out not a weapon but a folded document, creased and sweat-stained and travel-worn. His commission. I had seen it a dozen times on the road; he touched it the way other men touch saints' medals.

"I carried this for eleven years," Ansel said, and his voice carried — the river helped, bouncing it off the water and up to the rails, and every sailor on every ship leaned in without meaning to. "It fed me when I had nothing. It gave me a place to sleep and a reason to get up and a dead man's name to live up to. I have been grateful for it every single day." He unfolded it. "And three days ago I stood in a flooded chapel and read my own grandfather's name on an order that drowned a nation, and I understood that this paper was never a badge. It was a payment. The crown paid me in belonging so I wouldn't ask what it had taken to buy my silence."

"Ravensworth—" Corvane began.

"You offered me erasure of my debt." Ansel raised the commission. "Here is my answer. I erase it myself."

He stepped to the brazier and fed the commission into the flames.

The sound the fleet made was not a gasp. It was quieter than a gasp and far more terrible — a collective intake of breath, seven ships' worth, the sound of several hundred people understanding at the same moment that something irreversible had just happened in front of them. The paper caught, curled, went orange at the edges. Ansel held it until the fire licked his fingers, then let the ashes go spinning out over the shingle.

"I am not a ranger," he said to the silent ships. "I am not a debtor. I am the grandson of Aldous Ravensworth, who signed the sluice-gate order of the ninth of Frostmonth, and I am here to return what his signature stole." He lifted the oilskin bundle high. "This is the crown's own hand, sailors! Its own seals, its own dates, its own orders — proof that Vessia was not lost to the river but given to it! Read it yourselves! Every one of you has a grandmother who sang the drowning songs — ask her which verses are true!"

"Seize them," Corvane said, sharp now, the clerk's mask finally cracking. "Both of them. Burn the—"

"No one moves."

The voice came from the rail of the flagship, and I knew it before I turned — the deserter, Tallis, the man who had sold us to the fleet for amnesty, standing among the officers with his amnesty papers still in his fist. His face was grey. He was looking down at the shingle, at the ashes of Ansel's commission drifting on the wind, and his jaw was working as though he were chewing on something too large to swallow.

"My grandmother was from Lowmark," he said, loudly, to no one and everyone. "She sang the second verse different from the way they teach it in the fleet hymnal. Different words. I always thought she'd just forgotten it proper." He crumpled the amnesty papers in his fist. "She hadn't forgotten anything."

Down the rails, one by one, sailors began to turn toward their officers. Not mutiny — not yet, not with blades — but something worse for Corvane than mutiny. Attention. Hundreds of pairs of eyes, suddenly awake, suddenly asking questions that had slept for eighty years.

Corvane saw it too. I watched him understand it — watched the shallow-water eyes go flat and cold as he realized the net had closed around the wrong fish. His hand moved toward the officer beside him, toward a whispered order, toward whatever last instrument he kept for days like this—

And that was when the first stone arced out from the lead ship's deck and shattered on the shingle ten feet from the admiral's boots.

The shattering of it broke something open in the fleet. A shout went up from the foretop — one voice, then five, then fifty, wordless at first and then not, and I heard my grandmother's verses rising raggedly from a hundred throats, garbled, half-remembered, *wrong* in a dozen details and unmistakably, eternally hers. The drowning songs. Sung by the grandchildren of the drowned, at the man who had drowned them.

Ansel's hand found mine in the chaos. His palm was burned where the commission had died, and he gripped me with it anyway.

"Tide's turning," he said.

And then the first marine rank broke, and Corvane drew the hidden blade from his belt, and everything happened at once.
