## 17. The Reading

The water had fallen so far overnight that the wreck of the *Vessia's Grace* lay bare along her spine like a whale beached a century ago and forgotten. Barnacled ribs of black oak rose from the mud in a long arc, and the morning sun caught them and turned them the color of old bronze. Ansel stood on the hull with his boots planted between two ribs, his wounded side screaming with every heartbeat, and watched the fleet come for them.

Nine ships. He counted them twice because he could not believe it the first time. Nine hulls sliding down the last deep channel, anchors dropping in a crescent that closed around the shallows like fingers around a throat. Longboats were already in the water — six of them, eight, more — oars flashing, marines in crown blue packed shoulder to shoulder, boarding hooks coiled at their feet like sleeping snakes.

"They brought everyone," Odile said beside him. Her voice was flat, which he had learned meant she was afraid. "Corvane didn't want witnesses left on the ships."

"He wanted no one to see what we found."

"We're standing on what they'll see." She looked down through a gap in the planking, where the strongroom gaped below, its door torn open by Ansel's crowbar two tides ago. The ledger was wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, strapped tight under her coat. The second half of the manifest was folded into her shirt, over her heart, where she'd kept her grandmother's songs all these years without knowing they were instructions.

The first longboats grounded in the mud fifty yards out. Marines spilled into the muck to their knees and began wading, and the sight of it — forty armed men floundering toward two people on a dead ship — should have been the end of every hope Ansel had carried since the flash flood gave up its chest. Instead he felt something stranger: clarity. The fever had burned a lot out of him. What remained was simple.

"Odile."

"I know."

"They'll take the ledger off your body if it comes to that."

"I know that too."

"So it can't come to that." He turned to face her fully. Below them the mud sucked and gulped around the advancing marines. Above them the sky was the pale washed blue of the dry season, merciless and clear. "You told me once that your grandmother sang the cargo lists to you before you could read. That the verses were the only way Vessian mothers kept what belonged to them when everything else was taken. Do you remember what you said after? In the waystation?"

She did not answer, but her chin came up a fraction, the way it always did when she was about to do something reckless.

"You said: *a thing sung is a thing owned by everyone who hears it.*"

"That's not law," she said. "That's just what my grandmother believed."

"It's better than law." Ansel looked past her, down the hull, to where the marines were close enough now that he could see their faces — young, sunburned, most of them conscripted from the river towns, from the very villages whose names were carved in the drowned chapel. "Corvane has nine ships and four hundred men. But he doesn't have this." He touched the folded paper over her heart, lightly, through the cloth. "Sing it."

* * *

They had talked about this, in the way people talk about things they hope never to need. On the mudflats, in the dark, while Ansel's wound festered and Odile kept him alive with river water and stubbornness, she had recited the manifest to him until he knew the opening lines himself. *Twelve holds of salt-cured timber, three of iron, one of glass.* It was a work song. It was designed to be remembered by exhausted people who had lost their books and their papers and nearly their language. The crown had written the original manifest as verse, centuries ago, because the salvage crews were illiterate — and then the crown had flooded the nation those crews built, and the verse had survived in the mouths of grandmothers while the ledgers rotted.

Odile climbed onto the highest rib of the wreck. She stood against the sky with the oilcloth-wrapped ledger held high in both hands, and Ansel saw her take one breath, the deep deliberate breath of a woman about to spend the only coin she had left.

Then she sang.

Her voice was not beautiful. Ansel had heard trained singers in the capital's opera house, funded by the same crown that had signed the sluice-gate orders, and none of them would have survived ten seconds in Odile's family. Her voice was rough and low and slightly sharp on the high notes, the voice of someone who had learned to sing over wind, over argument, over the noise of a household trying to stay alive. But it carried. It carried across the mud and the shallow water and into the longboats, and the strangest thing happened, which is that the marines stopped wading.

"*Twelve holds of salt-cured timber,*" she sang, "*three of iron, one of glass; the Grace rides heavy for the escarpment, pray the channel lets her pass.*"

Ansel stood at the base of the rib with his hand on his knife and watched the faces below. A marine in the second longboat — a big man with a rope scar across his jaw — went still with his oar half-dipped. His lips moved. Ansel realized, with a jolt that ran colder than the fever ever had, that the man knew the song. Not the words. The tune. Some version of it, worn smooth by generations, sung at funerals or festivals or over cradles, its meaning sanded away until only the melody remained — and here was the melody wearing its true clothes again, standing on the bones of the ship it described.

"*One hold sealed by the admiral's order, none may ask what lies inside; the crown's own seal upon the manifest, the crown's own hand upon the tide.*"

A voice from the boats, uncertain, half-swallowed: "What is that?"

"Vessian," said another voice. Older. "My gran used to hum it. I never knew—"

"It's a cargo list," someone else said, and laughed, and the laugh died quickly, because the next verse was not funny at all.

"*Three gates opened on the night of the flood, though the rain had barely begun; the order signed in the capital's ink, the order signed by Ravensworth's son.*"

Ansel felt that verse go through him like a blade finding the gap in armor. His grandfather's name. His own blood, set to music, sung across the water to four hundred strangers. He did not look away. He made himself stand there and be named.

The wading marines had stopped entirely now. They stood knee-deep in the mud, forty men, looking up at the wreck and at each other, and the officers in the lead boat were shouting — Ansel could hear Corvane's outrider captain bellowing *advance, advance, that's a direct order* — and the men were not advancing. They were listening. One of them, a boy who could not have been nineteen, had begun, unconsciously, to hum the harmony line. Odile's grandmother's harmony. The one she'd taught Ansel during the fever nights, saying *everyone knows this part, they just don't know they know it.*

"*And the families scattered east and south, with nothing but the songs they knew; and the songs held what the ledgers held, and the ledgers held what the crown must owe.*"

"Seize them!" The captain's voice cracked. "Cut the singer down! That's mutiny, that's treason, that's—"

"That's my grandmother's song," said the big marine with the rope scar. Loudly. Into the silence. He dropped his oar into the bottom of the boat, deliberately, where everyone could hear it land. "I don't know what's in your orders, Captain. But I know that tune. My mother sang it to me in Tarlow's Reach, and Tarlow's Reach was a Vessian village before it was anything else, and nobody ever told me why we lived in other people's houses."

Another oar came down. Then another. The sound of it — wood settling into boat-bottoms, one after another, arrhythmic, unstoppable, like rain beginning — was the most extraordinary sound Ansel had ever heard. He had spent his whole life believing that the law was a wall, load-bearing, and that the only choices were to uphold it or break it. He had crossed that line days ago and expected to feel damned. What he felt watching those sailors was something else entirely: the wall was not stone. It had never been stone. It was men standing shoulder to shoulder, and men could turn.

* * *

The captain got thirty of them moving again — the newest conscripts, the ones with no grandmother's songs, driven forward by threats and the animal fear of being seen to disobey. They reached the hull and began to climb, hooks biting into barnacled oak, and Ansel met the first man over the rail with the flat of his blade and a shove that sent him back into the mud. He was not trying to kill anyone. He understood, with the fevered lucidity that had carried him down the escarpment, that killing a sailor here would end everything the song had started. Every refusal would curdle into loyalty. Every doubt would harden into obedience. Corvane needed blood; blood would let him call it piracy instead of inheritance.

So Ansel fought like a man holding a door, not a man winning a battle. Shove, parry, shove. Beside him Odile had not stopped singing — her voice rawer now, cracking on the high notes, and somehow the cracks made it worse for the attackers, made it human, made it the voice of every grandmother they had ever failed to listen to.

"*Count the dead by the chapel stones, count the living by what they lost; the debt accrues at the rate of tides, and the crown will pay whatever the cost.*"

A marine halfway up the hull stopped climbing. Just stopped, hook in the wood, head bowed. The boy behind him swore at him, pulled at his boot, and the boy's own voice broke on the swear word, and he stopped too.

On the flagship, far out in the channel, Ansel saw movement at the rail — officers clustering, a signal flag running up, some new order being screamed into a speaking trumpet. He could not hear it over the singing, and he thought: *good*. Let every order drown in it. Let Corvane learn what it costs to give commands that cannot be heard over a lullaby.

The rope-scarred marine waded to the base of the wreck and stood there, unarmed now, looking up at Odile with an expression Ansel recognized because he had worn it himself at the dry wash, the day the chest surfaced: wonder cracking through suspicion, wonder winning. "The second verse," the man called up. "The one about the gates. Sing it again. Slower."

Odile sang it again. Slower. And this time other voices joined hers — ragged, uncertain, coming in late, finding the harmony line the way she'd promised Ansel everyone could — and the verse about the sluice gates rolled across the shallows in twenty throats, and the captain of the outriders stood alone in his longboat shouting orders that no one pretended to hear.

Ansel lowered his blade. There was no one left to fight. The marines who had climbed the hull were sitting on the ribs now, within arm's reach of the two people they'd been ordered to arrest, asking questions instead. *Is it true about the gates? Is the ledger real? Can I see it? My village — is my village on the list?*

Odile unwrapped the oilcloth. Her hands shook. She let the nearest sailor — the rope-scarred man, hauling himself up onto the hull — hold one edge of the ledger while she held the other, and together they turned it toward the light, and the crown's seal gleamed on the page like a brand.

"There," she said, and her voice was almost gone, worn down to thread. "Read it yourself. It was never mine to keep. It was never anyone's to steal. It's owed."

The rope-scarred man read. His lips moved. His face changed the way a coastline changes, slowly and then all at once, and when he looked up his eyes were wet and furious and awake.

"Boats!" he roared, turning, cupping his hands, roaring down at the fleet with a voice that had clearly commanded men before the crown bought it. "Every boat! Bring the officers' boats here! We're done taking orders down-channel — we're reading this out loud, all of it, every name, and every soul in this fleet is going to hear—"

The signal flag on the flagship dropped and ran up again: a different pattern, urgent, jagged. Ansel squinted against the glare and read it the way he'd been taught to read flags a lifetime ago, back when he still trusted what they stood for.

His stomach dropped.

"Odile," he said quietly. "Stop the reading for one breath. Look at the flagship."

She followed his gaze. The great ship's broadsides were running out — gun ports opening along her flank like a slow, terrible smile — and at her bow, small with distance but unmistakable, a figure in an admiral's coat stood at the rail with a speaking trumpet raised, and even across the water Ansel could hear the shape of the words:

"*By authority of the Crown, this vessel and all aboard are declared pirates. Any man who does not return to his station shares their sentence. You have until the third bell.*"

The singing stopped. Four hundred men hung between two loyalties, and the third bell began to toll.
