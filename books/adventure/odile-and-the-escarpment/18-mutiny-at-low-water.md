## 18. Mutiny at Low Water

The singing did not stop when the first sailor refused.

That was the thing Odile would remember afterward — not the shouting, not the steel, but the sound of a hundred voices carrying her grandmother's verses across the shallows while Admiral Corvane's authority came apart beneath him like wet rope. She stood on the exposed hull of the *Vessia's Grace*, the wreck listing out of the falling water like the ribcage of some drowned leviathan, and she sang, and the fleet listened.

She had not planned it that way. There had been no plan past Ansel burning his commission in front of the assembled crews — no plan past the look on Corvane's face as the ash of twenty years of service drifted down between them and settled on the mudflats like grey snow. After that there was only the ledger in Ansel's hands, the sluice-gate orders written in a chancellor's neat hand, and the choice Odile had made without knowing she was making it: to open her mouth and let the dead speak through her.

*Count the gates, count the rain,* her grandmother's voice said in her memory, in the old Vessian cadence that the crown had tried to burn out of three generations of survivors. *Count the boats that never came.*

The verses were a manifest. They had always been a manifest — cargo lists disguised as lullabies so they could be carried in the heads of the drowning, passed mother to daughter through the camps and the salt towns where paper rotted and tongues remembered. And half the sailors standing waist-deep in the shallows of the escarpment river had grandmothers too. Half of them had grown up hearing fragments of these same songs without knowing what they held.

A bosun near the lead longboat lowered his boarding pike. Not dropped — lowered, deliberately, the way a man sets down something he has decided never to carry again. Then the man beside him. Then three more.

"Pick them up," Corvane said. His voice carried the way voices carry from men who have spent decades making it carry. "That is an order. Pick up your arms."

Nobody moved. The water lapped against the hull. Somewhere downstream, a gull cried once and went silent, as if even the birds understood what was happening.

Odile kept singing. Her throat was raw from two days of salt wind and fever-watching, and her voice cracked on the high verse — the one about the chapel bells rung underwater by the current — but the crack didn't matter. A woman in the second longboat picked up the next line. Then a boy who couldn't have been older than sixteen, his voice breaking in a different way entirely. The song moved through the fleet like fire through dry grass, and Odile stood in the middle of it with tears cutting tracks through the salt dust on her face, singing her inheritance back to the people it belonged to.

Corvane turned to his first officer. "Arrest them. All of them. Start with the woman."

First Officer Sera Ilves was a tall, grey-haired woman with a face like a cliff — Odile had watched her through the whole confrontation, unreadable, hands folded behind her back, saying nothing while her admiral offered pardons and threats in equal measure. Now she stepped forward, and Odile's heart lurched, because for one terrible moment she thought Ilves would obey.

Ilves stopped beside Corvane. She looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, quietly enough that only the quarterdeck heard, though every eye aboard watched her lips: "Sir. I read the ledger last night."

The words fell into the silence like a stone into a well.

"Deserter Brannock's copy. He showed me before he ran." Her voice did not waver. "I checked the dates against our own fleet archives. The sluice-gate orders are authentic. The signatures are authentic. We have been sailing under the seal of the government that drowned Vessia, sir, and you have known since the day we launched."

"I have known," Corvane said, "since the day I was briefed, which is not the same thing—"

"It is exactly the same thing."

* * *

Afterward, Odile could never reconstruct the order of what happened next. Chaos does not arrange itself into narrative; it simply happens, all at once, everywhere, and the mind picks up pieces afterward like shells after a storm.

She remembered Corvane drawing his sword — not at the mutinous sailors, not at Ilves, but turning toward Ansel, toward the ledger, because a man cornered by history will always go for the document rather than the witness. Documents can burn. Witnesses multiply.

She remembered shouting Ansel's name, and the strange slowness of everything, the way danger stretches time like taffy.

And she remembered the blade — not Corvane's sword but a knife, thrown or thrust, she never learned whose — coming out of the confusion of the shallows where the loyalists still fought, arcing toward her chest while her hands were full of nothing but song.

Ansel was faster than a man with a healing rope-wound and a fever-burned body had any right to be. Later she would understand: he hadn't been fast. He had simply already been moving, had seen the throw begin before she did, because he had spent twenty years reading violence the way she read locks. He stepped into the space between her and the steel.

The knife took him below the collarbone, angling down.

He made no sound. That was the worst of it. He folded around the blade like it was a debt come due, and his eyes found hers over the hilt, and they were not afraid — they were apologetic, as if being stabbed in front of her were a failure of manners.

Then the world sped up again, all at once, and Odile caught him as he fell.

* * *

The hull of the *Vessia's Grace* became a battlefield, then a barricade, then something neither side had intended: a meeting ground. Because the mutiny did not spread like a riot. It spread like recognition.

Odile dragged Ansel behind the broken ribs of the ship's sterncastle, pressing her palms against the wound while the fight churned through the shallows below. Blood welled between her fingers, warm and shocking against the cold spray off the river. His breath came in short, shallow pulls.

"Keep pressure," he managed. "I've had worse."

"You have absolutely not had worse."

"The fever," he said, and almost smiled. "The fever was worse. This is just — punctual."

"Shut up. Shut up and breathe." Her hands were shaking. Twenty years of thieving, of lockpicks and forged writs and running from rangers, and none of it had taught her what to do with this — with the specific weight of a man she loved bleeding out against a ship her people had died aboard. "Ansel. Look at me. You don't get to do this. You burned your commission. You don't get to die unemployed."

That startled a laugh out of him, which turned into a cough, which frightened her more than the blood.

Above them, the fight was ending the way Ilves must have known it would end — not with victory but with arithmetic. Corvane's loyalists numbered perhaps thirty men, clustered on the flagship's deck, and even among those thirty, blades were lowering one by one as the verses drifted up from the shallows, where sailors had begun singing without any prompting at all, the way people sing at funerals, the way people sing when they finally learn the name of what they lost.

Corvane fought alone in the center of it. He was good — better than good, a swordsman formed by forty years of fleet discipline — and for a few terrible minutes it seemed he might cut his way to the longboats, might reach the shore, might find the ledger where Ansel had hidden it in the strongroom's rubble.

He got as far as the shallows.

Sera Ilves waded in after him, unarmed, her first officer's coat dark with spray, and planted herself in his path like a sea wall.

"Stand aside, Sera," Corvane said. "That is the last order I will ever give you."

"That is why I'm standing here, sir." She did not draw a weapon. She simply stood, and behind her stood a dozen of his own crew, and beyond them the whole mutinied fleet, and beyond them the escarpment rising red in the afternoon light with its drowned villages sleeping underneath. "You taught us that the fleet serves the crown. But the crown serves the law, and the law says what you did to Vessia is murder. I am not disobeying you, Admiral. I am finally obeying everything else."

For a moment — Odile saw it clearly, from the height of the hull, the whole tableau framed in gold evening light — Corvane looked less like a defeated man than like a man waking from a long dream into a room he did not recognize. His sword arm sagged. The water pushed gently against his legs, patient as tide, patient as consequence.

Then he broke. Not surrendering — lunging, sideways, wild, toward the flagship's boat, screaming something about the capital, about the chancellery, about how the truth would drown them all if it surfaced. Two sailors caught his arms. A third took the sword. It was over in seconds, undignified and small, the mighty admiral of the salvage fleet hauled dripping onto his own deck by men he'd commanded for a decade.

It should have been over.

But Odile had learned long ago that endings are where the desperate make their last withdrawals, and Corvane had one asset left: a man in the third longboat whom nobody had been watching, a quiet sailor with a fire-pot from the flagship's signal locker, and his orders were not to fight but to burn — to put flame to the wreck, to the strongroom rubble, to the ledger's hiding place, and let the river take the proof the way it had taken everything else.

Odile saw the arc of the fire-pot leave his hand.

She was too far. Ansel was bleeding under her hands. The pot tumbled end over end toward the dry timber of the exposed hull, toward the oil-slick planking, toward everything —

— and Sera Ilves caught it.

Out of the air, one-handed, like a woman catching a fish. She held it a moment, looking at the fuse spitting sparks against the evening, and then she walked to the gunwale of the nearest longboat and dropped it over the side into six feet of river, where it hissed once and went dark.

"No," she said, to no one and everyone. "We keep the evidence. All of it. That is what separates us from him."

* * *

They brought Ansel's surgeon — the fleet's surgeon, a Vessian-descended woman named Maren who worked with tears running silently down her face because the ballad the thief was singing was her grandmother's ballad too. She dug the knife out of Ansel's shoulder by lantern light on the *Grace*'s tilted deck, with half the fleet standing watch in the shallows and the other half sitting where they stood, exhausted, weapons abandoned, listening to Odile sing the low verses to keep him conscious.

"Stay with me," Odile said, forehead pressed to his temple. "You hear me? You stayed with me through the fever. You owe me a symmetry."

"Symmetry," he breathed. "Rangers don't... use words like that."

"This one does. He learned it from a thief."

Maren packed the wound with moss and salt-rum and stitched it closed while Ansel gripped Odile's hand hard enough to grind bone, and he did not cry out, and when it was done he looked up at her with the grey, fever-bright eyes she had first seen across the salt flats all those weeks ago, when he was chasing her and she was running from everything.

"You took a blade for me," she said.

"You were singing." His voice was a ruin. "Couldn't let them... cut the song short."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not." His hand rose, trembling, and touched her cheek. "Odile. If I'd been slower—"

"But you weren't." She caught his hand and held it against her face. "You weren't slower. You're never going to be slower again. That's the deal. That's the writ. I'm forging it right now and my forgeries are very, very good."

He laughed, weakly, and winced, and kept laughing anyway, and above them the first stars came out over the escarpment, and the river, lower now than it had been in living memory, whispered against the bones of the drowned nation it had taken and given back.

* * *

Near midnight, Ilves came to find her.

The first officer stood at the edge of the lantern light, hat in hand, looking suddenly older and younger at once — a woman unburdened and terrified in the same breath. Behind her, on the flagship, Corvane sat bound in his own cabin under guard of his own crew, and the fleet's council of officers was arguing itself hoarse over what came next.

"They want to throw him in the hold and sail for the capital," Ilves said. "Trial. Courts. The whole machinery." She paused. "Some of them wanted to throw him in the river. I talked them down. Barely."

"Thank you," Odile said. "For the fire-pot."

Ilves waved it off. "Don't thank me yet." She glanced back toward the flagship, and her voice dropped. "There's a problem, Mistress Odile. The ledger survived. The verses survived. But a trial needs witnesses, testimony, chain of evidence — and there are men on that council who served under Corvane for fifteen years and still can't quite believe their own eyes. By the time we reach the capital, the chancellery will have lawyers waiting, and lawyers are a kind of flood too. They drown things."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying the evidence is safest in the open." Ilves looked at her steadily. "You sang it once today and half my fleet turned. Sing it again tomorrow, properly, all of it, before every crew — and let it travel. Sailors carry songs home. Songs can't be seized by the court, discredited by lawyers, or burned by admirals." A thin, tired smile. "Your grandmother knew what she was doing, putting a manifest in verse. Make it unstoppable."

Odile looked down at Ansel, sleeping at last beneath a borrowed blanket, his bandaged shoulder rising and falling, rising and falling. She thought of her grandmother's hands teaching her the cadence, tapping time on a salt-town table. She thought of the names carved in the drowned chapel. She thought of the final page of the manifest, still waiting, still unread — the page that listed the families owed reparations, the page that would tell her, at last, whether her own name was written somewhere in a dead language on sunken paper.

Tomorrow she would sing until her voice gave out. Tomorrow the fleet would carry the truth to the capital in a hundred throats.

Tonight, she climbed down into the strongroom rubble one more time, by lantern light, to find the last page — and found, wedged beneath the fused ledger, a second document no one had noticed. Smaller. Older. Sealed with wax stamped not with the crown's mark, but with Vessia's.

Addressed, in careful script, *To whoever the river chooses.*
