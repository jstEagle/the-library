## 19. Aftermath of Tides

Pain woke him before anything else did — a deep, tidal ache in his side that rose and fell with his breathing, as though the wound had its own weather. Then came the smell: tar and brine and woodsmoke, the particular stink of a beached hull drying in the sun. Then voices, many voices, low and uncertain, the way men talk when they are not yet sure what world they have woken into.

Ansel opened his eyes to a canopy of patched sailcloth strung between two broken masts. Light came through the canvas in amber coins. He was lying on something soft — an actual mattress, he realized dimly, dragged from some officer's cabin — with a blanket tucked to his chin in a manner no ranger would ever have permitted.

"You snore," said Odile, from somewhere near his feet. "Like a man drowning in mud. It's very undignified."

He turned his head. She sat cross-legged on a sea chest, her hands wrapped around a tin cup she wasn't drinking from, her eyes rimmed red with two days without sleep. Her voice was rough as rope. But she was whole. Unmarked, as far as he could see through the haze. That was the first thing, and it took him a long moment simply to look at her and let it be true.

"The blade," he managed. His throat felt scoured with salt.

"Went in under your ribs and out again. Missed everything that matters." A ghost of her old crooked smile. "The surgeon says you're mostly empty space anyway."

"The ledger—"

"Safe." She set down the cup and leaned forward, and her face changed — the humor draining out of it, leaving something older and more careful underneath. "Wrapped in oilcloth, guarded by four sailors who volunteered before anyone asked them. Corvane is chained in his own brig. His own *brig*, Ansel. His first officer put him there."

* * *

They came to see him that evening, when the light through the sailcloth went copper and long. First Officer Sera Vane — a tall woman with grey shot through her black braid and a voice like a hatch closing — arrived with three captains behind her, and stood at the foot of his makeshift bed with the awkwardness of people who had rehearsed this conversation on the way over and were now finding it insufficient.

"Ravensworth." She looked at him the way he imagined one looked at a chart with a coastline drawn where no coast should be. "You burned your commission."

"I did."

"In front of two hundred witnesses."

"That was rather the point."

One of the captains — a squat man with a beard full of tar — made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Vane silenced him with a glance.

"I've read the ledger," she said. "All of it. The sluice-gate orders. The dates. The signatures." Her jaw worked once. "My grandmother came from Vessia. From the low quarter, by the eastern weir. I used to think she invented the stories about the water coming too fast, too early, before any storm. I thought grief embellished." She stopped. Started again, flatter. "It didn't embellish."

"No," Ansel said quietly. "It didn't."

"So." Vane planted her feet. "Here is what remains of the salvage fleet's command structure has decided, such as it remains. We are not going back to our moorings to await orders from the capital, because the orders we'd receive would come from the same offices that signed those gates open. We are going *to* the capital. All of us. With the ledger under guard of every crew that will stand for it — and as of this morning, that is most of them."

She paused, and something in her bearing shifted, the officer's armor thinning just enough to show the person beneath.

"There are men on these decks whose grandfathers drowned in that flood," she said. "There are men who signed on with the fleet because it was the only work that would take Vessian names. They've spent their lives hauling salvage off their own dead country and calling it honest labor. They deserve to carry this themselves."

"And Corvane?" Ansel asked.

"Charged by his own officers. Mutiny against lawful command, they're calling it — which is rich, given the circumstances, but the law has always been generous to whoever holds the pen." Her mouth twisted. "He'll face trial in the capital. In front of the courts. In front of whatever's left of the truth after the lawyers finish chewing it."

"He'll argue the fleet acted illegally," Ansel said. "That we're pirates."

"He'll argue," Vane agreed. "And then Odile of the Vessia road will sing, and two hundred sailors will sing with her, and the courts can decide whether they want to drown that song in front of the whole capital." She straightened. "Get well, Ravensworth. You're a witness now, not a corpse. Try to stay both."

She turned to go, then hesitated — a small human hitch in all that iron discipline.

"My grandmother's name was Maren Vane," she said, not looking at him. "It's carved on the chapel wall. Third column, near the bottom. I read it yesterday, while the singing was going on." A breath. "I never knew where she'd come from until I saw it cut in stone."

Then she was gone, and the three captains filed out after her like a tide going out, and Ansel lay back against the mattress and discovered that his eyes were wet, and decided not to examine why.

* * *

The days that followed had a strange, suspended quality, like the hour between ebb and flood when the river seems to hold its breath.

The fleet lay anchored in the shallows below the escarpment, riding out the last of the dry-season drop, waiting for the capital-bound convoy to organize itself. Sailors came and went past his sickbed berth in the repaired great cabin of the flagship — some curious, some reverent, one ancient quartermaster who simply stood in the doorway for a long time, looking at Ansel as if checking that he was real, then touched his brow with two fingers and left without a word.

Odile was there when she could be, which was less often than he wanted and more often than her body could afford. She sang twice a day on the exposed hull, teaching the verses to anyone who'd learn them — the manifest turned to music, the cargo list turned to inheritance, her grandmother's songs doing the work that lawyers never could. Her voice cracked on the third day and kept cracking, and the sailors started singing *for* her, taking up the lines before she faltered, so that the song rolled across the shallows in waves and she only had to stand among them, conducting with small movements of her hand, tears running freely down her face while her mouth stayed closed.

He watched her from the cabin's open door, propped on one elbow, his side screaming whenever he moved too fast, and understood that he was watching something being born that had no name yet. Not a mutiny. Mutinies wanted power. This wanted something else — something quieter and far more dangerous.

On the fifth day he could walk. On the sixth, he walked to the rail and stood in the wind like a man relearning his own legs, and Odile appeared beside him without seeming to arrive, the way she always did.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I feel magnificent."

"Liar." But she slipped her arm through his, carefully, on the unwounded side, and they stood together watching the water fall away from the wreck's black ribs below.

"They're leaving tomorrow," she said. "Vane's convoy. Four ships, the ledger, two hundred singers." She was quiet a moment. "Corvane's crew voted to keep him aboard in chains rather than leave him for the garrison here. They want him tried where everyone can watch. They want witnesses."

"Good," Ansel said. "Witnesses are the one thing the crown can't confiscate."

She glanced up at him. "Listen to you. A week ago you were a man who believed in writs and warrants."

"A week ago I was a man who hadn't read the ledger." He looked down at her, and the wind pulled a strand of hair across her face, and he reached over and tucked it back with fingers that were still unsteady, and she let him, and neither of them said anything about it, because some things said aloud become smaller than they are when unsaid.

"What happens to us?" she asked finally. It was the question she'd been circling for days, he knew — since the blade, since the burning commission, since the singing. "After the trial. After the reparations, if they come. You're not a ranger anymore. I'm not—" she huffed a laugh, "—well. I'm still technically a thief. Habit, mostly. But the papers will make me a claimant. A respectable woman with a case number." She said *respectable* the way other people said *plague*.

"We'll figure it out," Ansel said. "I'm told that's what people do."

"People," she repeated skeptically. "Us, you mean."

"If you'll allow the comparison."

She laughed — really laughed, the first time since the night raid, since the chest cracked open, since the blade — and the sound carried out over the falling water, and somewhere below them a sailor on the wreck's hull picked up the verse-manifest's opening line and began to sing.

* * *

It was near dusk on the seventh day that she came to him with the document.

He knew before she spoke that something had changed. She came into the cabin quickly, too quickly for her usual unhurried prowl, and she was holding a bundle of oilcloth against her chest the way one holds a child, or a wound, and her eyes were wide and wet and frightened in a way he had never seen them, not on the cliff trail, not during the raid, not even when Corvane's net closed around them.

"Odile." He pushed himself upright, ignoring the hot wire of pain. "What is it?"

"The last page," she said. "From the strongroom. The one I went down for, the night after the singing." She sat on the edge of the bed, close, her knee against his. "I told you I found something wedged under the ledger. Smaller. Older. Sealed with Vessia's wax instead of the crown's."

"You said you hadn't opened it."

"I haven't." Her hands tightened on the bundle. "I've been carrying it for six days and I haven't opened it. Do you understand? I broke into the Marquis d'Ossery's vault when I was sixteen. I've picked locks in three fortresses. I once opened a sealed evidence crate in a magistrate's office *while he was eating lunch ten feet away*. And I cannot bring myself to break this seal."

"Why?"

"Because of what's written on it." She turned the bundle over. Through the oilcloth he could see the shape of the document within — flat, heavy paper, folded twice, the wax seal visible where the cloth parted. Dark green wax, stamped with the wave-and-tower sigil of Vessia, pressed by a hand that had been steady and deliberate. And above the seal, in careful script, faded but legible:

*To whoever the river chooses.*

"It's addressed to no one," Odile whispered. "Or to everyone. Or—" She stopped, swallowed. "My grandmother used to say the river keeps what it loves and gives back what it owes. She said someday the river would choose someone. I thought it was a story. A comfort for children who'd lost everything." She looked up at him, and her eyes were blazing and terrified at once. "Ansel. What if it's not a story? What if my grandmother — what if someone *knew*? Before the flood. What if they sealed this knowing the water was coming, knowing it would end up in the wreck, knowing that someday—"

"Hey." He took her hands, the bundle and all, and held them still. "Breathe."

"She wrote verses that were really cargo lists," Odile said, half laughing, half crying. "She taught me songs that were really maps. What else was a story, Ansel? What else was real?"

He had no answer. He looked at the dark green wax, at the careful script, at the sigil of a nation that had been murdered by its own king's arithmetic and had somehow, from beneath sixty years of water, arranged to speak.

The fleet sailed for the capital at dawn. Whatever was inside that fold of paper would be read eventually — by the courts, by the crown's clerks, by hands that had no right to it. Or it would be read tonight, by the two people the river had dragged through flood and fever and fire to this exact rail of this exact ship.

"Whoever the river chooses," Ansel said slowly. "Sixty years of tide, and it washed a chest to the feet of a ranger chasing a thief. It gave the manifest to the granddaughter of the woman who hid it in song. If the river chose anyone, Odile—" he turned her hands over, placed the bundle back against her heart, and closed her fingers around it, "—I don't think it was choosing blind."

Her breath caught.

"Break the seal," he said softly. "Whatever's in there, we read it together. Tonight. Before the courts get their claws on it. Before anyone else decides what it means."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then she nodded, once, and drew a breath like a diver's, and took out the little knife she wore at her wrist — the same knife that had opened a hundred locks and one ranger's garrison strongbox, in another life, on the other side of the chase.

The wax parted with a sound like a sigh.

She unfolded the paper. Her eyes went down the page once, fast. Then again, slower. And Ansel watched her face change — watched the color leave it, then flood back, watched her lips move silently over words that were not a cargo list, not a map, not a ledger of crimes.

"Odile," he said. "What is it? What does it say?"

She looked up at him, and her voice, when it came, was barely above the sound of the water against the hull.

"It's a list of names."
