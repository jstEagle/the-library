## 20. Papers

The courier came at dawn, riding a mule that had given up all pretense of dignity. I heard it first as a sound like the escarpment clearing its throat — hooves on shale, slow and complaining — and I was out of the tent with a knife in my hand before my mind caught up to my body. Old habits. Ansel followed me out a moment later, one arm still bound against his chest where Corvane's blade had found him, his hair flattened on one side from sleep.

"It's a mule," he said.

"It's a mule *carrying something*," I said, which was different.

It was carrying something. The rider was a fleet clerk — or a former fleet clerk; after the mutiny at low water, half the admiralty's paperwork had changed hands along with half its ships — and he dismounted stiffly, unrolled an oilcloth satchel, and produced a document sealed in three places: the provisional court at the capital, the first officer who now commanded what remained of the salvage fleet, and the crown's own chancery, which had apparently decided that if you couldn't drown a truth, the next best thing was to notarize it.

I didn't touch it at first. I stood there in the gray light with the river muttering behind me and read the opening lines twice, because the words kept rearranging themselves into something I didn't dare believe.

*In the matter of the Vessian reparations claims, heard before the circuit court of the capital: the ledger recovered from the wreck of the* Meridian's Grace *having been authenticated, and the sluice-gate orders of the ninth year of King Aldous's reign having been entered into evidence, the court finds for the claimant families.*

Claimant families. Plural. As though we were a category instead of a scattering — as though the eleven thousand souls the crown had let drown for the price of a cheaper harbor could be gathered up into a phrase and filed.

Ansel read over my shoulder. He was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "Read the last page."

"The last page is signatures and seals."

"Read it anyway."

So I did, standing in the cold with the paper trembling in a way I told myself was the wind.

* * *

We had buried the chest two days after the verdict's first rumors reached us, but the manifest itself we had kept — dried page by page over the chapel fire, pressed flat between boards Ansel cut from a pew that had spent forty years underwater and still remembered how to be furniture. The verse-manifest, my grandmother's songs made into law without ever knowing it, hung now in oilcloth inside the tent like a relic, which is what it was.

But the final page — the page the water had nearly taken, the page I had pried from the fused mass of ledger and manifest with a knife and my fingernails and, I will admit, tears — that page I had not been able to read. The ink had run. The columns of names had blurred into gray ghosts of themselves, legible only in fragments, and every time I tried, I saw my grandmother's face instead of the letters, so I folded it away and told myself I would read it when I was stronger.

I was not stronger. I was thinner, and my hands still shook some mornings, and Ansel's wound had gone from red to angry pink to a scar he pretended didn't ache. But the court's letter said the names were known. The court's letter said they had been transcribed by clerks working from our copy, authenticated under seal, and appended to the judgment.

So I sat down on an overturned crate outside the tent, with the river going gold at the edges, and I read the list of the drowned and the owed.

There were four hundred and six families. I counted them the way you count anything unbearable — mechanically, lips moving, finger tracing down the clerk's neat columns. Fishers of the lower terraces. Weavers of the salt-works. The keepers of the chapel itself, whose names were carved in stone not fifty paces from where I sat. Family by family, trade by trade, each entry followed by a sum: the assessed value of a life's work, a house, a boat, a burial that never happened, owed now with interest calculated across forty years of a debt the crown had hoped would rot at the bottom of a reservoir.

And there, in the third column, ninety-one entries down:

*Raveneau, Mireille — net-mender, lower terrace. Raveneau, Tomas — her husband, pilot. Raveneau, Sefa — daughter, age nine.*

My grandmother's name. My grandmother, who had been nine years old when the water came, who had been carried out on her father's back through the escape tunnels while her neighbors' lanterns went out behind them, who had grown up singing cargo lists to a child who thought they were lullabies. She had never once said the word *reparations*. She had said *what they took*, and she had said it quietly, the way you speak of the dead, and she had died in a rented room in a smugglers' port with her family's name worn smooth from changing it.

Sefa. That was her. That was the girl she had been, before she became Mireille-the-survivor, before she became Grandmother, before she became a story I half-believed. Nine years old. The clerk had written *age nine* in the same hand, the same even ink, as everything else, and something about that undid me more than the sums. Somewhere in the capital a man with good penmanship had written *age nine* about my grandmother, and it was the truest sentence anyone had ever put on paper about her.

Ansel crouched beside me. He didn't say anything. He had learned, somewhere between the toll bridge and the mudflats, when to be silent next to me, and I loved him for it in a way I still hadn't found words for — loved him the way the river loves the channel it has cut, patiently, by persistence, by never leaving.

"Say it," he said finally. "Out loud. Once."

"My family's on it." My voice cracked in the middle like river ice. "Ninety-one. *Raveneau.*"

"That's your name."

"It was." I wiped my face with the back of my wrist, roughly, the way you do when you don't want the crying to count. "Before we started trading it for whatever name kept us alive. Delacroix in the salt ports. Marchand for two years in the estuary towns. I've been more names than I can remember, Ansel. I stole under all of them."

"And now?"

I looked at the letter again. At the bottom, beneath the seals and the sums and the clerk's patient columns, there was a space. A ruled line, blank, waiting. *Signature of claimant or authorized heir.*

"They want a signature," I said. "From the heirs. To begin disbursement."

"Then sign it."

"With which name?" I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and sharp. "That's the joke, isn't it? Forty years of hiding, and the one thing they need from me is the thing I gave away."

Ansel took the letter out of my hands — gently, the way he handled the manifest, the way he handled me on the rope-down when the cliff crumbled — and set it on the crate between us. Then he looked at me with those ranger's eyes that had once tracked me across thirty miles of empty salt, eyes that had learned to see me instead of just follow me.

"You didn't give it away," he said. "You buried it. There's a difference. Buried things can be dug up."

* * *

I went to the chapel that afternoon alone.

I hadn't meant to. I'd meant to sit with the letter until the shaking stopped, meant to be practical, because practicality was the armor I'd worn since I was old enough to know what happened to girls who weren't. But my feet took the path along the reservoir's edge, past the reeds that had grown up through what used to be someone's front garden, up the slick steps to the half-drowned doorway where the bell tower leaned like a drunk uncle at a wedding.

Inside, the waterline marked the walls in a tide-ring of pale stone, and above it, carved into the dry rock by hands that must have worked fast and afraid, were the names.

I had seen them before — the night we sheltered here, the night Ansel found his grandfather's signature on the evacuation order and went white as salt. But seeing is not reading, and reading is not searching, and this time I searched. I ran my fingers along the grooves the way my grandmother must have run hers along the doorframes of every house she ever rented, checking, always checking, for the exits.

*Raveneau.*

There. Third row from the top, where the carver had still been careful, before grief made his letters hurried. *Tomas Raveneau. Mireille Raveneau.* And beneath them, smaller, added later by a different hand — a survivor who came back, maybe, when the water dropped enough to wade in with a chisel: *Sefa, carried out alive. May she live long enough to come home.*

I put my palm flat against the stone. It was warm from the afternoon sun, warmer than stone had any right to be in this place, and I stood there a long time with my forehead almost touching the wall, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that the carving didn't already know.

*May she live long enough to come home.* She hadn't. She had lived long enough to raise a granddaughter on stolen bread and borrowed songs, long enough to teach me that the world owes you nothing and takes plenty, long enough to die believing the drowned fleet was a story she'd embroidered to make poverty bearable. And it wasn't. It had never been. Every verse she sang me was testimony. Every lullaby was evidence. She had carried the truth across forty years and two hundred leagues and handed it to me disguised as a bedtime song, and I had carried it as a thief carries loot — close, guilty, ready to drop it and run.

I wasn't running anymore.

When I came back down to the camp, the sun was low and the courier's mule was grazing on the tether rope it had chewed halfway through, and Ansel was sitting on the crate with the letter, a pen, and an inkpot he'd evidently begged off the clerk, arranged in front of him with the solemnity of a man laying out surgical tools.

"I practiced," he said.

"What?"

"Your name. In case your hand shakes too badly to write it clean. I practiced it." He turned over a scrap of paper, and there it was, in a ranger's blunt handwriting, five attempts ascending toward competence: *Raveneau. Raveneau. Odile Raveneau.* "The clerk says the spelling matters. Court documents."

I stared at him. "You practiced my name."

"I wanted to get it right." He shrugged, and winced, and kept shrugging anyway, because Ansel would rather reopen a wound than leave a sentence unfinished. "You've spent your whole life being called the wrong thing on purpose. I'm not going to be the one who writes it down wrong by accident."

And that — that ridiculous, careful, stubborn man with his scrap of practice paper — that is what broke the last of it loose in me. Not the seals, not the sums, not the four hundred and six families. Him, sitting in the last light with ink on his fingers, learning to spell a dead woman's name so her granddaughter could take it back.

I sat down. I took the pen. My hand did shake, and I let it.

*Odile Mireille Raveneau.*

I wrote it slowly, each letter deliberate, the way you carve stone. It was the first time in eleven years I had put that name on anything — not a forged writ, not a false register, not a smuggler's tally. A claim. My grandmother's name in the middle of mine like a bridge, like a rope thrown down a cliff, like a song finally sung all the way to the end.

The pen scratched. The ink dried. The river kept moving past us, indifferent and generous, the way it always had — giving things up, keeping things down, remembering in silt what the living tried to forget.

"There," Ansel said softly.

"There," I agreed.

I rolled the letter, sealed it with wax from the clerk's kit, and handed it back to the man with the mule, who tucked it away among his documents with the tenderness of someone who understood he was carrying more than paper. Then I stood there watching him ride north along the reservoir road, getting smaller, taking my name with him into the machinery of courts and clerks and kings, and I felt — lighter. Stripped. The way you feel after setting down a load so heavy you'd forgotten your own shape without it.

For eleven years I had been a thief because the world left me no other trade. Now the world, in its slow and grudging way, had admitted it owed me, and the strangest part was that I didn't want the money. I wanted the *name*. I wanted to walk into a town and hear it spoken aloud and not flinch. I wanted my grandmother's ghost to stop looking over her shoulder.

Ansel came to stand beside me. His shoulder touched mine, carefully, on the uninjured side.

"So," he said. "Odile Raveneau. What does a claimant do next?"

I watched the sun go down behind the escarpment, gilding the cliffs where the drowned villages slept, and I realized I knew exactly what came next — had known it since the night I read the last page, since the night in the chapel, maybe since the night a flash flood gave up a chest and changed both our lives.

"Now," I said, "we go and find the other four hundred and five."

Because the court had sent letters to the addresses on file, and the addresses on file were forty years old, and half the scattered families had drifted like seed on the current — into the salt ports, the estuary towns, the bandit country we'd walked through, the places where people change their names to survive. The crown would post notices. The crown would wait politely. And the crown would declare unclaimed funds forfeit in three years' time, because that was what crowns did; I had read the clause twice.

Three years. Four hundred and six families. One woman who knew, better than any clerk alive, how to find people who didn't want to be found — because I had been one of them.

Ansel was quiet a moment. Then he smiled, slow and crooked, the smile of a man with no commission, no debt, and no reason left to be anywhere except wherever I was going.

"Well," he said. "I do still have a ranger's knack for tracking."

I laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the blade, since the water, since the night the fleet's lanterns ringed the shallows — and went to pack.
