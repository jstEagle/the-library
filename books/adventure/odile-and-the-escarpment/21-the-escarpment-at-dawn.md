## 21. The Escarpment at Dawn

The salt flats were the same. That was the first thing Ansel noticed, standing where the chase had begun all those weeks ago — the flats did not care what had happened to him. The crust stretched white to the horizon, cracked into a million hexagonal plates like the shell of some enormous sleeping thing, and the morning light came up amber and slow behind the escarpment, gilding every ridge until the whole drowned country looked like it was on fire in the gentlest possible way.

His boots stood in the same shallow depression they had stood in on that first dusk, when he'd crouched over a set of small, quick footprints and thought: *thief*. He'd been so certain of everything then. Certain of the law's shape. Certain of his own. Certain that a man could pay down a debt by walking a straight line long enough.

He turned his hand over. The rope scar across his palm had closed to a thin pink seam, still tender when he flexed his fingers. He flexed them anyway, deliberately, the way Odile had taught him — *scars stiffen if you baby them, ranger* — and watched the sun clear the escarpment's shoulder.

No commission. No badge. The letter of resignation he'd burned before Corvane's assembled crews had been more than theater; the ash had drifted out over the water and settled on the fleet's decks, and there was no un-burning it. His captain's memory sat quieter in his chest now than it had in years. He'd thought, for a long time, that honoring Captain Veyra meant keeping the law even when the law was wrong. It had taken a drowned nation and a woman with her grandmother's songs to teach him that Veyra would have wanted the truth carried, not the uniform.

He was free. The word still fit strangely, like a boot bought for another man.

Behind him, gravel crunched.

He didn't reach for a weapon anymore either. He'd broken that habit somewhere around the toll bridge, or maybe the night she'd read verses over his fever, or maybe — if he was honest, and he was trying to be honest these days, it was harder work than tracking but it paid better — he'd never actually broken it at all. He'd just stopped needing it.

"You're up early," Odile said, coming to stand beside him.

"I'm always up early."

"You're always up early *when you're chasing something*." She looked out at the flats, at the place where a dry wash had swallowed her whole one dusk, and something moved through her face — not quite a smile, not quite grief. "I used to run this stretch at night, you know. When I was sixteen. Grandmother said the salt remembers everyone who crosses it, so I'd walk out here and say names to it. Practice. In case I ever forgot how to say them out loud."

"Did it remember?"

"It remembered." She glanced at him sideways. "It also apparently kept excellent records of a fool ranger stomping around after me for three days."

"Four days," Ansel said. "You lost me at the wash on day four. I counted."

"You never caught me. There's a difference between losing someone and letting them think they've almost got you." But she said it without edge now, the old mockery worn soft as river glass, and she leaned her shoulder against his arm — lightly, the way she did everything, as if commitment were a thing you approached sideways and only admitted to once you were already inside it.

They stood together while the light climbed. Somewhere out on the flats a salt-wren sang its two-note song, and far off, the river glinted in its new channel, running lower every day but running still.

* * *

They had come back to the flats because of a list.

The ledger from the strongroom — Corvane's sluice-gate orders, the crown's deliberate drowning of Vessia written in an admiral's neat bureaucratic hand — had gone east with the mutinied fleet's officers, under guard of men who had refused their own orders and meant to see it through. But the manifest's final page, the one listing the survivor families owed reparations, Odile had copied out in her own careful script before surrendering the original to the courts' courier. She kept the copy folded inside her shirt, over her heart, the way other people wore lockets.

And beneath the list of families, in the old verse-form her grandmother had sung to her, the manifest held one more passage. Ansel had heard her translate it twice now, once by firelight and once in the chapel among the carved names, and both times her voice had gone quiet in the middle.

*"Count the doors beneath the water. Count the windows, count the wells. What the crown has drowned in silence, let the living name aloud."*

"It's an instruction," she'd said. "Not just a record. Whoever wrote the manifest — whoever hid it in the strongroom — they knew the truth might come out someday, and they wanted more than reparations. They wanted the dead counted. Every village. Every door. So no one could ever say it was an accident, or a tragedy, or nobody's fault. A map of exactly what was taken."

That was why they stood here now, at the edge of the escarpment, where the drowned country began.

Ansel had spent the last week recovering in the river town below the fleet anchorage — bandages changed daily, broth forced on him by Odile with the stern tenderness of a woman who had already nursed him through one fever and refused to do it again if she could help it. And in that week, he had discovered what a man was when nothing owned him. It had frightened him for about two days. Then Odile had caught him staring at the ceiling of their rented room at dawn, and said, "You look like a hound with no scent," and laughed until she had to sit down, and he'd realized the fear was just the old shape of himself emptying out, making room.

Now he took the folded map from his satchel — the survey chart of the escarpment region he'd bought from the trader Wex, who had charged him half price and thrown in a flask of plum brandy "for the wedding I assume is coming, since everyone on this river is betting on it" — and spread it on a flat stone between them.

Odile looked at it. Then at him. Then at it again.

"You bought a map," she said.

"I bought a map."

"Of the escarpment."

"Of the escarpment."

"With *money*. Voluntarily. Ansel Ravensworth, who once told me maps are how the law pretends it knows where people belong." She pressed the back of her hand to his forehead theatrically. "No fever. You've simply changed."

"The map's incomplete," he said, ignoring her, though his mouth wanted to betray him. "Wex's surveyors marked eleven villages along the escarpment shelf. The manifest verse mentions nineteen. Nobody's ever walked the full length of the drowned shore at low water and recorded what's actually there. Where the chapels stand. Where the doors were. Which families lived at which bend of the river." He tapped the blank stretch of coastline west of the old harbor. "Nobody's done it because nobody had a reason. Now we do."

"We," Odile repeated softly.

"If you want." He made himself meet her eyes. This was the part he'd rehearsed alone on the flats this morning, and rehearsal hadn't helped; the words still came out plain and unarmored, which was, he suspected, the only way she'd ever accept them. "I'm not asking you to choose me over anything. You spent your whole life being asked to choose — the family or the road, the theft or the debt. I'm not doing that. I'm telling you what I'm going to do, and asking if you'll do it with me. Walk the escarpment. Map the villages. Sing the names back to the water. Take however many seasons it takes. Because the courts will rule on reparations eventually, and lawyers will argue about numbers, and numbers can be argued with. But a door is a door. A well is a well. If we count them, one by one, with our own hands—" he flexed the scarred palm again, "—then what happened stops being a claim and becomes a fact. Facts don't drown."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. The salt-wren sang again. Out on the flats, the heat shimmer began to rise off the white crust like breath.

"You understand," she said finally, "that this is the least profitable plan you have ever proposed. There's no salvage in it. No bounty. Half the drowned villages will be mud and bones and things I'll cry over, and you'll have to watch me cry, and you're terrible at watching me cry, you go all stiff and offer me water like a ranger delivering a citation."

"I've been practicing offering water less stiffly."

"And the bandits are still in the hill country, and the dry season will make the low-water walks brutal, and your rope wound will ache every cold morning for the rest of your life—"

"Probably."

"—and you'll be doing it with a thief. A convicted, confessed, entirely unrepentant thief whose family will feed you too much at every festival and ask invasive questions about your intentions."

"That last part sounds genuinely dangerous," Ansel said. "I accept the risk."

She laughed — the real laugh, the one he'd only heard a handful of times, the one that started low in her chest and broke upward like water finding a channel — and then she went quiet again, and her hand came out and flattened against the map, over the blank western coast, over all those uncounted doors.

"My grandmother used to sing me to sleep with a verse about the harbor quarter," she said. Her voice had gone rough. "I always thought it was just a lullaby. It wasn't. It was a street. Fourteen houses and a bell tower, and she was singing me the way home to a place that was underwater before I was born." She blinked hard, twice, and looked at him, and her chin came up in the old defiant way that he loved, that he had loved since a dry wash at dusk, though it had taken him until a flooded chapel to know it. "Yes. Obviously yes. But I want it understood that I'm not doing this for you, ranger. I'm doing it for her. You're just — coming along."

"Just coming along," Ansel agreed gravely. "As ballast."

"As ballast," she said, and kissed him, brief and fierce, tasting of salt and plum brandy and the particular dawn air of a country beginning to remember itself.

* * *

They broke camp an hour later, in the full gold of the morning.

There wasn't much to break. Two bedrolls, a cook pot, the water skins, Odile's copy of the manifest, and the emptied chest itself, which they had argued about carrying and finally decided to bring — not for any use it had, but because Odile said the river had given it up for a reason and she wasn't going to be the one to tell the river it had been wrong. It rode lashed atop Ansel's pack, riding lighter than it ever had, its Vessian seal scoured clean by flood and time and, in one corner, by the blade of a fleet deserter.

They walked north along the escarpment's base, where the old shoreline ran, and by midmorning they reached the first of it: a row of stones breaking the surface of a shrinking pool, laid in a pattern too regular to be natural. A foundation. A house.

Odile crouched at the water's edge, unwrapped her copy of the manifest, and began to sing — quietly, uncertainly at first, the harbor-quarter verse, matching the melody against the stones the way her grandmother must once have matched it against the living street. Ansel took out the survey journal he'd bought with the map, opened it to the first blank page, and wrote the date, and beneath it:

*Village the first. Doors: counting.*

The wren sang its two notes. The water fell another finger's width, uncovering the corner of a doorstep, worn smooth by feet that had gone into the sea generations ago and were, at last, being waited for.

Far to the south, barely visible, a courier's dust rose off the flats toward the capital — verdicts moving, slowly, the way justice moved. But that was downstream, and later. Here there was only the escarpment, and the falling water, and the two of them, and the long shore ahead, and the first name ready on Odile's lips.

She sang it. Ansel wrote it down.
