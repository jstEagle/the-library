## 1. The Debt Collector's Tide

There are two kinds of people who visit a cemetery in Puerto Aliso: the grieving and the paid. I belong to the second category, which is why I was six feet down in the Marisol plot with a shovel and a bad attitude when the tide turned and Marlo Quist came walking through the gate like he owned the ground I was standing in.

Which, technically, he did. He owned most of it.

I heard him before I saw him — the particular crunch of expensive shoes on coral gravel that no one who grew up here ever makes. Locals walk the cemetery paths like they're apologizing to the dead. Quist walked them like he was doing the dead a favor by visiting.

"Vasquez." His voice carried down into the hole the way voices do at the coast, flat and salted. "You're hard to find."

"I'm not hard to find," I said, without looking up. "I'm just expensive to talk to."

The shovel bit into clay that hadn't seen sun since the last century. The Marisol family had bought this plot back when Puerto Aliso still pretended it had a future, and now old Elena Marisol was going into it, and I was the one making the introduction. Grave digging isn't a trade people romanticize, but there's an honesty to it. The ground tells you what it wants. Clay argues. Sand gives up too easy. This particular soil — dense, rust-colored, threaded with shell — wanted to be persuaded, not forced. I'd been persuading it for three hours.

Quist's shadow fell across the grave mouth, cutting off my light. I kept digging.

"You know what I admire about you, Ansel?" He crouched at the edge, careful to keep his trousers out of the dirt. Silver-haired, soft-jawed, dressed like a man who'd read somewhere that money should look tired. "Work ethic. Your mother had none. Your father had plenty, but it went sideways on him. You, though — you'll dig all day for wages that wouldn't keep a gull fed."

"My father's dead," I said. "You can stop using him as a knife."

"He's missing. There's a difference. Missing men have a way of turning up when their daughters owe enough money." He smiled. Quist's smiles were famous the way weather is famous — everyone talked about them, nobody enjoyed them. "Speaking of which."

He let the silence do some work. Men like Quist always let the silence work; it's cheaper than interest.

I straightened up, leaned on the shovel, and finally gave him the courtesy of my face. Sweat had glued my hair to my temples and there was clay to my elbows and I knew exactly what I looked like, because I'd spent thirty years being looked at exactly this way by exactly his kind of man. So I looked back. That's the one advantage of having nothing — nobody can take your stare away from you.

"How much today, Marlo?"

"Today? Nothing. Today is a courtesy call." He produced a folded paper from his jacket anyway, because courtesy calls from Marlo Quist travel with paperwork the way storms travel with wind. "Your mother's account. Final reconciliation. She was thorough in her own way — borrowed from four lenders, all of whom eventually sold to me, because I buy everything eventually. Principal, accumulated interest, and my administrative fees—"

"You invented those fees."

"—come to eleven thousand, four hundred and sixty pesos." He said it gently, the way a doctor says *tumor*. "I've rounded down. Consider it a kindness."

Eleven thousand pesos. I dug graves at forty a day when there were graves to dig, and there weren't always graves to dig, because even death takes holidays in a town of nine hundred people. I did the arithmetic the way I did everything — quickly, bitterly, and alone. Two hundred and eighty-six days of holes. If anyone died that often, we'd have an epidemic.

"I don't have it."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You don't. But here's what you do have." He unfolded the paper and held it where I could see. My mother's signature marched down the margin, getting shakier toward the end, the ink fading as she faded. She'd been dead eight months and I still couldn't look at her handwriting without my chest doing something structural. "This instrument, signed over the house. And the house sits on land that will be worth considerably more once the harbor expansion goes through. Which it will. Everything here gets worth more eventually, Ansel. It's the town's only talent."

"The house stays."

"The house stays if the debt clears by the spring equinox." He folded the paper away again, unhurried. "That's ninety-one days. After that, I exercise the lien, the bank takes its cut, and you can watch strangers renovate your childhood from whatever room you're renting by then."

The tide was coming in below the cliffs. I could hear it — that long, patient dragging sound the sea makes when it's collecting things. It comes in twice a day and takes what it wants and nobody calls the sea a loan shark, but the difference is mostly paperwork.

"Why the equinox?" I asked. It wasn't the question he expected, and I watched him decide whether to answer honestly or charmingly. With Quist they were usually the same thing.

"Because the permits expire then," he said. "The salvage concessions on the San Telmo waters. Thirty years of lawyers fighting over a wreck nobody's found, and it all comes due at the equinox. After that, the government auctions the seabed to whoever bids highest, and I intend to be holding assets that appreciate." He spread his hands, showing me the cemetery, the cliffs, the whole gray-green sweep of Puerto Aliso laid out below us like something on a plate. "Everything moves at once, Vasquez. Money's tidal. You'd know that better than most."

"And if I just walked? Let you take the house?"

He considered it. Genuinely considered it, the way a cat considers a door left open — not because it wants outside, but because it likes knowing the option exists.

"Then I'd take the house," he said. "And I'd wonder about you. Wondering is worse than owing, in my experience. A debtor with nothing to lose stops being predictable, and I've built forty years on predictability." He stood, brushed nothing off his knees, and looked down at me in the hole one more time. "Ninety-one days. Eleven thousand. Or the house, and I start wondering about you."

He left the way he'd come, expensive shoes on coral gravel, and the gate didn't even bang behind him because he'd had someone oil it. That's the kind of man Marlo Quist is. He oils gates. He rounds debts down. He does every small courteous thing except leave you alone.

* * *

I finished the Marisol grave because the dead don't renegotiate, and because Elena Marisol had once paid my mother's electric bill during the winter my mother couldn't get out of bed, and because a debt you can actually pay is a luxury I've learned not to waste.

By the time I tamped the last of the clay and set the marker straight, the light had gone long and coppery and the tide below the cliffs was high enough to lick the cave mouths in the rock face. From up here the water looked calm, almost kind, which is how the sea around Puerto Aliso gets you. It's a liar with excellent posture.

I sat on Elena's fresh mound — disrespectful, probably, but she'd have laughed — and took stock.

Inventory: one shovel, rented, returnable. One pair of boots with maybe two months of sole left. One house under lien, containing furniture I'd already sold half of. And in my shirt pocket, wrapped in a handkerchief because I'm not stupid, a brass compass.

My father's compass. The only thing Tomás Vasquez left behind when he vanished twenty years ago, unless you count the shame, and shame doesn't fit in a pocket. He'd given it to me the week before he disappeared. I was nine. He'd knelt down in the doorway of our house — the house Quist now held like a knife against my throat — and put it in my hands and said, *Ansel, listen. Ground never lies. People lie, maps lie, but ground always tells you the truth if you know how to ask.* Then he'd kissed the top of my head and walked down to the harbor and out of every record the territory keeps.

Everyone said he ran because of the San Telmo. The wreck that ate this town's imagination whole — a treasure galleon lost beyond the reef in 1741, hunted by salvage fleets for decades, never found, the permits for searching its waters passed around like a joint at a party until half the coast claimed a piece of it. My father had been a surveyor for one of those fleets, back when he still had a reputation instead of a cautionary tale. When the permits turned out to be forged — signatures faked, land claims stolen from the Chumara families — his name was the one on the worst of the documents. He vanished the same season. The town did the math out loud, in front of me, for years.

I'd done the math myself, quietly, for longer. And the math never closed. Because I'd grown up watching him read ground, and a man who could tell you what lay under a hillside by the way the grass grew does not forge documents clumsily. Whatever my father signed, he signed scared, or signed trapped, or signed for reasons the town never bothered asking about because the truth was less fun than the scandal.

Ground never lies. People lie. I'd built a whole personality on that sentence, and it had gotten me exactly one skill — I could read soil like other people read faces — and zero pesos.

Below me, the tide kept rising, patient as interest.

* * *

Ferro found me at the Cantina Reja an hour later, which wasn't unusual. Ferro finding me anywhere wasn't unusual; the man had a homing instinct for other people's bad moods, possibly because misery recognized misery. He was seventy if he was a day, built like driftwood, and he drank one beer per hour with the discipline of a monk and the eyes of a man keeping count of everything in the room.

"You talked to Quist," he said, sitting down across from me without asking. In Puerto Aliso, chairs were communal and so were disasters.

"What gave it away?"

"You've got your digging face on. The one where you're calculating how deep a hole would need to be." He signaled the barkeep for two beers, which was extravagant by Ferro standards and therefore alarming. "How much?"

"Eleven thousand. Ninety-one days. Equinox deadline."

Ferro whistled low. "He tied it to the permit expiry."

"He tied it to the permit expiry."

"That's not a debt collection, girl. That's positioning. He wants you desperate on a schedule." He pushed one of the beers across. "Drink. Desperation dehydrates."

I drank. Outside, the harbor lights were coming on, and past them the black line of the reef, and past that the open water where a galleon full of silver had been sleeping for two hundred and eighty years while lawyers billed each other into retirement.

"Ferro. What do you know about the spring tides this year?"

His eyebrows went up, slow as drawbridges. "Equinox tide's a big one. Once-a-generation big, the old men say. My grandfather swore the year it last happened, the sea pulled back so far below the north cliffs you could walk into caves that haven't seen air since the flood." He studied me over his beer. "Why?"

"Because I want to know what the water's hiding," I said. "And I've got ninety-one days to find out."

He looked at me for a long moment — at the clay still under my fingernails, at the compass-shaped lump in my shirt pocket, at whatever it was he saw in my face that made retired fishermen go quiet.

"Your father used to say that," he said finally. "Word for word. Right before everything went wrong for him."

"Yeah," I said, and turned my beer slowly on the sticky table. "I'm counting on it."

Because here's the thing about a deadline: it cuts both ways. Quist thought he'd cornered me, and maybe he had. But a corner has walls, and walls have edges, and I'd spent my whole life reading edges — where the ground drops, where the water runs, where the tide pulls back far enough to show you what it's been keeping.

In ninety-one days, the equinox tide would drain the cliffs below the cemetery where I dug graves for a living.

I intended to be standing on the bottom of the sea when it did.
