## 2. What the Equinox Uncovered

The tide did not come in that morning. It left.

I'd set my alarm for three, which felt like a joke — I dug graves for a living and here I was getting up before the dead did. The cemetery was a gray smear above me as I picked my way down the cliff path, the kind of dark that makes you trust your feet more than your eyes. Below, the sea had pulled back like a fist unclenching, exposing a hundred meters of seabed that hadn't seen air since before I was born. Tide tables called it a perigean spring equinox low. Old Ferro called it *the sea showing its pockets*. Either way, it happened maybe once a generation, and the last time it happened, my father was still alive.

I didn't let myself think about that yet.

The cave was where the old charts said it would be — not my father's charts, those were gone with him, but the fisherman's lore every kid in Puerto Aliso absorbed with their mother's milk: below the third notch in the cliff, past the shelf the waves had chewed into something like stairs, there was a mouth in the rock that breathed twice a day and swallowed anything curious enough to get close at the wrong hour. Two men had drowned in it when I was nine. The town put up no marker. Towns don't mark their warnings; they just repeat them.

But tonight the mouth stood open and dry, a black archway eight feet tall, dripping the last of its ocean down its chin. The air coming out of it smelled of stone and old salt and something else — something organic and sweet-rotten, like the inside of a sealed room.

I stood at the threshold longer than I should have. Not afraid, exactly. It's hard to be afraid of a hole in the ground when you spend your days making holes in the ground for other people. But there's a difference between a grave you dig and a grave the sea dug first, and I could feel that difference on my skin, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm.

"Okay," I said to nobody. My voice came back off the wet rock, flattened, unimpressed with me. "Ninety-one days early. Sue me."

Because here's what Quist didn't know, or maybe did know and didn't care about: the equinox wasn't one tide. It was a season of them, a slow unwinding, each low draining a little deeper than the last until the deepest one bottomed out on the equinox itself. Tonight's was the first real one. Whatever the sea kept in its pockets, tonight it was showing the small change.

I went in with my headlamp and my father's brass compass in my jacket pocket, because I'm not a superstitious woman but I'm not an idiot either, and some weights you carry on purpose.

* * *

The cave went back farther than the drownings suggested. That was the first surprise. The second was the floor.

Not sand. Not rubble. Flagstones — cut, fitted, mortared with something dark that had hardened to the consistency of iron. Somebody had built this floor, and they hadn't built it yesterday, and they hadn't built it for a fishing shack. My headlamp found tool marks in the walls above the waterline, chisel scars in rows, deliberate as stitching. Further in, the passage widened into a chamber, and the chamber had been a room once — a storeroom, maybe, or worse. There were iron rings set into the walls at waist height, green with age, spaced too evenly to be mooring points.

I know what cargo holds look like. I've read enough of my father's old survey reports to know what a wreck site looks like even when the wreck has been hauled out, broken up, and built into somebody's harbor wall over two centuries. This cave had been a harbor once, or an anchorage, or a loading point — some piece of the coast's business that Puerto Aliso had agreed, collectively, to forget.

The Chumara families hadn't forgotten. Nobody ever asked them.

I shook that thought off. It wasn't mine yet. It belonged to a version of this story I hadn't earned.

The chamber narrowed again at the back, funneling into a crack barely wider than my shoulders, and that's where the smell was strongest — that sweet rot, concentrated. My lamp caught a glint that wasn't mineral. Wax. Candle wax, old, melted and re-hardened in drips down the wall, and beneath the drips, wedged into a man-made niche at chest height, a shape wrapped in oilcloth and tarred canvas, sealed with pitch, tied with cord that had gone brittle as hair.

Somebody had cached something here. Somebody who knew tides, knew caves, and knew that a package left loose would be gone within a season — eaten by salt, scattered by storms, or found by someone who'd sell it for beer money.

My hands were shaking. I want to be honest about that. Not from cold, though the cave was cold, that deep cellar cold that stone keeps like a grudge. From recognition. Because I knew the knots.

Sailor's lashings, doubled, with the working end tucked under — the way Tomás Vasquez tied everything, including the laces on my shoes when I was six, including the twine on the parcels he mailed me from his dig sites, back when he still mailed things. You don't forget your father's hands. They're the first handwriting you learn to read.

I cut the cord with my grave-digging knife, which felt appropriate in a way I couldn't articulate and didn't try to.

* * *

Inside the wrapping: a satchel, waterproofed, military surplus by the look of it, the kind of bag surveyors carried before everything went digital. Inside the satchel, wrapped in waxed paper layer after waxed paper layer, dry as the day it was sealed:

Charts.

Survey charts, hand-drawn, inked on linen-backed paper, the linework fine and confident and so familiar it hurt. I'd grown up falling asleep to that linework. Contours, soundings, bearings, depth annotations in a cramped right-leaning hand. And across the top of the first sheet, in block capitals:

**SAN TELMO — SITE SURVEY — T.V.**

T.V. Tomás Vasquez. My father. Dated the year he walked out of Puerto Aliso and never came back. Dated the year Marlo Quist started asking around town about him, the year the salvage interests started circling, the year my mother stopped saying his name out loud and started saying *that man* instead, the year she took the first loan she couldn't pay and started the slow financial drowning that Quist's people finished collecting on eighteen months ago.

There were eleven sheets. I counted them twice because my hands weren't steady enough to trust the first count. Soundings of the outer shoal. A sketch of the reef pass with tidal windows penciled in the margins — numbers, times, moon phases, a whole secret arithmetic of when the San Telmo could be reached and when it couldn't. A plan view of the wreck itself, or what my father believed was the wreck: hull outline inferred, mast field plotted, a hold section marked with a symbol I didn't recognize and a note in Spanish, small, almost apologetic:

*No está todo aquí. Lo que importa no es el oro.*

*Not everything is here. What matters isn't the gold.*

I sat down on the flagstones, in a cave the sea visits twice a day, and read my dead father's handwriting by headlamp, and understood exactly two things.

First: he hadn't vanished. Vanished men don't cache supplies. Vanished men don't seal satchels against saltwater with knots their daughters would recognize. He had *planned*. He had come down here in some other generation's low tide, or rigged a dive, or bribed some fisherman whose name was probably still known to Ferro, and he had hidden his life's work in a hole in the cliff, and then he had gone away and gotten himself killed or disappeared or whatever verb the truth turned out to want, and he had left this here the way you leave a letter you're too much of a coward to mail.

Second: the charts were worth a fortune.

That's the part I keep circling back to, because I want to tell you I sat in that cave thinking noble thoughts about history and justice, and I'd be lying. Here's what I actually thought, in order:

*The San Telmo.* The wreck the salvage fleet has hunted for forty years. The concession permits, the dredge operations, the government auctions, the whole industrial apparatus of greed that parks itself on our coast every decade like a migratory bird. Every fisherman in Puerto Aliso could draw you the approximate location from lore, and every fleet that ever came went home empty, because lore is approximate and the reef doesn't forgive approximation. But these charts weren't lore. These were soundings. Bearings. A fix.

Here's what I thought, second: *Quist gives me ninety-one days.*

Here's what I thought, third, and this is the one that scared me, sitting alone in the dark with my father's ghost leaking out of a waterproof bag: *I could clear the debt. All of it. My mother's debts, Ferro's mortgage, maybe half the harbor's, if I played it right. One sale. One buyer. One set of coordinates.*

And underneath all three thoughts, quiet as groundwater, a fourth one I didn't want to look at directly: *What matters isn't the gold.* Then what did? Why hide eleven sheets of linen-backed certainty unless the thing they pointed at was worth more than the silver everyone assumed was down there? My father hadn't been a treasure hunter. He'd been an archaeologist, disgraced or not, and archaeologists don't risk their lives charting wrecks for bullion. They chart them for what bullion sits on top of.

I repacked the satchel the way I'd found it, minus the cord I'd cut. I wrapped it in my spare shirt, zipped it inside my jacket, and pressed my palm flat against the niche in the wall, the way you touch a headstone to make sure it's real.

"You could have told me," I said. My voice did that flat echo thing again. "You could have mailed it. You had years."

The cave said nothing. Caves are good at that. I've spent enough time in cemeteries to know the dead's whole repertoire, and silence is most of it.

* * *

Coming out was harder than going in, because the tide had turned while I read. The sea was already licking back up its flagstone corridor, ankle-deep and rising, patient as a debt collector. I waded the last thirty meters with the satchel held over my head like an offering, and the water took my boots, my knees, my hips, cold enough to make my breath go ragged, and all I could think, absurdly, was *Quist would appreciate this metaphor.*

I climbed the cliff path as the sun came up over Puerto Aliso — a dirty gold light on tin roofs and drying nets, the harbor already stirring, and far out past the reef, right on schedule, a smudge of smoke on the horizon that hadn't been there last week. Industrial smoke. Diesel and ambition.

The Corvina was coming. Reyes's fleet, with its permits and its sonar and its lawyers, steaming toward waters that my father had measured to the fathom twenty-some years ago and sealed in a cave for whoever was brave enough or stupid enough to go looking.

I stood at the top of the cliffs with the satchel against my ribs and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. Six tide windows between now and the permit expiry, if the marginal notes meant what I thought they meant. Six chances to reach a wreck that a professional fleet with state backing was also hunting. I owned a shovel, a knife, a compass, and a debt.

What I needed was a boat. What I needed was a crew. What I needed, specifically, was a retired fisherman named Ferro who owed money to the same vulture that owned me, and who had a boat called Mercy that he loved more than he'd ever admit and trusted less than he should.

Below me, the town was waking up. Above me, gulls were doing their usual commentary. In my jacket, against my heart, my father's charts weighed less than a Bible and more than a coffin.

I'd gone into that cave looking for the sea's small change.

I'd come out holding the deed to a fortune — or the map to my own grave, depending entirely on who I decided to be in the next six tides.

The smoke on the horizon got a little closer.
