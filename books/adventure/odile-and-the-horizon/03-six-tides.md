## 3. Six Tides

I spread the charts across my kitchen table the way other people spread out a winning lottery ticket — except mine was water-stained, salt-crusted, and signed by a dead man whose signature I hadn't seen since I was nine years old.

The satchel had held more than I'd first thought. Beneath the main survey chart of the San Telmo's resting place were six smaller sheets, each one a tide table drawn in my father's cramped, slanting hand, annotated in two colors of ink: black for what he'd observed, red for what he'd calculated. There were also three pencils worn to stubs, a brass diviner's rule I didn't recognize, and a note that said only *Marea viva — contar hacia atrás* — spring tide, count backward.

My father had always believed in leaving instructions for people who weren't there yet.

I made coffee I couldn't afford and settled in to do the one thing I was genuinely good at, which is reading ground. Or in this case, reading water pretending to be ground.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about archaeology: it's mostly arithmetic wearing a romantic costume. My father taught me that before he taught me anything else. Before the disgrace, before the vanishing, back when I still thought his job was treasure and adventure instead of measurements and betrayal, he'd sit me on his knee with a site map and say, *Ansel, mi vida, the past doesn't hide. It just waits for someone patient enough to do the math.*

So I did the math.

The main chart showed the coastline from Punta Aliso down to the reef shelf called the Dientes — the Teeth — a jagged limestone barrier that had chewed up fishing boats for two centuries and kept everyone with sense on the harbor side. Beyond the Teeth, marked in red ink with a precision that made my chest ache, lay the San Telmo. Not where the salvage fleet had been dragging their magnetometers for thirty years — they'd been working the wrong side of the reef entirely, seduced by an old wreck fragment that had washed into the shallows decades ago. My father had found the real site in deep water past the Teeth, tucked into a sand channel at forty-one meters, which explained why no free-diving fisherman had ever stumbled over her.

Forty-one meters. That's not diving; that's visiting another country without a passport. You get maybe twelve minutes of useful bottom time if you're careful, less if you're me, because I hadn't been below ten meters since before my mother got sick and I sold my gear to pay for the first round of treatments that didn't work anyway.

But depth wasn't the real problem. The real problem was written all over those six smaller sheets.

The channel through the Teeth — the only gap wide enough to bring a boat and dive gear through without becoming part of the reef's collection — ran barely four meters deep at high slack. On a normal day, the surge through that gap would tumble a swimmer like laundry. It was passable only during specific windows: the last hour of the ebb and the first hour of the flood around each spring tide, when the current dropped below something my father had calculated as *nodoso* — manageable, knotted but not strangling.

I counted the tide tables. Six sheets. Six windows.

Then I checked the dates against the calendar pinned above my sink, the one I used mostly to track when Marlo Quist's collectors came by, and felt the coffee go cold in my stomach.

Six windows. Six tides between now and the twenty-first of the month. And on the twenty-second, the Reyes Salvage Concession — the permit that gave Captain Ilsa Reyes exclusive rights to survey these exact waters — expired. The government auction would follow within the week. After that, whatever happened beyond the Teeth belonged to whoever could buy the biggest dredge.

Six chances. Then the door closed forever.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what I do professionally, which is digging, though this time it was only Mrs. Abernathy's plot in the cliffside cemetery, and she was in no position to complain about my distraction. Her grandson had paid in advance, cash, which put me exactly eleven percent of the way toward what Quist said I owed him. I shoveled and calculated.

Eleven percent per grave. Quist wanted payment by the equinox. Even if Puerto Aliso suffered a biblical plague, I couldn't shovel fast enough.

Which meant the charts weren't just a discovery anymore. They were the only arithmetic left that added up.

A silver cargo manifest from 1847 — my father's notes listed the registered cargo, cross-checked against port records he'd apparently copied somewhere — put the San Telmo's hold at roughly sixty thousand pesos of the old coinage, plus ingots the crown never bothered registering because they never intended the crew to survive the voyage. At today's scrap value alone, before anyone got sentimental about numismatics, we were talking about enough money to erase my mother's debt twice over, buy Ferro's mortgage out from under Quist, and still have change for a headstone that didn't lean.

One dive. One good recovery. One sale to a buyer who didn't ask questions, because buyers like that exist everywhere money does.

That was the fantasy version. Let me tell you the version with numbers attached.

Problem one: a boat. I owned a rowboat with a soft patch amidships that I used to check crab pots. The Teeth would eat it in the first minute of surge. I needed something with a keel and an engine, and in Puerto Aliso that meant Ferro, and Ferro meant cutting him in, and Ferro meant explaining why a gravedigger suddenly needed to run the reef at dawn.

Problem two: a crew. A forty-one-meter dive solo, through a tidal gap, off a small boat, with no surface support, was how you ended up as a name read aloud at the fishermen's memorial mass. I needed at least one person topside who could keep a boat on station and pull me up if my brain went soft from nitrogen. Ferro again, probably. Ferro, who hadn't spoken to me in three years, not since my mother's funeral, where he'd shown up late, drunk, and honest — three things that don't mix well at gravesides. He'd told me my father was a thief and walked out before the eulogy. He wasn't entirely wrong, which was the problem.

Problem three: gear. Regulators, tanks, weights, a lift bag capable of raising even one ingot. All of it existed in Puerto Aliso in exactly two places: the harbormaster's impound shed, and aboard the Corvina, which was due in port within days with the full weight of the state smiling behind it.

And problem four, the one I kept circling back to while Mrs. Abernathy's grandson watched me work with the wary respect people give gravediggers: my father had found this wreck, charted it, and then hidden the charts in a cave instead of selling them. Tomás Vasquez was many things — careless with truth, careless with loyalty, careless with me — but he was not careless with money. If he'd found sixty thousand pesos of silver and walked away from it, either he'd found something else down there too, something that scared him more than poverty did, or someone had made sure walking away was safer than staying.

Both possibilities pointed at the same ugly conclusion: the San Telmo wasn't just a treasure. It was a wound somebody wanted kept closed.

* * *

That night I sat with the compass — the brass one, the only inheritance that mattered — and turned it over in my lamplight. My father had given it to me the year before he vanished. I was eight. He'd crouched down to my height on the harbor seawall and said, *This doesn't point north, Ansel. It points true. North is where everyone agrees to look. True is where you actually are.* I'd thought it was a riddle. It took me twenty years and a waterlogged satchel to hear it properly.

The compass needle wobbled and settled, the way it always did, a degree or two off magnetic north, corrected by some internal adjustment my father had built in. Pointing true.

I laid the six tide sheets out in sequence and looked at them until the pattern stopped being numbers and became a schedule. First window: four days out, dawn flood, ninety minutes of usable calm. Second: five and a half days later, mid-morning. The intervals stretched after that — the springs fading toward neaps — so that the sixth and final window fell on the twentieth, one day before the permits died. My father had counted backward from the expiration date, just like his note said. He'd known, the year he disappeared, exactly when the clock ran out. Which meant he'd known about the concession before it was public knowledge. Which meant he'd probably helped write it.

I poured the last of the coffee and drank it cold, because cold coffee is honest coffee.

Here was the ledger, then. Assets: one set of irreplaceable charts, one talent for reading ground, one brass compass pointing true, and a body strong enough to dig graves, which is not the same as strong enough to haul myself up a shot line from forty-one meters against a running tide. Liabilities: eleven percent of a debt paid, a boat I didn't own, a crew I hadn't asked, gear I couldn't touch, and a salvage captain arriving in port with state fanfare and my father's forged signature on her paperwork — though I didn't know about the forgery yet, not for certain. I only knew the shape of it, the way you know a grave is occupied before you see the marker.

Cold arithmetic. That's all I had. Cold arithmetic says: six tides, one wreck, and every reason in the world to believe that whoever moves first owns the story.

Quist moved on his schedule. Reyes would move on hers. The government auction would move on paper and stamps and men in offices who'd never smelled brine.

Which left me, and the tide, and four days.

I rolled the charts, sleeved them in oilcloth, and went looking for the one man in Puerto Aliso who owned a boat stubborn enough to survive the Teeth and a grudge big enough to need spending.

Ferro owed my father a debt of hatred. But hatred, I've learned, is just attention with the polarity reversed, and attention can be negotiated with.

The Mercy was moored at the north quay, riding low and rust-streaked, and Ferro was sitting in her stern like a gargoyle with a beer, watching the water as if it owed him an apology.

I started walking.
