## 4. The Fleet Comes In

The Corvina came in on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the day Puerto Aliso buried its dead and paid its debts, and the salvage fleet was going to be both.

I was up at the cemetery when the first horn sounded across the harbor — three long blasts, the kind ships use for arrivals that expect applause. I straightened over the open grave I'd been cutting for old Marta Ilves, wiped my hands on my thighs, and looked down at the water below the cliffs. The morning fog had burned off early, and there she was: a gray-white slab of a vessel with an orange crane arm folded against her superstructure like a mantis foreleg, escorted in by two smaller tenders flying pennants I didn't recognize and one flag I did — the territorial standard, snapped out crisp from the lead tender's stern, which meant somebody in the capital had signed off on this parade personally.

"Well," I said to the hole in the ground. "There goes the neighborhood."

Marta, being dead, declined to comment. I finished her grave anyway, because the dead of Puerto Aliso have always gotten better service than the living, and by the time I'd tamped the last spadeful the whole town was already streaming down the cliff road toward the harbor like iron filings toward a magnet. Even the church let out early. Father Benítez locked the doors himself, which told you everything — the man hadn't locked anything since the hurricane year, on the theory that God's house should stay open to strays. Apparently even strays could wait for the fleet.

I walked down with the crowd because not walking would have been its own kind of announcement, and I'd spent eleven years learning how to be furniture in this town. Furniture doesn't run from parades.

* * *

The harbor had been dressed for the occasion. Bunting on the fish sheds, which nobody had repainted since before I was born. A banner strung between the harbormaster's office and the chandlery: WELCOME CORVINA — PROSPERITY FOR PUERTO ALISO. Somebody had spelled "prosperity" wrong on the first attempt and painted over it, and if you stood at the right angle you could still read the ghost letters. PROSPERITY. The town's whole relationship with fortune, rendered in cheap latex.

They'd cleared the east pier for the docking. On it: a podium, a row of folding chairs occupied by men whose suits had been made somewhere with air conditioning, and a sound system that crackled like frying fat. The harbor itself had been swept — actually swept, divers sent down to clear the crab pots and mooring chains from the approach channel so the Corvina could come alongside without embarrassing anyone. Fishermen who'd fought each other over pot lines for thirty years had been ordered to move them overnight, and had moved them, and were now standing along the pier rail wearing expressions I can only describe as funereal hospitality.

And then Captain Ilsa Reyes stepped off her own gangway before it was even fully lowered, and I understood why they'd bothered with the bunting.

She was not what the town had been promised. The brochures — and yes, there had been brochures, left in stacks at the chandlery and the cantina for weeks — showed a broad-shouldered woman in a hard hat, jaw set, eyes on the horizon. The woman who came down the gangway wore no hat, a linen jacket the color of dry sand, and the easy smile of someone who has never once needed to be liked but finds it useful anyway. She was smaller than the photographs. She moved like the deck was already hers, which, to be fair, it was.

"Captain Reyes!" The harbormaster, Delgado, had his good voice on, the one he saved for inspectors and his mother. "Welcome to Puerto Aliso."

"Thank you, Señor Delgado." Her voice carried without effort, pitched exactly to the back of the crowd. That's a skill. Most people either shout or mumble; Reyes spoke the way a lighthouse turns — steady, aimed, indifferent to whether you wanted the light. "It's a fine harbor. Deep water, honest approaches. You've kept her well."

You could feel the town lean into that like cats into sun. *You've kept her well.* Three words, and half the pier forgot about the crab pots.

She worked them for twenty minutes. I'll give her that — it was craftsmanship. She shook hands with the fishermen and used their names, which meant she'd done her homework, which meant she'd done more homework on this town than the territorial government had done in forty years. She admired Ferro's Mercy — actually stopped, put a hand flat on the old boat's gunwale, and said, "Cedar over oak frames? They don't build them like this anymore. This is a working hull." Ferro, who trusts nothing that floats higher than his own waterline, went pink to the ears. She asked after the school. She asked after the cannery closure. She said the word *jobs* four times, and each time she said it slightly differently, the way a jeweler turns a stone to catch different light.

Then she took the podium, and the state men behind her arranged their faces into agreement.

"Puerto Aliso," she began, and paused, and let the name sit there like she was tasting it. "For sixty years, the San Telmo has been a story you tell your children. A ghost ship. A rumor with gold in it." Laughter, warm, complicit. "I'm not here for rumors. I'm here with sonar, saturation dive systems, a concession from this government—" a nod to the suits, who nodded back like bobbing toys "—and a schedule. In ninety days, we will either raise the San Telmo or prove, with data, that she cannot be raised. Either way, Puerto Aliso stops waiting on a ghost."

Applause. Real applause, too — not polite, hungry. I stood at the edge of it with my arms crossed and my heart doing something complicated, because I knew something the applause didn't.

I knew where the San Telmo lay. To the meter.

* * *

Here's the thing about watching a con from inside the mark's skin: you see the seams.

While Reyes talked, the work went on behind her, unglamorous and constant. Tenders ferried crates from the Corvina's hold to the east warehouse — the one Delgado had leased to the fleet that same week, over the objection of exactly nobody with money. Crate stencils: SONAR ARRAY, DIVE SYS, COMPRESSOR. And one crate, longer than the others, handled by two men instead of one, stenciled with nothing but a file number.

I drifted along the fence line while the crowd pressed forward to shake the captain's hand. Nobody watches the fence when there's a captain to watch. I got to the warehouse side door, which was propped open with a coil of rope for the loaders, and I stood in the shade of it looking like a woman waiting for someone, and I watched them unload the long crate.

The lid came off right there on the dock — no, they wouldn't store documents sealed, they'd need them immediately for the permit inspections, the state men would want their copies — and a man in a fleet polo lifted out a document tube, cracked it, and spread a roll of paper on a sawhorse under a work lamp.

I know paperwork. I dig graves; I also fill them out. Death certificates, burial permits, plot deeds — Puerto Aliso's municipal forms have passed through my hands for eleven years, and before that, through my father's. Tomás Vasquez taught me to read a survey plat before he taught me to swim. He drew charts the way other people pray: obsessively, beautifully, with a hand that curved its capitals like waves and crossed its sevens with a long, deliberate slash.

The document on the sawhorse was a coastal survey annex. Bathymetric readings, reef offsets, a coordinate block for a designated salvage zone. And at the bottom, in the certification line, a signature:

*T. Vasquez.*

My father's name, in handwriting that was almost his.

Almost. The capitals were right — that lovely wave-curve. But the hand that wrote them had hesitated. There's a tremor in forgery, a place where the pen slows to check itself, and once you've seen it you can't unsee it. The *V* started confident and then corrected mid-stroke, a hair's width of doubt inked forever. And the seven-slash — my father's slash swept down-left, aggressive, a man slashing at the page. This one lay flat. Careful. A signature drawn, not written.

Someone had practiced. Someone had practiced a great deal. And they'd gotten it almost perfect, which is worse than getting it badly wrong, because a bad forgery gets caught and an almost-perfect one gets believed, and my father's name was now sitting on the legal foundation of the largest salvage operation this coast had ever seen.

The date on the certification was fourteen years ago. The year he vanished.

I stood in the shade of the doorway and felt the fear I'd been carrying since the cave — the low, animal fear of a broke woman holding something valuable — change state. Not evaporate. Change, the way water changes when it hits a hot pan. Fear becoming something with a temperature.

Because here's what I knew that the document didn't say: my father never certified that survey. He couldn't have. Fourteen years ago, in the spring, he'd sat at our kitchen table with a bottle of rum he wasn't drinking and told my mother — thinking I was asleep in the next room, and I was, mostly — that the government men wanted his name on papers for ground he'd never walked. That he'd told them no. That saying no to those particular men was like standing on the beach and telling the tide to stop.

He vanished that autumn. Everyone said ran. Everyone said debts. My mother said nothing at all, ever again, about anything, until she died owing Marlo Quist money I'm still paying for.

And now his forged name was on a crate being unloaded with fanfare, under a banner with a misspelled word for prosperity, while a woman in a linen jacket told my town that the ghost ship was finally going to pay out.

* * *

"You're not clapping."

Ferro, beside me at the fence. I don't know when he'd come up. Old men move quietly when they decide to; it's the only stealth most of them have left.

"Didn't seem to need mine," I said.

"Hm." He chewed on something — tobacco, judgment, hard to say. "Reyes is real. That part's true. The Corvina could lift a cathedral. Question is what she's lifting it *for*, and who signed the paper that lets her."

"And if the paper's a lie?"

He looked at me then, properly, the way he looked at weather coming over the reef. "Then it's not a question for the pier," he said. "It's a question for whoever knows better. And women who know better and stand at fences looking like that—" he nodded at whatever my face was doing "—end up in the water, one way or another. Best decide which way before the tide does it for you."

He shuffled off toward Mercy, and I stayed at the fence a while longer.

Out past the harbor mouth, beyond the reef, the sea lay flat and blue and innocent as a ledger awaiting entries. Somewhere under it, six tide windows' worth of access, ninety days of concession, a fortune in silver, and a dead man's doubting signature holding the whole structure up like a keel.

Reyes had come for the San Telmo with sonar and lawyers and the state's blessing.

I'd come for it with a stolen satchel, a mortgaged boat I didn't own yet, and a talent for reading ground.

On paper, she was winning. But then, on paper, so was my father's signature — and I was the only person on this coast who knew it was a lie, and the anger had finished boiling now, clean and clarifying, burning off the last of the fear like fog off the headland.

Ninety days. Six tides.

I turned away from the harbor and went to find out how much a boat called Mercy would cost me — and how much refusing to sell might.
