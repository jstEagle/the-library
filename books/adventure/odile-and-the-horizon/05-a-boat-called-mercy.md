## 5. A Boat Called Mercy

The trouble with being old in a harbor town is that everyone knows your price before you do.

I was mending nets on the dock behind my house — the same nets I'd been mending for three years, because mending is cheaper than fishing and fishing is cheaper than thinking — when Ansel Vasquez came down the ramp with the tide. She walked like her father used to walk, like a woman reading the ground under her feet for lies.

"Ferro," she said.

"Vasquez." I didn't look up. "Come to buy fish? Because I don't have any. Come to sell something? Because I don't have money."

"I need a boat."

I let that sit between us while I finished the knot I was working. Some conversations you have to let age, like meat or grudges. "There are boats," I said finally. "The harbor's full of them. Reyes's fleet alone could carry this whole town to the mainland and back twice."

"Not those boats. Your boat."

"My boat." I laughed, and it came out with rust in it. "Mercy isn't mine. Mercy belongs to Marlo Quist, same as half this dock, same as — " I stopped, because I'd been about to say *same as you*, and there are ways to be cruel by accident and ways to be cruel on purpose, and at sixty-one I've learned to tell them apart. Mostly.

"That's what I heard," she said. "Which is why I'm here and not at the marina."

She sat down on an upturned crate without being asked, which I respected, and put her hands on her knees, which I didn't, because it meant she planned to stay. Up close she looked worse than the last time I'd seen her, which had been at her mother's funeral, where she'd dug the grave herself rather than pay the cemetery crew. There was a bruise-yellow shadow along her jaw I chose not to ask about. In Puerto Aliso you learn which questions cost more than the answers.

"How much do you owe Quist?" I asked.

"Wrong question."

"It's the only question that matters when a Vasquez comes asking to borrow a boat."

Her jaw tightened, and for a second I saw the girl she must have been — the one who followed Tomás around the dig sites asking why the ground kept secrets. Then it passed, and what was left was harder and sadder and about my own age in spirit if not in years.

"Enough," she said. "More than enough. And I'm not asking to borrow. I'm offering a partnership. Fifty-fifty."

"On what?"

She looked out at the water the way you look at a door you're not sure you want to open. The Corvina sat at anchor beyond the breakwater, all cranes and floodlights, lit up like a carnival that ate towns instead of entertaining them.

"You know what the equinox tide did," she said.

"Everyone knows. Drained the caves below the cliffs for the first time in thirty years. Half the town went down to gawk at the starfish."

"And you?"

"I stayed home. Caves full of water one day and air the next — that's not a miracle, that's arithmetic. Miracles kill more fishermen than storms do."

Something moved across her face. Not quite a smile. "My father would have liked you."

"Your father liked nobody, as far as I remember. That was his problem."

"No," she said quietly. "That was other people's problem. His problem was signing things he shouldn't have."

I set down the netting needle then, because my hands had gone still on their own, the way they do before weather changes. Tomás Vasquez. Twenty years gone, and the name still worked on this harbor like a hook working on a gutted fish. Half the town called him a fraud who sold the Chumara land claims to men with dredges. The other half said he was pushed. Nobody said both out loud, because both together were too heavy to lift.

"What did you find in that cave?" I asked.

"A satchel."

"With what in it?"

"Charts."

"Charts of what?"

She met my eyes, and I'll give her this: she didn't flinch, and she didn't lie, and she didn't say it either. All three at once takes a kind of discipline most people can't manage standing still.

"If I tell you," she said, "you're in it. And being in it with me means being in it against Captain Ilsa Reyes, whose ship is sitting right there, and against Marlo Quist, who owns your boat and my life. So before I answer, you should decide how much of your remaining years you want to spend afraid."

I looked at my hands. Fisherman's hands, scarred and knuckled, good for nothing now but knots and cards. I looked at Mercy riding her mooring in the dusk light, gray hull, patched deck, the only thing I'd ever built that lasted longer than a season. I looked at the Corvina, and I thought about what Reyes's advance crews had been doing all week — buying drinks, buying silence, buying men I'd known since they were boys stealing bait off my lines.

Then I thought about the mortgage papers in the tin box under my bed, and the date written on them, and how many months stood between me and the day Quist's people came for the boat.

"Sit still," I told her, and went inside, and came back with two glasses and a bottle of aguardiente that had been waiting for an occasion worth regretting. I poured us each three fingers.

"To partnerships," I said. "May we both live long enough to resent them."

We drank. She coughed, which pleased me unreasonably. Then she opened a canvas roll on the crate between us, and I looked down at charts drawn in a hand I recognized from twenty years of harbor gossip and one year of actual acquaintance — Tomás Vasquez drew numbers the way some men draw saints, with devotion and no perspective — and what the charts showed was a wreck past the outer reef, in water deep enough to drown every ambition I'd ever had.

"The San Telmo," she said.

I sat very still. Every kid in Puerto Aliso grows up on the San Telmo the way kids elsewhere grow up on pirates. Silver from the colonial mines, a hold full of it, lost on the reef in 1789 with forty souls aboard — and, if you believed the older stories, with Chumara laborers chained in the bilge who never got names in any official account. The salvage concessions had chased it for decades. Permits expiring, auctions pending, the government selling the sea in parcels to whoever promised the loudest.

"This is real?" I said. "Not a dead man's fantasy?"

"He surveyed it himself. Before he disappeared."

"Before he ran, you mean."

Her eyes came up sharp. "You knew him."

"I drank with him. Different thing, mostly." I turned the chart toward the lantern light. "He came to me once, near the end. Asked if Mercy could handle the outer passage in a blow. I told him no boat could, and he smiled like I'd confirmed something, and three weeks later he was gone and the whole coast was looking for a body that never washed up."

Ansel's breath caught — just barely, just enough. "What else did he say?"

"That whatever he was doing, it wasn't for the money." I took the glass and finished it. "I thought he was drunk. Men who talk like that are usually drunk, or dying, or both."

* * *

We talked until the lantern burned low and the tide turned beneath the pilings, and by the end of it I had agreed to the stupidest thing I'd done since I voted in a municipal election.

Fifty-fifty. Her charts, my boat, my hands on the wheel. Six tide windows before the permits died and the auction opened the reef to Reyes's industrial teeth. We'd run the outer passage at slack water, dive the shoal, and see whether a dead man's arithmetic held.

"One condition," I said, as she rolled the charts away. "Nobody dies. Not you, not me, and especially not anybody we have to explain to afterward."

"You think Reyes will extend us the same courtesy?"

"Reyes doesn't matter. What matters is that when this goes wrong — and it will go wrong, Ansel, everything goes wrong, that's the one law this coast has never broken — I want to be able to stand on my dock and say I did what I said I'd do. It's all a man like me has. Say it and do it. Everything else gets repossessed."

She studied me for a long moment. "You're not what I expected."

"Nobody ever is. That's why expectations are free."

She almost smiled. Then she stood, tucked the canvas roll inside her coat, and paused at the top of the ramp.

"Ferro. Why? You could sell me out tonight. One call to Quist, one call to Reyes, and your mortgage problem solves itself. They'd probably throw in a new engine."

It was a fair question, so I gave it a fair answer, or as close to fair as I could get while telling the truth.

"Because twenty-two years ago I fished these waters with your father, and when the Chumara families got pushed off their claims, I said nothing. I told myself it was politics, above my head, none of my business. My business was fish." I looked at my hands again. "And then my wife got sick, and the fish stopped coming, and I learned exactly how much of my business was actually mine. None of it. It was all always somebody else's business, and I'd spent my life pretending otherwise because pretending was comfortable."

"So this is penance."

"This is interest," I said. "Penance would be doing it for free. Fifty-fifty means I'm still a selfish old man, and I intend to stay one."

This time she did smile, briefly, like sun through squall clouds. Then it faded, and she said the thing I'd been waiting for her to say since she sat down on my crate — the warning, the one that separates the brave from the merely lucky.

"Ferro. If Reyes offers you something — anything — you take it seriously. Don't laugh at it, don't refuse it fast. People refuse fast when they're proud, and proud people end up in the water."

"She's already tried buying the town."

"She's bought half of it. The other half she hasn't needed yet." Ansel looked back at the Corvina's lights, and her voice dropped into something flat and cold. "I've read her permits, Ferro. I know what she does with paper. Paper is how she buries people without digging holes. But she keeps shovels too. Ask anyone who's refused her and can still talk."

"And you're not afraid."

"Oh, I'm terrified. I'm terrified the way you're terrified of a riptide — respectfully, and while swimming anyway." She pulled her collar up against the night wind coming off the water. "First window opens in four days. Slack tide, dawn. Have Mercy ready."

She went up the ramp and into the dark, walking like her father, reading the ground.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Old men don't waste nights sleeping when there's regret to do instead.

I sat at my kitchen table with the aguardiente and the tin box, and I read my mortgage papers until I had them memorized, and then I did what I always do when a decision won't sit still: I talked to Marta. Not to her grave, up on the cliff — to her memory, which lives in the kitchen, in the way the light falls at certain hours, in the second chair nobody sits in.

"Four days," I told the chair. "Our girl Vasquez wants to take Mercy past the reef after silver and ghosts."

The chair said nothing, the way chairs do, but I heard her anyway, the way I always do. *You'll say yes,* she said. *You always say yes to drowning men. It's why I married you and why you'll die broke.*

"Broke is a condition," I said aloud. "Ashamed is a state. I've been ashamed longer than I've been broke."

That wasn't entirely true, but marriage teaches you that truth is a net — you keep what it holds and let the rest swim on.

Near midnight I went down to check Mercy's mooring, because I couldn't help it, and found a man standing on my dock.

He was young, clean-shaven, wearing a jacket too nice for Puerto Aliso and shoes that had never touched a deck. He had the particular relaxed posture of a man being paid to seem relaxed.

"Evening," he said. "Ferro Salas?"

"Depends who's asking."

"Name's Doyen. I work fleet logistics for Captain Reyes." He said it the way priests say blessings — automatically, expecting it to mean something. "Word is you've got the best handling of the outer passage on this coast. Captain's putting together local consultants. Good rates. Steady work through the concession term."

"Consultants," I repeated.

"Local knowledge. Reef lines, tide behavior, that sort of thing." He smiled. "Exclusivity clause, of course. Can't have our consultants running private charters on the side."

There it was, faster than even Ansel had predicted. Four days hadn't even elapsed; the offer had beaten the tide windows to shore. I wondered who had talked. The harbormaster's boy, probably, who'd seen Ansel come down my ramp. Or maybe Reyes simply bought everyone preemptively, the way some men salt fields they haven't conquered yet.

"Steady work," I said. "Good rates. And if I decline?"

Doyen's smile didn't move, but something behind it did, the way a shark's eye moves. "Captain Reyes prefers not to hear no. Not because she punishes it. Because she doesn't understand it." He set a card on the bollard, weighted it with a coin — a gold coin, old, worn smooth, the kind of coin that comes out of wrecks or out of stories about wrecks. "Three days. Think it over."

He left the way he'd come, quiet as paperwork.

I stood on my dock a long time, listening to the water slap the pilings, holding the coin. It was beautiful. It was also a message: *we know what you know.* Or possibly: *we know what you might know.* With people like Reyes, precision doesn't matter. Fear works fine at any resolution.

In the morning I sent word to Ansel through Sela Chumara's granddaughter, who sells bait on the north quay and asks no questions, which makes her the most valuable information broker on this coast.

One line, because I've never trusted long messages:

*They came. Window one stands. Bring the charts.*

And then I went and started patching Mercy's hull, because whatever happened in four days, my boat would be ready, and ready was the only prayer I knew how to say anymore.
