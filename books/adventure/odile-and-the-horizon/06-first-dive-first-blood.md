## 6. First Dive, First Blood

The tide didn't wait for me to be ready, which is the tide's whole personality in a nutshell.

I'd spent two days doing arithmetic with my father's charts, and the arithmetic kept coming out ugly. The San Telmo's mast field — the scatter of spars and standing rigging that marks a wreck like a headstone marks a man — lay beyond the outer shoal, in water that only opened up during specific windows. Six of them, by Tomás's careful notation, before the concession permits expired and the government auction turned the whole bay into a lottery ticket for whoever could afford the most lawyers.

Six tides. I had a boat held together with prayer and paint, one retired fisherman who hated me on principle, and lungs that had been negotiating with me since I was nineteen.

It would have to do.

* * *

Ferro was waiting at the dock before dawn, which surprised me until I saw the coffee. He'd brought two cups, and he handed me mine like it was a business transaction, which, in fairness, it was.

"You look terrible," he said.

"Thank you. You look like a man who slept in his boat."

"I did sleep in my boat." He said it without shame, the way other men announce their golf scores. "Wanted to make sure nobody touched her overnight."

"Anybody try?"

"Somebody looked." He squinted out at the gray water. "Reyes's people have been walking the docks since Tuesday. Writing things down. Boat names, hull numbers, who talks to whom." He gave me a sideways glance. "You're on somebody's list now, Vasquez. Congratulations. It's the only list in this town that gets longer instead of shorter."

I drank my coffee and let that sit where it landed. "You can still back out."

"I'm seventy-one years old and mortgaged to Marlo Quist. Backing out is the one thing I can't afford." He spat over the side, an old man's benediction. "Get in."

Mercy was a thirty-foot fishing boat that had been beautiful sometime around the invention of the diesel engine. She smelled of salt, bilge, and forty years of Ferro's cigarettes, and she handled like a drunk aunt at a wedding. But she moved, and she knew these waters the way I knew grave dirt — by feel, by memory, by all the small betrayals of the ground underneath.

We ran east past the point while the sky went from charcoal to bruise. Ferro navigated without instruments, which should have terrified me but mostly just made me jealous. He'd point at nothing and say "reef there," and there the reef would be, right on schedule, like he'd ordered it in advance.

The outer shoal was a broken line of white water half a mile past the reef's elbow. Beyond it, the chart showed a sandy basin at eighteen fathoms — deep enough to hide a ship, shallow enough to reach on compressed air if you were stupid or motivated. I was both.

"This is as close as I take her," Ferro said, killing the engine. "Current runs through there like it owes somebody money. You go down, you come up fast, and you come up *here*, not wherever the water feels like putting you."

"And if I don't come up?"

"Then I'll drink your coffee too." He was already rigging the mooring line to the old anchor buoy, hands moving with the economy of someone who'd done this ten thousand times. "Tide window's four hours. Don't waste them sightseeing."

* * *

The water took me the way cold water always does — like a hand closing around the chest.

Eighteen fathoms is not a depth you visit casually on old scuba gear bought secondhand from a man who may or may not have been honest about its service history. My regulator hissed. My ears screamed their objections and then, grudgingly, filed them under *shut up*. And the light came down in long gray columns, moving with the surge, so the whole seafloor seemed to breathe.

I followed my father's bearing line off the compass — his compass, the brass one, hanging from my wrist on a lanyard because I couldn't bear to leave it in the boat and couldn't explain why. North-northeast, forty meters, then let the shoal's shadow guide you down the slope.

Forty meters. Fifty-five. Sixty.

And then the sand rose up to meet me, and there they were.

Masts. Dozens of them, lying in ranks across the basin floor like fallen soldiers, furred with two centuries of coral and bryozoa, some still standing at drunken angles out of the sand. A forest that had drowned standing up. Between them, half-buried, the dark shapes of iron ballast blocks and what might have been chain, running off toward deeper water in a line too straight to be accident.

My father had drawn this. Somewhere in the year he vanished, Tomás Vasquez had swum down into this same breathing light and mapped every spar, and then he'd folded the map into a satchel and hidden it in a cave, and then he'd gone and gotten himself killed by men who wanted what he knew.

I hung there in the middle of the mast field, breathing borrowed air, and felt something turn over in my chest that wasn't grief exactly. Closer to inheritance.

There — the third rank, per his notes. A spar with a distinctive double band of copper sheathing, the marker he'd used to orient toward the hold. I swam to it, found the bearing, and followed it across the sand. The bottom shelved down. The coral thickened. And beneath an overhang of dead reef, black against the pale sand, there was a shape too regular to be geology.

The hold. Or the top of it, anyway — buried to its gunwales in two hundred years of sediment, with silver somewhere inside it and ledgers somewhere inside *that*, if my father's notes told the truth, and God help me, everything else had.

I couldn't dig it today. Not alone, not on this air, not with the current already starting to tug at my fins like an impatient creditor. But I could confirm. I pulled the slate from my belt and scratched the coordinates, the depth, the orientation of the overhang. Evidence. The first hard fact in a family history made entirely of rumors.

That's when I noticed the sand cloud.

It came from the direction of the shoal — a brown plume rolling along the bottom, wrong for the tide stage, wrong for the current. Boats kick up plumes like that when they drag anchor. Or when they maneuver where they shouldn't be.

I turned, and the light columns above me flickered. Something had crossed between me and the sun.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about panic underwater: it doesn't feel like panic. It feels like arithmetic. Your brain starts doing calculations very calmly and very fast — air remaining, distance to surface, ascent rate, deco time you don't have — while your body does something completely different, which is thrash.

I made myself stop. Hung in the water. Counted.

The plume was moving away, northward, fast. Whatever had made it wasn't coming for me. It was leaving. Which was somehow worse, because things that leave have usually finished what they came to do.

I ascended fast and dirty, dumping air, watching my depth gauge spin backward, knowing I'd pay for it in joints and headaches later. The surface came up to meet me like a lid.

And Mercy was gone.

No — not gone. *Moving.* Drifting, rather, slow and aimless, maybe sixty meters off her mooring buoy, turning lazily in the current with nobody aboard. The mooring line trailed from her stern cleat into the water, and even from twenty meters away through spray and morning glare, I could see it wasn't attached to anything anymore. Just a frayed end whipping gently in the swell, like a finger pointing at nothing.

My blood went cold in a way the ocean hadn't managed.

I swam. I swam the way I haven't swum since I was a kid racing the ferry wake, arms burning, regulator dangling, kicking off fins when they started to feel like anchors. Sixty meters has never been so long. The boat drifted another ten while I chased it, and I had the lunatic certainty that I was going to watch Ferro's whole life float away over the horizon while I drowned in flat calm water fifty feet from shore.

My fingers hit the transom ladder. I hauled myself up and over the gunwale and lay on the deck gasping like the world's saddest fish, and it took me a full minute to notice Ferro wasn't there.

"Ferro?"

Nothing. Just the slap of water on the hull and the tick of the cooling engine — no, wait. Not cooling. Warm. The engine housing was warm. Somebody had been running it recently, or idling alongside, or—

"Ferro!" I got upright. The cabin door stood open. Empty. His jacket was gone. His coffee cup sat on the chart table, half full.

Then the sound reached me: oars. Rhythmic, unhurried, coming from the direction of the mooring buoy. And there was Ferro in his little tender, pulling back toward us with the grim strokes of a man rowing toward a problem he already understood.

He came alongside, shipped the oars, and looked at me dripping on his deck. He looked at the frayed mooring line. He looked at the warm engine housing. He didn't ask a single question, because he'd already answered all of them.

"Line was cut," he said. "Not chafed. Cut. Clean blade, one pass. And whoever did it used my own knife from the bait box — left it sitting right there on the thwart, wiped, polite as a churchgoer." His jaw worked. "They wanted the boat to drift. They wanted *you* to come up and find her drifting, or not find her at all."

"How long was I under?"

"An hour, near enough." He climbed aboard and stood looking at the frayed line like it had insulted his mother. "Whoever cut it knew your dive profile. Knew you'd be down long enough to drown before you figured out what happened. That's not vandalism, girl. That's arithmetic."

I wrapped my hands around the rail because they wanted to shake and I had decided they weren't going to. "Where were you?"

"Asleep below. Woke up when the boat's motion changed — forty years on the water, you feel it in your spine before your eyes open. Came up, saw her adrift, saw you nowhere, dropped the tender and started rowing toward your last bubble trail." He shrugged, a small bitter motion. "Too slow. You beat me to the ladder."

"Your knife—"

"Was in the box when I went below. I checked twice." He said it flatly, and I understood what it cost him: the certainty that someone had been aboard his boat, close enough to touch him sleeping, and he'd never stirred. Seventy-one years old and someone had made him weightless in his own home.

I looked north. The plume had long since dispersed, but the water beyond the shoal was empty in the specific way water is empty when something has just left it. Except—

"There." I pointed. Far off, at the edge of visibility, a low white shape sat on the horizon. A tender. Fleet-built, twin outboards, the kind Reyes's operation ran a dozen of. It wasn't approaching. It wasn't leaving, either. It just sat there, patient as a debt.

Ferro found it with his eyes and made a sound low in his chest. "Sitting outside the three-mile line," he muttered. "Clever. Nothing illegal about anchoring in open water. Nothing provable about a cut line, either — no witnesses, no tool but my own knife, and a drowned woman tells no tales about who held it."

"They tried to kill me."

"They tried to *remove* you." He corrected me the way he corrected everything, precisely and without heat. "Killing's loud. Accidents are quiet. A diver comes up, finds her boat gone, swims for shore or drowns trying — either way, the story writes itself. Tragic. Careless. Nobody's fault." He finally turned to face me fully, and his eyes were flint. "Except Reyes will know whose fault it looks like. A Vasquez, diving alone, on charts everybody says are forged. Drowned chasing her father's ghost. The town would swallow it whole. They're already halfway to swallowing it."

The wind picked up, carrying the smell of the fleet's diesel from the harbor behind us. I thought about the mast field down there in the breathing light, the copper-banded spar, the black shape of the hold. I thought about my father swimming this same water, mapping it, hiding the truth in a hole in a cliff, and dying anyway because he'd trusted the wrong silence.

Something in me finished setting. Like bone knitting. It hurt and it helped at the same time.

"They know I found something," I said. "Which means they watched me find it. Which means they know the coordinates are good — or they suspect, and they'd rather kill me than risk being wrong."

"Or Quist told them." Ferro said it quietly, and it landed like a hook in soft flesh. "You owe that man money, Vasquez. Men like Quist sell information the way other men sell fish — fresh, cheap, and to anybody who'll cause trouble with it."

I hadn't thought of that. I thought of it now, thoroughly, the way you think of cold water closing over your head. Marlo Quist knew I'd been asking questions. Knew I'd been out to the cliffs. Maybe knew about the satchel — Quist had a way of knowing things, the way rot knows wood.

"Doesn't matter," I said, and heard my own voice come out steadier than I felt. "Five tides left. If they want me dead, they'll keep trying, and if they want me scared, they've already failed, because I was scared before and it got me nothing but interest payments."

Ferro studied me for a long moment. Out on the horizon, the white tender finally turned and began to move away, unhurried, its work evidently done.

"You nearly died down there," he said.

"I nearly died in a lot of places. This is the first one worth it."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to witness, like a cliff deciding to become a beach. Then he bent, gathered the severed mooring line, and began coiling it with the care of a man handling a wound.

"We rig a second line next time," he said. "And a third. And I sleep topside with a shotgun across my knees, and anyone who boards my boat again learns why they call me Ferro." He tied off the coil and straightened. "But understand what happened here today, girl. This stopped being treasure hunting the moment that line parted. Somebody has declared a war, and they fired first, and they missed."

I looked south, toward Puerto Aliso, where Captain Ilsa Reyes's dredge sat gleaming at the dock like a promise the town couldn't afford to keep. Then north, toward the empty water where the tender had been. Then down, through the deck, through the breathing light, to the masts and the hold and the ledgers full of unpaid names.

"No," I said. "They didn't miss."

Ferro raised an eyebrow.

"They hit exactly what they were aiming for." I picked up the slate with my coordinates scratched into it and held it where he could see. "They just found out I don't scare. Now get me back to shore — I need to read some paperwork before tonight."

"What paperwork?"

"The kind with my father's signature on it." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like wearing someone else's hat. "The forged kind."
