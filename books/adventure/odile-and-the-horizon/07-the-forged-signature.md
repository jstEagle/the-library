## 7. The Forged Signature

The harbormaster's office in Puerto Aliso was built the way most official buildings in poor towns are built: to look like it had money it didn't. Whitewashed walls gone gray at the waterline, a coat of arms over the door that some contractor had painted slightly wrong so the eagle looked like a seagull with ambitions, and a lock on the archive room that hadn't been changed since before I was born.

I knew this because I'd buried the man who installed it.

Old Mateo Ruiz, harbormaster for thirty-one years, dead of a heart in his chair at sixty-eight, and I'd dug his grave on a Tuesday while his replacement — a nervous young man named Peralta who'd come from the capital and still wore shoes too clean for these streets — gave a eulogy about continuity of service. I'd stood there with dirt on my hands and thought: *continuity*. If only he knew. The locks were continuous. Everything else rotted.

I spent the afternoon being visible, which is its own kind of camouflage. I bought coffee at Doña Ines's stand where everyone could see me looking like a woman who'd nearly drowned that morning and wanted everyone to know she didn't care. I let Ferro tell the story twice, embellishing it each time until a mooring line fraying in the current became an act of sabotage by persons unknown, which became, by the third telling, pirates. Ferro enjoyed piracy. It made him feel young.

Meanwhile my hands wouldn't stop shaking, and not from the cold water.

Here's what nobody tells you about almost dying: it doesn't make you careful. It makes you precise. There's a difference. Careful people stay home. Precise people decide exactly which risks are worth it and stop pretending the others don't exist. Someone had cut my mooring line while I was forty feet down with no boat above me, and they'd done it in daylight, in front of witnesses, because they could. Because Captain Ilsa Reyes's fleet had arrived with state fanfare and a harbor full of men who suddenly remembered whose bread they'd been eating for years.

So yes. Precision.

* * *

Peralta locked up at seven. I watched him do it from the shadow of the net-drying racks, saw him check the door handle twice the way new officials always do, as if the building might be embarrassed into honesty. Then he walked toward the cantina, because Tuesday was dominoes night, and in Puerto Aliso even fear of your superiors loses to dominoes.

I gave him ten minutes. Long enough to order his first drink. Short enough that if someone came looking for him, he'd be found mid-game with witnesses, blameless.

The side window was the one I'd have used when I was fourteen and needed to steal back a letter my mother had written to the harbormaster complaining about dock fees. She'd been alive then, and furious about fees, and I'd been her accomplice in everything. The window still opened the same way — lift, tilt, slide — because nobody fixes what isn't broken, and nobody checks what nobody thinks about.

Inside smelled of paper, diesel, and the particular staleness of rooms where important things go to be forgotten. Moonlight came through the salt-hazed glass in pale bars. I didn't light the lamp. My father taught me to read charts by moonlight off the stern of a rocking boat; a dark office held no terror for me.

The archive room was at the back, behind a door with a brass plate reading CONCESSIONS & LEASES, which was the bureaucratic way of saying *everything worth stealing*. The lock was a Corbin pattern from the forties. I'd learned picks from a locksmith in the capital who'd owed my father money, back when owing Tomás Vasquez money was still a thing people did with their heads attached.

It took me four minutes. Two longer than it should have. My fingers were still remembering the cold of the morning water.

Inside, the files lived in green steel cabinets arranged by year, and the year I wanted was the year my father vanished. I pulled the drawer and it stuck, groaned, and gave — the sound enormous in the empty building, though it couldn't have carried past the wall.

Concession permits for coastal salvage, Puerto Aliso district. I found the San Telmo file by its tab, thin as a funeral program, and carried it to the window where the moonlight was strongest.

And there it was. My father's name, signed at the bottom of a survey certification, dated eleven days before he disappeared.

I'd know that handwriting anywhere. I'd grown up inside it — on grocery lists and birthday cards and the margins of his field notes, little jokes in Greek I wasn't supposed to be able to read yet. Tomás Vasquez wrote the way he did everything: fast, slanted left, with a crossbar on the T that hooked downward like a man leaning into wind. His sevens had a strike-through. His loops closed. He pressed hard enough that you could feel the words on the back of the page, braille for anyone who loved him.

The signature on the permit was smooth.

That was the first thing. No pressure. I ran my fingertip across the back of the page and felt nothing — no groove, no ghost of the pen. Whoever wrote it had been tracing, or copying slowly, or practicing on fifty sheets of scrap paper first. And the crossbar on the T went up. Up. Like a man leaning away from something. Like a signature drawn by someone who'd studied the shape of it but never understood the weight.

The sevens had no strike-through.

I sat down on the floor right there, file in my lap, and laughed once — a short, ugly sound that bounced around the archive room and came back sounding like grief. They'd forged my father's name badly. Clumsily. With the carelessness of people who never imagined anyone would look, because who would? Who looks at paper? Paper is how the powerful tell the powerless what's already been decided.

But I'd been raised on paper. On the weight of ink. On a man who believed a signature was a promise you made with your whole hand.

Then I turned to the second document in the file, and stopped laughing.

Beneath the salvage concession — layered under it like sediment, the way claims stack on claims in these territories — was the land title abstract. The legal foundation the whole permit rested on. *Clear title derived from original grant,* it said, citing a parcel number, and I copied the parcel number onto my wrist in pencil because I had nothing else to write on, and pulled the corresponding drawer.

The grant went back ninety years. Ninety years of transfers, consolidations, and quiet little transactions conducted between men with capital-letter names. But the root of it — the original entry, handwritten in a ledger book older than the cabinets holding it — named the land differently.

Not *Parcel 114-C, coastal district.* 

*Chumara communal lands, surveyed per agreement, held in trust.*

Held in trust. Four words that meant nothing and everything. I sat there in the moonlight doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. The Chumara families. Sela Chumara's people, who worked the salt road and fished the reef and had lived on that coast since before there were parcels or districts or eagles painted like seagulls. Their land, entered into trust — and somewhere in ninety years of paperwork, the trust had become a title, the title had become a grant, and the grant had become the foundation under a salvage permit signed with my father's stolen hand.

I checked three more permits. Reyes's fleet held concessions on four parcels total. Every single one traced back to the same root. Every signature bearing my father's name was wrong in the same ways — the upward T, the naked sevens, the ink sitting on top of the page instead of sinking into it.

He hadn't just surveyed the wreck. He'd certified the papers that stole the ground it lay under. Or — and here was the thought I kept flinching away from, the splinter working deeper — he'd been made to. Coerced. Trapped. A disgraced archaeologist with debts of his own, handed a pen and told *sign or else*, and then vanished eleven days later so thoroughly that even the sea kept the secret.

I closed the file and held it against my chest like something broken that might still be mended.

This was bigger than my debt. Bigger than Quist and his soft threats, bigger than Reyes and her dredges. Somewhere in those green cabinets was proof that the entire salvage fleet's claim to these waters rested on a forgery stacked on a theft stacked on a trust that had been quietly emptied. My father's name was the mortar in all of it.

Which meant his name could bring it down.

* * *

I was relocking the cabinet when I heard the front door.

Not opening — unlocking. Keys, the particular jingle of the harbormaster's ring, and then footsteps that hesitated in the main office, the way footsteps do when a man notices something slightly wrong and can't yet name it. The lamp switch clicked. Light spilled under the archive door in a yellow blade.

Peralta. Back early, or never gone, or sent back by someone who'd watched me go in.

I looked at the window. Ten feet across the room, openable, but the sill would creak and the moonlight would show me moving like a shadow play. I looked at the supply closet instead — barely wide enough for a broom and a man's regret — and got inside it just as the blade of light reached the door.

The door opened. Peralta stood in the archive room breathing the way nervous young men breathe, quick and shallow, and I watched him through the gap in the closet door as he crossed to the cabinets. He pulled the San Telmo drawer. Checked something. Pulled out a folded paper from his jacket — fresh, typed, official-looking — and slid it into the file.

Then he took the old certification out, looked at it for a long moment under the lamp, and put it in his pocket.

My blood went cold and stayed there.

He locked the cabinet. He stood a moment with his hand flat on the green steel, like a man touching a coffin lid, and then he said — quietly, to the empty room, or to me, or to himself:

"I'm sorry."

And he turned off the lamp and left, taking my father's forged signature with him, leaving behind whatever new paper he'd planted in its place.

I waited until the keys jingled away and the front door clicked and the night outside swallowed the sound of his shoes. Then I opened the closet on legs that felt borrowed, retrieved the file, and read the inserted page by the window's last light.

A survey amendment. Dated tomorrow. Certifying — in my father's clumsy forged hand — that the original coordinates had been *erroneous*, superseding them with coordinates that matched, to the decimal, the false marker I'd planted on the reef two tides ago.

They weren't just erasing him now.

They were using him to erase *me*.

I folded the page into my shirt, next to my skin, where the compass hung, and climbed out through the window my mother once sent me through, and walked home along the cliff path with the tide coming in below me, filling the cave where my father's charts slept, thinking one thought over and over, steady as oar-strokes:

Sela Chumara needed to see this paper. Tonight. Before they came for it.

Because whoever held the truth of that land held the fleet by the throat — and I had just watched them prove they'd kill to keep it.
