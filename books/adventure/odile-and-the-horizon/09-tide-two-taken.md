## 9. Tide Two, Taken

The Corvina's divers went into the water at first light like a company of men who had rehearsed their own funerals.

I watched from the cliff path with a thermos of coffee gone bitter and cold, counting them out of the tender: eight figures in black rubber and yellow tanks, dropping off the gunwale in pairs, each pair towing something that caught the sun and threw it back in hard white flashes. Sonar buoys. Six of them, maybe seven. They planted them in a wide arc beyond the reef, spaced like fence posts, and by nine o'clock the water over the San Telmo was no longer open water. It was property.

There's an old saying among grave diggers — my other profession, the one that pays worse — that every plot you sell is a promise you make to strangers. This ground is yours now. Sleep here forever. The fleet had done the same thing to the sea, only faster, and with paperwork instead of shovels. A buoy drops, a permit flashes, and suddenly three hundred years of tide belongs to Captain Ilsa Reyes.

Ferro stood beside me at the rail of nothing in particular, arms crossed, watching through eyes narrowed against the glare. He hadn't spoken since we'd left the harbor. His silence had a texture to it, like rope gone stiff from salt.

"They'll grid it by noon," he said finally. "Sonar doesn't miss much."

"Sonar misses what it isn't looking for."

"And what's it not looking for?"

"A lie," I said. "It's very good at finding truth. Terrible at finding fiction."

He looked at me then, and I saw him doing arithmetic of his own — not the cold kind I did with charts and tides, but the older kind, the kind a man does when he's deciding how much of another person he can afford to trust. I didn't blame him for the math. I'd been doing it about myself all morning, and the numbers kept coming out strange.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about lying: it gets easier the way calluses get easier. The first time you put your hands on a shovel before dawn, you blister. The hundredth time, you don't feel it at all.

Two days ago I had been a woman who dug graves and paid what she could and lied only in small denominations — to Quist's collectors, mostly, telling them checks were in the mail that would never be mailed. Now I stood on a beach at low tide with a forged survey marker in my duffel bag, planning to plant it in government water like a flag on conquered soil, and the strangest part wasn't the danger. The strangest part was how little my hands were shaking.

The marker itself was Ferro's idea, though he'd deny it under oath. An old concrete mooring weight from Mercy's junk locker, drilled and fitted with a brass plate I'd engraved myself — I have steady hands; you learn that digging in clay — reading *HYDROGRAPHIC SURVEY STATION 4-C*, with a date stamp and a fictional permit number I'd copied from the harbor archive during my break-in. To anyone paddling out to check, it would look exactly like what Reyes feared most: evidence that someone else had surveyed these waters first, and recently, and somewhere else.

Because that was the trick of it. You don't hide a wreck by moving it. You hide it by convincing the hunters they've already found where it isn't.

The false marker would go down two miles north, on a stretch of shoal where the sand shifted every storm and the sonar returns were already garbage. If Reyes's people found it — and they would, because I'd make sure they found it — they'd spend their remaining tide windows dredging a hole full of nothing, while the real coordinates sat quiet beneath their buoys like a coin under a cup.

"You understand," Ferro said as we loaded the dory, "that if they catch you doing this, it's not a fine. It's not a warning. Reyes has lawyers who can make a crime out of breathing."

"I've been making crimes out of breathing my whole life. Nice to know it goes both ways."

"That's not funny, girl."

"No," I agreed, pushing the dory into the shallows. "But it's true, and I've found those are usually the same thing."

* * *

We went out under a sky the color of unpainted zinc, running north first, loud and legal, a fisherman and his niece checking pots — Ferro's story, delivered flat enough that even I half believed it. We ran the false line past the northern buoys, close enough to read their hull numbers through binoculars, and Ferro called out observations like a man logging weather: buoy four, listing slightly; buoy five, fresh paint, new line. Then, at the edge of the shoal, in water shallow enough to work without tanks, I went over the side.

The bottom there was a desert. White sand rippled like the surface above it, starfish scattered like dropped coins, and nothing else — no mast field, no ballast stones, no history at all. Perfect. I walked the marker along the bottom in slow hops, found a hollow between two sand waves where it would sit naturally, as if it had been there for years, and set it down. The brass plate faced up. I fanned sand around its base with my fins until it looked settled, looked *old*, looked like a thing that had always been part of this desert.

Then I did something extra, because the exhilaration had gotten into me like rum into a cheap wine drinker, warm and stupid and faster than sense. I took my dive knife and scratched a second mark into the plate's underside — a tiny compass rose, the same one Tomás used on all his charts, the one I'd known since I was six years old. If anyone ever examined that marker closely, they wouldn't just find a rival survey station. They'd find *my father's hand*. And Ilsa Reyes, who had spent twenty years believing Tomás Vasquez's charts were hers by right of forgery, would tear that shoal apart down to bedrock looking for what she thought he'd hidden.

It was vanity. It was bait dressed as a signature. I knew that even as I carved it, and I carved it anyway, because some part of me wanted her to know — wanted her to stand on the deck of the Corvina and read my father's name written in the sand and feel what I felt reading her permits.

That should have frightened me more than it did. I noted that, distantly, the way you note weather coming. Filed it. Kept working.

* * *

The data log was Ferro's fault too, or maybe mine; by then our plans had stopped having clear parentage. On the run back south, hugging the buoy line, he slowed beside station three — the closest buoy to the reef, the one whose sonar cone would cover the true wreck site if anyone bothered to aim it properly — and said, conversationally, "Those things record everything. Days of it. Every return, every ping, timestamped."

"Yes."

"And whoever pulls that log knows exactly what's down there. Has known since yesterday."

"Yes."

"So the question," he said, easing the throttle forward again, "is whether you're the kind of fool who leaves that sitting in Reyes's cabin, or the other kind."

I was, apparently, the other kind.

We came back at dusk, when the fleet's tenders had gone home to their generators and their card games and the buoys blinked alone on the darkening water like patient green eyes. Ferro killed the engine two hundred yards out and we drifted, silent, while I pulled on fins and mask — no tank this time, no bubbles to catch a watchman's ear, just lungs and nerve. The swim was long and cold and I counted strokes to keep from counting fears. At the buoy I hung in the black water, listening to it tick and hum to itself, a little machine faithfully mapping a graveyard for people who had no right to the map.

The access port was a standard marine housing. I'd practiced on Ferro's depth sounder until my fingers knew the catches blind. It took ninety seconds in water that wanted me dead, prying the log module free and sealing the empty housing behind me so the loss wouldn't show until morning. Then I swam back to the dory with a brick of sealed electronics pressed to my chest like a stolen heart.

Ferro rowed us out of there without one word, and it was only when we were a mile clear, engine muttering low, that either of us breathed normally.

"Well?" he said.

I turned the module over in my hands. "Well what?"

"How do you feel?"

I considered the question honestly, which was itself becoming a rare habit. My heart was still going like a drum in a parade. My fingers ached from the cold. Somewhere behind my ribs, a voice that sounded suspiciously like my mother was asking what exactly I thought I was becoming.

"Like I'm good at this," I said. "Which worries me more than getting caught would."

Ferro grunted — his version of agreement, sympathy, and disappointment all compressed into one syllable — and turned the boat for home.

* * *

I opened the log that night in Ferro's wheelhouse, door locked, lamp shaded, while he pretended to mend nets and watched the harbor through the glass. The data came up in columns of timestamps and coordinates, and it took me less than a minute to find what I was looking for, because it was sitting right there in the third entry of the first day, bold as a signature.

A target return. Strong. Rectilinear. At coordinates that matched my father's charts to within thirty meters.

Dated *before* the buoys were deployed. Before the permits were even countersigned. Which meant the Corvina hadn't come to Puerto Aliso to search for the San Telmo at all. She'd come to collect her. Reyes had known where the wreck lay from the day she docked — probably for years — and the whole theater of sonar and survey and scientific patience was a costume hung over a theft already in progress.

"She's not hunting it," I said aloud. "She's fencing it. Keeping everyone else out while her divers strip it on night tides."

Ferro laid down his net needle. "You're sure the coordinates—"

"They're Tomás's. Give or take a boat length." I stared at the screen until the numbers blurred. "She's been diving my father's wreck with his own stolen charts, under permits signed with his stolen name, and the town is cheering her for it."

Something moved through me then that I couldn't file under exhilaration or fear. It was older than both. It was the feeling from the graveside, from the equinox cave, from every moment since the tide went out and showed me my father's handwriting: the feeling of standing on ground that had been lied about, and deciding — not choosing, *deciding*, the way ground decides — that the lies ended with me.

"There's one more thing," Ferro said quietly. He nodded at the screen. "Look at the last entry."

The final timestamp was from that evening. Two hours ago. A surface contact, small craft, loitering near the northern shoal — near my false marker — logged and flagged, with a note appended in plain text: *possible rival survey. Recommend retrieval and assessment.*

They'd seen us. Or seen something. And by tomorrow morning, Reyes's people would be pulling my brass lie out of the sand, turning it over in their gloved hands, reading the compass rose on the underside.

Reading my father's name.

Ferro met my eyes across the wheelhouse. "Congratulations," he said dryly. "I think you just got her attention."

Outside, far out past the breakwater, the buoys blinked on in the dark — six green eyes, and every one of them now pointed, patiently, toward whatever I would do next.
