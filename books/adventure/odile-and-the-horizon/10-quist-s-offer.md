## 10. Quist's Offer

I should have known Marlo Quist wouldn't come to me in the dark. Men like Quist don't skulk. Skulking is for people who are afraid of being seen, and Marlo Quist had spent thirty years making sure Puerto Aliso understood that seeing him was a privilege.

He came at noon, in the full light, while I was eating cold rice and beans standing up in Ferro's kitchen because sitting down felt like admitting I'd be there long enough to need a chair. The knock was polite. Two raps, unhurried. Ferro answered it with a filleting knife still in his hand, which I appreciated, and then went very still, which I didn't.

"Señor Vasquez," Quist said from the doorway, though of course that wasn't my name anymore and he knew it. "Or do we say Ansel now? You were Ansel last time we spoke."

"Last time we spoke you put a man's teeth on my mother's kitchen table."

"Marbles," Quist said, with the mild regret of a man discussing weather. "They were marbles. The teeth were a different client entirely." He stepped inside without being invited, the way water steps into a hull breach. He was smaller than I remembered, or maybe I'd grown. A neat man, silver at the temples, wearing linen that probably cost more than Mercy's mortgage. Behind him, in the yard, two of his people sat in a gray sedan with the engine off and the patience of furniture.

Ferro hadn't moved. "This is my house."

"It is," Quist agreed pleasantly. "Mortgaged through Coastal Mutual, which is mortgaged through me, which means — forgive the arithmetic — it is also mine, temporarily, the way a coat is yours until you stop paying the tailor." He smiled at Ferro like they were old friends at a funeral. "Don't worry, Ferro. I'm not here for the boat. I'm here for what she found."

The rice went cold in my stomach. "You don't know what I found."

"Ansel." He said my name the way you'd set down a glass you didn't want to break. "I've known about the San Telmo since before your father drew his first chart of her. I financed two expeditions to find her. I lost money on both, cheerfully, because the third one always pays for the first two. What I did not know — what nobody knew — was where Tomás hid his work." He turned, taking in the kitchen, the nets on their hooks, the window with its view of the harbor where Reyes's buoys blinked their green patience even in daylight. "And then an equinox tide drains a cave that hasn't opened in eleven years, and within forty-eight hours someone is diving past the reef with charts nobody has seen since 1998. I employ patient people, but not *that* patient."

"You had me followed."

"I had the water watched. You happened to be in it." He folded his hands. "Which brings us to business, and I'll be brief, because I know you have a tide window coming and I respect that about you. You're your father's daughter in more ways than you'd like."

* * *

Here is what temptation looks like when it has your face memorized: it doesn't loom. It sits down. It asks if you want coffee. It knows your mother's maiden name and the exact figure of her medical debt, down to the centavos, including the interest that accrued during the three weeks she was dying faster than the paperwork could keep up.

"Your total obligation," Quist said, laying a single sheet of paper on Ferro's table, "is four hundred twelve thousand, six hundred and thirty pesos. I'm prepared to call it zero. Not deferred. Not restructured. Zero. And Ferro's paper goes in the same fire." He glanced at the old man. "You'd own Mercy outright by Friday. Free hull, clear title. You could fish until your hands give out and leave the boat to whoever deserves it."

Ferro said nothing. His jaw worked once, like a man chewing something too tough to swallow and too proud to spit out.

"And in exchange," I said.

"The charts. The originals. Everything Tomás left in that cave, and anything else of his you're holding back — and you are holding something back, Ansel, don't insult us both. I take the survey work, I take the salvage rights as I can secure them, and you take your life back. You go back to digging graves, which I understand you're good at, and in five years this whole season will feel like a fever you broke."

"That's it. That's the whole deal."

"That's the whole deal." He reached into his jacket, and every muscle in Ferro's kitchen tightened — the old man shifted his weight toward the knife block, and outside, faintly, a car door opened and closed — but what came out was not a weapon. It was a photograph. Old, creased soft from handling, the color gone amber at the edges.

He laid it face-up beside the debt figures.

My father looked younger than I remembered him, which is the cruelest thing a photograph can do. He was standing against a wall — cinderblock, whitewashed, a wall I recognized because I'd stood against it myself once, in Quist's back office, at fourteen, waiting for my mother to finish her business. Tomás was thinner than the man who'd taught me to read ground. There was a bandage above his eye. And he was smiling. Not happily. It was the smile of a man who has decided something, the smile of a door closing from the inside.

"This was taken," Quist said quietly, "eleven years ago. Three days before the storm that took his boat out past the reef. You were told he drowned running from creditors. That's mostly true. He owed me. He ran. The sea did the rest." He tapped the photograph once, gently, as if the man in it could feel it. "What the story leaves out is that for nine days before that storm, Tomás Vasquez was in my custody. My guest. He ate at my table. And on the night he left — and he did leave, Ansel, no one carried him, no one threw him; he walked out of a room I had, quite deliberately, left unlocked — he took a set of documents from my office that I have wanted back ever since."

"Documents," I said. My voice sounded fine. It sounded like a voice belonging to a person whose knees weren't shaking.

"Permits. Claims. The original paper trail for the San Telmo concession — the true one, the first one, the one that made everything since possible." He met my eyes, and for just a moment the pleasantness dropped away and I saw the machinery underneath, all gears, no warmth. "Your father stole from me. Then the sea stole him back. And now his daughter is diving on a wreck using stolen charts, and I am offering to forgive four hundred thousand pesos for the privilege of closing a very old account."

"An accident," I said. "That's what you're saying. He died by accident."

"I'm saying," Quist said, "that accidents and negligence look identical from the outside, and the difference between them is usually determined by how cooperative the family turns out to be." He let that sit in the room like smoke. "There will be an inquiry someday. There always is, eventually, when men like Reyes overreach and governments change hands. When it comes, the record of those nine days will read however I decide to write it. If you sell me the charts, it reads *tragic accident, regrettable, the man was fleeing legitimate debts.* If you don't—" He spread his hands, a small honest gesture, almost humble. "Then other facts become available. Witnesses. Logs. Photographs taken from angles less flattering than this one."

* * *

After he left — and he left the way he'd arrived, unhurried, pausing at the door to compliment Ferro on his gutters, which was somehow worse than any threat — I stood at the kitchen window for a long time.

Ferro poured himself aguardiente and didn't offer me any, which was its own kind of mercy. "You're going to take it," he said.

"No."

"You're going to stand there looking at that window like it owes you money, and then you're going to take it, because four hundred thousand pesos is four hundred thousand pesos, and dead men don't care what their records say."

"He cared." I turned around. "Eleven years hiding paper in a wet hole in the rock. He didn't hide it to protect himself, Ferro. He was already gone. He hid it so somebody would *find* it."

"So find it and get rich. That's what finding things is for."

"Is it?" I looked at the photograph, still lying on the table where Quist had left it, because of course he'd left it; it wasn't evidence, it was seed. "Look at him. Look at that smile. He knew exactly what he was doing when he walked out of that room. He knew what it would cost. And Quist wants me to believe the same man who planned that carefully forgot to plan his own exit." I picked up the photo. My father's closed-door smile stared up at me, and beneath the amber fade I could see what I hadn't seen at first: his hand, at his side, slightly blurred. Moving. Reaching for something out of frame. Or putting something *into* frame. Into a pocket. Into a sleeve.

Into a satchel, maybe. Waterproofed. Hidden somewhere only a low equinox tide could reach.

"Ferro," I said slowly, "what if the documents Quist wants aren't in the cave at all? What if what's in the cave is a copy — and the originals went down with my father's boat?"

The old man went quiet in the way fishermen go quiet, which is to say completely, the way the water does before it changes its mind.

"Because that," I said, "would mean Quist isn't buying charts. He's buying insurance. And he thinks I'm holding his policy."

Outside, the green buoys blinked on in the early dusk, six patient eyes — and beyond them, far out past the reef, under six fathoms of cold dark water, whatever my father had truly hidden was waiting for the next tide to ask it questions.
