## 11. The Middle Tide

I have been on the water for fifty-one years, and I will tell you the truth about awe, since nobody else in Puerto Aliso seems willing to: awe is fear wearing its Sunday clothes. Every man who ever told you he was awestruck by the sea was terrified and too proud to say so. I include myself. I especially include myself that morning, running Mercy past the reef with the third tide window opening like a door somebody had left unlatched.

Ansel Vasquez sat in the bow with her father's charts spread across her knees, weighted at the corners with two sinkers and a coffee mug she'd stolen from my galley without asking, which I noted for later billing. The dawn came up gray-gold behind the headland, and the water over the outer shoal lay flat as poured pewter. Slack tide. Forty minutes of it, maybe fifty if the swell stayed polite.

"Reef marker," she said, not looking up.

"I see it."

"You're steering south of it."

"I'm steering *around* it, which is what a man does when he'd like to keep his boat." I eased the wheel over. "You dive wrecks like you read a grocery list."

"My father drew these charts. I figure he knew where things were." She said it flat, but I heard the crack under it, the same crack she carried every time she mentioned Tomás Vasquez, which was often and carefully, like handling a jar of nitroglycerin. "Portside passage, then the sand channel. We anchor in eleven fathoms."

"Eleven fathoms," I repeated. "On a slack tide. With a storm building out of the southeast that every bone in my body has been complaining about since Tuesday."

"Your body complain about anything else? Your mortgage, for instance?"

Ah, there it is. That's the girl. Marlo Quist held paper on my boat and paper on Ansel's mother's grave-debt, and the two of us had made our bargain the way broke people always do — quickly, in the dark, with a handshake that left both parties slightly ashamed. She gets the wreck's coordinates confirmed; I get a cut big enough to buy Mercy free. Simple. Clean. The kind of deal that works right up until it doesn't.

I dropped anchor in eleven fathoms and watched the line pay out, and I thought, not for the first time, that old Ferro here had finally found the exact depth of his own foolishness.

* * *

Ansel went down first. I ran the surface watch, which meant I sat in a boat with a rope in my hands and did arithmetic I didn't like. Thirty meters down, on a single tank, working a wreck she'd never seen, following charts drawn by a dead man. My wife — may she rest, and may she also stop nagging me from wherever she rests — used to say I had a talent for getting attached to lost causes. She said it about dogs, mostly. Then she said it about me.

The rope twitched twice. Signal. I pulled myself down the line hand over hand, ears popping, the light going from gray to green to that brown-green murk where the world ends and your imagination takes over the night shift.

And there it was.

I want to describe it properly, because I've earned the right after half a century of hauling fish and watching other men get rich off the sea's leftovers. The San Telmo lay on her beam in the sand channel, broken at the waist, her ribs standing up out of the seabed like the carcass of some animal that had been eating ships and finally choked. Coral had taken her the way moss takes a fence post — patiently, completely, with no intention of giving her back. Her masts were down. Her hull gaped. And through that gap, catching the thin light we'd brought with us, glinted the cargo.

Silver.

Not coins. Ingots. Bars stacked in the hold the way a miser stacks firewood, packed tight, cradled in rotted dunnage, furred white with forty years of the sea's opinion of them. Hundreds of them. Maybe more. Enough to buy Puerto Aliso three times over. Enough to buy Mercy outright and the harbor she floated in and the cliffside cemetery where everybody who ever chased this wreck now slept.

I am not a young man and I am not a sentimental one, but I'll confess I put my glove against one of those bars and just stayed there a moment, breathing through my regulator, feeling the cold of it come up through the leather into my palm. Fifty years I'd watched the salvage fleets come and go, Reyes and her predecessors, sonar buoys and concession permits and men in good boots talking about *heritage* and *opportunity*, all of them hunting this. And here it was, and here was I, and nobody in the world knew but me and a gravedigger's daughter thirty feet away in the murk.

Ansel tapped my shoulder and pointed. Not at the silver.

At the deck beneath it.

* * *

Here is what I know about holds, having loaded and unloaded them since I was fourteen: nothing sits at the bottom of a cargo hold by accident. Whatever's lowest went in first. And whatever went in first, on a ship out of the colonial routes, in the year Tomás Vasquez's charts were dated — whatever went in first wasn't treasure. Treasure rides high and gets unloaded first. What goes in first is ballast. Or worse.

The ingots had been stacked on top of wooden crates. Long crates. Flat crates, lashed down with iron strapping gone to rust-weep, sealed with pitch that had held better than the ship deserved. Ansel worked a pry bar — she'd brought a pry bar, which tells you something about how well she'd read those charts, or how well her father had taught her to think — and the pitch cracked away like old bread.

Inside: oilcloth. Inside the oilcloth: leather. Inside the leather: books.

Ledgers. Sealed ledgers, wax-stamped, bound in hide gone stiff as board, and there must have been twenty of them, laid in rows like they'd been tucked in for the night. Ansel got one loose and turned it toward my dive light, and I saw the cover stamp — a shipping house crest, worn but legible — and the title burned into the spine in faded gilt:

*CARGO MANIFEST — SAN TELMO — MANIFESTO DE CARGA.*

She opened it.

I watched her face change. I've watched faces change underwater before — divers who see a shark, divers who see their gauge reading wrong, divers who see the thing they came for and realize they should have stayed home. Hers did something I hadn't seen. It went still. Completely still, the way a room goes still when bad news walks in.

She turned the book so I could read.

Columns. Names. That's all a manifest is — columns and names. But these weren't barrels of salt pork and cases of rum. These were people. Line after line, in a clerk's neat copperplate: *Chumara laborers, transported under contract.* Names. Ages. Some of them children, the ages written small and shameful. And in the wage column, every line, every name, every soul: *paid — nil*. *Paid — nil*. *Paid — nil*. Page after page. Book after book. Twenty ledgers of it, sealed in pitch and sunk under silver, exactly where nobody was ever supposed to find them.

The San Telmo hadn't been carrying treasure. The treasure was the excuse. The cargo was people — Chumara families hauled out from the coast under contracts that promised wages, and the wages had never been paid, and the proof had been ballasted under the silver so the silver would look like the whole story. Sink the ship's secret under the ship's shine. Anybody who ever found this wreck would grab the pretty metal and run, and the books would stay down here in the dark, holding their names, until the sea ate the ink and the last of them.

My father fished these waters. His father fished these waters. Sela Chumara's people worked the salt road when I was a boy, before the displacements, before the permits, before the word *concession* became the most expensive word on this coast. I'd known. You grow up knowing, the way you grow up knowing which families eat and which families don't, and you file it under *the way things are* and you keep hauling your nets.

But knowing a thing and reading four hundred names in a drowned man's handwriting are different animals entirely.

We surfaced into a morning that looked exactly the same as it had an hour ago, which felt like an insult. The sky still gray-gold. The swell still polite. Gulls still arguing over the reef. The world carrying on as though the bottom of it hadn't just opened up and shown us what it kept down there.

Ansel sat on the gunwale with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth on her lap, dripping, and didn't say anything for a long time. Neither did I. Mercy rocked under us, patient, mortgaged, honest.

"The silver," I said finally, because somebody had to, because the arithmetic doesn't stop just because the world turns out to be rotten. "It's still there. It's still worth—"

"I know what it's worth."

"A cut of that buys back my boat. Clears your mother's debt. Puts Quist out of business in a single afternoon, and good riddance to him." I said it fast, the way you say things you're afraid won't survive being said slowly. "One load. Nobody would ever—"

"Ferro." She looked at me, and her eyes were red-rimmed from the salt water or something else, and her voice was quiet in the way that a knife is quiet. "These are the names of Sela's family. Of her *family*. Wages never paid. Children transported. This isn't a treasure with paperwork attached. It's a crime scene with silver scattered around it, and we're standing in it."

"And if we report it, the state seals the site pending inquiry, and the inquiry takes ten years, and by then Reyes's lawyers have eaten everything including the inquiry." I heard my own voice climbing and made myself stop. Made myself look at the water instead of her. "I'm sixty-eight years old, girl. I don't have ten years. I barely have ten tides. I've got a boat that Quist owns more of than I do, and hands that don't work in the cold anymore, and I agreed to this run because for one morning of my life I thought the sea was going to give something back."

She didn't answer right away. The swell lifted Mercy and set her down, lifted and set down, the way the sea does when it's thinking.

"We take the ledgers up," she said at last. "All of them. We take them to Sela. And then we decide together, in daylight, with her people in the room, what happens to the silver."

"Together," I said. "In daylight. With a committee."

"Yes."

"That's how treasures die, Ansel. Committees and daylight."

"No," she said, wrapping the ledger tighter, the way you'd wrap a child against cold. "That's how *lies* die. Treasures are just lies that haven't been caught yet."

* * *

I raised anchor. I'll be honest about why, since honesty is the only currency I've got left after this morning: I raised anchor because I needed my hands busy, and because the storm out of the southeast had started making itself known on the horizon, a bruise-colored line low over the water, and because I could not sit in that boat one more minute next to a woman holding four hundred drowned names and calling it a reason.

We ran for home with the tide turning under us. Ansel sat in the bow with the ledgers — we'd brought up six, the ones we could reach without collapsing the stack, the rest waiting below with the silver — and she read them the whole way in. Out loud, sometimes. Just names, just numbers, her voice going out over the water and coming back flattened by wind. I pretended not to listen. I listened to every word.

And I'll tell you what I was thinking, since apparently this chapter of my life is going to be spent telling truths I'd rather drown than say. I was thinking about the silver. All through the reading, all through the names, some crooked little room in the back of my skull kept doing the arithmetic — bars times weight times price, minus Quist's cut, minus the risk, equals Ferro free, Ferro dry, Ferro done. Sixty-eight years old and I couldn't make that room shut up. It just kept counting while the dead kept naming themselves.

By the time the harbor lights came up through the thickening weather, I'd made a decision I hadn't admitted to myself yet. And Ansel, damn her, saw it on my face before I saw it myself. She stood up in the bow, ledgers clutched to her chest, and looked at me across the length of my own deck with an expression I recognized, because my wife used to wear it.

"Don't," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to."

The harbor mouth opened ahead of us, and the storm light made the water go the color of tin, and I put the helm over and headed Mercy in — and that's when I saw them. Harbor patrol launch, sitting fat off the breakwater. Two of them. Running lights on in broad daylight, which they only do when somebody's paid for the electricity.

Somebody had called ahead.

I looked at Ansel. Ansel looked at me. And neither of us asked the question, because we were both afraid the other one already knew the answer.
