## 12. What Gold Buys

The thing about silver is that it lies to you.

Gold, now — gold is honest. Gold sits in your palm and says *I am heavy, I am rare, I will ruin you.* Silver just glitters. Silver looks like moonlight on water, like a promise you'd swear you never made. Forty years of hauling nets out of the cold Pacific had taught me every kind of lie the sea tells, and I thought I was immune. Then Ansel Vasquez put her hand flat against an ingot in the hold of a ship dead two hundred years, and I felt my own heart do something stupid.

"Look at this," she said, and her voice came through the regulator line all wrong — too soft for a woman crouched in a flooded tomb.

"I'm looking," I said. "It's money."

"It's a name." She turned the bar so my lamp caught the stamp: *SAN TELMO — 1798 — CARGA REAL*. "This isn't smuggler's hoard, Ferro. This is crown cargo. Registered, manifested, insured by men who wore powdered wigs."

"Then it's registered money," I said. "Even better."

She looked at me the way she looks at ground she's about to dig — measuring, weighing what's under the surface. We were forty feet down inside the wreck, in a hold that still smelled of salt and old wood and something older than both, and I was doing arithmetic in my head that had nothing to do with history. Forty ingots visible. Probably more beneath the collapsed decking aft. At even a conservative weight, at even a discreet buyer —

I'm not proud of the arithmetic. But I'd been doing it since I was nineteen years old, when my father mortgaged Mercy's hull to pay a doctor who couldn't save my mother anyway, and I've been doing it ever since. Debt doesn't make you greedy. It makes you precise.

"We take three," I said. "Maybe four. Enough to clear me, enough to clear you, and nobody's the wiser. The rest stays where it's been for two centuries. The sea can keep its secrets; it always has."

Ansel pulled off her mask. Water ran down her face and she didn't wipe it. "And the ledgers?"

I'd almost managed not to look at them. Under the ingots, wedged into a rotted strongbox with brass fittings gone green, we'd found books. Sealed in waxed canvas, dry as a sermon inside. Manifests. Cargo lists. And not cargo — not really. Names. Columns of names, Chumara names, with dates of embarkation and columns marked *wages due* that had never once been marked *paid*. Two hundred people hauled aboard the San Telmo as labor, listed between the silver and the salt beef like they were ballast.

"They're paper," I said. "Old paper. It proves what everybody already knows — that rich men stole from poor ones a long time ago. You put those in front of a magistrate and he'll yawn. You put three bars of silver in front of him and suddenly he remembers his oath."

"That's exactly why they have to come up," she said. "Because everyone yawns. Because Quist yawns and Reyes yawns and the whole coast has been yawning for fifty years while they forge permits over graves."

"Fifty years ago," I said, "nobody asked us to fix it. Fifty years ago I was pulling sardines and minding my own business, and it worked fine."

"And how's that going for you?" she said. "Minding your business? Your boat's mortgaged to the same shark who owns my mother's debt. Your pension is a net patch and bad knees. Minding your business got you here, Ferro — diving a dead man's ship because a stranger with a compass promised you a cut."

That stung, mostly because it was true, and true things sting worse than lies because you can't return them.

We surfaced into gray morning light, hauled ourselves onto Mercy's deck, and stood there dripping, the argument hanging between us like fog. She wrapped the ledgers in oilcloth with hands that were steadier than mine wanted them to be. I wrapped one ingot in a rag and didn't tell her about the second one I'd already stowed under the bait locker.

* * *

Here's what I'll say for myself: I did try to meet her halfway. That afternoon, anchored in the lee of the point while the tide turned, I laid it out plain.

"Three bars each," I said. "We raise them next window, quiet, no witnesses. Then you can take your papers to whoever you want — Sela, the magistrate, the bishop if it makes you happy. I won't stop you. But the silver comes up first, and it comes up clean."

"You don't see it," she said. She was sitting on the gunwale with her father's brass compass open in her palm, turning it over and over like a worry stone. "If we take the silver first, that's all it ever is. Treasure. A story about money. The ledgers become an appendix — *oh yes, and some people died, incidentally, while this was being loaded.* But if the ledgers come first, if they're public before a single bar surfaces, then the silver means something else. It means restitution. It means the Chumara families have standing to claim the whole wreck, not just the moral high ground, which pays no debts but buys futures."

"The courts move slow."

"The courts move slow," she agreed, "but Reyes moves fast, and Quist moves faster, and every tide we wait is a tide closer to their dredges. Which is exactly why the paper has to be safe before the metal is. Paper burns, Ferro. People burn easier."

I looked at her — this grave-digger's daughter with salt in her hair and her father's hunger in her eyes — and I saw Tomás Vasquez in her, or what I imagined of him, because I'd only met the man twice and both times he'd talked like the world owed him a hearing. She had that same look. Like truth was a tool she intended to use on somebody.

"And what happens to me while your truth is being heard?" I said. "Quist holds my mortgage. One phone call and Mercy is impounded, and I'm standing on a dock watching forty years go to auction. You've got nothing to lose but a compass. I've got everything that floats."

Her jaw tightened. "So that's it. The silver."

"So that's it," I said. "The silver. Welcome to being poor, Ansel. It's not noble. Nobody writes novels about it."

She closed the compass with a snap that sounded final, though I didn't know yet how final. "No," she said quietly. "They write ledgers about it."

* * *

We argued until dark. Not shouting — shouting would have been better, shouting burns clean. This was the other kind, the low kind, two stubborn people saying the same things in different orders until the words wear grooves in you. She called me a coward with a boat. I called her a zealot with a dead man's maps. She said the Chumara families had waited two hundred years and could they not wait six more tides. I said waiting was all they'd ever been given and I wasn't going to hand them another installment of it dressed up as principle.

Around midnight she went ashore in the tender. Said she needed to think, said she'd be back before the third tide window. I watched her climb the dock ladder and disappear into the town's dark, and I sat in the cockpit with the rag-wrapped ingots and told myself the knot in my chest was just the beans.

I want it on record that I slept badly. Whatever else happened, whatever anyone says, I slept badly.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater. I was coiling line when I heard the engines — not one engine, several, the flat purposeful burble of harbor patrol inflatables coming around the point at speed.

Three boats. Six officers. Coast authority colors, and behind them, further out, a gray hull I recognized with a feeling like swallowing ice: a customs cutter, riding at anchor where no customs cutter had any honest reason to be.

I had maybe ninety seconds. Long enough to run. Not long enough to hide anything, because there was nothing to hide — except the ingot under the bait locker, which I covered with fish guts with the calm of a man who has hidden things from wives, tax collectors, and one memorable priest.

They came alongside without hailing. The senior officer was young, wet-eared, apologetic in the way of men who enjoy their work and apologize for it anyway.

"Ferro Odalys? Master of the vessel Mercy?"

"Owner," I corrected. "Master implies I get paid."

"You're detained pending investigation of smuggling of cultural patrimony. Section fourteen, maritime heritage act." He read it off a card like a boy reciting catechism. "You'll come with us. The vessel is impounded."

"On whose complaint?" I asked. Procedure matters. Procedure is the last weapon a poor man owns.

He checked his paper, and I watched his eyes flick across the line that mattered. "Anonymous," he said. "A tip regarding salvage activity in restricted waters."

Anonymous.

I stood on my own deck, hands cuffed in front of me — they were polite about it, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse — and I did the arithmetic I should have done days ago. Who knew where Mercy was moored? Who knew what we'd found? Who knew the exact shape of what lay in that hold, silver and paper, and knew precisely which half of it would hang me?

Ansel had gone ashore at midnight. Ansel had argued for the ledgers until her voice went raw. Ansel had watched me wrap that ingot with eyes like her father's, and I'd seen her clock the bait locker — I'd *seen* her see it, and I'd been too tired, too old, too sure of our partnership to care.

Or.

Or someone else had followed us. Someone else had watched the dive. Reyes had tenders on the water; Quist had ears everywhere; the sea off Puerto Aliso that season had more listeners than fish. Any of them could have made a call. Any of them would have enjoyed making it.

But here's the thing about suspicion: it doesn't need proof. It needs only a face. And when the patrol boat towed Mercy toward the harbor, past the breakwater, past the Corvina riding fat and smug at her berth, I looked back at the empty anchorage where our argument had lived and died, and all I could see was a woman climbing a dock ladder in the dark, walking away from me toward town, toward telephones, toward whatever version of right she'd decided I was an obstacle to.

Betrayal, I've learned, is rarely certain. That's what makes it eat you. If I knew — *knew* — she'd called it in, I could hate her clean and be done. Instead I got to spend the ride in wondering, replaying every word, hunting for the moment the knife went in and finding only the moment I'd handed it to her handle-first by wrapping silver while she wrapped paper.

At the harbor steps, the young officer paused. "For what it's worth," he said, not meeting my eyes, "the tip specified the bait locker."

The bait locker.

Nobody watching from shore could have known about the bait locker. Nobody idling offshore in a Reyes tender could have seen through Mercy's hull to a rag-wrapped bar under a false floor. Only two people alive knew what was under that bait locker, and one of them was currently being rowed to jail.

I sat in the holding cell that afternoon with salt drying stiff on my skin and did the last of the arithmetic. Quist owned my mortgage. Quist owned Ansel's debt. A call from Quist's people would explain everything — except the bait locker, which explained nothing but her.

Through the cell's barred window I could see the harbor, and beyond it the headland, and beyond that, faint as a rumor, the reef that hid the San Telmo. Somewhere out there the tide was turning again, the third window opening like a door, and the water above a dead ship filling slowly with other people's greed.

I'd spent forty years believing the sea keeps its secrets. Lying on a bunk that smelled of disinfectant and other men's mistakes, I finally understood my error.

The sea keeps nothing. It only holds things for people patient enough to come collect — and I had just taught Ansel Vasquez exactly where everything was buried.

The question that kept me company in the dark was simple, and it had teeth:

When she came for the rest of it — and she would, she'd come alone if she had to, that was written in her the way channels are written in charts — would she be coming to finish what we started?

Or would she be coming to make sure I never spoke of it again?
