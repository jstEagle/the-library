## 13. Held at the Harbor

I heard about Ferro's arrest before I heard it was an arrest. That's how news travels in Puerto Aliso — first as weather, then as rumor, then as fact, and by the time fact arrives everyone has already decided what they believe.

The morning after Mercy came back alone, I woke to gulls screaming over the harbor like they'd been paid to do it. By seven, Doña Reyes at the panadería was telling me — through a mouthful of someone else's business — that the harbor patrol had boarded a fishing boat at dawn and found silver ingots under the netting. By eight, it had a name attached. By nine, it had my name attached too, because of course it did. In this town, guilt is a tide. It comes for everybody eventually; the only question is whose door it reaches first.

I walked down to the harbor with my hands in my pockets and my heart doing arithmetic it didn't like.

Mercy sat impounded at the customs dock, tied off with official orange line that looked obscene against her old gray hull. A patrol skiff idled alongside her like a dog chained to a corpse. Two men in state uniforms were going through her hold with the thoroughness of people being paid by the hour, and a small crowd had gathered on the quay to watch — the way crowds gather for fires, or fights, or anything that lets them feel the day has given them something.

Ferro stood on the dock between two officers, his wrists cuffed in front of him, which struck me as unnecessary. He was seventy-one years old and built like driftwood. But bureaucracy loves its theater, and the cuffs were part of the script.

He saw me coming and something moved across his face — not relief, not anger. Something worse than either. The look of a man checking whether the person he suspects of betraying him is sorry enough.

"You look terrible," he said, when I got close enough. "Like you slept in a ditch."

"I slept in my bed. It just feels the same."

One of the officers shifted, and Ferro lowered his voice without lowering his eyes from mine. "Anonymous call came in last night. Harbor patrol says a concerned citizen reported suspicious cargo movements out past the reef." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Concerned citizen. You know what I think, Ansel?"

"Tell me what you think, Ferro."

"I think somebody knew exactly what was under my netting. Exactly where. Exactly when I'd be coming back through the channel." He leaned in, close enough that I could smell salt and diesel and the particular stubbornness of him. "And I think there are only two people alive who knew all three things."

The second person was me. We both knew it. The crowd pretended not to listen while listening with every pore.

"Ferro—"

"Don't." He straightened up, cuffs clinking. "Don't say it here. Whatever you're going to say, say it where there aren't forty ears and a customs camera." The officers took his arms then, gently enough, and he let them, but he turned his head back toward me one last time. "They're taking me to the harbormaster's office. Come argue my case if you've got the stomach for it. Or don't come, and I'll know everything I need to know."

They walked him up the quay. The crowd parted for him the way water parts around a rock — reluctantly, and closing again behind.

* * *

The harbormaster's office smelled like mildew and carbon paper, a room that hadn't believed in change since the last century. Harbormaster Ochoa himself was a soft-bodied man with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, the kind of official who administered misfortune rather than causing it, and who therefore felt no responsibility for any of it.

"He'll be processed today," Ochoa told me, shuffling papers he'd already read twice. "Smuggling of protected maritime antiquities. That's serious, Ms. Vasquez. State charges, not local ones. There's nothing I can do."

"There's plenty you can do. You just don't want to."

He had the decency to wince. "The report came through channels. Anonymous tip, verified sighting, contraband recovered aboard. My hands are—" He gestured at them, these helpless hands, as though they belonged to someone else.

"Whose channels?"

"That's not public information."

"Ochoa." I put both palms flat on his desk and leaned in until the glasses slipped down his forehead. "An anonymous tip doesn't board a boat at four in the morning with two skiffs and a customs inspector already awake and dressed. Somebody paid for that kind of efficiency. Who?"

He looked at me over the rims of his glasses, and for one naked moment I saw the truth in him — the tired, frightened truth of a small man who knows exactly which currents run beneath his little office, and has decided his survival depends on never naming them aloud.

"Ms. Vasquez," he said quietly, "you should be more worried about yourself right now than about me. The tip mentioned two names."

* * *

I didn't go home. Home was a room above the chandlery with one window and too much silence, and silence was the last thing I could afford. Instead I walked the long way around the harbor, past the fish market, past the fuel dock, letting my feet move while my mind ran the trap backward, wire by wire.

Here's what I kept circling: the anonymous call had named the cargo. Not just a boat acting strange, not lights beyond the reef — the actual silver, under the actual netting. Only three people knew what we'd raised: me, Ferro, and whoever had watched us do it. And only one person in Puerto Aliso had the reach to turn a phone call into a dawn raid with customs inspectors pre-briefed.

Marlo Quist.

It had his fingerprints all over it, and Quist was careful enough never to leave fingerprints — only outcomes. This was an outcome. Ferro in custody, Mercy impounded, and me standing alone in a town where every ally had just become leverage. Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it? He didn't need to threaten me. He needed only to make the cost of refusal visible. Every day Ferro sat in a cell, every day Mercy rotted at the customs dock accruing fees, that cost compounded. Quist didn't send messages. He sent invoices.

I stopped at the seawall and stared out at the reef line, where the water lay flat and innocent over the San Telmo, and I hated myself a little for the thought that arrived next, uninvited, wearing my own voice: *You could still fix this. You have the charts. One meeting, one envelope, and Ferro walks free.*

That was the thing about temptation. It never showed up looking like a devil. It showed up looking like a solution.

* * *

Captain Ilsa Reyes chose that afternoon to discover her philanthropic side.

She held court on the Corvina's aft deck with the gangway open to the public — an open house, she called it, champagne in paper cups, the crew in clean coveralls, a state tourism liaison at her elbow nodding along like a metronome. She'd invited the town aboard to see the salvage operation's future: the jobs, the museum she promised to fund, the prosperity lying just offshore waiting for permits and ambition.

I went aboard because hiding was worse than walking into the fire, and because I wanted to see her face when she talked about helping.

She worked the crowd like a trawler works a net — slow circles, drawing everything in. When she reached me, she didn't miss a beat. If anything, her smile warmed by a degree, calibrated for maximum visibility.

"Ms. Vasquez," she said, loud enough for the cluster of listeners around us. "I'm so glad you came. I wanted to offer my assistance, actually — regarding your friend Mr. Ferro."

"His name is Emilio Ferro, and he doesn't need your assistance. He needs a lawyer."

"And he shall have one! The very best." She spread her hands, magnanimous as a statue of a saint. "The Corvina's parent company retains counsel throughout the territory. It would be my honor to extend those services to Mr. Ferro pro bono. A man his age, facing state smuggling charges — he needs advocates, not enemies. And I want Puerto Aliso to know that this operation stands with its community. Even members of the community who have made... regrettable choices."

There it was. Delivered with champagne warmth, in front of thirty witnesses: *your friend is guilty, I can buy his defense, and your whole town will remember who was generous.*

"You're offering to defend him," I said, "from charges connected to cargo that came from a wreck your fleet has spent forty years trying to claim."

"I'm offering to help a troubled man navigate a difficult system." Her eyes stayed pleasant, but behind them the machinery turned, cold and well-oiled. "The sea gives to whoever holds the permits, Ms. Vasquez. Everything else is just weather."

A woman beside me — Chumara, one of Sela's granddaughters maybe — set down her untouched champagne cup and walked away. Reyes watched her go with the serene expression of someone cataloguing a problem for later.

"Think about my offer," she said to me, softer now, almost kind. "Counsel for your friend. Good terms. All I ask in return is that if you ever come across information relevant to our concession area — and I understand you're a woman who reads ground, who reads charts — you bring it to me first. Before anyone else. That's not a bribe. That's a partnership."

She pressed a card into my hand, cream stock, heavy weight, her name embossed in letters that probably cost more than Ferro's boat. Then she moved on to charm the harbormaster's wife, and I stood there holding the card like it might bite.

* * *

Sela found me at dusk on the seawall. I heard her cane tapping before I saw her, that deliberate rhythm she used like a knock on a door.

"They arrested the old man," she said, sitting down beside me without asking, the way matriarchs do. "For touching silver that was carried on the backs of our dead."

"Yes."

"And you sit here watching the water instead of burning something down." It wasn't quite a question. She studied my face with eyes gone pale as fog glass. "My granddaughter tells me Captain Reyes offered you a partnership today. In front of the whole town. Very generous woman, that one. Generous the way a hook is generous to a worm."

"I didn't take it."

"No. But you're holding her card." She nodded at my hand, where the cream stock had gone soft with sweat. "In my day, we had a word for a piece of paper like that. We called it a leash."

"Sela, I'm trying to figure out which way the leash pulls."

"Mm." She was quiet a moment, watching the fishing boats come home with their running lights on, one by one, like slow embers drifting in. "Do you know what I think? I think Marlo Quist did not do this to punish you. Punishment teaches. This is different. This is a man clearing the board. He takes your partner, he takes your boat, and then he waits to see what you love enough to trade. That's not anger, girl. That's appraisal."

The words landed harder than I wanted them to. Appraisal. That's exactly what it was. I was an asset in distress, and distressed assets sold cheap.

"If I go to him," I said slowly, "he wins. If I don't, Ferro rots and Mercy rusts. If I go to Reyes, I hand her the wreck. If I tell the town the truth about the ledgers, half of them won't believe me and the other half will sell me to Quist for the reward money. Everyone who can help me can also be taken away from me. That's the design. That's the whole architecture of it."

Sela tapped her cane once against the stone, sharp as a period at the end of a sentence.

"Then stop trying to find an ally who can't be leveraged," she said. "There isn't one. Not me, not you, not the harbormaster. Find the thing they can't leverage instead." She rose, joints cracking like ship's timbers, and looked down at me. "Your father hid something in that cave because he trusted stone more than people. Maybe the stone keeps better counsel. Go ask it."

She left me there with the leash in my hand and the tide starting to turn, and I sat until full dark, doing the coldest arithmetic of my life.

Quist owned Ferro's freedom. Reyes owned the narrative. The town owned its fear. And somewhere under the reef, in a flooded hold full of unpaid names, sat the only asset in Puerto Aliso that nobody could arrest, impound, or buy — because nobody else knew precisely where it lay, and the tide windows were bleeding away, one per day, like sand through a fist.

Two tides left before the false coordinates went stale and Reyes started looking in earnest. Three before the permits expired and the auction opened.

Which meant I had one night, one borrowed boat, and no crew — unless I counted a dead man's charts, a matriarch's blessing, and whatever was buried in the cave that my father had trusted stone to keep.

I dropped Reyes's card over the seawall into the black water, watched it float for a heartbeat, then soak and sink.

Then I stood up and went to find out what else Tomás Vasquez had hidden below the cliff — because tomorrow, at slack water, I was going back into the cave alone.
