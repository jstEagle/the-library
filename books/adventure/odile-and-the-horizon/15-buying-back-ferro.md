## 15. Buying Back Ferro

Quist chose the cemetery for the meeting, which told me everything about his sense of humor. Marlo Quist had exactly two settings — menace and sentiment — and he liked to run them both at once, the way a bad engine runs on two cylinders and shakes itself apart eventually.

I found him standing at my mother's grave, hat in hand, looking mournful as a paid mourner. He'd probably been a paid mourner once. He had the build for it: soft in the middle, hard in the eyes, dressed in a linen suit that cost more than Ferro's boat had ever earned in a season.

"Your mother," he said, "owed me eleven thousand when she died."

"Fourteen, last you told me."

"Interest." He said it the way a priest says *amen*. "You see the trouble with the dead, Ansel. They stop paying, but the debt keeps living. It's the only part of them that's immortal."

"I've dug a lot of graves, Marlo. I've never once seen a debt buried in one."

"No," he agreed, turning. "You bury people. Debts you carry." He smiled, and it was almost warm, which was the most frightening thing about him. "So let's talk about what you're carrying."

* * *

We walked the cliff path while the tide came in below, filling the cave's throat with that slow, green swallowing I'd come to think of as the ocean closing its ledger. Quist talked the whole way, and I let him, because every word was a coin he was showing me, and I wanted to count the whole purse before I named my price.

He knew about the charts. Of course he knew. Reyes's divers had been crawling over the outer shoal for two tides now, buoying water that led nowhere, and a man like Quist didn't need sonar to know when something was moving under the surface of a town. He needed only to watch who stopped sleeping.

"Here's my offer," he said, stopping where the path narrowed, the sea loud below us. "You give me the charts. Complete. Every coordinate, every sounding your father ever took. In exchange, your debt dies — all of it, yours and your mother's, paper burned in front of you. And Ferro walks out of the harbor office tomorrow morning, and the *Mercy* sails home with her papers in her own drawer."

"And if I say no?"

"Ferro's charge becomes a conviction. Smuggling, resisting, whatever the harbor patrol feels like writing down. The *Mercy* gets auctioned to cover impound fees, and I'll be standing in the crowd bidding four hundred pesos out of spite." He shrugged, a small, tidy motion. "And Reyes finds the wreck without you, and your father's name stays exactly as dirty as it's been for eleven years."

"You've thought about this."

"I think about everything, Ansel. It's why I'm rich and you're digging holes."

I looked out at the water. Somewhere under that flat gray sheet, the *San Telmo* sat in her grave with silver in one hold and a crime in the other, and two men were negotiating over her bones like she was furniture. I felt the old hunger — the one the charts had woken in me, the one that whispered *fourteen thousand pesos, a boat, a door that locks* — and I let it speak, and I let it finish, and then I set it down like a tool I wasn't going to pick up.

"Half," I said.

Quist's eyebrows went up. "Half the charts?"

"Half the debt. You burn fourteen. The rest I pay when I'm paid — because if your salvage partner finds that wreck, you'll be flush enough to forgive a girl a few thousand pesos."

"That's not how forgiveness works."

"It's how *mine* works."

He laughed, genuinely, and for a second I understood why people trusted him right up until they didn't. "You're your father's daughter," he said. "He negotiated too. Right up to the end."

The sentence landed the way he'd built it to land — soft on top, hooks underneath. *Right up to the end.* He watched me take it. He wanted to watch me take it.

"Tomorrow," I said. "Sunset. The cave path. I'll bring the charts."

"And I'll bring your debt and Ferro's freedom." He put his hat back on, and the sentiment left his face like a light going out in a window. "Ansel. A word of professional advice. Don't be clever. Clever people end up in the ground you dig."

"Then I'll keep it shallow," I said. "Easier on everyone."

* * *

Sela Chumara came to my door that night, which she had done exactly twice before, and both times someone's life had changed shape by morning.

She didn't sit. She stood in my kitchen with her arms folded, an old woman built like a mooring post, and she looked at the charts spread across my table — the real ones, the complete ones, which I had no intention of showing anyone, and which I had covered with the incomplete copy the moment I heard her knock.

"Quist was seen on the cliff path," she said.

"He made an offer."

"You're taking it."

"I'm trading him a copy. Incomplete. The shoal coordinates shifted eleven years ago — the sand moved after the '09 storm, any fisherman could tell him that. He'll dive his heart out over a mast field that's drifted two hundred meters off his map."

Sela's eyes went to the table, to the charts under their cloth, and I had the distinct feeling she could see through cloth. "And Ferro?"

"Walks free tomorrow. The *Mercy* comes home."

"And the ledgers stay down there. And the names stay down there. And Reyes keeps dredging the wrong sand for two tides until her divers are good enough to find the right one." She said it without heat, which was worse. Heat I could argue with. "You're buying time."

"I'm buying *Ferro*. Time is what's left over."

"No." She unfolded her arms, slowly. "Time is what you're spending. Ferro is what you're buying. There's a difference, girl, and the difference is who pays."

I wanted to tell her she was wrong. I wanted it so badly my jaw ached with it. Because she was right, and we both knew it, and the trade I'd made had a shape I recognized from my worst arithmetic: I was spending the two tides of Reyes's confusion — the only lead we had — to buy back one old man and one old boat. It was a good trade for Ferro. It might be a fatal one for the *San Telmo*, for the ledgers, for every name in them.

"Call it cowardice," I said. "Everyone else will."

"I didn't say cowardice." Sela moved to the door, then stopped with her hand on the frame. "Cowardice is when you run from a thing because you're afraid. You're not running. You're standing in a room with a fire and choosing which child to carry out first." She looked back at me, and for one unguarded moment there was something in her face that wasn't judgment — something tired and old and familiar with exactly this kind of arithmetic. "Tomás made the same kind of trade, once. Signed his name to keep his daughter fed. I hated him for it for eleven years." A pause. "I'm not sure I hate him correctly anymore."

She left. I sat with the charts for a long time, and the lamp burned low, and I traced my father's hand across the soundings — *T.V., 19—* in the corner of every sheet, that confident slant — and I thought about how a signature can be a confession, a weapon, or a price, depending on who's holding the paper.

Then I got out the tracing paper and began to make the copy that was going to buy a man's freedom and cost me everything else.

* * *

I made the copy honest in every way that could be checked and wrong in every way that mattered.

The coastline, true. The reef, true. The soundings — I aged them, shifted them, worked the drift of the '09 storm into every depth figure so that the whole chart sat rotated off the wreck by a distance only someone who had actually dived the site would catch. I added a false detail, because the best lies are specific: a notation in my father's style — I'd been reading his hand for a week; I could forge him better than Quist's people ever forged him — marking a secondary debris field southeast of the shoal. A trail of breadcrumbs leading to clean sand.

The hardest part was the compass rose. His had a small flaw, a nick in the north point where he'd dropped the brass original as a boy, and he'd carried the flaw into every chart he ever drew, an honest man's scar repeated in ink. I gave the copy the scar. If Quist's people were good enough to check that, they deserved to be fooled by it.

I worked until the lamp oil was gone, and then I sat in the dark and listened to the tide turn below the cliffs, and I did not feel clever. I felt like a woman selling a map of her own father's grave, and the only comfort I could find was that the grave wasn't where the map said it was.

* * *

Sunset, the cave path. Quist came with two men who stood far enough away to be deniable and close enough to be useful, and he came alone to the tide pool where I waited with the rolled charts in oilcloth.

"Before we trade," he said, "a demonstration of good faith." He produced an envelope and handed it over. Inside: Ferro's release order, signed by the harbor magistrate, dated tomorrow morning — and beneath it, the *Mercy*'s impound papers, stamped RELEASED, and Ferro's mortgage note, the one Quist's cousin held, marked PAID IN FULL in red ink that looked wet enough to still be bleeding.

"Signed but not delivered," Quist said. "You give me the charts, I give you the envelope, and tomorrow morning a runner delivers it to the magistrate's desk before Ferro's hearing. Everyone walks away whole."

"And if I just take the envelope and keep the charts?"

"Then Ferro spends five years in the territorial prison and I sell his boat to a man who'll scrap her." He smiled. "But you won't. You're your father's daughter. You pay what you say."

That's the thing about being known — it can be a knife as easily as a gift. He knew I'd pay. He'd built his whole trade on it.

I handed him the oilcloth roll. He unrolled it right there, kneeling on the wet stone like a merchant at market, and checked what he knew to check: the compass rose, the signature, the date, the density of soundings. His finger paused on the southeast debris notation.

"This wasn't in the survey notes Reyes's people described."

"The notes were a summary. The charts are the work." I kept my voice bored. "Southeast field is probably the stern section. Broke off in the sinking. Your Captain will want to start there — it's shallower."

He rolled the charts up and stood, and for a moment he looked at me with something almost like respect, which from Marlo Quist was the same as a hand on the back of your neck.

"Your father made a similar trade once," he said. "Charts for time. It bought him eleven days." He tucked the roll under his arm. "I'd have taken the whole debt, you know. All you had to do was ask."

"I know," I said. "That's why I didn't."

He laughed all the way down the path. The two deniable men fell in behind him, and the sea came up the cave throat below us, right on schedule, swallowing the light.

* * *

Ferro walked out of the harbor office at nine the next morning, thinner and meaner-looking than a week of bad food should have made him, and he came straight up the hill to my door without passing go, without going home, and stood in my kitchen looking like a storm that had decided to make landfall indoors.

"Eleven days," he said.

"I'm sorry?"

"Eleven days I sat in that cell while you traded my boat's papers to Marlo Quist like a sack of onions." He dropped the envelope — the release order, the mortgage note — on my table. "I read everything they let me read. I know what you gave him."

"You'd be in a cell for five years otherwise."

"I *know*." He said it loud, then again, quieter, like he was making himself believe it. "I know. And I'd have made the same trade, and I'd have hated myself for it, which is why I'm standing here hating you for it, because it's cheaper than the alternative." He lowered himself into the chair across from me, joints cracking like rigging, and put his scarred hands flat on the table. "How long do we have?"

"Two tides. Maybe three, if their divers are bad. The sand moved in '09 — his charts are rotated off the wreck by two hundred meters. But Reyes has sonar, and sonar doesn't care about charts. Sooner or later her buoys paint the mast field, and then it's over."

"So we have two tides."

"We have two tides."

He was quiet for a while. Outside, the gulls were working the fish waste behind the cannery, that ragged argumentative sound the town made instead of thinking.

"The ledgers," he said finally. "They're still down there. Under the silver."

"They're still down there."

"And the claim documents. Your father's cache."

"Still down there."

He looked at his hands. When he looked up, the storm in him had settled into something harder and more useful. "The blowhole passage," he said. "My father took me through it before you were born. It runs behind the reef, under the point, comes up inside the cave when the surge is right. It's a night passage, a bad-weather passage, and no fleet pilot alive knows it, because no fleet pilot ever had a father crazy enough to run it." He stood, joints cracking again, and there was almost — almost — the ghost of the old Ferro grin. "You bought two tides, girl. Let's see what we can steal in them."

* * *

I went to see Sela that evening, because a plan made without her was a plan made against her, and I had learned which of those I could survive.

She was on her porch above the salt road, shelling beans into a bowl, and she listened to the whole thing — the trade, the false charts, the two tides, the blowhole — without looking up from her work. When I finished, the shells made a small dry sound in the bowl, and the sea made its larger one below.

"Two tides," she said.

"Two tides."

"And if the fleet finds the wreck in the first one?"

"Then everything I paid Quist was for nothing, and Ferro's freedom is the only thing I bought, and it will have to be enough."

Sela set down the bowl. "You know what the old ones called a tide like this? The one that runs out too far, that shows you ground no one's seen in a generation?" She looked at me, and there was no forgiveness in it and no condemnation either — just the flat appraisal of a woman deciding whether to put her family in a boat. "They called it a lying tide. It shows you the bottom, and the bottom looks like a gift, and it drowns more people than the storm does."

"I know what I paid, Sela."

"You know what it *cost*. Those aren't the same thing, and the difference is going to come due." She picked the bowl back up. "Tell Ferro I'll have boats at the point tomorrow night. Tell him the Chumara families remember the blowhole too — we were running it before his father was born." She went back to her shelling, and I was dismissed, and as I walked down the salt road in the last of the light I passed the harbor, and I saw them.

The *Corvina*'s tenders, out past the buoy line, dropping a new pattern of sonar floats — tighter, closer inshore, working their way toward the shoal with the patience of something that had all night and every night after.

Two tides. I stood on the seawall and counted them like coins, and for the first time since I'd pulled the satchel from the cave, the arithmetic came out on the wrong side of zero.

Behind me, up the hill, the cemetery gates stood open in the wind, and I thought — not for the first time, and not, I suspected, for the last — about how much of my life I'd spent digging holes for other people's endings, and how the next hole I stood over might be my own.

Tomorrow night, the blowhole. Tonight, there were charts to burn, lies to feed, and a tide coming in that I intended to lie to.
