## 16. Tide Five, Storm Rising

The storm came in off the water the way bad news always does in Puerto Aliso — slowly at first, so you can pretend it isn't yours, and then all at once, so you can't.

I was on my porch shelling beans when the light went wrong. You learn to read a sky the way you read a face. This one had the look of a man about to ask you for money. The wind swung south, then east, then quit entirely, which is the worst thing a wind can do before a squall. The gulls went inland. The dogs went quiet. Old Marta Chumara — no relation to me by blood and every relation by stubbornness — came up my steps without knocking, which she has not done since her husband's funeral, and said only: "Barometer's falling like Quist's reputation."

So that was that. We were going to have weather, and we were going to have it soon, and we were going to have it exactly when we could least afford it.

I want to say something about tides before I go any further, because everyone in this story keeps talking about them as if they were appointments you could reschedule. A tide is not an appointment. It is a debt coming due, and I have lived seventy-one years on this coast, and I will tell you the one law I have never seen broken: the sea collects. The only question is what you'll be holding when it does.

* * *

Ansel Vasquez came to my kitchen that evening looking like a woman who had been arguing with herself for three days and losing.

She stood in the doorway with rain already spotting her shoulders — the first scouts of the front — and she didn't sit until I told her to, which I noted and approved of. Her father had the same manners when he was frightened. Tomás Dunmore sat where he was put and looked at his hands and spoke in surveyor's numbers, as if coordinates could keep a man honest. They didn't. But that is an old grief and I have carried it long enough that it has worn smooth, like a stone in the pocket. Some nights I even take it out and look at it without hating it.

"Two tide windows left," Ansel said. "Maybe one, if the squall does what it looks like it's going to do."

"Then say what you came to say."

"The cave's watched. Reyes posted men on the cliff path two days ago — armed ones, not harbor patrol, private contract. And there's a launch anchored off the point with floodlights." She set a hand-drawn chart on my table, weighted at the corners with four of my own bean jars, which I found either respectful or presumptuous. "We can't go through the front door anymore."

"You never could," I said. "That was the whole point of your father's plan, if he had one beyond hiding things from people braver than him."

Something crossed her face — anger, maybe, or its exhausted cousin — and I let it cross. I am not a kind woman. Kindness is a currency and I stopped carrying small change years ago. But I refilled her coffee, which in my house is the same thing said more quietly.

"There's another way in," she said. "Isn't there. Ferro mentioned it once — half-mentioned — the blowhole passage. He said only locals know it."

"Ferro mentions a great many things after his third glass. Most of them are true, which is the trouble with Ferro."

"Will you show me?"

I looked at her for a long moment. Rain was starting properly now, ticking against the window like a slow applause for something nobody had done yet.

"No," I said. "But I'll take you. There's a difference, and the difference is that if you drown, I'll be there to see it done properly."

* * *

Here is what the town knows about the blowhole, which is nothing, and here is what the Chumara families know, which is everything, and here is why those two facts have stayed separate for a hundred and forty years:

When our people were moved off the salt road — and I use the word *moved* the way you'd use it for furniture, because that is how the government's paperwork used it — the elders made a decision. They would give the new town their labor, their fish, their songs at the founding festival, everything that could be taken anyway. But they kept the map of the coast under their tongues. Every cave, every spring, every passage the limestone hid below the waterline. Not out of spite, though there was spite enough. Out of arithmetic. People who own nothing else can still own the way home.

My grandmother showed me the blowhole passage when I was nine years old. We went at dead low water, in the hour before dawn, and she made me swim it twice — once with a lamp, once without — and when I came up gasping in the inner chamber she said, "Now you can never be locked out of your own country again." Then she hit me with an open palm for scaring her, which is also inheritance, depending on the family.

The passage runs from a sink pool in the salt grass above the cliffs, down through a flooded throat of limestone, and out into the cave itself — the same cave where Tomás Dunmore hid his satchel, and later his letter, and later still, God help us, a case of documents so rotten with fraud that paper should have refused to hold them. At high water the throat floods completely. At low water there is air in it, a lungful of dark and dripping stone, enough to breathe through if you are calm, and not nearly enough if you are not.

It is not a route. It is a test with a very short pass list.

* * *

We met in Marta's net shed on the second night of the storm, eleven of us, and I will record the count because counts matter. Eleven: myself; Ansel; Ferro, who arrived wet, uninvited, and indispensable; Marta; her sons Emilio and Rafael; old Benicio, who is seventy-eight and claims he swam the passage last year, which is a lie, but a useful one, because it makes the young men ashamed to hesitate; the Ocampo sisters, Luz and Pilar, who between them run every boat worth running in this town; and two of the younger generation, Tomasito Ocampo — named for a man none of us will forgive, which is its own kind of joke — and a girl called Nena who is nineteen and dives like she was invented underwater.

Ferro spread the chart across the bait table and pinned it with knives, which is how all honest maps get pinned.

"Reyes has six men on the cliff path," he said. "Rotating watch, lamp signals every twenty minutes. Launch sits off the point with a searchlight sweeps the cave mouth every ninety seconds when the fog lifts. She's not guarding a hole in a rock, friends. She's guarding a courthouse. Everything in that cave is going to be evidence someday, and she knows whose courtroom it ends up in."

"And the passage?" Ansel asked.

"Suicide at spring flood," Ferro said. "Doable at the back side of the squall, if the barometer turns and gives us four hours of ebb. Four hours, maybe five. After that the throat closes and stays closed till the next generation of fools."

Marta snorted. "The next generation is sitting at this table."

Which was true, and everyone went quiet about it, the way people do when a truth walks into the room wearing work clothes.

I let the silence sit. Then I did the thing I had been dreading, which was the thing I had come to do.

"I'm not going to order anyone," I said. "This is not the salt road. Nobody owes me labor, and nobody owes this coast anything — least of all us, who've paid on it for a century and a half and never held the deed. So hear the terms plain, and then decide like free people, because that's what they tell us we are, and I'd like to try it once before I die."

I laid it out. If we went, some of us might not come back. The passage in storm swell is a throat that swallows. If Reyes caught us on the water, we wouldn't be arrested — arrest is for people with paperwork, and we have never qualified. And if we succeeded, if we brought up the ledgers and the claim documents, then every family in this room would spend the next ten years in courtrooms fighting men with better lawyers and worse grandmothers.

"But," I said, "if we don't go, the permits expire at noon on the sixth day, the auction opens, and Reyes buys the water over our ancestors' bones with money she made selling the lie that took the land in the first place. And our grandchildren will dig clams above a wreck full of their own names, and never know the names were written down somewhere, waiting."

Nena spoke first. Nineteen years old, and she spoke first.

"My great-great-grandmother is in those manifests," she said. "They have her name in a box at the bottom of the sea and they call it cargo. I'm going."

Emilio said the boats would go. The Ocampo sisters argued about who would pilot and settled it in eleven seconds, which is a family record. Benicio volunteered to swim the passage, was refused by unanimous vote, and volunteered again, which is also inheritance. Even Ferro — Ferro, who wants silver and retirement and a porch somewhere the past can't find him — even Ferro said, grumbling, that someone had to keep these maniacs from drowning, and it might as well be a maniac with forty years of local water.

Ansel said nothing through most of it. When I finally turned to her, she was looking at the chart, at the little penciled X her father had drawn thirty years ago in a hand I used to trust.

"They'll say we stole it," she said quietly. "Reyes. Quist's people. The papers. They'll call it theft."

"It is theft," I said. "We are stealing back what was stolen. That is the oldest trade on this coast, and the rates are terrible, and we're taking it anyway."

* * *

The plan, for those who like plans, was this:

At the turn of the barometer — Ferro swore he could smell it coming, and I believed him, because a man who has been wrong about weather as often as Ferro and still alive must be right sometimes by sheer statistical necessity — three boats would run north from the old salt-road landing, well away from the watched cliff path, and hold position in the lee of the reef until the ebb opened the sink pool. Sela's people — my people, our people — would go down the throat in relays: divers first with lamps and line, then runners hauling sealed drums for the ledgers and the document case, waterproofed with wax and canvas. Ansel would make the final descent into the flooded chamber where her father's cache lay, because it was her father's cache, and because — though I said this to no one — I wanted her to be the one who reached into that dark and pulled out what he left. Let her carry it up. Let her feel the weight of what he couldn't finish.

The surface boats would hold against Reyes's launch if it came. With what, you ask? With nets, with hulls, with bodies, with the oldest fleet in the world, which is people who have decided.

Rafael asked the question everyone was thinking: "And if she brings the Corvina around?"

"She won't," Ferro said. "Corvina draws too much for the reef in this swell. She'll send the launch and her divers. And her divers—" he paused, and I watched him decide whether to say the next part, and say it, "—her divers are working men. Half of them grew up here. When it comes to it, in the dark, with no cameras — men don't fight as hard for a paycheck as they think they will."

"That's a gamble," Marta said.

"Everything is a gamble," I said. "The only sure thing is the tide, and the tide is against us on a schedule. Get some sleep. All of you. Tomorrow night we find out what our inheritance weighs."

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat on my porch instead and listened to the storm work itself up to its full argument, and I thought about my grandmother hitting me with an open palm in the dark water fifty-nine years ago, and about Tomás Dunmore sitting in this same kitchen twenty-some years ago, asking me — actually asking me — whether I thought a man could sign a thing and stay clean of it, if his heart was in the right place.

I told him no. He signed anyway. And then, being Tomás, he spent the rest of his life building a trapdoor under his own signature, and died before he could spring it, and left it to a daughter he barely knew to do the springing.

There is a sermon in that, probably. I've never cared for sermons. What I care about is that tomorrow night, a girl named Nena who is nineteen years old is going to swim a flooded limestone throat to bring up a ledger with her great-great-grandmother's name in it, and that name is going to be read aloud in daylight, in front of the whole harbor, and no one — not Reyes, not Quist, not the entire state registry — will be able to put it back in the box at the bottom of the sea.

That is worth a storm. That is possibly the only thing that has ever been worth a storm.

Near dawn the wind backed around to the northeast, and Ferro appeared at the foot of my steps, soaked, grinning like a shark that has heard dinner bell.

"Barometer's turning," he called up. "Ebb starts at moonset. Four hours, maybe five."

I looked at the sky, which was the color of a bruise healing, and at the sea, which was already rehearsing.

"Then wake them all," I said. "Tell them to bring lamps, line, and their grandmothers' names. We're going to need all three."
