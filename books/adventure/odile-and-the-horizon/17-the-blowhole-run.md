## 17. The Blowhole Run

The storm arrived two hours early, the way storms do when you've scheduled your life around them.

I stood on Ferro's dock with rain already needling the back of my neck and watched Sela Chumara's people load three boats by lantern light — no electric lamps, nothing a patrol launch could spot from a mile out. Old men who'd spent forty years reading water moved crates of rope and air tanks like they were setting a dinner table. A teenage girl coiled line with hands so quick they blurred. Nobody talked much. When people talk before something like this, it's usually to convince themselves, and everyone there had already done their convincing alone, in kitchens, over unpaid bills and older griefs.

"You're late," Sela said, without looking at me.

"I'm exactly on time."

"Then the storm's late." She finally turned. Rain ran off her jaw and she didn't bother wiping it. "Reyes posted four men on the cliff path above the cave. My nephew counted them at dusk. If they hear engines, we lose everything."

"So we don't use engines past the point."

"We use paddles and prayers." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Your father used to say the sea doesn't care which of us is right. Only which of us is patient."

"He said a lot of things."

"Yes," she said. "And hid the important ones where only the desperate would look." She handed me a lantern and turned away, and that was the whole conversation, because Sela didn't waste words on things she'd already decided.

* * *

We went out through the harbor mouth under a sky the color of a bruise going bad. The swell was running eight feet, maybe ten, and Mercy took each one like an old woman climbing stairs — grudgingly, with complaints in the timbers. Ferro had the wheel and I had the chart in a plastic sleeve against my chest, and between us we said maybe twenty words in an hour.

The blowhole wasn't on any chart worth trusting. It was a crack in the headland south of the cave, a chimney the ocean had drilled through rock over some obscene number of millennia, and at high tide with a storm pushing behind it, it breathed. Water shot thirty feet into the air with a sound like the coast clearing its throat. On the seaward side, below the spray line, there was a slot — six feet wide at slack, less tonight, a black seam in black rock that you could thread a small boat through if you were either very skilled or very tired of owing money.

Ferro was both.

"Last chance to be sensible," he said, killing the engine a hundred yards off the rocks. He'd said it twice already. It was becoming our liturgy.

"Sensibility's for people with collateral," I said, and took up my paddle.

He snorted. Behind us, Sela's two boats did the same, engines off, oars out, riding swells toward a hole in the world that wanted to kill us politely.

Here is what threading a blowhole in a storm actually feels like: you paddle hard when every instinct screams at you to stop, because stopping means the next wave picks your boat up and files it against the rock. You steer by lightning flash, one white stroboscopic frame at a time — rock, foam, gap, rock — and you trust a man you've known for a month to call the sets. Ferro called them. "Now. NOW. Pull—" and then the world went vertical and sideways and loud, and then, all at once, quiet.

We were inside. The slot had spat us into a chamber beneath the headland, a cathedral of wet stone where the storm was just a rumor overhead and the blowhole breathed above us, exhaling mist that fell back down like slow rain. Sela's boats slid in behind ours, one scraping stone with a sound that made three people flinch. Lanterns came up, hooded, and the light found walls streaked white with salt and a ledge of black rock along the eastern side, dry enough to stand on.

And beyond the ledge, the inner pool.

I'd known it would be there — Tomás's charts showed the flooded chamber connecting to the main cave, the route he'd marked in pencil with a note in the margin: *only at flood, only calm inside even when it isn't outside.* Inside the headland, the surge died to a slow breathing swell, three feet up, three feet down, patient as tide itself. The pool went down into dark and kept going.

"That's where he went," I said. Not a question. I'd been saying it to myself since the archive, since the letter, since the second cache told me my father had stood on this exact ledge with forged signatures in his pocket and a conscience he couldn't drown.

Sela came to stand beside me. For a long moment she looked at the water the way you look at a grave you never got to visit.

"My mother fed him in this room," she said quietly. "Before he signed. Before we knew what he'd signed. He sat right there—" she pointed at the ledge, "—and ate fish stew and told me the horizon was the most honest thing in the world, because it never pretended to be closer than it was." She exhaled. "Get the ledgers, Ansel. Bring me the names. That's the only honesty left to collect."

I stripped to my wetsuit while Ferro checked my tank, my regulator, my knife, my line — his hands moving with the economy of a man who'd rigged gear through forty years of weather. He tied the safety line to my harness himself and cinched it.

"Ninety minutes of air," he said. "You come up at eighty or I'm coming down after you, and I'm too old to drown well."

"Noted."

"And Ansel." He held my shoulder, and in the hooded lantern light his face was all creases and stubbornness. "Whatever's down there — silver, paper, ghosts — you bring yourself back first. Everything else is cargo."

I almost smiled. "Since when are you sentimental?"

"Since I mortgaged my boat to help a grave-digger rob a shipwreck. Sentimental's the only thing left I can afford." He pushed me gently toward the edge. "Go."

* * *

The water swallowed me whole and the storm disappeared.

That's the thing nobody tells you about diving in bad weather: three feet under, it might as well not exist. All that violence, all that wind and thunder and the fleet hunting us a quarter mile north — reduced to a distant shudder in the rock, a heartbeat in the stone. Down here there was only my own breath, loud as surf in my ears, and the beam of my torch cutting a cone through water thick with stirred silt.

Tomás's chart said the flooded chamber ran sixty meters inland, descending to twelve, then opening into a fissure that connected to the San Telmo's broken stern section — the part of the wreck that had driven him half-mad trying to map, the part Reyes's sonar buoys had never quite resolved because the reef ate their signals here. His margin notes were maddeningly terse. *Watch the third arch. Silt lies. Trust the compass.*

I trusted the compass. My father's brass compass, the one thing of his I'd carried through every bad year, hung on a lanyard against my chest now, its needle steady in the housing while silt ghosted past like dirty snow. Somewhere above me, the blowhole exhaled and the whole column of water shivered.

The third arch was a lie, exactly as advertised. What looked like the passage on approach was a dead-end throat of rock; the real way in was a slot beneath it, barely wider than my shoulders, and I went through it exhaling, scraping my tank, feeling the rock press close the way it does in dreams where you're late for something. On the far side the fissure opened and dropped away and my torch couldn't find the bottom.

But it found wood.

The San Telmo's stern loomed out of the murk like the ribcage of something that had died standing up. Two hundred years of sea had eaten her soft parts and dressed her bones in coral and anemone, and the hold gaped where the hull had split, a wound in the wreck that Tomás had marked with a single word: *here.*

I went in.

The silver was still there. Of course it was. Stacked ingots furred green-black with age, a king's ransom lying in the dark doing absolutely nothing for anyone, and I felt Ferro's argument rise in my chest like heartburn — *raise it quietly, retire, be done* — and I understood it, God help me, I understood it completely. That much metal could buy a hundred lifetimes free of Marlo Quist. Free of tides and debts and digging holes for strangers' dead.

I put my hand on an ingot anyway and lifted it aside, because underneath the silver, Tomás's letter had promised, were the ledgers. And the ledgers were the actual treasure, the kind you couldn't melt down or fence, the kind that once read aloud in daylight could take a fleet apart.

They were wrapped in oilcloth, tarred canvas over that, sealed with wax that had gone amber and brittle. Three bundles, each the size of a hymnal, and my hands shook so badly working them free that I had to stop and press my palm flat against the ingots until the shaking passed. This was it. Names. Wages owed across two centuries. Chumara laborers loaded aboard like cargo, recorded in a clerk's neat hand, every entry a theft with a date on it.

I stuffed the bundles into the mesh bag clipped to my harness. And then my torch swept the back of the hold, where the chart showed a second cavity behind a collapsed bulkhead — and stopped.

A case. Steel-reinforced, diver's grade, modern, with a brass latch gone green. Tomás's initials scratched into the lid beside a date from the year he vanished.

My father had been here. After the forging, after Quist, after whatever happened in whatever room he'd run out of — he'd come back down through this same black water with his lungs burning and cached the original claim documents, the fleet's true founding papers, the ones that proved the permits weren't just forged but built on land stolen twice over. He'd hidden the evidence in the belly of the crime itself, and then he'd gone out into the world carrying nothing but a compass and a daughter he probably thought was better off without him.

I worked the case loose. It was heavier than the ledgers, somehow, though the scales would have disagreed. Some weights don't register on instruments.

My gauge said fifty minutes gone. Time to go home.

* * *

Coming up through the blowhole pool was like being born in reverse — pressure easing, darkness thinning, and then my head broke the surface into lantern light and rain-smell and Sela's hand closing around my arm before I'd even finished spitting out my regulator.

"The bag," she said.

"Hello to you too." I hauled myself onto the ledge and unclipped the mesh bag, and when the tarred canvas came into the lantern light, Sela Chumara went very still. Stillness, on her, was louder than shouting. Around us, her people pressed in — the old men, the quick-handed girl, faces lit orange and wet — and someone made a sound low in their chest that wasn't quite a word and wasn't quite a prayer.

"Three ledgers," I said, peeling back the wax. "Manifests. Names, wages, dates. And this—" I set the steel case on the rock between us. "The fleet's original claims. My father cached them. He hid them here and let the world think he'd run."

Sela knelt and touched the case lid, tracing the scratched initials, and for a moment the matriarch was just an old woman in the rain touching a dead man's handwriting. Then she straightened, and the moment was over, folded away somewhere the rest of us wouldn't see.

"Up," she said. "All of it goes up. Nothing stays in this hole another hour."

We were loading the boats when Ferro's hand closed hard on my shoulder and turned me north, toward the chamber mouth, toward the sea beyond.

A light was sweeping the cliffs above the cave entrance. Not a lantern. A searchlight, white and industrial and patient, playing across the rock where Reyes's watchers had been posted — except now there were more of them, six, eight, silhouetted against the glare, and below them, visible through the storm-haze at the cave mouth itself, a launch. Black hull, twin engines idling, riding the swell like it owned it.

On its deck, a figure in a yellow foul-weather coat raised a megaphone, and even over the storm, even across the water, I knew the voice before the words arrived.

"Ansel Vasquez." Captain Ilsa Reyes, conversational, almost warm, the way a knife is warm after it's been used. "You have my ledgers. And I have your exit."

Ferro's grip tightened. Sela's people went silent as stones. The blowhole breathed above us, exhaling its slow white plume, and I stood on the ledge with two centuries of stolen names strapped to my chest and watched the woman who'd bought my father's signature smile up at the cliff she intended to keep me inside of.

"Come out slowly," Reyes called, "or I seal it behind you."
