## 19. Cave-In

The charge went off at 3:47 in the morning, and I know the time because I was looking at Ferro's watch when the world under the cliff decided to rearrange its furniture.

We were in the lee of the blowhole rocks, Sela's people hauling the last of the waterproof cases into the boats — ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, my father's claim documents sealed in tin like they were communion wafers. Which, in a way, they were. The whole history of a theft, packed for transport.

Then the sound came up through the rock instead of the air. That's the thing about explosions underwater — you don't hear them so much as you feel them in your teeth, your ribs, the soles of your feet. The sea hiccuped. A column of spray stood up out of the blowhole like a ghost changing its mind about leaving.

"Ansel." Sela's hand closed on my arm. Her grip was iron with salt water in it. "The divers."

I'd already counted. Reyes had brought six divers on her launch — I'd watched them gear up by floodlight an hour before, professional and patient, men who did this for wages and didn't ask what the wages were for. When the charge blew, two of them were still inside the flooded chamber, cutting the blowhole's throat from below so the collapse would finish the job the explosive started.

Two divers. Inside a cave that was now trying to become rubble.

"They knew the risk," someone said behind me. One of Sela's nephews, maybe. His voice was steady, which meant he was either very brave or hadn't thought it through.

"They were told to be there," Sela said quietly. "That's different from knowing."

Ferro put his hand on Mercy's gunwale like he was steadying himself against a decision. "Tide's turning. You go in there now, you've got maybe twenty minutes before the surge fills that passage wall to wall."

"I know."

"And the ceiling's coming down in pieces. You felt that last shift."

"I know, Ferro."

He looked at me for a long moment. In the dark I couldn't see his face properly, just the shape of him, the same shape that had argued with me over silver ingots three tides ago and sailed off alone into a harbor patrol's arms because of it. We hadn't finished that argument. We might never finish it. But he didn't say don't.

He said, "Your tank's only half full," and started unclipping the spare.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about going back underwater to save people who were sent to kill you: your body does it without asking your opinion. My hands checked my gauges. My legs kicked me off the rocks and down into the black. Somewhere above me, Sela's voice carried across the water, giving orders — get the cases out, all of them, every name in those books comes up tonight or none do — and then the sea closed over my head and there was only the regulator's rasp and the thin blade of my dive light.

The inner chamber had been a cathedral when we came through it two hours ago. Vaulted stone, the tide breathing in and out of it, our lights making gold coins of the silt. Now it was a demolition site. Chunks of the ceiling lay across the floor like dropped masonry, and the water was thick with suspended rock flour, turning my beam into a wall of gray milk. Visibility: nothing. Sound: groaning. The cave was talking to itself, settling its bones, deciding which parts of itself it could afford to lose.

I swam toward where the divers should be.

Let me be honest, since honesty is the one currency I've got left after tonight: part of me wanted to turn around. Not most of me. Not even half. But some small cowardly clerk in the back office of my skull was filing a memo that read *these men helped rig a charge to bury you and everyone you care about, and the ocean is offering you a free pass.* I let the clerk finish the memo. Then I filed it under things my father would have done differently, and kept swimming.

Because here's the other thing, the thing I'd learned in a cemetery digging holes for strangers: a grave is a grave. It doesn't matter whose. You dig it straight, you dig it deep, and if you find somebody alive at the bottom of one, you get them out. Marlo Quist never understood that. Ilsa Reyes understood it and built a career on not caring. My father understood it too late, and it killed him, but he understood it.

I wasn't going to understand it too late.

The first diver found me, actually. Or his light did — a wild strobing sweep through the murk, panic made visible. He was pinned at the ankle under a slab the size of a door, wedged against a fallen rib of the old mast field that had drifted in here over decades. His mask was fogged with his own breathing, fast and shallow, the rhythm that burns through a tank in ten minutes. I grabbed his shoulder and he grabbed me back hard enough to bruise, and for a second we were just two people holding on in the dark, enemies by payroll.

I pointed at his ankle. He nodded. I pointed at my knife. He went very still, and I'll give him this — he trusted me, or he had no better option, which in a flooded cave amounts to the same sacrament.

The slab had settled on a pivot of rubble. I couldn't cut him free; I had to lift. I got my shoulder under the edge of the stone and pushed, and the cave answered with a low grinding note, like a ship complaining in heavy weather. Dust snowed down through my light. My shoulders screamed. Somewhere above us another piece of the ceiling let go and the shockwave shoved us both sideways.

The diver slid free.

He looked at me through his mask. I couldn't see his eyes, just the reflection of my own light. He pointed up, then at me, then pressed his fist to his chest — thank you, or I owe you, or something a man says when language has drowned. Then he kicked for the surface and the gray took him.

One.

* * *

The second one was worse.

I found him by sound — a tapping, faint and rhythmic, metal on stone. Three taps, pause, three taps. Somebody's training holding hands with somebody's terror. I followed it along the chamber's far wall, feeling my way more than seeing, until my light caught a boot, then a leg, then a man folded into a pocket of the collapse like a letter jammed in a mailbox.

The roof of his pocket was a shelf of rock the size of a dining table, and it was moving. Slowly, in the way of enormous things, tilting as the rubble beneath it shifted with the surge. Every time the tide breathed in, the shelf sank a centimeter. Every breath out, it stayed where it had sunk. Arithmetic. Cold arithmetic — I was good at that once, over charts, counting tides. Now I was counting centimeters between a living man and a dead one.

He saw my light and stopped tapping. His regulator was out of his mouth, hanging loose, and he was breathing the pocket's trapped air, which was going bad — I could tell by how gray his lips were behind the mask. I put my spare regulator in his hands and mimicked: breathe, slow, breathe. He did. His hands were shaking so hard the mouthpiece chattered against his teeth.

Getting him out was a problem with no elegant solution. The space was too tight to lever the shelf. The only way through was the gap beneath it, a slot maybe eighteen inches high, floored with broken rock. He'd have to exhale everything, flatten himself, and crawl while I pulled. And he'd have to trust that the shelf wouldn't come down while he was under it, which no amount of trust can guarantee.

I tapped three times on the rock beside his head. Held up one finger. Then I pointed at the gap, and at him, and made the flattest shape I could with my hand.

He shook his head. Pointed past me, toward the open water, then drew his thumb across his throat. Go. Leave. The gesture of a man doing arithmetic of his own and not liking the sum.

I took his face in both hands, mask to mask, and shook my head at him the way you'd shake a child awake. Then I pointed at the gap again, and this time I didn't ask.

What happened next took four minutes and cost me a fin, a good deal of skin, and whatever remaining belief I had that adrenaline feels like triumph. It doesn't. It feels like cold water in your sinuses and a voice in your head reciting numbers. He exhaled, folded, and went into the slot like a man diving under a fence, and I hauled him through by the harness while the shelf above us groaned down that final centimeter and touched the rubble where his shoulder had been with a click soft as a lock turning.

Then we were both in open water, and the cave exhaled around us, and I have never been so grateful to be in a place I hated.

We went up together, his arm locked in mine, through the throat of the blowhole passage where the surge was already running — the tide turning, Ferro's twenty minutes spent, the water pushing us sideways like a crowd shoving toward an exit. My gauge needle was in the red. His was worse. We broke the surface inside the blowhole's chimney, in the narrow circle of storm-black sky, and gasped at air that tasted like rain and gunpowder and, honestly, like the best thing I'd ever had in my mouth.

Above us, silhouetted against the clouds, hands reached down from the rocks. Sela's people. They hauled the diver out first — I pushed him up ahead of me, and nobody argued, which told me something about what kind of night it had been. Then they took my arms, and the last thing I saw before the rocks took my weight was the inner chamber's mouth below us, pulsing with the surge, swallowing the rest of the cathedral whole.

It didn't make a sound when it went. Big destructions usually don't. It was the small ones — Quist's photograph, Reyes's offer, my father's signature on paper he didn't write — that had always made the noise.

* * *

They laid the two divers on the flat rock by the boats, coughing seawater, alive, wrapped in every dry garment the Chumara families had brought and didn't need anymore because the night was running hot with everything except temperature. The older one — the one from the slot — stared at the sky and breathed like a man renegotiating terms with God.

Reyes's launch was gone. Withdrawn into the storm, lights doused, captain presumably recalculating. She'd sent six men into a cave to die for a paperwork problem, and the paperwork problem had pulled two of them out. I wondered what story she'd tell them. I wondered what they'd believe.

Sela crouched beside the younger diver and said something to him in the old language, low and unhurried. He stared at her. She said it again, and this time I caught the shape of it even though I didn't know the words — it had the cadence of a question, and the gravity of one.

"What did you ask him?" I said.

"If he knows whose hold he was sent to destroy." She rose, joints cracking like the cave had cracked. "And I asked him if he wants to know. Because tomorrow, when the manifests are read in the harbor, he will hear names. Some of those names are my grandmother's brothers. And he was swimming over them with a torch, following orders." She looked down at him, and there was no mercy in her face and no cruelty either, just the terrible fairness of bedrock. "I want him to choose to listen. That is worth more than his apology."

The diver turned his head and looked at her. Then at me. Then at the tin cases stacked in the boats — the ledgers, the documents, the whole drowned archive of a crime that had paid for a fleet and forged a signature and orphaned a daughter twice over.

"Read them all," he said. His voice was gravel and salt. "Every name. Don't skip mine, either. I signed on with her the same way my father signed on with the company. Somebody should say it out loud."

Nobody answered him. There wasn't anything to say that the dawn wouldn't say better.

* * *

Ferro found me sitting on the gunwale of Mercy, watching the horizon perform its slow gray trick of becoming a day. My hands were shaking now that they'd decided they were allowed to. He sat down next to me without a word and handed me a thermos cap of coffee that tasted like burnt rope and salvation.

"You went back in," he said.

"The ledgers are safe. That was the point."

"That's not what I asked."

I drank the coffee. Out past the reef, the sea was working itself into whitecaps, the squall dragging its skirts north, taking the storm with it and leaving us the wreckage of a clear morning. Six tides we'd raced. This was the last one, and it was coming in at dawn with the permits expiring at noon, and somewhere in the harbor a crowd was gathering that didn't yet know what it was about to hear.

"Ferro," I said. "When I read those names today — my father's going to be one of them. Forged. On the permits, on the land claims, in the ledger margins where he wrote *paid in coercion* like a man keeping honest books about dishonest work." I turned the brass compass over in my palm, the one thing he'd left me besides the mess. "I spent six tides thinking I was clearing his name. Turns out I'm clearing it by hanging it in public."

Ferro was quiet for a while. A fisherman's quiet, the kind that has weather in it.

"My father ran cargo for men like Quist," he said finally. "Forty years. Everybody in Puerto Aliso knew and everybody ate, so everybody shut up. When he died, I found a notebook in his wheelhouse — every run, every payment, every name. Never showed anyone. Told myself it was loyalty." He looked at me. "It was cowardice with a coat on. You're doing the other thing. It costs more. That's how you know it's the other thing."

Below us, on the flat rock, Sela was helping the older diver to his feet. He stood swaying, a man returned from a country he'd been sent to conquer, and Sela put his arm over her shoulder and walked him toward the boats like he was family, which by morning, one way or another, he would be.

The tide was coming in. The last one. And the harbor bells had started to ring.
