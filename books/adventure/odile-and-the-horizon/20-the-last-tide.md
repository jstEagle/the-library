## 20. The Last Tide

I hadn't slept. That seemed fitting, somehow — the last tide of the six deserved a witness who was still awake to see it come in.

Dawn over Puerto Aliso came the way it always did, gray bleeding into gold behind the headland, and the harbor filled with light the way a wound fills with blood: slowly at first, then all at once. I stood on the seawall with my father's brass compass in one hand and a canvas satchel in the other, and I watched the water drop away from the pilings like the sea itself was holding its breath.

The ledgers were in the satchel. Three of them, salt-stiffened, their spines cracked from forty years underwater but their pages — God help me, Tomás Vasquez had waterproofed them better than he'd ever waterproofed anything about his own life — legible. Names in careful columns. Wages owed. Wages never paid. And on the flyleaf of each, the concession signatures that had made it all legal, all tidy, all theft.

One of those signatures was mine by inheritance. My father's hand, forged under coercion, confessing nothing and everything in every looping V.

* * *

The crowd gathered the way crowds do when they smell both a spectacle and a stake. Fishermen who should have been out past the reef an hour ago. The chandlery women with their arms folded. Old men who remembered the Chumara families being moved off the salt road when they were boys, and young people who'd only heard it as a story told quietly, the way you tell shameful things. Sela stood at the front of them, straight-backed despite the storm still working itself out of her shoulders, and beside her were four of her grandchildren carrying the recovered claim documents in oilcloth bundles like relics. Which is what they were.

At the far end of the seawall, the auction platform stood where the state surveyors had raised it two days ago. A bunting-strung podium. A clerk with a ledger of his own — the kind written in ink that dries fast and means less. The Corvina sat at anchor beyond the breakwater, riding high and wrong, her dredge arms stowed, her decks empty of everything but a skeleton crew who wouldn't look at shore. Captain Reyes wasn't aboard. I'd checked twice.

Noon was the deadline. The permits expired then, and whatever the gavel said before noon would stand for twenty years.

The auctioneer was a mainland man named Pell, all teeth and cufflinks, and he opened his mouth at half past eleven to begin the litany of lots — "the submerged wreck of the San Telmo, together with all salvage rights appurtenant—" — and that's when Sela looked at me.

She didn't nod. Sela Chumara didn't nod for anyone. She simply turned her face toward me the way a lighthouse turns, and the crowd turned with her, and there was no way left to be quiet.

I climbed onto the low stone of the harbor steps, because I refused to stand on their platform. Their platform was part of the problem.

"My name is Ansel Vasquez," I said, and my voice did something embarrassing in the middle of it, so I started again. "My name is Ansel Vasquez, and I'm going to read you some names."

Pell's clerk started forward with his official posture, and Ferro — released three days, mortgage papers back in his fist, a bruise on his jaw he wore like a medal — stepped into the man's path without a word. Just stood there. A wall with a boat.

"The San Telmo went down in 1889 carrying silver," I said. "That's the story. That's the story the fleet bought permits with, the story the auctions are for. Here's the rest of it."

I opened the first ledger.

"Manifiesto de carga, San Telmo, passage manifest, third hold." My voice steadied the way a rope does when you finally put your whole weight on it. "Aurelio Chumara. Laborer, contracted wage eight pesos per month. Paid: zero. Died aboard: yes."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

"Rosa Chumara, laundress, contracted wage five pesos per month. Paid: zero. Disembarked: no record."

I kept reading. Name after name after name, and the crowd stopped being a crowd and became something else — became the families themselves, standing up out of history one by one as I spoke them. An old woman near the chandlery began to weep without any noise at all, just tears running into the creases of her face, and I understood suddenly that she was hearing her grandmother's name spoken aloud for the first time in a hundred and thirty years.

"Total wages recorded as owed in this manifest alone," I said, "eleven thousand four hundred pesos. Total wages paid: none. And the land these laborers were taken from — the salt road, the cove lands, the cliff fields —" I held up one of Sela's oilcloth bundles — "was deeded to the shipping concern by this."

I unfolded the claim document. Held it high.

"This signature certifies that the Chumara families voluntarily relinquished their title. This signature is a forgery. It is a clumsy forgery — the descender on the T is wrong, the ink is a decade newer than the paper, and the man whose name it carries, Tomás Vasquez, surveyor, signed it in 1987 under threat, because Marlo Quist's people had already put one bullet through his door and promised the second one would find him."

Somewhere in the crowd someone said my father's name out loud, softly, like testing whether it burned.

"He hid the originals," I said. "In a cave below the cliffs, in a satchel sealed against the tide he knew would come. He couldn't bring them up himself. He was dead within the year — died in Quist's custody, an accident they never investigated because nobody with power wanted it investigated. He hid the truth and hoped someone braver than him would finish it." I looked down at the compass in my other hand. "He hoped, I think, it would be me. I'm not brave. I'm just the one who showed up."

Sela came forward then. She took the claim document from my hands and held it up herself, and her voice, when it came, was the voice of a woman reading a death notice for an empire.

"I am Sela Chumara. My grandmother's name is in that book. So is her mother's. These documents were recovered from the wreck and from the cave cache two nights past, in the presence of witnesses." She turned, slow, letting the crowd see her face. "For forty years the fleet told us our ancestors' bones were cargo. For forty years the state sold the water over their heads. I am here to say the debt is not expired. Debts like this don't expire. They wait."

Pell tried. I'll give him that; he had a living to make. He rapped his little gavel and called for order and announced that the auction would proceed on schedule, that allegations were not evidence, that the courts would sort out claims —

— and that's when the harbormaster walked down the seawall with his archive key still swinging from his finger and a face like a man arriving late to his own funeral. Behind him came two officials from the provincial capital, the ones Reyes had charmed at the docking ceremony, the ones now wearing expressions of men doing arithmetic they didn't like.

The harbormaster had been in that archive the night I broke in. He'd seen the real signature next to the forged ones. He'd said nothing for nine days, and I think it had been eating him alive, because he stepped up beside Sela now and said, in a voice that carried, "I certify the comparison. The concession signatures are false. I should have said it sooner. I'm saying it now."

The provincial officials conferred. It took four minutes. I counted them off against the tide charts in my head, watched the water keep dropping, the last of the six windows draining toward noon.

Then the taller one cleared his throat and announced, in the flat tone of bureaucracy trying to sound decisive: "The concession is suspended pending formal inquiry. The auction is void. All salvage operations in territorial waters are frozen effective immediately."

The sound the crowd made was not a cheer. It was bigger than a cheer — it was the sound of a hundred and thirty years exhaling. Old Aurelio's granddaughter fell against her son. Somebody started ringing the church bell, badly, joyfully, off-rhythm. And out past the breakwater, the Corvina's skeleton crew quietly began lowering her tender, because even vultures know when the carcass has stood up and walked away.

* * *

Afterward, when the bell had worn itself out and the crowd had thinned to stragglers and officials arguing happily about injunctions, Sela found me sitting on the harbor steps with the empty satchel across my knees.

"You read every name," she said.

"All of them. Both manifests. Took forty minutes."

"I know. I timed it." She lowered herself onto the step beside me with the care of a woman whose joints had earned their opinions. For a while she said nothing. Then: "Your father came to me the week before he ran. Asked if I'd hide him. I said no." Her jaw worked. "I despised him for what he'd signed. I let him walk into Quist's hands rather than dirty mine with him. If I'd said yes, he might be alive, and you might have grown up knowing him."

"That's not yours to carry either," I said. "There's enough of that going around."

"No," she agreed. "But it's mine, and I'll carry it. That's the difference between us and them, Ansel. We carry what's ours. They sell theirs and call it ownership." She looked out at the water, at the reef line showing its teeth in the ebb. "The families will file for standing over the wreck. Ancestral property. The lawyers say we have a case now — a real one, with documents. There will be hearings. Years of them."

"And the silver?"

"Stays down until the courts say otherwise." She glanced at me sideways, and there was something almost gentle in it. "You could have had your fortune this morning. One word to Pell before I arrived, and you'd be clear of every debt you ever inherited."

"The debts died with Quist anyway. Arrested yesterday — Ferro's impound records surfaced, and apparently forging permits isn't the only thing the man's empire was built on. Turns out empire-building leaves paperwork too."

"So it does." She rose, stiffly, and put her hand on my head briefly, the way you'd steady a child or bless a sailor. "You read the names. Remember that. Whatever else you are, that's what you did."

She walked off along the seawall toward her family, and I sat there watching the tide turn — the sixth tide, the last one, beginning its long slow climb back up the pilings, covering the reef, closing the water over the San Telmo like a hand closing over something it intended to keep safe.

Vindication, I decided, tastes like grief. Like salt. Like saying your dead father's crimes and courage out loud to a town that needed both to hear them. Like a bell rung badly. Like names.

Ferro came and sat beside me eventually, handed me a coffee gone cold in the wind, and asked the only question left.

"So what now, muchacha?"

Out past the breakwater, the horizon was doing what it always did — lying there, wide open, waiting to see who'd claim it honestly.

"Now," I said, "we go see what the courts do with a town that finally has proof. And then —" I turned the brass compass over in my palm, its needle swinging lazy and true — "then I think somebody owes me a boat ride past the reef."

Ferro grinned through his bruise. "Mercy's yours whenever you want her."

"Not mine," I said. "Just borrowed. Everything worth having is."

Behind us, the first of the inquiry notices was already going up on the harbormaster's board, and a boy was reading it aloud to people who couldn't read, and the tide came in, and came in, and came in.
