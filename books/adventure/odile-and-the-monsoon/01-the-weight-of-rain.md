## 1. The Weight of Rain

The first rain of the monsoon came to Sember the way debt collectors do—suddenly, and with every intention of staying.

Ulyana Underhill felt it arrive before she heard it. The air had been pressing on her all morning, thick as wet wool, and the harbor water lay flat and sullen beneath a sky the color of old tin. By three o'clock the tourists were complaining about the humidity in the clipped accents of people who had never once been uncomfortable by accident, and by four the sky simply gave up pretending and opened.

She stood under the awning of the Kestrel Guesthouse and watched it come down. Not rain, really—rain was what fell politely in other places, in thin silver threads you could walk through. This was a wall of water, a curtain drawn across the world, hammering the corrugated roofs of the fish market until speech became impossible. The gutters of Sember ran brown and fast. Somewhere down the waterfront, a tarpaulin tore loose and flapped like something wounded.

"Miss Wren." One of her clients, a Mr. Aldous-Pryce, appeared at her elbow, clutching his brand-new waterproof jacket around himself as though it were failing him personally. "The boat. Will we still make the crossing tomorrow?"

"The boat will make the crossing," Ulyana said. "Whether we're on it is another question."

He laughed uncertainly. They always laughed uncertainly. It was part of the service.

She was listed in the guesthouse ledger and on the laminated cards she handed out at the ferry office as *Mara Wren, Licensed Mountain Guide, Gentle Routes for Discerning Travelers*. The name fit her like a borrowed coat—close enough not to itch, loose enough that no one ever thought to look underneath. In six years no one had. She had made sure of that. Six years of sanitized routes: the waterfall trail with its handrails and its tea stall at the halfway point, the temple ridge where porters carried the daypacks of anyone who tipped, the sunrise viewpoint from which wealthy, restless people could photograph a wilderness they would never have to suffer. She measured her life now in miles walked and kilometers gained, in client satisfaction scores and the exact number of steps from the guesthouse door to the harbor wall—four hundred and twelve, she'd counted once, out of boredom or something worse than boredom.

Miles were safe. Miles didn't ask questions. A year was a dangerous unit of measurement; a year asked what you had done with yourself, and Ulyana Underhill had done nothing worth counting since the inquiry board in another country, another lifetime, had read her name aloud in a room that smelled of floor polish and ended everything.

So she counted miles instead. Four thousand, one hundred and sixty since she'd come down off the real mountains for good. She kept the tally in the back of a battered route notebook, in pencil, in a cipher of her own devising that would mean nothing to anyone who found it. Which no one would. No one looked at Mara Wren twice.

* * *

The storm broke fully in the evening, and Sember went dark street by street as the power grid surrendered district by district. Ulyana ate alone in the guesthouse kitchen by lamplight—rice, dried fish, tea brewed strong enough to strip paint—and listened to the rain assault the roof like it held a grudge. Marek, the cook who worked the dinner shift at the hotel two doors down, had waved at her through his steam-fogged window on her way home. She had raised a hand. That was the whole of their friendship, and it suited them both.

At half past nine, someone knocked.

Not knocked, precisely. Something struck her door—a slow, dragging percussion, knuckles and then the flat of a palm, then nothing, then again. Ulyana set down her cup. Her room was at the end of the ground-floor corridor, the cheapest and dampest, chosen because it had a back door onto the alley and a clear line to the harbor road. Six years of habits, each one a small brick in the invisible life.

She opened the door on the chain first. Old habit again.

A man stood—or leaned—in the doorway, and the lamplight behind him caught him in pieces: a face seamed like old rope, a beard gone entirely white at the chin, a canvas coat so saturated it had stopped being clothing and become a kind of armor of water. He was sixty if he was a day, and he was going down. Even as she watched, his knees buckled and he caught himself against the doorframe with a sound that was half gasp, half apology.

"I'm looking—" he said, and coughed, deep and wet. "Mara Wren. Guide. They said at the ferry office."

"You found her." Ulyana did not open the chain. "Come back when the rain stops."

"It won't stop." He said it with terrible certainty, the way men say things they've decided are true. "It won't stop till August. I don't have August."

Something in his voice snagged on something in her, an old hook set long ago in waters she didn't fish anymore. She unchained the door. It was, she told herself later, simple triage—he was hypothermic, or close to it, and letting a man die in her doorway was bad for business even by the relaxed moral standards of Sember—but the truth was simpler and less flattering. She had spent six years being no one to everyone, and here was someone who had crossed a flooded country to be somebody to her.

She got him inside by main strength. He weighed nothing, which frightened her more than his collapse had; there was a kind of terrible efficiency in a body that had burned through everything it had left. She hauled him onto her cot, stripped the sodden coat off him—it took effort, the wet canvas clinging like a second skin—and got her emergency blanket around his shoulders. His hands were blue to the wrist. His lips were gray.

"Whisky," he rasped. "In the coat. Left pocket."

There was a flask, dented brass, and also in the left pocket a tube—aluminum, sealed at both ends, wrapped in waxed cloth and rubberized tape. Waterproofed the way prospectors waterproofed things, which was to say obsessively, the way other men prayed. She took the flask and left the tube.

He drank a swallow of whisky, coughed for a full minute, and surfaced looking marginally more alive. Up close, his eyes were extraordinary—pale gray, almost colorless, with the flat calm of high-altitude light in them. Eyes that had looked at things above the treeline for a very long time.

"Odile Voss," he said. "You'll want the name later."

"I don't want anything later."

"Everyone wants something later." He almost smiled. "That's the whole trouble with people."

* * *

By midnight the storm had settled into its long rhythm, and Odile Voss had settled into hers—the shallow, rattling breathing of a man running on fumes. Ulyana sat across the small room with her lamp turned low, watching him, and doing arithmetic she didn't like.

He had asked for her by name. Not *Mara Wren*, guide of gentle routes—she was fairly sure of it, though she'd need him coherent to confirm. There had been a weight on the way he'd said it, a specificity. And he had come in this weather, at this hour, dying by inches, which meant whatever he wanted was worth more to him than caution. People who valued caution did not cross the monsoon to find a woman who sold handrail hikes to tourists.

She should wake him, ask her questions, put him out into the storm's edge and go back to bed. That was the protocol of the invisible life. You did not take in strangers who knew your name. You did not let cracks form.

But she looked at him—at the rope-seamed face, at the hands folded over the aluminum tube resting on his chest like a child holds a toy it refuses to surrender even in sleep—and she found that her feet had no intention of moving.

It had been years since anyone had needed her for anything real. The tourists needed her the way they needed a good restaurant recommendation. The mountain needed nothing; the mountain had never needed anything, that was the whole point of it, and it was why she loved it and why it had ruined her. But this man had come out of the weather carrying something he thought she was the only person alive to receive, and the loneliness she had built her life around, carefully, brick by brick, developed its first crack—and through the crack came a sound she had almost forgotten: the feeling of mattering to a single human being, which is either the sweetest or the most dangerous sensation there is, depending on how much you value your solitude.

Toward dawn, he woke.

His eyes found her immediately, focused, lucid, as though he'd merely been waiting for her to stop pretending to read her route notebook. The lamp flame bent in some draft from the ill-fitted window. Rain drummed on, patient, eternal.

"Mara Wren," he said slowly, tasting the name. Then, with the mild precision of a man setting a card face-up on a table: "That's not who you are."

Ulyana went very still. Outside, the storm roared through the empty streets of Sember, and somewhere a shutter banged and banged against its frame like a heart.

"Underhill," Odile Voss said. "Ulyana Underhill. Daughter of Tomas Underhill, survey team lead, Kharsu Range, 1998. Blacklisted in two countries. The best high-route guide I ever saw work, and I saw you work, twenty-two years ago, before you learned to hide." He drew a breath that whistled faintly. "I've been looking for you for eleven months."

Her mouth was dry. "Then you've wasted a trip."

"I'm dying, Miss Underhill. I don't have time to waste, and neither, it turns out, do you." His hand moved to the tube on his chest—not lifting it, just resting two fingers on it, the way a man touches a compass he trusts. "I have a map. A valley in the Kharsu interior that isn't on any survey, any concession filing, any chart. Your father died looking for it. I nearly did too, twice." The pale eyes held hers, and there was no mercy in them, only necessity. "And I need you to take me home before the season's permits run out."
