## 2. A Map for a Promise

The stranger did not die on Ulyana's doorstep, though he tried hard enough at it.

She found him at dawn, when the rain had thinned to a gray whisper and the drains of Sember ran brown with the mountain's runoff. He was folded against her door like a dropped coat—old canvas jacket gone black with water, a beard the color of wet ash, hands so cracked they looked like riverbeds in drought. One fist was locked around a metal tube the way a drowning man locks around driftwood.

Ulyana had spent eleven years learning not to open doors. She stood over him now with her hand on the latch, weighing the arithmetic she'd built her life on: strangers were liabilities, liabilities were questions, questions were names. In Sember she was Mara Vell, a competent mid-tier guide who took wealthy tourists up sanitized switchbacks to photograph waterfalls. Nobody asked Mara Vell about 1998. Nobody asked Mara Vell anything.

But the man's lips were the blue of glacier ice, and his chest was moving in that shallow, stuttering way that Tenzin—the doctor she hadn't met yet, the doctor she wouldn't meet for another week—would later teach her to call agonal breathing's less dramatic cousin.

"Damn it," she said to the rain, and opened the door.

* * *

He came back to himself by afternoon, propped in her one good chair under three blankets, drinking broth she'd salted too heavily because her hands weren't steady. The storm had returned in full voice outside, hammering the tin roof like it wanted entry.

"Odile Voss," he said, when he could manage words. His voice was gravel dragged through tar. "You're the guide. The one who takes the bankers up the goat paths."

"Mara Vell."

Something moved behind his eyes—not quite a smile. "Sure," he said. "Mara Vell."

The name landed wrong, the way a foothold lands wrong: solid-looking until you put your weight on it. She kept her face still. Twenty years of practice. "You collapsed on my step. You should be in a hospital."

"Hospitals keep records." He said it mildly, the way other men discussed weather, and she understood two things at once: he knew what she was, and he was the same species as her. A creature living in the gaps between registries.

"I'm dying," Odile Voss continued, conversationally. "Lungs. Forty years of Kharsu dust and kerosene fumes. The doctor gave me a season, maybe two if I'm stubborn." He coughed then, a long tearing sound, and when it passed he sat very still with his eyes closed, conserving something. When he opened them again they were clear and terribly patient. "I need you to take an expedition into the interior before the concession window closes. Six weeks. Maybe seven if the monsoon breaks early."

"No."

"You haven't heard the offer."

"There's no offer worth the interior in monsoon season." She took his bowl. "The passes flood. The slopes let go. Every year someone dies proving it."

"Every year someone dies staying home, too. They just do it slower." He watched her with those patient eyes. "I've been asking around about you for three months, Ms. Vell. You're the only guide in Sember who's turned down Halloran money twice. You know what that tells me? It tells me you know exactly what Halloran Concessions does to the places they strip-mine. And it tells me you'd rather be poor than complicit. Both of those things are rare enough to be valuable."

"Both of those things are none of your business."

"The second one became my business when you carried me inside." He lifted the metal tube from where it leaned against the chair leg and set it across his knees. Water still beaded on its casing. "Do you know what this is?"

"A map case."

"It's a map. Singular. Of a valley in the central Kharsu that does not appear on any survey, any satellite pass, any government registry going back sixty years. I found it in 1979. I've spent forty years making sure no one else found it again." His breath whistled. "And now I'm out of time, and Halloran's permit window opens in six weeks, and if their survey crews get into that valley before I'm dead, everything I spent a life protecting gets turned into tailings and a company town."

"So burn it."

"I tried that." For the first time, something cracked in his voice. "Forty years, and I couldn't do it. Isn't that a stupid thing? All that weight for nothing, and I couldn't make my hands let go." He looked down at the tube. "So instead I'm going to give it away. Which is worse, probably."

"To whom?"

"To whoever swears the vow."

* * *

He named the terms that evening, while the storm worked itself into a fury and the lamp guttered and steadied between gusts.

Three conditions. First: she would assemble a crew and lead them into the interior before the permits lapsed—his permits, old ones, grandfathered and half-forgotten, valid for one final season. Second: every person who went in would come out alive. Not most. Not all but one. *Everyone.* "I don't care what the mountain asks," he said. "You pay it whatever it wants except that."

"And third?"

He was quiet a long moment. Rain filled the silence, drumming, insistent.

"What you find there—you tell no one. Not the government. Not the newspapers. Not the crew, beyond what they need to walk the route. You go in, you see it, and you carry it out sealed behind your teeth until you die. Can you do that? Because if you can't, say so now, and I'll find some other ruined guide to ruin further."

Ulyana stood at her narrow window with her back to him, watching water sheet off the eaves into the dark. She thought about how carefully she had dismantled herself—how she'd sanded off her name, her history, her father, until what remained was smooth and blank and safe as river stone. Eleven years of miles. Miles measured instead of years, because years accumulated and miles just passed.

"My father," she heard herself say, "was a surveyor. Elias Underhill. He went into the Kharsu in '98 with a mapping team and never came out." She didn't turn around. "His last letter mentioned a valley. He wrote that the tributary drainage didn't match the official charts—that there was more country up there than the maps admitted. He sounded—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "He sounded happy. Happier than I ever heard him. Six weeks later he was a missing-persons file."

Behind her, Odile Voss was silent.

"Is it the same valley?" she asked. "Tell me honestly or I walk you to the door right now."

"Yes," he said simply. "It is."

The word went through her like a piton driven into old ice. Twenty years of not-knowing, of a mother who died waiting, of a girl who grew up measuring distance because distance was the only thing that stayed honest—and here it was, delivered to her door in a waterproof tube by a dying man who smelled of kerosene and endings.

She should refuse. Every instinct she'd honed since 1998 said refuse: the interior in monsoon season was suicide dressed as salvage; the vow was a cage; the valley was a wound she'd spent two decades learning to walk around. Say no. Burn the map herself. Go back to being Mara Vell, back to the bankers and the waterfalls and the beautiful blank miles.

Instead she heard herself say: "If anyone dies, the expedition ends. We turn around and bring everyone home, map or no map. That's not negotiable either."

"That's the whole point of the vow."

"And I choose the crew. No Halloran men, no fixers, nobody who answers to a permit office. People who've got nowhere else to be."

"They usually climb best anyway," Odile said. "People with nowhere else to be."

She turned. He was watching her with an expression she couldn't name—relief, maybe, or grief, or the particular peace of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long his spine had grown around it. He held out the tube.

"Six weeks," he said. "Don't waste them."

Ulyana took the map case. It was lighter than she expected, and colder, and it fit in her hands like something that had been waiting for them.

She said yes before she could talk herself out of it—which, she would understand much later, was the only way she was ever going to say yes to anything that mattered.

* * *

Odile slept in her chair that night, breathing in shallow pulls, and near dawn she heard him murmur coordinates in his sleep—numbers she wrote down without deciding to, numbers that matched nothing public, numbers that matched her father's handwriting in a letter she'd read ten thousand times.

By morning he was strong enough to sit upright, and he studied her across the cold stove with those patient eyes.

"You'll need a doctor," he said. "Someone who can handle altitude and trauma and doesn't ask too many questions. There's a man at the mission hospital—Tenzin. Good hands, bad reputation, drinks. He owes me."

"And the rest?"

"That's your problem. I'm buying the route, not the crew." He coughed, waved off her look. "One more thing. Halloran knows the window's closing. They'll have survey teams working the approaches already, and they don't hire polite people. Whatever route you take, assume you're being watched from the moment you leave Sember."

"Wonderful."

"Adventure always is." He almost smiled. Then the smile faded, and he looked at her with sudden, terrible directness. "Your father, Ms. Vell. When you find out what happened to him—and you will—I need you to remember the third condition. What's in that valley stays in that valley. Even from you. Especially from you."

"He might be alive," she said quietly. "Twenty years. People have survived longer."

Odile Voss looked at her for a long time, and whatever he saw in her face, he chose mercy over truth, or truth over mercy—she never learned which.

"Get your crew," was all he said. "The rain won't wait, and neither will I."

Outside, the monsoon hammered the town like a fist on a door, and somewhere upriver, in the drowned green dark of the Kharsu foothills, a valley that wasn't on any map waited with her father's bones—or his heartbeat—in its keeping.

Ulyana weighed the tube in her hands one last time, then began, in the only language she trusted, to count the miles.
