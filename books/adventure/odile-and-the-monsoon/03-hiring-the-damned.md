## 3. Hiring the Damned

Ulyana found the doctor in the back room of the Chandra Rest, where he'd been living for eleven days on credit and refusing to answer to his own name.

The landlady had told her: *Tenzin. Used to be somebody. Expedition medicine, high altitude, the real thing. Now he drinks and patches dockworkers for beer money.* She'd said it the way people in Sember said everything—loudly, and with a shrug that meant *not my problem.*

The room smelled of antiseptic gone sour and rice wine gone worse. Tenzin sat on a cot too small for him, a man built broad once and going soft at the seams now, stitching a fisherman's gashed forearm by lamplight with hands that were still, despite everything, impossibly steady.

"You're drunk," Ulyana said from the doorway.

"I'm working." He didn't look up. "Drunk is later."

"Odile Voss sent me."

That got his head up. The fisherman took the opportunity to yelp, and Tenzin muttered an apology in three languages and tied off the stitch. When the man had shuffled out clutching his arm like a rescued bird, Tenzin wiped his hands on his trousers and looked at her properly. His eyes were bloodshot but quick behind them, the way a locked house is dark but not empty.

"Nobody's said that name out loud in two years," he said. "Not since the Kanchen side. Not since—" He stopped. "What do you want?"

"An expedition. Kharsu interior. Six weeks, maybe eight. High passes, bad weather, no evacuation possible past the second ridge."

"No."

"I haven't offered terms yet."

"It doesn't matter." He stood, and the room tilted around him, and he pretended it didn't. "I lost my license. Did Odile tell you that? Two countries' worth of license. There was a climber on the Lhotse face, whiteout, cerebral edema, and I made a call with a dexamethasone dose that the inquiry called reckless and I call the only reason the man's children have a father. They disagreed. They won. That's the whole story. You don't hire a story like mine."

Ulyana leaned against the doorframe and let the rain speak for a moment against the tin roof. She knew this speech. She'd given versions of it herself, in three towns under three names.

"I was blacklisted in Nepal," she said. "And before that, in Sikkim. And before that there was a thing in Karakoram that doesn't have a word yet, only inquiries." She watched him go still. "Odile Voss is dying. He has a map. He wants one last expedition led before the concession window closes, and he asked me to find people who know what it costs to be thrown away, because those are the only ones who'll keep a secret worth keeping."

Tenzin sat back down slowly, as if the floor had become negotiable.

"What kind of secret?"

"The kind you swear to before you earn it."

He laughed—a short, rusted sound, like a gate opening after years. "You're insane."

"Probably. Are you in?"

He looked at his hands. The hands of a man who had saved lives and been punished for the saving. Then at the lamp, the cot, the sour room that had been his whole horizon for eleven days.

"When do we leave?"

* * *

Priya came to them, though Ulyana hadn't gone looking—or told herself she hadn't.

The girl appeared at Marek's kitchen door on the third morning, soaked through, a canvas satchel clutched to her chest the way other people clutched infants. Inside it: a wireless set older than she was, its casing held together with solder and prayer.

"Marek said," she began, and stopped, and started again. "Marek said you need a radio operator. I'm a radio operator."

"You're fifteen," Ulyana said.

"Fifteen and a half." The girl lifted her chin. Rain dripped off her braid onto Marek's clean floor, and she didn't notice or didn't care. "I've been running frequencies since I was eleven. My uncle taught me in Darjeeling before he—before. I can build an antenna out of fence wire and a bamboo pole. I can read weather off static better than your barometer can. And I'm not going back where I came from, so whatever you're paying, half of it can be deferred, because I just need to be somewhere that isn't my father's house when the monsoon ends."

Marek, stirring a pot big enough to bathe in, said nothing, but his silence had a shape, and the shape was *take the girl.*

Ulyana crouched to the satchel. "May I?"

Priya hesitated, then opened it. The set inside was lovingly maintained—every dial cleaned, every wire re-wrapped, a hand-lettered frequency card taped inside the lid in a child's careful script, updated year over year until the ink changed from round letters to slanted ones.

"Whose handwriting is the oldest?"

"My uncle's." Priya's voice went flat, which was its own kind of loud. "He's dead. My family says it was an accident. My family says a lot of things."

Ulyana closed the satchel gently. She thought of Odile's tube, of maps and inheritances, of the things we carry because the people who gave them to us can't anymore.

"There's a condition," she said. "When we go upriver, you follow my orders exactly. If I say drop the set and run, you drop the set and run. The set can be rebuilt. You can't."

Priya looked at her the way young things look at old lies—hunting for the trap, finding none, distrusting it anyway.

"Deal," she said.

* * *

Tenzin

He'd expected the cook to be the easy one. A debt, a grudge, a debt again—that arithmetic always balanced.

It did not balance.

Marek Dvorak stood six foot four in the doorway of his kitchen with forearms like mooring posts and a face that suggested every expression had been taxed heavily and he'd stopped paying. He listened to Tenzin lay out the expedition—the route, the pay, the dying man's name—and then he put down his ladle with the deliberation of a man setting down a weapon he'd decided not to use.

"Voss," Marek said. "1979. The Talung glacier approach."

"You were there?"

"I was the porter who turned back." He said it without inflection, which somehow made it worse. "Twenty-two years old, frostbitten toes, and Voss carrying a man heavier than himself on his back because I couldn't carry myself. He never mentioned it. Not once, in twenty years of eating at my kitchen. Never let me pay for a meal either, which was his way of mentioning it." Marek picked the ladle back up. "So yes. I owe him. But understand something, doctor—I'm not coming to die on a mountain for sentiment. I'm coming because a debt unpaid is a stone in the boot, and I am too old to walk crooked."

"And if the mountain asks more than cooking?" Tenzin asked. "Past the second ridge, everyone carries. Everyone hauls. Everyone holds a rope."

Marek looked at him for a long moment. Whatever he saw—a drunk, a failure, a fellow discarded thing—he apparently found sufficient.

"Then I hold the rope," he said. "But if anyone complains about the food, I feed them to it."

* * *

Ulyana

They gathered in Marek's kitchen on the last night before the barge, four people and a storm, and Ulyana laid out the rules the way Odile had laid them out to her: the vow, the deadline, the silence.

"Whatever we find stays found," she finished. "No photographs sold, no stories told, no coordinates spoken aloud even among ourselves beyond what the route requires. Odile's terms. Mine now."

Priya frowned. "Then why bring a radio operator? If we're not telling anyone anything?"

"Because if something goes wrong up there, someone down here should know where to start looking. There's a difference between a secret and a grave." Ulyana met each of their eyes in turn. "I need you all to understand what you're agreeing to. This isn't a job. It's a promise, and promises are the one cargo that never gets lighter."

Tenzin raised his tin cup of tea—tea, at his own insistence, and everyone had been polite enough not to comment on the miracle. "To the damned," he said.

"To the discarded," Marek corrected, and poured.

Priya looked at her cup, then at these three strangers who had, in the space of five days, become the closest thing she had to a future, and drank.

Ulyana drank too, and felt the strange vertigo of it—not the tea, but the company. For twenty years she had moved through the world like weather, touching nothing, touched by nothing. Now there were three people whose fates she'd promised to a dying man, and whose fates had, without permission, entangled themselves with hers.

She didn't trust it. Trust was a rope anchored in rock she hadn't inspected. But she felt it anyway, warm and alarming, like sun through storm cloud.

Outside, the monsoon redoubled. Priya's wireless, warming on the table, crackled suddenly with a fragment of signal—weather traffic, routine, the coast guard listing closures. Then, beneath it, another band, brief and businesslike: a survey vessel requesting clearance upriver, Halloran Concessions, permit verification pending.

Ulyana went cold.

"Priya," she said quietly. "Play that again."
