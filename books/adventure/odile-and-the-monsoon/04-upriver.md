## 4. Upriver

The barge was called the *Lakshmi's Patience*, which Priya thought was a joke, because nothing about it suggested patience or luck. It was a flat-bottomed river tub with a rusted crane arm, a hold full of rice sacks that smelled of diesel and damp, and an engine that coughed like a chain-smoker every time the pilot pushed past half throttle. There were four crew bunks in a cabin the size of a closet, a galley where Marek had already claimed territory with his knives, and no rail worth trusting.

Priya stood at the bow anyway, because the bow was the only place on the boat where nobody could stand behind her.

The Kharsu River was brown and swollen, carrying whole trees sideways, and the rain came down in sheets that made the far bank a rumor. Above them the clouds sat so low she could have touched them if she'd been fool enough to climb the mast. The monsoon wasn't weather here. It was a country they were traveling through, one with its own rules and its own moods.

"You'll want your hood up," Ulyana said, coming to stand beside her. "That rain gets inside your bones by evening."

Priya pulled her hood up. She was seventeen, and she had decided—somewhere between the last town and this river—that she would not be treated like a child on this expedition, even if everyone aboard was twice her age. "I've been rained on before."

"Not like this." Ulyana's voice was mild, but Priya had already learned that mildness was her way of being serious. "This is the first week of six months of it. The river will rise another two meters before it crests. The passes will close. Everything we do from now on, we do against the clock."

Priya watched her out of the corner of her eye. Ulyana Underhill—or *Vasquez*, or whatever name she was using this month; Priya had heard Marek mutter something about names and hadn't asked—stood at the rail the way other people knelt in church. Like the water was something she owed attention to. She'd been counting things since Sember: kilometers of river, hours of daylight, sacks of rice, coils of rope. Priya had started keeping her own list just to see what Ulyana saw, and what she saw was: everything, measured, weighed, filed away.

"Where exactly are we going?" Priya asked. "You still haven't said."

"Upriver."

"That's not an answer."

Ulyana smiled without turning her head. "It's the only answer I have right now. Ask me again when we're past Karcham Bridge."

*Past Karcham Bridge.* Priya turned that over. The bridge was three days upriver, and beyond it the maps went thin—the survey charts in the radio office back in Sember showed contour lines fading into blank paper, the cartographer's equivalent of a shrug. Which meant Ulyana knew exactly how far they were going, and exactly where the knowing had to stop.

Priya had run away from an arranged marriage, not from questions. Questions were the one thing she'd never been afraid of.

* * *

On the second day they passed the Halloran barge.

Priya saw it first because she was on the radio, as she was most hours, headphones clamped over her ears, scanning the bands. The monsoon chewed up the frequencies, but voices came through in bursts—a fishing cooperative arguing about fuel prices, a weather advisory, and then, crisp and corporate against all the static:

*"—Halloran Concessions survey team, requesting confirmation of transit clearance through the Kharsu gorge sector, over."*

She sat up straight. Checked the frequency. It was a working band, unencrypted, the kind of channel any boat might drift across. But the voice was wrong for a fisherman—too clean, too bored, the accent of someone who'd learned English in an office tower.

"Halloran," she called into the cabin. "On the band. They're asking for gorge transit clearance."

Everything in the cabin stopped. Tenzin looked up from the suturing kit he was repacking. Marek set down his knife. Ulyana came through the doorway fast, took the headphones from Priya, and listened with her eyes closed, the way she did when she was memorizing something.

The voice repeated its request. A different voice answered, farther away, giving coordinates. Priya watched Ulyana's lips move slightly, repeating numbers to herself.

Then Ulyana handed the headphones back and said, "Change our call sign. We're the *Patience* until further notice—no surnames on the air, no port of origin. If anyone hails us, we're a rice charter running ahead of the flood."

"Do you know them?" Priya asked. "Halloran?"

"I know the type." Ulyana's face did something complicated and then settled into nothing at all. "They buy permits the way other people buy shoes. Whatever window Odile's map is aimed at, they're aiming at it too."

"But how would they even know—"

"They don't need to know anything. They need to know *less than us and move faster*. That's the whole business." She squeezed Priya's shoulder once, brief and hard, and went out into the rain to talk to the pilot about speed.

Priya put the headphones back on. Her heart was going quicker than the situation justified, and she knew why. It wasn't fear. It was the small hot pleasure of being useful—of hearing something first, of having the thing everyone else needed. Back home, in her father's house, she had been the daughter who was spoken *about*, never the person who was spoken *to*. Here, on a rusted barge in the teeth of the monsoon, she was the ears of the expedition.

She liked it more than she wanted to admit. And liking it made her notice things she might otherwise have let go.

Like the fact that Ulyana had memorized Halloran's coordinates without writing them down.

Like the fact that she'd changed their call sign before anyone asked whether Halloran was actually looking for them.

* * *

That night the rain eased to a steady drumming and the sky cleared just enough to show stars, low and smeared behind cloud. They moored against a mudbank where the jungle came down to the water, and Marek produced a curry that tasted like someone had decided flavor was a moral obligation. Tenzin told a story about a patient who'd swallowed a compass, and Marek told one about a goat, and for an hour the barge felt less like a hiding place than a kitchen with a view.

Priya sat apart with the radio log, transcribing the day's traffic, and that was where Ulyana found her. Not approaching—just arriving, settling onto the crate nearby with the ease of someone who'd decided long ago that silence shared was better than conversation forced.

"The coordinates," Priya said, before she could stop herself. "From the Halloran transmission. You repeated them under your breath. Gorge sector, grid references."

"Mm."

"Our route doesn't go near the gorge sector." Priya kept her eyes on the logbook. "I checked. I plotted both. We're going north of it, up the tributary nobody uses, and there's—" She hesitated. This was the moment where admiration curdled or didn't, and she felt it happening in her chest like milk turning. "There's nothing public north of it. No trails on any chart I've seen. No concessions, no surveys, no villages marked since 1998. The map you're following—it matches nothing anyone's published. So either it's wrong, or it's showing something everyone else agreed to forget."

The rain filled the pause. Ulyana didn't look angry. That was somehow worse. She looked tired, and old, and like a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"You plotted my route against public charts," she said.

"Yes."

"Good." Priya blinked. "Good?" "A radio operator who doesn't know where we are can't call for help if we need it. You did the work. That's the job." Ulyana pulled her hood back and let the drizzle hit her gray-streaked hair. "As for the rest—you're right. It matches nothing public. That's not an accident, and it's not forgery. Some places aren't on charts because nobody who mapped them came back to file the paperwork."

"Odile came back."

"Odile came back," Ulyana agreed quietly. "Once. Twenty years after some others didn't."

Priya waited. She was good at waiting; it had been her whole childhood. But Ulyana only looked upriver, into the dark where the water ran fast and the mountains were a blacker black than the sky.

"When we're past Karcham Bridge," Ulyana said, "I'll tell you what I can. Not because you've earned suspicion—I haven't earned your trust yet either. Because you'll be inside it with us, and people should know what they're standing in."

"And until then?"

"Until then, you keep the log, you keep the watch, and if Halloran hails us again, you wake me before you answer. Even if it sounds like me." She stood, and paused. "Especially if it sounds like me."

She went aft toward her bunk, and Priya sat with the radio humming and her own pulse loud in her ears. *Especially if it sounds like me.* What kind of expedition was this, where the leader warned you against her own voice?

She opened the logbook and wrote the day's entries in her careful hand. Then, below them, in smaller letters, she wrote the coordinates she'd memorized off Ulyana's lips—Halloran's grid references—and beside them, the bearing Ulyana had given the pilot on the first morning, the one nobody had questioned because she'd questioned everything else so well.

Two lines of numbers. They pointed at each other like drawn knives.

Whatever valley Odile's map promised, Halloran was aiming down the same throat of the same mountain range, and they were moving faster, and they had money, and Priya had the sudden, cold understanding that the lie Ulyana told so smoothly—to the harbor master, to the pilot, to all of them—wasn't a wall built to keep danger out.

It was a wall built to keep something in.

The radio crackled. Static, then a fragment of voice, gone before she could catch it. Priya reached for the dial, and upstream, past Karcham Bridge, the dark was waiting with whatever it was that twenty years of empty maps had been keeping safe.
