## 5. What the Mountain Keeps

The mountain spoke first, before any of us saw it move.

I was checking Priya's frost-nipped fingers by the fire when the sound came—a deep, grinding exhalation, like the range clearing its throat. Then the white wall came down off the western flank of the pass, a thousand tons of snow unspooling in slow, terrible silence across the trail we'd meant to take at dawn.

Nobody screamed. That was the strange part. We stood there in the blue half-light and watched the Kharsu close its main artery, and Ulyana just nodded, as if the mountain had confirmed an appointment she'd already made.

"Detour," she said.

Marek spat into the snow. "There is no detour. There's the pass, and there's nothing."

"There's the old survey line." She said it lightly, too lightly, the way you set down something fragile so it won't break. "South of here. It'll add four days."

Priya looked up from her radio, which had been spitting static for two days straight. "The 1998 survey?"

Ulyana's back was to her, coiling rope with those precise, economical hands. "Older than that. Come on. We're losing light."

But I saw her face when she turned—saw the thing cross it, quick as a bird's shadow. I've spent thirty years reading faces across sickbeds and base camps, watching men decide whether to tell their wives the truth about a diagnosis. I know what concealment looks like. It looks exactly like Ulyana Underhill—or whatever her name was—turning toward the southern ridgeline like a woman walking back into a room where something bad had happened to her.

* * *

We climbed for two days through country that felt wrong in a way I couldn't name at first. Not dangerous wrong—the Kharsu was dangerous everywhere, indifferent as weather. It was *witnessed* wrong. There were old iron survey stakes rusting in the scree, their numbers still legible. A cairn built in a style none of our maps used. Once, Marek found a tin plate fused to a rock by decades of storms, and turned it over in his big hands without saying anything, and put it back.

Priya noticed everything. That girl missed nothing; she'd been raised to survive by noticing. "These stakes are from a government survey," she said on the second evening. "Pre-digital. My grandfather worked on ones like this."

"Your grandfather sounds like an interesting man," Ulyana said, and changed the subject so smoothly you'd have missed the seam if you weren't looking.

I was looking.

That night we made camp in the lee of a moraine, and the storm came down hard enough that even Marek stopped complaining about the cold and started helping me rig the tarp over Tenzin's-sorry, *my* medical corner—I keep doing that, thinking of myself in the third person, thirty years of writing charts will do it—and we huddled around the stove while the wind tried to peel the mountain down around our ears.

It was Priya who asked. Of course it was. She has the fearless rudeness of the young, the kind that isn't really rudeness at all but hunger.

"You knew this route," she said. "Before tonight. You didn't check the map once today."

Ulyana fed a stick into the stove. The firelight moved on her face like water.

"I guided in the Kharsu for eleven years," she said. "You don't forget terrain."

"That's not what I asked."

Marek grunted from his bedroll—"Let the woman breathe, girl"—but he didn't close his eyes either. None of us did. We were four people who had all been thrown away somewhere else, and we had learned the particular hunger of the discarded: we wanted to know each other's wounds, because knowing them was proof we were allowed to stay.

Ulyana looked at the fire for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had the flat calm of someone reciting something rehearsed—which is not the same as lying. Rehearsal can be a form of grief.

"Two countries blacklisted me," she said. "Nepal first, then here. You've probably guessed it wasn't for incompetence. Incompetent guides don't get blacklisted; they get quietly not-hired."

"Then what?" Priya whispered.

"A client died." A pause, measured out like medicine. "A rich man's son. I told him the route was beyond the season's margin. He paid double to override my judgment, and I took the money, and the mountain took him. Both countries agreed the guide bears responsibility. So."

The wind screamed down the col. Nobody moved.

"It's mostly true," she added, and something in my chest went tight, because I knew that construction. *Mostly true* is what patients say when they are protecting someone—usually themselves, sometimes the dead.

"And the rest?" I asked.

She looked at me then, and her eyes in the firelight were the color of old ice, and for one unguarded second I saw not a guide but a daughter. "The rest," she said, "is that I stopped guiding under my own name, and I got very good at counting miles instead of years, and that's all any of you need to know to trust me on this mountain. Do you trust me on this mountain?"

"Yes," said Marek, immediately, gruffly, as if offended by the question.

"Yes," I said, slower, meaning it in a way she probably didn't want examined.

Priya hesitated. Then: "Yes. But you'll tell us the rest someday."

"Someday," Ulyana said, "the mountain might tell you first."

* * *

I didn't sleep. Old habit—on expedition I sleep in fragments, surfacing every hour to listen to the group's breathing like a doctor counting pulses. Around two in the morning I surfaced and heard Ulyana awake, sitting against the moraine with her map case open on her knees, though there was no light to read by.

I crawled over. She didn't startle. I think part of her had been waiting for company.

"Your client who died," I said quietly. "What was his name?"

"Why?"

"Because you never said it. People say the names of their dead. It keeps them human. You gave me a case file instead."

For a long moment I thought she wouldn't answer. The storm filled the silence, and beneath it, faintly, the mountain groaned—settling snow, shifting weight, the sound of a landscape holding secrets under pressure.

"He doesn't matter," she said finally. "He's the story I give people so they stop asking. The truth is longer and it isn't mine alone to tell." Her hand rested flat on the map case, protective, unconscious. "My father surveyed this range. Nineteen ninety-eight. He walked into these mountains with six men and a concession contract, and none of them came back. They found two bodies. They found his last letter. And then both governments closed the file, sealed the valley approaches, and told everyone the Kharsu had simply eaten them, which mountains do."

I kept very still. "And you think—"

"I think the mountain keeps things," she said. "And I think tomorrow we walk into the place where it's keeping mine." She rolled the map case closed, and her voice went back to its flat professional calm, the door shutting. "Get some sleep, Doctor. Four days of climbing ahead."

I lay back down and stared at the tarp ceiling, listening to the storm, and understood two things with clinical certainty.

First: the woman had lied to us tonight—not about the dead client, perhaps, but about the shape of herself. She had handed us a confession the way you hand a child a decoy, and we had taken it, and trusted her more for it, and she knew we would. Confession as currency. I'd seen it work on wards for decades.

Second: I didn't care. That was the alarming part. Thirty years of exactitude, of charting every symptom, and here I was willing to prescribe trust on incomplete information—because I had watched her check Priya's frostbite twice a day without being asked, watched her give Marek the sheltered side of the tent, watched her count us at every rest stop like a woman counting something she was terrified of losing.

Bring everyone home, Odile Voss had made her swear. I was beginning to suspect the vow weighed on her heavier than the pack.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless. The storm had spent itself, and the southern ridge stood exposed before us: a long white shoulder studded with the rusted bones of the old survey line, stakes marching upward into cloud like a procession going somewhere it never arrived.

Marek cooked porridge in silence and handed out bowls. Priya keyed the radio one last time, got nothing but static, and shut it off with a small decisive click, choosing us over the voices behind her.

Ulyana stood facing the ridge, pack on, ice axe in hand, and I watched her take one breath that shook slightly at the bottom of it—the breath of a person standing at the edge of a room they left twenty years ago.

"All right," she said. "Single file. Stay between the stakes. If I say drop, you drop."

We started up. And a quarter mile along the traverse, Priya—who had drifted to the rear beside me—tugged my sleeve and pointed with her chin at the snow ahead of us, just off the line of stakes.

Footprints. Old ones, half-filled with fresh fall, but unmistakable: boot prints, modern gait, a whole line of them curving up toward the ridge.

Someone had come this way recently. Someone walking exactly where we were walking.

Ulyana had seen them too. She hadn't said a word. She had only quickened her pace, almost imperceptibly, toward whatever the mountain was keeping.
