## 7. Permits and Perfidy

Marek smelled them before he saw them—diesel and starch, the scent of men who ironed their shirts for mountain work. He was hauling water from the cistern below the terraces, a chore he'd taken on without being asked, because a kitchen was a kitchen whether it had walls or not, and the valley's cooks had grudgingly accepted him at their fires after he'd made them his grandmother's plum dumplings with their bitter high-altitude greens. Three days in the valley and his hands had already stopped smelling of barge fuel. That was the trouble with places like this. They got into you.

He heard the helicopter first, a wasp-drone threading up the gorge, and set down the water buckets with the deliberate care of a man who had learned, in kitchens from Gdańsk to Kathmandu, that panic and hot oil were a fatal combination.

By the time he reached the stone houses, the machine had landed in the barley field below the third terrace, flattening a week's harvest into a green-brown bruise, and six men in company rain gear were unloading cases with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no by weather, terrain, or law.

Ulyana was already there, standing between the strangers and the elder, Ashok, whose spine was straight as a surveyor's rod. Tenzin had come down from the sick house on a crutch he didn't need, because that was the kind of doctor he was—the kind who showed up. Priya hovered at the edge with the radio, and Marek took his place beside her without thinking about it, the way you take your place at a station during a dinner rush.

The man in front wore a Halloran Concessions windbreaker and the smile of a regional manager about to ruin someone's decade. He had a folder under his arm, waterproof, government-issue yellow.

"Morning," he said. "Name's Davorin Kessler, Halloran Concessions, advance survey team. We're here under provisional permit 44-C, granted by the regional land office in Sember on the fourteenth." He said the numbers the way other men said scripture. "You're camped inside our concession boundary."

"We're not camped," Ashok said. "We live here."

Kessler's smile didn't flicker, which was its own kind of answer. "That's not what the registry says."

* * *

Marek had cooked for mining camps. He knew the species. Kessler was the field version of the men who signed the checks—clean boots, clean conscience, a satellite phone in a padded case that he set on a stone wall like a man setting down a chess clock. Behind him, his crew was already unrolling surveying equipment, driving stakes into the barley with little orange flags, and one of them had a camera drone humming up over the terraces like a curious hornet.

The valley people came out of their houses and stood in doorways. Marek counted them without meaning to—old men, young women, children with the pale eyes and sharp cheekbones he'd been noticing for days and not naming. Descendants of survey teams, Ulyana had said. People who had chosen to disappear. And now a drone was photographing their faces for a file that would live forever in some server in a country none of them had ever seen.

"Those stakes come out," Ulyana said. Her voice was flat, the guide's voice, the one that brooked no argument at altitude. "This field is planted."

"Ma'am, I'd love to stand here all day discussing agronomy with you, but the permit window closes in nineteen days, and my company has invested—"

"Your company invested in a lie." She said it quietly, and Marek saw Kessler register it, the way a man registers a hairline crack in load-bearing ice. "The registry filings for this quadrant are falsified. You know it. Whoever cut your paperwork knows it. And when this gets to a hearing, it's going to come apart in your hands."

Kessler looked at her for a long moment. Then he took out a card and held it out to her. "Then you'll want to be at the registry hearing in Sember. You seem like someone who knows things." His eyes moved over her, cataloguing. "And you are?"

There was a pause. Marek had spent enough years around liars and around honest people to know the difference in a silence. Ulyana's silence had a seam in it.

"Underhill," she said. "Ulyana Underhill. Expedition lead."

"Underhill." Kessler wrote it on his cuff with a fountain pen, an old-fashioned touch that Marek distrusted immediately. "Well, Ms. Underhill. Enjoy the valley while it's still off the books."

* * *

That evening Marek found the papers.

It wasn't snooping, or it wasn't only snooping. Kessler's crew had camped at the valley mouth—Halloran's men didn't leave when the meeting ended, they simply moved two kilometers downriver and set up orange tents, and their supply porter, a wiry kid named Bhim who Marek had fed on the barge and who owed him the way hungry people owe cooks, came up after dark with a bundle he'd been told to burn at the next village.

"Kessler said burn them," Bhim said. "But there's a stove right there and you're always saying waste is a sin."

The bundle was misfiled paperwork, mostly—carbon copies of permits, supply manifests, a Halloran internal memo about "the Voss claim." Marek fed the true junk into the fire and read the rest by its light, and that was where he found it: a personnel annex, the kind companies attach to concession applications to list their own experts. A page of names. And there, third from the bottom, a photograph ten years old and a name that was not Underhill.

*Ulyana Wierzbicka. Guide, Kharsu and Karakoram routes. License revoked, Republic of Georgia, following the Dvali expedition fatalities. Blacklisted, Pakistan, 2011, cause undisclosed. Subject is known to falsify route documentation. Do not contract.*

Marek sat with the page for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere up the terraces, Priya was teaching a valley kid Morse code, and he could hear the little tap-tap-tap of it, patient as rain.

He thought about the Dvali expedition. Everyone in the trade had heard the story, the way everyone had heard about the Donner Party or the Dyatlov Pass—a story you told to make yourself feel smarter than the dead. Three clients lost in a whiteout on the Dvali glacier. The guide who walked out alive. The inquiries that stripped her licenses in two countries. Marek had always assumed the guide had cut and run. It was what the story was built to make you assume.

But he'd spent eleven days watching Ulyana Underhill. He'd watched her count heads twice at every rest stop, count them the way a man counts money he's afraid of losing. He'd watched her sleep two hours a night and spend the third hour checking the rope coils. He'd watched her walk the retreat route from the failed bridge three times in the dark to make sure it would hold for Tenzin's stretcher, and he'd watched her carry the stretcher herself, the heaviest share, and give the lightest share to the youngest, and lie about why.

*Known to falsify route documentation.* Marek turned that over. He'd watched her lie about their route to the Halloran survey crew on the river, smooth as butter on a hot pan. He'd assumed it was tactics. Now the paper said it was character.

Except. Except that the route lie had kept Halloran off their backs for four days. Except that every lie she'd told since he'd known her had been a wall built around someone else—the valley, the crew, the promise to a dying woman. He thought about the difference between a man who lies to open doors and a man who lies to close them. He had been in kitchens with both kinds. He had been, in a bad decade he didn't talk about, both kinds.

The page was evidence. That was the thing that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone. If Halloran was attaching her real name to their filing, they already knew who she was, which meant Kessler's little cuff-note was theater, which meant they were building a case, and the case would be: *the expedition that found this valley was led by a disgraced guide with a falsified identity, therefore nothing she says can be trusted, therefore the valley is ours.* They would feed her to the hearing and the valley with her.

He could hand the page to Ulyana tonight. That was the honest thing. That was what a man did with the truth—he served it, hot, and let the chips fall.

He looked up the terraces at the tap-tap-tap of the Morse lesson. He looked at the smoke rising from the kitchen where the valley cooks were keeping his dumplings warm without being asked, which was as close to love as Marek had gotten from a workplace in thirty years.

Then he fed the page to the fire, page by page, and held it under with a stick until the photograph was ash and the name was ash and the word *blacklisted* was ash, and he sat there until Bhim came back and asked if the burning was done, and Marek said, "Done. Tell them the bundle was all junk."

It was the first time in his life he had ever destroyed a document, and he was surprised to find it felt less like a sin and more like seasoning—something you added at the end, when you knew what the dish was supposed to be.

* * *

He didn't sleep well, which was normal, but he slept badly for new reasons. In the gray before dawn he went to find Ulyana. She was at the ridge overlook, doing what she did every morning—counting. Crew, supplies, weather. He handed her the tea he'd brought and stood beside her looking down at the orange tents at the valley mouth, small and wrong, like an infection.

"They're not leaving," he said.

"No."

"And the hearing's in Sember in what, twelve days?"

"Eleven. The permit window closes the day after." She drank her tea. "Kessler's filing will go through unless someone stands up in that room and says the registry documents are forged. And to say it with any weight, they have to be someone Halloran can't dismiss. Not a valley farmer. Not a teenage girl. Not—" she almost smiled, "—a cook."

"A cook has seen things," Marek said.

"I know you have. That's why I'm telling you this, Marek, and not the others." She turned to face him. "If I go to that hearing, I go as Underhill, and Underhill is a fiction, and a fiction's testimony is worth nothing. If I go as who I am, they'll take me apart. They'll dig up Dvali. They'll dig up everything. And the valley gets buried in the mud they throw at me." She looked back at the tents. "So I need to know what you'd do. You're the only one here old enough and stubborn enough to give me a straight answer."

Marek thought about the ash in his fire pit. He thought about the photograph with the younger face, the name that wasn't hers, and how he'd chosen already, last night, without letting himself know he was choosing.

"I'd say," he said slowly, "that a man who spends his whole life being one thing can decide, one morning, to be another. And the second thing is the true one. The first was just the costume he wore getting here."

Ulyana looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—relief, or suspicion, or both, because she was the kind of woman who couldn't fully receive a gift without checking it for wires.

"You know something," she said.

"I know plum dumplings need to rest before you boil them," Marek said. "Everything else is gossip."

She almost laughed. Down in the valley, the first light hit the terraces and turned them to hammered gold, and for one moment it was possible to forget the orange tents.

Then Priya came up the trail at a dead run, radio in her fist, and Marek watched Ulyana's face do the thing it did when the mountain changed under her feet.

"Ulyana." Priya was breathing hard, and her eyes were wide. "It's Odile's lawyer. From Sember. She's been trying to reach us for two days." She held out the radio. "The hearing's been moved up. It's not in eleven days."

Marek felt the morning tilt.

"It's in three."
