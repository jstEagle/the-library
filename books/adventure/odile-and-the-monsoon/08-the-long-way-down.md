## 8. The Long Way Down

The storm found us on the third morning of the retreat, the way a debt collector finds you—patiently, and then all at once.

We had left the valley at dawn under a sky the color of wet slate, Dorje the elder walking point with a staff cut from birch and a face that gave away nothing. He had insisted on coming. "The old trails remember me," he'd said, which was the kind of sentence I'd stopped questioning somewhere around the terraced fields. Behind us, the valley folded itself back into cloud like a secret being kept.

I walked second in line because Ulyana put me there—"where I can hear you if you start thinking something stupid," she said—and Marek carried Tenzin's pack along with his own, grumbling that the doctor better not die before he could bill him for it. Tenzin himself walked fourth, favoring the ankle he'd turned two days ago on the scree, waving off every offer of help with the serene stubbornness of a man who patched other people for a living and resented being a patient.

By noon the rain arrived. Not falling so much as arriving sideways, a gray wall of it that erased the ridgeline and turned the trail into a stream. My radio died first—I wrapped it in oilcloth and felt the loss like a pulled tooth. Then the light went, and then the temperature, and we moved through a world reduced to bootprints and rope.

"Storm shelter," Dorje shouted over the wind, pointing down-slope to a black seam in the rockface. "Old survey cache. Half a day."

"Half a day is too long," Ulyana said. She stood with rain sheeting off her hood, studying the terrain the way she studied everything—not the mountain as it was but the mountain as it might become. "There's a bridge over the Kharsu gorge ahead. Cross it before the water rises or we're pinned on this side for days."

"The bridge is forty years old."

"So am I," said Marek.

Dorje looked at her for a long moment, rain running off his beard. "Then we go fast and we don't look down."

* * *

I have crossed bridges my whole life without thinking about them. This one taught me otherwise.

It hung across the gorge in long catenary dips, planks missing like teeth, the cables furred green with moss and shivering in the wind. Below it the river had already swollen to a brown roar, carrying whole trees sideways, and the spray came up at us like breath from something enormous and hungry.

Ulyana crossed first, testing each cable with her weight before committing, calling back the pattern: *left foot high side, right hand low cable, three steps, pause.* Then Dorje. Then me, which surprised me until I understood she wanted me across early—if anyone fell, she wanted the radio operator on the far side with the only working flare gun. Then Marek, hauling both packs, cursing continuously in Polish and English in roughly equal measure.

Tenzin went last.

He was halfway across when the anchor let go.

Not the near anchor—the far one, the one we'd all trusted with our lives ten minutes earlier. I heard it more than saw it: a crack like a rifle shot, swallowed instantly by thunder, and then the whole bridge swung sideways with a lurch that put my stomach in my throat. The cables sagged. The far end dropped six feet, eight, ten, and Tenzin slid down the curve of it toward the gorge wall, grabbing cable, boots skidding off rotten planks.

"TENZIN!" Marek's voice cracked apart on the name.

He caught himself against the rock face, one arm hooked through the cable, and for a moment he just hung there, small and soaked and impossibly far out over the water. Then his grip slipped an inch, and another, and I understood with terrible clarity that his injured ankle had nothing left to give.

Ulyana was already moving. She anchored herself to a boulder with the spare rope, paid it out down the collapsed slope of the bridge, moving with a speed I'd only seen from her once before, on the scree above the pass. "Tenzin! Look at me! Left hand—higher cable—NOW!"

He found it. She reached him, clipped him to her harness, and together they came back up the sagging bridge while the whole structure groaned and swayed and the river below ate everything the mountain fed it. When they finally rolled onto solid rock beside us, Tenzin was white-faced and shaking, and his left leg was wrong—bent below the knee at an angle legs don't bend, even through the rain shell.

"Fracture," he said through his teeth, before anyone asked. He was already, absurdly, diagnosing himself. "Simple, probably. Splint it. Don't be gentle."

Marek splinted it with a tent pole and two bandanas, and Tenzin made exactly one sound, a short hiss through clenched teeth, and then started giving instructions like a man dictating a grocery list. But when we tried to get him upright, his face went gray, and Ulyana crouched in front of him, and I watched something pass between them that neither of them said out loud.

"I can't walk on it," Tenzin admitted. "Not this terrain. Not in this."

"Then we carry you," Marek said simply.

"We can't carry him and move fast," Ulyana said. Her voice was flat, controlled, the guide-voice, and I hated it a little. "And we can't stay here. The gorge floods in weather like this. We're on the wrong side of the water with a broken man and no radio."

That was when the voices came through the rain.

* * *

They came up the trail behind us—four men in matching orange shells, Halloran Concessions stenciled on the backs, led by a compact man with a satellite phone in a waterproof case and the unhurried confidence of someone whose employer owns the weather report. Varga. I remembered him from the barge dock, from the way he'd smiled at Ulyana like he was doing arithmetic.

"Well," he said, taking in our little tableau—the splinted leg, the sagging bridge, the elder with his birch staff. "You people look like a shipwreck."

"Enjoying the view?" Marek growled.

Varga ignored him. He crouched by Tenzin with professional sympathy. "That's a bad break, Doctor. And that bridge isn't taking anyone else across today, and the storm's got another thirty-six hours in it, minimum." He tapped the satellite phone. "But we've got comms. We've got a helicopter on standby at the forward camp, ninety minutes out once the cell window opens tomorrow morning. We can have your man in a hospital in Sember by tomorrow night."

Tenzin's eyes closed briefly. Even I felt the pull of it—warmth, light, morphine, plaster.

"One condition," Varga said, standing, brushing off his knees. "The map. Voss's map. You hand it over, and we take all of you out, not just him. Consider it a rescue package."

The rain filled the silence.

I looked at Ulyana. She stood very still, hood back now, rain running freely down her face, and I watched her do the thing she did—that terrible, honest arithmetic. Tenzin's leg. The valley behind us, its stone houses and its children and its elder who had walked three days in the storm to guide us home. The promise she'd made to a dying man: *bring everyone home, tell no one what it finds.*

Both halves of it were standing in front of her, and they could not both survive.

"No," she said.

"Underhill—" Varga began, and stopped, and smiled slowly, and I saw him decide to spend what he knew. "Or should I say Underhill. Yes. Ulyana Underhill. The Khatmandu inquiry rang a bell the moment I saw your route filings. Blacklisted in two countries. Interesting company you keep, Doctor. Interesting woman to trust with your life."

Nobody moved. Marek's fists closed. Tenzin, gray-faced on the ground, said quietly, "Is that true?"

"It's true," Ulyana said, not looking away from Varga. "And it doesn't change my answer."

"You'd leave your friend to rot rather than give up a piece of paper?"

"I'd leave *you* standing in the rain," Ulyana said, "rather than sell the people behind me. The map isn't mine to trade. Neither is he." She knelt, suddenly, and took Tenzin's hand. "Your call, Doctor. I won't spend your blood without asking. If you want the helicopter, say so, and I'll find another way to keep my word."

The rain hammered down. Tenzin looked at her, and then at Varga, and then he laughed—a short, pained, genuinely amused sound.

"She's asking *me*," he told Varga, wonderingly. "Do you see? Nobody's ever asked me anything; they've always just decided." He squeezed Ulyana's hand. "No deal. Carry me down the old trails, you beautiful idiot. I've survived worse doctors than this mountain."

Varga's smile died. "You're making a mistake."

"We make them professionally," Marek said.

Varga signaled his men back down the trail, but at the last moment he turned. "The permits expire in nine days, Underhill. Whatever you're protecting—it won't matter after that. Paper beats promises. It always has."

"Then I'll bring better paper," Ulyana said, and I didn't understand her until later, in Sember, at the hearing, when I finally understood what she meant.

* * *

We carried Tenzin for two days.

Marek took the head and shoulders, Dorje and I took turns at the legs, and Ulyana rigged a litter from the failed bridge itself—cut cable, salvaged planks, woven with birch bark cordage while Dorje watched and nodded like a craftsman grading an apprentice. We moved down the old survey trails, the ones cut in '98, half-swallowed by the forest, and Dorje read them like handwriting. Here a blaze mark on a cedar, there a rusted survey stake. Once he stopped at a cairn no bigger than two fists and stood with his bare head bowed for a full minute, and none of us asked, and later Ulyana told me: some of the men from the lost teams. The valley had gone back for them, years after the world stopped looking.

Tenzin faded in and out, feverish by the second night, and I sat up with him through the worst hours holding a tin cup of willow bark tea while he muttered clinical instructions to no one. At some point he surfaced enough to find my hand.

"Priya. If I'm slow getting out of these mountains—"

"You're not dying. Shut up."

"—the radio frequencies. In my kit. Memorize them, don't write them down." His eyes were bright with fever and completely serious. "Halloran will have someone listening on every public band within a week. Promise me."

"I promise."

"And Priya—" he almost smiled, "—you're better at this than you think. Whoever you were running from, they trained you badly and you fixed yourself. That's rarer than talent."

I didn't say anything. I just held the cup to his mouth and watched the rain come down through the trees, and thought about how three weeks ago I had been nobody's anything, and now there were four people in the world whose pain I couldn't stand to imagine, and one of them was delirious and one of them was carrying him uphill and downhill and uphill again without complaint, and one of them had given up her own name to strangers to keep a promise to dead men and living ones.

On the fifth morning we came down out of the forest onto the river road, and the barge was waiting where Dorje swore it would be, and Tenzin was lifted aboard still feverish but breathing easy, and Marek sat beside him and did not move for the entire ride downriver.

Ulyana stood at the rail the whole way, watching the Kharsu Range recede into cloud. I went and stood next to her.

"They'll file the claim anyway," I said. "Nine days."

"Yes."

"What do we do?"

She was quiet for a long time. Rain dripped off the rail between us, and the river ran brown and fast toward Sember, toward hearings and registries and men with paper.

"We get there first," she said.
