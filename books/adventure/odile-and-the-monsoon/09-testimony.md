## 9. Testimony

The rain had followed them down out of the Kharsu like a debt collector, patient and thorough, and by the time Ulyana reached Sember it had flooded the lower streets and turned the harbor into a brown churning argument between river and sea. She came into the city alone. The others had scattered to hospitals and boarding houses and, in Marek's case, to the back kitchen of a noodle shop where the owner owed him a favor and didn't ask questions. Priya had wanted to come with her. Ulyana had told her no, and for once the girl hadn't argued—she'd only looked at Ulyana the way you look at someone walking onto thin ice, memorizing the route in case she has to come get you.

The boarding house room she'd kept for eight years, paying a season ahead in cash, smelled of mildew and old smoke and the particular loneliness of a place where no one had ever been invited. She sat on the bed with the map tube across her knees and did not open it for a long time. Outside, the monsoon worked at the shutters. Somewhere below, a dog was losing an argument with the weather.

*Never reveal what it finds.*

She had promised a dying woman in a room that smelled of camphor and copper. She had promised a valley full of people who had spent seventy years teaching the mountains to keep their secret. She had promised Tenzin on the rope bridge, screaming over the water, *we don't give them anything, we don't give them a single thing*—and Tenzin had nodded with blood in her teeth and let them carry her down the old survey trails on a litter of cut bamboo while Halloran's men watched from the far bank with their satellite phones and their rescue that was not a rescue.

But promises, Ulyana had learned, are not the same as silence. Silence was a wall. A promise was a door you chose what to carry through.

She took the map out of the tube and unrolled it on the bed one last time. Odile Voss's handwriting crawled across the margins in pencil gone soft with age—elevations, water sources, a note in a language Ulyana didn't recognize that she now suspected was the valley's own. And in the lower corner, in Odile's careful surveyor's hand, the coordinates. The numbers that would put Halloran's drill rigs and road crews and legal machinery at the throat of the only place on earth that had ever answered her family's oldest question.

Her father's letters had described a valley like a man describing a dream he wasn't sure he'd had. *Terraces to the treeline. Stone houses older than the survey. They fed us and asked us to forget them, and God help me, I think I will.* He had not forgotten. He had gone back. That was the wound under the wound—the 1998 survey hadn't simply vanished. He had chosen it, or it had chosen him, and either way he had walked out of Ulyana's life and into those mountains and never walked back, and she had spent twenty years telling herself he'd been taken so she wouldn't have to consider that he'd *stayed*.

She folded the map along its old creases, carefully, the way you handle something that has already survived more than it should have.

Then she went down to the courtyard, into the rain, and fed the coordinate pages into the brazier the landlady used for burning rubbish.

The paper caught reluctantly, the way paper does in wet air. Ulyana stood over it with her hood down, rain running off her chin, and watched Odile Voss's numbers curl and blacken and lift as ash into the dark. It took less than a minute. Twenty years of a father's ghost, a dead woman's last hope, a valley's invisibility—all of it gone to smoke in the time it took the kettle to boil.

What remained was the map itself, blank of coordinates, useless to anyone who didn't already know the way. She would keep that. Some things you keep.

The hearing was in three days.

* * *

She spent those days the way she'd once spent the nights before hard crossings: checking gear that couldn't be checked, rehearsing routes that existed only in her head, sleeping in fragments. Tenzin came by on the second day, her arm in a proper cast now, her color better than it had been on the mountain, and sat on the end of the bed uninvited and looked at Ulyana with the flat clinical gaze she usually reserved for bad X-rays.

"You're going to say your name," Tenzin said. Not a question.

"I'm going to say my name."

"Ulyana Underhill. Blacklisted in two countries. The woman whose testimony can be dismissed before she finishes her first sentence."

"That's the one."

Tenzin was quiet for a while. Rain ticked against the window. "You know what I keep thinking about," she said finally. "On the bridge. When it went. There was a half second where I thought, *this is it, this is how it ends for me*, and do you know what I felt? Not fear. Relief. Because I'd finally found people I didn't want to leave, and I got to stop pretending I didn't care whether I lived." She looked at Ulyana. "You've been doing the pretending longer than any of us. Whatever you're about to do in that room—don't do it as the woman who's been hiding. Do it as the woman who came home."

Ulyana laughed, short and rough. "When did you become the wise one?"

"Since I got carried down a mountain on a bamboo litter by people who owed me nothing. It rearranges a person." She stood, awkward with the cast, and paused at the door. "Marek says if they jail you, he'll bring you food that makes the guards cry. Priya says she'll testify too, and I told her not to, and she said she wasn't asking permission, which—frankly—is your own fault for teaching her that."

When she was gone, Ulyana took out her father's last letter—the real one, not the one she'd shown the crew on the mountain—and read it again by lamplight. *If you ever find this valley, little star, you'll understand why some things are worth more unnamed.* She had been eleven. She had thought he meant gold.

* * *

The land registry hearing was held in the old customs house, a hall built for the empire's paperwork and now hosting the empire's paperwork's ghosts: high windows streaked with rain, a ceiling fan turning with the doomed patience of a wounded bird, rows of wooden benches half-filled with clerks, lawyers, and the professionally bored. At the front, a panel of three—two registry officials and a magistrate with a face like a folded map—sat behind a table heaped with documents in evidence sleeves.

Halloran Concessions had sent four lawyers. They had good suits, good haircuts, and the particular confidence of men who had never once been rained on.

Ulyana sat in the back row through the morning's proceedings and listened to them dismantle a valley without ever naming it. The permits were valid, they said. The survey data was comprehensive. The interior concession block had been properly registered under the provisions of the Act, and the objections of—and here the lead lawyer paused delicately—"unnamed and unverifiable parties" were without standing. There was no evidence of any settlement in the block. No census record. No tax roll. No church, no school, no graves registered. *No people*, the paperwork said, in the language of paperwork.

The absence they had spent seventy years building was being used as the instrument of their destruction.

When the panel called for testimony regarding the interior expedition of the current season, Ulyana stood up.

The walk from the back row to the witness table took eleven steps. She counted them the way she counted everything, out of twenty years of habit, and then—deliberately, for the first time since Sember—she stopped counting.

"State your name for the record," said the clerk.

The hall was quiet. Rain filled the silence against the windows. Somewhere in her chest, the old machinery of hiding—false name, false history, the smooth lies she'd told Priya on the barge, the half-truth she'd told the fire on the mountain—all of it waited to engage, smooth and practiced and safe.

"Ulyana Underhill," she said.

The word went through the room like a crack through ice. She saw it land: the clerk's pen stopping, the lawyers turning in unison, the magistrate's folded face unfolding an inch. *Underhill.* The name that had ended a career in two countries. The name on the incident reports, the inquest findings, the blacklists that lived in the same filing systems as the permits on that table.

"Underhill," the magistrate repeated. "The guide?"

"The guide," Ulyana said. "Blacklisted in this country and in Chandrapore following the 2009 Dzara expedition, in which three clients died in a whiteout I chose not to wait out. The findings were accurate. I was arrogant, I was behind schedule, and I treated weather as an obstacle instead of a sovereign. I have spent the years since guiding tourists on routes so sanitized they barely qualify as mountains." She let that sit. Let them look at her. Let the lawyers begin, visibly, to sharpen their knives. "I am not here to rehabilitate that record. I am here because I am possibly the only person in this room who has read the Halloran interior survey data and knows what's wrong with it."

The lead lawyer was already on his feet. "The panel's indulgence—this witness's credibility—"

"Her credibility," Ulyana said, over him, to the magistrate, "is exactly the point. You don't blacklist a person for incompetence. Incompetence gets you fired. Blacklisting requires that you were good enough to be dangerous. I have spent twenty years being dangerous in the Kharsu Range, and I am telling this panel, under oath, that the Halloran permit documentation for the interior block contains falsified survey dates, fabricated summit-season weather windows, and boundary coordinates drawn from a source the company does not own and has never surveyed."

She watched the lawyers regroup. Watched the magistrate's clerk begin, hurriedly, to pull the permit files. The rain hammered on. She thought of Odile Voss's hands, spotted and steady, pressing the map tube into hers. *Bring them home. Tell no one what you find.*

She could keep that promise. She was about to prove it.

For two hours they worked her. The Halloran lawyers tried first to destroy her—her record, her motives, her association with "persons of unclear legal status," by which they meant Priya, and Ulyana felt the old protective fury rise and said, evenly, "The radio operator was eighteen and better at her job than your entire survey department." They tried to catch her on the route: *Where exactly did your expedition enter the interior block?* And she said, "Through terrain I will not describe," and when they pressed, "I can testify to what your documents falsify. I cannot and will not testify to what the mountains contain. My obligation to the dead who hired me outranks this panel's curiosity, and I'd remind the panel that a witness may decline to disclose information that would expose third parties to harm."

"Third parties," the lawyer said, pouncing. "You admit there are third parties. Persons living within the concession block."

The hall went very still. Ulyana looked at him for a long moment, and found, to her surprise, that she was calm—calm the way the deep water is calm, the way she'd been calm on the rope bridge with the river tearing at Tenzin's litter. Fear had been the thing she'd fled into anonymity to escape. But fear, she understood now, had never been the enemy. The enemy was the belief that safety was worth more than the people who made a life worth keeping.

"I admit," she said, "that the interior block contains exactly what your own falsified surveys were designed to make the panel believe it does not contain. And I am not the only witness to that. Your advance team carried satellite photographs, Mr. Ansel. Photographs of terraced fields. I would ask the panel to subpoena them—before the company has time to lose them."

It was a gamble. She watched it land: the magistrate leaning forward, the second official writing fast, Ansel's composure developing its first hairline fracture. She had no way to know if the photographs existed. But she had watched Halloran's men on the far bank of the river, and she had seen the tablets they carried, and men like that photographed everything. They could not help themselves. Arrogance, she knew from the inside, always documented itself.

The hearing adjourned at dusk with the permit files impounded and a subpoena issued against Halloran's survey records. As the hall emptied, the magistrate paused beside her table.

"Underhill," he said. "You understand your testimony today ends whatever life you had under the other name."

"Yes," Ulyana said.

"And you did it anyway."

She thought of a brazier in a rainy courtyard, and paper turning to ash, and a valley that had fed her father and asked him to forget it, and a crew of discarded people who had carried each other down a mountain because that was what family meant—not blood, not paperwork, but the carrying.

"I did it anyway," she said.

* * *

The summons came a week later, delivered by a runner through flooded streets: the panel had ruled the interior permits provisionally void pending investigation, and Ulyana Underhill was required to remain in Sember for the inquiry. Her guiding licenses—what was left of them—were suspended. She was, in the eyes of the law, a woman with nowhere to be and nowhere to go.

She read the notice twice, standing in the doorway of Marek's new kitchen while he argued with a delivery of fish, and then she did something she had not done in twenty years.

She unpacked.

Not the bag—the life. She hung her father's letter on the wall. She put the blank map, Odile's blank map, in a frame above the bed of the room she was still renting, in the boarding house she was still paying for, in the town she had always meant to leave. Outside, the monsoon settled in for the long season, and for the first time she did not count the days until it ended.

There was a letter on her table that evening. No stamp, no return address—hand-delivered, the paper smelling faintly of woodsmoke and high altitude. The handwriting was old and sure.

*Ulyana Underhill,* it began. *Your father asked us to send this to you when you stopped running. We have been waiting a long time. He kept a journal. It is coming down the mountain with a trusted hand, and it will arrive when the rains ease. Until then: the valley remembers you, and the terraces are planted, and we are not afraid.*

Ulyana sat down slowly on the bed, the letter in both hands, and laughed—a cracked, wet, astonished sound—and outside the rain came on and on, and she let it.
