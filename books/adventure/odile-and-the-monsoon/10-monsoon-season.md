## 10. Monsoon Season

The verdict came on a Tuesday, delivered by a clerk who smelled of ink and mildew and who could not look Ulyana in the eye while he read it aloud.

*Cleared of all charges. Guiding licenses revoked in perpetuity.*

She had expected both halves of it, and still the second landed like a stone dropped into still water—the ripple going out and out through everything she'd built her life around. Twenty years of certifications, endorsements, route registrations, the laminated identity of *Beatrix Whitlock, licensed mountain guide*, gone in a sentence spoken by a bored man in a damp room.

Outside the registry hall, the monsoon was doing what the monsoon did: falling with the patience of something that intended to outlast you. She stood under the eaves and waited for the old reflex to arrive—the itch in her feet, the mental inventory of bus schedules and border crossings, the arithmetic of escape she'd performed so many times it had worn grooves in her like water wears stone.

It didn't come.

She stood there for a long time, rain hammering the awning above her, waiting to become a woman who ran. And what she felt instead was something so unfamiliar she had to name it twice before she believed it: she was tired. Not the good tired of a summit day, not the earned exhaustion of distance covered. She was tired of leaving. Tired of packing light. Tired of being a woman whose entire biography fit in one duffel bag and a false name.

*You can't guide anymore,* said the voice that had kept her alive on ice for two decades. *So what are you?*

She didn't have an answer. But for the first time in twenty years, she found she wanted to stay long enough to find out.

She walked home through the flooded streets—her home now, the rented room above Marek's kitchen with its leaking window and its view of nothing—and she did not check the ferry timetable even once.

* * *

Sember in monsoon season was a town holding its breath underwater. The harbor turned the color of tea left too long; fishing boats knocked together like restless teeth; the whole waterfront smelled of tar and wet rope and the green rot of things growing where they shouldn't. Tourists were gone. The sanitized routes she'd once guided were closed, washed out, irrelevant.

Ulyana discovered that without miles to count, days became strange, shapeless things. She learned their new measure slowly, clumsily, the way you learn a language past the age when languages come easy.

She learned that a morning could be measured in whether the rain let up enough to hang laundry—that it sometimes did, briefly, treacherously, and that Priya's school uniform drying on the line was worth more than any clear horizon she'd ever stood under.

She learned that an afternoon could be measured in the sound of Tenzin's crutch on the stairs—one flight now, unassisted, where three weeks ago he'd needed her shoulder and his teeth clenched against the pain. The doctors said the leg would heal crooked but functional. "Like the rest of me," Tenzin had said, and laughed, actually laughed, the first time she'd heard him do it since the bridge.

She learned that an evening could be measured in the noise rising from the kitchen below—Marek's kitchen, *The Damned Ladle*, opened with the last of Odile's debt money and a hand-painted sign that Ulyana suspected Priya had lettered, because no one else would have added the small defiant mountain range under the name. It filled every night with dockworkers and clerks and the occasional Halloran employee who came to eat and pretended not to know whose cooking they were praising.

And she learned that a night could be measured in how long she stayed awake listening to the rain—not counting it, not tracking it against the schedule of some climb, just listening, the way you listen to a voice you've decided to trust.

* * *

Priya came home from her first week of school soaked to the knees and glowing like a lamp.

"They let me take mathematics *and* radio engineering," she announced, dumping her satchel on the table hard enough to rattle the cups. "There's a coastal station that takes student apprentices. Two years. Maybe I sit my certification before I'm seventeen."

"You'll sit it before you're sixteen," Ulyana said, "if you stop talking and start eating."

Priya grinned and obeyed, and then, mouth full, ruined the moment: "My mother wrote again. Through the temple intermediary." Her grin dimmed to something more complicated. "They've found another match. A shipping family. Older."

Ulyana set down her spoon. "What did you write back?"

"Nothing yet." Priya pushed rice around her plate. "I keep drafting letters in my head. Angry ones. Polite ones. Ones where I explain everything." She looked up. "What do you say to people who loved you and also tried to sell you?"

Ulyana thought about her father, twenty years dead or disappeared, whose last letters she'd carried across two continents like stones in her chest. She thought about Odile Voss, dying alone in a port town, trading a map for a promise because he had nothing else left to give.

"You tell them the truth," she said finally. "Once. Plainly. Then you live your life so loudly that they can't mistake it for a phase." She reached across and took the girl's wrist, brief and firm. "But you don't owe them the valley. You don't owe anyone the valley. Some things we keep."

"Some things we keep," Priya repeated, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch, like a load redistributed.

* * *

Tenzin's letter arrived in September, though he lived four streets away—a fact he acknowledged in the first line with the dry humor that had survived the rope bridge, the storm, and the carrying-down through the old survey trails.

> *Whitlock —*
>
> *I'm told by my physician (an idiot, but a well-credentialed idiot) that I may resume practice in the spring, limited to non-emergency work. I have therefore accepted a post at the mission clinic upriver, which is neither glamorous nor lucrative and is exactly what I want. They have never heard of my drinking, my debts, or my disgrace, and I intend to keep it that way until such time as I earn the right to confess them properly.*
>
> *I think often about the night on the glacier when you told us your half-truth. You should know I've worked out most of the rest of it—I'm a doctor, we notice what people don't say—but I want you to hear this clearly: whatever you didn't tell us, you led us home. Every one of us. That's the only entry in your file I care about.*
>
> *Come visit when the passes open. Bring nothing. You carry too much as it is.*
>
> *— Tenzin*

She read it three times by lamplight, then folded it into the tin box where she kept the things that mattered: her father's last letters, the burnt corner of the map she hadn't been able to destroy completely, the permit stub from the hearing. The box was getting fuller. She noticed that. Once, it had held almost nothing, because almost nothing was all a running woman could afford to keep.

Marek's contribution came not as a letter but as a plate of food set down in front of her one evening, accompanied by a grunt and a single sentence: "Odile would've hated this place. Too many people being happy in it." Then, after a pause, gruffer: "He'd have eaten here every night, though. Sit. You're thin."

From Priya there was no letter at all—just the school report card she slapped onto Ulyana's table with theatrical indifference, top marks in mathematics, a note from the radio instructor reading *most promising student in a decade*, and, tucked inside the folder where she thought Ulyana wouldn't find it until later, a photograph: the four of them on the barge going upriver, taken by some deckhand with a cheap camera. Ulyana in the center, squinting into the sun, looking—for the first time in any photograph of herself she could remember—not like a woman measuring the distance to the next exit.

* * *

The letter from the valley came in October, borne downriver by a trader who claimed not to know what he carried, which was exactly how Elder Sonam would have arranged it.

Ulyana knew the handwriting before she broke the seal. She'd seen it on the survey documents in the community's keeping, on the grave markers of the ones who hadn't survived the choice to disappear. Sonam wrote the way he spoke: carefully, formally, each word placed like a stone in a wall meant to last.

> *Daughter of Underhill,*
>
> *The rains are heavy this year, as they were the year your father stayed with us. He repaired our mill race and argued with our teachers about star navigation and taught our children a song about a mountain in a country none of us will ever see. When he left us, he left this journal in my keeping, with instructions I have honored for twenty years: give it to no one, unless someone comes bearing his name and keeps our secret besides.*
>
> *You have done both. So it is yours now, as he perhaps always intended it to be.*
>
> *Read it slowly. He was a man who wrote as if racing weather, and there is much weather in these pages. But the last entries are calm. I will tell you what they say, in case the ink has faded: he wrote that he had found the thing he'd been searching for, and that it was not a valley at all. It was the understanding that a man can be lost his whole life and found in a single place, if he is brave enough to stop moving.*
>
> *He chose to go back down the mountain. We have never known why. Perhaps the journal will tell you. Perhaps it will only tell you that he loved you, which we already know, because he spoke of you the way our own people speak of the ones they climb toward.*
>
> *The terraces are planted. The children are well. Your name is spoken here kindly.*
>
> *Walk gently,*
> *Sonam*

The journal was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft with age. Ulyana held it in both hands for a long time before she untied the knot.

Her father's handwriting. Not the careful script of the old letters—this was field-hand, hurried, slanting with the slope he must have written against. She turned to the final pages, as Sonam had said she would, unable to help herself, and there it was, dated nine days before the 1998 survey's official disappearance:

*Found them. All of them, living. God forgive me, I understand now why they erased themselves, and I understand what Halloran's people would do with coordinates. I am going back down to report the valley empty. Whatever they do to me for a false report, it will be worth it. If anyone ever reads this—tell Ulyana her father was not lost. He was exactly where he chose to be, doing exactly what he chose to do. Tell her that stopping is not the same as failing. Tell her—*

The entry ended there, mid-sentence, the way lives do.

Ulyana sat in her room above the kitchen while the rain came down and down, and she understood, at last, why her father had gone back down the mountain: because a false report needed a witness willing to be destroyed for it. Because he had made the same promise she had. *Bring everyone home. Tell no one.* He had kept it. It had cost him everything except the part that mattered.

She closed the journal and pressed it flat against her sternum, and outside the monsoon roared on, indifferent and enormous, and for the first time in her life she did not experience the sound as something to be endured, or tracked, or outrun.

It was just the season. It would pass. And she would be here when it did.

* * *

On the last night of October, the rain stopped—not the treacherous pause that fooled you into hanging laundry, but the true cessation, the sky exhaling after months of holding its breath. Ulyana climbed to the roof with the journal and a cup of Marek's terrible coffee and watched the clouds tear open over the harbor.

Below her, the kitchen glowed. Priya's voice rose through the floorboards, arguing cheerfully about antenna theory with a customer. Somewhere Tenzin was packing for the clinic upriver. Somewhere a valley full of people who had chosen erasure and been granted, instead, survival, were planting their winter fields under a clearing sky.

Ulyana opened the journal to the first page and began, at last, at the beginning.

She had spent her whole life measuring in miles—distance covered, distance remaining, distance between herself and everything that could hurt her. But miles were a young woman's currency, and she was done with them. From now on she would measure in seasons: the monsoon and the dry, the planting and the harvest, the passes closing and opening like slow breathing. She would measure in letters answered, in students certified, in meals served, in secrets faithfully kept.

She would measure, she realized, turning her father's first page, in stories finished.

The first line of his journal read: *Day one. Left Sember with six good people and a map I don't fully trust.*

Ulyana smiled, and drank her coffee, and stayed.
