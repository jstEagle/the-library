## 3. A Name I Stopped Saying

The photographs wouldn't stay still.

Jonah had tried everything in the three days since the cave—air-gapped the files onto a drive he kept in a coffee tin behind the flour, printed nothing, told no one. But the images lived in him now, brighter than any screen. He'd close his eyes and see the spiral lintel again, see the baskets stacked like sleeping animals, the wall of markings that ran on for six feet in a script no regional survey had ever recorded. He'd wake at 3 a.m. with his heart going like a tide gauge in a storm and lie there staring at the ceiling of his rented room above the hardware store, listening to the sea work at the headland.

He should have felt triumphant. Instead he felt watched.

The permit filings were the mistake. He knew it even as he'd made them—a coastal survey application, deliberately vague, *stratigraphic assessment, private funding*—but the state system logged coordinates to six decimal places, and his father had spent forty years teaching graduate students that coordinates were confessions. Kenji Ishikawa could read a grid reference the way other men read faces. Jonah had known that when he filed. He'd filed anyway, because the law required it, because some stubborn buried part of him—the part that still signed his name the way his mother had taught him, careful and complete—believed that rules were how you stayed honest.

Now there was a white university van parked outside the Salt Line Diner, and a second vehicle behind it with a trailer full of equipment, and half the town standing in the diner windows pretending to drink coffee while they stared.

Jonah saw the van from the bluff path and stopped walking.

Six years. Six years since the funeral, since the reception where his father had taken three phone calls from colleagues before the casseroles were even covered, since the argument in the driveway that Jonah could still feel in his chest like a hairline fracture—*you were in the building, Dad. She was dying for eleven hours and you were in the building.* And Kenji, gray-faced, holding his car keys like an artifact he couldn't classify: *I was finishing the field notes. Your mother would have understood.* And Jonah, saying the thing he'd never been able to take back: *No. You just hoped she would.*

He hadn't said his father's name aloud since. Not *Dad*, not *Kenji*, not *Dr. Ishikawa*. The name had become a room in the house he walked around.

He stood on the bluff path with the wind pushing at his shoulders, and below him the van's door opened, and there he was.

His father had gone white at the temples and stooped through the shoulders, but he came up the path the same way he'd climbed every dig site of Jonah's childhood—head down, hands loose, reading the ground. He stopped ten feet away. Neither of them moved. Gulls argued over the tideline below, and the sound filled the silence the way water fills a hole.

"You filed a survey permit," Kenji said. No hello. Of course not. Hello was small talk, and small talk was for people who hadn't spent their whole lives avoiding each other.

"State law requires one."

"The coordinates put you within four hundred meters of the Kestrel Point headland." His father's eyes came up then, and Jonah braced himself for hunger—for that look he remembered from digs and conferences, the look that turned every discovery into territory. It was there. It was always there. But underneath it, unexpectedly, was something older and more tired. "Four hundred meters," Kenji repeated. "Do you know how many times I've surveyed that headland? Nineteen eighty-seven. Ninety-four. Two thousand and three. There's nothing there."

"There wasn't," Jonah said. "The storm took the dune."

Something moved across his father's face—too fast to name. Excitement, maybe, or grief for all the storms he'd missed.

"Show me."

"No."

* * *

They ended up at Jonah's table in the back of the Salt Line Diner because Wren Okafor, behind the counter, pointed at the booth with her chin and said, "Sit down before you two scare my customers," in a voice that permitted no archaeology. Coffee arrived without being ordered. Jonah wrapped both hands around his mug and studied the chipped glaze while his father sat across from him like a man visiting a country whose language he'd once spoken fluently.

"Your team," Jonah said. "How many?"

"Eleven. Grad students, two conservators, a surveyor." A pause. "A funder's representative. Marcus Hale. He arrives tomorrow."

"A funder's representative." Jonah let the words sit there, souring. "On a survey."

"Hale's foundation underwrites the department. He wanted to see the site personally." Kenji's jaw tightened; some things hadn't changed, including how much his father hated owing anyone anything. "It's standard now. You'd know that if you'd ever—"

"Don't." Jonah said it quietly, and his father stopped, which was somehow worse than arguing.

Flashback, unbidden: age nine, kneeling in a test pit behind their house in Davis, his father's hands over his, guiding the trowel. *Feel the resistance change? That's compaction. That means someone was here. That means someone mattered enough to leave a floor.* He had loved that. God help him, he had loved it—loved being the small hands inside the great hands, loved the certainty that the past was knowable if you were patient. He had wanted to be exactly this man. That was the wound nobody talked about: not disappointment, but the death of wanting to be him.

"I'm not showing you the cave," Jonah said now. "Not yet. Not until I decide what happens to it."

"It belongs to the record, Jonah. It belongs to everyone."

"Does it?" He looked up. "Who found it?"

His father held his gaze. To his credit—he'd always had that credit, honesty like a bone that set crooked but set—"You did."

"And what does the record do to the people who live here?" Jonah thought of Wren's face when she'd heard about the discovery, the way she'd gone very still, the way she'd said only, *We need to talk,* and hadn't yet. He thought of the stories he'd grown up hearing from the old-timers about places you didn't dig, and how he'd filed them under superstition the way his father had taught him to. "You'll bring eleven people and a foundation man into a site nobody's touched in maybe a thousand years, and in five years there'll be a parking lot and a gift shop with plastic spirals in it. Or in five months the cave takes the tide back and there's nothing left but your papers. Either way, the town that kept it safe gets a footnote."

"That's not how science works—"

"That's exactly how science works. I learned it watching you."

The words landed harder than he'd meant them to. Across the table his father flinched—not at the insult but at something beneath it—and for a moment Jonah saw him clearly: an old man in a field jacket, alone in a diner in a town where nobody knew his name except as the name of the son who'd left him.

Flashback: the hospital corridor, fluorescent light, Jonah seventeen and shaking. His mother's hand had gone cool around midnight and the nurses moved gently and somewhere down the hall his father stood at a window with his phone against his ear, saying *no, hold the section, we can't lose the stratigraphy,* and Jonah had hated him so completely and so cleanly that the hatred had felt like clarity. It had taken him years to learn that hatred that clean is usually grief wearing a mask. He still took it off only rarely.

"I read your thesis," Kenji said suddenly.

Jonah blinked. "What?"

"Two years ago. *Sediment Transport and Site Formation on High-Energy Coastlines.* You cited me twice." A ghost of something crossed his mouth—not quite a smile. "Once correctly."

"I cited you correctly both times."

"The second citation was generous." His father looked down at his coffee. "It was good work, Jonah. Rigorous. Better than mine at that age." He said it like pulling a splinter. "I wrote to you. care of the journal. I don't know if—"

"They forwarded nothing." Jonah hadn't known about any letter. Something in his chest shifted, treacherous, and he pressed it flat. "Why are you really here? Not the cave. Why come yourself? You haven't left the department for fieldwork in a decade. Priya Chen runs your trenches now, everyone says so."

Kenji was quiet long enough that the gulls outside got loud again.

"Because it was you," he said finally. "Because I've spent thirty years looking for this coast's missing chapter, and my son walked out of the ocean with it, and I couldn't ask another man to describe it to me over email." He turned his mug a quarter turn, aligning the handle with the table edge—his lifelong tic, ordering small things because large ones refused order. "And because I'm sixty-eight years old, and I am running out of time to say whatever it is I'm supposed to say to you, and I don't know what that is. So I came to stand in the place where you found it. That's all I have. I know it isn't enough."

Jonah stared at him. The speech was the most words his father had ever spent on feeling, and it should have cracked something open, and instead it mostly made him tired—tired and angry at the tiredness, angry that after six years the best the man could produce was an apology-shaped weather report.

"The team can stage at the grange hall," Jonah said, standing. "Permits are posted at the county office. I'll take you to the headland at low tide tomorrow, the approach only. Nothing more until I say."

"Jonah—"

"That's the offer. Take it or survey blind."

His father nodded slowly, and there was that hunger again, banked but burning, and Jonah recognized it with a lurch because he'd seen the same expression in his own bathroom mirror three days ago, standing over the coffee tin with the drive in his hand.

Outside, the wind had swung north and carried a smell he didn't like—cold iron, pressure, the sea clearing its throat. Wren caught him at the door, pressed a folded paper into his palm, and walked away without a word. He opened it on the step.

One line, in her small square handwriting:

*Your father's funder—Hale. My grandmother warned us about men like him. We need to talk tonight. Bring the stone.*

Jonah looked up. Down the street, a lean man in a city coat was photographing the diner from across the road—photographing it, and then, noticing Jonah, lowering the camera with a smile that had too many teeth in it.

The storm smell thickened. Somewhere beyond the headland, under the sand the sea had given back, the cave waited with its spirals and its silence, and Jonah understood, with the certainty of a man watching a tide chart fail, that the secret had already begun slipping out of his hands.
