## 4. The Crew Assembles

They arrived on a Tuesday, in three vehicles too clean for the gravel lot above the cove, and Jonah watched them unload from the ridge like a man watching a tide he couldn't hold back.

His father came first, of course. Kenji Ishikawa moved down the trail with the economy of someone who had spent forty years making his body irrelevant to his mind, and behind him came the others, one by one, each of them already becoming a problem Jonah would have to solve.

* * *

The grad student's name was Priya Raghunathan, and she was the first one Jonah decided he might not hate.

He found her at dawn the next morning, kneeling at the cave mouth with a brush she hadn't yet used, just looking. Not photographing. Not cataloguing. Looking, the way you look at something you understand is older than your right to be there.

"You're the son," she said, without turning around. She'd heard his boots on the scree; everyone always did.

"I'm the guy who filed the permit."

"That too." She stood, brushing grit from her knees, and studied him with an openness that was almost disarming. She was maybe twenty-six, wire-thin, with ink stains on her fingers and a camera slung across her back like it had grown there. "Dr. Ishikawa talks about you. Did you know that?"

"He talks about the site."

"He talks about both." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "I've been his student for four years. I've read every paper he ever published, including the ones nobody cites anymore. The ones where they called him obsessive." She paused. "The spirals are real, aren't they? You've seen them."

Jonah looked past her, to the dark seam in the headland where the sea had done its work. The tide was low, and the lintel stone caught the early light, its carved spirals throwing small shadows into their own centers.

"They're real," he said.

Priya smiled—not triumph, but relief, like a woman who'd been told the stars were painted on and had finally been allowed to look up. "Then everything he gave up was worth something. That matters to me. I hope it matters to you."

She went back to her knees before he could answer, and Jonah walked away feeling the first crack in a wall he'd spent six years building.

* * *

Marcus Hale arrived last and made sure everyone knew it.

Jonah marked him immediately: the man who stepped off the trail in leather boots that had never met honest mud, who wore a fleece worth more than Jonah's truck, who shook hands with his father like he was conferring a favor. He introduced himself to Jonah twice—once as if they'd never met, once as if they were old friends—and both times his eyes did a slow inventory of the camp, the equipment, the cave mouth, and Jonah himself, weighing each item against some private ledger.

"Jonah Ishikawa," Hale said, the second time, gripping his hand a beat too long. "The discoverer. Well—the *finder*, anyway. There's a difference, and it's going to matter legally, financially, in about six different ways. Have you thought about representation?"

"I thought about telling you to get off my beach."

Hale laughed, warm and easy, a laugh designed for boardrooms. "See, that's exactly the instinct we're going to have to manage. The Meridian Institute has committed significant resources to this project, Jonah. Significant. And what Meridian gets for those resources is narrative control. First publication, media access, naming rights if it comes to that. Your father understands this. It's how the modern world says thank you to people like him."

"People like him."

"Brilliant men who were never good at the other part." Hale said it kindly, which was the cruelest way to say it. "Look—I read your survey notes on the flight out. Preliminary mapping, sediment cores, the photographic record. Impressive work for a solo operator. Very thorough." His eyes flicked up, quick and flat as a bird's. "Almost too thorough, in places. Some of these passages don't match the topography."

"The sea moves things."

"The sea moves things," Hale agreed pleasantly. "We'll see."

He clapped Jonah on the shoulder and moved off toward the tents, and Jonah stood in the wind doing arithmetic of his own: Hale had read the notes closely enough to catch the alterations. Which meant Hale knew there was something to hide. Which meant the deepest chamber—the baskets, the wall of markings, the small carved stone currently wrapped in a sock at the bottom of Jonah's pack—was already, in some sense, no longer his secret alone.

That night he checked the pack twice. The stone was still there, cold through the wool, patient as a held breath.

* * *

Wren Okafor he'd known since childhood, which meant he should have been prepared for her, and wasn't.

She came with the expedition as contracted local guide—the town council had insisted, over his father's mild objection that they didn't need one—and she worked the site for two days saying almost nothing, watching everything. She knew the tides the way Priya knew citations and Hale knew contracts: totally, and for reasons that ran deeper than profession. When she pointed at a stretch of water and said *not yet*, nobody argued, because the water always agreed with her.

But it was the way she watched *him* that set the hair on his neck up. Not the way the others watched him—as the son, the finder, the asset. Wren watched him like a woman reading weather.

On the third evening, she cornered him at the woodpile behind the cook tent, out of sight of the camp, with the directness of someone who had rehearsed nothing because she'd never needed to.

"Your grandmother's name was Elsie," she said.

Jonah kept splitting wood. "What about it?"

"Elsie Ishikawa and my grandmother Ruth used to walk this coast together when they were girls. Before either of them married. Before anyone drew lines about whose family knew what." Wren leaned against the chopping block, arms folded. "Ruth told me stories, Jonah. About a door in the headland that opens when the sea is angry enough. About the old people who lived here before the maps, and why nobody wrote them down."

The axe stopped mid-swing. Jonah set it down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"There are stories," Wren said, "in my community. Old ones. About a place the water keeps. And about families—two of them—who agreed a long time ago that some things stay buried, because buried is how they survive." She tilted her head. "You went in alone. Before the storm even finished. Before anyone. What did you find?"

"What makes you think I went in?"

"Because the sand at the threshold was disturbed in a pattern that doesn't match wind or water, and because you've been walking around for a week like a man carrying something breakable in his chest." She said it without accusation, almost without interest, a fact among facts. "I'm not here for your father, Jonah. I'm here because when your cave got exposed, my phone rang five times in one night. Elders. People who remember. They wanted to know if the door was open, and what was coming through it."

The wind came off the water, cold and smelling of kelp and iron. Down at the camp, lantern light moved between the tents—his father's silhouette bent over a field table, Hale's voice rising and falling in some negotiation, Priya's camera clicking at the dusk.

"My grandmother never said anything," Jonah said quietly.

"No. She wouldn't. That was the agreement." Wren unfolded her arms. "But agreements are between living people, and the people who made this one are mostly gone, and now there's a funded expedition and a man with a ledger and photographs that could put this place in every textbook on the continent by spring." Her eyes held his, steady and dark. "So I'm asking you plainly, one keeper to another: what did you take out of there?"

The question landed like a hand on the stone in his pack. Jonah looked at her for a long moment—at the woman who had grown up beside him sharing a coastline and apparently sharing something else besides—and felt the strange, vertiginous relief of being seen all the way through.

"A carved stone," he said. "Small. Spirals. It was sitting in a niche like it was left there on purpose. Like it was waiting."

Wren exhaled slowly, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch. "Show me."

"Not here."

"Tonight, then. After they sleep." She pushed off the chopping block and turned toward the camp, then stopped. "Jonah. My grandmother's stories say the door doesn't open for strangers. It opens for the coast letting something go—or letting something come home. We need to decide which one this is before Hale's arithmetic decides for us."

She walked back toward the lantern light, and Jonah stood alone with the axe and the wind, understanding that the expedition had just changed shape. It was no longer his father's obsession, or Hale's acquisition, or even his own discovery.

It was two secrets, meeting in the dark, deciding whether to become one.

And below them, unseen, the tide was already beginning its long climb back toward the door in the headland.
