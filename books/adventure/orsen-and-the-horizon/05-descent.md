## 5. Descent

The tide chart said they had four hours. Jonah gave them three and a half, because the sea had never once honored a piece of paper.

He stood at the cave mouth as the expedition assembled behind him on the wet sand—headlamps, dry bags, the electric hum of Marcus Hale's camera rig charging in its case. The storm had reshaped the entrance again overnight; a shelf of collapsed dune now sloped down to the lintel like a ramp built for exactly this purpose, and Jonah tried not to think about how it felt less like luck than invitation.

"Remember," his father was saying to the group, in the lecture voice Jonah had grown up hearing through a closed study door, "we document, we sample, we do not remove. Anything you find stays where it lies until catalogued."

Jonah's hand drifted, without permission, to the side pocket of his pack, where the carved stone rode against his hip like a second heartbeat.

Wren Okafor stood at the edge of the group, checking her own gear with the unhurried economy of someone who had done this a thousand times in her head. When she caught him looking, she didn't smile. She just tapped two fingers against her temple—a small salute, or a warning—and turned back to the water.

* * *

The first passage swallowed them in stages: boots, knees, shoulders, light. Jonah went first, as he'd insisted, and behind him came Priya with the survey instruments, then Kenji, then Hale's cameras on their gimbal arm, floating ahead of the man like a mechanical familiar. Wren took the rear, where she could watch everyone's faces.

The rock here was black and slick, veined with quartz that caught their headlamps and threw it back in fragments. Jonah moved by memory more than sight—left at the split where the wall wore a groove like an old scar, duck under the shelf that hung at collarbone height, count thirty paces along the channel floor before the ceiling lifted. He'd walked this route alone six times since the storm. He'd walked it once more last night, alone, in the dark, without a lamp, just to be sure he could do it blind if he ever had to.

"You've been busy," his father said quietly, falling into step beside him. There was something in his voice Jonah couldn't name—pride, maybe, or its close cousin, grief.

"Somebody had to be," Jonah said, and heard how it landed, and didn't take it back.

Ahead, Hale's camera swung toward a side passage branching off to the right—the one that led, after forty minutes of crawling, to the deepest chamber. To the baskets. To the wall of markings. To everything that mattered.

"Not that way," Jonah said, louder than he needed to. "Unstable. I nearly brought the ceiling down on myself in there two weeks ago. We stick to the main gallery."

Hale lowered the camera, but not all the way. Its lens stayed trained on the dark mouth of the side passage for a long moment, drinking it in, before he turned. "Of course," he said pleasantly. "Safety first." His smile had the warmth of a ledger.

* * *

Wren watched Hale the way her grandmother had taught her to watch the water: not at the surface, but at what moved underneath.

She'd known men like him her whole life without meeting them—men who arrived with clipboards and good intentions and left with everything that wasn't nailed down, and some things that were. Her grandmother used to say the government surveyors of 1961 were polite men too. They'd mapped the coastline, thanked the elders for their time, and published coordinates that took three generations to bury again.

Now the cave was open, and there was nothing anyone could do about that. But what got written down—that was still a fight worth having.

She kept her eyes moving: Hale's hands hovering near Priya's data loggers. Kenji Ishikawa touching the walls with a reverence that looked almost painful, like a man greeting a relative he'd wronged. And Jonah, up front, calling out hazards, setting the pace—setting it slow, deliberately slow, she realized, eating minutes off the tide clock so the team would have to turn back early.

Smart. She filed it away.

They reached the main gallery as the tide reached its lowest ebb, and the room opened around them like a held breath finally released. Headlamps swept across a floor of packed shell and ash, across the low stone hearth ringed with fire-blackened rocks, across the first of the carved niches in the far wall. Priya made a sound that was half gasp, half prayer. Even Hale went quiet—for about nine seconds.

"This is extraordinary," Kenji whispered. He knelt at the hearth and did not touch it. His hands hovered over the stones the way you'd hover over a sleeping child. "Jonah. Do you understand what this is?"

"I understand it's not ours," Jonah said.

His father looked up at that. Something passed between them—too fast, too tangled to be called an argument, gone before it became one.

Wren crouched near the gallery's eastern wall, ostensibly photographing tool marks, actually watching the shadows. From here she could see the seam in the rockface that Jonah's headlamp kept conveniently sweeping past without pausing—the hairline crack, almost invisible, that she knew from stories led deeper. The hidden way. The place her grandmother called *the room behind the world*.

Jonah hadn't told them. Good.

But he'd have to tell *someone*, eventually, if the worst happened—if the tide came wrong, if the main passage flooded, if people needed saving and the only road out ran through the secret. She watched him work, methodical and guarded, and made her decision the way her grandmother would have: quickly, and with her whole heart.

She drifted over to him while the others catalogued the hearth. "Nice geology," she murmured, nodding toward the eastern wall, her voice pitched under the ambient clatter of equipment. "That fracture line. Must go somewhere interesting."

Jonah's headlamp snapped to her face. For one full second neither of them moved.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"No," Wren agreed mildly. "You wouldn't." She let the silence sit between them, then touched his elbow, brief and deliberate. "My grandmother used to say the coast keeps what matters out of sight until it finds the right hands. I'm just saying—" she glanced across the gallery at Hale, whose camera was creeping, inch by inch, toward the eastern wall, "—some rooms are better left off the map. Whatever your reasons are, they're good enough for me."

Then she walked away, leaving him standing in the lamplight with his secrets suddenly heavier and somehow lighter at the same time.

* * *

Jonah worked the next hour in a state of controlled double vision. Outwardly: the diligent site lead, dictating measurements, flagging samples, keeping the team clustered in the main gallery. Inwardly: a map, redrawn every time Hale's camera drifted east, a constant arithmetic of sightlines and angles.

Twice he redirected the lens with invented hazards. Once he physically stepped between the camera and the seam, complaining about a draft. Hale accepted each deflection with the same pleasant blankness, and each acceptance frightened Jonah more than an argument would have. Men like Hale didn't push. They waited, and wrote things down.

It was during the sample collection that Wren found her moment. She appeared beside him at the gallery's western edge, both of them ostensibly examining a midden deposit, close enough that their conversation was private by geometry.

"The tide turns in forty minutes," she said. "Maybe less. My grandmother's charts disagree with yours."

"By how much?"

"By enough." She met his eyes. In the lamplight her gaze was steady and dark and completely unafraid. "When it happens—if it happens—you'll need to move these people fast, and there's exactly one way out that doesn't flood. You know that. I know that." A pause. "Nobody else needs to know that yet."

Jonah felt the ground shift under him, though the rock never moved. "You'd trust me with that?"

"I'd trust my grandmother's judgment of you," Wren said. "Which is strange, because she died before you ever dug your first hole out here. But she told me about a boy who'd come someday. Said he'd find the door and hate himself for opening it, and that he'd know which things to keep shut." She shouldered her pack. "I'm still deciding if she meant you. But I'm leaning yes."

Across the gallery, Hale's camera flashed once—a calibration burst, he'd claim later—and the light swept the eastern wall, catching the seam for half a heartbeat before Jonah's body blocked it, casual as a man stretching.

Hale looked at him over the rig. Smiled.

And in that smile Jonah saw the shape of the next chapter already being drafted: the offer that would come, the arithmetic that would follow, the price list with his father's name at the top of it.

Behind them, far back down the passage, the sea drew its first long breath inward.

"Time," Wren said quietly. "Everyone up. Now."
