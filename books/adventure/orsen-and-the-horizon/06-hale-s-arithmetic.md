## 6. Hale's Arithmetic

Marcus Hale had made a career of reading people the way accountants read ledgers: not for what they said, but for what the columns didn't add up to. In eleven years of shepherding grant money through the soft tissue of academic ambition, he had learned that every project carried a discrepancy somewhere—a figure that didn't reconcile, a name that appeared twice, a page that had been rewritten by a hand other than its author's. Most people thought fraud announced itself loudly. It didn't. It whispered, in the margins, in the space between what was measured and what was reported.

He found Jonah Ishikawa's whisper on the third night, in the blue light of a headlamp, sitting in the survey tent with the team asleep around him and the sea muttering against the headland like a creditor at the door.

The field notebooks were stacked in the equipment crate the way Priya organized everything—precise, chronological, tabbed. Hale hadn't gone looking for them, exactly. He had gone looking for leverage, which was a different thing wearing the same coat. Marcus Hale was not, by his own accounting, a dishonest man. He simply understood that truth, like capital, was inert until someone moved it. The Whitmore Foundation had sunk two hundred thousand dollars into Dr. Kenji Ishikawa's obsession, and the Foundation's board did not fund obsession. They funded results. Results required narrative. Narrative required someone willing to do the unglamorous arithmetic of what could be claimed, what could be deferred, and what could be quietly buried.

He opened the third notebook because the third notebook was wrong.

It was subtle. That was what struck him first—not the alteration itself but its craftsmanship. Jonah's original survey sketches were there, visible in ghost-lines where erasure had been imperfect, and over them a newer hand had redrawn the passage geometry of the cave's eastern branch. The redrawn lines narrowed the main chamber. They shortened the depth readings. They placed the tidal sump—the drowned throat of the cave, the one thing every experienced caver on this team would want mapped with obsessive accuracy—a full forty meters shallower than the sonar readings in the adjacent notebook said it should be.

Someone had made the cave look smaller. Safer. Less worth the Foundation's continued attention to its deepest reaches.

Hale sat very still for a long time, listening to the canvas breathe in the wind, and felt the particular pleasure he always felt at the moment a ledger balanced—the click of numbers agreeing with each other against everyone's interest.

* * *

He watched Jonah the next morning with new eyes.

The younger Ishikawa moved through the camp the way his father never had—efficiently, unostentatiously, with the economy of a man who wanted to be overlooked. Kenji Ishikawa filled every room he entered; his son seemed to be trying to leave every room he entered, even when he was standing in it. Hale had catalogued the dynamic within a day of arrival. The old man orbited his son like a planet around a sun it had burned too close to once already, hungry for warmth, rationed to a few degrees. The son gave him exactly what he gave everyone: competence without intimacy. He led the survey team flawlessly. He answered his father's questions in complete, correct sentences. And he watched the tide tables the way a man watches a door he's planning to leave through.

Hale found him at the tide pools below the camp, alone, crouched over a channel of water with the patience of someone who had learned solitude the way other people learned languages.

"Ishikawa." He let the name land without the *doctor*, without the *junior*. "Got a minute?"

Jonah didn't look up. "The anemones don't need a chaperone, Hale."

"I've been reading your survey notes."

That got him the look. Jonah rose slowly, brushing sand from his knee, and Hale watched the calculation happen behind his eyes—the same calculation, Hale would have bet his bonus, that had happened at the survey desk with an eraser in hand. Not guilt, exactly. Weighing.

"Priya does the copies," Jonah said. "If there's an error—"

"There's no error. That's the point." Hale kept his voice pleasant, conversational, the voice of a man discussing weather. "Your eastern branch measurements are off by forty meters. Consistently. In one notebook. In your hand." He paused, savoring it. "The erasures were very good, by the way. Better than most grad students manage. You've done this before."

The sea filled the silence between them, long and gray and patient.

"You want something," Jonah said finally.

"I always want something. It's how I know I'm alive." Hale smiled, and made himself comfortable on a barnacled rock, deliberately lowering his center of gravity, making himself the smaller animal. "Here's what I think. I think you found something down there you didn't put in your report. Something in the deep chamber. And I think you redrew the maps to keep the Foundation's cameras and the Foundation's money away from it. Which tells me it's valuable. Which tells me it's *claimable*."

"You think a lot of things."

"I think you're protecting it from us." Hale let that sit. "And here's what's interesting, Ishikawa: I don't actually care. Not about the chamber, not about whatever you're hiding. Artifacts are just assets with provenance. What I care about is the narrative, and the narrative has a problem, and you're the only person in this camp who can solve it."

Jonah's jaw tightened. "Which is?"

"Your father." Hale said it gently, the way you'd name a terminal diagnosis. "Kenji Ishikawa has spent thirty years being laughed out of conference halls. I've read the file. *Ishikawa's fables*, they called his settlement theory. He lost his funding in '09, his marriage—well. You'd know about that better than I would." He watched the younger man flinch and knew he'd hit bone. "The board gave me one season to produce something defensible, or the name Ishikawa goes back to being a punchline. And you—" he gestured at the coast, the headland, the drowned cave beneath it, all of it, the whole buried inheritance— "you're sitting on the thing that would vindicate him. And you're *burying it deeper*."

"I'm not sitting on anything."

"Of course you're not." Hale stood, unhurried, brushing off his coat. "So here's my offer, and I'll only make it once. Help me control the story. The discovery gets announced—properly, publicly, with the full weight of the Foundation behind it. First authorship goes where it belongs: Kenji Ishikawa. His theory, his vindication, his name on the paper that rewrites the regional history of this entire coast. All you have to do is restore your real survey data and let the deep chamber be documented. That's it. That's the whole price." He paused at the edge of the tide pools, and let the last of it out softly, the way you slip a hook under a fish's jaw. "You'd be giving your father the only thing he's ever wanted. And all it costs is a secret that was never going to stay buried anyway."

* * *

That night the camp gathered around the fire, because the wind had dropped and Kenji Ishikawa had produced a bottle of cheap whisky and declared, with the unguarded brightness of a man twenty years younger, that they had earned a night off.

Hale watched from the edge of the light, and watched Jonah watch his father.

It was a masterclass in hunger, the old man by the fire. Kenji held forth on the settlement theory—third time that week, Hale estimated, and the grad students nodded along anyway, because Priya was loyal and Wren was unreadable and the firelight made even repetition feel like revelation. Kenji's hands carved shapes in the air: trade routes, migration patterns, a civilization where the textbooks said there was nothing but transient fishing camps. His voice cracked on the word *evidence*. He had waited his entire professional life for evidence. He had, Hale knew, a filing cabinet in a rented office in Vancouver containing forty years of rejected grant applications, each one a small paper tombstone for a piece of himself.

And now his son sat three meters away from him, holding a tin cup of whisky he wasn't drinking, and looked at that hungry old face with an expression Hale had seen before and had never learned to price.

It wasn't hatred. Hale had expected hatred, had budgeted for it. It was worse. It was *wanting*. Jonah Ishikawa looked at his father the way a man looks at a door he sealed himself, checking, even now, for drafts.

*Some lies serve love,* Hale thought, and turned the phrase over, testing its weight. It was the kind of sentence that sounded like wisdom and spent like currency. He had built a career on sentences like that. But watching the two Ishikawas orbit each other across the fire—close enough to touch, separated by six years and one dead woman and whatever had happened in a cave that the son's own maps insisted wasn't there—he felt, for the first time in a long career of moving other people's truths, something adjacent to doubt.

The offer stood. Hale was patient. Patience was, in the end, his only real asset.

He was turning toward his tent when Wren Okafor materialized beside him, silent as driftwood, her eyes on the fire and not on him.

"You should know," she said quietly, "that the tide comes in faster than your charts say it does. The old families here don't use your charts."

Hale smiled his boardroom smile. "Noted."

"No," she said, still not looking at him. "I don't think it is."

She walked away toward the dark of the headland, and Marcus Hale stood alone at the edge of the firelight, holding his ledger of wants, and noticed—for the first time since arriving—that the wind had changed direction, and that somewhere out past the bar, the sea had begun, very softly, to sound like it was being pulled backward.

The barometer in the survey tent, he would find later, had started to fall that afternoon. Nobody had checked it. Everybody had been watching each other.

The storm was thirty hours out, and no one knew it yet.
