## 8. What We Carry Out

The water found them before the light did.

Jonah went first through Wren's back route, one hand on the slick limestone wall, the other trailing a guide rope that Priya had anchored at the last junction. Behind him came Wren, then his father, then Marcus Hale with his ruined camera bag clutched to his chest like a life vest, then Priya, sweeping her headlamp beam across everyone's heels as if she could hold them all above the waterline by sheer attention.

The passage was a throat. It had been dry when Wren first showed it to him two summers ago, in the way of things shared without explanation—a walk at low tide, a hand parting sea grass, a gap in the rock no wider than a coffin lid. Now it breathed. He could feel it breathing, a cold exhalation against his cheek every time the sea surged somewhere behind them, and each breath smelled more of salt than the last.

"Talk to me, Jonah." His father's voice, hoarse from shouting over the storm they'd outrun by minutes instead of hours.

"Fifty meters to the fork," Jonah said. "Then we climb."

"And after the climb?"

"After the climb we're above the surge channel. After that—" He didn't finish. Nobody finishes sentences like that anymore. Not down here.

His pack rode high between his shoulder blades, and inside it, wrapped in a wool sock and a ziplock bag, the carved stone pressed against his spine like a second heart. He'd been aware of it for three days now the way you're aware of a wound—every movement a small negotiation with its presence. Through the flooded gallery. Through the collapse that had sealed the main passage behind them with a sound like the world clearing its throat. Through Hale's increasingly precise questions about what exactly Jonah had documented on his solo survey, and why the notes didn't match the photographs' metadata.

Hale knew something. Jonah had watched him do the arithmetic by firelight, lips moving slightly, an accountant finding the discrepancy in a ledger he intended to own.

* * *

The fork came up faster than he wanted. The left branch—the one Wren's grandmother's stories called *the dry throat*, though nothing down here deserved the word *dry* anymore—angled upward through a chimney of broken stone. Water sluiced down it in a thin, constant sheet, but it was passable. It had to be passable.

"Wren," he called back. "You're sure about the ledge?"

"I'm sure my grandmother was sure," Wren said, and there was no fear in it, only a kind of inherited certainty that made Jonah's chest ache. "She walked this route out of a flood when she was nineteen. She said the mountain remembers who asks politely."

"That's not a geological assessment," Hale said.

"It's better than one," Wren said. "Geology doesn't have to live here afterward."

Jonah went up first. The chimney took him hands and knees and then just hands, water running into his sleeves, his boots finding holds that existed because generations of feet had asked them to. At the top was a shelf—a genuine shelf, wide enough for four people to crouch, angled above a black pool that heaved and sighed thirty feet below. He hauled himself onto it and turned to pull the others through.

Wren came up fluid as water herself. Then his father, slower, grunting, his sixty-three-year-old shoulders trembling with effort until Jonah gripped his forearm and dragged him over the lip. For one stupid, suspended moment they were face to face in the lamplight, close enough that Jonah could see the gray in his father's stubble, the cut above his eye from the collapse, and something in both their faces almost softened—

Then Hale came up swinging.

Not literally—not yet. But his headlamp swept the shelf like a searchlight, and his eyes did too, and they stopped on Jonah's pack with the flat satisfaction of a man who has finally located the item on his list.

"The chamber," Hale said. He was breathing hard from the climb, and it made his voice ragged, urgent, almost reasonable. "The deep one. The one your survey notes conveniently omit. I've been through your photos, Ishikawa. There's a shadow on the wall of the third gallery—a doorway shape. You photographed everything else within an inch of its life. Why not that?"

"It was unstable," Jonah said. "I didn't want anyone going in."

"You didn't want anyone *knowing*." Hale crouched, water dripping off him, and his voice dropped into the register of a man making an offer he believed was generous. "Look around you. The main passage is gone. Whatever happens next, this site is finished for excavation—for years, maybe forever. Which means the only thing that matters now, the *only* asset left, is documentation. And I think you're sitting on the best of it." He extended a hand, palm up, absurdly formal, rain-water running off his fingers. "Your father spent thirty years being laughed out of conference halls for insisting there was a pre-contact maritime settlement on this coast. Thirty years. Give me what you're hiding, let me frame the announcement, and Kenji Ishikawa dies famous. That's the trade. That's the *generous* trade."

The pool below them surged. Somewhere in the dark, rock shifted with a groan like a ship settling.

"Dad," Jonah said quietly. "Tell him no."

But his father wasn't looking at Hale. He was looking at Jonah—at the pack, at the set of Jonah's mouth—and Jonah saw the hunger there, the thing he'd hated since he was fourteen years old: that bright, bottomless appetite for proof, for vindication, for the world to finally say *you were right, Kenji*. And he saw, worse, that the hunger was considering.

"There were baskets," his father said softly. Not to Hale. To Jonah. "In your photos. Baskets don't survive six hundred years unless the chamber sealed fast and stayed sealed. What else is in there?"

"Dad—"

"What. Else."

The water answered before Jonah could. It came up the chimney behind Priya in a fist—a true surge, not the polite sheet they'd climbed through—and threw her onto the shelf coughing, her headlamp spinning wild arcs across the ceiling. The pool below rose visibly, swallowing the ledges they'd have used for the final traverse. The dry throat was closing.

"We move or we drown discussing this," Wren said, already moving along the shelf toward the crack at its far end. "Jonah. Lead."

He got three steps.

He got three steps and then Hale's hand closed on his pack strap and wrenched him backward, and the world became noise and lamplight and the smell of wet nylon. Hale wasn't strong the way climbers are strong; he was strong the way desperate men are strong, beyond his own body's voting rights, and he had Jonah half off balance, both hands digging for the top of the pack, saying—actually saying, in a voice cracked clean through with want—"Just the stone, I know there's a stone, Priya told me about the weight distribution in your—"

"Priya would never—"

"Priya keeps *notes*, you idiot, everyone keeps notes—"

Jonah's boot skidded on the wet shelf. The pool lunged up toward him. And in the half-second where physics was deciding whether this ended in a broken leg or something worse, his father hit Hale like a man tackling a door.

They went down together, two grown men in a scrum of limbs and headlamps, and Kenji Ishikawa—who had not touched his son in six years, who had shaken his hand at the funeral like a colleague—wrapped his arms around Jonah's pack and his son both and held on, his back to Hale, his whole slight body a wall.

"No," his father said. Not loudly. The water was louder. But Jonah felt the word through his father's chest, through his own ribs, the way you feel a bell. "*No.*"

"He's hiding the find! Our find—"

"*My son's* find. If there's anything hidden, it's his, and if he's hiding it, he has a reason, and I—" His father's voice broke, and reassembled itself lower. "I spent thirty years teaching myself that nothing matters more than being right. I lost—" A breath. "I lost everything practicing it. Get off us, Marcus. Get *off*."

Hale got off. Whether it was the words or the rising water or the sight of Wren standing over them with a climbing axe in her hand, Jonah never learned. He sat up, shaking, and his father knelt in front of him, and the headlamps made a small theater of the two of them while the sea rehearsed its ending around the walls.

"The stone," his father said. "Show me."

Every instinct Jonah owned said *no*. Every instinct except the oldest one, the one that had walked him into a cave alone because some part of him had always been walking toward this man.

He opened the pack. Unwrapped the sock, the bag. The carved stone sat in his palm, small and dense, the spiral-and-wave motif worn nearly smooth by hands centuries dead—and beneath the spiral, on the side he'd never shown anyone, a line of marks that matched nothing in any published record, because the record didn't exist, because the people who'd written it had kept their writing exactly the way Wren's people had kept their cave.

His father made a sound Jonah had never heard him make. Not triumph. Something older and sadder than triumph, the sound of a man seeing the horizon and understanding, finally, that he was standing on it.

"There's a chamber," Jonah said. "Deeper than anything on my survey. Tools, textiles, a wall of markings—it's a library, Dad, or the start of one. It's theirs." He nodded toward Wren, who stood watching with the axe loose in her grip and her grandmother's stories standing guard behind her eyes. "It was never ours to announce. It was never even ours to *find*."

The water reached the shelf then, cold as judgment, and Wren shouted the word that ended every argument ever conducted in a flooding cave: "*Move!*"

* * *

They moved.

Through the crack at the shelf's end, into a crawl that Wren navigated by touch and inheritance, counting turns under her breath the way other people count rosaries. The water chased them the whole way, patient and total, and twice Jonah passed his father's pack forward and once his father passed his back, and neither of them said anything about it, because some reconciliations are conducted entirely in logistics.

Behind them, faint through the rock, came a long grinding roar—the surge channel giving way, the sea taking back another room of the house it had lent them.

Ahead, impossibly, a thread of gray light.

And in Jonah's pocket, against his hip, the stone rode warm as a coal—carried out, at last, by someone who finally understood that the heaviest things you carry out of a place like this aren't artifacts at all.

The light widened. Wren reached it first and stopped dead, and the look on her face was not relief.

"Jonah," she said. "You need to see who's waiting for us."
