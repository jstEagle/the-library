## 1. The Man Who Kept Moving

The town was called Hesselund, which is a fine name for a place you intend to leave.

I came in on the afternoon barge, wedged between a consignment of salt fish and a man who talked for four hours about his hernia. The river was low and brown and patient, the way rivers are before the monsoon makes up its mind, and the town sat on its bank like something that had been set down there by accident and never picked back up again. Drying racks. Rope walks. A customs house with a roof like a dropped pie. Somewhere behind it all, the rails ran out of the hills and stopped, the way they do now, in stubs of rust and gravel that go nowhere and are maintained anyway because the Northern Rail Company maintains everything, including its mistakes. Especially its mistakes.

I had a work permit in my coat pocket that said I was Anders Voss, assistant gauge inspector, seconded from the Kolvik office. It was a good forgery. I know, because I made it myself, and I have made enough of them over twenty years to have developed standards. The seal was slightly too red — I'd never quite solved the pigment problem — but inspectors look at seals the way most people look at their own faces in shop windows: a glance, a nod, an agreement not to examine anything too closely.

My real name was folded up inside the same pocket, under the false one, written on nothing at all. That's how it travels best.

The satchel went over my shoulder. Inside it were maps. Eleven years of maps, or nineteen, depending on where you start counting, and I've started counting from different places at different times of my life, whichever starting point hurts least that year. Survey charts. Gauge ledgers. A hand-copied section profile of the Vellmark line that no living man should still own, drawn in ink so old it had gone the color of dried blood, which felt appropriate. Every sensible voice I have ever owned has told me to burn them. I have owned many sensible voices. They sound like my father. They say: *paper burns, Isandro, paper is the one thing in this world that forgives.*

I keep the satchel anyway. You can call it sentiment if you want. I call it evidence, and I am the only witness who refuses to lose it.

* * *

Hesselund had one tavern worth the name and one records annex worth avoiding, and I did both, in the wrong order.

The annex first. This is a habit I should break, the way a man should stop scratching a scar, but there's a particular itch that comes with arriving in any town that once had a rail office, and the itch always says the same thing: *check the ledgers. See what they wrote. See what they left out.* Twenty years of this. Twenty years of walking into dusty rooms and pulling down bound volumes of other men's handwriting, looking for my own name in the margins like a sinner checking scripture for mention of himself.

The annex clerk was a boy of maybe nineteen with ink on his fingers and the posture of someone who had been told twice already that day that he wasn't paid enough. He looked at my permit. He looked at me. He looked at the permit again, longer, and I felt the familiar small animal stir in my chest — the one that lives just under the sternum and wakes whenever anyone reads anything with my name near it, even a name that isn't mine.

"Assistant gauge inspector," he said. "From Kolvik."

"That's right."

"We don't get many of those."

"No," I said. "You wouldn't."

Because nobody inspects gauges anymore. That's rather the point of closed lines. The company keeps the titles alive the way it keeps the pension rolls alive — technically, expensively, and without any intention of honoring them. A forged permit works best when the position it claims is one nobody wants and nobody checks, and *assistant gauge inspector, seconded* is the bureaucratic equivalent of a room nobody visits. I've held that rank in nine districts. In two of them, the clerks were dead and I stamped my own papers. There's a freedom in that sort of thing that I try not to enjoy.

He waved me through to the stacks.

The old line ledgers were where they always are: bottom shelf, far wall, spines cracked, dust thick enough to write in. I pulled the volume for the northern district — 411 through 417, the years that matter, though I could recite them the way other men recite the names of their children — and carried it to the reading table by the window, where the light was good and the door was visible, because I always sit where I can see the door. Twenty years will teach you that much, if nothing else.

Here is what I was looking for, though I knew better than to hope: some correction. Some marginal note. Some later hand, some braver clerk, some auditor with a conscience who had gone back through the Vellmark closure file and written, in the small dry language of bureaucracy, *this report is wrong.* It has never been there. I check every time. It has never once been there. What's there instead is my own signature, reproduced in triplicate, certifying that the Vellmark Pass tunnel was surveyed, found structurally unsound beyond economic repair, and sealed in accordance with company regulation — eleven workers' tools logged as recovered, eleven workers themselves logged as *accounted for elsewhere*, a phrase I invented at three in the morning in a company hotel while a man named Director Haas stood behind my chair breathing through his nose.

*Accounted for elsewhere.* Four words. I have tried for twenty years to find five words that would undo them. There aren't any. Language doesn't work in reverse; that's the first thing they should teach you about language and the last thing anyone learns.

I was halfway through the volume, not reading so much as touching the pages with my eyes, when the boy came back.

"Sir? There's a gentleman asking after you."

The small animal under my sternum stood all the way up.

"What gentleman?"

"Company man. Grey coat. Says he's from the regional office." The boy shrugged, apologetic, holding a slip of paper. "Says an Anders Voss was expected on the morning barge, not the afternoon one."

I closed the ledger with a care that cost me more than slamming it would have. "A discrepancy in the manifest," I said. "Barges run late. It's why we inspect things."

"He's writing it down."

"Let him. Bureaucracy loves a discrepancy. Gives it something to do."

The boy almost smiled. Then he said the thing that turned my evening from unpleasant into instructive:

"He asked what you looked like. Before I told him you were here."

* * *

I returned the ledger to its shelf, thanked the boy, walked out of the annex at the pace of a man with nothing whatsoever on his mind, and did not go to the tavern.

That last part deserves explanation, because going to the tavern was the whole plan. Hesselund sits at the mouth of the Ullwater, and the Ullwater is the last navigable route toward the high country before the monsoon turns the tributaries into brown rope and snaps them. If a man wanted to go upriver — really upriver, past the checkpoints, into the drowned country where the maps in my satchel stop being history and start being destinations — he would need a boat, and boats in river towns congregate in taverns along with their owners, the way trouble congregates around me. So the tavern was the plan. The tavern was the entire architecture of my next three days.

But a grey coat who knows your arrival time is wrong, and asks what you look like before confirming you exist, is not doing routine paperwork. Routine paperwork doesn't need your face. Faces are for warrants.

So instead I bought a pipe of cheap tobacco from a chandler, sat on the sea wall where the fishing boats came in, and watched the customs house across the water while the light went long and gold and then gave up entirely.

Let me tell you about exhaustion, since I'm apparently the leading authority. People imagine it's tiredness, but tiredness sleeps. Exhaustion is a kind of eyesight. After twenty years of moving — Kolvik to Brenmar, Brenmar to the coast towns, always downstream of whatever I'd last touched, always the man who kept moving — you develop a vision that operates separately from your eyes. It counts exits. It notes which constable walks which beat and at what slackness. It registers, without being asked, that the woman selling eels on the corner has seen your face twice now and will see it a third time tomorrow, and that third times are how men get remembered, and being remembered is how men like me end up in files, and files are how men like me end up in places like Vellmark Pass, except on the outside of the wall, holding the paperwork.

I was tired the way stone is tired. Stone doesn't complain. Stone just erodes.

And here is the joke, the one I keep in the pocket where other men keep prayer: I built the machine that's chasing me. Not the company — the company was old and cruel before I ever signed anything. But the *system*. The clean, closed, certified version of the world where inconvenient tunnels get walls and inconvenient men get *accounted for elsewhere*. I drew the blueprint. I signed it in triplicate. And now I live inside it, forever, a fugitive from my own neat handwriting, and the only map out is the one I'm not allowed to read.

The lamp in the customs house window came on. A figure crossed the lamplight inside — grey coat, broad, unhurried, carrying a folder the way soldiers carry rifles, which is to say like an extension of the arm. He stopped at the window. He looked out across the water, directly at the stretch of sea wall where I sat, and for a moment the lamp put his face in silhouette and I couldn't read it, which frightened me more than a readable face would have. Readable faces belong to men doing a job. Unreadable ones belong to men doing an errand.

Then he turned away, and I breathed, and hated myself for the relief, because relief is just fear that hasn't finished its arithmetic yet.

* * *

I went to the tavern at half past ten, when the grey coat had been absent from the customs house window for two hours and the fishing fleet's crews had filled the front room with noise enough to hide in. A different entrance than the main door — there's a yard gate by the wood store that the boatmen use, and I used it, because I am, above all else, a creature of side entrances. Twenty years of side entrances. If they ever carve my headstone, it should say *he came in through the yard.*

The tavern was called the Drowned Bell, which is either a local legend or a warning, and the ale was bad in the honest way, and the room smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular despair of men whose season depends on weather that hasn't decided yet. Perfect. Boatmen in the week before monsoon are the most negotiable men on earth. Their holds are empty, their debts are ripe, and the river is about to become impassable, which means the only cargo worth carrying now is the kind that pays double for urgency.

I had one foot through the doorway and one eye on the room, cataloguing — the lockkeeper's cousin by the fire, the pair of dredge hands arguing over dice, the lean woman at the corner table with forearms like hawser and a boatwoman's weathered stare, sitting alone and drinking like someone who intended to remember the evening — when I felt the touch on my elbow.

"You're the gauge man."

He was sixty if he was a day, and fever had gotten into him the way damp gets into a hull: quietly, everywhere, and past the point of patching. Yellowed skin, sweat-slick hair, eyes burning with the terrible clarity of a man whose body has stopped wasting energy on anything but the one thought left in it. His coat was prospector's canvas, stiff with old mud from country I recognized. From country that is in my satchel.

"I'm sorry," I said, "you have me confused—"

"I watched you come off the barge." His grip tightened. Under the tar-and-ale smell of him there was another smell, mineral and cold, the smell of rock dust and cave water, and it went through me like a spike through an old wound. "Voss, the manifest says. Fine. Voss it is." He leaned close, and his breath was heat and sickness, and his voice dropped to the register men use for things they can't afford to say twice. "I've got something to sell you, gauge man. Something you've been carrying the ghost of for twenty years. And I think you know exactly what I mean, because you flinched just now like a man hearing his own name read out at a funeral."

Behind him, through the tavern's fogged front window, across the dark water, the lamp in the customs house went out.

Not dimmed. Out. Deliberately, like a lid coming down.

And the prospector smiled with cracked lips, following my gaze, and said, softly: "Best decide quick, friend. I don't think either of us is the only one in this town who knows what's up that river."
