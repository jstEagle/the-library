## 2. A Trade at Dusk

There is a particular kind of silence a man makes when he has decided to lie to you. It isn't the absence of sound. It's the absence of *breathing* — the small mechanical pause while the machinery inside him swaps one truth for another. I have spent twenty years listening for that silence in other men, mostly because I know exactly how it's manufactured. I own several of the machines myself.

The prospector went silent like that when I asked him where he'd gotten the chart.

"Found it," he said.

"Found it," I repeated.

"In a dead man's coat."

"And the dead man?"

"Fished him out of the Vellmark outflow two springs back." He said it the way you'd report the weather — flat, practiced, a story worn smooth from handling. "He was past talking."

I looked at him across the tavern table and did the arithmetic that men like me are always doing: what does this person want, and how much of what he's saying is scaffolding around the thing he actually wants? The tavern was called the Drowned Bell, which struck me as either very honest or very stupid naming, and it smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular sourness of beer poured too close to the river fog. The lamp above our table had a cracked chimney and gave off light the color of old teeth.

"You're feverish," I said.

"I'm *determined*," he said. "Feverish is what they call determined men who don't have anyone to write their eulogies."

His name was Oskar. He'd told me that much without my asking, which was either honesty or misdirection, and with prospectors the two are usually the same animal in different coats. He was maybe fifty-five and built like something the river had been chewing on for years and kept spitting back out — all sinew and gristle and one hand missing two fingers to frostbite or a winch cable, hard to say. His eyes were the problem. Fever had lit them from behind like paper lanterns, and behind the fever was something worse: certainty. Men who are merely sick look at you and see a doctor, a priest, an exit. Men who are certain look at you and see a *tool*.

And I had made the unforgivable mistake, an hour earlier, of letting him see me read.

Not just read — *read properly*. The Drowned Bell kept its walls papered with old freight schedules and company notices, the way river towns do, layers of them going back decades, and while I waited for a beer I had let my eyes do what they always do, which is hunt. A notice from '09 about revised tonnage limits on the upper reach. A schedule fragment showing the Vellmark spur still listed, six months after the collapse, before someone at Northern Rail got around to striking it. My finger had moved — I felt it move, the way you feel yourself falling — tracing the line of the closed pass on yellowed paper, and my lips, God help me, had almost shaped the numbers. Elevens and twelves. Gauge widths. Load tolerances for rock that no longer existed because it had come down on top of eleven men while I sat in a warm office certifying that it wouldn't.

Oskar had been sitting three tables away, drinking nothing, watching everything.

So now we were here, at his table, because he had stood up with the unhurried menace of a man who has already decided how the evening goes, and said: "You'll want to hear what I've got. Or you'll want to run. Either way, finish your beer first. You look like you need it."

Which was true, and insulting, and effective.

* * *

"Let me see it again," I said.

He glanced around the tavern — the barman polishing glasses that would never be clean, two deckhands asleep over their dice, a woman by the stove mending a net with the rhythmic patience of someone who has decided the world can wait — and then, under the table, passed me the oilcloth packet for the second time.

I unwrapped it the way you'd handle a bird with a broken wing.

It was a survey map, hand-drawn, and it was good. That was the first thing that frightened me. Not accurate in the way a company drawing is accurate — no title block, no engineer's stamp, no tidy north arrow — but accurate in the way of a man who has walked every step of it and argued with every step. The Vellmark Pass ran through the middle of it like a healed wound, the old rail bed marked in faded ink, and around it, in a different, newer hand, annotations in pencil: seep lines, air currents, a shaft marked with three exclamation points as if the drawer had been shouting on paper. Water channels. A chamber. And near the eastern throat of the pass, a small symbol I recognized from a hundred freight manifests and had spent twenty years trying not to recognize:

A circle with a cross through it. The mark the company used for sealed structures.

Except someone had drawn a second mark next to it. A crude little boat. And a date.

Three years ago.

I put the map down before my hands could shake visibly. "This is worth money," I said. "To collectors. To the company, even — they pay for loose surveys of closed lines. Why bring it to me?"

"Because you're not a collector." Oskar leaned forward into the bad lamplight, and the fever in his face made him look briefly like a man carved out of a burning log. "Collectors buy paper. I need a man who reads *rock*. You stood there reading that freight notice like it was scripture. You counted the gauge off the top of your head — I watched your mouth move. Twelve-foot clearance on the old Vellmark cut, am I right? Nobody remembers that unless they built it or buried it."

"Or read about it," I said.

"Sure." He smiled. Cracked lips, gray gums. "Read about it."

Outside, the rain had started again, that fine monsoon drizzle that doesn't fall so much as accumulate, like the sky is slowly deciding to become the river. Against the window, the dark water of the harbor moved with the slow muscular indifference of something that has drowned better men than either of us.

"Here's the trade," Oskar said. "You take me upriver. You get me into the pass before the spring melt closes it — four weeks, maybe five, then the water comes up through the lower galleries and it's shut until autumn. In exchange, the map's yours. All of it. Every note."

"And why," I said carefully, "does a prospector want into a collapsed rail tunnel?"

For a moment — just a moment — the fever receded and something older and colder looked out of his face. "Because my brother's name is on the manifest," he said. "Number seven of eleven. And because twenty years ago somebody signed a piece of paper saying there was nothing in that pass worth digging for, and I'd like to ask him why, and I'd like to ask him *in person*, and if he happens to be dead I'll settle for asking the mountain."

The beer in my glass had gone flat and warm. I drank it anyway, because my hands needed employment.

* * *

Let me tell you what I remember about the certification, since apparently the universe has decided I'm going to be reminded regardless.

It was spring, twenty years ago. I was thirty-one and hungry in the way young engineers are hungry — not for money exactly, but for the thing money stands in for, which is to say permanence, a name on things, a life that didn't evaporate when you left the room. I'd been with Northern Rail four years, running surveys on the mountain lines, and I was good. That's not vanity; that's the tragedy's load-bearing wall. If I'd been mediocre, none of it works. Mediocre men don't get asked to sign things.

The Vellmark cut had been troubled from the start. The rock through the eastern throat was schist — flaky, treacherous, water-loving — and the spring thaw that year had been early and violent. There were seeps in the lower gallery that the work crews had named, the way crews name things they're afraid of. The Widow. The Weeper. The night shift refused to work the east face after a shot came loose and killed a mule, and the foreman filed a report, and the report went up the chain, and somewhere between the foreman's desk and mine it was transformed — the way reports are transformed, alchemically, by pressure and ambition — from *the mountain is unstable and men will die* into *minor seasonal seepage, manageable with standard drainage*.

My superior — I won't give him the dignity of a name; he died rich, which is its own punishment, though not the sufficient one — my superior put the draft certification in front of me and said, "Isandro. The line opens in June. The board is in June. Read it and sign it."

And I read it. That's the part I can't forgive, the part I've never managed to make peace with: I *read* it. I saw the figures that didn't match my own measurements. I knew the seepage numbers were fiction. I knew the drainage plan was a sketch drawn by a man who had never been underground. And I signed it anyway, because I was thirty-one, and because refusing meant being the difficult man, the man who costs the company its opening date, and I had watched what happened to difficult men. They were given excellent references and sent to survey marshes until they resigned.

Eleven men went in on a Tuesday in late May to clear the east face for the final ballast run. The Widow let go at four in the afternoon. Two hundred tons of schist, moving at the speed of geology accelerated by bad drainage and a falsified report, and the pass closed like a book.

They sealed the mouths within the week. Officially: to prevent further collapse. Unofficially, and everyone unofficially understood: recovery was expensive, the tunnel was a loss, and eleven dead navvies were cheaper as a memorial plaque than as an excavation. The company paid widows. The company planted trees. The company promoted me to chief surveyor for the northern district, with a salary that made the signing feel, in the ledger of my conscience, less like a crime and more like a purchase.

I told myself for years that the men died instantly. I know now — I've always known — that collapse galleries seal rather than crush, sometimes. That pockets form. That air finds its way through schist the way rumor finds its way through a town. I told myself the instant-death story the way you take a poultice to a wound you refuse to clean: it doesn't heal it, it just keeps you from looking at it.

And now here was a feverish stranger sliding a hand-drawn map across a tavern table, with a boat drawn beside the seal mark and a date three years old, and asking me — no, *telling* me — that we were going upriver together.

* * *

"The answer's no," I said.

Oskar nodded, unsurprised, and produced from inside his coat a flask, unscrewed it, and drank with the grim discipline of a man dosing himself. "Course it is. Say it again in an hour."

"There's nothing in that pass but rock and water and—"

"And whatever's been living on the seep side of that seal for twenty years, if the air shaft holds." He tapped the map, right on the penciled chamber. "You think I drew this from a dead man's coat and stopped there? I've been to the outflow. Twice. There's smoke residue on the rocks downstream of the third vent. *Recent* smoke. Somebody down there is burning wood, friend, and last I checked, trees don't grow in tunnels."

The tavern noise seemed to recede, the way it does when your blood decides it has more important business. I thought of the scratch-marks I'd never seen and had always imagined. I thought of the memorial plaque in the company yard, eleven names in bronze, and how bronze is a metal chosen precisely because it doesn't rust, doesn't change, doesn't accuse.

"If someone survived," I said, "they'd have come out."

"Through what?" Oskar laughed, and it turned into coughing, and he rode the cough down like a man riding a horse he hated. "Out through two hundred tons of rock? Out past a company that sealed the mouths within the *week*? You think they didn't try? You think nobody hammered on that stone?" He wiped his mouth. "Maybe they did come out. Maybe some did. But somebody's burning wood down there *now*, and the melt closes the low galleries in a month, and after that it's another season, and I—" he gestured at himself, the ruined body, the lantern eyes, "—I don't have seasons left. So I need a man who can read the old drawings and talk to locks and cranks, and you're the only man in this town whose eyes did what yours did tonight."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I find another." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him; I watched pain cross his face like weather. "There's always another engineer. Half the railway men who drink in towns like this are running from something. It's practically the uniform." He leaned back. "But you won't refuse."

"No?"

"No. Because when I mentioned the pass, you didn't ask *where's Vellmark*. You asked *who else knows you read that notice*." He smiled his terrible smile. "You've been waiting twenty years for someone to knock on your door, friend. You just hoped it'd be the police, so you could finally stop waiting."

I should have walked out. I want that on the record, wherever records go — I should have stood up, put a coin on the table, and walked out into the rain, and taken the morning barge south, and found some other town to be invisible in. That was the sensible play. That was the play I had executed flawlessly in nineteen towns across twelve years.

Instead I heard myself say: "The map stays with me. You get it back when we're inside."

Something flickered across Oskar's face — surprise, maybe, or respect, or the simple relief of a dying man who has finally handed his burden to someone else's back. "Done," he said.

We shook on it. His grip was hot as a stove door.

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I took a room above the tavern because leaving would have looked like flight, and I lay on the narrow bed listening to the rain thicken against the roof and doing the thing I do instead of sleeping, which is inventorying exits.

Toward midnight I heard boots on the stairs. Heavy, deliberate, two sets, pausing at each landing. They stopped outside my door for the length of ten heartbeats — long enough to listen, short enough to deny — and then continued up to the floor above, where they stopped again, and this time stayed.

Company men traveled in pairs. Company men wore boots with iron heel-plates, because they expected to be standing in places where standing softly didn't matter. I had spent twelve years learning the signature of Northern Rail enforcement the way a hunted animal learns the signature of dogs, and those boots had it.

I rose quietly, dressed, and sat by the window with the map inside my coat, watching the harbor turn silver-black under the rising rain. Somewhere out on the water a boat lamp swung at anchor. Tomorrow I would need a boat, and a boatwoman willing to run the upper reach in pre-monsoon water, and I had exactly one introduction and no alternatives.

Behind me, upstairs, the boots crossed the floor and stopped.

Below my window, emerging from the shadow of the customs house wall, a figure in an oilskin coat stood motionless in the rain, face turned up toward the tavern windows, checking them one by one. Checking, I understood, for a light. For a man awake at this hour, packing, planning.

The figure moved on toward the docks. And I sat in the dark with a dead man's map against my ribs and a living man's fever one floor below, and understood that the choice Oskar had offered me at the table was never really a choice at all.

The only question left was who reached the river first.
