## 3. The Boatwoman

The man came down the dock stairs like he was trying not to be seen, which is the surest way to make me look at someone. My mother taught me that before she taught me to read water: the ones who move quietly are the ones carrying something heavy. The ones who move loudly are usually just drunk.

He was carrying something heavy. I could tell by the shoulders. Drunk men lean; guilty men hunch.

I was mending net on the *Solveig*'s deck, or pretending to, which is the same posture with less blisters. The *Solveig* is my boat—forty feet of river freighter, half cargo hold, half cabin, all of it held together by tar, stubbornness, and a hull my father laid before I was born. She takes paying passengers upriver when the cargo doesn't fill her, and she takes them without questions, because questions slow a departure and departures are money.

This one stopped at the foot of the gangplank and looked at the boat the way men look at boats when they're calculating. I've been looked at that way my whole life. Usually it means they're wondering if she'll float. Sometimes it means they're wondering if she's for sale. Once it meant they were wondering if she could be made to sink, but that's a story for another season.

"You take freight?" he called up.

"Depends on the freight."

"Passenger freight."

I set down the net and gave him the full look. Tall, but folded up small, like a man who'd learned to take up less room than the world allotted him. Grey at the temples, hands that had done real work but hadn't done it recently—engineer's hands, or clerk's, and there's a difference you can see if you've hauled enough rope. A satchel across his chest, worn at the strap. Eyes that kept flicking to the dock behind him like it owed him something.

"Passenger freight runs dear this time of year," I said. "Monsoon's coming early. River's already up a hand at Kellum's gauge."

"I can pay."

"They all say that."

He climbed the gangplank without being invited, which I noted and did not forgive, and set a fold of notes on my cargo hatch. More than fair. Too much, in fact, which is its own kind of answer. Men who overpay for passage are men who can't afford to be refused.

"Where to?" I asked, though I already knew the answer had to be upriver, because nobody comes to Torv's Landing to go anywhere else.

"Vellmark."

There it is. I'll say this for my life: it has a sense of timing. Three years I've run this stretch of river, and three years I've asked every smuggler, prospector, and drunk with a memory what lies past the Vellmark closure, and three years the answer has been *nothing, girl, there's nothing there but rock and the company's bad conscience.* And now a stranger with a guilty walk and too much money says *Vellmark* like it's a town with a train station.

"Vellmark's closed," I said. "Closed twenty years. There's no dock. There's no road. There's a mountain sitting on a hole in the world, and the company will fine you the shirt off your back for looking at it."

"I know what it is."

"Do you." I folded my arms. "Because men who know what it is usually don't want to go there. And men who want to go there usually don't know what it is."

He looked at me then—really looked, the first time—and something in his face shifted, the way water shifts when something big passes underneath. Not a smile. More like the decision to stop pretending to be one kind of man in front of another kind.

"Can I show you something?" he said. "Not here."

* * *

"Not here" turned out to be the back room of the Anchorline, which is the least back-roomy back room on the river—it's a storage closet with a lamp and a table that wobbles—but Hal the keeper rents it to people who want to talk without the whole dock hearing, and Hal doesn't remember conversations, a professional courtesy I have benefited from more than once.

He unrolled the map on the table and weighted the corners with two mugs, a candle stub, and my knife, which I'd put on the table in a way that suggested I knew how to use it. I hadn't needed to. He'd already seen the knife.

It was a prospector's chart, hand-inked, the kind that costs a man half his eyesight over a winter. The Vellmark gorge ran down its center like a healed scar. Old survey lines in faded brown. And in fresh ink, still sharp enough to smell, a route: up the Sorrel branch, past the drowned lock at Cold Harbor, into the closed pass itself. Whoever drew it had been there. You can't fake that kind of line-work. The river bends were drawn the way they actually bend, not the way maps wish they did.

"Who drew this?" I asked.

"A prospector. Oskar Vey. He's dying—fever, lungs full of river fog. He wants the pass opened before the monsoon seals the high route for the season."

"Opened. The pass that's been shut since before I could walk."

"He says there's a way in. An air shaft, old workings, a route through to the far side." The man's voice did something careful around the next words, like a man stepping on river ice. "He says there are people who never came out."

I looked at the map. I looked at it the way you look at a thing you've been looking for without admitting it, and my chest did that thing it does, the slow squeeze, the one I've gotten good at ignoring.

Because here is what I have never said out loud in three years, not to Hal, not to the river, not to the dark at the top of the mast where I go when the boat's too quiet: my brother Anders went into the Vellmark country three years ago with a survey crew's worth of ambition and a company contract, and he did not come out. The company said *lost, presumed drowned, the gorge is dangerous, here is a form.* The form was for a compensation claim that paid out forty percent of what it should have, and the clerk who processed it didn't look up from his desk. I checked. I stood in that office for two hours and the clerk never once looked up.

And here is what I have also never said: the mark on this map—the route, the approach, the little careful cross where Oskar Vey had inked a point of interest—sat within a mile, maybe less, of where Anders's last letter said he'd be. *Going up the Sorrel branch, Mar. There's work in the old pass if a man's willing to look. Don't worry.*

I worried. I have been worrying in a continuous unbroken line for three years, the way a river worries a bank, patient and total.

"How much do you know about the closure?" I asked him.

"Enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I can afford right now." He said it without flinching, which I'll grant him was either honesty or very good practice. "Ask me anything else."

I asked him his name. Isandro Iversen. I asked his trade. Railroad engineer, survey and works. I asked if the Northern Rail Company knew he was in Torv's Landing, and there—the flinch. Small, fast, a fish under the surface. *No.*

"Then they'll want to," I said. "Men like you don't travel alone. Men like you travel with men behind them."

"You're not wrong."

"At least you're not a liar about everything."

"I'm a liar about most things," he said, and the terrible part was how tired he sounded saying it, like a man confessing to a chronic illness. "But not about the map. Not about what it's worth. Not about the people it might lead to."

I looked down at the map again. At the cross. At the Sorrel branch. At the distance between that cross and the place where my brother's letters stopped, which was no distance at all.

Here's the arithmetic I did, standing in that wobbling room with the lamp hissing: a stranger I don't trust, carrying a map I can't verify, offering to take me to the one place on this earth I have both prayed toward and prayed never to see. Against: three years of nothing. Three years of forms and clerks and *presumed drowned* and a grave with no body and a mother in the churchyard ash who died not knowing, which is the cruelest inheritance a family can leave you—the not-knowing, packed up like linens, handed down.

I don't trust him. I want that on the record, in ink, notarized. I don't trust the way he watches the door, I don't trust the money, I don't trust a railroad engineer with a reason to hide from the railroad, and I trust least of all the fact that he flinched at the company's name and then steadied, which means the flinch was the honest part and the steadying was the lie.

But I need him. That's the other half of the ledger, and the river doesn't care which half you like better.

"Terms," I said, and sat down, because negotiations go better seated and I wanted my knees to stop deciding things for me. "One. You pay the passage, and it's non-refundable, and if you die in the gorge I keep the fee and the map."

"Fair."

"Two. We go up the Sorrel branch first. Before anything else. Before your air shaft, before your pass, before whatever you're actually doing, which I know isn't what you've told me." I put my finger on the cross. "Here. First."

He looked at the mark. Then at me. And I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd done—watched him weigh what I might be to him against what he needed—and I understood in that moment that we were both holding maps of each other with large blank spaces on them, and both deciding the blanks were survivable.

"First," he agreed.

"Three. If the company comes asking, you were never on my boat. You're a fish buyer from downcoast. You stink of herring and disappointment. I've never seen you before in my life."

"That one I can play convincingly."

"I believe you," I said, and hated that I believed him, and hated more the small cold thought that a man who can lie that smoothly has probably lied to me already, is probably lying to me now, will probably lie to me again before the monsoon's done. "Four. You tell me the truth about the closure when I ask. Not all at once—I can see it's a long story and I can see you're not ready—but when I ask a question, you answer it true. Even if it costs you."

That one took him a long moment. Long enough that the lamp guttered and he trimmed the wick, buying time the way men buy time, with their hands, so their faces can catch up.

"Even if it costs me," he said.

"Then we have terms." I stood and rolled up the map before he could, because it was going on my boat and my boat, my rules. "We load tonight and run the tide at first light. The river's rising and I want the Sorrel branch while it's still navigable and not yet a waterfall. Bring whatever you're bringing and don't tell me it's maps, because I already know it's maps."

* * *

He came aboard at dusk with the satchel and one canvas grip and the look of a man traveling light because everything heavy is in his head. I stowed him in the forward cabin, which has a bunk, a hook, and a porthole that doesn't open, and told him the galley was shared and the head was aft and if he touched the engine room without invitation I'd introduce him to the propeller shaft personally.

Then I went up to the wheelhouse and did what I always do at dusk, which is stand at the window with my hands on the wheel I'm not steering yet, and look upriver.

The Sorrel branch came down out of the hills in the last light, brown and swollen already, dragging whole trees along like it was collecting them. Somewhere up that water was a cross on a map and a mile of gorge and maybe—*maybe*, the word I'd been rationing like hardtack for three years—maybe a brother. Maybe bones. Maybe worse than bones, which is a man who walked out and chose not to write. I'd made my peace with bones. Bones you can bury. Bones end things.

I heard him come up behind me—I let him think I didn't—and he stood at the wheelhouse door a while, not entering, watching the same water.

"Your brother," he said finally. "The one from the manifest."

I turned. "What manifest?"

He had the grace to look caught, which was at least one honest thing on him. "Oskar's papers. There was a crew list from three years back, a survey contract. An A. Voss. I assumed—"

"You assumed right." I faced the window again. "Anders Voss. My brother. He went up the Sorrel with a company survey subcontract and stopped writing in the fourth week. The company says drowned. The company says a lot of things."

The wheelhouse got very quiet. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"Is that why you're really taking this passage?" he asked.

"It's why I'm taking it for the fee I'm charging, which is a third of what I should." I looked at him straight. "You want to know something, engineer? I'd have taken you for free and I'd have hated you for it. The fee is so I can tell myself this is business. So when it goes wrong—and it will go wrong, everything upriver goes wrong—I can say I was paid and it was trade and I'm a boatwoman, not a fool."

"And is it working?"

"No," I said. "It never works. But it's what I have."

He nodded slowly, and for a second—just a second—his face did the thing that guilty men's faces do when they meet a grief that rhymes with their own. Not pity. Recognition. Like a man hearing a knock and knowing exactly who's at the door, because he's been answering it for years.

"Iversen," I said, as he turned to go. "Whatever you're not telling me about that pass—however bad it is, however much it costs you to say it—remember your terms. Because I will ask. And I always ask."

"I know," he said, and went below, and his footsteps on the ladder were quiet, the way guilty men's footsteps are quiet.

I stood at the wheel until full dark, watching the black water come down from the hills, carrying its trees, its silt, its secrets. First light we'd run the tide. First light we'd be on the Sorrel branch, him and me and the map, two strangers lashed together by a cross of ink and three years of not-knowing.

I don't trust him. But I've trusted the river all my life, and the river has drowned better men than him, and I still go out on it every morning. Trust isn't the thing that gets you up the gorge. Need is. Need and a sound hull and a hand on the wheel.

I checked the hull twice that night anyway.

And at the dock below, in the last of the light, I saw two men in company grey standing at the head of the stairs, not coming down, just watching my boat the way men watch a boat when they're calculating.

I went and woke Iversen.

"You said they didn't know you were in Torv's Landing," I said.

"They didn't," he said, and reached for his satchel, and I swear to the river his hands were steady, which frightened me more than the grey coats did. "They just know the map is."
