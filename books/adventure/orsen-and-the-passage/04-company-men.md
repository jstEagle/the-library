## 4. Company Men

The checkpoint announced itself an hour before we reached it, which is the way of checkpoints—they travel ahead of themselves like bad weather. First it was the smoke from the fuel barge, a greasy black thread against the monsoon-grey sky. Then the sound: hammers on timber, men building something in the middle of a river that had never asked to be built upon. Then the flag itself, red and black, snapping above a pontoon of rough planks lashed to empty oil drums, and beneath it two figures in company grey who stood with the particular stillness of men paid to wait.

Maren saw them and said nothing, but her hands changed on the tiller. I'd been aboard three days by then and had learned to read her that way—not through words, which she rationed like a woman crossing a desert with a single waterskin, but through her hands, her shoulders, the set of her jaw. Her hands went quiet. That was all.

"Checkpoint," she said finally, as if I might have missed the flag, the pontoon, the armed men, the general atmosphere of institutional menace.

"I see it."

"Routine," she added, in a tone that suggested routine was a word other people used.

I sat on her cargo crates—dried fish, lamp oil, a consignment of nails bound for some upriver settlement that would pay for them in barter and complaint—and did what I had gotten very good at over twenty years: I arranged my face. It's a skill like any other. Carpenters have their joints, masons their mortar, and liars have the small muscular work of the face, the loosening of the eyes, the arrangement of boredom over fear. I had once believed that practice made it easier. The truth is that practice only makes it faster.

The map rode against my ribs.

Oskar's chart—I had taken to thinking of it as Oskar's chart rather than mine, though possession is a strange thing when what you possess wants you dead—was folded into a oilskin packet sewn inside the lining of my coat, flat against my left side, where a man might carry letters from home if he were the sort of man who received letters from home. I had slept in the coat. I would have bathed in it if the river hadn't been the color of strong tea and moving at the speed of regret.

"Who are they looking for?" I asked, too casually. Casualness, I've found, works best when you announce it.

Maren gave me a look that said she'd heard the announcement and filed it under evidence. "Everyone. That's the point of a checkpoint. They don't know who they're looking for, so they look at everyone, and everyone pays the toll of being looked at." She swung the boat toward the pontoon with the unhurried economy of a woman who had done this a hundred times and resented every one. "Keep your mouth shut unless they ask you something. And if they ask you something, lie badly."

"Lie badly?"

"Men who lie well are interesting. Interesting men get searched." She brought us alongside with a bump that was almost gentle. "You strike me as a man who lies well."

There are moments when being known is worse than being caught. I said nothing, which was its own kind of confession, and she turned away to deal with the lines.

* * *

They boarded one at a time, because company procedure loves a queue even when there are only two men in it. The first was tall and narrow, with the complexion of someone who had spent his career indoors reading other people's paperwork, and he carried a leather folio the way a priest carries scripture—close, reverently, and with the confidence that what was inside applied to everyone. The second was shorter, thicker through the shoulders, and carried nothing except a truncheon and the expression of a man whose entire personality had been decided for him by his equipment.

"Papers," said the tall one, to Maren.

She handed over her river license, cargo manifest, tax stamp. He went through them with a fingernail, separating each page as though hoping to find another hidden between. I watched him do it and thought about how much of the Northern Rail Company's power lived in exactly this gesture—the slow page-turn, the pause that costs the pauser nothing and the paused everything. They didn't need to find anything. They needed you to stand there while they didn't find it.

"And him?"

"My deckhand," Maren said.

I am not, and have never been, a deckhand. I am a railroad engineer with twenty years' service, a certified surveyor, and a man whose signature appears on documents that closed a mountain pass with eleven living men inside it. But I said nothing, because the first rule of a good lie is to let someone better positioned tell it for you, and Maren told it beautifully—flat, bored, faintly insulted, the voice of a woman inconvenienced by bureaucracy she'd long since stopped expecting fairness from.

The tall one looked at me. I looked back with the mild vacancy of a man whose most complicated thought that morning had been about nails.

"Name?"

"Anders," I said. Anders Holm. I had been Anders Holm before, in another province, another year, working a dredging contract under a name I'd bought for three days' wages from a clerk who owed me more than money could cover. Names are like coats. You wear one until the weather changes.

He wrote it down. I watched him write it down and felt the small cold pleasure of a lie landing cleanly, followed immediately by the thing that always follows it, which is disgust. Not fear—disgust. Fear is for the moment before; disgust is for after, when you realize how little it cost you, how smoothly the machinery still ran, how many years you'd spent keeping this particular engine oiled. There is a version of myself, somewhere, who fumbles. Who says *Isandro* when asked, who goes pale, who makes the mistake that ends the flight. I have spent two decades making sure that version stays buried, and every time the machinery runs smooth I think: *this is what it cost you. Not the danger. The ease.*

"You're not local," the tall one said.

"Coast," I said. "Work's thin on the coast."

"Work's thin everywhere. That's why you're going upriver, is it? Where the work isn't?"

"That's generally how it goes."

Something flickered across his face—not suspicion exactly, but the professional appetite of a man who suspects professionally and is disappointed most days. Behind him, the short one drifted along the gunwale, looking at things without appearing to look. Cargo crates. Fish barrels. The tarp rolled at the stern. My coat, hanging on its peg by the wheelhouse door, because I had put it there deliberately—an hour ago, while we were still out of sight of the smoke—because a hidden thing in plain sight draws less attention than a hidden thing kept hidden, and a coat on a peg belongs to the boat, belongs to Maren, belongs to nobody worth searching.

It was the best idea I'd had in weeks and I hated myself for having it.

"We're looking for a man," the tall one said, opening the folio at last. "Possibly traveling by river. Possibly posing as a worker." He produced a paper—a printed notice, company letterhead, the red-black seal—and read aloud with the relish of a man who enjoyed the sound of official language. "Wanted for questioning in the matter of stolen property belonging to the Northern Rail Company. Property described as survey materials, maps, and documents of a sensitive engineering nature."

"Mm," said Maren.

"Theft of company property is a serious offense."

"So I've heard."

He ignored her, which was wise, and turned to me, which was not. "Anders. You've seen nothing. No travelers on the road, no strangers at the docks, no one selling papers or asking after old lines?"

And here is the thing I will not forgive myself for, though I suspect I will also never stop doing it: the answer came out of me warm and ready, shaped before he finished the question. "Old lines?" I said, and let just enough puzzlement into it—not too much, puzzlement is a spice, and too much ruins the dish—"What old lines?"

"The closed lines. Vellmark. Corren Halt. The passes shut after the collapses." His eyes stayed on mine, level, patient. A good interrogator's trick: patience reads as omniscience. Stand still long enough and guilty men invent crimes for you.

"Before my time," I said. "I lay track, mister. I don't ask where it goes."

It was a good line. It was, in fact, a perfect line—the humble laborer's shrug, the deliberate smallness, a man defining himself by what he doesn't know. It came out of me with the fluency of long rehearsal, and as it landed I watched it land, watched the tall one's shoulders come down a half-inch, watched the professional appetite recede, and I thought: *twenty years.* Twenty years of this. Twenty years of being fluent in my own erasure, so practiced at the smallness that I sometimes couldn't find the large man underneath, the one who had once drawn bridges on the backs of envelopes because he loved the shapes of them, the one who had signed—

The one who had signed.

"No," the tall one said, closing the folio. "I suppose you don't."

The short one came back from the stern. "Nothing," he reported, in a voice like gravel settling. "Boat's clean."

"Mind the wake past the boom," the tall one told Maren, handing back her papers. "Channel markers are moved this season. Company water now, above the bend."

"Company water," Maren repeated, taking the papers without thanks.

"Everything's company water eventually." He smiled at his own wit, found it sufficient, and stepped onto the pontoon. The short one followed. For a moment the four of us hung there—two liars on a boat, two men on a drum-lashed platform, the river pushing all of us somewhere none of us had chosen—and then Maren cast off and the checkpoint began to shrink behind us, flag and hammer-smoke and all, folding itself back into the grey.

* * *

We ran an hour before either of us spoke. The banks narrowed, the current picked up, and the rain started again, that fine monsoon drizzle that isn't weather so much as a condition, like damp grief. I retrieved my coat from the peg and put it on, and felt the map press against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and thought about the notice. *Survey materials, maps, and documents of a sensitive engineering nature.*

They weren't looking for Oskar's chart. Not specifically. They were looking for *me*—for whatever I'd carried out of the district office the night I finally ran, the ledgers, the line reports, the correspondence I should have burned and hadn't because burning them meant admitting they existed, and admitting they existed meant admitting what I'd done, and I had built twenty years on the load-bearing wall of *not that*. So I'd carried them. From town to town, job to job, forged permit to forged permit, a satchel of evidence against myself that I couldn't destroy and couldn't use, and now the company wanted it back, and Oskar had died—or so the world claimed—for a different map entirely, and both maps were riding against my ribs in one oilskin packet like two snakes in a sack.

"You lied well," Maren said at last, not looking at me. Her hands worked the tiller in small corrections, reading water I couldn't read.

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment." She adjusted our angle around a snag. "A man who lies that well has had practice. A man who's had that much practice has been running that long. And a man who's been running that long—" she glanced at me, briefly, the grey eyes doing their grey arithmetic, "—is worth more than deckhand wages to whoever's chasing him. Which means I'm not carrying fish anymore. I'm carrying trouble."

"Maren—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and final as a hatch closing. "Whatever it is, I don't want the speech. I want to know one thing, and you'll answer it or I'll put you off at the next landing and you can walk." She let the boat drift a moment, letting me feel the current take us, the river's indifference. "Those men. Are they following you, or following the map?"

And there it was—the question I'd been circling since the checkpoint, the one I'd been careful not to ask myself, because the honest answer was worse than either option alone. Because the truth was that the men and the map had become the same thing. Oskar's chart showed the pass. My papers proved the pass was murdered. Anyone hunting one would inevitably find the other, and anyone holding both was no longer a fugitive but a threat, and companies do not chase threats, they erase them.

"Both," I said. "It's the same trail now."

She held my gaze for a long three seconds, the rain beading on her oilskin, the river shoving at the hull. Then she faced forward and gave the tiller a correction and said, "Then we go faster," and I understood that I had just been tried, sentenced, and reprieved by a court of one, and that the sentence was suspended pending behavior.

That night we tied up in the lee of a drowned orchard, the trees standing waist-deep in floodwater with their last unharvested fruit going soft, and I took the first watch because I didn't trust myself to sleep and because watching is a form of penance if you do it long enough. The river breathed. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered like a committee that couldn't agree.

And it was during that watch, in the hour before dawn, that I saw it: a light on the water behind us. Far back, at the edge of sight, where the channel bent—too steady for lightning, too low for a house, moving the way a boat moves when it's moving carefully.

Someone had come through the checkpoint after us.

Or someone had never needed the checkpoint at all, and had simply been waiting on the water, patient as the company itself, letting us lead them upriver to whatever it was they wanted found.

I stood in the flooded orchard with the rain coming down and the map against my heart and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the dock at Harrow's Bend: they hadn't stopped us at the pontoon because stopping us would have been an admission. An admission that the map existed, that Oskar had talked, that there was something upriver worth killing for. Better to let the thief show you where the treasure is.

Better to follow the boatwoman home.

I went below, quietly, and woke Maren with a hand on her shoulder, and told her to look at the light.
