## 5. First Water

The light was wrong, and I knew it before I opened my eyes.

I've spent twenty-two years on this river, and the river talks to you in colors. Dawn on the Vell is a gray-green thing, patient as an old woman waiting for bread to rise. But the light coming through the cabin boards that morning was the color of weak tea left too long — a brown-yellow murk that meant one thing only: the high country had let go.

Monsoon water. Early by a full fortnight, and coming down at us like a debt collector.

Isandro stood at the cabin door with his hand still on my shoulder, and I'll give him this much — he didn't waste words on good morning or don't panic. He just said, "The river's up," like a man reporting a fire he'd already decided not to be surprised by.

I was on deck before my boots were properly on. The Vell had gone from a river to a rumor of a sea overnight. Where yesterday there'd been sandbars and the lazy brown crawl of low season, now there was a single broad muscle of water, flexing, carrying whole trees sideways past us like matchsticks. A dead goat went by, then half a fence, then something I chose not to identify. The rain came down in ropes.

"Kessa's teeth," I said, which is what my mother used to say when the sky broke bad, and which is as close as I come to praying.

"We can't run this," Isandro said behind me. He was looking at the water with an expression I didn't like — not fear exactly. Recognition. Like he'd seen this face before and knew its name.

"Can't run it *today*," I corrected him. "That's different."

"Maren." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something heavy. "Look at the banks."

I looked. And my stomach did what stomachs do when they know more than their owners want them to. The willows along the near bank were already underwater to their shoulders. The old ferry landing at Tarnholt — the one that floods maybe three days a year, the one my father called the river's belt buckle because when it goes under, everything above it goes under — was a set of black posts sticking out of moving water.

Three days. That's what we had. Maybe less, if the high passes were still holding snowmelt behind the rain, which they always are, which every boatwoman knows and every engineer apparently doesn't, because Isandro was doing some kind of arithmetic with his eyes and arriving at numbers I could see were wrong.

"The pass mouth," he said slowly. "If the water rises—"

"It seals." I finished it for him, because watching a man arrive at your own knowledge at his own pace is worse than useless when the sky is falling. "The lower galleries flood. Every year. By mid-season you couldn't walk into that mountain with diving bells and a bishop's blessing. We have days, engineer. Not weeks. Days."

He absorbed that. I watched him do it — watched the news go through him like a stone through a window, glass everywhere but no sound. Then he nodded once, and something in me registered, unwillingly, that here was a man who took bad news the way a keel takes a rock: hard, and without capsizing.

"Then we run it today," he said.

"Today we run it *carefully*," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is whether we're alive tonight to argue about it."

* * *

The first ten miles were the river showing off.

I won't pretend I wasn't good. I was good. The *Sable Wren* answered her helm like she knew my mind, and I read the water the way you read a letter from someone you love and distrust at once — every line meaning two things, every smooth patch hiding a lie. Where the current smoothed flat and glassy, that wasn't calm; that was the river drawing breath before it dropped something. Where the water boiled white over nothing visible, there was rock underneath wearing a coat of foam, and you gave it room. Where the current lines braided together into a single dark rope, that rope would pull you where it wanted, and you either joined it willingly or fought it and lost.

Isandro stood at my shoulder through all of it and did the most useful thing a landsman can do on a flooded river, which is nothing. He held the rail. He watched. Twice he pointed — once at a submerged fence line I'd already marked, once at a whirlpool forming off our starboard bow that I'd have seen anyway — and both times he pointed early, and both times he was right, and I hated how much I noticed that.

"You've run water before," I said, between rapids. It wasn't quite a question.

"Lakes. Canals. Lock systems." He paused. "Rivers like this? No."

"But you read it."

"I read structures." He said it simply, without false modesty, and I found I preferred that to the smooth modesty he'd worn at the checkpoint like a coat. "Water is a structure. It has loads and stresses and failure points. It's just... faster than concrete."

"Faster than concrete," I repeated. "You're a poet, you are."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, visiting a house where smiles used to live.

* * *

It was mile eleven that the river stopped showing off and started collecting debts.

The narrows at Skell's Elbow — named, according to my grandmother, for a man who lost both of them there, though she never explained how the second one went — came at us the way it always does: too fast, too narrow, with the current funneled into a single roaring throat between rock walls that had no business being so close together. In low water it's a chute. In flood it's a gun barrel, and we were the bullet.

I took us in on the green line — the seam of smoother water just off the main boil, the thread you follow when the river gives you exactly one thread to follow. The *Wren* bucked. Water came over the bow in solid sheets. I had both hands on the wheel and my whole body braced against the pitch, and I was doing fine, I was doing *fine*, right up until the river hit us with the crosscurrent.

It comes off the eastern wall at the elbow's turn — a side channel that runs high and mean in flood, slamming across the main chute at an angle, and every year it takes something. A raft. A swimmer. Once, a whole barge of timber that came apart like a dropped plate. I'd timed my run past it a hundred times. This year the flood had changed the timing, because flood water changes everything, because the river is a cheat and always has been.

The blow caught us quarter-on and shoved the *Wren*'s nose toward the western wall, and I hauled the wheel over and the engine screamed and for one long moment we hung there, broadside to the current, the worst place a boat can be, the place boats go to die —

— and then Isandro was beside me, not touching the wheel, never touching the wheel, but shouting over the roar: "Throttle down! Half! Give her half!"

"She'll broach!"

"She's broaching NOW — cut the power and let the current take the stern, swing her nose INTO it — trust the water to turn you!"

Every instinct I had said fight. His voice had the flat certainty of a man reading figures off a page, and God help me, I was out of better ideas, so I did it. Throttle to half. Wheel hard over the other way. And the river — the cheating, murderous river — did exactly what he said it would. The current took our stern and swung it, and the *Wren* came around nose-first into the boil like a horse brought back under rein, and we shot through the rest of the elbow riding the green line as if we'd planned it.

We came out the far side into comparatively gentle water, and I discovered I was shaking, and that Isandro was already gone below.

"Where are you going?" I shouted after him.

"To find out why she handled wrong," he called back. "She shouldn't have come around that hard. She was fighting herself."

* * *

He found it in the bilge, forty minutes later, and I will say for the record that I felt it before he found it. Boatwomen feel things in their hulls the way mothers feel things in their children — a wrongness in the vibration, a note off-key in the engine's song. For the last hour the *Wren* had been singing flat, and I'd been telling myself it was debris fouling the screw.

It wasn't debris.

Isandro came up from the hold with a lantern in one hand and his sleeve soaked to the shoulder, and his face told me before his mouth did.

"How bad?"

"There's a crack. Port side, on the garboard strake, just forward of the keel joint." He set the lantern down and crouched, drawing on the deck boards with his finger — quick, sure strokes, the shape of a hull seen from inside. "Maybe two feet long. Hairline at the ends, open in the middle — wide enough to fit two fingers. It's weeping, not gushing. Yet."

"Yet," I said.

"Every mile of this water is working it wider. The planking's flexing with each wave strike. It's an old crack — see the staining? — that the flood stress has opened." He looked up at me, and there was something almost apologetic in it. "Your boat's tired, Maren. She's been patched well, but she's been patched often."

"Don't talk about her like that."

"I'm sorry. It's how I talk about things I'm trying to save."

And there it was again — that plainness. At the checkpoint he'd lied to company men like breathing, silk-smooth, and I'd watched him do it and thought: *here is a man made entirely of doors that close.* But below decks, with bilge water on his sleeves, he talked straight. I didn't know yet what to make of a man who was honest about boats and lies about everything else. I'm not sure I ever fully worked it out. But I noticed it. I want that on the record. I noticed.

"So we beach her," I said. "Haul out at Corren's Staithe, patch proper, lose two days."

"Two days puts the pass beyond reach?" He didn't ask it as a question. He'd already done the arithmetic.

"Corren's is four miles back the way we came, against this current, in a leaking boat. There's nothing ahead until Hallow Ford, and Hallow Ford's a mudbank with a chapel on it." I rubbed my face. Rain, bilge smell, and the particular exhaustion of a woman whose day has gone entirely to plan except for the parts that matter. "Or we push on and hope the crack holds."

"It won't hold," Isandro said. "But it can be made to hold. Do you have canvas? Tar? Oakum?"

"I have tar. I have canvas. I have oakum enough to caulk a cathedral, because my father believed in preparing for disasters the way other men believe in saints." I was already moving toward the stores locker. "What's your scheme, engineer?"

"Underwater patch. Canvas saturated in tar, pressed against the crack from outside, weighted and bound. It'll hold if the water pressure holds it on — and the faster we go, the harder it presses. Which means—" and here he almost smiled again, that visiting ghost, "—the answer to a leaking boat is to go faster, not slower. Counterintuitive. But the physics is friendly for once."

"And who's putting this miracle patch on the outside of a hull in flood water?"

Silence. The kind of silence that has a job offer in it.

"No," I said.

"The water's cold but not killing-cold. You need someone small enough to work under the counter and strong enough to hold position against the current. Someone who can tie off underwater and knows this boat's lines blind." He shrugged. "I can do the engineering. I can't do the swimming. That part has to be you."

"You've known me five days."

"And in five days I've learned you don't ask people to do anything you wouldn't do yourself, which is why you're not asking me to dive, and why I'm not offering to." He met my eyes. "I'm asking you to let me keep you alive while you do it. Rope harness, tethered to the mast step, me on the line. If you signal twice, I pull. No thinking. No discussion. I pull."

I should have argued. A sensible woman would have argued, or charged him more, or turned the boat around and written the whole mad business off as a fever dream bought from a dying prospector. But the pass was sealing, my brother was somewhere behind thirty years of company paperwork, and this stranger with his closed doors and his honest hands had just read my boat's sickness in forty minutes flat.

"All right," I said. "But if I drown, I'm haunting you specifically. Not the company. You."

"Noted," he said gravely. "I'd deserve it."

* * *

Diving a hull in flood water is exactly as pleasant as it sounds, which is to say it is not, and I will spare you the details except for these: the water was colder than a creditor's heart; the current tried to peel me off the hull twice; and the tar-soaked canvas, once pressed home over that ugly little crack, gripped the planking like a leech with a grievance.

What I remember most isn't the cold. It's the line going taut against my harness — once, when the current grabbed me — and then easing immediately, controlled, metered, Isandro paying out and taking up slack with the touch of a man who understood exactly how much force a human body could take and refused to exceed it by one ounce. He couldn't see me down there. He was working blind, off feel alone, reading the line the way I read the river. And he got it right. Every time.

When I came up, gasping and streaming and foul-tempered, he hauled me aboard with both hands under my arms, set me on the deck boards, and put a blanket around my shoulders before he said a single word.

"Well?" he asked.

"Held," I managed, through chattering teeth. "She's tight. Or tight enough."

"Tight enough," he agreed, and sat back on his heels, and for a moment he looked — I don't know. Lighter. As if some weight had shifted a few inches, not gone, but moved to a pocket where it rode easier.

I drank the tea he made — strong, scalding, too much sugar, the tea of a man who'd learned cooking from manuals — and I studied him over the rim of the cup while he pretended not to notice. Rain drummed the canopy. The *Wren* ran sweet beneath us, her song back on key, the patched hull pressing itself tighter with every mile.

Here's the thing I didn't say to him, and I'm saying it now because somebody ought to have said it to his face eventually: competence is a kind of honesty. A man can lie with his mouth all day long, but his hands tell the truth, and Isandro's hands had spent that afternoon telling me the truth about who he was — careful, exact, stubbornly attentive to things everyone else walks past. Whatever he was running from, whatever doors he kept shut, the man himself, the working core of him, was sound.

I trusted the hands. I told myself that was enough. I told myself a lot of things that week.

"Get some sleep," I told him instead, nodding at the cabin. "I'll take her till dawn."

"You should sleep. You dove in flood water."

"And you'll stand watch on a river you've admitted you can't read, on a boat you've known less time than I've known you? Get below, engineer. That's not a request, it's a crew assignment. Congratulations. You're crew."

He hesitated — and then, for the first time since Harrow's Bend, he laughed. Short, rusty, startled out of him like a bird from cover, as if he'd surprised himself by owning such a thing. "Aye, Captain," he said, and went below.

I took the wheel and the night and the rising river, and I steered us north through the rain toward the mountain that had eaten my brother, with a patched hull, a borrowed engineer, and a feeling in my chest I refused to name because naming things gives them power.

Near midnight, the rain thickened, and the wind swung round, and far upstream — so far it might have been imagination — I saw a light. Not lightning. Lightning doesn't travel *with* you, keeping pace, mile after mile, always the same distance behind.

Somebody else was running the flood.

And they were following us.
