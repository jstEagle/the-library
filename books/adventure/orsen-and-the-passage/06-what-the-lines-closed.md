## 6. What the Lines Closed

Sleep doesn't come to me on boats. It never has. There's something about the way a hull breathes against the water—the small creaks, the slap of current against planking—that sounds too much like a mountain settling, and I have heard a mountain settle exactly once in my life, and I remember every syllable of it.

So I lay awake in the narrow bunk below decks while Maren kept the night watch above, and the river did its patient work of wearing me down, and eventually the memory came for me the way it always does: not all at once, but in pieces, like water finding cracks.

* * *

Twenty years ago, in the spring of my twenty-ninth year, I was the youngest assistant surveyor the Northern Rail Company had ever posted to a divisional office. I was proud of that. I want you to understand that about me first, because everything else follows from it: I was *proud*. Proud of my instruments, my neat hand, my reputation for precision. Men trusted my figures. That was the whole architecture of my early life—men trusting my figures—and I built it carefully, plank by plank, the way you'd build a boat, without ever asking what waters it would have to cross.

Vellmark Pass was supposed to be the crown of the northern extension. Fourteen miles of tunnel and cut through the spine of the mountains, linking the coast to the interior valleys, cutting freight time by eleven days. The company had been driving the heading from both ends for six years when I arrived. Eleven hundred men worked inside that mountain on any given shift. They called the pass *the Throat*, because the air came out of it warm and wet like breath, and because—though nobody said this part aloud—it was the kind of place that could swallow you.

My job was ordinary. Verification surveys. I checked the alignment of the completed galleries against the original plans, took instrument readings at the ventilation shafts, certified the drainage grades. Honest work. The kind of work where if you do it right, nobody ever learns your name.

I learned later that this is precisely why they chose me.

* * *

It started with a drainage report. The eastern galleries had been weeping water all winter—not dangerously, not yet, just a dampness in the rock that the old-timers dismissed with shrugs. *Mountain's always been wet there,* they said. But the seepage was coming through the fault zone, and the fault zone ran through a stratum of shale that turned to soup when it got wet, and I knew this because I had cored it myself, kneeling in the muck with my sample tubes, watching the material come up soft as old bread.

I wrote the report honestly. That's the detail that has kept me awake for twenty years—I wrote it *honestly*. I documented the saturation levels, flagged the shale, recommended reduced blasting charges and additional timbering through the eastern section. I signed it. I dated it. I submitted it up the chain the way a good engineer does.

Three days later, Divisional Chief Aldous Vey summoned me to his office. Vey was a big man gone soft at the edges, the kind of executive who wore his decades of service like armor and his cufflinks like medals. He had a bottle open on the credenza at ten in the morning, which I understood even then to be a message.

"Sit down, Iversen."

I sat.

He slid my report across the desk. My own handwriting looked strange to me, seen from the wrong side of a superior's desk—smaller somehow, more tentative.

"Good work," he said. "Thorough." He tapped the page. "But you've made an error here."

"I don't believe I have, sir."

"No," he agreed pleasantly, "you haven't. That's the error." He leaned back. "The eastern section opens to freight traffic in five weeks. The minister is coming for the ceremony. Do you know what it costs to delay an opening? Not in money—in *confidence*. You don't put doubt into a mountain, son. Doubt spreads. Doubt gets into the newspapers, and then it gets into the bond markets, and then men who lent us four million crowns start asking questions that don't have tidy answers."

"The shale won't hold under full traffic load," I said. "Not without the timbering. It's not doubt, sir. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic." He smiled at me then, and I have spent twenty years trying to forget that smile, and twenty years failing, because the worst thing about it was how *kind* it was. He wasn't a monster. That's what nobody understands about how these things happen. He was tired, and he was loyal to the company the way a man is loyal to his own body, and he genuinely believed—perhaps he even did believe it—that the risk was acceptable. Mountains are dangerous. Railways are dangerous. Everything worth building is dangerous, and the men who dig tunnels know what they've signed up for.

Except they hadn't signed up for what I was about to sign away.

"There's a revision procedure," Vey said. "Standard form. You re-run your figures with the corrected load assumptions—the ones the design office issued in the autumn, before your posting. You'll find they accommodate the conditions adequately." He pushed a folder across the desk. Inside were figures I recognized. Figures that had been superseded. Figures that assumed dry rock.

"And if I find they don't?"

The kindness left his face then, but only partially, the way fog lifts off a valley floor—one corner first, the rest lingering. "Then I'll be disappointed," he said, "and you'll be reassigned to the coastal gauge survey, which is a two-man post in a town with one road, and your career will consist of measuring culverts until you retire or drink yourself to death, whichever arrives first. Whereas if you find the figures adequate—" he refilled his glass, unhurried, "—there's a senior verification post opening in the capital next year. Youngest man ever to hold it. Your name would be on the certification itself. Isandro Iversen, Principal Certifying Engineer. Twenty-nine years old. Think about what that does for a man's life."

I thought about it. God help me, I thought about it for perhaps ninety seconds, and then I said, "I'll re-run the figures."

Here is the lie I told myself afterward, the one I carried like a forged permit in my chest for two decades: *I didn't falsify anything. I just used the approved assumptions.* As if the numbers were weather, something that happened to me rather than something I chose. But a survey is a choice. Every figure you certify is a sentence you write about the future, and I wrote one that said *this mountain will hold*, knowing the mountain had already begun to disagree.

* * *

The eastern section opened on schedule. There was a brass band. The minister wore a grey coat and shook hands with foremen whose palms were still calloused from the rock, and I stood three rows back in my new dress uniform and watched my signature travel upward through the ranks like smoke.

They gave me the promotion eleven months later. By then the seepage had worsened—I know because I read the maintenance logs, quietly, from the capital, the way a man reads letters from a place he's fled. The eastern drainage pumps were running double shifts. Requests for additional timbering went up the chain twice and came back denied twice, marked *deferred pending budget review*.

And then, on the ninth of March, at fourteen minutes past three in the afternoon, the mountain answered the question everyone had been careful not to ask.

* * *

I wasn't there. That's another piece of the arithmetic I can't make come out clean. If I had been there, I might have died with them, and there are nights when that seems like the cleaner ledger. Instead I was four hundred miles south, in the capital, in a committee room with walnut paneling, presenting a paper on standardization of rail gauges, when a clerk came in with a telegram and handed it to the chairman, and I watched the chairman's face do something complicated and terrible, and I knew—before he read it aloud, before anyone said the word *Vellmark*—exactly what it said.

Eleven men. A maintenance crew, working the eastern drainage gallery on the day shift. The shale let go at the fault zone, and eight thousand tons of it came down in ninety seconds, sealing the gallery behind them. No explosion, no warning anyone could act on. Just the sound—which the survivors in the western sections described, in the inquiry testimony I read forty times, as *the mountain clearing its throat*.

They sealed the opposite end within the week. Officially, for safety. Unofficially—for what? Recovery was declared impracticable. The bodies were left where they fell. The company paid settlements to the families, quiet ones, calculated to the crown, and the pass was closed, and the maps were revised, and the whole fourteen miles became a blank space on the network diagram with a hatched line through it: *CLOSED — STRUCTURAL.*

My name was on the original certification. Nobody ever said this to me directly. Nobody had to. Vey sent a personal letter expressing confidence in my continued service. The inquiry found the collapse attributable to "unforeseeable geological conditions." Unforeseeable. I had foreseen them in writing, in triplicate, and the foreseeing was locked in a divisional archive under a seal I no longer had the rank to break.

That was the trade, though I never signed anything so crude as a bargain. The promotion bought my silence; my silence bought their story; their story buried eleven men under a phrase. And I took the salary, and the office with the window, and eventually the habit of moving—because here is the thing nobody tells you about guilt: it doesn't pin you down. It *chases* you. It makes a man restless. I have lived in nine towns in nineteen years and I have slept badly in all of them, and every time a new employer asked for my history I gave them a smoother version, and every smooth version cost me something I couldn't itemize.

I kept the reports. That's the final absurdity, the one Maren would laugh at if she knew—the engineer who falsified the survey couldn't bring himself to destroy the evidence. Somewhere in my satchel, between the working maps and the forged permits, sits a copy of my honest report, the first one, the one with the true figures. I should have burned it years ago. I tell myself I keep it in case there's ever an inquiry. But inquiries need someone willing to testify, and I have been running from that word—*testify*—for twenty years, the way other men run from creditors.

* * *

The boat knocked against something—a log, a snag—and I came back to the present with my heart going hard, lying in the dark listening to Maren's boots move overhead, slow and steady, a woman pacing her grief the way she paced her deck.

Her brother had gone into those mountains three years ago. I had known it since the dock, since she studied my map with eyes that missed nothing. And somewhere in the dark of the hull, doing the arithmetic I could never finish, a number kept surfacing that I refused to look at directly: the number of names on the settlement rolls from Vellmark, the ones I had memorized against my will during the inquiry years, and whether any name on Maren's family papers might match a name I already knew.

The river ran. The hull breathed. And far upstream, past the checkpoints and the rising water, the Throat waited with its eleven dead—or whatever the truth was, because lately, reading Oskar's fevered chart, I had begun to notice things on it that shouldn't be there. Markings in fresh ink. Notes in a hand that wasn't Oskar's.

Someone, it seemed, had been adding to that map recently.

And the newest mark sat exactly where the air shaft used to be.
