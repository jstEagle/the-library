## 7. The Leaking Hold

The boathouse at Sarnholt had been built by optimists. That was my first thought, poling the *Vela* through its half-drowned doors while the rain came down like the sky had a grudge. The roof sagged in the middle like a tired back. The floor was a hand's depth under brown water. Somebody's old nets hung from the rafters, furred with mold, and a family of rats watched us come in with the calm of landlords who'd seen worse tenants.

"Home sweet ruin," I said.

Isandro didn't answer. He was already at the rail, looking down into the bilge like a man checking on a sick relative he suspected of lying about how sick.

I'd known the hull was cracked since First Water. You know it in your hands before you know it anywhere else—the wheel goes light, the boat answers a half-beat late, like a horse favoring a leg. What I hadn't known was how bad. When we grounded her on the boathouse ramp and pumped her out, the crack showed itself along the garboard seam, a black grin two feet long, weeping river water even in a dry bilge. Another day of that rapids running and we'd have been swimming with our boots on.

"Two hours to patch," Isandro said, crouching, running his thumb along the seam. "Three if the wood's rotten behind it."

"It's not rotten. I keep my boat."

"I didn't mean—" He stopped. He'd developed a habit of stopping sentences halfway, like a man stepping back from a ledge. "It's good wood. Whoever built her knew what they were doing."

"My father built her." I let that sit. He nodded, and I watched him decide not to say *I'm sorry*, which was the first thing I'd liked about him. The river was full of men who said *I'm sorry* the way other men said *move along*. Isandro's sorries, when they came, cost him something. I'd seen it in his face at the checkpoint, after he'd lied to those company men so smoothly it had made my skin crawl. He'd looked like he'd bitten tinfoil.

We worked. That's the honest thing to say about the night, before it stopped being honest: we worked. He had a way with a seam that surprised me—engineer's hands, precise, patient, the hands of a man who'd spent years making things fit that had no business fitting. He mixed pitch and resin in a rusted tin while I dried the planking with a burner flame, and the smell of hot pitch filled the boathouse until it was the only smell in the world, and I could almost pretend the water outside was just water, and the man across the boat was just a passenger, and the name I'd seen on that manifest was just a name.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The night had an order to it, and the order matters.

* * *

The first hour was pitch and fire and not talking. The second hour was talking, because the rain on the roof was so loud that silence felt like shouting.

"Where did you learn boats?" Isandro asked. He was holding the flame while I worked the resin into the seam, and he held it steady, which I'll grant him.

"Same place everyone on this river learns. In the water, usually." I blew a strand of hair off my face. "My mother ran cargo. My father ran cargo. I ran cargo. The Vela was supposed to go to my brother after me."

"Was?"

The word came out of him too fast, and I heard it land wrong, and I watched him hear it land wrong.

"Is," I said. "He'd have taken her. He liked her better than me, anyway. She never argued with him." I kept my eyes on the seam. "He went upriver three years ago. Vellmark direction. Looking for what everyone looks for up there."

"And?"

"And nothing. That's the end of the story so far. That's why it's a bad story." I took the flame from him, maybe harder than I needed to. "Your turn. Why does an engineer with a forged permit know the Vellmark lines better than the pilots who used to run them?"

He was quiet long enough that the burner hissed twice. "I worked on the surveys," he said finally. "Years ago. Before the closure."

"Before the collapse, you mean."

"Before the collapse," he agreed, and his voice did something I'd not heard it do before—it went flat, the way water goes flat just before it goes over the edge. "The wood behind this seam is sound. Your father knew what he was doing."

And that was it. That was the whole of the second hour. He gave me a plank of the truth and called it the tree.

I want to be fair to myself here. I want to say I saw through him, that some boatwoman's instinct smelled the rot behind his sound seam. But the truth is that I was tired, and my hands were burned in two places, and the rain was coming through the roof in six places, and a man who tells you a piece of the truth is more dangerous than a man who tells you none, because he buys your trust at a discount and you let him.

I let him.

* * *

By the third hour the patch was holding. We'd set a backing plank over the seam, bolted and pitched, ugly as a scar and about as permanent. Isandro lay on his back in the bilge with his arms up inside the hull, torquing the last bolt, and I sat on the ramp with the manifest.

I should explain the manifest. It came with the boat, the way rust comes with a nail. My father kept every paper the *Vela* ever generated—bills of lading going back thirty years, toll receipts, crew lists, cargo manifests from every port from the delta to the headwaters. I'd been through them a hundred times since Tor went missing. Not looking for anything, exactly. Looking is when you think a thing might be there. This was something else. This was the reading of a name over and over the way you press a bruise—not because it might tell you something, but because it's the only part of him you can still hold.

Tor Iversen—no. Tor Voss. My brother's name was Tor Voss, and I'll thank you to keep the two names straight, because by the end of that night I couldn't, and that's a thing I don't forgive easily.

The manifest was from Hessel Port, dated the spring Tor went upriver. He'd signed on as deckhand on a supply barge, one of the last runs before the company closed the upper water to civilian traffic. His name was on the crew list in his own hand, that looping, impatient hand of his, the *T* crossed like a man in a hurry. I'd read it so many times I could draw it blind.

The boathouse was dim. I'd one lantern lit, and the rain had put a skin of water over the ramp, and the manifest was soft as cloth from years of handling. I sat with it in my lap and read the name, and pressed the bruise, and the river ticked against the hull, and I did not hear Isandro come up out of the bilge.

What I heard instead was his breath change.

You learn to hear breath, on a boat. Wind in the rigging, water under the hull, a man's breathing two feet away in the dark—these are instruments, all of them, and they don't lie the way faces lie. His breath stopped. Then it started again, wrong, shallow, the breathing of a man reading something.

I looked up. He was standing over me. He hadn't meant to be standing over me; he'd meant, I think, to walk past to the fire, but his eyes had gone to the paper in my lap the way a man's eyes go to a wound, and now he was frozen there, and his face was doing something complicated that I had three full seconds to read.

And here is the thing I will carry to my grave, whatever grave the river leaves me: in those three seconds, I thought he was looking at my brother's name with pity. That's what I told myself. Pity, or awkwardness, the ordinary discomfort of a stranger who's stumbled onto a grief. It was only later—later, when the pieces were in my hands and refused to fit any other way—that I understood what I'd actually seen. Recognition. The face of a man who has met a name before and hoped never to meet it again.

"Give me that."

I didn't shout it. I want that recorded too. I stood up slowly, and the manifest hung from my hand, and the lantern threw both our shadows huge up into the rotten rafters, and I said, quiet, in the voice I use on drunks and company men: "Give me an answer instead. Why did my brother's name stop your breath, Isandro Iversen? And don't tell me it didn't. I've read water for twenty years. I can read a man's throat."

"I—" He put out a hand, not for the paper, just out, open, the way you'd calm a dog. Then he thought better of the gesture and took it back, which was the smartest thing he did all night. "I knew the name. From the surveys. Vellmark was a company project. There were manifests. Crew lists for the supply runs. His barge—"

"The *Kestrel*."

"The *Kestrel*," he said, and closed his eyes, and I understood he'd known the name of the boat too, and had been hoping I wouldn't say it.

The rain filled the silence. It was a warm rain, monsoon rain, the kind that doesn't fall so much as arrive, all at once, like an army. Somewhere out in the flooded fields a bird was making a sound like a hinge.

"Sit down," I said. He sat. On the ramp, in the water, not caring, which told me something about how bad he thought this was going to be. "You're going to tell me what the company manifests said about the *Kestrel*. All of it. And then you're going to tell me why you didn't say any of it three days ago, when I told you my brother went up that river and didn't come back, and you sat there and let me sign on a stranger with a forged permit because he was the only man who could read the pass."

"Maren."

"You'll notice I'm not asking nicely. I've been nice. I fed you. I let you patch my boat. I let you lie to those company men with your smooth engineer's face and I didn't ask what a man with clean hands is doing with a forged permit, and I let you say *before the collapse* in that flat voice like you were reading a freight schedule. So no. Not nicely."

He looked at the fire. He looked at the water coming through the roof. He looked at everything in the boathouse except me and the paper, and then, finally, he looked at the paper, and I watched him make a decision, and I want to be honest: I believed, in that moment, that he was going to tell me everything. I believed it the way you believe in a bridge as you step onto it.

"The *Kestrel* was on the last run into the pass before the closure," he said. "Your brother was listed as deckhand. The barge went in with a cargo of timber props and blasting powder—mine, my survey team's, for the shoring work. It never came out. The collapse came three days later. The company listed the *Kestrel* as lost with the mountain." His voice stayed flat, but his hands didn't; they were turning over each other in his lap like something trying to escape. "I read the crew lists. I read all of them. I was twenty-six years old and I read them and then I signed the closure report anyway."

"Signed it."

"The report that certified the pass as unworkable. That closed it. Sealed it." He swallowed. "There were men inside when it came down. Eleven on the work crews. The company said there was no way to know if anyone survived, and no safe way to dig, and the report said the cost of recovery exceeded—" He stopped. "The report said a lot of things. I wrote some of them."

The bird outside made its hinge sound again. My hands were steady, which surprised me. I'd have expected them to shake. But grief, real grief, the old kind that's been sitting in you for three years like silt, doesn't shake you when it's finally touched. It just settles.

"Tor was inside," I said. "When it came down."

"I don't know. The *Kestrel* went in. The mountain came down. Whether he was in the galleries or back at the staging camp—" He spread his hands. Empty hands. "I don't know. Nobody knows. That's what the report was *for*, Maren. It was for making sure nobody would ever know."

"And you're telling me this now because you got caught. Not because you grew a spine in the last three days. Because I caught you looking at his name."

"Yes," he said. And then, because he was apparently determined to make it worse: "That's not all of it."

"Of course it isn't."

"There are things I can't tell you yet. Not won't. Can't." He was leaning forward now, elbows on knees, and his voice had lost the flatness and found something underneath it that sounded almost like the man I'd watched crank a pump for six hours without complaint. "If I tell you all of it now, in this boathouse, tonight, you'll turn this boat around. And the thing I'm carrying up this river is the only chance your brother's name ever gets out of that mountain with the truth attached to it. I need you to keep poling north for one more day. Then I'll tell you the rest. On my mother's life, I'll tell you the rest."

I looked at him for a long time. The lantern guttered and steadied. The water under the ramp rose a finger's width, patient, the way the monsoon does everything.

"Here's what I know about you now, Isandro," I said. "You helped close that mountain. You knew my brother's name and let me carry you toward it in ignorance. And you're still lying to me, right now, tonight, with your *can't* and your *one more day*—because a man who can't tell the truth is a man who's chosen not to, and dressed the choice in a coat of necessity." I folded the manifest and put it inside my coat, against my chest, where the important papers ride. "But you told me this much without me breaking your fingers, and you told it straight, and you didn't say *I'm sorry*, which I notice you were dying to say. So we go north one more day."

He let out a breath.

"But understand the arithmetic," I said. "Every day from here, the price of the rest of the truth goes up. And I collect."

He nodded. He picked up the pitch tin and carried it to the fire like a man going to confession, and I sat back down on the ramp with my brother's name against my heart, and I told myself the worst was behind us.

I told myself a lot of things that night. The rain told me better. It kept coming down on that rotten roof like a knock at a door—patient, and repeated, and certain that someone inside was going to have to answer it eventually.

* * *

I woke before dawn to the sound of the bilge pump running and found him already at it, working in the dark, keeping my boat alive. That should have softened me. It did soften me, a little, and I hated it.

Then he handed me his satchel to hold while he re-coiled the pump line—handed it to me without thinking, the reflex of a man who was starting to trust me—and I felt inside it, in the two seconds he had his back turned, the edge of a folded paper that was not a map.

It was a ledger page. Old. And written across the top of it, in a hand I now knew better than my own heartbeat, was a heading I'd read a hundred times on a hundred manifests:

*Northern Rail Company — Vellmark Survey — Personnel.*

My brother's name was on it. So were eleven others. And next to each name, in fresh ink—ink that had not faded in twenty years, ink that had been written there recently, by him—someone had begun numbering the men. One through eleven.

And after eleven, the numbering had not stopped.
