## 8. River Ghosts

The rain had followed us upriver for two days, patient as a debt collector, and by the third morning I'd stopped pretending it wasn't going to catch us.

Maren had tied the boat off at a fuel depot half a day below the Vellmark confluence—a rickety platform of blackened pilings where a man named Hessel sold diesel out of rusted drums and asked no questions, which in my experience meant he asked them of someone else. She went ashore to haggle. I stayed aboard with the map case between my knees like a sleeping animal I was afraid to wake.

I should have spent those hours studying Oskar's chart. Instead I spent them listening to the rain on the canopy and doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. Two men had boarded at the checkpoint four days ago. Company men—Voss with his neat gray suit that refused to wrinkle no matter what the river did to it, and Brandt, the big one, who said almost nothing and looked at everything. They'd asked about a stolen map. I'd lied. It had come out of me smooth as water off oilcloth, and I'd hated myself for it in the specific way you hate a scar: not because it's ugly, but because it proves something healed wrong.

They'd disembarked at the next lock. That was the part that bothered me. Men like Voss don't get off a boat going the direction they want to go unless they already know another way there.

The rain thickened. Downriver, around the last bend, I heard an engine—not Maren's kind of engine, which coughed and complained like an old man climbing stairs, but something bigger, something with money in it. A company launch, twin-screw, running against the current like the current owed it deference.

I put the map case under the floorboards before I'd fully decided to. My hands knew the work. Twenty years of hiding things will teach your hands things your mind never agreed to.

* * *

Maren came back down the plank with two drums' worth of fuel secured and a look on her face that said Hessel had mentioned visitors. She was halfway across the deck when she saw my face and stopped.

"No," she said.

"They're coming upriver."

"How far?"

"Ten minutes. Maybe less."

She stood very still, rain running off her hood, and I watched her make the calculation I was making—the same one, but from the other side of the ledger. For her, turning back meant losing her brother's last trace. For me, it meant losing considerably more than that, though at that moment I'd have traded all of it for her safety and told myself later it was noble rather than convenient.

"There's a side channel," she said finally. "Behind the depot. It's shallow and it stinks and it goes nowhere useful, but it goes somewhere."

"Then we go nowhere useful."

"That's the spirit."

We were casting off when the launch came around the bend. No point pretending we hadn't seen each other; the river doesn't allow for subtlety. Voss stood at the rail in his gray suit, dry somehow, impossibly dry, holding a leather folder the way a priest holds scripture. Brandt was at the wheel. The launch swung alongside and Voss stepped across without asking permission, landing on our deck light as a rumor.

"Engineer Iversen." He used the title like a hook. "Small river."

"Gets smaller every year," I said. "Dredging budgets being what they are."

He smiled. He had a good smile, the kind they must teach at whatever academy produces men like him, and I felt the old familiar sickness of recognizing craft in someone whose craft is you. He opened the folder.

"A warrant," he said. "For the recovery of stolen property. Northern Rail Company documents, survey materials, proprietary charts. Taken from the estate of one Oskar Reinholt, prospector, deceased as of—" he consulted a paper with theatrical care—"eleven days ago. Drowned, they say. In three inches of water, in his own room, in a town with two doctors." He looked up. "The company takes an interest in its property. Even after death. Especially after death."

I kept my face doing nothing, which is the hardest work I know. Behind Voss, Brandt hadn't moved except his eyes, which were cataloguing our boat the way an assessor catalogues a dead man's goods.

"I'm sorry for the man," I said. "Did I know him?"

"You were seen drinking with him. Twice. In the Salt Anchor, in Corren's Landing." Voss produced a photograph then, and this was the thing I'll remember longest, because I had braced for the warrant and the questions and even the threat, and I had not braced for the photograph.

Oskar, taken from above and slightly behind, unaware. His gray beard uncombed, his coat collar up against some cold I could still feel in my own bones from that night in the tavern. He was leaning over a table toward someone just out of frame, and his hand was flat on the wood, fingers spread, the way a man's hand spreads when he's pushing something across it. Pushing it toward me.

"He came to you," Voss said. Not a question anymore. "A dying man with a dying obsession. They always find an engineer. Someone who can read the lines. Someone who understands what a closed pass is worth." He tucked the photograph away with the tenderness of a man putting away a knife. "The company would very much like to know what he gave you."

"The company would very much like a lot of things," I said. "That's why it employs you."

Something moved behind Voss's smile—not anger, worse, professional appreciation. "You know what I admire about you, Iversen? Your file says you left the company under honorable circumstances. Promotion, transfer, quiet exit. But men who leave honorably don't flinch when their old employer says hello." He glanced past me at Maren. "Your boatwoman has a brother. Tomas. Went into the pass three years back on a supply run and never came out. Interesting coincidence, a boatwoman with pass grief picking up a passenger bound for the pass. Almost like a plan."

Beside me, Maren had gone very quiet, and I understood that Voss had just done more damage in ten seconds than I'd managed to avoid in twenty years. He'd made her visible to them. Whatever happened now, they would remember her name.

"We're carrying grain," Maren said flatly. "You're welcome to search the hold."

"Oh, we will," Voss said pleasantly. "Eventually. Today we're just saying hello. Reestablishing acquaintance." He stepped back to the rail, paused. "One more thing, Engineer. Oskar Reinholt's death is being written up as misadventure. It's a generous write-up. It requires only one thing to stay true—that no chart of the Vellmark Pass surfaces anywhere. Because if it does, the story changes. The story becomes: a man was killed for a piece of paper, and everyone who touched that paper becomes either a witness or an accomplice. And witnesses—" he tapped the folder against his palm—"witnesses have such short lives on this river."

Then he was gone, across the gap, and the launch turned, and the sound of its engines faded upriver ahead of us, which was the worst detail of all. They were going where we were going, and they would arrive first.

* * *

Maren didn't speak until we were through the side channel and the depot was a smudge behind us. Then she cut the engine and let us drift in the drowned-farm silence, water standing in the fields like grey glass, fence posts poking through it marking graves of fences.

"Tomas," she said.

"I never told them about him."

"You didn't have to. He's in the manifests. He's in the missing-persons rolls. They've known about him since the day he disappeared—they just never cared until caring became useful." She turned and looked at me, and the rain had gotten into her hair and she didn't bother with it. "What did he mean, witnesses have short lives?"

I looked at the floorboards, under which the map case sat like a heart I was keeping alive by lying to it.

"He means Oskar didn't drown," I said. "Three inches of water. Two doctors in town. A man who'd survived thirty years of backcountry alone, and he drowns in his bed the week after he trades away the only valuable thing he owned." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it goes when the alternative is shaking. "They wrote it up as misadventure because writing it up as murder invites questions. Questions invite attention. And the whole architecture of the Northern Rail Company—the pensions, the promotions, the quiet exits—it runs on nobody asking questions."

"You think they killed him."

"I don't think anything. I know how reports get written. I used to write them." I made myself look at her. "Oskar gave me that map because he wanted the pass opened before he died. He died anyway. Which means the map is the only thing left of him that wants anything, and the company needs it destroyed so badly they'll kill for it twice."

Maren was silent a long moment. Water dripped off the canopy. Somewhere in the flooded fields a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"So we're not carrying a map," she said slowly. "We're carrying a corpse. Everything he was. And they want to bury it again."

"Yes."

"And us."

"If they can. If it's tidy. Bodies in floodwater are very tidy, Maren. The river writes its own misadventure reports."

I expected fear in her face, or anger, or the cold arithmetic of a woman recalculating whether a passenger was worth the cargo. What I saw instead took me a moment to recognize, because I'd only ever seen it aimed at other people: resolve. She untied her hood and shook the rain out of it, briskly, like settling an account.

"My brother went into that pass," she said. "Either he's dead in there, or he isn't, and both of those facts belong to me, not to a company that closes doors and calls it engineering. So here's what happens now. We stop sneaking. We stop drifting. We run for the pass like the water's behind us—because it is, and so are they." She put her hand on the throttle. "And Isandro—if they come aboard again, you give me the map case, and you get in the water if you have to. You swim. You're worth more wet than dead."

"That's the first kind thing anyone's said to me in years."

"It wasn't kind. Dead men can't read drawings." She started the engine, and it caught on the second try, complaining, faithful. "Now show me Oskar's channel marks. If Voss is running the main line, we take the old barge cuts, and we arrive at that lock tired, filthy, and alive."

I knelt and lifted the floorboard, and brought the map out into the daylight for the first time since the tavern, and unrolled it between us on the wet deck. Oskar's hand was shaky but sure—every mark placed by a man who had walked the ground he drew. And there, in the margin, in ink browner than the rest, a note I hadn't been able to read by lamplight and could read now:

*They sealed eleven. Ask Iversen why twelve names were signed.*

I stared at it. Twelve. The report bore eleven names—I had copied them myself, one by one, into the document that closed the mountain. Eleven workers accounted for at shift's end. Eleven coffins' worth of closure. But Oskar, who had prospected those slopes for thirty years, who had drunk with the crews, who had counted heads at the tavern gates—Oskar said twelve.

"Maren," I said, and my voice came out strange, belonging to a younger man, a man standing in a survey office twenty years ago watching a foreman cross out a line and rewrite a number. "There were supposed to be twelve names on that report."

She looked at the marginal note, then at me, and the rain kept falling on the drowned fields, and somewhere upriver a company launch was waiting at a lock for us to arrive, and none of it mattered as much as the number twelve sitting on the deck between us like a stone pulled from a grave.

"Then who's the twelfth?" she asked.

I had spent twenty years not answering that question. Every forged permit, every town I'd left at dawn, every ledger I'd checked and burned—it had all been one long refusal to answer it. And now the answer was coming up the river behind us, wearing a gray suit, carrying a folder full of photographs of dead men.

"I don't know yet," I said. "But I think Oskar did. And I think that's why he's dead."

Maren pushed the throttle forward, and the old boat leaned into the current, and we ran for the pass with the rain behind us and the twelfth name ahead—while, at the lock above, I knew, a man in a dry gray suit was opening his folder again, and this time it would not be a social call.
