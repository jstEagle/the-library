## 10. Confession in the Rain

The rain came the way debts do — all at once, and with interest.

We'd been running the drowned farmlands for two days by then, threading the boat through what used to be barley fields and now belonged to the river again. The water was the color of strong tea and moving like it had somewhere important to be. When the sky finally split open, it didn't rain so much as relocate an entire sea onto our heads.

Maren found us a mudbank — if you can call a shelf of collapsing earth with three willows on it a harbor — and we dragged the boat up as far as her winch would allow, then strung the tarp between two trunks and crawled underneath like animals. Which, I suppose, is what we were. Everything else had been washed downstream years ago: my name, my trade, my right to be anywhere in particular.

I sat with my back against a willow and watched the water rise around our little island and thought about how many times I'd told myself I was a man who kept useful secrets. Useful to whom, that was always the question I declined to ask.

Maren wrung out her sleeves and said nothing. She'd been saying nothing for most of a day, which from Maren was louder than shouting.

* * *

You should understand something about silence on a small boat. There are silences that are comfortable — the kind you get after two people have hauled lines together through rapids and don't need words anymore. And there are silences that are load-bearing, holding something up that will come down on your head if anyone speaks.

Ours had been the second kind since the fuel depot. Since she took a graze across the ribs shielding the map case and then refused, absolutely refused, to discuss it beyond "it's a scratch." Since I'd watched her bleed for a piece of paper that would, if she ever read it properly, tell her exactly who I was.

She knew pieces. She knew I'd been a surveyor for the Northern Rail Company. She knew I'd signed things. In the boathouse at Cold Harbor she'd caught me reading the old manifest — her brother's name, Torben Vask, third column, listed as lost with the Vellmark work crew — and I'd given her half the truth, which is to say the half that made me look like a man haunted rather than a man responsible. I told her I'd surveyed the pass before the collapse. I did not tell her what my survey said, or who had asked me to say it, or whose signature was at the bottom of the report that sealed eleven men behind a wall of rock while they were still breathing.

Half the truth is worse than a lie. A lie is a wall; you know where you stand with it. Half the truth is a door left open just enough that you keep hoping someone will walk through it honestly, and they never do.

The rain hammered the tarp. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the valley like a barrel going down stairs.

"Water's at the second root," Maren said, nodding at the willow. "If it hits the third, we're swimming."

"I know."

"You always know. That's the annoying thing about you." She pulled her knees up. Her side was stiff — I could see her favoring it when she thought I wasn't looking. "Isandro. Ask me why I'm still here."

I looked at her. Rain-light came through the canvas in a gray wash, and her face was all angles and patience, which is a dangerous combination in a woman holding a boat hook within reach.

"Because the river's high," I said.

"Wrong answer." She let it sit. Then, quieter: "Ask me properly."

"Because of Torben," I said. "Because you think I can find him."

"And can I? Find him?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dodging since the dock at Vellmark town, since the map changed hands in Oskar's fevered grip, since the first time she looked at me and saw not a passenger but a key. Can you find him.

What I said was: "I think so."

What I meant was: I already know where he isn't.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I'd been doing for weeks, the kind you do at night when the fire's low and the other person is asleep and you're free to be honest with yourself because no one can hear it.

Torben Vask went into Vellmark Pass with the winter maintenance crew in the last week of the working season. Eleven men. The collapse came two days later — or rather, the collapse was declared two days later, which is not the same thing. I was twenty-six years old and the junior surveyor on the closure assessment, and my job was to measure the rockfall and certify whether the tunnel could be cleared.

It could. I measured it myself. Three days of shoring work, maybe four, and the gallery would have opened enough to bring men out. I wrote that in my field notes. I have never stopped writing it in my field notes; every survey I've done since has had that sentence buried somewhere in it, like a splinter working toward the heart.

Then Halvard Brann, the district engineer, sat me down in a warm office with a bottle of aquavit and explained the economics. Four days of shoring against the cost of abandoning the line entirely — because the company had already decided to close Vellmark, had decided it months before, and my honest report was an inconvenience standing between them and a tidy write-off. Eleven dead men were a tragedy. Eleven rescued men were a liability with pensions. He said it more politely than that. They always do. That's the whole trick of it: ruin dressed in reasonable language until it sounds like weather.

I signed the report that said the gallery was unrecoverable. The line closed. The men stayed inside. I got a promotion, a transfer, and eventually — when the guilt got loud enough — a forged permit and a satchel of maps and twenty years of keeping moving.

And Torben Vask, Maren's brother, was one of the eleven. I didn't know that when I signed. I learned his name later, off a manifest, the way you learn the names of strangers in a newspaper. It should have mattered less, knowing he was a stranger. It has never once mattered less.

* * *

The water reached the third root sometime after dark. We moved the boat twice, hauling it up the mudbank by lantern light while the rain tried to drown us where we stood, and ended up wedged beneath the tarp with everything we owned between us and a rising brown flood. Maren's wound had opened a little during the hauling. I saw the fresh red on her shirt and she saw me see it, and dared me with a look to mention it.

So I didn't. I mentioned something else instead, God help me, something safe — the lock station marked on Oskar's map, six miles upstream, the last working gate before the pass mouth. I talked about gear ratios and rusted spindles the way other men talk about football. Engineering as anesthesia. If I kept explaining things, I couldn't feel anything.

Maren listened with her chin on her knees. Then she said, "Stop."

"Stop what?"

"Explaining. You do it when you're hiding. You've done it since the checkpoint." She turned to face me fully, and the lantern threw her shadow huge against the willow trunk, like something about to step forward. "I've watched you lie to company men so smoothly I almost believed you myself. I've watched you talk your way past two blockades. You're very good at it, Isandro. And every time you're good at it, I wonder what else you're good at talking your way out of."

"Maren—"

"My brother went into that mountain," she said. "Three years ago. Everyone says the collapse killed him. Everyone says it quick, the way you say things you don't want to look at straight-on. But you've seen the engineering drawings. You know what a collapse looks like. So tell me — and don't explain, don't survey, don't be clever — tell me whether a man could have lived in there."

The rain filled the pause. I could have lied. I want to be clear about that: lying was available, easy, practically upholstered. I'd have gotten away with it too, probably, the way I'd gotten away with everything else. Twenty years of practice doesn't evaporate in one wet night on a mudbank.

But she was bleeding for this. Literally bleeding, for a map, for a hope, for a brother — and I was sitting on the one fact that made all of it real, hoarding it like a coward hoards coins.

"No," I said. "Not in a collapse."

Her face didn't change. "Meaning?"

"Meaning the Vellmark collapse wasn't a collapse." My voice came out strange, flat, like someone else's. "The gallery held. The rockfall was survivable. Clearable. I measured it. I recommended clearing it. And then I signed a report that said otherwise."

The lantern hissed. Water lapped at the roots below us.

"Why," she said. Not loudly. That was the worst part — she didn't shout. She asked it the way you'd ask a merchant why the price changed.

"Because the company wanted the line closed, and my report was in the way. Because a man named Halvard Brann bought me with a promotion and I was young and poor and flattered, and I told myself the men were already dead. I told myself that for twenty years. It's easier to sign away dead men than living ones." I heard my own breath shaking. "Eleven men went in, Maren. Eleven men were alive behind that rockfall for at least three days — probably longer, there was seep water, there was air off the old shafts — and I am the reason nobody came."

She was very still. Stillness, in Maren, is not calm. It's a drawn bow.

"Torben," she said. "Was Torben one of them."

"Yes."

For a long moment nothing happened at all. The rain fell, the river rose, the lantern burned. And then she moved — fast, faster than I'd have credited with that rib — and her hand closed on my collar and she hauled me half off the ground, and her face was an inch from mine and full of something I'd only ever seen in her once before, at the checkpoint, when the enforcers put their boots on her deck.

"Three years," she said. "Three years I rowed this river looking for a body. I dredged. I posted notices. I sat with my mother while she died calling his name. And you knew. You knew the whole time, you sat in my boat, you ate my food, you let me bleed for your map, and you knew my brother was sealed in a hole because you signed him in there."

"I didn't know he was yours," I said, and even as I said it I understood it was the smallest sentence I had ever spoken. "When I signed, I didn't know—"

"You didn't know their names?" Her grip tightened. "Or you didn't care?"

"There's no difference," I said. "That's the whole of it. There's no difference, and I've spent twenty years pretending there was."

Something went out of her arm then — not forgiveness, nothing like that, but maybe the recognition that hitting me wouldn't reach the part of me that deserved it. She let go. I sat back down in the mud, and I didn't wipe my face, because the rain was doing it for me and it seemed fitting that even that shouldn't be my own doing.

* * *

She stood at the edge of the tarp for a long time, looking at the water. I knew what she was calculating, because I'd have calculated the same thing in her place: the boat, the current, the distance to the far bank. Whether a man was worth leaving on a mudbank in a monsoon to drown among the drowned barley.

I almost hoped she'd do it. That's the truth, or one of them. There's a kind of relief in being sentenced promptly.

Instead she crouched, picked up the map case — our map case, blood-priced — and held it in her lap, and said, "This is mine now."

"It was always yours."

"Don't be noble. It makes me want to hit you again." She turned the case over in her hands. "If you get on that boat tomorrow, it's because I need your eyes on those drawings. Not because I forgive you. Do you understand the difference?"

"I understand."

"Do you?" She looked at me then, and her eyes were wet, though she'd have denied it to her grave. "Because here's what I understand. My brother might be dead because of you. Or he might be dead anyway, twenty years cold, and nothing you do changes either version. But there are drawings in that case, and a lock upstream, and a mountain with a sealed chamber in it, and you are the only man on this river who can read them. So you're coming. And every mile, you're going to remember why."

The rain eased near dawn, the way storms do, grudgingly, like a debt collector leaving for the night but meaning to return. The water stopped climbing at the fourth root and began, slowly, to fall. Maren slept sitting up with the map case strapped across her body like a child. I lay awake in the mud and listened to the river run, and for the first time in twenty years the silence inside me wasn't load-bearing. Things had come down. It hurt like hell. It also, strangely, held.

We pushed off at first light. The drowned fields steamed. Six miles upstream, according to Oskar's careful hand, stood the last lock before the pass — and Maren stood at the tiller with her back to me, and neither of us spoke, and the boat ran quiet and fast toward whatever the mountain had kept all these years.

It wasn't until we rounded the first bend that I saw it: fresh timber bracing on the lock tower, new rope, a lamp still burning in a window that should have been dark for a decade.

Someone had been keeping the gate ready.
