## 11. Cold Harbor

I want it on the record—my record, the one I keep in my head because paper burns and rivers eat everything else—that I did not stay on that mudbank out of forgiveness.

I stayed because of a drawing.

Isandro had spread it across the tarp while the rain hammered us like a creditor who wouldn't leave. It was one of his old survey sheets, water-stained and soft as cloth at the folds, showing the Vellmark Pass in cross-section: galleries, shafts, the lock works, the air line. He read it the way my mother read weather—not looking at it so much as listening to it.

"Whoever's in there," he said quietly, "if anyone's in there, they'd have gone toward the air shaft. Here." His finger stopped on a thin line running up through the mountain like a vein. "It vents near the third gallery. If they could reach it, they could breathe."

"Could they reach it?"

He was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence for him. "I don't know. The drawings say maybe."

The drawings say maybe. Twenty years of engineering education, and that was what it came down to. But I looked at that sheet—at the careful hand-lettering, the little stamped seal of the Northern Rail Company in the corner, the same seal that had been stamped on the notice that took our land, our wharf, our name—and I understood something that made me sick and steady at once.

I couldn't read this. Almost nobody alive could read this. And the man who could had signed the lie that closed it.

So no, I didn't stay for him. I stayed the way you keep a bad tool because it's the only one that fits the bolt. I told him as much, too, because I'd promised myself after the confession under the tarp that I would never again let him mistake my silences for absolution.

"I'm not doing this for you," I said, poling us off the mudbank into the brown flood of the river.

"I know," he said. "Thank you for saying it. It's easier than pretending otherwise."

"You're welcome," I said, and hated that some small traitorous part of me had wanted him to argue.

* * *

The river changed character above the Detour. Where we'd come through drowned farmland—fence posts standing in six feet of water like drowned men waiting patiently to be noticed—the channel narrowed and the banks rose up steep and black with spruce. The monsoon had been at work for days now, and the water ran high and fast, carrying whole trees end over end like it was juggling them. Twice we had to backwater hard to let a log the size of a church pew spin past the bow. Isandro worked the lines without being told. He'd learned the boat faster than I liked. Men who learn your trade quickly are either gifts or thieves, and I hadn't decided which he was.

The lock station appeared around a bend the way ruin always does—suddenly, and as if it had been waiting for someone specific.

It sat on a shelf of rock above the east bank, a squat stone building with a slate roof half shrugged off into moss, and below it the lock itself: two great gates of blackened timber closing off a channel cut straight into the mountain's shoulder. Even from the water I could see the gates were shut. Not broken-shut, not storm-shut. *Closed*-shut. Bars dropped, padlocks swollen green with age, chains wrapped twice and pinned.

"They locked it from the outside," Isandro said. His voice had gone flat, which I'd learned was how he sounded when feeling was trying to climb out of him and he was sitting on the lid.

"Of course they did," I said. "That's what locks are for. Keeping things in."

He looked at me then, and I saw him decide not to say whatever he'd been about to say. Good. We were saving words for later; I could feel it, the way you feel weather coming in your knees.

We tied off in the lee of the boathouse—a lean-to against the station wall, its doors hanging open like a man asleep with his mouth agape—and climbed the wet steps to the station proper. The door wasn't locked. Nothing inside was worth locking, or nothing had been for twenty years.

Inside it was dim and dry-smelling, the particular dryness of places rain has given up on. A potbellied stove, cold. A desk with its drawers pulled out and left gaping. A wall calendar from a year I won't repeat here, though I'll admit my eye went to it first and stuck there, the way an eye does, on the month when everything ended. Some clerk had drawn a little X on the fourteenth. Just the one X. Whatever happened on the fourteenth, somebody had thought it worth marking, and then nobody had ever turned the page again.

Isandro went straight past all of it to the drafting table by the window. There were still papers on it. That stopped even me.

"Company procedure," he said, half to himself, squinting. "Lock stations kept duplicate sets of the pass drawings. In case the main office burned." He laughed—one short, ugly syllable. "In case the main office *burned*. They were more afraid of fire than of what they were burying."

"Can you use them?"

"I can use them." He was already sorting, quick hands, the competence I resented and relied on moving through the sheets. "These are older than my survey. Look—" He flattened one against the desk and weighted its corners with a fuse tin and two brass fittings he found in a drawer. "The original air shaft. Before they re-routed it. See the change in grade? Someone revised this line three times." His finger traced the pencil ghostings beneath the ink. "Three revisions. You don't revise ventilation three times unless—"

"Unless people needed it to work."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. We stood there together in the dead man's office, reading the arithmetic of a crime in pencil ghostings, and for a moment I forgot to hate him, which frightened me more than the river had all week.

* * *

We spent the afternoon on the lock gates, because hope is useless if you can't get through it.

The mechanism was rusted but honest—old iron doesn't lie the way men do; it just refuses. Isandro crawled along the gate beams with a hammer and a can of boiled oil from the boathouse, freeing pins, and I worked the capstan bar until my shoulders screamed. By dark we had the upstream gate cracked open a hand's width, groaning like something waking from a long sleep, and water began to move through the gap with a sound I felt in my teeth.

"It'll take the night to equalize," Isandro called down. "Then we can winch them both. Tomorrow we go through."

Tomorrow we go through. I stood on the gate beam with the river pulling at my boots and looked up at where the passage entered the mountain—a black arch, taller than my mast, breathing out cold air that smelled of stone and, underneath the stone, faintly and impossibly, of smoke.

Old smoke. Or not-so-old smoke. I couldn't tell, and I filed the smell away next to the X on the calendar and said nothing, because I've learned that if you speak every fear aloud you spend them all before you need them.

* * *

It was Isandro who found the wall.

Not through skill—I want to be clear about that, since I'm giving him enough credit already—but through the ordinary animal habit of a man looking for a place to relieve himself. He'd gone around behind the station at dusk, and I heard him stop, and then I heard him call my name in a voice I hadn't heard from him before. Not the flat voice. Not the smooth checkpoint voice. Something younger and worse.

"Maren. Bring the lamp."

The back wall of the station, where it met the rock of the cut, had been faced with brick—company brick, you could tell by the frog stamp, the same little stamped hollow in every one, thousands of them, a wall made of the company's own signature. But a section of it, maybe four feet wide, was different. Newer mortar, sloppier. And scratched into the bricks themselves, low down, at the height of a kneeling man—

Letters. Words. Cut deep, cut slow, cut with something harder than fingernails and softer than patience.

I held the lamp close. The flame leaned toward the wall as if it wanted to read too.

**WE ARE 9 ALIVE**

Below that, another hand, shallower:

**AIR GOOD HERE**

And below that, smaller, cramped, the letters wobbling like a drunk walking a line:

**DAY 40 — THEY KNOW WE ARE HERE — NO ONE COMES**

And below that, the last one, the one I will see every time I close my eyes for the rest of my life:

**IF ANYONE READS THIS WE WENT DEEPER TO THE AIR LINE — TELL OUR NAMES**

There were names. Eleven of them, scratched in a column, some confident, some barely legible, one just initials and a mark like a cross. I read them the way you drink water when you're drowning—too fast, desperately, missing half of it. My eyes went down the column hunting one name the way a tongue hunts a broken tooth.

His name wasn't there.

Eleven names, and Torben Voss was not among them, and I did not know yet whether that meant he was one of the nine who went deeper, or one of the two who didn't make it to the wall at all, or—God help me—whether the names on the wall weren't even the right eleven, whether the company's count and the mountain's count had never matched, whether my brother had gone into that pass under a number instead of a name and come out of the ledger entirely.

"Maren." Isandro's voice, very quiet. "Maren, look at the dates."

There were dates. Scratched beside some of the messages, small as secrets. I read them twice because the first time my mind refused the cargo.

They were dated *after* the closure. Months after. One of them, the last one, the one that said *NO ONE COMES*, was dated nearly a year after the day the company sealed the pass and certified eleven men dead and paid out eleven widows' settlements and promoted a young surveyor who kept his mouth shut.

"They lived," I said. My voice came out strange, borrowed. "Someone lived. For months. Maybe years."

"Yes."

"In the dark. On seep water. Writing on a wall that their own—" I stopped, because the word *murderers* was right there and I couldn't tell anymore whether I meant the company or the man holding the lamp beside me, and both of us knew it, and neither of us said it.

Isandro put his hand flat against the bricks. Gently, the way you touch a grave marker or a sleeping child. "Nine alive," he read aloud, as if saying it might make it truer. "They went deeper. Toward the air line." He turned to look at me, and the lamplight did him no favors; he looked like a man reading his own sentence. "Maren, if nine men reached the air shaft—if they got out through it, or if their children—"

"*If.*"

"If," he agreed. "But it's more *if* than there was yesterday."

Hope arriving in the shape of horror. That's what I thought, standing in the cold with a lamp shaking in my fist. Because you cannot un-know a thing like this. Yesterday my brother was dead, and dead has a shape, and grief fits it like a glove. Now he was possibly alive, possibly dead, possibly old, possibly mad, possibly a mile inside a mountain writing on walls, and grief had no shape at all anymore—it was just water rising, and I was the boat, and boats don't get to choose what they float on.

"We go through tomorrow," I said. "First light."

"The gate needs the full night to equalize, and the winch—"

"First light, Isandro." I held the lamp up between us so he could see my face and understand that this was not a negotiation. "There are names on that wall that have waited twenty years for someone to come read them. I am not making them wait for a winch."

He nodded. Then, because he was apparently incapable of leaving honesty alone: "For what it's worth—I signed the report that says those men died on the fourteenth. I wrote the numbers myself. Nine alive." His laugh came out cracked down the middle. "I was wrong. God help me, being wrong is the best news I've ever had."

I should have hit him. Some part of me notes, for the record, that I considered it.

Instead I blew out the lamp, because the flame was guttering and because I couldn't stand to watch his face while mine did whatever it was doing, and we walked back around the station in the dark, and behind us the scratched words hung on their bricks in the blackness like stars that had fallen low enough to be read.

*TELL OUR NAMES.*

Tomorrow, I promised the wall. First light.

And somewhere inside the mountain, in the dark where nine men had once counted their days on brick, something seemed to shift—a draft, a settling, the faintest breath of smoke-smell moving through the locked gates—as if the pass had heard me, and had been waiting twenty years for exactly that promise, and intended to collect.
