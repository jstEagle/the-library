## 12. The Survivor's Mark

I have spent my life reading things that were never meant to be read. Survey notes in margins. The hesitation in a foreman's voice when a number didn't sit right. The particular silence of men who know something and have agreed, without saying so, to carry it together.

But I had never read anything like the wall.

Maren held the lamp high while I knelt in the dust of the old lock station's back room, my nose a hand's width from the brickwork behind the collapsed shelving. The mortar there was newer than the rest—someone had bricked it over in a hurry, badly, decades ago—and someone else, later, had decided that hiding was finished. They had scratched into the soft mortar with something sharp. A nail, maybe. A pit spike. The letters were shallow and uneven, cut by fingers that must have been shaking, or weak, or both.

*DAY 41. WATER STILL SEEPING. SIX OF US.*

Below that, lower down where a man would write kneeling:

*DAY 58. JORGEN DIED IN THE NIGHT. FIVE.*

And below that, in a different hand—smaller, more careful, the letters of someone who'd had schooling:

*DAY 74. WE MOVED THE STONES. AIR COMES FROM THE NORTH SIDE. THERE IS A WAY.*

I sat back on my heels and the dust rose around me and for a long moment I could not make my lungs work properly.

"Isandro." Maren's voice came from somewhere above me, very far away. "What is it? What do the dates mean?"

The dates. She wanted the dates explained, as if they were a timetable, as if this were a schedule I could read out and she could plan around. And God forgive me, my first instinct—the instinct twenty years of survival had ground into me—was to soften them. To round them down. To find some merciful arithmetic.

Because I knew the closure date of the Vellmark Pass the way other men knew their wedding anniversary. The eleventh of Harrowmonth. I had signed the certificate on the twelfth. The company seal went on the thirteenth. The tunnel mouths were blasted shut on the fifteenth, and I stood at a professional distance with my notebook open and wrote *structural entombment confirmed, no survivors possible* in my neat engineer's hand while the dust was still settling over eleven men.

Day 41 put those scratchings at late summer. Months after the blast. Months after I had written *no survivors possible* and taken the promotion that came with writing it.

"Isandro." Maren crouched beside me now. Her hand closed on my shoulder—not gentle, Maren was never gentle when it mattered—and turned me toward her. "Say it."

"Someone lived," I said. My voice came out like gravel shifted underfoot. "After the collapse. After the sealing. Someone lived for—at least seventy-four days."

The lamp flame shivered in her grip. "Seventy-four days." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "They wrote that they moved the stones. That air was coming from the north side." Her eyes went past me to the wall, then back. "That's deeper in. Toward the pass proper."

"Yes."

"And the company report said—"

"The company report said no one could have survived the initial collapse." I heard my own voice doing what it always did: reciting, precise, dead. "Air volume insufficient. Seepage contamination. I wrote the section on atmospheric viability myself."

I wrote it myself. Four words that should have been carved above my heart like a brand.

Maren stood up abruptly, walked three paces, stood with her back to me. When she spoke again her voice was controlled in the way water is controlled behind a dam. "My brother went in with a survey crew two weeks before the closure. Torben Vass. He was twenty-three. The company paid my mother a settlement and a letter saying his remains were unrecoverable." She turned. "Unrecoverable. While six men were scratching their names into a wall forty-one days after the world ended."

"I didn't know," I said, and it was true, and it was worthless, and we both knew both things.

* * *

We worked through what remained of the daylight because working was the only alternative to thinking, and neither of us could afford to think yet.

The back room of the lock station had been a maintenance office once; there was still a rusted filing cabinet with its drawers fused shut, and a drafting table leg sticking up out of rubble like the femur of some buried animal. It was on the underside of this table—upside down, so that anyone standing in the room would see nothing—that we found the rest.

Letters scratched into the wood, dense and small, the work of many sessions. Not a message. A record. Route notes.

I lay on my back in the filth with the lamp wedged against a beam and read them aloud, slowly, translating the cramped shorthand of men who wrote for themselves and God, not for posterity.

*"North gallery blocked at chain thirty. We cut through the old ventilation drift instead. Air moves steady—means it connects to shafts above the snowline."*

*"Ration count: nine tins, hardtack, seep water is drinkable if you let the silt settle. Halvard says the mules' feed store survived. We are rich, by mountain standards."*

*"Day 90. We voted. Staying near the mouth means dying near the mouth. The air shaft on the north ridge—if it reaches surface, it reaches sky. We go deeper to come out."*

Deeper to come out. I closed my eyes and saw it immediately, the way I see all of it, the whole pass laid out in my memory like a body on a table: the north ventilation shaft, sunk in '09 during the original construction, three hundred feet of timbered rise connecting the upper galleries to the ridge above the snowline. It had been capped, not sealed—capped with a plug of stone and timber that any determined man with tools and time could work through from below.

Determined men with tools and time. Six of them, five of them, however many remained, with ninety days of nothing but determination ahead of them.

"They could have made it," Maren said. She was reading over my shoulder, upside down, her braid hanging into the lamplight. "The shaft. If the air was moving, the shaft wasn't fully blocked. They could have climbed out."

"Or died trying," I said, because I owed her honesty even when honesty was cruelty. "Three hundred feet of vertical climb, in the dark, on empty stomachs. Or they reached the cap and couldn't shift it. Or they got out and—" I stopped.

*And never told anyone.* Because who would they tell? Men who crawled out of a mountain the company had declared a tomb were either ghosts or liars, and either way they were evidence, and the Northern Rail Company had a long history with evidence. If they came down off that ridge and learned what happened to people who contradicted the official record—

"There's more here," Maren said. "Look. Day numbers keep going. Past a hundred."

She was right. The scratches continued, sparser now, the handwriting degrading and then—this was the part that stopped my breath—changing. A new hand. Younger. Then another. Then entries that weren't day counts at all but tallies, marks in groups of five, the way you count stores or seasons or children.

Children.

"Maren." My mouth was dry. "These last marks. They're not survival tallies."

She looked. I watched her arrive at the same place I had arrived, watched it land on her face in stages: confusion, then calculation, then something that broke open behind her eyes.

"Those are birth records," she whispered. "Someone was born down there. More than one."

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat in the derelict office with the map spread across my knees and Oskar's dying gift under my lamp, and I did the arithmetic I had been avoiding since sundown.

Here is the terrible arithmetic of Vellmark Pass, done honestly, the way I should have done it twenty years ago instead of the way the company paid me to do it.

Eleven men sealed in. Six alive at day 41. Five at day 58. At least four writing route notes at day 90. If the ventilation drift connected as described, if the shaft cap yielded, if even two men reached the surface—then the death toll of the eleventh of Harrowmonth is not eleven.

It might be seven. It might be five. It might be fewer still, because the tallies kept going, year after year, mark after mark, generations of marks, and a hidden community does not survive twenty years on seep water and hope unless it found something better than seep water, unless it found the smugglers' routes Oskar's map showed threading the high country, unless it grew.

Which meant that every year since the closure, the company's crime had not been eleven murders.

It had been an ongoing one. A village-sized one. A lie maintained annually, budgeted for, defended—by men like the ones chasing us, with warrants and photographs and dynamite.

And the rescue? Here is the cruelest figure in the whole ledger. A rescue had been possible. At day 41, six men alive behind a wall of loose brick. A crew with drills could have been through in a week. I knew the geology. I knew the seepage patterns. I knew, because I had written the falsified survey myself, exactly which galleries were stable enough to dig through.

Twenty years too late for the men whose hands cut those first letters. But the marks kept going. Which meant somewhere inside that mountain, right now, in this season, in this year, there might be people. Descendants. Children who had never seen the sky, or who had seen it only as a rumor at the top of an air shaft.

A rescue twenty years too late—or not.

Oskar had known. That feverish, drowning man in the dockside tavern had known what he was handing me, and he had traded it for a promise I thought was sentiment: *open it before the monsoon seals it.* He hadn't meant open it for cargo. He hadn't meant open it for history.

He had meant open it for them.

I looked at Maren, asleep sitting up against the doorframe with the map case strapped across her chest even in sleep, her wound from the depot still seeping through its bandage, and I understood finally why she had stayed. Not for me. For the wall. For the marks. For the possibility, however thin, that her brother's name was written somewhere in that mountain in a living hand.

Torben Vass. Twenty-three years old. *Went in with a survey crew.* I had certified the survey crews' departure manifests. I had checked names off lists. Had I checked his name off? Had my pen passed over him the way it passed over all of them, efficient and blind?

Tomorrow we would go in. Through the mouth of the pass, past the blast-sealed galleries, following the route notes scratched by dead men's hands toward the north drift and whatever waited at the end of them—a community, a graveyard, a letter in my brother's hand, I don't know which, Maren had said, and she was right, none of us would know until we stood inside it.

I banked the lamp low and took out my notebook, the same model of notebook I had used to certify the closure, and on a fresh page I began to copy out the route notes exactly, every date, every tally, every name that appeared. If we died in there, the record would survive in duplicate. I had spent twenty years making documents disappear.

It was time I started making them appear.

Somewhere far off, thunder rolled along the ridge line—the monsoon, arriving on schedule, indifferent to our plans. And beneath it, fainter, so faint I might have invented it, I heard something else. A sound from deep in the mountain. Rhythmic. Metallic.

Like a hammer, ringing on stone.

Like someone, somewhere inside the dark, still building.
