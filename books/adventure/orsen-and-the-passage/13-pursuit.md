## 13. Pursuit

**Isandro**

The fuel depot at Kessel's Landing was the kind of place that existed only because the river demanded it—a shelf of corrugated tin and rusted drums bolted to the last flat ground before the gorge, run by a man named Doss who had the eyes of someone who had stopped asking questions around the same time he'd started selling diesel to anyone with coin.

I should have known better than to stop anywhere with a roof.

"We have half a tank," Maren said, cutting the engine as we slid against the dock. "Half a tank doesn't get us through the gorge."

"Half a tank gets us away from here," I said.

"Nothing gets us away from here except fuel." She was already tying off, already moving, and I understood then that she had decided we were stopping before I'd finished deciding we weren't. That was how it went between us now. She moved; I followed; I told myself it was strategy.

The rain had eased to a fine grey drizzle, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang in the air waiting for you to walk through it. The depot smelled of diesel and wet creosote and something faintly sweet—rotting fruit from a crate nobody had bothered to empty since the war, probably. Doss stood under his awning with a ledger and a pencil worn down to a stub, and he looked at us the way a man looks at weather: without hope of changing it.

"Forty liters," Maren said. "And whatever canned food you've got that isn't older than my boat."

"Everything's older than your boat," Doss said, but he was already shuffling toward the drums.

I hung back by the rail, doing what I had done for twenty years, which was counting exits. Two: the dock behind us, the gravel track ahead climbing toward the old haul road. A third if you counted the water itself, but the water was running brown and fast and angry, and I wasn't desperate enough for it yet. Almost, but not yet.

That's when I saw the boat.

It was moored fifty yards downstream, a company launch, grey hull, canvas over its cargo, and no one aboard. My stomach did the thing it always did when the past got close—it dropped, quietly, like a stone through still water, leaving barely a ripple on the surface. Twenty years of practice. You learn to swallow fear whole so it doesn't show in your face. It's not a skill I'm proud of. It's the only skill I've ever been paid well for.

"Maren," I said, low.

She heard it. Whatever was in my voice, she heard it, because she stopped mid-haggle and turned her head slightly, like a heron deciding whether the shadow overhead is cloud or hawk.

"Company launch," I said. "Downstream."

"Could be anyone."

"It could be anyone," I agreed. "But it isn't."

Doss came back with the fuel hose and a crate of tins whose labels had faded into abstraction. "Twelve kroner the fuel," he said. "Three the food. Cash."

Maren paid him. She counted it out slow, deliberately slow, and I realized she was buying time, keeping Doss talking, keeping all of us standing in plain sight because—her logic ran this way—running looked like guilt, and looking guilty invited pursuit. There was a cold competence in it that I admired and feared in equal measure. Admired because I could never do it. Feared because it meant she'd thought about being hunted long enough to develop a doctrine.

The first shot took the lamp above Doss's awning.

Glass rained down into the mud, and the world went from grey to chaos in the space of a heartbeat. Doss dropped behind his ledger counter like a man who had rehearsed this, which told me everything about Kessel's Landing I needed to know. Maren grabbed the food crate. I grabbed the fuel nozzle, which was still locked open in the drum filler, and I did the arithmetic of a coward and an engineer simultaneously: forty liters pumped, maybe thirty-five in the tank, and two men coming up the gravel track with rifles and the unhurried gait of professionals.

They weren't rushing. That frightened me more than the shooting would have. Men who rush are afraid you'll escape. Men who don't rush are afraid of nothing, because they've already read the map and know there's nowhere to go.

"Hold!" one of them shouted, and it was Voss—I knew the voice, gravel poured over iron, the voice that had asked me polite questions at three checkpoints in four hundred miles. "Iversen! We just want the map!"

*We just want the map.* As though Oskar hadn't died for it. As though the map were a thing separate from the eleven names written inside my skull, separate from the survey I'd signed, separate from every mile of track I'd helped lay over ground that still held men. They wanted the map the way a man wants a debt note burned—not the money back, just the proof gone.

Maren was already at the boat. "Isandro!"

I threw the nozzle down. Diesel slopped across the boards. And then I made the decision that I will be explaining to myself for the rest of my life, whichever shore that turns out to be: instead of running straight for the boat, I ran *away* from it, up the gravel track, waving my arms like a lunatic, shouting things I don't remember, drawing their fire, drawing their eyes, giving her the seconds she needed.

Because that's the thing about loyalty they never put in the pamphlets. It isn't a feeling. It's a direction you run when every instinct is screaming the other way.

A round kicked gravel past my ear. Another punched through the tin wall somewhere behind me. I cut left, off the track, into the scrub, and heard—distinctly, unmistakably—the *Thessa's* engine catch and turn. Good girl. Good, furious, unforgiving girl.

I came down the bank on my heels and palms, sliding in mud, and Maren leaned out over the gunwale with her arm extended like the figurehead of some furious merchant saint, and I caught her wrist, and the river took us both.

Behind us, Voss stood on the dock. He didn't shoot again. He just watched us go, and raised two fingers to his hat brim—not a salute, exactly. An appointment.

**Maren**

He ran the wrong way.

I want that written down somewhere, in case anyone ever asks me why I keep the man alive: when the bullets came, Isandro Iversen—liar, coward, the man who signed my brother's coffin shut with a fountain pen—ran *toward* the guns so I could get the boat clear. I have spent three years learning to hate him in advance, hating him on credit, hating him for things I was sure he'd done before I even knew his name. It is deeply inconvenient to discover he runs the wrong way.

The bullet found me anyway. Of course it did. The river doesn't care about gestures.

It happened at the bend below the depot, where the current swings hard against the far bank and the smart move is to hug the inside line. I was steering with one hand and hauling the map case closer with the other—Oskar's oilskin-wrapped chart, the whole rotten reason for our existence—and the second shot came from the treeline, not the dock, which meant there had been three of them, not two, which meant Voss had planned this the way engineers plan things, which meant I'd been outmaneuvered by my own species again.

The round went through the meat of my left shoulder and out the back, or mostly out the back; there was a piece of me that stayed with the splinters of the gunwale, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt almost funny: *that's going to fester.*

I didn't drop the case. I want that written down too. Blood everywhere, shoulder on fire, boat slewing sideways in the current, and my hands stayed closed around that oilskin like it was my brother's actual hand.

Isandro reached me before I finished falling. He had the tiller in one hand and me in the other and he was shouting something—my name, probably, or God's, or both—and I remember being furious about that too, furious that his hands were steady, furious that twenty years of flight had built a man who could hold a bleeding woman and a course through white water at the same time without spilling either.

"Stay down," he said.

"I'm steering."

"You're *bleeding.*"

"I can do both." And I could, is the thing. Pain is just information. My father taught me that on this same river, thirty years ago, with a dislocated thumb and a net full of fish and no help coming. You file the pain where it belongs—in the body, which is temporary—and you keep your mind on the water, which is forever.

We ran the bend. We lost them. The treeline gave way to rock, the rock gave way to the long brown throat of the gorge country, and after a while there were no more shots, only rain and engine noise and my own blood ticking onto the deck boards, patient as a clock.

Isandro got me below eventually, when the water smoothed out enough to trust an autopilot hand. He cut my sleeve away with a rigging knife and looked at the wound the way he looks at cracked hulls—with that terrible professional calm, cataloguing damage, estimating service life.

"How bad?" I asked.

"Through and through. Missed the bone by a finger's width. Missed anything important by less." He was cleaning it with river-boiled water and the good spirits, the ones we'd been saving for the engine. "You'll live. You'll hate it, but you'll live."

"The case?"

"Intact. Dry." He tied the bandage off with a surgeon's knot, which surprised me until I remembered: railways. Men lose limbs on railways. Somebody on every crew learns to tie knots that hold under load.

Then he opened the supply locker, and I watched his face do something complicated.

"What," I said.

"Fuel's fine. But the food crate—" He lifted out the tin Doss had sold us, and water poured from it. Bullet hole, clean entry, clean exit. The whole crate beneath it was soaked through, half the stores ruined, and the fresh water cask had taken a graze and was weeping into the bilge. "Half of it," he said quietly. "Maybe more. We lost half of everything."

I lay back against the bulkhead and laughed, which hurt, which made me laugh more, which is a loop I don't recommend. "So we're wounded, hunted, short on food, short on water, and down to fumes."

"Yes."

"And the pass seals in nine days."

"Eight," he said. "The monsoon's early."

I looked at him—at this man I had carried across three hundred miles of river like ballast I couldn't throw overboard, this man whose confession was still sitting between us like a third passenger, this man who had run toward gunfire for me and whom I had not yet forgiven and might never forgive and would apparently die beside regardless.

"Then we don't stop," I said.

He didn't argue. That was the strangest part. A month ago he'd have argued—argued beautifully, argued like a lawyer, argued until the argument became a fog and the fog became an exit. Now he just nodded, once, and went forward to bail the bilge, and I lay in the dark of the cabin with my shoulder burning and the map case under my good arm, and I understood that something had been settled between us that neither of us had said aloud.

Blood does that. It's a terrible currency—spends fast, costs everything—but it clears all debts of doubt. He had run the wrong way. I had kept my hands closed. Whatever we are to each other now, we're it permanently.

The river carried us down through the rain toward the gorge, and eight days, and the mountain that kept my brother's name.

Somewhere behind us, I knew, Voss was reading the water too. And somewhere ahead, in the dark of the pass, the air shaft Isandro had deciphered from those scratched messages was breathing—slow, patient, alive.

Eight days. Half the supplies. One working arm between us.

It would have to be enough. It was going to have to be.
