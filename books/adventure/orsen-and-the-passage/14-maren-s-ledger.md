## 14. Maren's Ledger

The fever came on the second night after the depot, riding in on the wound like a passenger who'd paid for the whole berth.

I knew the signs. Da had run fevers every spring when the floodwater got into the bilges and stayed there, breeding things in the dark. He'd lie in the cabin of the *Halden Maid* with his teeth chattering and his eyes bright as coins, talking to my mother about prices for eel that had been dead twenty years by then. I was eleven the first time I nursed him through one, and I learned the important lesson early: fever lies. Fever tells you the water is warm when it's freezing, that you can row when your arms are gone, that the person sitting beside you is someone else entirely.

So when I woke in the bottom of the boat with the tarp sagging over me and rain drumming on it like a creditor at the door, and Isandro's face swam above mine wearing an expression of careful calm—the kind of face men make when they're trying not to look worried because they think worry is contagious—I did what Da taught me.

I told the truth before the fever could tell its version first.

"It's in the arm," I said. "Not just the arm. It's moving."

"I know." His voice was level. Too level. "You've been talking in your sleep."

"What did I say?"

"Doesn't matter."

"That bad?"

He almost smiled. Almost. "You called me a liar twice and a railroad man four times. You apologized to a heron."

"Then I was lucid." I tried to sit up and the river tilted sideways, the whole world going broadside in the current. His hand caught my shoulder—careful of the bandage, careful of everything—and pressed me back down onto the folded sailcloth. "Where are we?"

"Backwater off the main channel. Old timber camp, maybe. There's a shed." A pause. "There's a stove."

A stove meant dry heat, and dry heat meant I might keep the arm, or at least keep the rest of me. I closed my eyes and let him carry me, which I hated, and which I noticed—some small stubborn clerk in the back office of my mind made a note of it—I didn't hate quite as much as I should have.

* * *

The timber camp had been dead thirty years. The shed still stood because it was built of heartwood by men who expected their buildings to outlive their pensions, and inside there was a rusted iron stove, half a cord of split logs gone gray but sound, and a wall covered in tally marks where some foreman had counted something—days, boards, grievances—until he stopped counting.

Isandro got the fire going while I lay on a workbench he'd cleared, watching the smoke rise through the hole in the roof and trying to hold the world still. The world declined to cooperate. It kept sliding. The fire doubled itself, then tripled, then arranged itself into shapes: a lock gate, a ledger page, my brother's handwriting on the last letter he ever sent me, which said *the pass pays better than the coast and I'll be home by ice-out*, and which arrived two weeks after the mountain came down.

"You need to drink," Isandro said, holding a tin cup to my lips.

I drank. Willow bark, something bitter under it. "You found willow?"

"There's a stand by the waterline. I used to know bark from ore tailings; bark isn't harder." He set the cup down. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady, and I had learned in eight days that this steadiness cost him something visible, like a man carrying a full cup across a moving deck. "Maren. Your arm—"

"Is fine."

"Is hot to the touch and the redness is climbing past the bandage. If it goes bad—"

"If it goes bad you'll cut it off, and I'll be a boatwoman with one arm, which is one more than some people manage with." I looked at him. The fever made him swim, but it also made him simpler to read, the way lamplight simplifies a chart. He was afraid. Not of the arm. Of what I was about to say, though he couldn't have known it yet. Men who live by concealment develop a general-purpose fear, worn like oilskins against all weather. "Sit down, Isandro."

He sat. On an upturned crate, knees nearly to his chest, a grown man and a railroad engineer folded up like a letter someone meant to send and never did.

"I want to talk about what happens if I'm not thinking clearly," I said. "Because I'm not thinking clearly now, and later I'll be worse, and there's a thing I need done that requires a witness with a working memory."

"Maren—"

"Let me finish. I've watched you for eight days. You lie well. You lie *comfortably*, which is worse than lying well. When Voss's men boarded us you lied so smoothly I nearly believed you myself, and I was standing right there knowing the truth. And I've thought about why that frightened me, and it's not because I think you'd sell us out. It's because I think you'd do it kindly. Gently. For our own good. You'd bury the names again to protect us, and you'd weep while you did it, and you'd call it mercy."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the rain kept up its bookkeeping.

"That's not—" he started.

"It doesn't matter what it is or isn't. What matters is what gets promised now, while I can still hold you to it." I pushed myself up on my good elbow, and the shed spun, and I let it spin and kept my eyes on his face because his face was the fixed point, the lighthouse, the one true bearing. "Promise me the names go public. Whatever we find in that pass. Eleven men or one man or a hundred years of hidden people living in the dark off seep water—if there are names, they come out. They get carved, printed, shouted from the courthouse steps if it comes to that. Every name the company buried, dug up and laid in the sun."

"And if it destroys me?" His voice was quiet. Not defensive. Genuinely asking, the way a man asks about the depth of water he's about to wade into.

"Especially then."

He looked at the fire. I watched him do the arithmetic—he was good at arithmetic, it was practically his religion, tonnage and gradients and load limits—and I watched him arrive at the sum he'd been avoiding since Oskar put that map in his hands. The falsified survey had his signature on it. Going public meant the signature went public. Meant the promotion bought with silence was revealed as exactly what it was: a price. Meant everything he'd spent twenty years outrunning caught him in an open field with nowhere left to run.

"They'd take my license," he said. "Probably my freedom. There are statutes. Obstruction, falsification of record—it wouldn't be a fine, Maren. It would be years."

"Yes."

"And you're asking me to promise anyway."

"I'm not asking." The fever surged and I rode it, teeth set, hands gripping the edge of the bench. "I'm telling you what I need to hear before I go under. Because if I go under and wake up in Vellmark and you've made some arrangement—traded the map, traded the truth, saved us all quietly the way you save everything quietly—I will spend the rest of my life wondering what my brother's name was worth to you. And I don't want to wonder. Wondering is the company's weapon. They built a whole railway out of people wondering."

Silence. Long enough that the rain filled it completely.

Then Isandro Iversen, who lied comfortably, who lied smoothly, who had lied to inspectors and enforcers and boards of directors with a straight face and steady hands, looked me in the eye and told me something true, and I could tell it was true because it cost him visibly, like blood leaving a face.

"I promise," he said. "Whatever we find. The names come out. Even if it destroys me."

"Say the rest."

"The rest?"

"Say what destroying you means. Say it out loud. A promise without terms is just weather."

His jaw worked. "It means I lose everything I have left. The license. The name. Twenty years of being a man who builds things instead of a man who buried them." He stopped. Started again, lower. "It means I stop running. Which is the thing I've been most afraid of and the only thing I've wanted, and I've never once said that sentence aloud until now."

"Good," I said, and lay back, and the relief hit me harder than the fever had, which is saying something, because the fever felt like drowning. "That's the ledger entry. Witnessed. You're witnessed."

"By you."

"By me. And I keep books better than any company you've ever worked for."

* * *

The fever broke near dawn, or maybe it didn't break, maybe it just changed tactics. I drifted. The shed came and went. Isandro was there and not there, feeding the stove, changing the poultice on my arm with hands that were gentle in a way that seemed to surprise him each time he caught himself doing it.

At some point in the gray hours I surfaced enough to find him sitting by the fire with my brother's manifest page in his hands—the one we'd taken from the harbor records, the one with *Joren Vask* written in a clerk's neat hand, assigned to Gallery Four, Vellmark Pass. He wasn't reading it. He was holding it the way you hold a coal you can't drop and can't close your hand around.

"You knew him," I said. My voice came out scraped. "Before. You knew Joren."

Isandro didn't startle. That was the thing about him—even caught, he moved like a man who'd already heard you coming. "I knew the crews. I signed the rosters. I signed the safety certifications that let them go underground." He set the page down carefully, squared to the bench edge, engineer to the bone. "I didn't know your brother. But I knew his name before you ever showed me that manifest. It was on the list I memorized. The list of eleven."

"You memorized them."

"Every night for twenty years. In order. Aldous Rehm. Petter Salm. Joren Vask." He recited them the way other men say prayers, and I understood suddenly that for him they were prayers, the only kind he permitted himself, the kind that absolve nothing. "I could stop anytime. I've tried. They come back like the tide."

I should have hated him then. Some part of me had been saving up hate for exactly this moment, a sealed jar of it, three years' vintage, waiting for the confession. And the fever undid the seal, and what poured out instead wasn't hate. It was something worse and better: recognition. Here was a man drowning in the same water I was, and we had both been swimming alone, and the swimming together was the only honest thing either of us had done in years.

"Joren was the funny one," I said, because the fever had burned away whatever part of me rationed speech. "Everyone says that about dead brothers, that they were funny, but he actually was. He could imitate the harbor master so well the harbor master laughed. He went into the pass for the same reason everyone does. Money. Our father's boat was mortgaged to the company store, and Joren figured six months underground would buy it free." I laughed, and it hurt, and I kept laughing anyway because the alternative was the other thing. "Six months. The mountain gave him three weeks."

Isandro reached over and took my hand. The unwounded one. He held it without ceremony, without speech, the way you hold a line in rough water—not pulling, just keeping it from running out.

"When we open the pass," he said, "if your brother is alive in there, I'll carry him out on my back. And if he's dead, I'll carry his name. Either way, Maren, I'm not putting anything down until it's delivered."

"Fever makes liars of everyone," I murmured, going under again. "Even engineers."

"Not this one," he said, to the fire, to the rain, to the dark. "Not anymore."

* * *

I woke properly on the third day, weak as wet rope but clear-headed, the fever's passengers all disembarked. The redness in my arm had retreated behind the bandage. Isandro had rigged a stretcher-pole arrangement in the boat and stockpiled what food remained, which was not much, and stood looking at the river with the map case slung across his chest like a bandolier.

"We lose a day resting," he said, "maybe two. The monsoon won't wait."

"Then we don't wait either." I got upright, tested the arm, found it would obey about sixty percent of orders. Good enough. Boats run on sixty percent all the time. "And Isandro. Last night—or the night before, the nights are all one night—I made you promise something."

"You did."

"Do you remember the terms?"

"Names public. Even if it destroys me." He said it flatly, without flinching, and I searched his face for the loophole, the escape hatch, the smooth comfortable lie waiting in the wings. I didn't find one. What I found was scarier: a man who hadn't decided yet whether he meant it, standing at the door of meaning it, hand on the latch.

"Good," I said. "Hold onto that. And when we get to the pass, and you see whatever it is we're going to see—remember that I'm watching. Not to catch you. To witness you. There's a difference."

He nodded once, and helped me into the boat, and we pushed out of the backwater into the brown swollen current, toward the gorge, toward the eight days, toward the mountain.

Behind us the timber shed shrank to a gray tooth in the green. Ahead, low on the horizon, half-hidden in rain, the peaks above Vellmark Pass had appeared for the first time—dark, close, patient as a debt.

And on the far bank, maybe half a mile downstream, a thin thread of woodsmoke rose where no camp should be.

Someone else was on the river.
