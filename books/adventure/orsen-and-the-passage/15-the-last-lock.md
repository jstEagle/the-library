## 15. The Last Lock

The lock appeared out of the rain the way old sins do — gradually, then all at once.

One moment we were pushing up the last reach of the Vellmark channel with the monsoon chewing at our wake, and the next there it was: two stone towers rising out of the flood like the shoulders of something buried alive, and between them, a gate. A wall of green-black iron, furred with twenty years of moss, holding back nothing in particular because the water on both sides had long since agreed to be the same height. The upper gates stood open to the sky. The lower gates — the ones that mattered, the ones that would let us into the pass itself — were shut.

Jammed, Maren said, when she saw my face.

Rusted, I said, which was worse, because rust is honest. Rot can be patched. Rust is just time, made visible, and I owed this gate twenty years of it personally.

We tied off under the leeward tower, where an overhang of rotten scaffolding gave us a roof that leaked only philosophically, and I climbed the wet iron rungs to the lock keeper's platform while Maren watched from the deck with her arms crossed and her expression arranged into its usual verdict: *I am here because I have no better option, and don't you forget it.*

Fair enough. I'd have crossed my arms too, if I'd been her.

The mechanism was exactly as I remembered it, which is to say it was exactly as I had helped design it, back when I still believed designing things well was a form of virtue. Two capstans, one per gate leaf, geared down through a worm drive so that twelve men — the standard lock crew, four to a bar, three bars per capstan — could swing forty tons of iron against a current. The Northern Rail Company built everything for crews of twelve. It built nothing for one man with bad shoulders and a guilty conscience, though I suppose that market hadn't occurred to them.

I put my hand on the first capstan bar and the wood came away in my glove like stale bread.

* * *

"Tell me again why we can't go around," Maren called up, not really asking. She'd asked twice already on the approach, and both times I'd given her the engineer's answer, which is the answer you give when you want the questioner to stop asking.

This time I gave her the true one. "Because the pass mouth is flooded to the crown of the arch. The only way in is to raise the water level in the lock chamber so we float over the sill — and the only way to do that is to open these gates, and the only way to open these gates—" I patted the capstan like a horse you're about to ask a terrible favor of, "—is this."

"And the company?"

"The company has a launch. The company has a warrant. The company does not have a key." I looked down at her through the rain. "Nobody comes through here anymore. That's rather the point of everything."

She absorbed that the way she absorbed most things — silently, and with an accounting I wouldn't see until later, when she presented the invoice. Then she climbed up beside me, boots slipping on the rungs, and stood at the second capstan without being asked.

"There are two," she said.

"There are two."

"How many men did they design this for?"

"Twelve."

She looked at me. Rain ran off her chin. "Then we'll be slow," she said, "and we'll be angry about it," and she set her shoulder to the bar.

I want to record, for whoever finds this account, that Maren Solheim worked a lock capstan designed for four grown men, alone, for six hours, in a monsoon, with a healing wound in her side, and complained exactly once. The complaint was about my singing. She was right to complain. I sing like a man drowning in a different river than the one he's actually in.

* * *

Here is what cranking a dead machine for hours does to your head: it empties it. Not peacefully, the way meditation promises. Emptily, the way a gutted house is empty — every room exposed, every floorboard creaking under inspection.

The worm gear turned one degree for every full revolution of the capstan. I know because I'd specified it. One degree. The lower gates needed perhaps ninety degrees of swing to clear their pockets. Do the arithmetic a man does when his body is occupied and his mind is not permitted to be: ninety degrees, times however many revolutions, times two gates, divided by two people, equals a very long time to think about what you've done.

So I thought about it.

Not the collapse itself — I'd been thinking about that for twenty years, professionally, the way a man thinks about a debt collector. I mean the smaller thing underneath it, the thing I'd never said aloud even to myself in plain words: that I had not been forced. That's the confession I keep circling and never landing on. Yes, Halvard Renn held the promotion out like bait. Yes, he told me what the report would say before I wrote it. But there was a moment — I can place it precisely, in the survey office at Vellmark Junction, lamplight, the smell of ink and cold tea — when I understood perfectly what signing would cost eleven men, and I signed anyway, because I was twenty-six and the alternative was a career spent in the mud with the mud-people, and I chose the lamp over the mud.

Every turn of the wheel since dawn has been an installment on that choice. I counted them for a while. Then I stopped counting, because counting was a way of making it finite, and it isn't.

"You're doing the face," Maren said around hour four.

"What face?"

"The one where you're being tried by a court only you can see." She heaved her bar through another quarter-turn; the gear answered with a groan like a waking animal. "Save it. Courts that only you can see always acquit. It's the ones other people can see that matter."

"That's remarkably cynical."

"I'm remarkably experienced." She paused, breath ragged, hands re-gripping. "My brother used to say the opposite, actually. He said conscience was the one judge who couldn't be bribed." Her jaw tightened. "He was nineteen. What did he know."

I didn't answer. There was nothing I could say that wasn't either a lie or a wound, and I'd spent enough of both for one lifetime.

* * *

At midday the river behind us announced itself.

You feel it before you hear it — a change in the boat's tether, a shiver in the mooring lines, the kind of pressure drop you get in your ears before thunder. Then the sound arrived, rolling up the channel: not the monsoon's ordinary roar but a deeper note underneath it, the voice of a river remembering it used to be a glacier's errand boy.

The surge. Early, even for an early monsoon. Somewhere upstream, the headwaters had taken the week's rain all at once, and all of it was coming down the Vellmark channel toward us, toward the lock, toward the pass — and if the pass stayed sealed, toward the drowned farmland and the low country beyond, which was exactly how Maren's family had lost everything the first time.

"We don't stop," Maren said. She hadn't looked up from her bar. She'd heard it too. Everyone who lives on water learns to hear it in their spine before their ears.

"We don't stop," I agreed, and spat on my palms, and kept turning.

There is a particular misery in physical labor performed against a clock you cannot see. My shoulders had gone from burning to numb somewhere in the third hour, which I understood to be a warning rather than a mercy. The capstan bars — I'd replaced the rotted originals with spars from the boat's spare rigging — flexed visibly under load now, because the gates had begun, at last, to move, and moving iron resists differently than resting iron. Every quarter-degree was a negotiation. The gate would groan, catch, hold, and then give all at once, swinging a hand's width through black water, and the whole platform would shudder as if the mountain itself had shifted its weight.

By late afternoon the first gate stood open a body's width. Water began to move through the gap — not much, a dark tongue curling into the lock chamber, tasting it.

Maren sat down hard on the grating. Her hands were ruined; the rope burns from the depot ambush had reopened, and now blisters had opened on top of those, and beneath it all her knuckles were the color of storm bruise. She looked at her palms with clinical detachment, the way you'd look at someone else's problem.

"One more gate," she said.

"One more gate."

"And then what? We pole into a flooded mountain while a flood chases us, to find—" She stopped herself. I watched her decide not to finish the sentence, and I loved her a little for the deciding, and hated myself a great deal for knowing what the unfinished half was: *to find whether anyone is left to blame you.*

"And then whatever's there," I said. "That's all any of us ever get. Whatever's there."

* * *

The second capstan was worse.

The starboard mechanism had flooded at some point in its long abandonment — the grease pits were full of silt and standing water, and the worm gear had seized in three places. I spent an hour lying on my back in the gear pit with a pry bar and a tin of tallow we'd been saving for the boat's stern gland, persuading rust to remember it used to be iron. Maren held a lantern over me and offered commentary.

"You're smiling," she said at one point.

"I'm concentrating."

"You're lying in filth, in the rain, fixing a machine for the company that ruined both our families, and you're smiling."

I lay there a moment, wrench in hand, water dripping off the gear teeth onto my face. She wasn't wrong. And the reason wasn't complicated, only shameful: this was the first work in twenty years I'd done without flinching. Every job since Vellmark had been a disguise — surveyor, inspector, clerk, ghost. This was repair. Actual repair, of an actual broken thing, that would stay fixed. My hands remembered how to love this work, and my hands had apparently never gotten the memo about what the rest of me had done.

"It's the penance rate," I said finally. "It's very competitive."

She snorted. From Maren, that was applause.

The gear freed with a crack that echoed off the towers like a rifle shot, and we both froze, listening, half-expecting the sound to bring company down the channel. Nothing came but rain. But we'd both heard the deeper note again, closer now, and neither of us mentioned it, and we went back to work faster.

* * *

Dusk. The second gate cracked open a foot, then two, and the lock chamber began to fill — slowly, through the narrow gaps, the black water rising up the mossy walls in a tide line you could watch climb if you had nothing better to do, which we didn't, because the capstans were done and there was nothing left but waiting and the river's voice getting louder by the minute.

We got the boat into the chamber while there was still light enough to see the sill. Maren handled the lines; I handled the pole; between us we committed a series of maneuvers that would have made my instructors weep, and the *Vela* — Maren's boat, scarred, patched, cracked below the waterline in a way I'd temporarily persuaded her not to think about — slid into the stone throat of the lock and sat there, rising, while the gates hung open behind us like a held breath.

And then, in the last grey light, I saw the marks.

They were on the inside of the near gate, at the height of a man's reaching arm, cut into the rust with something harder than a fingernail but not by much. Tallies. Rows of them, grouped in fives, hundreds of them, thousands — climbing the iron in columns, some neat, some desperate, some trailing off mid-stroke as if the hand had been called away.

Maren saw them at the same moment. Her lantern found them one group at a time, and I heard her breathing change.

"Somebody counted," she said quietly. "Somebody stood right here and counted days."

"Or waited," I said. "Counting and waiting look the same after a while."

Her lantern rose higher. Above the tallies, cut deep and deliberate, was a single word. The rust had half-eaten it, but the letters were scored deeply enough to survive:

**OUT**

Just that. No name, no date. One word, scratched by someone who had stood in this exact spot, in this exact dark, and wanted whoever came next to know that leaving was possible.

Maren touched the letters with two fingers, the way you touch a face in a photograph. Three years she'd carried her brother's disappearance like a stone sewn into her coat, and here was a stranger's word on a stranger's gate, and it hit her harder than any comfort could have, because hope doesn't care whose it is.

Behind us, downstream, the river raised its voice another octave.

The surge had reached the lower channel. I could hear it taking the boathouses, the pilings, the drowned orchards — a long grinding roar, patient and total, coming for the lock at the speed of gravity. When it hit the open gates it would tear them off their hinges, and the chamber would become rapids, and the *Vela* would become driftwood unless we were through the pass mouth and into the high tunnel before it arrived.

"How long?" Maren asked. She didn't look at me. She was still looking at the word.

I listened to the river the way I'd taught myself to listen twenty years ago, back when reading water was my trade instead of my alibi. "An hour," I said. "Perhaps less."

She turned. In the lantern light her face was exhausted and lit from somewhere underneath, and she pointed at the pass mouth ahead — the black arch where the mountain swallowed the canal, air breathing cold out of it, carrying a smell I hadn't smelled in twenty years and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a voice:

Woodsmoke.

Fresh woodsmoke, drifting out of a mountain that had been sealed and abandoned for two decades.

"Someone's in there," Maren said. "Someone's *alive* in there."

"Yes," I said, and took up the pole, and pushed us into the dark.
