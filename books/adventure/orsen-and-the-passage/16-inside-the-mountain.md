## 16. Inside the Mountain

**Isandro**

The mouth of the Vellmark Pass was smaller than I remembered.

That was the first lie my memory told me, standing there in the drizzle with a lantern hissing in my hand. In the survey drawings it was a grand arched portal cut into granite, wide enough for two locomotives abreast, crowned with the company's name chiseled in letters you could read from the river. What remained was a wound. The arch had come down in the collapse—my collapse, I still couldn't say *the* without flinching—and what the engineers hadn't bothered to clear had been left to slump into something like a cave mouth, half-swallowed by twenty years of alder and blackberry.

Maren stood beside me with her arm bound tight against her ribs, gray-faced, refusing to be gray-faced about it. She'd slept badly. We both had. The lock station behind us smelled of rust and mouse droppings and old paper, and neither of us had wanted to go back inside it after we found the wall.

"Say something," she said. "You've been staring at that rock for a full minute."

"I'm trying to remember where the original heading was driven."

"And?"

"And I'm remembering that I only ever saw it on paper." I adjusted the lantern's wick. "I certified the closure of a tunnel I never walked. That should tell you everything about how the Northern Rail Company did its arithmetic."

"It tells me everything about you," Maren said, but there was no edge left in it. Somewhere between the manifest and the scratched messages behind the bricked wall, her anger had stopped being a weapon and become a weight we were both carrying. I didn't know which was worse.

We went in at noon, because I wanted daylight at our backs for as long as it lasted. Inside the first gallery the daylight gave up almost immediately, and it was lamplight or nothing.

The air changed within thirty paces. Anyone who has spent time underground knows the feeling—the way a tunnel breathes when it's been shut, the stale mineral smell of stone that hasn't been disturbed, cold as a cellar and twice as final. I braced myself for it.

It didn't come.

"Maren," I said. "Stop."

She stopped. "What?"

"Breathe. Tell me what you taste."

She breathed. Her eyes found mine in the lantern glow. "Smoke," she said slowly. "Old smoke. And—" She turned her head, testing the dark like a boatwoman reading current. "Moving air. There's a draft."

There was. A faint, steady pull toward the interior, cool against my wet face, carrying with it the ghost of woodsmoke and something else—tallow, maybe, or boiled greens. The smell of people living. My hands started shaking so badly I had to set the lantern on a fallen tie until they steadied.

Twenty years. Eleven men sealed behind a certified collapse. And the mountain was breathing like something with lungs.

"Isandro." Maren's voice had gone very quiet. "If there's air moving, there's an opening somewhere. If there's smoke—"

"Don't say it."

"—someone's been in here."

I picked up the lantern because my hands needed a job. "The messages on the wall were dated months after the closure," I said. "We knew someone survived the first days. But this—" I gestured at the draft, the smoke-smell, the whole impossible warmth of it. "This isn't survival. Survival is a week of screaming and then silence. This is *keeping a fire.*"

"Then stop standing here doing arithmetic," Maren said, "and start walking."

* * *

**Maren**

He walked like a man going to his own trial, which I suppose he was, though not the kind he expected. I followed close enough to see the lantern light on his back and no closer, because I'd learned something about Isandro Iversen in the past weeks: he was most honest when you watched him from slightly behind, where he forgot to perform.

The first gallery was a graveyard of the company's ambitions. Rails bent into corkscrews where the rock had come down. A handcar crushed flat as a pressed flower. Timber shoring split lengthwise, furred white with some pale mold that ate the lamplight. Water ran everywhere—not rushing, just seeping, a hundred small voices whispering down the walls. My boots found water over the ankles within the first hundred meters, cold enough to make my teeth ache, and my arm throbbed where the bullet had torn through it at the fuel depot, and I thought: *Eirik walked through this.*

My brother. Three years missing, and I'd spent them telling myself the river took him, the river takes everyone eventually, it's honest that way. Now I was walking into a mountain following a trail of smoke, and I didn't know if I wanted to find him or find his grave, and both possibilities hurt in different places.

"How far to the chamber?" I asked.

"The drawings show a junction about four hundred meters in. The main heading forks—one branch runs up toward the air shafts, one continues to the far portal." Isandro's voice echoed strangely, flattened by water and rubble. "The collapse was at the junction, if my memory serves."

"Does your memory serve?"

"It served me a promotion," he said bitterly. "Whether it serves anything else is an open question."

We climbed over a fall of rock where the ceiling had let go—a slope of shattered granite, sharp as broken crockery, that we crossed on hands and knees with the lantern held high. On the far side the tunnel narrowed and the water deepened to mid-shin. Isandro tested each step with a probe made from a broken tent pole, and I noticed he counted under his breath, and I realized he was measuring—the engineer's habit, pacing out distances, building the map in his head even now. Even here. Especially here.

"You could have been a good man," I said, before I could stop myself. "With that head. You could have built things."

The lantern light bobbed. He didn't turn around. "I did build things," he said. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

**Isandro**

The junction was exactly where my memory said it would be, which frightened me more than anything else that day. Because if my memory was right about the geography, it might be right about other things too—about the tonnage of rock above us, about the drainage, about the eleven men who had been working the night shift when the charges meant to open the new branch brought the old roof down instead. About the report I signed saying the collapse was unforeseeable. It had been foreseen. I had seen it. I'd written the numbers myself and then written different ones above them, because Halvard Sørensen told me to, and because the alternative was a career that ended at thirty-one.

The junction itself was a wall of collapsed material, forty feet of it, spilled from ceiling to floor like a poured foundation. No one was getting through that. No one ever had.

But the draft was stronger here. And it wasn't coming from the rubble.

It was coming from the side gallery to the north—the one that ran up toward the air shafts. And someone had cleared it. Not recently; the work was old, the rubble stacked with care along the walls in low dry-stone courses, almost neat. But the passage beyond stood open, head-height, floored with planking laid over the mud. Planking. Cut and fitted and maintained.

Maren put her hand flat against one of the boards. "Someone keeps this path," she said.

"Someone has kept it for years." I knelt and looked closer. The planks were worn smooth in the center, polished by feet, by decades of feet maybe. Along one edge, small notches had been cut at intervals—surveyor's marks, or a tally, or a prayer. "Maren, look at this timber. This is dressed lumber. Company grade. Someone has been salvaging and cutting and fitting down here for—"

"For longer than three years," she said. Her voice had gone strange. "Isandro. If they've been here longer than three years—"

"Then your brother may have found more than a grave." I stood. The draft moved against my cheek, warm now, unmistakably warm, and threaded with woodsmoke and boiled greens and underneath it all the faint animal smell of human habitation. "He may have found a door."

We went on. The plank path climbed gently, following the old haulage gradient, and the water fell away behind us, and the air grew warmer still. Twice we passed side alcoves where the rubble had been cleared and shelves had been built—rough shelving, holding clay jars sealed with wax, bundles of rag, coils of good rope hung on pegs. A storeroom. A *larder*. Twenty years underground and someone had built a larder.

At the third alcove Maren stopped dead. One of the jars had broken and spilled its contents across the shelf: dried fish, silvered with salt, perhaps a season old. Beside it lay a tin cup, dented, and a folding knife with a handle wrapped in tarred cord.

I knew the knife. Not the knife—I knew the *wrapping*. Every deckhand on the river boats wrapped their knife handles that way; it was a fashion of the wharves, a dozen years old at most.

Maren picked it up with both hands, the way you lift something holy or explosive. "This is Eirik's wrapping," she whispered. "This is—he learned it from me. I taught him the cord wrap when he was twelve." She turned to me, and her face in the lantern light was terrible and lit with hope at once, hope like a fever. "He was *here*. He came through here. He—"

"Maren."

"He went *out*, Isandro. Whatever's written on these walls, whatever happened twenty years ago—Eirik walked this same floor. Maybe weeks ago. Maybe—"

"Maybe," I said, because I couldn't give her more than that, and I wouldn't give her less.

She put the knife in her coat, against her chest, next to the map case. Then she wiped her face with her sleeve, once, briskly, the way she did everything, and said, "Keep walking."

* * *

**Maren**

The chamber opened without warning. One moment the passage was squeezing us between walls furred with mold; the next it widened into a vaulted space tall enough that our lamplight couldn't find the ceiling, a natural cavern the company must have used for storage or shelter during the driving of the tunnel. And it was warm. Genuinely, blessedly warm, the first warmth I'd felt since the monsoon caught us on the river, and I hadn't known how cold I'd been until the cold began to leave.

In the center of the chamber burned a fire.

Not a large fire—a careful one, banked low in a ring of stones, feeding on driftwood and coal scraps, drawing air through some channel we couldn't see. It had been tended. Recently. The coals glowed orange and patient, and above them hung a tripod of green branches with a blackened kettle swinging from it, and beside the fire, arranged with a tidiness that undid me completely, sat three wooden bowls, a stack of folded blankets, and a child's carved toy boat no bigger than my hand.

A child's toy. Underground. In a mountain that was supposed to be a tomb.

Isandro stood absolutely still, the lantern hanging forgotten from his fingers, and I watched twenty years of certainty die on his face. He had carried this place inside him as a grave. He had confessed to me in the rain that he'd signed its death certificate, and I had hated him for it, and I had stayed anyway, and now here was the proof that the certificate had been a lie all along—that the mountain he'd helped bury alive had refused, quietly, stubbornly, generation after generation, to be dead.

"They never left," he said. His voice cracked in the middle of it. "They never—they *lived*."

"Look at the fire," I said. "Look at the kettle. Someone's coming back, Isandro. Soon."

And that was when the awe curdled, exactly as he'd once described fear to me—awe curdling into urgency, milk into poison. Because if people lived here, then the company men with their dynamite and their warrants weren't hunting a map anymore. They were hunting *this*. A community. Children. The eleven names and everyone who'd grown from them, and the company had already shown us at the fuel depot what they did to witnesses.

"We have to find them first," I said. "Before the enforcers find the smoke."

Isandro nodded slowly. Then he raised the lantern, and its light swung across the far wall of the chamber—and stopped.

The wall was covered in writing. Chalk, charcoal, paint, generations of it, names upon names upon names, columns of them, dated, some faded to ghosts and some fresh enough to smear. And at the top of the newest column, in a firm river-wharf hand I would have known anywhere, because I'd seen it on every letter Eirik ever sent me:

*Eirik Voss — went out — coastward — will send word.*

Below it, fresher still, in different ink:

*Word not yet come.*

Maren read it twice. Then she reached out and touched her brother's name, and the fire popped, and somewhere deeper in the mountain—down a passage none of us had entered yet—we heard footsteps. More than one pair. Coming toward the light.
