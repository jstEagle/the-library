## 17. The Living Record

The footsteps stopped.

That was the worst part, I think. Not the coming—coming you can brace for, the way you brace for a wave—but the stopping. Whoever walked down that passage had heard us too, and was now standing in the dark doing exactly what Isandro and I were doing in the light: deciding whether the strangers at the fire were going to be trouble.

"Hello?" I called. My voice went down the tunnel and came back thinner. "We're not company."

A long silence. Then a voice, dry as old rope: "Everyone says that first."

Isandro's hand found my shoulder—not pulling me back, just anchoring, the way he'd learned to do somewhere between the rapids and the confession and the mudbank where I'd nearly left him. He stepped forward with the lamp raised, and I watched his face do the thing it did when engineering met reality: the quick flick of assessment, the tallying of load-bearing stone and air flow and escape routes, all of it running under the fear like current under ice.

"We followed the scratchings," he said. "Behind the bricked wall at the lock station. The dates—"

"We know what the dates say." The voice came closer, and with it a shape: a woman, small and broad-shouldered, gray-haired but moving like someone who still hauled nets for a living. She carried her own lamp, and behind her, half-shy, half-suspicious, came two others—a young man with coal dust worked so deep into his knuckles it looked like tattooing, and a girl of maybe twelve who stared at me with the frank hunger of someone who has not seen a new face in a very long time.

"You've been down here," I said. Stupidly. Obviously. But my mind was still snagged on Eirik's name on that wall, on *word not yet come*, and everything else came out crooked.

"Sixty-one years," the woman said. "My grandmother was born behind that wall. My father never saw the sun until he was nine years old and strong enough to crawl the air shaft." She looked at Isandro then, and something in her face hardened like mortar setting. "You'll be the railway man."

It wasn't a question. It was a verdict awaiting sentencing.

"I'm the railway man," Isandro said quietly. "Yes."

"My name is Sela. This is Torren. The girl is Wren, and she's mine, and if either of you brought company up that river, you'll answer to me before you answer to them." She turned. "Come away from the wall. There are things you should see before you say another word about names."

* * *

They took us deeper.

Not far—the mountain didn't allow for far—but through a low gallery where the ceiling dripped a slow count into a channel cut by hand, generations of hands, guiding seep water into a cistern the size of a rowboat. Past sleeping alcoves curtained with sacking. Past a rack of tools worn to shapes no factory ever made. The community wasn't large—I counted perhaps thirty souls' worth of bedding, though only these three seemed to be awake at this hour—but it was *kept*. Every surface scrubbed. Every rope coiled. A life maintained in the dark with the discipline of people who understood that sloppiness meant death, and had understood it for three generations.

And everywhere, everywhere, paper.

Sheets pinned to the stone with iron nails gone orange with rust. Ledgers stacked in oilcloth. A whole wall of them, in fact, and Sela stopped before it with the air of a woman presenting evidence at a trial she'd been waiting her whole life to hold.

"The Living Record," she said. "Grandmother started it. Her mother kept the first years on slate until we could get paper smuggled in. Every person sealed in this pass on the day your company closed it. Every one. Name, trade, age, what they were owed." Her finger moved down a column, and I realized with a lurch that the column was *long*. "Eleven went unaccounted for when the collapse came. Eleven families told their men were dead and buried, because dead men don't ask for wages and dead men don't have mothers who write letters. But eleven weren't dead. Nine survived the first weeks. And their children, and their children's children—" she gestured at Torren, at Wren, at the sacking curtains, the cistern, the whole breathing dark—"are standing in front of you."

I looked at Isandro. He was very still, the way he'd been still on the boat when I asked him what he knew and he gave me half of it. He was reading the wall the way he read everything—thoroughly, hungrily, guiltily—and I knew that somewhere in those columns were names he'd signed away with a surveyor's flourish twenty years ago, and that every one of them was a weight he'd been carrying without telling me its number.

"Show her," Sela said to Torren.

Torren lifted down a ledger—fat, water-spotted, its spine mended with fishing line—and opened it to a page near the end. The handwriting changed there, older to younger, generation to generation, like sediment layers in reverse.

"The roll of the sealed," Sela said. "Read it if you need to. Most folk from outside need to."

I didn't need to. I already knew what I was looking for. My eyes went down the page hunting it the way a tongue hunts a broken tooth, and there it was, third from the bottom, in a firm wharf-hand I'd have known anywhere:

*Eirik Voss — went out — coastward — will send word.*

Below, in different ink, newer:

*Word not yet come.*

"He left four years ago," Sela said, watching me. "Through the air shaft, same as the others who chose to go. There were six of them over the years. Four made it to the valley. Two we never heard of again." She tapped the ledger. "Your brother stayed with us eight months first. Helped widen the shaft. Good with knots, he was. Talked about a sister who ran boats and didn't take nonsense from anyone." A pause, and something almost gentle moved through her voice. "He talked about you the way people talk about a lighthouse."

I sat down on a supply crate because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about grief: it has a shape, and you build your whole life around that shape, the way a tree grows around a fence wire. For three years my grief had been a drowned man's shape—cold, heavy, final, a body at the bottom of a flooded pass that I carried upriver inside my chest. I had rehearsed finding him dead. I had rehearsed finding his bones. I had even, in the black hours between watches, rehearsed finding him alive and having absolutely no idea what to say to him.

What I had never rehearsed was this: finding out he had walked out of the mountain four years ago, crossed the valley, gone coastward—

—and not written to me.

*Will send word. Word not yet come.*

"Why?" I heard myself ask. It came out flat, which was a mercy, because underneath the flatness was a howl with a four-year fuse. "Why wouldn't he—"

"That," said Sela, "is a question we've asked ourselves every day since. And there's an answer we don't like, and an answer we like less."

"Give me both."

"The one we don't like: he reached the coast and died before he could send word. Accidents happen. Rivers happen. Men happen." She folded her arms. "The one we like less is harder to say, so I'll let the railway man say it, since it's his company's specialty."

Every head turned to Isandro. He had gone the color of the chalk on that wall, and I felt a flash of the old anger—*you knew, you always knew more than you said*—but it passed quick, because whatever he was carrying now wasn't concealment. It was arithmetic. I'd learned to tell the difference.

"If the company tracked Oskar," he said slowly, "they may have been watching for anyone who came out of the pass. Anyone connected to it. A letter from a man who officially died in the Vellmark collapse would be..." He stopped. Started again. "It would be a thread. You pull it, and the whole buried lie unravels. Eleven men alive after the closure means the report I certified was false. Means the company knew, or worse, arranged it. They couldn't allow a thread like that loose in the world."

"So he stayed silent," I said, "to keep *them* safe." I looked around the gallery—at Wren, at the sacking curtains, at sixty years of hidden lamps. "To keep *this* safe."

"And maybe," Isandro said, very carefully, "to keep you safe. If the company traced a letter from Eirik Voss to Maren Voss, boatwoman of the lower reaches..."

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Three years of small things rearranged themselves in my memory with a horrible new orderliness: the inspector at Harrow Quay who'd asked twice about my cargo; the season my brother's old friends stopped writing back; the way company men always seemed to know which landings I'd used before I used them. I had thought it was coincidence. I had thought a lot of things were coincidence.

"They've been watching me," I said. "This whole time. Because of him."

"Or because of me," Isandro said. "Likely both. We were two threads walking toward each other. That's why they sent enforcers instead of inspectors."

Wren, who had been silent the whole time, tugged Sela's sleeve and whispered something. Sela shook her head, but the girl persisted, and finally Sela sighed and translated: "She wants to know if the mountain is opening. She's never seen the sky except through the air shaft."

"Not yet," Sela said. Then, to us, with an edge: "Unless these two have brought the sky with them, or the company, or both."

* * *

We told them. All of it—or nearly all. The map, Oskar, the enforcers, the ambush at the fuel depot, the blood Maren left on the deck planks (that part cost me; I told it looking at the cistern rather than at Isandro), the lock, the wall, the scratchings. Sela listened the way old sailors listen to weather reports: motionless, cataloguing, waiting for the part that mattered to her.

When I finished, she was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "You blew nothing yet? You've opened nothing?"

"The final lock is behind us," Isandro said. "Jammed. I cranked it open by hand—it took most of a night. The pass mouth itself is still sealed by the collapse, as far as I know. The original bore is choked."

"But the air shaft isn't." Sela's eyes had gone bright and hard. "And neither is the old maintenance adit on the north face, the one your company's maps forgot because your company's maps were drawn by liars." She let that sit. Isandro took it like a man taking a punch he'd agreed to. "If the enforcers come with dynamite, they'll come for the mouth and the shaft. Those are the openings they know."

"How do you know what they know?" I asked.

"Because they've known about us for years." Sela said it simply, terribly. "Why do you think the supplies come smuggled? Why do you think we pay a man in the valley to buy our flour and salt? The company doesn't leave us alone because it's merciful. It leaves us alone because thirty people living in the dark, quiet, asking nothing, is a secret that stays buried. The moment anyone digs—" she nodded at Isandro, at me, at the whole doomed expedition of us—"the arithmetic changes. You haven't brought hope up that river, boatwoman. You've brought witnesses. And witnesses are the one cargo this mountain can't carry."

The words landed in my chest like ballast dropped from height. I thought of the ledger, of Eirik's name, of *will send word*, and of a coast I suddenly needed to reach more than I needed air.

"Then we move them out," I said. "All of them. Before the enforcers come. My boat won't carry thirty, but there are other boats—"

"On a monsoon river, with a wounded pilot, through a blockade." Sela's voice wasn't cruel. It was just true, the way rivers are true. "Girl, we've survived down here by never betting lives on a single crossing."

"Then we don't bet on one." Isandro had come forward, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not the engineer's cold tally, not the fugitive's flinch. Something steadier. "The north adit. Show me. If it's sound, it's a second way out, and a second way out means we don't need thirty berths—we need ten, twice. And if the company comes for the mouth and the shaft while we're moving..." He looked at the dripping ceiling, the hand-cut channel, the cistern, sixty years of stubborn living stonework. "There are ways to flood an approach corridor that look like accident. I designed two of them, once, for lines I'm not proud of."

Sela studied him for a long time. Wren studied him longer, with a child's merciless clarity.

"You'd use their own tricks," Sela said.

"I'd use the only language they respect."

"And afterward? When the water settles and the questions start? What happens to the railway man who certified the lie?" She jerked her chin at the Living Record wall, at the columns of names. "Those names go public. That was always the price of our patience. Grandmother's terms, written the year I was born: *when the mountain opens, the truth opens with it.*"

Isandro looked at the wall. At the eleven names that started it all. At Eirik's entry, and the bitter little postscript beneath it. Then he looked at me, and I saw him weigh the promise I'd wrung from him in fever, on the river, half-delirious—*whatever we find, the names go public, even if it destroys you*—and I saw him decide, finally and fully, to mean it.

"Every name," he said. "Mine included. Signed, dated, witnessed." A ghost of the old dry humor surfaced. "I've had twenty years of practice forging documents, Sela. Consider this the first honest one."

Something in the old woman's shoulders loosened—a centimeter, no more. She turned to Torren. "Wake the others. Quietly. And fetch the north-adit lamp."

Torren went. Wren lingered, staring at me with those hungry eyes, and then she did a strange thing: she reached into her shirt and drew out a small object on a cord, and held it toward me.

It was a whistle. River-wharf make, brass gone green, the kind boatmen's kin wear so they can be called home across a crowded quay. I knew it instantly. I'd bought it. I'd hung it around a fourteen-year-old's neck the day he signed onto his first crew, and I'd told him: *anywhere in the world, you blow that, and I'll hear it.*

"Eirik's," Wren said. "He left it with us. Said—" she screwed up her face, reciting words carried faithfully through four years of dark— "*said, if my sister comes, give her this, and tell her I didn't blow it because I wasn't lost. Tell her I knew exactly where I was.*"

I took the whistle. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Somewhere in the walls of my chest, the drowned-man shape of my grief shifted, cracked, and refused—with a stubbornness I recognized as family—to be either relief or despair. He was alive. He had walked out. He had chosen silence, for reasons that might be noble or cowardly or some river-mixed measure of both, and he was somewhere on a coast with a brass whistle in a pocket or a grave or a drawer, and *word not yet come*.

I closed my fist around it.

"Sela," I said. "How fast can thirty people walk?"

She smiled for the first time, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman who had spent sixty years preparing for exactly this night and had begun to suspect it would never come.

"Faster than dynamite," she said. "If we start now. Because Torren just told me something, and you'll want to hear it standing up: there's smoke on the north ridge. Two fires. Company green."

Above us, faint through sixty feet of stone, the mountain began, very softly, to drip faster—as if it too had heard, and was counting down.
