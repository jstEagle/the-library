## 18. Went Out

The elder's name was Halvard, and he poured tea like a man who had never once been in a hurry, which under the circumstances struck me as either profound wisdom or a very committed form of denial.

We sat in a chamber the size of a modest chapel, lit by oil lamps and a fire that had, by all appearances, been burning continuously since before I learned to lie on official documents. The walls were stacked with crates—flour, lamp oil, tinned fish with labels so faded they might have said anything. A rope line ran along one wall with children's clothing hung to dry. Children. There were *children* down here, born in a mountain I had helped nail shut.

"You've been here," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended. "All this time. Twenty years."

"Seventeen for me," Halvard said. "I was fourteen when the gallery came down. My father was one of the eleven." He said it the way you'd say *my father was a carpenter*. A fact worn smooth from handling. "The others went out through the air shaft over the years. In ones and twos. It opens above the tree line, on the north face. Dangerous climb, but not impossible."

"And those who went out—" Maren's voice cracked like ice underfoot. She'd been sitting rigid since we entered, her wounded arm cradled against her ribs, her eyes moving from face to face as if memorizing them. "They left. They just walked away."

"Some did." Halvard refilled her cup without asking. "Some couldn't. The dark does something to a person after enough years, miss. You stop believing in doors."

I thought about that longer than was comfortable. I had spent twenty years walking through doors at speed, always leaving one behind me, and I had told myself it was freedom. Apparently it could also be a symptom.

"The journal," Maren said. "The names. Show me again."

Halvard's daughter—a girl of perhaps nineteen with her father's unhurried hands—brought the ledger across the room. It was a railway supply book originally, its printed columns repurposed, and the handwriting inside changed three or four times as the years passed, as if the office of record-keeper had been handed down like an heirloom. Eleven names at the top, each with a fate beside it. *Died in collapse.* *Died of fever, year two.* *Went out, spring.* *Went out, autumn.*

Maren's finger stopped on the fourth line.

*Joren Vask. Went out, year six. Coastward.*

"That's him," she whispered. "That's his hand. He always wrote his V's like that, like little hooks." She looked up at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before—not hope exactly, because hope is a young person's currency and Maren had spent hers long ago—but something adjacent to it. Something that hurt worse. "He got out. Isandro. He *got out*, and he never—"

"He never wrote," I finished quietly.

"Why? Why would he not write?" Her voice rose, and the children near the fire turned to look. "Three years I've been dragging rivers for a drowned man, and he walked out of this mountain six years ago and couldn't be bothered to send a single—"

"Maren." I put my hand over hers on the ledger. She didn't pull away, which after everything was its own small miracle. "Men who come out of places like this don't always come out whole. Whatever kept him from writing, it wasn't indifference. Men like your brother don't forget their sisters. They just can't always face what remembering costs."

She stared at me. "And how would you know?"

"Because I'm one," I said.

That landed between us and sat there. Halvard cleared his throat with the delicacy of a man who had spent seventeen years mediating disputes in a space too small for them.

"There's more you should know," he said. "About why we're still hidden. The company never stopped looking for this place. Not for us—for the liability. Every year a survey crew comes up the valley, and every year we seal the inner chambers and go quiet until they leave. Old Torvald—he was a fitter on the original line, first of our keepers—he used to say the company doesn't fear the dead. Dead men don't sue. What they fear is living witnesses with documents."

My stomach performed a slow, professional turn. "Documents."

"Torvald collected things. Pay records. The blasting schedule from the week of the collapse. Letters." Halvard's eyes settled on me with terrible mildness. "Letters from a junior surveyor certifying the rock face as stable eleven days before it wasn't. We've had copies for fifteen years. We never knew what to do with them. Now, it seems, the author has delivered himself."

Maren looked at me. I looked at the fire. Somewhere in the dark beyond the lamplight, water dripped with the patience of geological time.

"Well," I said. "Small world."

* * *

We spent what remained of the daylight—I say *daylight*, though down there day was a rumor carried in by lamps—in preparation, because Halvard, whatever else he was, was not a fool. If the community had survived seventeen years of being hunted, it was because they treated every arrival as a threat until proven otherwise.

"We have two ways out," he explained, unrolling a cloth map drawn in the same careful hand as the ledger. "The air shaft, north face—it takes a day's climb, impossible for the old ones and the children in this weather. And the lock passage you came through, which floods to the roof when the monsoon peaks. Which, according to the sound of that river, will be tomorrow night."

"Then we go through the lock passage tomorrow morning, ahead of the peak," Maren said. "I know the water levels now. There's a window."

"There is," Halvard agreed. "If nothing else goes wrong."

I want it noted, for the historical record, that I did not say *nothing else will go wrong*. I have learned some humility in twenty years of flight. But I confess I felt something dangerously adjacent to optimism, standing there among the lamps with a plan and a boatwoman who hadn't yet abandoned me on a mudbank, and I should have known better. Optimism, in my experience, is merely dread wearing its good coat.

It was near what passed for midnight when the girl—Halvard's daughter, Sela—came running from the watch post at the pass mouth, and I knew before she spoke. You learn to recognize the shape of bad news arriving. It has a particular footfall.

"Riders," she said. "Six of them. Company grey. And they've got mules carrying crates, and Torvald's old spyglass shows—" her voice caught— "it shows dynamite. A lot of it."

The chamber went very quiet. A child began to cry and was hushed. The fire popped, indifferent.

"They found the boat," Maren said flatly. "At the fuel depot. They tracked us up the channel."

"They didn't need to track us," I said, and heard my own voice go cold and level, the engineer's voice, the one I used for reading gauges before disasters. "They've known where the pass mouth is for twenty years. They were waiting for someone to lead them to whether anyone was still inside. We just confirmed it for them." I closed my eyes. "Oskar's map. Oskar himself. They let him run because a prospector poking at a dead pass looks like madness, but a riverboat captain and a former company engineer going in together looks like *retrieval*. We weren't fleeing them, Maren. We were the reconnaissance."

She hit me. Open-handed, across the ear, hard enough to ring my skull like a bell, and I took it, because the accounting was fair.

"You used us," she said. "You used *me*. All that confession-in-the-rain nobility, and the whole time you were—"

"No." I caught her wrist, gently, before the second blow. "Listen to me. I didn't know. I swear it on every map I should have burned. But knowing it now changes nothing about what happens next, so save the rest of your swings for the men with the dynamite."

Halvard had risen during this exchange, and the whole community with him, in that silent way of people who have drilled for this moment for seventeen years. "How long do we have?" he asked me.

I thought about the terrain at the pass mouth—the narrow defile, the spoil heaps, the old portal stonework. I thought about six men and a lot of explosive and the monsoon swelling behind us like a held breath.

"If they set charges properly, dawn," I said. "If they set them the way company men set charges when nobody's watching—sloppy, fast, wanting to be gone before the flood—then maybe mid-morning. Either way, we need to be moving before first light."

"Moving where?" Sela asked. "Both ways are death."

"Not both ways," I said, and the idea arrived fully formed, the way the worst ideas do, wearing its best clothes. "The air shaft opens onto the north face. The lock passage floods. But there's a third way, isn't there, Halvard? The old maintenance adit off the second gallery—the one that runs parallel to the river and comes out below the high-water mark. Torvald's maps show it."

Halvard went still. "That adit hasn't been opened in twenty years. Half of it's collapsed."

"Half," I said. "Which means the other half isn't. And I spent the first ten years of my career shoring up tunnels half as bad. Give me lamps, timber, and every able pair of hands, and I'll open it." I looked around the chamber—at the children, the old ones, the smooth-worn faces of people who had done nothing wrong except survive the thing I certified as survivable. "Because I am not letting this mountain close on innocent people twice. I've paid for one. I won't carry two."

Maren was watching me with an expression I couldn't read—anger and something else, braided together. "And if you can't open it in time?"

"Then we fight at the pass mouth," I said. "Six men against everyone in this room and a very motivated boatwoman."

Above us, faint through forty feet of rock, came a sound like thunder deciding where to strike. Or perhaps it was only the monsoon. Down here, in the dark, with the lamps guttering and the ledgers open and the dynamite waiting at the door, it was getting harder to tell the difference between the weather and the reckoning.

Halvard rolled up his map. "Wake everyone," he said to Sela. "Bring me the tool chest."

And I stood there in the firelight, a liar with a shovel finally pointed at the right grave, and waited for the mountain to decide what kind of man it would let me be.
