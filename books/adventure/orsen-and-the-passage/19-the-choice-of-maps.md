## 19. The Choice of Maps

The community had a name for itself, which struck me as either the bravest thing I'd ever heard or the saddest. They called themselves the Undermount, and they said it the way other people said the name of their town—plainly, without apology, as though living in a sealed pass for three generations were simply a matter of local geography.

There were thirty-one of them. Children who had never seen open sky except through the air shaft. An old woman named Petra who had been born down here and had opinions about the weather she'd never actually stood in. And ledgers—God, the ledgers. Rows of them, oilcloth-wrapped, recording births and deaths and the price of smuggled lamp oil going back to the year of the collapse. The year I had signed my name to a report that said no one was down here at all.

I didn't have time to feel anything about that. The enforcers were at the pass mouth with dynamite, and Halvard was arming farmers with pry bars, and I was standing in the firelight doing the arithmetic of my own cowardice.

Six men at the mouth. Halvard said six, though he'd only counted lanterns. Dynamite enough to bring the entrance gallery down, and the entrance gallery was the only way out that didn't involve crawling four hundred meters of air shaft with children and the old woman Petra, who had never walked four hundred meters anywhere in her life.

"They'll wait for morning," Halvard said. "Men like that don't like working in the dark. It's the one honest thing about them."

"They'll wait because the river's still rising," I said. "The approach road floods at the bend. If they go in too early they drown with the rest of us." I knew that road. I had surveyed it. There was very little about this mountain I hadn't touched with a transit and a plumb line, and every inch of it was a small confession.

Maren stood at the edge of the firelight with her arm bound and her face gray, and she was looking at me the way she'd looked at me on the mudbank in the storm, when I'd told her the truth and she'd nearly left me there to be rained on forever. Except now there was no time for her to hate me properly, which I suspected she resented.

"You have a plan," she said. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation wearing a question's coat.

"I have half a plan."

"Those are usually worse."

"Yes," I said. "In my experience they're mostly just lies with ambition."

* * *

Here is the thing about lying, and I am perhaps the Northern Rail Company's foremost living authority on the subject: a lie is only ever as good as what it costs you to tell it. The cheap lies, the ones that cost nothing, are the ones that get found out. The expensive ones—the ones where you hand over something you'd rather keep, something that bleeds—those hold.

I had spent twenty years telling cheap lies. A forged permit here, a false name there, a smooth answer at a checkpoint while two company men smiled at me and I smiled back with a face like a locked drawer. None of it had ever cost me anything except, gradually, everything.

So I sat down by the fire with Oskar's map on my knees and I thought about what I owned that was worth something.

Oskar's map was worth everything, obviously. The hand-drawn chart of the Vellmark Pass, the drowned galleries, the air shaft, the old lock passage—the only complete record of the mountain's bones that existed outside the company's sealed archives. If the enforcers got it, they could seal every entrance, every shaft, every crack a rat could squeeze through, and the Undermount would die down here in the dark with their beautiful ledgers and their weather opinions, and it would be my fault in a way that even I couldn't lie my way out of.

And I had my own map. The falsified survey. The one I'd drawn in a warm office in Vellmark City while the dust was still settling on eleven men, the one with the galleries marked "unstable, uninhabited, no survivors possible," the one with my signature at the bottom like a tombstone with good penmanship. I had kept it all these years the way a man keeps a knife he was stabbed with. I'd told myself it was evidence. It was a confession I'd never had the courage to mail.

The enforcers wanted Oskar's map destroyed. That was the order—Halvard had heard it from a smuggler who'd heard it from a lockkeeper. Not recovered. Destroyed. Because a map of the pass, in the wrong hands, proved the company had known all along that people lived behind their collapse.

But here was what the order didn't say, and what the enforcers didn't know: they didn't actually know what Oskar's map looked like. Oskar had drawn it in secret, in fever, in a dockside tavern, on paper no bigger than a dinner napkin's cousin. The only man alive who had seen it and could describe it was me.

I looked at my falsified survey, spread out on my knees, twenty years old and smelling of the drawer it had lived in. And I understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only when you're completely out of decent options, that I had been carrying the trade goods for this negotiation since the day I signed it.

* * *

"You want to *give* it to them." Maren said it the way you'd say *you want to set yourself on fire*.

"I want to give them a map."

"The real one."

"They'll believe it's the real one because I'll make them believe it. I'm the only one who can vouch for it. I'm the engineer. I'm the man they've been chasing since the checkpoint. I walk out there with the map in my hand and I offer a trade: the map for the community. They take it, they verify it's genuine—" I tapped my falsified survey. "It is genuine. That's the beautiful part. It's the genuine falsified survey, in my hand, with my signature. The one document in the world that proves the company ordered the pass closed on a lie."

Halvard had stopped rolling up his map to listen. Sela had stopped pretending not to listen. Even Petra, who claimed to be deaf when conversations got interesting, had turned her good ear toward me.

"They'll destroy it," I said. "That's the order. They'll burn it at the pass mouth, and they'll think they've won, and they'll never know that the thing they burned was the company's own noose. Because that survey, in an honest courtroom, doesn't just damn me. It damns the men who ordered it. It damns the whole rotten chain of command that decided eleven men were cheaper than a delay. And I've already mailed copies upstream."

"You what?" Maren said.

"Three days ago. From the depot at Cold Harbor, before the ambush. A solicitor in Vellmark City who owes me—owes my silence, rather, which is a debt with interest. He has copies. If I don't send word by the end of the month, he opens them." I had not told anyone this. It had felt, at the time, like one more lock on one more door. Now it was the only door standing open. "So the map they burn tonight is worthless to them and priceless against them, and they'll strike a match and light it themselves."

"And you," Maren said. "What happens to you?"

"I walk out there and I trade myself in with it. That's the price of the lie holding. They don't get the community, they get the map and the man who stole it. Clean resolution. They can report a success." I smiled, and it felt like an old coat, familiar and wrong. "I've been a success for them before."

"No," Maren said.

"Maren—"

"No. You don't get to do that. You don't get to spend twenty years lying and then buy your way out with one grand honest *gesture*, like it's a fare you can pay and be done. You come back. You come back and you stand in whatever courtroom they drag you to and you say it out loud, all of it, with your own mouth, where it counts." Her voice cracked on the last of it, and I saw that what was underneath the anger wasn't anger at all. "I've had enough of men who fix things by leaving."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the deep galleries, water dripped with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world.

"All right," I said. "I come back."

"You swear it."

"I swear it on the eleven."

She held my eyes for a long moment. Then she said, "Then I'm coming with you to the mouth of the pass. And if I see one flicker of your checkpoint face—the smooth one, the locked-drawer one—I'll know you're planning to run, and I'll break your other arm."

"I only have the one broken."

"I'm resourceful."

* * *

Dawn at the pass mouth was a gray rumor. The monsoon had spent the night hammering the mountain, and the approach road at the bend was exactly as drowned as I'd promised the enforcers it would be—brown water moving fast over the roadbed, carrying branches and a fence post and, at one point, what looked like an entire chicken coop, sailing past with the dignity of a ship of state.

The six of them were camped under a rock overhang with their dynamite crated and their lanterns guttering. The one in charge was a broad, patient-looking man with a company badge and a face like a man doing sums he didn't enjoy. I recognized the type. I had been the type, once, on the other side of the desk.

I walked out along the roadbed with my hands visible and the map case under my arm, and Maren stood back at the tree line with a pry bar she had no intention of using for prying.

The broad man stood when he saw me. "Iversen."

"You have me at a disadvantage."

"Dane. Northern Rail, Internal Security." He said it the way a priest says his order. "You have something of the company's."

"I have something of the company's," I agreed. "That's why I'm here. I'd like to discuss terms."

"There are no terms. There's a warrant and there's a river, and one of them wants you."

"The river can have me later." I set the map case on a crate between us. "Here's the arrangement. Inside this mountain is a community of thirty-one people who have been living in the pass since the collapse. Some of them were born there. The oldest is eighty-one and has never seen rain. If your dynamite goes into that entrance, you will kill thirty-one people, and I will have made copies of everything I know, and the company will spend the next twenty years explaining to a magistrate why it murdered a village to bury a survey."

Dane's face did the sums again. I watched him do them. I watched him arrive at the number he didn't like.

"And if I don't light the fuse?"

"Then you get the map. The original. The only complete chart of the pass in existence, surrendered voluntarily, by the man who's been running from you since the checkpoint. You report a full recovery. The map is destroyed per your orders, the thief is in custody, the matter is closed. Everyone goes home a hero." I opened the case and turned it so he could see the paper inside—the survey lines, the gallery markings, my own signature sitting at the bottom like a signature on a death warrant, which is more or less what it had always been. "You can verify it. You'll find my hand all over it. Nobody else could have drawn it."

That, at least, was the truest thing I said all morning.

Dane looked at the map for a long time. Then he looked at me, and I gave him the checkpoint face—the smooth one, the locked-drawer one, the one I'd worn for twenty years—and behind me, at the tree line, I felt Maren's stare land between my shoulder blades like a hand checking for a runaway's rope.

"Your men search me," I said. "Your men verify the map. And then your men walk away from this mountain and never come back, or the solicitor in Vellmark City opens his envelope on the first of the month. Those are the terms. There is no version of this where you win by lighting a fuse, Dane. There's only a version where you lose quietly instead of loudly."

The rain came down. The chicken-coop river kept sailing past. Somewhere under forty feet of rock, thirty-one people were holding their breath, and I was holding mine, and the liar's oldest trick—tell them what they're afraid of, then sell them the exit—was the only honest tool I had left.

Dane picked up the map case.

He weighed it in his hand, the way you'd weigh a thing that might be a bomb or might be a pardon, and he said, "You'll come with us. Both conditions. Map and man."

"Map and man," I agreed.

And as he turned to give the order to his men, as the crates of dynamite stayed shut and the fuses stayed coiled, I caught Maren's eye through the rain—and gave her the smallest nod, the one that said *the lie is holding, the lie is holding, God forgive me, the lie is holding*—and I saw, past her shoulder, up the drowned road at the bend, a lantern where no lantern should be.

Someone else was coming.
