## 20. Water Rising

**Isandro**

There is a particular kind of quiet that comes just before you do something unforgivable to a mountain. I'd heard it once before, twenty years ago, standing in a survey tent while men younger than me drilled charges into Vellmark's western shoulder. It isn't silence exactly. The river still roars, the rain still hammers, the mountain still groans in its sleep. It's the quiet inside your own skull — the moment when every argument you might make against yourself runs out of words.

I was hearing it now, crouched in the throat of the old air shaft with a satchel of dynamite against my chest and water dripping down my collar like a patient hand counting seconds.

The elder — Halvard, they called him, though he answered to nothing so grandly — had shown me the shaft an hour before. A bore driven up through the rock in the pass's construction days, meant to bleed foul air out of the tunnels. The company had plugged it with concrete and a steel plate when they sealed the line, because a mountain that breathes is a mountain that remembers, and remembering was bad for shareholders.

"Water's above it," Halvard had said, lamplight carving his face into ridges. "Snowmelt lakes, sinkholes, the whole roof of the world sitting in a sponge. You put a charge under that plug, you don't just open a hole. You open a tap."

"That's the idea."

"You'll drown the lower galleries. The approach tunnel. Everything below the third level."

"And anyone coming through that approach," I said, "which is the point."

He'd looked at me for a long time then, the way you look at a man doing arithmetic with his own life in the left-hand column. "You understand there's no walking back up that shaft once the water starts."

"I've had twenty years of walking away," I told him. "I'm overdue at staying."

He hadn't argued. Men like Halvard don't argue with a debt being paid; they just hold the lamp steady while you work.

* * *

**Maren**

The lock passage smelled of wet iron and old rope, and the monsoon had turned it into a throat we were all trying to swallow down in the wrong direction.

Forty-one of them. That was the number I kept counting, the way you count stitches in a wound. Forty-one souls from the hidden community, plus my brother's name written in a dead man's journal, plus one engineer somewhere behind us in the dark doing something I'd told him not to do and he'd agreed to do anyway, which is marriage, if you ask me, or as close as either of us would ever come to asking.

"Keep them moving," I said to Halvard's daughter Sela, who had taken charge of the children with the brisk cruelty of someone who loves them too much to be gentle about it. "No lamps past the second marker. Hands on the guide rope. If the water comes, it comes fast and it comes from behind, and nobody stops to look at it."

"And where will you be?" she asked.

"At the back," I said. "That's where captains stand."

It wasn't entirely true. Captains stand wherever the ship needs holding together, and right now the ship needed holding together at the junction where the lock passage met the old service gallery — the one fork where a frightened crowd could turn the wrong way and walk straight into the flooded levels. I planted myself there with a lantern and my father's boat-hook, which I had carried upriver for three years without quite knowing why, and which now made sense: everything I'd ever carried was for this night.

The rain found us even underground. It came through fissures in the rock in cold threads, and above us — far above, through half a mile of stone — I imagined the sky wringing itself out over the pass, filling the sinkholes, leaning its whole grey weight on the plug of concrete that Isandro was even now wiring charges beneath.

I had kissed him before we split. I want that on the record, since I spent most of our acquaintance pretending I wouldn't have touched him with a barge pole. I kissed him in the lamplight with dynamite dust on his hands, and I said, "You come out the other end of this or I will find whatever afterlife keeps you and make it unbearable."

He'd smiled — that crooked, tired smile of his, the one that looked like an apology that had been rehearsed too long. "You'd need a map," he said.

"I have one," I said. "You drew it on me."

Which was sentimental nonsense, and I'm not a sentimental woman, but the rain was coming through the rock and forty-one people were trusting me, and a person is allowed one sentimental sentence on the night the world ends.

Then I turned and led them into the dark, and I did not look back, because looking back is how you drown.

* * *

**Isandro**

The plug was two feet of poured concrete faced with a riveted plate, and the company had built it well, because the company always built its secrets well. That was the bitter joke of my whole career. We were magnificent at making things stay closed.

I set six charges. Not enough to blast the plug out — I didn't need it out, I needed it *cracked*, cracked along the seam where the concrete met the older rock, so that the weight of the saturated mountain above could do the demolition work for me. Any fool can blow a hole. It takes an engineer to blow a crack. Twenty years ago they promoted me for knowing the difference. Tonight I finally earned it.

The fuse was a slow-burner, ten minutes if the damp didn't kill it, and the damp very much wanted to kill it. I coiled it dry inside my coat until the last possible moment, which meant the last possible moment arrived with me still crouched at the face of the plug, match in hand, listening to the mountain breathe.

And here is the thing I have not told anyone, not Maren, not the page of any confession: in that moment I was not afraid. I want to be honest about that, since honesty was the entire point of the exercise. I stood in the dark with a lit future in my hand and what I felt was *relief*. Twenty years of running, and the running had a finish line, and it was a wall of water with my name on it. There are worse things a man can be given. I've been given most of them.

I thought about Oskar, feverish in the dockside tavern, pressing his chart into my hands like a man passing a fever on. I thought about eleven names I could recite faster than my own birthday. I thought about Maren saying *you come out the other end*, and I thought: I will try. That was true. I did try. But trying and meaning are different currencies, and I have spent my life confusing them.

I struck the match.

The flame caught, steadied, bent toward the fuse. I touched it home and watched the spark begin to eat its patient orange path into the dark, and then I ran — actually ran, legs pumping, because I had promised her, and because a man should die reaching rather than waiting, and because the body is more optimistic than the soul and I let mine have its say.

Behind me, muffled by rock, the charges spoke.

* * *

**Maren**

The sound came up through the floor first — a deep triple knock, like the mountain clearing its throat — and then the rope in my hand went taut with the vibration and every child in the column felt it through their boots.

"What was that?" Sela hissed at me.

"The past," I said. "Keep moving."

Then the air changed. I've spent my life reading water and I'm telling you water announces itself before it arrives — a pressure in the ears, a coldness moving ahead of the coldness, a smell like stone opened for the first time in years. The lantern flames all leaned sideways at once, forty little faces going pale, and I shouted, "High ground! Everyone off the passage floor, up onto the machinery shelf, NOW—" and the crowd became a single animal, which is the most frightening and the most useful thing a crowd can be.

The water came the way Isandro must have known it would, because he was the only one of us who thought in the mountain's grammar. Not a wall — a *rising*. It filled the service gallery below us in minutes, black and boiling with torn-up silt, climbing the walls with the patience of something that had been waiting twenty years for permission. It took the lower passage. It took the approach tunnel. It took everything that led back the way the enforcers had come, and somewhere in that drowned dark were four company men with dynamite of their own and a warrant for a map, discovering that the mountain had filed its counterclaim.

A boy near me started crying. His mother pulled him close. And I stood on the machinery shelf with the floodlight of my lantern shaking in my grip and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do since the charges went off: the water came from behind us, from the shaft, from the plug, from *him*.

Nobody walks up a flooding air shaft. He'd known that when he wired the charges. He'd known it when he kissed me. He'd known it when he smiled and said *you'd need a map*, the lying, brave, damnable man, and I had drawn maps my whole life and never once learned to read the important ones until it was too late.

"Hold the line here," I told Sela, and my voice came out flat and captain-calm and I have never hated anything about myself more. "When the water stabilizes, move them on to the lock. Don't wait for me."

"Where are you going?"

"Back," I said. "Someone has to check whether the river gave anything back."

She grabbed my arm. "Against that current? You'll—"

"I know what I'll do," I said. "I've been doing it for three years. Now let go, because I'm better at it than you are."

* * *

**Isandro**

The surge caught me at the second bend of the shaft, which by my own calculations was thirty meters further than I should have managed, so credit where due: my legs kept their promise even if my prospects didn't.

One moment I was climbing through roaring dark with water at my heels, and the next the water wasn't behind me anymore, it was everywhere, it was the whole world rotated ninety degrees, and I understood with a strange professional clarity that the shaft had vented faster than modeled — the plug hadn't cracked, it had *failed*, catastrophically, and the lake above had come down all at once like a verdict.

I remember thinking: *the galleries will hold. The people will be fine. Maren got them out.* I remember thinking it with the calm of a man signing off on a survey, and I remember understanding, in the same breath, that this was the last lie I would ever tell myself, and that it happened to be true.

Then the water took the ledger of me — every map, every name, every year of flight — and folded it shut, and carried it down into the mountain's oldest pages, and the dark closed over the place where I had been standing like a hand closing over a coin.

The last thing I saw, or dreamed I saw, in the white roar before the black: lamplight, far above, swinging closer. Someone coming back down against all sense, the way boatwomen do.

I reached for it. Old habit. Reaching for light.

* * *

**Maren**

The shaft mouth was a waterfall running backward, if such a thing can be imagined — the whole flooded gallery churning where the surge met the standing water, a cauldron the size of a church. I went in on a rope tied to the machinery housing, because I am reckless but not stupid, though Sela would dispute the distinction later.

Three times the current tore me off the wall. Three times I climbed back. I was shouting his name into the roar, which was pointless, which was worse than pointless, which was the only thing left to do, and the lantern showed me nothing but brown violence and broken timber and the pale ghosts of bubbles rising from somewhere deep.

On the fourth attempt the rope ran out.

I hung at its end in the churning dark, lungs burning, and looked down into the black water that had taken him, and I said — out loud, to the mountain, to the river, to whatever keeps the books down there —

"No. You don't get him. I've lost enough to water. Give him back."

The mountain, being a mountain, said nothing.

But the river — the river has always liked me. And far below, at the very edge of the lamplight, something pale turned over in the dark and began, slowly, to rise.

I dove.

* * *

The water held him the way the mountain holds its dead — loosely, reluctantly, as if it had only borrowed him. I got an arm across his chest and the rope around us both and hauled, and the current fought me for every meter, and I cursed it with language my mother would have washed my mouth out for, and in the end it was not strength that won but stubbornness, which is the stronger force, ask any boatwoman.

I dragged him out onto the machinery shelf under the astonished lamps of forty-one people who had been told to keep moving and hadn't. He wasn't breathing. His face was the grey of old concrete, of plugged shafts, of everything the company ever buried.

I knelt over him in the water and did the only engineering I know, which is the kind done with fists against a sternum, and between compressions I told him exactly what I thought of him, at volume, in front of the children, and if that doesn't shame a man back to life nothing will.

Nothing happened.

Again.

Again.

And then — a cough. Small. Ugly. The most beautiful sound I have ever heard, and I have heard rivers in spring.

His eyes opened halfway, found my face, and focused with visible effort. Water ran out of the corner of his mouth along with, I suspect, several decades of lies.

"Maren," he rasped.

"Don't talk."

"The map," he whispered. "The real one. Left side pocket. Get it upstream." His hand found my wrist, gripped with what he had left. "Names go public. I promised."

"You promised *me*," I said.

"Same thing now," he said, and his eyes closed again, but his chest was moving, ragged and shallow and unmistakably moving, and around us the water in the galleries had begun, very slowly, to fall.

Above us, faint through half a mile of rock, the monsoon went on hammering the roof of the world — and beneath that, deeper, wrong, a new sound was working its way down through the stone. A rhythmic knocking. Deliberate. From the direction of the pass mouth.

Somebody was still trying to get in.
