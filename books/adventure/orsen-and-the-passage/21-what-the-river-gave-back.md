## 21. What the River Gave Back

The river spat him out the way it spat out everything it had no further use for—branches, fence posts, a drowned goat, and finally Isandro Iversen, facedown in a backwater the color of weak tea.

I'd been dragging my pole through the shallows for the better part of two hours, calling his name until my voice went hoarse and then calling it anyway, because the alternative to calling it was admitting what the alternative was. The flood was falling. You could read it in the mud lines on the drowned willows, each one a little lower than the last, the river ticking downward like a clock running out on something. And every foot it fell was a foot of water less to hide a drowned man in.

So when I saw the pale shape tangled in the roots of an alder, my first thought—not proud of this—was *at least I'll know.*

Then the shape coughed.

I have hauled nets heavier than I felt in that moment. I have pulled my boat off a sandbar alone, at night, in rain. None of it was like hauling Isandro out of that water. He was heavy the way drowned things are heavy, heavy with river, and when I got an arm under him and rolled him onto his back his face was gray and his lips were blue and there was a long red scrape down one side of his skull where the water had used him as a whetstone.

"Isandro." I slapped him. Not gently. He didn't respond, so I slapped him again, and this time he made a sound like a door in a bad house, and I decided that was enough slapping for one lifetime.

Getting him into the boat was its own small war. He was a tall man, long in the limbs, and unconscious men are famously uncooperative. I got him over the gunwale by main strength and bad language, and then I just knelt there on the bottom boards with his head in my lap and my hand on his chest, waiting for it to move.

It moved.

I'm not going to tell you I wept. I'm going to tell you the rain was still coming down and there was water on my face and it was nobody's business which of it came from the sky. The rain was still coming down, and I sat in it, in the middle of a drowned farm field that used to belong to my family before the Northern Rail Company decided their straight line mattered more than our living, and I held the head of the man who had signed the report that sealed eleven men into a mountain, and I was *glad* he was breathing.

You can measure a person by the contradictions they can carry. By that measure I was carrying enough that day to sink the boat.

* * *

The backwater had been somebody's pasture once. I knew that the way I knew all this country—by the grief in it. The fence posts still stood out of the water in a crooked line, and the top of a stone wall made a little island where a heron stood like it owned the place, which, given the circumstances, maybe it did. I ran the boat up onto the wall-top shallows and got a fire going on the driest stones I could find, because Isandro was shaking so hard his teeth chattered in a rhythm I could have set a metronome by.

Getting his clothes off a half-drowned man is a job I wouldn't wish on my worst enemy, which tells you something, because I had spent a fair portion of the last week considering him exactly that. He was wearing the oilskin I'd made him take before he went down into the air shaft, and under it he was soaked to the skin and bleeding from a dozen small places where the flood had bounced him off whatever it found. His hands were the worst. The palms were flayed raw, torn up by rope and rust and stone, and I remembered the last thing he'd said to me before the water took him, shouted through the roar: *Turn the wheel, Maren! Whatever you hear, keep turning!*

I had kept turning. The lock gates had opened. The community had come out through the passage, forty-one souls, blinking in daylight like things raised from the bottom of a well. And Isandro had been standing on the lock gantry when the surge came through, and the surge had taken him the way the river takes everything it has no further use for.

I fed the fire. I wrapped him in the boat's spare canvas. I sat with his raw hands between mine and thought about how a man's palms could be ruined like that from cranking a wheel built for twelve, and how he'd done it anyway, and how knowing that made it no easier at all to forgive him, and how I was going to have to forgive him anyway, and how I resented every second of it.

He woke at dusk.

Not all at once. First his fingers twitched in mine, then his eyes moved under the lids, then he made the door sound again and turned his head and was sick up most of the river, which I suppose was fair, since the river had been carrying him around like a borrowed coat. Then he lay back and looked at the sky and said, in a voice like gravel poured through a sieve:

"Did they get out?"

"All of them. Forty-one. The last ones came through the lock as the water hit."

He closed his eyes. I thought he'd gone under again, but he hadn't. He was just lying there with his ruined hands on the canvas and tears running sideways into his ears, and he wasn't making a sound about it, and I decided that was the most Isandro thing I had ever seen—the man could cry without admitting it, could drown without letting go of the wheel, could ruin himself completely and apologize for the inconvenience.

"The enforcers?" he said, when he could talk again.

"Gone." I'd heard it from the elder, Halvard, before I went back out searching. Two of them had been caught by their own dynamite when the flood took the lower gallery. The third had run, and run again, and by the time he reached the railhead at Vellmark the documents were already there waiting for him—Isandro had mailed them upstream from the fuel depot, days before the ambush, the lying liar, planning his lies like a general plans a battle. The falsified survey. The correspondence. The orders, with names and signatures, his own among them. The stationmaster at Vellmark had a cousin who worked for the district magistrate, and by the time the third enforcer came stumbling in asking for help, there were more people asking *him* questions than he had answers for.

"Your papers did it," I said. "The ones you mailed. The magistrate has them. There's a warrant out for the company's regional director, and the *Herald* in the capital has the eleven names." I paused. "Your name's in there too. The one who signed."

He nodded, slow, like his neck was a rusted mechanism. Which, I suppose, after that wheel, it was.

"I know," he said. "I wrote it that way on purpose."

"You signed your own confession into the public record."

"I did."

"You absolute—" I ran out of words, which for me is nearly unprecedented, and settled for throwing a stick at him, which he didn't have the strength to dodge, and which he took with an expression I can only describe as *grateful.*

* * *

He slept. I didn't.

I sat by the fire and listened to the river going down and thought about debts.

Here is what I had promised myself on the mudbank, the day I nearly left him: that if he ever lied to me again I would put him ashore and never look back. Here is what I had promised him in the fever, when my arm was going bad and I couldn't tell the rain from the sweat: that whatever we found, the names went public, even if it destroyed him. He had kept his promise. The names were public. It hadn't destroyed him, but only because the river had tried first and failed.

And here is the thing I couldn't get around, no matter which way I walked it: I had gone back out into that flood for him. I had spent two hours calling his name into brown water. And when I found him I had felt a relief so enormous and so furious that it had no floor to stand on, and grief came up through the hole in it, all my grief—the brother who walked out of the mountain and never wrote, the father who drank himself dead over the rerouted line, the farm at the bottom of this very pasture, under my boat, under my fire, under my knees—and the relief and the grief had gotten so mixed together that I couldn't tell anymore which one was making my eyes sting.

That's the trouble with loving someone you have every reason to hate. The ledger won't balance. You put hate in one column and it keeps migrating.

He woke again in the small hours, and this time his eyes found me and stayed.

"You should have left me," he said.

"I know."

"The boat's cracked. The season's gone. You've got nothing left to—"

"Isandro." I fed the fire. "I spent three years learning how to live with a name on a manifest. *Torben Vask, went out, no further record.* Do you know what that's like? It's like a door left open in winter. You can't close it and you can't stop the cold coming through." I looked at him across the fire, this gray, wrecked, stubborn, lying, honest man. "You gave me a door that closes. One way or another. You gave me that, and you nearly drowned doing it, and I hate you, and I went in after you. That's the whole ledger. That's everything. I'm not going to pretend it balances."

He was quiet a long time. The river murmured over the drowned fence line. Somewhere across the black water a night bird was asking its one question over and over.

"Maren," he said finally. "When I was under the water. At the end. I stopped fighting." He held up his flayed hands, looked at them like they belonged to a stranger. "I'd spent twenty years keeping moving. Town to town, permit to permit, always one step ahead of the ledgers. And down there, in the dark, with my lungs full, the thought that came wasn't fear. It was—*finally.*" His voice cracked on it. "And then I thought about you on the wheel, and I started fighting again, and I don't entirely know why, and it frightens me more than the drowning did."

I put another stick on the fire, because my hands wanted a job.

"Don't frighten yourself on an empty stomach," I said, which was as close as I could come to what I actually meant, and by the way his mouth twitched, he knew it.

* * *

Morning came up thin and washed. The flood had fallen another foot in the night; the fence posts stood taller, and the heron had been joined by two gulls who were arguing over the drowned pasture's drowned offerings. I got Isandro sitting up, wrapped in canvas, propped against the stone wall, and got broth into him—Halvard's people had loaded me with dried fish and a sack of barley before I left, and I'd been making it stretch like a miser.

He ate like a man remembering how. Then he looked out over the water for a while and said, "The community. Where are they?"

"Camp at the old lock station. Halvard's got them organized—he says they've been waiting twenty years for a spring with the pass open, and they're not going to waste this one. They're already talking about the mine galleries that can be cleared, the seep channels. They want to stay. Not hide. *Stay.*"

"And the rail company?"

"Discredited. For now. The director's under warrant, the enforcers are dead or fled or answering questions, and there's talk in the capital—real talk—of reopening the line. Properly, this time. With the names on the station wall and compensation for the families." I hesitated. "There's a survey commission coming. In the spring, when the water drops enough. They'll need someone who knows the old drawings. Someone who knows what was built and what was buried and why."

He went very still.

"I can't go back," he said. It came out automatic, the old reflex, the man who kept moving. "They'll know my name. It's in every—"

"Every paper in the country, yes. Along with the fact that you're the one who put it there. Along with the fact that you opened the pass." I crouched in front of him and took one of his ruined hands, careful of the raw places, and he let me. "You've spent twenty years running from a thing you did. It caught you. It drowned you, near enough. And you're still here, which means the running's finished, whether you like it or not." I squeezed, gently. "There's a difference, Isandro, between a man who can't stop moving and a man who's finally found where he stands. I've carried the second kind in my boat before. My father was one. They're heavier, but they don't fall overboard."

He looked at me for a long time. The gulls argued. The river went on going down, patient as arithmetic.

"The commission will need a boat," he said at last. "Someone who knows these waters. The side channels. The drowned farmland." A pause, and then, carefully, the way a man steps onto ice he isn't sure of: "And the pay is supposed to be decent."

I stood up and started coiling the pole line, because my hands wanted a job again, and because if I looked at him one more second he was going to see my face do something I wasn't ready to have it do.

"Broth first," I said. "Survey talk after. And if you think you're touching my tiller with those hands this season, you're madder than Oskar ever was."

He laughed—a cracked, rusty, half-drowned laugh, the first one I'd ever heard out of him—and then he stopped, and looked past me, upriver, and went still in a way that had nothing to do with laughter.

"Maren," he said. "There's a boat coming."

I turned. Far up the drowned channel, where the water ran silver under the thinning rain, a boat was coming down toward us—small, single-masted, moving with the easy economy of someone who knew these waters the way I knew them, which is to say, in the bones. And standing in the stern, one hand on the tiller, was a man whose shoulders I would have known in the dark, in a crowd, in a dream.

I'd spent three years learning to live with a name on a manifest. *Torben Vask. Went out. No further record.*

The record had just come sailing down the river to find me.
