## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The rain in Corrick's Landing did not fall so much as settle, the way dust settles in a house no one loves anymore. It came down in grey sheets that had been falling, as far as Noor Crane could tell, since before the war, since before the lines closed, since before her father died with his name still wet on a stack of unpaid invoices. It fell on the rusted gantries above the freight yard and made them weep orange. It fell into the flooded cutting where the last locomotive had sat for eleven years, its boiler breathing nothing, its wheels sunk to the axles in black water like something that had lain down to die and been too proud to admit it.

Noor stood under the awning of the freight office with her collar up and her father's map rolled inside her coat, against her ribs, where it had lived for three days now like a second heartbeat.

She had not meant to come here. That was the lie she told herself, and she told it well, because she was good at lying to herself about exactly one thing: that she was not running.

The freight office smelled of mildew and lamp oil and old paper gone soft at the edges. Behind the counter, a man named Dreever — she knew him by reputation only, which was to say she knew he paid his debts slowly but did pay them — was arguing with a clerk about tonnage rates for a cargo that did not exist yet. Noor waited, dripping, watching water find its way through a dozen small failures in the roof and thread down the wall in bright veins.

When Dreever finally turned, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up its purpose years ago, he looked at her the way men in rail towns looked at women who arrived alone in the rain: first with dismissal, then with arithmetic.

"You're the engineer," he said. Not a question. "Crane."

"I'm an engineer," she said. "Whether I'm *the* engineer depends on what you need."

"Resupply run." He said it flatly, testing her. "Upriver. Halloway expedition. They're surveying a route through the escarpment for the new line, and their supply arrangement has — developed complications." He let the word hang there, fat with everything he wasn't saying. "They need forty days of provisions, instruments, spare parts, and someone competent enough to get it all upriver without losing half of it to the rapids. The season's turning. Most pilots won't run it."

"And you're asking me because?"

"Because your name is on the certification rolls and nobody else will take the contract." He smiled without warmth. "Because you're cheap. And because, Ms. Crane, I am given to understand that you are currently between engagements."

There it was. The reputation, arriving ahead of her as reputations do, soaked through worse than her coat. *Between engagements.* A polite word for what had actually happened — the inquiry, the findings, the way her signature on those inspection reports had become worth less than the paper it was written on. She had signed off on bridge bearings along the eastern spur, and when the eastern spur failed — not collapsed, thank God, nothing so dramatic, just settled, just enough to scare the inspectors — every report bearing her name had been dragged into the light and shaken until the coins fell out. Her father's debts had done the rest. Debts she hadn't known existed until the letters started coming, addressed to a dead man and then, increasingly, to his daughter, as though grief were a transferable asset.

"I'll take it," she said.

Dreever raised his eyebrows. "I haven't named a figure."

"Name one. If it covers my board for six weeks, we have an agreement."

He named one. It covered her board for six weeks and, more importantly, put distance — real distance, river distance — between herself and the creditors' agents who had begun appearing at whatever boarding houses she haunted, patient men with soft voices and hard copies of her father's obligations folded inside their coats.

She signed. Her hand did not shake, which surprised her. Perhaps because signing her name had stopped meaning anything some months ago, and a signature that means nothing cannot tremble.

* * *

That night she took a room above the Anchorline Hotel, the only hotel, its sign hanging by one chain and swinging in the wind like a man trying to flag down a train that would never come again. The room had a bed, a chair, a window that didn't quite close, and a table scarred with the initials of a hundred travelers who had passed through Corrick's Landing when passing through meant something.

She unrolled the map.

It was her father's — drawn in his own hand, which is why she kept it, why she carried it through every eviction and every interrogation and every long walk out of town with her bag over her shoulder. Aldous Crane had been a surveyor before he'd been a debtor, and a good one, and his maps were things of terrible beauty: contour lines fine as hair, watercourses inked in blue that had faded to the color of old bruises, marginal notes in his cramped leftward-slanting script. *Bearing suspect here.* *Cut too deep, watch the drainage.* He had annotated the whole valley the way other men annotated their Bibles.

And there, running up the western side of the escarpment like a scar across the page, was the line. The Corrick Extension. Marked CLOSED in red ink so old it had gone brown, in handwriting that was not her father's — some clerk's hand, some committee's verdict, applied years after the collapse that ended it.

Eleven years ago the tunnel at Kilnrock had come down. Seven men inside. The inquiry blamed faulty supports, and faulty supports were blamed on the inspection regime, and the inspection regime's failures were laid at the feet of the senior surveyor of record — Tomas Vane, a name Noor knew only as a name, a man who had died in disgrace before any of this touched her life. The line closed. The company wrote off its losses, collected its insurance, and moved its capital east, and the valley began its long slow exhale into ruin.

Her father had surveyed that extension too. Early on. Before.

Noor set her compass on the map.

It had been his as well — a heavy brass thing, dented on one side where he'd dropped it off a trestle in '09 and never forgiven himself or the trestle. It was a good instrument. It had always been a good instrument, true within a degree, the needle settling north with the calm certainty of a thing that knows its business.

She watched it now, and it did not point north.

It pointed west-northwest. Up the map. Along the brown-red scar of the CLOSED line, toward the escarpment, toward Kilnrock, toward the tunnel mouth where seven men had gone in and the mountain had decided otherwise.

She lifted the compass. Set it down again on a different part of the map, near the yard at Corrick's Landing itself. The needle swung, wavered, and settled — west-northwest again, faithful as a dog, pointing at the closed line as if the map were full of iron filings and the line were the only magnet in the world.

"It's the table," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the rain. "Iron bracket under the wood. Something."

But she picked the compass up and tested it against the window frame, against the washstand, against the floorboards, and everywhere it pointed the same way, steady, insistent, a finger pressed against the glass of the world saying *there. There. There.*

Her father had carried this compass for thirty years. He had died with it in his coat pocket, and the coroner's report noted it among his effects with the dry indifference such reports reserve for objects that once mattered to someone. And now it pointed at the disaster that had ruined another man's name, emptied a valley, and — though the connection was thin as wire and she could not yet see where it ran — tangled itself somewhere in the ledger of debts that had followed Aldous Crane's daughter out of every town she'd tried to make a home in.

Restless hope. That was the feeling, and she hated it, because hope was a currency she'd learned not to carry; it got stolen, or it devalued, or you spent it on something that turned out to be counterfeit. Hope was what had kept her father signing reports for a company that was bleeding the valley dry. Hope was expensive and she was broke.

So she called it something else. She called it professional curiosity. She called it the fact that the contract paid and the work was upriver anyway and a compass was just a compass and iron was iron and rain was rain.

She called it indifference, and she almost believed it.

* * *

In the morning the rain had thickened from sheets to ropes. Noor walked down to the docks with her contract copy in an oilskin wallet and her father's map against her ribs and the compass in her pocket, where she could feel it through the fabric, faintly warm, or imagining it warm, which amounted to the same thing at that hour of the morning.

Corrick's Landing had two kinds of boats: the flat-bottomed barges that hauled gravel and wouldn't go past the first rapids, and the long timber boats that used to haul men and rails up to the extension works before the works died. Of the latter, most were rotted at their moorings, sinking gently into the history they belonged to. One was not.

One sat tied at the end of the pier with fresh tar on its seams and its gear stowed like a boat that expected to leave, and standing in the bow of it, coiling rope with the economy of a man who had never once wasted a motion, was someone she would spend the next weeks learning the hard way.

She didn't know his name yet. She didn't know that he would refuse her money, or what he would ask for instead, or how the argument would start before she'd even stepped aboard. All she knew was what she saw: a pilot's boat, ready for water, in a town where every other hull had given up.

And in her pocket, faint and stubborn, the compass leaning west-northwest, pointing upriver, pointing at the escarpment, pointing at the line marked CLOSED — as if it had known all along, and had simply been waiting for her to stop pretending she wasn't going.
