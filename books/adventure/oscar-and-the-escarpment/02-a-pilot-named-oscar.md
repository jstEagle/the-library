## 2. A Pilot Named Oscar

The docks at Corven's Landing smelled of tar, rot, and the particular sourness of money that had stopped moving. Noor Crane came down the plank road in her father's oilskin coat, too big for her in the shoulders, and stood a while beneath the leaning crane gantry to watch the river decide what it wanted to be.

In dry season it was brown and patient, wide as a rail yard. Now, with the monsoon still two weeks out by every almanac but already sending its outriders north in gray columns, it ran high and green-black, hauling whole trees past the pilings like matchsticks. The water made a sound she'd only ever heard from trains before — that deep continuous grind of something enormous being moved against its will.

There were eleven boats tied along the landing. She counted them the way her father had taught her to count anything: twice, and then once more for doubt. Six were netters, too shallow-keeled for the escarpment run. Three were barges with no engines worth trusting. One was a government patrol skiff with a bored constable asleep under an awning, and one — moored apart from the others, at the end of the rotten pier where nobody else would tie up — was a long, low riverboat with a patched hull and an engine cowl painted over so many times its original color had become a rumor.

A man sat on its gunwale mending a net he clearly did not use for fishing.

Noor had spent the morning being refused. The harbormaster had laughed first and then gone cold when she mentioned the escarpment reach. The chandler had quoted her a price designed to insult. A barge captain named Doss had listened to her whole offer, looked at the sky, and said, *Lady, in season that stretch eats boats and the men who love them,* which she suspected was the most honest sentence anyone in Corven's Landing had spoken all year.

So she walked out to the end of the pier, boots knocking on wet planks, and stopped where the man could see her without turning around. He was younger than she expected — mid-twenties maybe, though the river had a way of making ages uncertain — lean in the way of someone who ate when there was food and worked whether there was or wasn't. His hair was cut short and uneven, as if by his own hand and a knife. He wore no shoes. There was a scar on his forearm, old and white, running elbow to wrist like a survey line.

"I need a pilot," she said.

"You've got a map." He didn't look up from the net. "Maps are cheaper."

"Maps don't run rapids."

"No," he agreed. "They drown people on them, though. That's something."

She let the silence sit. Her father had taught her that too: never fill a quiet with words you'll want back later. Rain began again, fine and needling, spotting the planks dark. Upstream, thunder spoke once, conversationally, like a man clearing his throat before bad news.

"The Halloway expedition," she said. "Survey party, nine souls, camped above the escarpment. They're due to resupply and they can't come down — the route's tied up with their instruments. I'm contracted to carry freight up. Food, batteries, chain, spare parts. Two days' run if the water cooperates."

"It won't cooperate."

"Then you'll earn your pay."

That got him to look up. His eyes were the color of the water in shadow, and they went over her the way she went over a bridge: load-bearing members first, then everything else. Coat, boots, the canvas satchel, the rolled charts under her arm, and lastly her face, where they stopped.

"You're the engineer," he said. "The one from the Kestrel Line."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped wrench. "I'm an engineer."

"You're *the* engineer. The one whose bridge—" He stopped himself, and something in his face closed, not cruelly but completely, like a door drawn shut against weather. "Everyone on this river knows your name, Miss Crane. It was in the papers for a month."

"Then you know I'm cheap."

"I know you're desperate. Different thing." He set down the net at last and stood, and standing he was taller than sitting had promised, all tendon and economy. "Who told you to come to me?"

"No one. You're the only boat not laughing at me."

"That's because I haven't heard the number yet." He glanced upstream, reading the sky the way she read a gradient. "What are you offering?"

"Three hundred for the run. Half now, half on delivery."

"And back?"

"Back's included."

He shook his head slowly, and she felt the whole day tilt toward failure. "It's not enough for what you're asking. Not because of the money. Because of where you're going." He crouched, pulled back a corner of tarp amidships, and showed her: a coil of new rope, a block and tackle, a wooden box with a padlock, and beneath the box, half-hidden, a folded photograph gone soft and gray at the edges from handling. He covered it again before she could see more than a sleeve and a shoulder.

"My price isn't three hundred," he said.

"Name it."

"Five hundred, and you look for someone."

* * *

His name was Oscar Vane, though he said it the way a man says a word in a language he's stopped speaking. They sat in the boat's small cabin while rain worked the roof overhead, and he poured tea from a dented kettle without asking whether she wanted any, and told it plainly, without performance, which somehow made it worse.

His father had been a linesman on the escarpment division. Back when the line ran. Before the collapse, before the inquiry, before the company sealed the tunnel mouths and wrote the whole reach off as geology's fault. His father had been blamed — negligence, falsified inspections, the usual liturgy — and had gone upriver one night in the flood season and hadn't come back. The river kept him. The inquiry closed its file. The family lost the house, the pension, the name's good standing, everything but each other, and then even that, in time.

"They say drowned men drift down to the landing eventually," Oscar said. "Weeks, sometimes. Never him. Eleven years." He turned his cup in his hands. "You're going up past the old tunnel anyway. Freight contract or no. So you look. Not for a body, necessarily. For anything. A marker, a camp, a grave somebody dug. My mother died not knowing, and I'd rather not do the same."

Noor held her tea and thought about debts. Hers were written on paper, itemized, stamped, transferable, the kind that could be sold to men in offices who sent letters with red borders. His was the other kind — unwritten, unpayable, compounding silently for eleven years. She understood suddenly that they were both fleeing something upriver, and that the river was the only direction either of them could afford.

"Five hundred," she said. "And I look."

"And you don't lie to me about what you find."

"I don't lie about findings. It's the rest of my reputation that's ruined."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had been demolished and the rubble left standing. "Fair terms."

They shook on it, and his grip was rope-burned and strong, and hers was ink-stained and calloused in the wrong places, and neither of them commented on the difference.

* * *

The argument happened at dusk, over the chart she spread on the cabin table and weighted with the salt cellar and her father's compass.

"We run the eastern channel," she said, tapping it. "Company survey, revised four years ago. Deep water the whole reach, clear of the gravel bars. Here—" her finger traced the line, "—and here, the channel narrows past Sable Rock, but the soundings say six fathoms even at low stage. We'll have better than that with the water up."

Oscar looked at the chart the way other men looked at forged checks.

"Where did this sounding come from?"

"The company hydrographic office."

"The company." He said it flatly. "The same company that surveyed the line before it fell down?" He leaned over the table, and she caught the smell of engine oil and woodsmoke on him. "Sable Rock moves. Every flood season it sheds gravel downstream and the channel shifts west. Your six fathoms is a four-year-old number for a river that reinvents itself every spring. Run the eastern channel and you'll put us on the bar at dusk with the current setting hard downstream, and we'll spend the night breaking our hull on gravel while the water drops around us."

"The chart says—"

"The chart says what the company paid it to say." He took the compass from under her hand — she stiffened, but he only turned it, studying it, then set it down between them like a piece in a game. "This is a good instrument. Old. Your father's?"

"Yes."

"He trusted paper too, maybe." Oscar pulled a pencil from behind his ear and drew, freehand, without reference, a line of the river from the landing to the escarpment — every bend, every bar, every submerged wing dam and deadfall snag, marked with little hieroglyphs she gradually realized were depth readings, current sets, the memory of floods laid down year over year like tree rings. It took him ninety seconds. When he finished, the western channel — the one every chart marked as unnavigable, choked, abandoned — ran clean and deep as a rail bed.

"That's not possible," she said. "The western channel's been silting since the closure. It's on every closure notice. That's why the reach is dead."

"The reach is dead because the company killed it and called it natural." He tapped the western channel. "The bar at the mouth flushed out in the big flood eight years ago. Nobody resurveyed, because nobody runs it. Except me." He looked up. "Your chart is a story the company tells about this river. I'm offering you the river."

Noor stared at the pencil line. Everything in her training recoiled from it — no soundings, no dates, no stamp, no authority but one barefoot man's memory. And everything in her instinct, the instinct that had kept her alive through the inquiry and the disgrace and the slow humiliation of creditors' letters, whispered that she was looking at the most accurate document she'd seen in years.

Her father had taught her that a survey was only ever as honest as the man walking the ground. She had forgotten that. Or been persuaded to forget it.

"If you're wrong," she said, "we lose the boat and probably the cargo and possibly ourselves."

"If you're wrong, we lose all that in the dark on a gravel bar with no one in sight. If I'm wrong, we lose it in daylight with help nearby." He shrugged. "Pick your disaster, Miss Crane."

Outside, the rain thickened into earnestness. Thunder rolled down the valley now continuously, a long freight-train rumble with no train attached, and the river against the pilings had changed its note — deeper, hungrier. She thought of the expedition waiting upriver with their silent schedule, of the almanacs all agreeing the monsoon was early this year, of the red-bordered letter folded in her satchel next to her father's compass.

She picked up the pencil and, on the company chart itself, in firm engineer's strokes, struck out the eastern channel and drew the western one in its place. It felt like signing something.

"First light," she said. "We run your channel."

Oscar nodded once, and for the first time he smiled properly — brief, crooked, quickly gone, like a fish surfacing. Then he was up and moving, checking lashings, testing the bilge pump, doing the hundred small tasks of a man about to take a boat into country that ate boats, and Noor stood in the cabin doorway watching the black water slide past the pier and listening to the storm assemble itself somewhere upriver, beyond the escarpment, where nine strangers were waiting and one lost man had waited eleven years.

She slept badly in the cabin's narrow bunk, the compass on her chest rising and falling like a small tide. And in the hour before dawn, half-dreaming, she heard it — faint, threaded through the rain and the river-grind: a voice on some distant band, repeating, repeating, then swallowed by static.

She told herself it was thunder.

By the time she came out on deck with the first gray light, Oscar was already at the wheel, and he was looking upstream with an expression she would learn to dread — the expression of a man who has just heard the river say something wrong.
