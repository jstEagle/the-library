## 3. Radio Silence

The river changed its voice on the second morning.

Oscar Vane heard it before he saw it — a new note under the hull, a hollower thrum where the water deepened over drowned gravel, and beneath that, faint as a held breath, the sound of the current quickening. He had grown up with this river in his ears the way other children grew up with lullabies. It had a voice for every mood: the lazy chuckle of low summer, the flat menace of flood, and now this — the sound of a river being fed from somewhere far upstream, gathering itself, remembering what it could do.

He said nothing about it. He trimmed the throttle instead, easing the boat out of the main channel where the brown water ran fast and into the shadow of the cutbank, where the current slackened and the air smelled of wet clay and crushed leaves.

Behind him, Noor Crane sat amid the cargo like a woman guarding a secret. Crates of rice and tinned meat, drums of kerosene, coils of wire rope, a crate stenciled HALLOWAY EXPEDITION — SURVEY PARTY that she checked against her manifest every few hours with the grim diligence of someone paying off a debt she hadn't incurred. Her father's brass compass sat open on her knee. Oscar had noticed it the first day, noticed how often her thumb found it, turning it over, closing it, opening it again, as if it were a question she kept asking and it kept giving her the wrong answer.

"Channel's narrowing ahead," he said. "We'll run slow through the narrows."

She looked up. Rain beaded on the canvas awning above them and dripped off the edge in a thin silver thread between them. "How narrow?"

"Narrow enough." He nodded at the water sliding past, already carrying more debris than yesterday — twigs, seed pods, a whole uprooted sapling spinning slowly in an eddy like something drowning. "River's coming up. You can see it."

"I can see we're behind schedule." She said it without heat, but not without weight. "Halloway's check-in is tonight. If we're not at their forward camp by tomorrow—"

"Then they'll have their supplies a day late and be no worse for it."

"You don't know that."

"No," Oscar agreed, and let the engine carry the rest of the sentence.

* * *

They made the narrows by early afternoon, and the narrows were wrong.

Oscar knew them the way he knew the back of his own hands — every rock that showed its head in dry season, every submerged one that didn't, the seam of green water where the channel ran deepest along the eastern wall. But today the seam had moved. The monsoon rains falling somewhere in the high country were pushing the river sideways, filling channels that should have been empty, erasing the distinctions between what was safe and what was not. A deadfall of stripped timber jammed against the western point, and the water piled against it in a long smooth ridge, deceptively calm, the kind of calm that meant power underneath, not peace.

"Off the throttle," Noor said, beside him now at the wheel. She'd been there since the narrows began, unasked, her eyes moving across the water with a focus he hadn't seen her give anything else. "There's a pull toward the jam. Feel it?"

He felt it. He was surprised she did. "You've run rivers."

"My father ran railroads. Same problem, different medium. Water finds the weak line; so does a train." She braced herself against the wheel housing as the boat yawed, corrected. "Starboard. Now."

He gave it starboard. The bow swung wide of the deadfall's reach, close enough that he could see individual branches shuddering under the current's grip, and then they were through, and the river opened into a long straight reach lined with forest so dense it looked poured rather than grown.

Oscar eased back on the throttle and found his hands weren't quite steady. Not from the narrows. From the trees.

This was where the country started to change. Behind them lay the settled river — fish traps, small farms, the last telegraph wire strung along the bank like a stray thread. Ahead, the escarpment country began, where the hills stood up in green walls and the old closed line had been cut into their faces, and where the government maps went blank and the good maps — his father's maps — went into a box Oscar hadn't opened in nine years.

Noor was watching him. He could feel it the way you feel weather coming.

"What happened to your family here?" she asked. Not gently, exactly. Directly. She seemed to be a woman who had decided gentleness was a currency she couldn't afford.

"The line closed," Oscar said. "My father was blamed for it. That's most of the story."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is upriver." He tipped his chin toward the escarpment, its crest lost in cloud. "Where we're going."

She didn't push. He noted that too — that she knew when to leave a thing alone, which was rarer than knowing when to dig.

* * *

Dusk came early under the storm light, the sky bruising purple in the west while the east still held a sour yellow glow, and at seven o'clock exactly, Oscar switched on the radio.

It was part of the contract — Halloway's expedition checked in nightly at seven, a scheduled call to the relay station downriver, and Noor's resupply agreement required her to monitor the band in case the survey party needed to redirect the delivery. Oscar had handled radios since he was twelve. His father had taught him Morse before he taught him to swim. On this stretch of river, with the escarpment rising between them and every station in the valley, the set crackled and hissed like something alive and unhappy, but at seven o'clock the relay's signal usually punched through anyway, bouncing off the rock walls, finding them in the deep folds of the country.

At 7:00, static.

At 7:05, static.

Noor sat across from him, manifest forgotten in her lap. "Maybe the weather's blocking it."

"Maybe."

"Or maybe their set is down."

"Or maybe," Oscar said, tuning slowly across the band, letting the dial sweep through the noise, "they're fine and just don't feel like talking."

"Do you believe that?"

He listened to the static a moment longer. There was a texture to radio silence, he'd learned. Dead air from a broken set sounded different than dead air from a set nobody was operating. This had the flat, unbroken quality of a carrier wave with nothing behind it — or of nothing transmitting at all.

"No," he said. "I don't."

He shut the radio off. In the sudden quiet, the river's voice came back to him, louder than it had been that morning. The current against the hull had changed again — quicker, deeper, more insistent. Somewhere upstream, beyond the darkening tree line, water was arriving faster than the channel could carry it.

Noor stood, went forward, and stood at the bow with her hands on the rail, looking upriver into the gloom. When she turned around, her face was set in the particular way he was beginning to recognize: jaw level, eyes bright, a woman arranging her fear into fuel.

"We go on tonight," she said. "Running lights off if we have to. I know these reaches well enough from the chart."

"The chart's wrong."

"Then correct it as we go. You said yourself you know this water better than any map."

"That was in daylight, in a river that wasn't rising." Oscar rose and joined her at the bow, and pointed with the flashlight — off, just a black shape in his hand — at the forest pressing close on both banks. "Look at the waterline. See the stain on the stones? We're six inches below where it was this morning, and climbing. Every hour it climbs faster. This river doesn't rise like a tide, Miss Crane. It rises like a decision. And when the front behind us breaks—"

"It hasn't broken yet."

"—it will break all at once, and everything this river has been holding back comes down the gorge together. Debris, logs, whole islands moving. The river closes like a trap. I've seen it take boats with twenty years of river in their bones."

"Then we'll be ahead of it." She turned to face him fully, and in the last grey light he saw how tired she was, and how completely tiredness failed to touch her resolve. "Two men are missing upriver, Mr. Vane. Maybe more. Their radio has gone quiet on the same night a storm is coming, and you want to anchor and wait for daylight. I understand why. But I signed a contract, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I owe debts I can't pay and I'm trying to pay the ones I can. Let me do this one right."

The rain began then, softly, a whispering scatter through the canopy, the opening argument of a much longer speech the sky intended to make.

Oscar looked upriver. Somewhere past the first bend, the escarpment waited with its tunnel mouths and its service roads and whatever truth Edmund Halloway had gone up there to find or to bury. Somewhere in that country, nine years ago, his father had walked into the same green silence and never walked out of it — not really, not with his name intact. And now the river was rising toward all of it, patient and enormous, keeping whatever it kept.

"All right," he said. "But we do it my way. Slow, inside the slack water, and if I say beach the boat, we beach the boat, and you don't argue."

For a moment he thought she would argue anyway. Then she nodded once, sharp, and moved aft to the engine.

Oscar took the wheel. The rain thickened. The forest leaned in over the banks until the channel felt less like a passage than a throat, and the current, quickening, carried them upriver into the dark — toward the place where the voice on the radio should have been, and wasn't.

Half a mile on, the flashlight beam caught something at the water's edge: a survey stake, fresh-cut, its orange flag sodden and streaming.

And tied to the next stake, half-submerged, a strip of torn canvas that had once been tenting — ripped, not rotted, the tear clean and recent, snapping softly in the current like a small dark flag of surrender.

Oscar killed the throttle.
