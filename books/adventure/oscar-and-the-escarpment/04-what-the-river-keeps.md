## 4. What the River Keeps

The river changed its mind about them an hour past the second bend.

Oscar felt it before he saw it — a loosening in the current, the water going from brown to something darker, tea-stained, tannin-rich, the color of country where the forest pressed close and gave nothing back. The banks narrowed. The trees came down to the water's edge like men leaning over a rail to watch them pass, their roots knuckling out of the mud in fists. Somewhere behind them, the storm front they'd been outrunning for two days sat on the horizon like a debt collector, patient, certain of payment.

Noor stood at the bow with her father's compass open in her palm, though there was nothing left to navigate but the channel itself. She'd been quiet since morning. He'd come to understand that her silences were not empty ones — they were full, packed like freight cars, and he had no wish yet to know what she was hauling.

"Camp should be on the left bank," she said, not turning around. "Halloway's survey notes put the forward position at the confluence. There's a gravel bar."

"There is," Oscar said.

"You've been here."

"Everyone has been here. That's what a confluence is." He eased the throttle back and let the boat drift, reading the water the way she read her maps — the V of a submerged log downstream, the smooth glassy tongue where the current ran deep, the boil marks where something waited under the surface with its hands out. "My father ran this stretch forty times a season. I could run it blindfolded in flood."

He hadn't meant to say that last part. It came out of him the way water comes through a cracked hull — under pressure, finding the flaw. Noor turned then, and looked at him with those gray eyes that missed nothing, and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than if she'd asked.

The gravel bar announced itself as a pale scar in the green. Oscar brought the boat in against the current, stern-first, the way you did when you didn't trust the bottom, and killed the engine. The silence afterward was enormous. Rain ticked on the canopy overhead, fat drops working their way down through leaves that had already given up everything they could hold. The air smelled of wet bark and rot and, faintly, of diesel — old diesel, days stale.

Noor smelled it too. Her head came up like a dog's. "That's not us."

"No." Oscar stepped onto the gravel, boots sinking, and stood very still. "It's not."

* * *

They found the camp fifty meters in from the bank, tucked into a shelf of flat ground beneath a stand of ironwood, exactly where a competent expedition leader would put it — high above flood line, shaded, defensible. That was the first wrong thing. Oscar had grown up around survey camps; his father had provisioned half of them. A forward camp was never tidy. It was a wound in the forest, all trampled mud and scattered crates and the particular chaos of men living hard in bad weather.

This one was arranged.

Supplies stacked in neat ranks under tarps, lashed down properly. Cook gear cleaned and hung. A tool cache squared away with corners aligned, as though someone had expected inspectors. The whole site had the look of a stage set between performances — dressed, lit, waiting for actors who had walked off and forgotten to strike it.

"Torn tent," Noor said quietly, crouching at the far edge of the clearing.

It was a wall tent, expedition grade, and it had been ripped open along one seam — not cut, torn, the canvas pulled apart with force. But when Oscar got close and put his hand to the fabric, he found the tear was clean-edged, the fibers frayed outward from a single point. Fabric tears ragged when it fights something coming in. This had been opened from within, deliberately, by someone who wanted it to look like violence.

He said so. Noor looked at him for a long moment. "You know tents?"

"I know lies." He heard his own voice go flat and cold. "Grew up around those too."

Inside the tent: bedrolls still rolled. A mess kit, washed. And the radio.

Oscar crouched over it and knew immediately, the way you know a dead body from a sleeping man. The set was a field transceiver, military surplus, the kind that survived being dropped off cliffs. Its case was dented inward — three blows, maybe four, concentrated on the tuning dial, driven through the faceplate into the guts of the machine. Anyone could see it and think *storm damage*, think *accident*, think *desperate men smashing their lifeline in madness*.

But the dents were inside-out. The case had been braced — he could see the scuff marks where someone had wedged it against a crate — and struck with something short and heavy, controlled, each blow placed to destroy the receiver while leaving the battery compartment intact. He checked. The batteries were still there, still sealed, still good. Whoever smashed this radio had wanted it dead but not useless.

"From inside," Oscar said. His mouth tasted like copper pennies. "Not outside."

Noor came to stand beside him. Together they looked down at the ruined machine, and he watched her do the arithmetic — he could nearly hear the numbers turning over behind her eyes. An expedition goes silent. A rescue contract gets written. Someone upstream needed the world to believe these men were lost.

"Halloway staged it," she said at last. Her voice was steady, but her hand, resting on the tent pole, was white-knuckled. "He faked the disappearance. Tore his own tent. Killed his own radio."

"Why?" Oscar asked, though the question was older than the asking. "Why does a man bury himself alive in monsoon country?"

She didn't answer. She was looking past him now, at the ground, and her expression changed — sharpened, went professional. "Boots."

* * *

There were prints everywhere, of course. A dozen men had lived here. But most of them wandered, circled, crossed themselves out. One line of prints did not wander. It left the camp in a straight, deliberate bearing — northeast, toward the cliff — and Oscar followed it because his feet decided before his head did.

The ground rose. The ironwood gave way to scrub pine clinging to broken rock, and the rain came harder now, drumming on the canopy, running in threads down the stone. And the boot prints kept going, deep-heeled, heavy-laden, a man carrying weight and walking with purpose.

Oscar knew the print. Not this specific print — he knew the *kind*. Ridged sole, steel shank, the issue pattern stamped by the Consolidated Rail Works stores, the same boots issued to every section crew on the escarpment line twenty years ago. The same boots his father had laced every morning before walking up to the tunnel that would be named after his disgrace.

The trees broke open, and there it was.

The tunnel mouth sat in the cliff face like a wound that had been sutured shut and then torn again. Concrete portal, cracked and weeping black streaks. The old service road ran to it and stopped at a barricade gone soft with rot, its warning sign faded to a ghost of letters: *LINE CLOSED — BY ORDER OF THE COMMISSION*. Beyond the barricade, darkness. The mountain had swallowed the rails whole, and the government had written a sentence about it — *collapse due to undermined supports, negligence of the section foreman* — and that sentence had been his father's coffin.

Elias Vane had died eight years ago, two winters after the closure, in a rented room in the low town with a bottle for a witness. But everyone in the valley knew the truth of it: the river pilot's son had been the boy whose father broke the line. Oscar had carried that name through every dock and every bar from the delta to the headwaters. *Vane*. The name people said quietly, the way you say the word for a disease.

And here, leading straight to that tomb, were the boots of a man wearing Consolidated Rail Works issue.

"He went in," Noor said behind him. She'd followed without being asked. Rain ran off her hood; her father's compass was in her hand again, and its needle wasn't pointing north. It was pointing at the tunnel. "Why would Halloway go into a closed tunnel during monsoon season?"

"Because there's something in there worth more than his life," Oscar said. "Or worth proving."

He walked to the barricade. Up close, he could see what twenty years of rain had tried to hide and failed: the collapse debris at the tunnel mouth wasn't rubble. It was *arranged*. Blocks set in courses, too regular, too deliberate — a demolition dressed as a disaster. He'd seen real collapses. Real collapses were chaos. This was masonry. Someone had brought down that tunnel with intent, and someone else had signed the report blaming a tired foreman with a sick wife and no friends in high places.

His father had told him once, drunk and weeping on the night before the inquiry closed, *I checked those supports myself, boy. I checked them twice. They were sound.* Oscar had been eleven. He had believed the report instead, because reports had stamps on them and fathers only had voices.

Now he stood in the rain looking at the lie with his own eyes, and the voice in his memory sounded different. It sounded true.

"Oscar." Noor's hand was on his arm. Light. Careful. As if he were something that might startle or break. "Your father. What was his name? His full name?"

"Elias Vane." He almost laughed. "You've heard it. Everyone's heard it. The man who dropped the mountain on the escarpment line."

"The man they *said* dropped the mountain." Her grip tightened. "Oscar — my father held the salvage contracts on this line. Every debt he owed, every creditor chasing me across the valley, it all traces back through the company that closed this route. If that collapse was engineered—"

"Then the debts are built on it." He finished it for her. "Both our inheritances. Yours is complicity. Mine is shame. Same lie, different pockets."

Thunder rolled down the valley, long and low, and the rain thickened from drumming to roaring. Behind them, far downstream, the sky had gone the color of bruised fruit. The monsoon wasn't coming anymore. It was here.

Oscar looked at the tunnel mouth a long moment. Somewhere in that dark, a man in Consolidated boots was hiding with whatever Halloway had buried — evidence, money, confession, he didn't know. Twenty years ago his father had walked into that same dark with a lamp and a clear conscience, and come out with a sentence around his neck.

"I'm going in," Oscar said.

Noor didn't argue. She looked at the sky, then at the tunnel, then at him, and took the crowbar from the supply pack like a woman deciding which end of a fight she preferred. "Then we're both going in," she said. "But Oscar — listen to me. Whatever your father found in there, whoever made sure he never came out clean—" her jaw set, "—we don't leave without it."

Lightning split the ridge above the escarpment, and for one white instant the whole cliff stood revealed — the road, the barricade, the black mouth of the tunnel — and in that flash Oscar saw, just inside the portal, fresh boot prints in the mud, still sharp-edged, still filling slowly with rainwater.

Whoever wore those boots had gone in recently.

And they weren't alone.
