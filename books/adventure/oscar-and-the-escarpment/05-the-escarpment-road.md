## 5. The Escarpment Road

The service road began where the map said it shouldn't.

Noor had been reading the contour lines since dawn, walking the old rail bed with her father's compass open in her palm, and the road appeared out of the fern brake like a sentence someone had tried to erase and only half succeeded. Gravel gone to clay. Culverts choked with a decade of leaf-rot. The cut banks still stood, though, clean as the day a steam shovel carved them, and the drainage ditches ran true — which meant, she thought, that whoever built this road had known water, and whoever abandoned it had not cared to know anything at all.

Oscar walked ahead with the boat's spare tarp rolled across his shoulders and his machete moving in short, economical strokes. He hadn't wanted to leave the river. He'd argued for it at first light, standing in the shallows with the current pulling at his knees, saying the escarpment was no place in a rising season, saying the tunnel was a grave and graves kept their dead. But the boot prints had gone this way, and Noor had learned in two days on the water with him that Oscar Vane could be moved by exactly one thing, and it wasn't argument. It was direction. Point him at the thing he feared most and he would walk toward it like a man walking into weather.

"Road's cambered wrong here," she said, crouching.

He stopped. Waited. It was how they talked now — she read the ground aloud, and he let her, because the ground was hers the way the river was his.

"See it? Crown slopes toward the cut instead of away from it. Somebody regraded this section after the line closed. Deliberately." She dragged her thumb through the wet clay. "You don't do that unless you want water sitting on your roadbed. You want the frost and the flood to do your demolition for you. Make the abandonment look natural."

Oscar looked at the camber for a long moment. Then he looked at her, and there was something new in his face — not warmth, exactly, but the recognition of a shared language. "My father said the engineers were the honest ones," he said. "He said the men who laid this line loved it more than the men who owned it."

Noor stood and wiped her hand on her coat. Her father had said something similar once, drunk on cheap brandy and older grief, tracing the same CLOSED line on the same kind of map: *They'll tell you the mountain ate it, girl. Mountains don't eat. Men feed them.*

They climbed.

* * *

The escarpment rose in terraces, and the road clung to its face like a scar that hadn't finished deciding whether to heal. Below them the river had become a brown ribbon stitched through green; above, the cliff went up and up into cloud, seeping water from every seam, so that the whole wall wept steadily and the air smelled of iron and stone. Orchids hung from cracks in the rock. Somewhere below, hidden in the ferns, water fell fifty feet without ever being seen.

Noor counted culverts. That was the discipline her father had beaten into her — count everything, trust nothing you haven't counted twice. Seven culverts in the first mile, all sound. Two trestle footings, their timber gray as bone but seated square in the rock. A signal post, rusted to lace, still plumb after eleven years of monsoons.

"This line didn't fail," she said quietly, somewhere around the ninth culvert. "Look at it. Eleven years abandoned and it's *standing*. Timber rots in three seasons in this climate if nobody treats it. This timber's treated. These footings are over-specified. My father—" She stopped. Started again. "Whoever built this built it to outlive everyone who profited from it."

"So what closed it?" Oscar asked. He already knew. She could hear in his voice that he knew, and was making her say it, because saying it was a kind of testimony and he wanted a witness.

"The collapse did. The one at the tunnel." She turned the compass over in her fingers. Its needle swung lazily, finding north through the iron in the cliff, and she thought about all the years she'd believed the story everyone told: unstable geology, catastrophic failure, twelve dead or displaced, an entire valley's livelihood sealed behind a rockfall and a signature. "Except I've been looking at the approach grade for two miles, Oscar, and it doesn't match a collapse. A collapse throws debris downslope. There's no debris field. There's no *spoil*. When a mountainside comes down, it leaves a wound. This leaves paperwork."

They came around a shoulder of the cliff in early afternoon and saw the tunnel mouth.

It was smaller than she'd imagined — a single bore, arched in cut stone, half-swallowed by vine. The rockfall that had closed it was still there, a fan of shattered limestone spilling from the arch like a tongue. And even from two hundred yards, even before she climbed the embankment and put her hands on it, Noor Crane knew.

She knew the way her father had known things: in the wrists, in the teeth, in some animal register beneath thought.

She climbed anyway. She always climbed anyway.

The rubble was limestone, all right. But it was *dressed* limestone — blocks, faced blocks, blocks cut from the tunnel's own lining, stacked and tumbled with a carelessness that was too careful. And beneath the third layer, wedged where no falling rock could wedge, she found a timber prop. Treated hardwood, the kind used for shoring. It had been cut through.

Not broken. Not crushed under load, splintering along the grain the way wood fails honestly. Cut. Sawn near-through and then snapped, so the fracture face showed bright raw wood inside the weathered exterior, and the kerf marks were still visible along the cut face, fine and parallel as piano wire.

Eleven years of rain hadn't erased them.

"Noor." Oscar's voice had gone very quiet.

"I see it."

"They said my father's blasting charges did this. They said he cut corners on the shoring to save money, and the mountain came down, and they took his license and his name and everything." His hands were fists at his sides. "There weren't any charges. There was a saw."

"There was a saw," Noor said. Her own voice sounded far away. "And a crew that came up here after dark, and a foreman who signed off on rotten timber that was never rotten, and an inspection report that lied, and an insurance settlement, and—" She stopped, because the arithmetic had arrived all at once, the way arithmetic does when you've been refusing to do it for years.

Her father's debts.

She sat down on the dressed stone of the tunnel arch, heedless of the wet, and made herself do the sums she had spent her whole adult life not doing. Her father had been the line's assistant engineer. When the collapse came, he'd taken the fall alongside Oscar's father — not the criminal charge, never that, just the quiet ruin, the blacklisted name, the contracts that dried up, the creditors who circled. He'd spent the last nine years of his life paying interest on obligations whose principal she had never once asked him to explain. And she had inherited those papers like a second skeleton, carried them from town to town, worked freight runs and resupply contracts and one humiliating season hauling tourists, all to service a debt that was built on—

On this. On a saw-cut prop and a staged rockfall and a lie with a corporate seal on it.

"They didn't just close the line," she said. "They monetized the closing. Insurance write-down. Salvage rights. Land options along the old right-of-way — I've seen those parcels sit empty for a decade, waiting. Someone bought a railroad's corpse at funeral prices." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "And then they let the men who built it carry the bill. Your father got the charges. Mine got the interest. Between them they paid for this tunnel twice."

Oscar crouched beside the cut prop. He touched the kerf marks with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a scar on someone you loved. "Say it," he said. "I want to hear an engineer say it."

"The supports were cut." Her voice steadied as she said it, because this at least was bedrock, this was load-bearing truth. "Cut from inside, before the fall, and the debris was placed to look like spall from the arch. This wasn't a collapse. It was a demolition dressed as geology, and every document that says otherwise is forged, and every debt collected against it is theft." She looked up at him. "Your father didn't destroy this line. He was destroyed *for* it. So was mine. We've both been paying rent on a lie our whole lives."

The wind came up the valley then, the first real breath of the storm front they'd been watching build for two days, and the orchids on the cliff face shivered, and far below the river changed its note from brown silk to something with muscle in it.

Noor stayed on her knees a moment longer. Fury is a strange thing in the body; hers arrived not as heat but as a terrible clarity, a cold white focus, like the moment before a bridge takes its first train. She thought of her father at the kitchen table with the brandy and the papers, signing, always signing, never explaining, and she understood at last that his shame hadn't been the debt. It had been the knowing. He had known what was done to this line, and he had been paid — in silence, in small settlements, in the mercy of not being prosecuted — to carry the knowledge quietly until it killed him.

Her inheritance was complicity. Fine. Then she would spend it differently.

She stood. Took out the folded map, the one with the CLOSED line running through the escarpment like a suture, and smoothed it against the tunnel arch. With her pencil she wrote, small and precise, in the margin beside the tunnel's marked position: *Sawn props. Dressed stone. No spoil field. Fraud.*

"You keep records," Oscar said, watching her.

"My father taught me. Count everything." She folded the map away. "Somebody falsified an inspection log to hang this on your father. Which means somewhere there's a real log. Original readings, original signatures, the numbers before they were rewritten. Paper survives, Oscar. Men burn paper, but they're careless about *where*."

Above them, at the top of the escarpment where the old right-of-way crested the ridge, there was a flat shelf of ground the maps called a maintenance yard and the locals, she suspected, called nothing at all. And on that shelf, faint but unmistakable in the storm-light, stood the tin roof of a structure that should have been demolished with everything else.

Smoke threaded from it, thin and gray, straight up through the still air before the wind took it.

Oscar followed her gaze. His machete hand opened and closed once.

"Somebody's home," he said.

The first drops of the monsoon hit the dressed stone around them like thrown gravel, and Noor started climbing.
