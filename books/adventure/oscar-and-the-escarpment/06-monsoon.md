## 6. Monsoon

The sky had been lying to them all morning.

Oscar felt it in the water before he saw it in the clouds — a wrongness in the current, a hollowness under the hull, as though the river were holding its breath. The air hung thick and green-smelling, heavy enough to chew, and the forest on either bank had gone silent in that particular way that meant every creature with sense was already hiding.

"It's going to break early," he said.

Noor stood at the bow with her father's compass open in her palm, though there was nothing to navigate here but water. She'd been holding it more often lately, he'd noticed. A talisman against something she wouldn't name. "The charts say three days yet."

"The charts," Oscar said, "say this river can't be run in season at all."

She looked back at him then, rain beginning to freckle the deck around her, and for once she didn't argue. That frightened him more than the sky did.

The first drop hit like a thrown stone. Then the world simply came apart.

* * *

Noor had seen storms off the coast, squalls that walked across the water on white legs. This was not that. This was the sky surrendering all at once, a solid grey weight of water that erased the far bank, erased the cliffs, erased everything but the immediate screaming present of the river rising beneath them.

"Down!" Oscar bellowed, and she dropped flat as the boat shouldered through a wave that shouldn't have existed ten seconds ago. The escarpment was funneling the whole valley's rain into this one channel. The river wasn't rising; it was *arriving*, decades of patience spent in an hour.

They rounded a bend and Noor saw it — a black lattice just under the surface where no black lattice should be. Flood-torn trees, a whole grove traveling submerged, invisible until it was killing distance away.

"Hard to port—"

"I see it—"

The hull struck with a sound like a door slamming in a cathedral. Timber shrieked along the keel, and then something punched through, and the river came in the way rivers do: instantly, totally, without negotiation.

They hit the shallows below the cutbank at speed. Oscar had steered for it without saying so — some pilot's instinct reading the water's color, finding the one place the flood would forgive them. The boat ground onto gravel and listed, taking water in great gulping breaths. They dragged what they could carry up into the treeline while the storm flayed them bare, and Noor turned once to watch their vessel die, its lantern still burning inside the flooded cabin, a small stubborn gold light going out.

"There." Oscar's hand closed on her shoulder. He was pointing at the cliff face, at a dark mouth low on the rock where the old service road met the escarpment — a culvert, a drainage throat bored through the mountain when men still believed the line would last forever.

They ran for it.

* * *

The culvert was dry. That alone told Noor how unprecedented the water was — a tunnel built to carry floodwater standing empty while the world outside drowned.

It was not empty of people.

A voice cracked out of the dark before Oscar's eyes adjusted. "That's far enough! I've got—" A pause. A wet, ragged laugh. "I've got a flare gun with one flare left and no idea how to use it properly."

A girl stepped forward from the shadows. Young — twenty at most — soaked to the skin, mud to the eyebrows, holding a surveyor's transit case like a shield. Behind her, propped against the culvert wall, lay a broad man whose leg was wrapped in strips of shirt gone brown with dried blood.

"You're from the Halloway expedition," Noor said.

"We're *from* the Halloway expedition," the girl said bitterly. "What's left of it. Ilsa Reyes, surveyor. That's Dombrowski, our foreman. Who are you?"

"Noor Crane. Resupply contract." She hesitated. "And Oscar Vane. Pilot."

Something moved behind Ilsa's eyes at the name — recognition, or the ghost of it. But the storm chose that moment to redouble, a pressure change so violent that Noor's ears popped, and they all flinched toward the culvert mouth where the rain fell in a single unbroken column, a waterfall standing between them and the rest of the world.

Dombrowski stirred. "Vane," he rasped. "Vane. Your father ran boats up this stretch. Before."

"Before," Oscar said flatly.

"Then you'll want to hear this more than anyone." The foreman pulled himself higher against the wall, wincing. "Because what happened to your father is what happened to us. Same lie. Same man's money behind it."

* * *

They built a fire from Ilsa's emergency kit, small and shielded, more for the light than the warmth. And in that guttering orange circle, with the monsoon roaring past the culvert mouth like a freight train that never finished passing, the story came out in pieces — Ilsa's pieces, sharp and precise as her drafting work, and Dombrowski's, slow and heavy, each word costing him.

"Edmund Halloway didn't disappear," Ilsa said. "Edmund Halloway *left*. Two nights ago, after we found something in the tunnel survey."

Noor's stomach dropped. "Found what?"

"The original inspection records. Sealed copies, hidden in a maintenance cache from before the closure. Halloway sent me and two others down to catalog them — said it was routine." Her laugh was awful. "Routine. He knew exactly what was down there. Cut supports, Mr. Vane. The line didn't collapse. It was *cut*, and the inspection log proves it was known, and the payout trail runs straight through the company that bought the land rights for nothing afterward."

"And Halloway?" Oscar asked. His voice was very quiet, which Noor had learned was his most dangerous register.

"Halloway took the logs and went upriver with his brother-in-law and the launch. Going to bury them somewhere even the inquiry won't look." Ilsa wrapped her arms around her knees. "He staged the whole disappearance — scattered gear, tore a tent, smashed the radio from the inside so nobody could call out and contradict his story. We were supposed to be part of the tragedy. Lost with him. Very sad, very final, no witnesses."

"But you ran," Noor said.

"Dombrowski carried me half a mile on a broken leg when they tried to stop us leaving." Ilsa touched the foreman's arm gently. "He's the reason there are any witnesses at all."

Oscar had not moved since the words *cut supports*. He sat with his elbows on his knees, staring into the fire, and Noor watched thirty years settle onto his shoulders — the displacement, the blamed father, the family scattered by a lie that had just been confirmed by strangers in a drainage tunnel.

"They framed my father," he said finally. "For the collapse. For cutting supports he never touched. And this whole time—" His jaw worked. "This whole time the man who did it has been running an expedition to find his own ghost."

"He needs the route surveyed," Noor said slowly, understanding arriving cold. "The restoration contracts. If the line reopens, the land rights balloon in value. He buried the evidence so he'd be the only one who knew the truth worth millions."

"And when the expedition 'vanished,'" Ilsa added, "the inquiry stalls, the reopening stalls, and the land stays cheap. Until he's ready."

Outside, the storm hammered on. Water sheeted past the culvert mouth in brown ropes, and somewhere upstream — Noor was certain of it, could feel it the way Oscar felt the river — a launch was fighting north with a dead man's secrets aboard.

Dombrowski coughed, wet and deep. "There's a problem," he managed. "Halloway's got a schedule. Storm like this, he'll hole up at the base camp above the tunnel mouth. Wait it out. Which means—"

"Which means he's close," Oscar said.

"Which means he's close," Dombrowski agreed, "and he thinks everyone who could testify is dead."

Noor looked around the firelight: a wounded foreman who couldn't walk, a surveyor with one flare and a head full of evidence, a pilot with thirty years of grief, and herself — a disgraced engineer with a map of closed lines and a compass that pointed at lies. None of them should have been here. All of them, she realized, had been brought here by the same ruin.

"We need a radio," she said.

Ilsa blinked. "Yours sank with the boat."

"Their radio didn't sink. Halloway smashed the *expedition's* set — but the supply launch carries a backup. Standard practice; my father wrote half those protocols." She was thinking aloud now, the engineer's habit, laying track in her mind. "If we can get that battery, get a set working—"

"You'd be broadcasting into a flood," Oscar said.

"I'd be broadcasting *through* a flood. Every station in the valley is monitoring emergency bands tonight, waiting for word about the lost expedition." Noor felt something kindle in her chest that she hadn't felt since before the creditors, since before the disgrace — purpose, clean and hot. "Halloway wants the valley to believe he's dead. Let's give the valley something else to believe instead."

Oscar studied her across the fire for a long moment. Whatever he saw there seemed to decide something in him.

"The base camp sits above the tunnel mouth," he said. "One approach by water, and the water's a killer tonight. But there's an old service road along the cliff — my father used to run it. If it holds..."

"If it holds," Noor agreed.

Dombrowski laughed, then winced. "You're both insane."

"Yes," Ilsa said quietly. She pulled the transit case closer and opened it: instruments, notebooks, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a single crumpled page she'd torn from the cache — a fragment of the original inspection log, enough to prove she was telling the truth. "But they're *our* insane people now. Crew, right? That's what you call it when the river puts strangers in one boat."

"The boat sank," Oscar said.

"Then we're crew without one," Noor said. "We'll manage. We always have."

Lightning lit the culvert mouth white, and in that frozen instant Noor saw the valley transformed — the river a mile wide and rising, the forest drowning, the whole world remade by water. Somewhere up there, Edmund Halloway believed himself beyond reach.

The storm would end. The water would drop.

And when it did, four people whom the river had refused to drown were going to come for him.
