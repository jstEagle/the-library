## 7. Hollow Signal

The culvert smelled of rust and old water, and by the third hour of the rain it had begun to smell like them too — wet wool, river mud, the copper tang of the foreman's bandaged arm where Ilsa had bound it with a strip of her own shirt. Noor sat cross-legged in the dark with the wrecked radio across her knees, and she thought about her father's hands.

He had taught her radios the way other fathers taught their children to pray: reverently, patiently, as if the voice traveling through the wire were a small soul that needed coaxing. *Don't shout at it,* he'd say, tapping the dial. *A signal is a promise. You make it clean or you don't make it at all.* She had been nine years old, sitting on a crate in a signal shed on a line that was still open then, still honest, before the collapse, before the inquiry, before his name became something men said quietly in offices where money changed hands.

Now she turned the radio over in the culvert's gray light and found the battery housing cracked but whole. The set itself was ruined — Halloway's people had smashed it from inside, Ilsa had sworn it, driven a boot heel through the tuning deck so no one upriver could call for help. But batteries didn't care about tuning decks. Batteries only cared about terminals.

"You're thinking something," Oscar said from the mouth of the culvert. He stood with his back to her, watching the rain come down through the trees in silver sheets, and he had not sat down once since they'd dragged the foreman in here. "I can hear you thinking."

"I'm thinking that a man who fakes his own disappearance still has to eat." Noor pried at the battery cover with the flat of her knife. "Halloway staged all this. Which means somewhere downstream there's a supply launch, a base camp, men waiting on a schedule. And a schedule," she said, working the corroded screws loose, "is just another kind of line. You cut it here, everything behind it goes slack."

Ilsa looked up from the foreman's side. She was younger than Noor had first taken her for — twenty-three, maybe twenty-four, with survey ink still stained into the creases of her knuckles — and she had the particular pallor of someone who had spent two days being lied to by her own expedition leader. "You want to send a signal."

"I want to send a *lie*." The cover came free. Inside, the battery was swollen with damp but intact, its terminals crusted green. "An all-clear. Halloway's voice went silent two days ago; as far as anyone downstream knows, we're either dead or found. If his own launch hears that the expedition is safe and moving, they'll bring the boat up to meet it. Men, supplies, whatever he's got left." She blew the corrosion off the contacts and felt her pulse tick faster, that old engineer's thrill she'd spent years trying to kill. "And whatever comes up that river comes up shorthanded."

The foreman groaned and shifted against the culvert wall. His name was Dario; he'd said little since they found him, mostly because of the gash along his ribs, partly because he'd been the one who helped carry the crates out of the tunnel mouth and knew exactly what had been in them. "He'll know it's false," he rasped. "Halloway checks everything twice."

"Then we make it true enough to pass." Noor looked at Ilsa. "You've heard him key up. His cadence, his callsign habits. Can you give me those?"

Ilsa hesitated — and Noor watched the hesitation happen, watched the young woman weigh loyalty against the memory of a smashed radio and a leader who had walked away from his own people in flood season. Then Ilsa nodded, once, hard. "He always signs off with the coordinates wrong. Deliberately. Said it kept the band clear of listeners." Her mouth twisted. "It kept everyone clear except the ones he paid."

* * *

They worked through the worst of the afternoon, while the monsoon hammered the escarpment like a fist on a door. Noor stripped wire from the radio's dead chassis and ran it to a length of antenna cable Ilsa had salvaged from the forward camp, splicing with wet fingers and swearing under her breath in the cadence her father used to swear, which was to say almost never, only when it mattered. The battery gave a weak, sputtering hum when she tested the circuit — enough, maybe, for a short transmission. Not enough for conversation. One message, sent blind, into the storm.

"Say it again," she told Ilsa. "Slow."

"'Forward camp secure. Party intact. Proceeding to rendezvous, marker seven, above the tunnel.'" Ilsa recited it with her eyes closed, shaping her voice into Halloway's flat, clipped rhythm. "'Crane party located. All clear.'"

Noor's hands stilled on the key. *All clear.* Two words that meant nothing and everything, that would travel out through the rain on a dying battery and land in the ear of some man on a supply launch who would nod, and start his engine, and carry Halloway's strength upstream to where Halloway wanted it — leaving the rest of him thin.

She keyed the set. Static roared, then steadied. She spoke the lie in Ilsa's borrowed cadence, signed off with the deliberately wrong coordinates, and let go of the key as if it were hot.

For a long moment there was only the rain, drumming on the culvert's concrete throat, and the river beyond it running high and brown and fast.

"It's done," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears — steady, when everything underneath it was shaking. Somewhere in the last three days she had stopped being a woman fleeing creditors and become something else, something with a purpose sharp enough to cut herself on. She wasn't sure she liked it. She was sure she couldn't stop.

Oscar turned from the culvert mouth. Rain ran off his jaw. He had listened to the whole thing without a word, and now he said, "Marker seven. That's my father's marker."

Noor looked up. "What?"

"The survey markers along the old line. Numbered when the line was built. Seven sits on the bluff above the tunnel mouth." His voice was level in the way she had learned was most dangerous about him — level the way deep water is level. "That's where they'll bring the launch. That's where he wants his men."

"Then that's where we need eyes before they arrive." She stood, knees cracking, and met his gaze. "Someone has to get there first and mark the position — for us, not for him. Count the men, find where the launch ties off, watch the approach. If we're going to take that logbook—" she saw him flinch, barely, at the word, because Ilsa had told them what Halloway kept in his base camp, the original inspection log, the paper ghost of Oscar's father, "—we do it knowing exactly how many guns are between us and it."

"I know the way," Oscar said.

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I'm answering." He shouldered his pack, already soaked through, already half gone in his manner, the way he went quiet and inward whenever the escarpment itself came up between them. "There's a game trail along the cliff seep. It floods knee-deep but it holds. Three hours in this rain, less if the water rises." He paused at the culvert's edge, and for a moment the lamplight — Ilsa's single surviving lantern — caught his face, and Noor saw something in it she hadn't seen before. Not fear. Something older and more tired than fear.

"They say my father died in the tunnel collapse," he said. "They never found him. They never found anyone, because no one was buried — the supports were cut, we know that now, your road proved it. But they never found *him*, Noor. Not his body, not his tools, nothing." He looked out at the wall of rain. "Whatever's up there, I've been walking toward it my whole life without meaning to. I might as well walk toward it on purpose."

"Oscar—"

"Three hours," he said. "If the launch comes early, don't wait for me. Take the boat and Dario downriver and radio every station on the band until someone listens." And then he stepped out into the deluge and was gone, swallowed by the gray, the way a fish is swallowed by deep water — instantly, completely, as if the forest had simply agreed to take him back.

* * *

The hours after he left were the longest of Noor's life, and she had lived through some long ones — the night the creditors took the house, the morning she signed the resupply contract with a name she was half ashamed of, the day the compass in her pocket stopped pointing north and started pointing, stubbornly, toward a line marked CLOSED.

She kept her hands busy because busy hands kept grief at bay. She re-spliced the antenna. She boiled water over a spirit stove and made Dario drink it with willow bark. She spread her father's map on the culvert floor and weighted the corners with stones, and traced with one finger the service road along the cliff face, the route she and Oscar had climbed two days ago, the route where she had knelt in the rain beside a cut support timber and understood, with a clarity that had felt like drowning, that her father's debts were built on a murder of paper — that the line hadn't failed, it had been *killed*, and the killing had paid for the loan that had broken her family.

*Your inheritance is complicity,* she had thought on that road, and the thought had burned like a brand. But now, crouched over the map in a culvert with a wounded stranger and a girl who'd been betrayed by her own expedition, Noor found the burn changing temperature. Fury was fuel if you could bank it. Her father had taught her that too, though he'd taught it with locomotives instead of words: *fire doesn't care what it burns, Noor. You're the one who decides what it boils.*

The light died. Ilsa slept fitfully against Dario's good side. Noor sat with the radio in her lap and the compass in her hand, turning it over and over, its needle swinging lazily toward the closed line, toward the tunnel, toward marker seven, toward Oscar walking alone through flooded forest to the place where his father had been erased.

Somewhere past midnight — though midnight was a guess, in that drowned world — she heard it.

An engine. Far off, low and diesel-heavy, laboring against the current, coming upriver.

She pressed her palm flat against the culvert's cold stone and felt the vibration of it in the rock itself, faint and rhythmic, like a heartbeat arriving through the earth. The launch. Halloway's launch, answering a lie she had sent into the storm on a dying battery, bringing his men and his supplies up to marker seven — to the bluff above the tunnel — where a pilot with his father's name in his mouth was already waiting in the dark.

Noor closed her hand around the compass until the brass bit her skin.

"Hold on, Oscar," she whispered to the rain. "We're coming."
