## 8. The Logbook

The rain had a voice now, and it was saying his name.

Oscar heard it in the drumming on the canopy, in the hiss of water running off the escarpment's brow in silver ropes, in the groan of the old service road below as the flood chewed at its edges. *Oscar. Oscar.* His mother had said it that way once, the last morning, standing in the doorway of a house the company was taking back. He had been nine years old. He had not answered to that name since — he'd let it wear smooth, like a stone in a riverbed, until it fit no tongue at all.

He climbed anyway.

Halloway's base camp sat on a shelf of cut stone above the tunnel mouth, a place the maps called Mile 41 and the river people called nothing at all, because nothing good had ever come out of it. Oscar came up through the flooded forest with the water at his chest and the current pulling at him like a hand on his collar, one arm hooked through the strap of Noor's dry bag, the logbook's hiding place already fixed in his mind from what Ilsa had told them: *the survey tent, the steel case under Halloway's cot, he never lets it out of arm's reach, he sleeps like it breathes.*

The camp was half-deserted. That was the shape of Noor's false all-clear working as she'd meant it to — Halloway's launch gone downriver to meet a rescue that didn't exist, taking four of his six men with it, leaving only the cook and a watchman who had built themselves a fire under a tarp and were arguing over cards, their voices thin against the rain.

Oscar crouched in the treeline, water streaming off him, and watched the tent.

It was a good tent, expedition-grade, staked into the old rail bed itself. The escarpment rose behind it, black rock streaked white with runoff, and set into that rock was the tunnel mouth — arched, bricked, its timbers rotted to lace, breathing out cold air that even the monsoon couldn't warm. His father had walked into that archway twenty-two years ago with an inspection lamp and come back with findings no one wanted, and three months later the line had collapsed, and his father's name had been nailed to the ruin like a signboard: *negligent. compromised. bought.*

Oscar had spent his whole life being the son of that word.

*Look for someone lost upriver years ago,* he'd told Noor at the docks, because he hadn't been able to say *look for my father's name, look for the truth, look for the thing I've been too much of a coward to dig for myself.* She hadn't asked. She'd just pointed her compass upriver and said, "Then we go."

He went now.

The cook and the watchman never saw him cross the open ground; the rain was that thick, a gray curtain you could hide a whole life behind. He slipped into the tent trailing water, and there was the cot, and there — just as Ilsa had sworn — the steel case, dented green paint, a padlock so old it was more habit than security. He worked it with a sliver of wire from his pocket, hands shaking, and told himself it was the cold.

Inside: paper. Reams of it. Contracts with letterheads he recognized from the framed certificates in the company office where his father had once been proud to work. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a ledger bound in cracked leather, its spine stamped with the railway's crest and a date twenty-three years gone.

*Original inspection log. Section 41–47.*

His father's handwriting. Oscar knew it instantly, the way you know your own face — the cramped, patient letters, the little checkmarks in the margins. He read by the light of a dying storm-lamp, rain hammering the canvas overhead, and the words went through him like ballast through a hull:

*Timber supports at Cut 43 show fresh saw marks. Not rot. Deliberate severance of load-bearing members. Recommend immediate closure pending investigation. Suspect contracted demolition disguised as failure. Names available on request.*

And beneath it, in different ink, different hand — a supervisor's hand, Halloway's own father's hand, or perhaps the younger Halloway's first signature, already practiced at lying — the word *DISPUTED*, and then a page torn out, and then the payoff trail, account numbers, disbursements routed through a dredging concern, a land speculation firm, sums that bought a collapse and sold a man's name to cover it.

His father had not been negligent. His father had been *right*, and rightness had cost him everything, and they had made Oscar grow up inside the shape of that lie like a seed growing around a stone.

Oscar did not hear the tent flap.

"You always were your father's boy."

Edmund Halloway stood in the entrance, soaked to the skin, a lantern in one hand and a boat hook in the other, water running off his jaw. Behind him, faintly, the cook was shouting something about the launch turning back — about the river — but Halloway's eyes never left Oscar, and they held no surprise at all. Only a kind of tired contempt, the contempt of a man looking at weather he'd been expecting.

"Put it down," Halloway said.

"No," Oscar said. It came out steady, which surprised him. "I don't think I will."

* * *

They circled the tent the way currents circle a snag, each waiting for the other to make the mistake. Outside, the world was coming apart. The river had left its banks entirely; Oscar could see it through the tent walls' gaps, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees, and the water was climbing the shelf of stone in surges, licking at the camp's edge, retreating, coming back higher.

"The line was dying," Halloway said conversationally, as if they were two men sharing shelter. "Freight was down. Maintenance costs were ruinous. The collapse was going to happen one way or another — I merely chose the date. Chose the insurance. Chose which names got attached to it." He tilted his head. "Your father chose wrong. He could have taken the settlement. Everyone else did."

"He wasn't everyone else."

"No," Halloway agreed, and something almost like respect crossed his face. "That was the tragedy of him."

The boat hook came up fast. Oscar threw the cot between them; steel rang off the frame, canvas tore, and then they were grappling in the wreckage of the tent, the logbook pressed against Oscar's ribs inside his jacket, Halloway's knee driving into his thigh, rain blowing in sideways through the ruined flap. The lantern went over and died. They fought blind, by sound and grip, two men drowning in slow motion on dry ground.

Oscar took the boat hook's shaft across the forearm and felt the bone sing. He took a fist under the eye and tasted copper and rainwater. But he was younger, and he was angry in a way that had been aging in him for twenty-two years like spirits in a barrel, and when Halloway reached for the jacket — for the book — Oscar caught his wrist and turned with the whole force of the flood behind the motion and put him down hard on the stones of the old rail bed.

Halloway landed badly. Something in his ankle gave with a wet crack, and he cried out — not loudly, but honestly, the first honest sound Oscar had ever heard from him.

And then the river arrived.

It came without drama, the way the worst things do — one moment the shelf was wet stone, the next it was shallows, the next the shallows were moving, fast, waist-deep, dragging the tent stakes loose one by one like teeth being pulled. The tarp over the cook's fire cartwheeled past, burning still. The watchman was screaming from high ground. The tunnel mouth behind them exhaled a gust of trapped air, and somewhere deep in the escarpment, something structural shifted with a boom Oscar felt in his back teeth.

"Halloway!" A voice through the rain, ragged, furious, alive. Noor Crane stood on the flooded road below with the patched boat grinding against a drowned fencepost beside her, Ilsa hauling on a line, the wounded foreman braced at the stern. Her hair had come loose and the wind had it wild and she looked, Oscar thought distantly, like every chart he'd ever ignored — like a woman reading water no one else could see. "The water's coming through the culvert cut! You have maybe two minutes before this shelf goes!"

"Let him drown," Halloway gasped, clutching his ankle, reaching with his free hand — not for help, Oscar realized, but for the steel case, still strapped to the cot frame, floating now. Contracts. Payoffs. Twenty years of careful paper. "Help me save—"

"You'd trade your life for ledgers," Oscar said. He hauled the case free of the frame, wrenched it open against his knee, and let the river take the contracts — page after page after page, ink blooming and dissolving, twenty years of profit unraveling into brown water and gone. Then he pulled the inspection log from his jacket, held it up in the lightning-flash, and shouted over the storm, loud enough for the whole valley, loud enough for the dead:

"This is the only paper that matters!"

Halloway lunged at him then — not for the book, but for his throat, all pretense burned away, a drowning man's fury — and Oscar might have gone into the flood with him if Noor's thrown line hadn't taken him across the chest. He caught it. Halloway caught him. For one suspended moment the three of them hung together — pilot, engineer, saboteur — tethered to a boat and to each other while the escarpment groaned and the tunnel mouth began, at last, to truly fail.

Stone by stone, the arch his father had inspected came down into the river, and the water rose up to meet it, and Oscar held on — to the line, to the logbook, to his father's name at last within reach — and screamed into the rain:

"PULL!"
